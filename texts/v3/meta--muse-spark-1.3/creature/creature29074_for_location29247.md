What lives here learned to live without being seen, even by itself.

You will not meet them with your eyes. You will meet them first as pressures in the air, as heat against your cheek, as a change in the pitch of rain. Then as touch. Then, if you stay still and hum low enough to be counted as harmless, as company.

**The Waders, the Boomers.**

They are the first largeness you learn. You hear them before you ever get close — a boom so low it isn't a sound so much as a loosening in your gut, in your molars. *Oom. Oom. Oom.* A kilometer away. Two kilometers. Answering each other across the peat basins.

When a herd passes near, the vine-ground bounces. That's how you know to step aside. The vines that make the second ground — thigh-thick, rooting every meter — trampoline faintly under their width.

They come out of the dark as columns of warmth and wet breath. Tall as a maintenance shack on stilts. Legs impossibly thin and long, jointed backward, ending in feet that splay like lily pads, three meters across when they set down, spreading the animal's weight over sucking hollow so it doesn't go to the knee. Above, a barrel body steams in the rain, shaggy with moss and fungal felt, hot to the touch. Their heads are small, almost vestigial, tucked low between shoulders. No eyes — just smooth, furred pits where eyes used to be rumored. What they have instead is a throat like a drum and two vast, mobile ear-fans on the sides of their chest that open and close, open and close, tasting the dark for shape.

They graze without grazing. They walk slowly through the aisles of ear-ferns, mouths hanging open, tongues lolling, combing the air for midge-sheets and spore-clouds with baleen-like sieves of keratin. And as they walk they sing, constantly, a low rolling infrasound that maps the herd to itself. Calf to mother. Bull to bull. If you put your hand on the peat when they are near you can feel them through your boots before you hear them: *oom* in the soles, *oom* in the knees.

Every animal on the Heights moves aside for them except the engines. Even the engines slow. It is bad manners, and bad maintenance, to scatter a herd.

**The Choir.**

Knee-deep in the sheet-water, in every braid and seep and quivering peat pool, live the frog-things.

Not frogs, really. When you finally touch one — cupped, shivering in your palm, no bigger than your thumb — you feel how wrong the word is. Their skin is slick but armored with tiny mineral beads, iron they filter from the seep and excrete as chain-mail. Their mouths are too wide, hinged almost all the way around. Their legs are not for jumping; the mud would swallow a jumper. They are for anchoring, splayed toes gripping sphagnum like hands.

They have no need to move. They sing.

Each pool is a choir of hundreds, thousands, layered so dense the water itself shivers and throws up tiny standing ripples. The trill starts high, insect-thin, then folds down in rounds, males and females and juveniles each taking a band of frequency and holding it, tuning to each other until the air above the pool grows heavy. This is not mating call as you understand it. This is work. Their combined voice — joined at dusk-that-never-comes by ten thousand other pools — seeds the clouds. The sound shakes droplets together in the saturated air until they are too heavy for the updraft and fall as warm rain. The marsh sings rain down upon itself, and the frog-things drink the falling.

Old-timers can lie belly-down on the vine-mat, ear to the water, and read the choir like a barometer. A sharpening in the upper third means rain in an hour. A sudden pocket of silence means something large is wading toward that pool through the mud, tearing long and wet.

If you stand in a good chorus at its peak, with rain beading on your eyelashes, you will feel your shirt grow heavier second by second, your hair drip, the leaves around you bow. That is their body, extended into weather.

**The Chitterers and the Tuners.**

Where there is choir there is hunger.

The midges come in sheets — not clouds, sheets, rippling curtains a hundred meters wide that tune to each other. You walk into one and the buzzing resolves around your head into a single metallic note that rises as you breathe, falls as you hold still. They navigate by that shared tone, each insect adjusting to hold the chord. They lay their eggs in still water, in pitcher throats, in the damp folds of your neck if you stop too long. They drink condensate off leaves, off skin, off each other's wings.

And hunting the sheets come the packs.

You hear them as clicks, dry and rapid, sweeping left to right through the fern aisles like a thrown net. *tk-tk-tk-tk.* The chitterers: dog-sized, low-slung, six-limbed, blind and whiskered from nose to tail with stiff vibrissae that read rain-disturbance. Their heads are all mouth and ear — a concave sonar dish of cartilage that swivels, throwing clicks and catching the echo off midge-wing and leaf-edge and your calf.

They do not hunt you. Usually. They sweep around your legs in a rush of wet flanks and hot breath and clicking, and are gone, and behind them the midge chord wobbles and re-forms. Follow a pack by ear and you will stay on solid mat; they know where the hollows are and won't waste a hunt on ground that sucks.

Their pups, if you are lucky and humming steady, will bump your boots with their snouts to test if you are edible, decide you are too rhythmic, and leave a smear of mud as apology.

**The Rooted Ones.**

The plants here are animals in slow motion, and the animals know it.

The ear-ferns — door-sized, black, rubber-thick, faintly warmer than your hand — unfurl their ribs and tremble. Each frond is a drumhead. They do not catch sun. They catch boom. The low songs of the waders, the throb of an engine two valleys over, the hiss of rain — they drink vibration and turn it to heat, to sugar from geothermal chemistry and rot. Put your palm near, not on, and you feel them lean, infinitesimally, toward a steady hum. People who walk alone sing to them without knowing why; the ferns remember good singers and thicken along favored paths.

Above them rise the fungal trees, twenty, thirty meters, trunks fibrous and damp as lung tissue, groaning faintly as they pump water up from the peat and breathe it out through shelf-canopies that drip continuously. Their wood is soft, hot, edible if you ferment it right. Their roots are not roots but cords, thick as your wrist, pulsing with nutrients, running for hundreds of meters to trade rot with the vines, with the moss, with the dead.

And the pitchers stand in the shallows with lids open like mouths. Big enough to drown a dog. They do not want flies. Flies are garnish. They want rain, iron seep, drowned midges, the occasional chitterer pup that missteps — anything dissolved. You hear them in the dark, a hollow *plonk* as condensate spills from lid to throat. Their digestive fluid smells rot-sweet, and the frog-things lay their mineral-armored eggs around their bases for protection, in a trade no one fully understands but everyone respects. Don't trip into one. The walls are waxed and down-turned. The locals will pull you out, but they'll scold you, and you'll stink sweet for days.

Moss covers everything that stays still. Including, eventually, you, if you stay.

**The Quiet Things.**

At intervals, something large displaces mud with a long wet tearing sound and then is quiet.

No one talks much about them, not because of taboo but because there is little to say that is useful. They are large. They are quiet on purpose. Where everything else here shouts its size constantly — *here I am, this big, moving this way, hum with me* — they withhold.

You will know one is near when the choir makes room. A pool will drop its upper third. The midges will flatten their chord. Even the rain on leaves will sound louder because the animals have gone careful. Then that tear: *shluuuck*, mud parting around a flank, a foot lifted very deliberately. Then nothing. A heat in the air where no heat should be. A smell of still water, of eggs and intentions.

They are ambush listeners. They hunt arrhythmia — the stumble, the unsynced splash, the loud-without-pattern that says *prey or storm.* Light drives them mad with curiosity; a match strike will bring one from a kilometer off, not in anger but in investigation, which amounts to the same thing if you are soft.

The waders know them and ring their calves when one is close, booming in tight synchronized pulses that make the air too thick with sound for the quiet thing to parse. The chitterers freeze. The people freeze, or hum lower and steadier, *I am large, I am known, I am not lost.* Most times it works. Most times you hear the tear recede, slow, patient, willing to wait for the next careless thing.

Do not try to picture them. Everyone who has touched one and lived describes something different: a slope of muscle with no head, a mat of whiskers walking, a mouth in the mud. The dark keeps them plural.

**The Engines.**

And then there are the ones that are not born but still live.

You hear them long before you feel them: a deep slow throb, piston-breath, tread-crush. *Chuff-suck. Chuff-suck.* The ground trembles faintly in your feet. The frog choir shifts pitch to make room in the spectrum. The air warms.

The Harvest Engines come like weather along the old riverbeds, routes worn over centuries into compressed peat veins that will hold even a wader, even you. Each one is a hill on legs or on wide mud-treads, moving at walking pace, blacked-out by compact and by survival, no porthole, no lamp, not even a spark leak. Inside their lightless bellies they take in peat, fungal timber, iron seep, water — and cook, ferment, distill, generate. They exhale columns of clean steam that rise and become cloud and become rain. They trail umbilical cables thick as your thigh that hum with current you feel in your teeth.

Put your hand out as one passes and you might brush its flank: riveted iron gone soft with moss, hot, vibrating, rain warping off its heat. Birds — small, soot-black, blind as everything else, with beaks tuned to pick parasites from joints — nest in their armpits and ride them for kilometers, singing sharp location pips that the crew uses to judge clearance in the dark.

For there are crews, or were. Some engines are still tended, single humans or pairs who walk alongside for weeks, listening to valves, slapping chains, bleeding steam condensate to drink, timing their venting to the biological crescendo to seed their own rainfall behind them as they harvest ahead. Other engines have gone feral, crews dead generations ago, still walking their circuits, still processing, still venting on time because the timing is built into cams and governors. The birds tend them now. The moss tends them. The marsh has adopted them as megafauna — too big to prey on, too regular to fear, warm islands in the wet where frog-things gather in the runoff ditch to sing louder.

People speak of them the way sailors speak of tides, by voice. *Old Three-Vent is north today, limping on her left tread. Don't take the west braid.* Each engine has its own throb, its own clank, its own particular valve-pop. Children — rare up here, precious, raised in way-stations — learn those voices before they learn words.

**The People.**

No cities. No glow. Just rope and rhythm.

They live single, or in pairs, moving between engines, between stone domes with cisterns that ping as they fill, following rope guides that run out in six directions, hand over hand through rain. A pump-house keeper on stilts, diesel puttering, who knows by smell when the iron seep runs rich. A tool-keeper in a shack sunk to its windows, every wrench hung by position so it can be found in perfect dark. A tender who walks with a feral engine for company, scraping its vents clean, sleeping in its lee, humming to the ferns.

They are blind the way you become blind here — not in the eye, but in the habit. They do not try to look. They count steps between drips. They learn the difference between living mat and sucking hollow through their soles. They drink rain as it falls, mouth open, or hot metallic bleed from a crawler's valve. Never still water. They hum as they walk, low and steady, so the marsh knows their size. Their clothes never dry. Their skin is pruned permanently, pale and soft from wet, veined with peat stain. Their hearing is surgical; they can pick out a single wayward valve in a storm of choir and rain, can hear a quiet thing holding its breath.

They keep the compact: no light. Not a match, not a lantern crack, not a screen. Light is violence. It blinds the hunters who hunt by echo, silences the navigators who sing the rain down, scatters the herds, starves the ferns. It brings the quiet things. To make light is to be exiled, or worse, to be left untended when the mud takes your leg.

If you stay with them long enough, standing still in the dark with rain beading and the whole vast wet height booming and trilling and throbbing in layers above and below, you will stop feeling like a visitor with broken eyes. You will start to feel, as they do, the scale of it not as distance but as presence — a continent of warm mud breathing under sealed cloud, crowded, lush, rotting and growing back in the same breath, tended by blind beasts and blind machines and the few blind-habited humans who learned to listen.

It does not need to be witnessed. It is complete without you. But if you hum steady, it will let you stay.
