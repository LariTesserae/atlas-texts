At first you think nothing lives here.

That is what it wants you to think. The mat is so wide, the light so total, the work so quiet that you walk for an hour between silver-green fields and hear only your own sealed breath in your mask and the soft *whump... whump...* through blankets, and you decide: this is a factory that outlived its workers. An automation left running.

Then you learn to look slower, and drier.

The first residents are the green ones, and you have to unlearn animal to see them as alive.

They do not bleed. Nothing here bleeds. Snap a strap-leaf and it cracks like a wafer. Inside: white starch packed tight as chalk, or a black coil of oil-resin that smells of turpentine and hot machinery, or nothing but air, honeycombed, a little cathedral to hold brightness. Their veins do not carry sap. If you hold a leaf up to that white sky you can see through it: dust moving, light moving. They drink brightness. They exhale lint.

And they work. That is the difference between these plants and the ones you knew before. Each one has a job and never stops doing it.

The scaffold-trees are the elders. Fluted trunks gray as poured concrete, bark furrowed deep as old hands, patched with graft scars where a limb was bent a hundred years ago and told to become a floor, a wall, a roof, and obeyed. They grow in grids because they were taught to, and now they maintain the grid themselves. Their crowns are pollarded flat — second floors, third floors where the light-collector leaves lie tilted up like solar panels, drinking noon until they glow faintly at dimming-time. Under their bark, in the service tunnels where the light goes blue, you can see their nerves: coppery stamens arcing faint blue, ticking once an hour. You feel it in your teeth before you hear it. Sixty-cycle hum. They filter the flowering fronts. When the gold dust comes they clog, and shudder, and cough — a soft, whole-tree cough — and clear themselves.

Under them, the conveyor-vines. Thick as your torso, running straight for kilometers without wavering, as straight as survey lines. They do not climb. They haul. Their hydraulic fibers contract with a long sigh and drag the vat-fruits along their backs, barrel-sized, thudding slowly forward. If you put your hand on one you feel the peristalsis, patient, warm from oil below. At the kinks — and there is always a kink every eleven kilometers, always in the same relation to three scaffold-trees and a vat-tree, rotated a little, weathered differently — the vine sags in the exact catenary it has held for decades. It has memorized how to sag.

The vat-trees are the stomachs. Squat, crotched, swollen. In each crotch a resin-lined gourd big enough to lie down in, sealed except for a puckered mouth. Inside is dark pulp and heat and the true chemists of this place: engineered fungi, pale sheets and yellow ropes, digesting cellulose into cool-burning oil. You can smell them when a tender cracks a vat for inspection — sweet rotless rot, hot oil, cut hay. The oil runs down through root-pipes to the engine-nodes below the underfloor, where it burns without flame to make electricity and warm mineral. The warmth rises. The high fruits ripen. The high fruits drop fiber. The fiber becomes floor.

Because the floor is alive too. Three, ten, thirty meters of interlashed stems, rhizomes, trunk-rafts, root-cables, leaf-thatch pressed into felt and then into board and then into floor. The looms never stop. Even as you stand on an aisle watching, the living tendrils at the edge are weaving new mat under your boots, white tips groping, knotting. The fields record you. Step off the polished underfloor path onto working leaf and it will hold you — it holds tons — but it will grow up around your footprint in an hour, a perfect cast, and the cast itches with curiosity. The rake-vines come at dusk-dim, long thin arms combing fallen leaves into windrows. The shredder mouths wait at the end of the windrows, patient round mouths in the mat, chewing all night with a sound like someone turning pages very far away. Leaf into fiberboard. Fiberboard into beam. Beam into tree into leaf.

It is plant-eats-plant-makes-plant, and there is nothing else to waste.

So where are the animals? You walk for days and think: none.

Then you see the black one, and understand the animals were redesigned to fit the dryness.

The maintenance beetle is as long as your forearm and matte, absolutely matte, drinking the glare. Not quite chitin, not quite casing. Six jointed legs that place themselves deliberately, silently, on vine and bark and loom. No eyes you can find, or eyes that are only pits for light. It walks along a conveyor-vine, stops at a sucker, and with its forelegs — shears, pruners, fingers — snips. Clean. Catches the cutting and tucks it under its belly to carry to a shredder mouth. It does not look at you. If you block it, it walks around you with the same pace, because you are loose moisture and loose shade and not its job. There are thousands of them, you realize, once you see one. The scuff-marks on the underfloor are not only boots. The slot edges are trimmed. Someone has been pruning, continuously, for longer than you have been alive. In the dim under-mat tunnels you hear them clicking, very faintly, tending the copper stamens.

And the white ones. The drifters.

Three, four paper-thin moths the size of your hand, riding a lint wind. They are so dry they are almost transparent. No mouths for nectar — there is no nectar. They land on a light-collector leaf and fold shut like a book, upper wings veined with faint circuitry that catches and holds charge. They drink light and static. They sit through the electrostatic snap — that hourly *tick* you feel more than hear as the shaking combs discharge — and shiver and open again, whiter. At dimming-time they glow faintly from stored brightness and lift, and go. Where do they lay? In the thatch canyons, in the layered arcades where brightness pools and arrives before it is made. If you stand there you will see your shadow enter a crossing a full second before you do, and if you are still, you will see the moths already sitting where your shadow will be, waiting for you to bring the dark.

People live here, too, but thinly, like moths. Not crowds. Never crowds — voices would carry in the padded air.

The masked tenders. A few. Humans who learned not to leak. Sealed bottles at the hip, sealed reclaimers, suits of waxed fiberboard-cloth that wick nothing, masks of moss-paper that filter gold dust without muting the *whump* underneath. They do not order. They negotiate. To build is to prune, and they are the pruners who talk. You see one kneel by a strap-leaf, tie it back, stake it down, cut six and lash them into a tube-tent that is still alive and will slowly tighten in the night unless reminded. The tender taps the lashings, hums low so the vibration loosens the fibers, leaves spit for the mat to drink in trade. They walk only the aisles, those long straight cuts where dun underfloor shows polished through, warm faintly from engines below through your soles. They sit down immediately when the flowering front comes — low silent gold wall moving sideways through light — and cover mouths and watch, motes on eyelashes, because they know the silence deadens steps and people have walked off service slots listening for themselves.

Mostly the tenders are old. Their masks are scuffed. Their gloves are stained black with oil-resin. They prune around light-lag the way you would prune around a bird's nest. They know which trunk-hall is bigger inside than outside — duck through a slit no taller than you into a sorting hall two hundred meters long, full of slow sorting arms lifting press-fruits into husk hoppers, rollers milling them with dull thuds — and exit three trees over though you walked straight, and they do not comment. They know the mat sometimes grows backward under you to keep tension, so the chimney-stalks you walked toward for an hour stay two kilometers away while your starting tree waits fifty meters behind. They let it. They know the tiling: the same module repeated to the white fraying horizon, gold-green-gold-green shimmering from a crown roof. They do not try to map it. They tend their eleven kilometers as if it were the only one.

At dimming — pearl-gray, not dark — you lie with them on the floor-mat, which gives slightly and is surprisingly warm, and you feel the whole population settle without sleeping.

Below, the engines think in heat. Above, the chimney-stalks, hollow jointed hundred-meter throats, exhale: *hahhhh.* Plumes of warm lint rise and flatten against the invisible ceiling and spread, catching stored light. Along the conveyor lines the collector leaves release phosphorescence, slow traffic of cool green moving barrel by barrel. The beetles keep clicking in the tunnels. The moths fold shut. A tender coughs softly behind moss-paper and checks a graft scar by touch.

Nothing rots. Things cure, husk, flake, powder. Nothing wastes. The continent drinks all loose moisture — even your sweat, even your breath for half a second — not maliciously. It drinks because it is alive, and vast, and dry in a way that has nothing to do with desert, and it has decided, long ago, to be both building and plant, both worker and work, and to keep itself, millimeter by millimeter, rustle by rustle, maintained.
