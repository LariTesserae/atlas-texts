At first, from the rim, you think nothing lives there. Only that it *is* alive — a yellow-green sea shivering in heat. Then you go down and it resolves, and resolves, and won't stop resolving.

Because nothing here is one thing. Everything is crowded into everything else.

Start with what holds it all up.

**The thatch itself is alive, and it behaves like an animal.**

Not trees. Stalks. Millions of them, wrist-thick to wire-thin, jointed, ribbed, hollow. Straw-blond and bone-white and ochre, with only ever a lick of green at the very tip where today's growth spears up — an inch while you watch, pale, blind, hungry — before it cures by tomorrow to yellow. They have no leaves, only dry ribbons and curled shavings that rattle and never wilt because there was never water to lose.

Cut one and it doesn't bleed. It coughs. A puff of resin-dust, a single bead of thick amber oil that skins over before it can fall. That oil is its blood. Dust is its flesh. When a stalk dies it doesn't rot. Nothing rots here. It shreds, and shreds finer, and becomes flour, and spider-fine anchor threads catch that flour in mid-air and haul it back up into the next green tip. The forest eats its own molt forever.

And it listens. That's the part you feel in your teeth before you understand it.

Many stalks are perfect drums — hollow tubes with thin windows, with membranes stretched taut across the nodes. They lean toward sound, not light. Seed-heads cock toward a fresh drumming. Green tips bend toward vibration. Where it is loudest, it grows fastest. Sound is a kind of sun.

So everything that lives on it plays it.

---

**The small loud ones own the air.**

You walk into a boil and it never thins. Cream moths big as your spread hand, flapping slow and shedding wing-scale like snow, laying seams of eggs in every wound in the pith. Black wasps traveling in long ribbons, leg-to-leg chains that sag between stems. Needle-flies that hang motionless and then are somewhere else. Buzzing beetles armored like seeds, heavy, clumsy, armored in chitin so dry it rings when they hit a stalk.

And the locust-things. Clouds of them that come through on a schedule only they know, darkening the whole sun-shaft for three, four seconds — a moving eclipse of wings and jaws — stripping a patch naked in a pass, leaving the stalks shivering clean, and gone. You learn to lie flat when their shadow comes. Everything learns that.

On the stems themselves, legions. Finger-long ants in columns wide as your arm, jaws clicking audibly as they saw ribbon-leaf, drumming in code as they go: *tap-tap-tapause-tap* on hollow nodes, and a whole column wheels left at the signal. Hump-backed crickets that climb inside the drum-stems backwards to amplify themselves, drawing ridged legs across ridged backs — *chirr-chirr-chirr* — millions of them, never quite in unison, until the valley rings like a struck gong and your ribs hum. Mantis-things in dead-leaf cloaks that don't move until they do, too fast to see. Spiders everywhere, stringing lines stalk to stalk that sing in the skylight wind, high thin E-strings, already netting the gap where an ant column just stripped a tip.

Crack any of them open and there is no wet. Powder and fiber and little tight sacs of oil. Dry to the touch. To be wet here would be to die — gummed wings, clogged spiracles. They excrete fine pellets that crumble to dust before they land. The plants snatch the nitrogen straight out of shed chitin. The whole economy runs on flour, oil, shed skin, and noise.

Under your boots, deeper in the springy, creaking mattress of fallen thatch, the larger rustlers move. Pill-bugs the size of dogs, rolling and unrolling with a clatter of plates, shedding translucent armor that hangs on stems and rattles for weeks after they've gone. And burrowers you will never see — only hear, threshing through the mat below, making the floor boing softly and then firm again, opening a path that closes behind you in minutes as new tips spear up to fill light.

Molted skins hang everywhere. The air is full of perpetual chaff — lint, pollen-dust, powdered husk, wing-scale — a low golden blizzard that itches gloriously and gets in your mouth and vanishes your sweat before it can bead.

Nothing rests. Stillness is death. Watch one patch for sixty seconds: green tip up, ants swarm it and strip it, locust shadow passes, neighboring seed-head pops with an audible *pop!* and fires its glass-fiber thistle-fluff into the updraft, moth lays eggs in the stripped wound, spider guy-wires the gap. Making and unmaking are the same gesture, fast as breathing.

---

**And then, once your eyes adjust past the boil, the tall quiet ones.**

People. Or close enough that you call them people.

You don't see them first. You see where they've been. A platform lashed ten feet up between three massive joint-stems — woven mat floor, thorn rail, a ladder of knotted vine swaying empty. A cache left behind: thorn-needles, stone flakes, a hammerhead made of dense knuckle-chitin, polished by use. A snare-line of twisted fiber nooses set across a beetle run, sprung now and full of dust. A hut like a wasp nest, chewed pith plastered over a stem frame, door turned away from the wind, inside a sleeping hollow still warm and lined with moth-wing blankets that shimmer when you breathe on them.

They cut at a single height. You see it everywhere once you notice: notched stems, all cut clean at knee or shoulder, twine-wrapped, repairable. No metal. No machines. Only edge and leverage.

They themselves, when you finally catch one — or the edge of one, painted dust-blond and chaff-pale, motionless in the roar — are dry the way everything is dry. Lean to desiccation. Skin oiled, not sweated; hair bound up in lint-cloth to keep the motes out; mouths wrapped in cloth because the air will pull the wet from your eyes and lips in three breaths. They don't carry water. They've learned the Hollow rule: no one drinks. They chew pith for flour, crack beetles for oil-sacs, collect the amber beads from fresh cuts before they harden, grind molt and seed-lint into cakes that crumble to powder. Oil under their fingernails instead of sap.

They are quiet, which here is camouflage. In a place that shouts constantly, to be silent is to be invisible. They move tidal, seasonal. They put up a platform when a patch ripens loud, harvest the beetle run, sleep in the wasp hut while the drumming is good, and abandon it when the mat eats it — and the mat always eats it. Already a green tip has pierced that platform weave. Already a spider has guy-wired it.

They farm sound. Of course they do. If loudest grows fastest, then you drum to grow. They tap the drum-stems at dusk with knuckle-hammers, calling crickets in, calling ants, stirring a patch into frenzy so tomorrow's tips spear higher, thicker, riper for harvest. They know when to make noise and when to lie absolutely flat and let the swarm pass over like weather.

They are not masters of the Hollow. No one is. They are just the part of the thatch that learned to tie knots, to keep a snare-line taut, to sleep ten feet up so the pill-bugs thresh harmlessly below. Light-handed. Temporary. Already half-dissolved back into fluff and chitin-dust even while they're still living there.

Stand in the center at noon, in the full shaft of normal gold-white sun, in the popping and whining and bassoon-booming and paper-on-paper roar, with chaff sticking to your oiled skin, and you feel them all at once — stalk and ant and cricket and moth and beetle and spider and pill-bug and burrower and quiet oil-skinned human — one single dry animal, meters deep and town-wide, thrashing to stay dry, shouting to grow, building itself upward stalk on molt on stem toward the lip, trying to climb out of its own womb.
