There is no one here to meet.

That is the first thing you learn, when you go looking for whoever lives here. You look for eyes, and there are none. You look for a face, a limb, a mouth, a footprint, a bite taken out of a leaf — and there is nothing. The leaves are perfect. Whole. Unchewed because there is nothing to chew them.

Do not mistake that for emptiness.

Stay longer. Let your own breath go quiet in the bath-warm dark until you can feel the quiet underneath it. What lives here does not come to you. It was already around you. It was already under you and over you. You were inside it before you thought to look.

What lives here is plant, but not plants.

At first you think: trees. Columns rising out of black mat, branching, meeting. Big sail-leaves. Barrel-buds. Thigh-thick vines strung between trunks. That is the easy image, the forest image, and it is wrong, or not enough. A forest is a place where many things live. This is a place that is one thing living.

It is young, and it is already continent-wide. That is possible because it never arrived. It was waiting in the warm water for somewhere to happen, and when low land coughed up from below — raw, fibrous, sharp-edged — it was already there to take it. Root, mat, peat, root again. The ground you thought you stood on is not ground. It is body. A felted rhizome-raft, fibers woven so tight water cannot fall through, holding a shallow black mirror of tannin-brown water trapped in cellulose. Iron and oil and green that never quite rots, because nothing here is allowed to end enough to rot.

Out of that raft, it puts up columns.

Not individuals. Uprisings. The trunks are not trees the way you mean trees. They are vertical pressure vessels, heartwood thick as iron, fissured cleanly from within where the pressure forced them open and then healed around the split. Inside them hot oily sap climbs hundreds of feet without a muscle — lifted only by heat and osmosis and time. You can hear the columns tick and creak as they swell. If you put your palm to bark, which weeps constantly in grown gutters and flumes, you feel the lift as a shudder.

Below, in the mat, are what you would first call roots. They are wrong too. The great knuckled bladder-roots are boilers. They sit half-submerged, steaming, taking in warm ground-water and heating it further with the slow geothermal blood of this low place until it pressurizes. Then —

*dun... dun... dun...*

minutes apart, so low you feel it in your sternum before your ears. A pressure pulse traveling up through wood and water and fiber for miles. That is its heartbeat, if a heart could be spread across a continent and beat that slowly. That is also how it thinks. Not in words. In pressure. A swell here must be vented there. A chamber filling must be braced by tension elsewhere. The dun is a message and a job at once.

Higher up, it breathes without air as you know it. The buds the size of barrels — you wanted to call them flowers and then stopped yourself — fill over hours, taut, slick, hot to the touch. Then a soft wet sigh: *chuff*. A plume of vapor and spores and warm oil-mist exhales into the dark, condenses almost immediately on the sail-leaves above, runs in slow rivulets from tip to tip to mat. Then the bud fills again. Exactly the same. It does not bloom and wither. It strokes. And strokes. And strokes. The spores it releases do not colonize. There is nowhere else to go and no need. They fall, wet, and are reabsorbed into the film-skinned pools that breathe steam in the hollows, that skim over and clear and skim over. Birth and death collapsed into maintenance.

Between the columns run the vines. At first they look like connection. Then you understand they are transmission. Thick as your thigh, horizontal, trembling. Not carrying goods. Carrying tension itself. When one column swells, the vine tightens. When a sail-leaf flaps once, heavily, to dump excess heat and settles again like a shutter, the tremor travels down the vine to brace a distant bladder. It is tendon and drive-belt and nerve in one wet rope. The whole stand adjusts without moving.

And across all of it, the prickle.

In absolute dark you do not see light, but you feel the charge in your teeth, metallic on your tongue, lifting the hairs on your arms. Leaf touches leaf — leather-thick shingles overlapped to make a roof that covers a world — and a spark no brighter than a dying ember jumps. Ozone blooms and fades. The air is so wet it conducts. Vast fields of wet cellulose turning, storing, releasing. The shingle-leaves are condensers and capacitors. The nodules of hardened sap and peat-iron that jut like fresh sharp rocks weep water and hold charge. The forest crackles faintly to itself, never arcing, never burning. Just cycling. Its nervous system is weather that never arrives.

This is how it senses, because it has no eyes to waste. It feels stretch. It feels turgor. It feels heat building behind a frond and the weight of a drop gathering at a leaf-tip in the exact place a drop always gathers. It feels voltage gradient across a mile of mat. It does not look at the world. The world is its interior, and it monitors its interior the way you monitor a held breath.

It does not eat. There is no mouth. Warm water, dissolved iron, sulfur, the slow upwelling mineral milk from below — drawn in through root-hairs fine as mildew, lifted, condensed, precipitated, re-dissolved. Water is blood and coolant and product. Condensation forms on your eyelashes, on bark, on the underside of leaves and falls and rises as vapor and falls again. The level never rises, never falls. The cycle is closed.

It does not grow old. A leaf does not age; it maintains its wax-fresh edge. A drop falls from the same tip to the same pool. A piston sighs. A boiler thuds. The continent hums. Time has been unplugged not by stopping, but by perfecting equilibrium. Young land, vast body, running at idle forever — an infant that emerged already enormous and decided to stay exactly as it is.

That is the strangeness. Nothing here was built. There are no straight lines, no walls, no roads. And yet everything works. Because the worker and the factory and the product are the same tissue. The organism is already architecture, already plumbing, already power grid. Light would only disturb it. An animal would be inefficiency, a leak, a bite that does not return. Sound would be waste heat.

So it stays in the dark it makes for itself, warm and wet and dense, a planet-sized engine of root and leaf and water and charge, patiently doing what machines dream of doing: continuing without end, without owner, without purpose except to keep the pressure up, the water cycling, the spark jumping from leaf to leaf in the black.

You float in it, between wet walls of life, and finally understand you are not observing inhabitants.

You are being circulated through one.
