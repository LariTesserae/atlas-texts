At first you think nothing does.

That is the trick it plays. You stand ankle-deep in warm water that has no ripple but the drip-ripples, you listen to the roar until it becomes pressure, you look at leaf after perfect leaf, lamp after empty lamp, and you think: emptied. Abandoned, but the switches left on. Green took it. Machines idle. No one home.

Stay longer. The place teaches stillness because there is nowhere fast to go. Let your eyes adjust past the sodium orange.

The first citizens are obvious — the green. But the green is not what you thought. It is not a weed invasion. It is a resident, settled, property-owning. Maidenhair out of every weep-hole, moss an inch thick on the north of everything, taro shoulder-high along the tea-colored canals, kudzu nets thrown across steam lines and living on the warmth like a cat on a pipe. Willow and ailanthus and the fig on the roof, rooting in silt that was brick-dust and soot a hundred years ago. They do not eat, except light and condensation. They do not move, except by a leaf-width a week. They do not get eaten. That is their whole contract: take any surface, hold any drip, double and layer and touch pipe and brick and itself until building and plant are not two things.

And because no mouth prunes them, they have become strange in their perfection. Lift a philodendron leaf big as a tray. No scallop. No bore-hole. No aphid constellation underneath, no snail rasp. Clean as poured plastic, beaded with condensate, dripping from its tip on a three-second rhythm you could set a watch to. It is too perfect. In a marsh, perfection means something is missing.

So you crouch. That is when the second citizens show themselves. Not in the air. Never in the air. The air here is uninhabitable if you need to fly or sing or keep your wings dry. It is too saturated, too loud, too hot. A moth that comes over the levee wall on a night wind does not circle the lamps. Its wings load with water in ten wingbeats. It flutters, sodden, falls to the inch of warm water over the slabs, sticks in the oil-sheen and duckweed, dissolves. In the morning you find only two dusted wings in a canal eddy, softening into pulp. The lamps stay clean because the climate eats fliers.

Look down, not up. Look at the water you are standing in.

It is clear, which is wrong. Warm standing water over concrete in a hothouse should be pea-soup. It isn't. It is gin-clear over cobbles, amber with lamplight, an inch deep and breathing faintly with heat. Something is keeping it clear. Something is grazing it night and day.

Wait until your shadow stops scaring them. The film resolves.

On the underside of every submerged slab edge, on the canal walls just at the waterline, on the drowned cobbles: a living skin, opalescent, olive-brown, trembling almost imperceptibly. Biofilm — algae, iron-bacteria, oil-eaters — laying itself down in sheets, digesting soot and petals and lamp-dust and the faint iron bleed from every bolt. And on the film, grazers so small and slow and pale you mistook them for water shiver.

White woodlice, blind as thumbs, the size of rice grains, herding in the moss drip-line, everting their guts onto rot and reabsorbing it. Glass flatworms, two inches long, the color of weak tea, gliding up the canal concrete after dark to fold themselves over a fallen canna petal and dissolve it externally. In the black silt where the slabs have lifted, blood-red tubifex knots — a hundred thread-worms anchored in one hole, waving in unison like a tiny anemone, filtering what the pumps stir. Springtails, not the jumping kind you know but lungless, wingless specks, riding the meniscus in rafts, eating spores that would otherwise mold. None of them chew a living leaf. There is no need. In a place where everything drips and falls and dissolves where it falls, the dead rain continuously. Fallen petals, duckweed die-off, fern spores, fly wings, skin cells from your own ankles — it all comes down to the film. They eat the rain.

And larger, in the canals where the water is knee-deep and tea-dark and warm as blood: hold still by a sluice and watch against the light. A slow V moves without sound. Eels, yellow-bellied, forearm-thick, that came up with the tide-press from below through culverts no man could map, and never left. They live in the sumps, in the boiler-house pit, looping through pipes warm enough to incubate them. And with them — if you lift a lifted slab very slowly, if you do not splash — the sirens. Neotenic salamanders, a foot long, slate-pale, almost translucent, external gills like drowned ferns fanning in the warm current. Eyeless, or eyed and unseeing; in this dusk eyes are a rumor. They do not croak. They have no lungs to croak with. They breathe skin and feather. They lie in the moss-root tangle at the canal edge all day, indistinguishable from a root, and at night walk on four soft hands to take tubifex and drowned moths and woodlice that stray too close to water. Silent, sodden, patient. They are why there are no mosquito larvae wriggling in the thousands of pools that should breed them. Any raft of eggs is vacuumed within hours.

They are drowning-lovers. That is the qualification for citizenship here. Lungs are a liability. Wings are suicide. Shells that need calcium get soft. Eardrums tuned for birdsong burst in the 60-cycle pressure. Only things that can live fully wet, fully dim, fully loud, remain.

There is a third citizenship, not soft at all.

Open — no, don't open, just stand before — the chain-link around the transformers, the green pilot eyes of the electrical cabinets sweating condensation. Put your hand on the engine-house doorframe and feel it: the fifteen-foot flywheel turning and turning, slow as a minute hand, pitman sighing, shaft disappearing into where looms used to be. No one oils it. The oil cups sweat oil on their own. No one shovels. The boilers tick. The empty conveyor rattles its slats around, carrying water and leaves from nowhere to nowhere. The pump thuds, the canal shivers and drops a finger's width, rises again.

Something is minding the store, and it is not human. It is dumber and more faithful than human.

A float on a rod in a sump house, rising a hair with the tide-press from miles away, tipping a mercury switch. A thermostat, bimetal curling in heat, cutting a compressor in, laboring, cutting out. A pressure governor, spinning balls rising and falling with the flywheel sigh, throttling steam by a thread. A photo-eye crusted with moss but still seeing enough orange to keep the sodium lamps buzzing on. Each one is stupid, each one is alive in the only way that matters here: it senses, it moves, it keeps a variable inside a limit.

Keep pumping or drown. Keep warm or cool. Keep lit or go dark.

They are animals. Slow reflex animals with brass bodies and glass eyes. They feed on heat and height-difference, they excrete steam and condensation, they shiver and hunt around a setpoint the way a salamander hunts around a pool. They have outlived their makers because their needs are so simple the green and the water meet them without intending to. The vines insulate the steam lines they ride. The condensation they make waters the moss that shades the cabinets that house them. The oil they weep feeds the biofilm that keeps the sump water clear enough for the float to move freely.

And above them all, the one animal that is all of them.

Stand at the far end with your back to the cattails pushing in through the collapsed packing room, look back at the jumble — brick shouldering brick, pipe stitching to catwalk to gutter to vine, orange pools on wet leaf and black water, steam rising to meet the low white kettle-lid sky, fluorescent blue flickering in broken mill windows — and you feel it as a body. Not a metaphor. A physiology.

The levee is shell. The warm standing water is blood, endlessly recirculated by pump-hearts. The steam lines are breath. The sluices spilling white from level to level are kidneys, gut. The moss and fern and taro are fur, holding heat in, condensing breath out. The rust blooming from a bolt by a micron a year, the lime tongues leaching white, the fig root tightening on the parapet — that is growth. The roar is not noise. It is the sound of a large, warm, dim beast idling, digesting fallen petals and old oil and time itself, exhaling water it cannot hold.

It has no mouth for living leaves because it does not eat that way. It eats warmth and difference and gravity. It has no eyes but pilot lights. It has no footsteps. Your footsteps, splashing too fast, too dry, too loud in your boots, are an irritation in its fur — noticed by a hundred blind white grazers freezing, by an eel pausing mid-glide, by a governor trembling near the red before settling.

It will not chase you. It does not hurry. A drip forms on a valve stem, swells, falls. Another forms. You will leave, drying as you climb back up the causeway into air that can hold itself, into birdsong and fly-buzz that will sound obscenely sharp. It will stay, pumping, warm, lit, layering green on green, film on film, keeping the terms it offers to anyone who would live there:

Be wet or die. Be slow or break. Eat what dissolves. Do not chew what lives. Tend the setpoint and you can stay.
