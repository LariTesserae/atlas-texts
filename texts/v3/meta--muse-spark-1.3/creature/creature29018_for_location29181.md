You will not see them first.

That is the rule here. Nothing that lives up here can afford to be seen moving. The space between things is too wide, the light too flat and white, and above the rim something huge and black is always tilting in a circle, watching without a single flap.

So at first you will be sure nothing lives here but frogs you never see and a deer that comes only at night. You will walk the ringing rock from green pit to green pit and think: lush and empty at once. The green does not travel.

Then you will notice that you are miscounting the domes.

It happens like this. You learn a crossing — that long pink-grey swell with the rust-orange lichen patch shaped like a lung, then fifty meters of bare shine, then the hollow with the tree-ferns. You walk it in the morning when everything weeps. You come back in the evening when the potholes are half-empty and the stone still holds the day under your back. And the swell is three paces to the left of where you left it.

You tell yourself it is the light. The light here lies. Full sun that burns your shadow into the rock, then a green gloom, then a shaft that lights one distant dome and nothing else. Of course a back looks different.

Only all roundness here is not quite roundness. Kneel. You have knelt before to see the fine striations, the ripple of ancient rivers frozen in rock. Kneel again, closer, where the grit has been swept clean. The striations are too regular. They fan out from a center, like growth rings. The lichen-black and rust-orange is not patchy at random — it thickens along the crown where the sun hits hardest, thins to pale grey underneath where it would be damp. And along the lower edge, where dome meets grit, there is a seam. A shadow line, no wider than a thumbnail, going all the way around. Inside it, dust has been pressed, not blown.

Put your hand there. The stone is warm. Everything is warm here. But this warmth gives back a fraction slower, the way skin does.

That is when you hear your own footfall come back wrong. You have heard it before — flattened, then half a second later too loud, as if the rock threw it back at you. Only this time the second sound comes up through the soles of your boots before it comes through the air. A low hum, felt in the molars. Wind moving in chambers below, you thought. Like the place breathing through its teeth.

It is breathing.

They are not mountains that gave up. They are what learned how to live after the mountains gave up.

Low, wide, impossibly heavy. An old one is the length of the bothy of stacked slabs and twice as wide, no higher than your waist when settled — a whaleback, exactly the pink-grey gneiss and quartzite polished to a shine. You could walk over it in full noon and take it for rock. That is the point. The carapace is rock, almost: silica laid down layer on layer over a lifetime longer than yours, polished by wind and rain until it rings when you tap it. Lichen grows in it and on it and becomes it. The orange and black you took for age is camouflage they cultivate by lying still for weeks, letting spores settle in the pits of their backs.

And their backs are pitted. Like the tableland itself, only smaller. Shallow pans and round potholes big enough to cup your hands in. In the morning they weep condensation that runs in beads along grooves toward the blunt head-end, where a slit mouth drinks without opening. Midday, nothing. In the afternoon storm, when the rain comes vertical and warm as blood and hammers so hard it bounces knee-high, they simply lower what little head they have and let it drown them. Every pan becomes a mirror. Steam will rise off them afterward, exactly as it rises off the rock.

Then, in the lull, life.

Because a back that holds water for even an hour in this place will be colonized. Moss first, in the cracks. Then a fern spore. Then an orchid with a waxy white throat, a ginger shoot, a bromeliad cup no bigger than a thimble — enough for a thumbnail frog to live its whole life in and, after rain, to sound its single glass-bell note. On the oldest ones the felt is complete: a traveling green pit, an island that walks. Once a year the white moths hatch all at once from that moss in a blizzard that lasts an afternoon, papering the potholes with their bodies when it is done. The glider high above the rim is not watching you. It is watching them, waiting for that hatch, waiting for a frog to move.

At night they move.

You have to be lying out to understand it. The roar thins to a hiss. Condensation ticks off big tin-stiff leaves into potholes — drip, drip, drip, impossibly loud. Far below, lightning flickers silently inside the cloud sea. The stone under your back is still warm and if you lie still you feel it vibrate, very faintly, with water finding its way down through ancient cracks.

Except it is not water. It is footfall, kilometers away, coming through stone, not air. Air up here is useless for talking. The wind funnels up through the chasms in a deep organ roar, whistles out of tune through arches, drums deafening on bare rock, screams white-noise off the falls. No throat can compete. So they talk with weight.

Four short legs, elephantine but splayed, footpads wide as dinner plates to spread load over thin shells. When settled the legs vanish completely into the seam-shadow; you would swear there are none. When walking — only in cloud-gloom, only at night, only in the hour after rain when the rock is cool and the temporary streams are still hissing toward the cracks — they lift and set down with a slow, deliberate boom that travels through the hollow chambers underneath. Boom. Pause. Boom-boom. The domes ring. Other backs lift their blind heads an imperceptible fraction to listen through jawbone pressed to stone. The old herders say the mountain listens. Put your ear to the warm stone at dusk and you will hear them checking where the damp is.

For that is what they do between the hollows. The green does not spread. It waits where the water waits. Unless it is carried.

An old one will settle for days beside a green pit, head tucked, looking for all the world like another swell. At night it will extend a surprisingly long, prehensile lip and crop — not greedily, never clean — moss, fallen orchid throats, vine curtains, the edge of the felt. It will drink the pothole half-empty and leave the rest for the deer-like thing that comes later, whose footprints you found in mud. It will pick up spores, seeds, frog eggs in the mud caked around its feet. Then it will walk fifty meters across nothing, across ringing emptiness no seed could cross in wind, and settle again. Where it settles, a new damp shadow forms underneath, condensation gathers longer into morning, seep lingers. In its footprints, dust and guano collect. In time, another green island.

They are slow the way rock is slow. They do not try to conquer. They hunker where they can and let the vast polished emptiness go — and then, very occasionally, they carry lushness across it on their backs.

People have learned not to herd them but to attend them.

That low dry-stone windbreak, waist high, curved like an arm around a hollow's edge — it is not to keep wind off people. It is to break the gust just enough that a young one, lighter, less polished, can settle without being staggered. The cairns with the stick thrust in and the scrap of cloth that snaps violently until it shreds — you navigate by lichen worn off by feet over centuries, yes, but in white-out cloud-boil you navigate by sound. Cloth snaps where wind is clean; goes limp in the lee of a settled back you might otherwise trip over and wake. The handful of tiny terraces below one dome, walls collapsing, where potatoes and quinoa were coaxed from grit and guano — guano scraped from backs after the moth hatch, grit swept from footprints. The stone quern left upside down so rain will not fill it and crack it. The digging stick, polished by hands, leaning against a rock that is not a rock, left there because you do not dig when She is sleeping.

No metal, or almost none. Everything stone on stone, wood, woven fiber. Things made to be mended with what is at hand, or abandoned. You do not hammer here. You listen.

If you stay long enough you will do what they do. You will learn the habits of dampness instead of looking for a river. You will drink from a back-pool cupped in your hands in the morning and be grateful it is half-empty by evening. You will stop shouting, because your voice comes back wrong, and press your jaw to stone instead. You will feel in your knees the rule of this place: the ground is finished, the sky is not. The rock has settled. Everything above it rages and creates and destroys itself a dozen times a day.

Lie out one night when the wind has dropped a fraction. Find a long low swell that was not quite there yesterday. Lie with your spine along its crown. It will be warm. It will tick as it cools. Far below, the cloud sea will flicker. Above, the stars will be close enough to hurt. And very faintly, through bone, you will feel the others moving out across the tableland — boom, pause, boom-boom — carrying their gardens carefully across the emptiness, breathing through their teeth while the mountain breathes with them.
