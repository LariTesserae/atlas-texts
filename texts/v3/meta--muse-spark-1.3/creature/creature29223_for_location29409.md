At first you think nothing lives here.

That's what the flat wants you to think. From the rim to the shivering blue edge there is no tree to hold a nest, no back moving against the ground, no standing figure. Only the great felted skin breathing under ordinary sun, and the wind combing the sideways reeds until they wheeze.

Then the ground crackles under your feet and you realize the crackle is jumping away from you.

Life up here does not stand up. It lies low, or it stays in the air, or it stays underneath. Weight is the price of everything, and everything that has lasted has learned how not to pay it.

The largest things that live their whole lives on the skin are the sheep, and the people who keep them.

You will see the sheep before you see the people, because there are more of them and because they are the color the place allows: dun, stone-grey, lichen-pale, a short tight wool that holds no dew and snags on nothing. They are small — a lowland ewe would look like a monster beside them — low to the mat, barrel-bellied, splay-toed. Their feet are the whole of their intelligence. A high sheep does not look where it steps, it listens with its soles. It will walk the long way around a lush green circle that to your eyes looks like the best grazing, because under that green the mat is thin over a throat and the updraft damp has fooled the moss into growing rich. The lambs learn by following the old ewes nose-to-tail in single file along the thick spines, and by freezing.

They freeze a dozen times an hour. When the insect chorus dips — and it does, in a sudden held-breath wave you will learn to dread — every sheep stops dead, head up, ears swivelled. It is not obedience. It is agreement. Something that hunts by silence is passing.

The people are fewer than the sheep by far and the same color at distance. A family to a hut, a hut half-sunk a mile or three from the next, so that from any one door you see no other smoke. They are light people, narrow-chested from the thin air, slow-moving by necessity, not from laziness. A child born up here learns to breathe shallow and to walk as if the world were an instrument: toe first, then the flat of the foot, weight eased down, pause to feel for the drum.

You will meet them first as a silhouette: a boy on the lookout platform, four lashed reed-poles with a turf mat on top, sitting all day with his knees tucked because there is nowhere to stand. He does not wave. He watches the flock line and taps his staff every so often against the platform leg — *tok tok* — not to drive them but to hear how the mat answers that day. Every herder carries that staff, a length of bundled reed as tall as they are, polished dark at the grip by hands. With it they test, three taps ahead before every third step. Thick mat returns a dull thud. Thin mat returns a hollow boom that you feel in your molars. They can hear the difference through the general roar that deafens you.

Up close they are hand-people. Their eyes squint permanently against glare and midge-smoke, their ears are wadded in the day with little twists of rolled felt because noon at full chorus will ring your skull for hours after. Take the plugs out at dusk and you find they hear better than you — they have to, to live inside loudness. The women keep their hair cropped short or bound tight in fiber net, otherwise the jewel-flies tangle in it. The men and women both go in soft shoes of layered moss-felt, no hard sole, laced with creeper cord, resoled constantly. Barefoot in the hut. Their hands are broad, cracked at the knuckles from squeezing water morning after morning — wring, catch in a cup-lichen, drink — and from peeling thatch.

That peeling is their farming. Where the mat runs three layers deep, and only there, they cut blocks with a bone knife, for walls, for roof-patch, for fiber. The cut reveals the stratigraphy: green living top, brown half-felt middle, black humus below that smells sweet and ancient. And in every hole they have cut, before they leave, they lay a flat stone. Give back weight, they say, so the wind cannot get a finger under the skin and start a tear. Children are taught this before they are taught names: a tear left alone will widen yards in a season, curl back like a lip, become a new breathing hole that swallows pasture whistling into the dark.

Their eating is mostly from the air and from below, not from the sheep, which are too valuable to kill often. At first light the women and older girls walk the reed beds with fine nets strung between two poles, sweeping the midge columns where they rise smoking off the breathing holes. A good morning is a sackful, pounded with root flour into dark cakes that sizzle on the fire stone. The boys go down. On knotted fiber ladders, down ten, twenty feet into the cool throat where cold air sighs up at noon, to scrape guano from ledges where thousands of swifts and swallows cling in the dark, and to fill skin bags drip by drip from seep, and to hunt.

What they hunt does not have eyes. In the shafts live the white crickets, big as mice, blind, fat on detritus and moss root, chirping endlessly through the night — that sound you thought was water ticking. The children catch them by hand in the lamplight, quick, and roast them in the coals. They taste like nut and damp. The guano goes back up to thin pasture patches, a handful smeared where grazing has worn the copper moss pale.

All day the air belongs to others.

If you count bodies, this is an insect plateau. Midges in pillars over every mouth, thrips and jewel-flies like thrown glitter, thumb-long grasshoppers that launch clicking from the sideways reeds until the whole ground seems to fizz. Under the mat edge, in the thatch tunnels, run the small furred things: burrowing cavies in troops, popping up to whistle and vanishing, and the blind mole-rats, huge-headed, fat as loaves on roots, pushing up soft molehills of stone-flour that the herders read like news. And the snakes — you will almost step on one before you see it — flat, slow, patterned exactly in grey-green cartwheel and copper, lichen become muscle, waiting where the cavies run.

And then the birds that make the hollow boom of wings at noon. Swifts and swallows and whiteback kites pour up out of the shafts at morning in tornadoes so dense they shadow the moss, screaming, feeding on the midge smoke all day, then pouring back down at dusk wing-on-wing to roost in the dark where no fox can follow. Higher and slower move the true grazers of the sky: skeins of long-necked geese and whistling ducks, moving pan to pan for that inch-deep morning mirror, landing with a thunderclap of webbed feet in water barely enough to wet them, quarrelling, preening, rising again when it evaporates by ten. They do not live here. They visit the wetness, as everything does.

What hunts here hunts by listening, because there is nowhere to hide on a table.

The striped shaft-fox is the size of a lowland fox but lighter, long-legged, splay-pawed like the sheep so it can cross thin mat without breaking through. Its back is striped in dirty white and moss-dark, disruptive against reed shadow. Its ears are enormous, translucent at the rim, and its forepaws are almost hands — it will stop, press a paw flat to the felt, and wait to feel your steps coming through the weave from a hundred yards off. Then it runs low, not straight but in a curve to cut you off at a thick spine where you must pass. The herders lose a lamb to it every season and speak of it without hatred. It tests, like they do.

Above it, the great mottled harrier hangs all afternoon without beating, wings in a shallow V, belly patterned like sun-bleached thatch and shadow-hole. It does not look for movement. It listens for the dip. When the insect sawing falters — a wave of quiet rolling across the flat for no reason your ears can find — it drops like a stone onto the place where a cavy or a young bird has frozen a half-second too late. The sheep know before you do; they freeze with it, heads up, until the chorus saws back up.

At night the herders go inside their turf huts and trim back the living roof so the smoke hole does not seal, and the plateau changes tenants. The reeds go quiet as dew weights their cut tips. The flocks are down in the earth. What is left is drip and tick and that endless cricket chirp from below, and fog pouring up out of the breathing holes in slow columns smelling of wet stone and resin and guano and thousand-year humus. The boy climbs down from his platform. An old woman banks the fire, coils damp fiber to dry by morning, checks the copper awl in its hide wrap — treasured, traded up from below, sharpened to a needle for mending nets and hides.

They sleep light. Everyone up here sleeps light, sheep standing with ears turning, fox with paw on the ground, crickets with no sleep at all, people with one ear unplugged to hear if the mat settles in the night with that deep whistling boom that means a new mouth has opened somewhere out in the dark.

In the morning there will be white. Condensation will bead every leaf. The pans will mirror for an hour. The midges will rise. The tornadoes will pour up screaming into ordinary gold-white light. And from rim to far blue edge the skin will look empty again — until your boot sinks half an inch and you remember it is totally, deafeningly covered in lives that have agreed, like the old mountains, to be flat, to be light, to be hollow and hold.
