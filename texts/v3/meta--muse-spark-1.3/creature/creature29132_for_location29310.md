Nothing lives here, you think at first.

That is the shock of it. Warmth and wet and green should mean business, hum, hunger. Here the green is laid out and nothing is eating it, nothing is flying over it, nothing is calling from inside it. The pools are so clear you keep looking twice because clear water in a warm place is never empty — except here it is. Sand, bleached leaves laid down leaf on leaf like paper that will not rot, a few spores sitting on the meniscus, unclaimed. No wriggle underneath. No dimple on top.

So you have to learn to see slower, lower.

What lives here does not move away from you. Does not flicker.

First, the ground itself is alive.

Not ground with things growing on it — ground that *is* the growing. Moss, deep and saturated just below the surface, springy underfoot, many greens at once, dark where it is sodden and almost luminous where it mounds up over a buried boulder. It swallows your step and fills your print back in with clear water. It rims the pools, softening stone into cushion. It climbs a little way up the rounded cliffs and then stops, in a line so even all around the bowl it looks agreed upon. If you kneel, you see it is not one thing but a country: tight cushions, loose wefts, tiny stars, felt. And rising out of it, here and there, hair-thin stalks no thicker than a hair, each tipped with a red-brown capsule no bigger than a seed. Thousands of them. They do not nod in wind — there is almost no wind — they stand straight in the humid air, fruiting into stillness.

Only after you have accepted that floor as living do the others resolve, set far apart in it like people who have chosen hermitage.

There is the fern.

One of them stands alone on its hummock with ten feet of bare wet stone around it, and that distance is part of what it is. Person-high, arching. Each frond comes up as a fiddlehead and unrolls with geological slowness, week by week, and then holds itself out to the mist and drips from every tip. *Plip.* Heavy, deliberate. It does not need anything to come to it. No bee, no bird. Spore dust on the undersides, wind enough to lift it a few feet, water enough to let it start again a few feet away — if it ever does. For now it is content to be one, lush unto itself, holding water, then letting it go.

Farther on, from a crack, the other kind: a clump of broad-leafed things, leaves as wide as shields, veined, cupped upward. After rain they each hold a small clear pool in the leaf itself. They do not offer it. They keep it until it evaporates or spills. Nothing drinks from them. They shine, undisturbed.

Lower still, skins. Liverworts spread flat over stone in green films and lobes, tight to the wet, no stem, no height, almost lichen in their patience. And film itself — that slick sheen on rock and in the seep-trails where water hangs as exhalation — a living slick, green-black, that does not hurry to become anything else.

And then, briefly, the fungi.

After a wet night they push up, white ears, ochre ears, soft uprights from the moss. By noon in the bath-warm air they have already collapsed back into slime. They do not last. They are the only thing here that hurries, and they do not succeed. They fruit and melt, fruit and melt, alone.

This is the whole population. No whine over the water. No throat pulsing under a leaf. No small feet. No flowers waiting, no fruit swelling for a mouth. Only the ancient contracts: spore to wind, spore to water, cell dividing to cell. Everything can do it alone, so everything does.

They live apart on purpose. No tangle, no press. Each presence keeps its own wet halo around it, moss between like silence between words. They do not compete in the way lowland things compete. They endure. A frond yellows over weeks. A rimstone lip around a pool adds a millimeter in a year. Fallen leaves do not rot as they should; they bleach and settle intact. Even death is slow to finish here.

If you stand still long enough, condensation beads on your arms. A drop gathers at your elbow, swells, falls to the moss and is taken without sound. Across the hollow the fern releases its gathered drop at almost the same moment onto the moss below it.

For an instant you are doing what the residents do. That is all they do, and they have been doing it, uninterrupted, unwitnessed, needing no other kind of life to continue: hold water close to the surface, let stone sweat, keep distance, make no noise that lasts, do not hurry.
