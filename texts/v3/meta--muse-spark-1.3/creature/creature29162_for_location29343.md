Nothing lives here at first.

That is what the place wants you to think. The light is too flat, the flats too empty, the distances between one grey dome of scrub and the next too deliberate. You walk and the pavement clicks under your boots and then stops clicking when you stop, and in that stop there is nothing. No call. No answer.

Then the nothing resolves, if you stay still long enough.

The first residents you learn to see are the ones that are the color of the place.

Far off by the wash, where the sand runs darker: the grazers. There are maybe a dozen, maybe twenty - you never get a good count because they never bunch. That is their first rule. One per bush, one per shadow. They are dust-pale, ash and bone, with bellies held high off the ground on absurd stilt legs, thin as bottle-tree branches and jointed backwards. It looks like a mistake until you crouch and feel the heat coming back up off the pavement in a wave against your face. Then you understand: they are holding themselves out of the fire.

They feed with their muzzles buried in the grey scrub domes, stripping the waxy leaves that have turned edge-on to the sun. Their ears are enormous, translucent at the rim, big as leaves and always moving - swivel, pause, swivel. When one freezes, they all freeze, all heads up at once. No snort, no bark, no alarm call. Just arrest. You realize after a while that they don't need to call. In air that eats sound within twenty paces, they have gone visual. An ear flick is a shout. A lifted forefoot, held, is a sentence. Late in the day they come down to the wash and dig, delicately, with those long forefeet in the dark sand for the damp underneath, and take water with their food and with the dew they lick off leaves at dawn.

Above them, so high at noon they are only flecks and dust in your eye: the kites.

Dozens. White. Turning without a single wingbeat in one vast slow thermal that belongs to the bowl itself. You never hear them. No cry, no mew. They are silent in a way that feels practiced, ancient. What do they eat in a place with no visible plenty? Lizards. Beetles. Anything that moves on the pavement. Sometimes a newborn grazer that mis-stepped away from its mother. They hunt by shadow - you see it if you watch the ground and not the sky: a small, fast coin of darkness sliding over pebbles, and then the blue-tailed lizards are gone as if they were never there.

At evening they do the strangest thing. They drop, tier by tier, out of the heat, and fold those wide white wings and sit, improbably heavy, in the bottle-trees. The swollen smooth trunks bob slightly under them. Tree after tree holds one, two, three white fruit that are birds, heads tucked, utterly still. They sleep there, in the open. Nothing bothers them there.

Because the hunter hunts low.

You will probably never see it clearly. Near the wash at late light, between bottle-tree and bottle-tree: a flow. Dun-colored, belly to ground, longer than a grazer's leg is tall, with a low head and eyes that do not reflect. It does not run. It pours from cover to cover. The grazers know long before you do - those leaf-ears all turn one way. No chase you will witness. It is not about chasing. It is about repositioning, night after night, for a hundred years, until the game trails are worn shin-deep into the fused pebbles. The cat-thing freezes too. Predator and prey holding the same silence, listening with their feet to the tick of the pavement.

And then, at your feet, if you get low and make yourself part of the shadow-puddle: the pavement comes alive.

Dark beetles, polished as seeds, herding in quick dashes from pebble to pebble, antennae tasting dew before it is gone. Lizards no longer than your finger, quick as sparks, tails a startling chemical blue - a flag in a world without sound. They do push-ups on hot stone. They lap. When the fog leans in at dawn and beads on everything, they run out with their mouths open to drink off rock.

And the bees. Thumbnail-big, amber and black, working the brief flowers that open for an hour after dawn and then abort. They don't buzz, or the buzz doesn't carry. You hear only the papery tick when one lands on a dry grass head. How do they find their way back with no scent on the wind, no sound? You watch one climb straight up out of a flower and fly, unerringly, not to a hive you can see, but to a crack in the too-precise cairn beside a needle.

That is when you understand the second population.

Seven of them. The needles.

From far they were mistakes. Up close they are residents. Fifteen feet of matte black, no thicker than your wrist, set at odd, unengineered angles as if dropped and left to root. Lichen has claimed the foot of one. Another is polished shoulder-high where generations of grazers have rubbed the itch of dry season off their hides. They are old the way the mountains are old - not built, but settled.

And they drink.

At dawn, before the sun clears the rim, the valley exhales. Low fog comes up out of the ground itself, out of the pavement, out of the scrub. It should dissipate. Instead it leans. Visibly leans toward the black spires. Dew gathers on your sleeves, on the scrub domes, on every fitted pebble, and then runs - uphill - along gradients you can't see, gathering into threads that walk the air and vanish into black. Each needle weeps without wetting. At its foot a perfect dark circle where water has fallen up out of the air and sunk. Do not touch them after they have drunk. The charge is in them. Your hand comes away numb for an hour, and your mouth tastes of metal, and the blue-tails will not come near you for the rest of the morning.

At noon they do the other thing. While heat shimmers off the central pan in sheets, around each needle is a column of perfect clarity, a cylinder of air where dust motes hang motionless, slowly turning. Toss a pebble near one and it falls wrong - a little too slow, a little sideways, corrected. The place has been taught a slightly different gravity and remembers it locally. The kites know. They give each needle a wide, respectful berth, never cutting across that column, as if it were a tree you could hit.

They are not machines the way a pump is a machine. They breathe. All day they hold. Toward evening, when the long copper shadows finally spill off the western cliff like cool water and the beetles retire under stone, the tip of each needle lessens - not light, exactly, but a refusal of dark. And straight up from each tip, unbent by any wind that moves the scrub, a thin column of mist rises. All night. Feeding the ring of towering white clouds that gathers over the rim and never enters, that rains its grey veils on the outer slopes while inside the bowl stays dry. By morning the ring is gone. By dawn the fog returns. Inhale. Hold. Exhale.

What lives in the columns? Perhaps nothing you would call an animal. Perhaps only the suspended dust, and spores, and the bee-lines that navigate by the field and not by sun. But something tends the cairns at their feet - those low piles of local stone that are too precise to be cairns, with courses fitted like scales, with a warm vent in the center that smells of resin and rain. Something uses the fallen arch by the pan - not stone, giving slightly under your palm like cooled skin - as a door, or as a shed skin.

And then there is the one who counted.

No one lives here now. Or no one admits to it. But near the seep under the eastern cliff, where the stone is black-green with algae and you can hear water moving under rock that you can't see, there is the white shell. Curved like a bivalve, door open, dust drifted halfway across the floor. Inside it is five degrees cooler and it smells, impossibly, of rain. On the inner wall, scratched with a stone or a nail: a tally of days, four long rows and then a fifth broken off, and beside it, carefully drawn, a kite with its wings out flat.

Whoever made it learned the rules the animals already knew. Drink at the seep in the morning; by afternoon the potholes are cracked mud. Stay out of the noon weight. Make no loud noise - not because it angers anyone, but because it is wasted. Shout and the thin warm air eats it. Watch the pan at three when the sky pushes itself back.

They sat, in the evenings, where you are sitting now, by the shell, listening to stone tick as it gives up the day, to water climbing invisibly into air, to the quietest active place in the world. They watched the white fruit settle into the bottle-trees. They learned to be spaced out, like the scrub domes. To be pale. To be silent. To drink fog.

They left, or they became part of the spacing. The rectangle of darker pavement where their tent stood is still there. The fire ring is cold for seasons.

What lives here, then, is not one thing. It is an agreement.

Scrub and grazer and kite and cat and lizard and bee and lichen and algae, all holding themselves apart at the right distance, all silent, all drinking dew that should not exist. And seven black gardeners, planted wrist-thin and fifteen feet tall, who make the dew exist, who hold the sky back in a ring, who teach gravity a local accent and keep a column of stillness for dust to turn in.

You stay a few hours and you start to keep the agreement too. You lower your voice without deciding to. You step around the dark circle at a needle's foot. You lap - metaphorically, then almost literally - at the morning.

Above the heat, but inside it, life has decided not to fight the bowl but to become its weather.
