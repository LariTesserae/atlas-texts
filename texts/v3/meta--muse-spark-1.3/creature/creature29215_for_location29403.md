At first you think nothing lives here. That is the altitude talking.

It is too high, too small, too exposed. A green coin and then nothing. You stand still to get your breath back and the place stands still with you — only water ticking off rock, only wind tearing over the top — and then, if you stay low and stay quiet, it starts to un-empty itself from the ground up.

It starts at your ankles.

A flicker that is not wind in the cottongrass. A rustle with intention in it. The whole floor, which you took for ground, is not ground. It is living mat, and it is moving.

Kneel. The sponge gives under your knee and sighs. Now you are at their height and you can see them: the gardeners.

Small, round, grey-brown as the slabs they live under, with round ears and no tail to catch the wind. Pika. At a distance they are only a *cut* of sound — that short, indignant whistle from the talus — and a dart. Up close, in the lee of a mossed boulder, one sits up on hind feet with a mouth so full of green it cannot close, stems sticking out like whiskers. Starry saxifrage. Sedge. Dwarf willow leaves no bigger than your fingernail. It is cutting summer and carrying it home.

They live underneath. That is the secret. The hollow looks like open meadow but underneath the locked talus and the cushion domes there is a city of runs: dry tunnels between slabs, haypiles tucked where the seep never reaches. Each pile is a careful, sun-cured stack, turned and re-turned. You find one under an overhang — a miniature hayrick of grasses and pink moss-campion petals, still giving off sweet cut smell. Its owner scolds you from three rocks away, whistle and then, half a second later, the rock throwing the whistle back at you, fainter, so you hear him twice. He dives. Then his nose reappears. They cannot afford to stay hidden. There are only three weeks of flowers and winter is already building over the next peak.

A little higher, where the sun hits the rubble longest, something heavier breathes.

You hear them before you see them — a rolling *chuck-chuck* and the long scrabble of claws on stone — and then the rocks themselves seem to have fattened. Marmots. A family, sunk into the sunniest breakdown like butter in a pan. The old female is almost the color of lichen, grizzled, her sides heaving fast in the thin air. Two young ones wrestle, all paws and open mouths, tumbling off a slab and righting themselves, squeaking. They do not dart like pikas. They sprawl. They own heat when they can get it, bellies pressed to rock that has stored noon. When the shadow crosses they are gone — not darted, *poured* — a liquid vanishing into a crack you would have sworn was too narrow. Then one nose, then one eye, then the chuck again, indignant that the sky interrupted sunbathing.

Because the sky is inhabited too, and it hunts.

First the owners of the edge. You hear a scream torn sideways by wind and then they are below you and above you at once, black birds with red beaks like fresh wounds, white flashes under the wings. Choughs. Three, four, tumbling off the open void and back up on the updraft the ridge curls over the hollow. They play with falling. One folds, drops ten feet straight at the tor, opens at the last possible second and *kyaahs*, and the back wall throws it back — *kyaah-kyaah* — from two directions. They pick at dung in the short turf between flights, prying for beetles, leaving thin, three-toed prints around the tarn that fill slowly with water.

Through them, faster than thought, cut the knives.

Alpine swifts. They do not perch in the hollow. They *pass* through it, at impossible speed, dark scythes that seem to slice the hum of insects in half. Their cry is a long, descending scream that echoes off wet rock. If you follow one backward you find where it lives: high in the summit tor, in cracks above the seep where the rock is black-green and never dry. A slit no wider than your hand, whitewashed with droppings. Inside, in dark, a peeping. They have built a nest of feathers and caught midges glued with spit, clinging to vertical stone in screaming wind. They live by never landing except to feed young, by drinking on the wing from the rain film, by sleeping in the air. They are here for the brief glut and nothing else.

And then everything stops for a second.

The pikas dive. The bees lift off the flowers in a single copper shimmer. The marmot chuck cuts off mid-call. A shadow, wide and slow, crosses the moss campion domes.

He is out over the void, but he owns the hollow. Golden eagle. Huge, the color of peat and sun, wings held flat as boards, primary feathers splayed like fingers feeling the air. He turns with almost no wingbeat, tilting once, head down. You can see the eye even from here — fixed. He is not circling emptiness. He is reading the sponge below: the runs, the haypiles, the sunning bellies. Everything small here grows sideways to avoid the wind, but it cannot hide from above. He has manured this bowl too, in his way, with the bones and pellets dropped over centuries under his plucking perch on the rim cairn. He will not stoop now, with you standing. He will wait. The hollow holds his shadow a half-second too long in the minds of everything under it.

Under all of them, holding all of them, is the hum.

The air over the tarn shimmers, not with heat but with wings. Midges in dancing columns over the black water, black flies low over dung, and working the flowers — white saxifrage, yellow cinquefoil, that violent pink campion — the copper-bright mountain bees, fat, furred against cold, loud out of all proportion to their size. Their hum is the constant wire in the air, the base note under drip and whistle and scream. They are frantic, methodical. Land, tongue, load pollen, lift, next flower open all at once. If you hold still one will bump your hand, cling to warm skin for a second, then go. They live only a few weeks, long enough to make the seeds that make the haypiles that make the pika that makes the eagle. In the tarn below them drift their dead: the skeletons of midges on the grit, fallen petals, larvae wriggling in the coldest seam. Even the black water is alive.

And under even that, slowest of all, the green itself.

It is not decoration. It is a resident. The moss campion in tight green domes so hard you could stand on them — each dome decades old, a hundred tiny shoots huddled together to make their own warmth, flowering all around the edge in pink stars. The dwarf willow no higher than your ankle but spreading wide as your arm, its catkins shedding fluff into the wind. The houseleek rosettes gone red at the edges. The stunted true ferns unfurling right where the rock weeps, in that black-green tongue that never dries. The sedge rimming the tarn, the cotton-bright heads of cottongrass nodding. Together they are the sponge that holds the rain and the melt and makes film into home. Step and water sighs up. That sigh is them drinking and releasing. Without them there is no dung held, no haypile, no hum.

That is who lives here. Not one thing. A stacked, hurried, noisy bargain: green mat to hold water, insects to move pollen, small furred cutters and sprawlers to turn green into meat and manure, screaming birds to turn air into nest, and high over all, the slow circler to keep everyone fast and low and sideways.

None of them built the windbreak or stacked the cairn or left the split bird bone in cold ash. People come up light, stay briefly, leave stone arranged to remember wind by, and go down. These others stayed. The rock cupped just enough water and broke just enough wind, and life, once it found it, refused to leave — pressed itself flat, furred itself thick, learned to work fast, to whistle twice, to sleep under stone and hunt over nothing, to bloom all at once because there may be no second chance.

Kneel long enough and your breath fogs toward shade, your knees go cold through from the wet, the back of your neck goes hot from sun, and the hollow goes on being crowded and loud around you without including you at all.
