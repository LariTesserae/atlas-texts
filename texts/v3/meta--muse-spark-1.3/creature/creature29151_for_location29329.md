No one lives here, at first.

That's what you think with your chin almost in the water. A frog wedged and not moving, a spider holding center, mites on a rotted flower. Guests. Tenants. Not residents. The room feels tended and busy and entirely empty in the way a mill feels empty after hours when the machines are still running.

Then you stop looking for eyes.

You put your weight wrong on your elbows and the floor gives — that moss grown so thick over old leaf litter it has become sponge-flesh — and it doesn't give like ground. It gives like back. Springy, green-black, rebounding a half-second too slow, exhaling brown-green water around your knuckles that smells faintly of iron. You lift your hand and it sinks back. The water clears. The hummocks settle into their final shapes again.

It breathes.

Not fast. Not lungs. The whole mat, no wider than your two arms curled around your knees, rises a millimeter and falls. You only catch it because your chin is near the surface and the skin of the little pan — that hand-wide pan skimmed with duckweed and pollen — tightens and loosens, tightens and loosens, in time with a low throb you have been hearing without hearing. Under the frog-chug and the bee-engines and the ant feet and the *plik plik* of the drips, there is a base note. A dynamo. You feel it more in your sternum and teeth than in your ears.

That is him. Her. It.

She is the floor.

Once you see it you can't unsee it, though there is almost nothing to see. She is spread impossibly thin for something so old — a felt, a pellicle, a living hide grown through the moss instead of under it, green-black from the algae she keeps in her tissue like livestock, so that from above she passes for plant. Everything here is plant, you thought. That was her first trick. She photosynthesizes a little. She filters a lot.

Her edge is nowhere. If you tease the moss back with a fingertip — and you won't, not twice, because the warmth on the back of your neck invites stillness — you'd find she has grown over and through every fallen leaf until leaf and animal are one laminate. Leaf falls, moss covers it, mites strip it, fungus softens it. She eats underneath it all. The floor gets thicker, not higher. That's her rule. She has eroded long ago and decided to stay.

She has almost no face. In the whole country there are only two or three mouths, and you can stare for a long minute at nothing but weeping stem and shivering water and miss them completely.

Look in the lowest hollow, at the rim of the pan where the water never rises or falls. A pore no wider than a grain of rice. A pale, muscular ring, furred with the same root-hairs that stand erect all over this pocket. It winks. Slowly dilates. Draws in a thread of duckweed, pollen, copepod shit, tadpole breath, one drowned ant still clutching its leaf-cut. Closes. A minute later, a few inches away, another one sighs open and jets clear water back out — distilled, filtered through felt. *Tick.* That hydraulic tick you thought was only bladderworts. The bladderworts are her borrowed stomachs, thousands of tiny valves rooting right in her flesh, opening and shutting, opening and shutting to catch what she misses.

She never stops pumping. That is the hum. Millions of collars beating inside her, moving water through felt as through factory felt, in through a thousand invisible ostia, out through those few oscula. Condensers overhead drip on schedule, moss-felt filters, she drinks. What condenses must drip. What drips must pool. What pools must be drunk by moss and breathed back out by leaves. She holds the level. She is the level.

That's why the air smells wrong. Crushed stems and warm rot, yes, but under it hot iron and machine oil and the clean burn before rain. That's her mucus, the oil-sheen she exudes to keep her pores from clogging, faintly metallic with concentrated lake-iron, faintly electric. The air in here carries a charge because she carries a charge. She pumps ions as she pumps water, setting up a weak field across her back, just strong enough to make fine root-hairs stand erect, to make your skin prickle, to lift your hair when a carpenter bee lumbers past and discharges. Power without wires. She stuns nothing, she just orients — pollen drifts toward her, wrigglers avoid her mouths, ants learn where to walk.

The ants walk on her back all day and she lets them. They are her conveyor belt. Leaf-cuts up, honeydew down, protein and sugar with mechanized indifference along the great wrist-thick stalks that bow inward to hold the ceiling. Those stalks weep from pores you can see if you look close — clear beads welling, swelling, detaching — because she keeps them turgid with pressure. Sap hisses under pressure. She pays the ants in sugar to bring her nitrogen.

The pale tree-frog wedged in the axil, throat pulsing and not moving, is not hiding. He is sitting on her breathing ganglion, in the one spot where her exhalant current aerates best, and with every throat-pulse he fans her. She lets him because his tadpoles, hanging like commas in the pan, stir the water and keep her ostia clear. Pistons made of frogs.

The orb-weaver strung between two hairs of moss, legs tucked, holding center in a web glinting with condensed beads, is her quality control. She lets her because the spider culls the flies that would lay too many eggs and clog her. The fallen pink flower rotting and hosting a civilization of mites is her compost pile, kept at exact distance.

She is sparse because she has to be. One mind spread thin. Vast green emptiness between mouths. Much space. Abundance was there all along, just very small — the wrigglers folding and snapping edge to edge in the pan are both her crop and her cleaners, eating bacteria she can't reach, excreting what she can eat.

She is old for something so soft. The edges of the big leaves overhead are nibbled to lace and healed to scar; she has healed that many times. Marshes around her flood and dry, leaves unfurl and fall, but she holds temperature, wetness, light, noise in equilibrium. When drought comes she will ball herself into hard, seed-like gemmules in the moss and wait, timeless, until water returns. The droplet on that leaf-tip has always been about to fall because she has always been holding her breath to catch it.

If you stay still long enough your own pulse drops into her time. Warm on the back of the neck, warm breath off the water. The sound does not stop or change — a bee leaves, another takes its frequency, a frog pauses, the ants fill the gap — because she does not stop or change. She is the shape the hum takes when it needs a body.

You back out on your elbows and the leaves reseal overhead with a soft vegetal sigh. The roar dulls to a hum, the hum to ordinary buzz. From outside it is nothing again. A tangle in the bottomland where water never quite drains.

Underneath, the felt rebounds where your knees were, pores reopen, water skin tightens and loosens. Wetly, loudly, forever.
