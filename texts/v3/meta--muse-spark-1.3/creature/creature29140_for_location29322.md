At first you think no one lives here.

That's the emptiness playing on you. The great lung in the middle is empty, the houses have no doors that lock, no smoke, no voices you can pick out. You stand on the midrib and the town seems to be happening without anyone to happen to.

Then you stay still long enough for the roar to separate, and you realize the roar *is* anyone.

No one species lives here. That is the first thing to unlearn. There is no town-dweller, singular. There is a chord that holds the shape of a town.

Start with the weather, because that's what they are at first: the midges.

A cloth of them, ten thousand wings, lifting off the moss-hollows in the morning-exhalation when the fog is knee-high. From a distance a gray-gold veil shifting down a side-vein street. Up close — if you let them settle on your arms, and you will, there is no avoiding it — each one is absurdly specific, eyed, legged, thirsty. They don't bite so much as taste. They are the crowd. The commuters. The ones who make an alley feel full for three minutes and then utterly abandoned. They live fast enough that a swarm you walk through at noon are the grandchildren of the swarm you walked through at light-shift. They die into the drip-pools and the tadpole-cups eat them.

Below them, literally, the sanitation.

Mites and springtails in the velvet between paths, a seething polish-crew you only see if you put your face to the moss. They comb the shed down out of the pitcher-rooms, they take the dead midges apart before rot can start — because rot is not permitted here, nothing dead can be allowed to stay dead — they keep the smooth paths smooth. Without them the town would felt over in a day. Ants do the long haul. Highways of them along the handrail ridges of the veins, jaw-to-jaw lines that never break, carrying wax crumbs, frog skin, midge wings, sap crystals. They are infrastructure with legs. Step over them. Everyone steps over them. Even the mantids won't break an ant highway; it's bad manners and bad traffic.

The gutter-keepers are the jewel beetles. Big as your thumb-joint, backs like spilled oil, too heavy to hurry. After an indoor rain they come out and walk the sunken vein-lets where sap runs clear and sweet, drinking and dredging, shouldering leaf-litter out of the flow. They are surly, solitary tenants. They own their stretch of gutter the way an old man owns a stoop. Tap one and it locks down, becomes a jewel, waits for you to die first.

Above them on the civic ladder, if ladder is a word that still works where walls are floor: the frogs.

Hundreds of tree-frogs the color of fresh sap, throats going like metronomes. You hear them before you ever manage to see one, and then you see them everywhere — cupped in axil-pools, flattened in the moss, lined shoulder to shoulder along a vein where it widens. They are the old-timers. They don't move much. They don't need to. Their job is to sing in the key that keeps the roof taut. When the cicada-pressure in the walls spikes and the ceiling bellies outward, it is the frogs who drop half a tone at dusk-light and pull it back. Young males try to show off, sing sharp, sing against — and the whole street will go quiet in a very pointed way until they fall back into the choir. That’s law. **Don’t sing against the choir.** It isn’t metaphor. If you do, the house you’re sleeping in trembles.

The walls themselves sing too. That baseline that sits on your sternum and makes your teeth ache: cicadas embedded in the living skin, or the skin itself vibrating like a cicada. Impossible to tell where insect stops and town starts. That’s intentional. That’s citizenship here.

Night belongs to the moths. All day they sleep packed in the pitcher-rooms, wingspans like folded handkerchiefs, breathing very slowly. At indigo-light they pour out and take the void — that fifty-sixty feet of trembling nothing in the middle that daylight leaves to the screamers. The screamers are the high clan, the swifts-that-aren’t-birds, membrane wings and a keel of chitin, carving endless circles up where the sap-lens in the ceiling shows you tiny to yourself. They never land, as far as anyone can tell. They mate, sleep, die on the wing. They eat moths. The moths know it and fly anyway, because the void has to be used or it sags. Their dust coats everything by morning. The mites sweep it.

And at every intersection, perfectly still, perfectly the color of the doorway they haunt: the mantids.

You want to call them predators, outsiders, danger. They aren’t. They are residents with a civic function no one voted for and everyone accepts. They prune the crowd. A mantid will take a midge-cloud’s edge, a drunk beetle, a frog too bold. They do it without breaking the choir. The frogs will sing right next to a feeding mantid. After, the ants will clean the legs. Their egg-cases are tucked like purses under the fungus-shelf balconies, guarded by wasps — narrow-waisted artisans who have learned to roll living leaf-edge into doors, into shutters for pitcher-rooms, into cradles. Wax is their mortar. Chew is their carpentry. Nothing is cut, only coaxed.

The wall-walkers know the town best. Geckos, flattened against bract and vein, eyes like wet seeds, toes splayed. They live in that negotiable gravity at the edges where down becomes sideways if you commit. You’ll see one walk straight off the floor and up a tenement wall and across the underside of a tendril-stair without changing gait. They hunt moth-dust and baby ants. They watch you learn it on your third day when you slip and stick and take three sideways steps before your weight remembers itself. They do not laugh, exactly. Their throats pulse.

Under all of it, the one you never see.

Lie still on the moss at quiet hour — there is no quiet, only slightly less loud — and you feel it moving under the floor. Slow, muscular, root-wise. It prunes from below. Keeps the streets from closing over. Without it, the midrib causeway would heal shut in a week, the side-veins would fill, the plaza where the floor is polished pale green by dancing or fighting or both would skin over. Every town needs someone to say no to growth. Here it’s blind, and huge, and never surfaces. You leave offerings without meaning to: spilled sap, dead skin. The floor drinks immediately, swells, sighs.

And the cups. The waxy axil-cups holding palmfuls of brown tea-water where tadpole-things flick. Don’t drink from them. Lick the walls instead. Those flicks are babies — frog, fly, something in between — a nursery district in every corner. They will grow up to be weather for an hour, then choir, then gutter-food. They resent interference because they are the town reproducing itself in public.

Because no one here is separate from the town-body, and the town-body is itself a resident. The walls, floor, sky are one continuous living mass. It shifts its weight with an audible groan of fiber. It peristalses — that’s why you count seven side streets going and nine coming back. It exhalates fog. It rains indoors when dew gets too heavy on the ceiling. It closed districts off when it outgrew them, doors opening onto solid flesh, streets narrowing to scar. At night you can hear traffic in those seals. Footsteps. A market. Something still living its urban life on the other side of the skin. The town remembers being larger. The town’s dead neighborhoods are still inhabited, just not by you, not on this side.

So who lives here? Everyone small enough to leave the middle empty. Everyone willing to be loud enough to hold the roof up. Everyone who understands you don’t dam the gutters, you don’t drink the cups, you don’t sing against the choir — not because you’ll be punished, but because the house will tremble, the ceiling will belly, the street will flinch under your feet.

In the morning-exhalation, fog drifting knee-high, light pulsing gold-green through the avenue-veins overhead, you’ll see them all at once and none separately: frog throats going on a gutter edge while ants stream beneath them and a gecko freezes above and a mantid waits at the corner and a beetle shoulders through and a cloth of midges lifts, and high above in the great empty lung the screamers carve their circles, screaming. Crowded and empty and deafening all at once.

No mayor. No locks. Just living mass, and hollow, and noise, and hunger in balance — and ten thousand generations of feet, claws, pads, bellies keeping the paths shiny.
