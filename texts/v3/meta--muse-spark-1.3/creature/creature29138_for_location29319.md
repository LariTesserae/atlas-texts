They do not look alive at first.

From the ridge they look like weather. A string of low amber coals strung out half a mile across the black moss, breathing — dim, bright, dim. You think lanterns. You think eyeshine. You think you are seeing things because the cold is in you and the altitude has thinned your thinking to a wire. Then one of the lights shifts ten feet sideways and stops, and another shifts, and you understand they are spaced like animals space themselves. Grazing distance. Herd distance. Nothing else moves that way.

What lives here lives low, and lives hot, and lives quiet.

Come closer. Crawl, because anything upright and loud is noticed here. The cushion moss gives under your knees like a lung and comes back rimed. Your breath falls out of you and pings as ice. And still the lights get no bigger until you are almost on them, because the air lies. Ten yards out, what you took for a hummock shudders and vents.

That is a boiler.

As big as a musk-ox, lower. Six legs, short and splayed, made to spread weight over moss that is meters deep and treacherous. Barrel body sunk almost to the ground. Shag so dense and strange it does not look like hair — each strand hollow and glass-clear, frosting white on the outside while it wicks condensed water inward to the skin. You can see droplets running the wrong way along it, toward heat. And over everything, growing on the back and flanks like a saddle, the living thatch: the same black-green and iron-red moss they eat, rooted in the guard hairs, damp, glittering. At rest, head down, heat shuttered, a boiler is indistinguishable from ground. That is the point.

Then it breathes, and the slit opens.

Along each flank, between the ribs, are seams — not wounds, shutters. When the animal inhales the plates part a finger's width and you see straight inside to furnace light. Orange, deep orange, pulsing with a double piston beat you feel more in your teeth than hear: *chuff — chuff — chuff.* A pause. A sigh of a valve somewhere under the shoulder. The faint tick-tick-tick of plates reseating. Inside is not stomach. Or not only stomach. They crop moss by the ton with a mouth that is not a mouth but a radial iris underneath the head, silent, toothless, that shears the wet heads off and funnels them inward where blood hot with dissolved iron and copper does slow smelting work. You can smell it when the wind turns sideways: hot stone, crushed greens, a metallic tang, ozone.

They are weather because they have to be. Each animal carries paired stacks on its neck, short chimneys of cartilage and skin, constantly feathering steam. Ten feet up it plumes, white against black, then collapses in the killing cold and falls back as fine warm drizzle in a ten-foot circle. If you stand in a herd's trail your shoulders bead. The ferns there grow twice a man's height, arching over in black cloisters, leaning generation after generation toward the paths the herds have always taken. Under each frond hang drops that never fall, swelling and shrinking. The moss where they have passed steams for hours after. Their dung drops glowing, ash-rich, dull red for a minute in the dark, then black — and days later a violent green upwelling where it landed. They farm as they walk, unwittingly.

They move as one slow mind. Three, seven, twelve. Old bulls at the edges carry thicker moss-manes and vent taller. When two bulls contest, they do not bellow — thin air would eat it, and quiet is survival. They turn flank-to-flank and vent straight up, twin white columns, and the pressure wave hits your sternum like a hand. Blue St. Elmo's fire crawls around the stack-tips and crackles. The air smells burned for a minute. Then they lower their heads and go back to cropping. The calves, if you are lucky and very still, stay tucked in the thermal shadow between two adults, little more than a brighter patch of moss with a faster pulse, riding in that two-foot envelope of warm air above their mothers' backs.

And in that envelope lives the second nation.

You don't see the iron-midges until a boiler breathes on you. Then the air over its back shimmers and resolves — a cloud, hundreds, thousands, almost invisible gnats, each no bigger than a frost crystal, living their entire frantic lives in warmth that extends only to your knees if you stood up. They ride the herd. They feed on condensed steam, on shed skin cells, on the salts that precipitate around the flank slits. They die in seconds if they drift out. So the plants have learned to keep them.

Lamp-moss grows only on the leeward side of boiler trails, in low velvet mats. By day — which is night — it does nothing. But its tips store stray photons leaking from the flank slits, photon by photon, and release them hours later as a slow green afterglow. From a distance the trail edges glow faintly green after a herd has passed. The midges cannot resist it. They swarm to it, dust it with spores and dung from other mats, pollinate what cannot be wind-pollinated in air this still. The moss lures the insect that cannot leave the animal that feeds the moss. A closed loop, lit green.

And where there are furnaces walking in endless night, there are things that have learned to hunt by temperature.

You will not see them until you have been out three nights and your eyes have finally learned scale. They are taller than the boilers and impossibly thin. Stilt-legged. White as rime, so that starlight passes through them at the edges. Blind — no eyes at all. Instead a wide, shallow dish for a face, veined with fine red radiator lines that flush and pale as they turn their heads, listening. Not listening for sound. Listening for heat, for that piston double-thump carried up through the legs from the moss.

They drift. That is the only verb. No footfall. No clank of plates. Between the amber lights they move at a slowness that makes you look away and look back. One terrace away. Then closer.

When a pale one kills, it does not pounce. It unfolds.

From its shoulders and flanks hang vast sail-like membranes, thin as frost-skin, white-gray. It finds a young boiler strayed a few feet from the herd's warm overlap, or an old one whose shutters stick half-open, and it folds itself over it — entire, tenting it, sealing the edges to the moss with its own weight. From outside you see only a white dome where an amber light used to be. Inside, it is doing the inverse of smothering a fire. It hides the infrared bloom so the herd cannot see, and then it drinks the difference. Its dish-face goes dark, then pink, then flushed. Its radiator veins stand out black. Frost forms on the outside of the membrane and thickens. Underneath, the piston beat slows. *chuff... chuff...... chuff.* You hear nothing. A mile away on a ridge you just watch one light among twelve go amber to red to black.

Hours later the membrane lifts. What stands up is taller by a hand, and faintly amber at the core where before there was only white. It walks away, warmer than before, leaving behind a cooled hummock already being reclaimed by moss, already steaming faintly less. Whether it fed, or bred, or became — whether the young boiler becomes the young pale — no one has stayed long enough in that cold to see clearly. The pale ones do not gather. They do not vent or glow or call. They simply persist, spaced even wider than the herds, one white stilt between valleys of black.

Under all of this, the rooted ones, who are not passive at all.

The cushion plants, tight as fists, a hundred years to make a dome a foot wide, give like lungs under your hand because they are lungs — trapping fog in combed hairs, in pits, in cold-traps that coax droplets out of vapor that should not exist. Clubmosses thick as your wrist crawl over old lava and make soil out of themselves, dying underneath while growing forward. The cold-ferns in the fog pools do the slow lean toward heat and toward dung-glow, red-tipped and black-stemmed, jeweled. Everything wears rime, everything glitters, everything is awake at a speed you have to lie belly-down for an hour to perceive: a frond unfurling a millimeter, a droplet swelling on a hair and being sucked in, a moss spore opening like a mouth when a boiler's drizzle passes.

No one made this. That is what gets you, on the fourth night when your heart has finally slowed enough not to deafen you. The basalt organ-pipes on the north scarp hum with wind though no hand raised them. Ice ticks in its polygons like a cooling stove. A string of breathing lanterns crests a far terrace, vents small private clouds that hang a moment and settle as rime to feed what they will eat tomorrow. Furnace, moss, midge, hunter, fog, frost — meat and light made out of cold and darkness and almost-water, night after night, without a sound, without an owner.
