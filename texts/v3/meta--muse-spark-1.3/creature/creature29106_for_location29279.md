At first you think nothing can live here.

That's what the brightness tells you. The dryness tells you. The crunch under your boots which is not sand but pulverized building tells you. This is too white, too level, too finished. A beach after everything has broken.

Then your eye catches the mustache.

Every fin, every slab, every worn-down tooth in this machine-forest wears one on its lee side — a dark contour line exactly where the thin blue shadow falls at noon. From far off it looks like soot. Up close it resolves.

It is wire-lichen. It is glassmoss. They are the base of everything, and they do not live *on* this world so much as *with* its ruin.

The wire-shrub is not a shrub. Put your face six inches away — in that strip of shade that smells faintly of hot stone and ozone — and you see a tangle a meter across of black hair-fine wire, each filament straight as a needle, branching at perfect right angles. No leaves. The wire *is* the leaf, the stem, the root. It anchors itself by etching a shallow pit into ceramic with acid, no deeper than a fingerprint, and then it holds. It does not sway. In wind it trembles at a frequency you feel in your molars, shedding dust and drinking light. It grows slower than stone wears. A big tangle, a meter across, may be ten thousand years old. It has no hurry. It needs no soil because there is no soil. It needs light, which is everywhere, and dust, which is everywhere, and time, which is the one thing this plain has in surplus.

Between the wires, where the shadow never quite moves until it does, is the moss that is not moss. Kneel. You will see thousands of pinhead spheres, clear as glass, packed like fish roe in a low mat. Each one is a lens. The brutal white-gold bath that would fry any green cell is caught, focused down by silica to a tolerable ember, and underneath, in that tiny orange hearth, lives a dot of black-green algae. The colony breathes as one. On some afternoons when the gaps between the fins fill with that golden fizz that makes compasses spin, the lenses go milky — shutters. You can watch them cloud over, acre after acre of mustache turning pale. They are farming photons the way the old builders stored them in the slabs.

Nothing here drinks. There is nothing to drink. The lichen and the lens-moss have learned to make their water and unmake it in the same breath, sealed tight as a circuit. They take hydrogen from dust — from the hydroxyl locked in ancient polymer-stone — and never let it go.

And where there are grazers of stone-plants, there are grazers.

You will not see the maintenance-beetle. You will see that it has been there. Look for the dotted lines of perfect spheres, matte white, each the size of a pea, laid in a wandering trail between wire-tangles. Those are its shit, and its gift: chewed lichen and glass-dust, kilned in its gut and excreted as fresh ceramic pellets — new building-stone. Pick one up. It is still warm.

The beetle that made it is fifty meters away, shut down into rubble. It heard the crunch of your boots through the floor long before you saw the trail. It is fist-sized, dome-backed, the dome a dull ceramic the exact color of the sinter, mottled with lichen-spore black. Flip one over — if you are fast enough, which you won't be — and underneath is the softness: six jointed legs folded like wire, mouthparts that are half mandible, half grinding mill, a belly plate that pulses very slowly with heat. Half animal, half leftover machine. No one knows which half came first anymore. It does not need to decide.

It lives to chew wire. It clamps onto a black filament and walks it down to the anchor pit, reaping light that the lichen spent a century collecting, and its gut reclaims every molecule of bound water with a chemist's stinginess. It never urinates. It never pants. It excretes only dry spheres. During the heat of noon it wedges itself into a flute-hole in a fin, legs tucked, and becomes temperature. At night it walks. When a dust devil wanders kilometers between the fins sifting fine white powder, the beetle rides out its edge to lay a single egg — a ceramic pellet with a living yolk — in fresh drift. The deep floor will keep it warm.

Once a day, perhaps, the glider passes.

You hear the almost-nothing first. Or rather you feel a lightness behind the ears — that very high, very faint choir as midday air moves across flutes tuned by erosion to just above human hearing. The beetles can hear it. The glider navigates by it.

It comes low down the thermal street between two ranks of slabs, a meter wide, pale as sun-bleached plastic, translucent enough that you see light through its wings. It does not flap. It has no muscles for flapping worth the name. It is a torn bag given intention: a stretched photovoltaic skin over a skeleton thin as wire-lichen, a head that is mostly two huge dark eyes and a slit mouth, a tail like a streamer. It rides. It tilts a millimeter and a whole kilometer of rising heat off the pale floor tips it into a new slot-canyon.

It is watching for movement — for the infinitesimal wobble of a beetle that thought you had passed. Then it folds. It drops like a leaf of ash, silent because sound dies fast here, absorbed by those thousands of little holes, and scoops. It does not land. It will never land on the hot sinter if it can help it. It eats on the wing, crushes the dome with a gut full of stones, extracts the water-fat and the stored light, and excretes a fine white powder from the air that joins the dust devils. At dusk you sometimes see three or four of them circling a fin-tip where St. Elmo's light is crawling, bathing in discharge. No one knows if they feed on that too. They nest — if nesting is the word — in the cool, dim, ribbed chambers inside hollow fins, hanging like pale fruit in the dark that hums just below hearing, raising one young at a time on chewed beetle paste.

And then there are the people. The ones who walk for years.

You will not see them either, at first. Their shelters are designed to be missed: a lean-to of fallen building-skins in the lee of a fin, set exactly where the mustache grows, low enough to be a shadow. Their cairns are little stacks of beetle-pellets, five or six high, left at cross-slots. A cache. A grave. A prayer. It's hard to tell.

They are thin in a way that has nothing to do with hunger. Dry-thin. Salt-cured by years of air that takes sweat before it beads. Their skin is powdered white with sinter dust in every crease. Their eyes are narrowed permanently against the managed brightness, their lips cracked and then healed into smooth enamel-like scars, like the cuts in the floor that are gone by morning.

They move the way the glider moves: slow, low, between the teeth of the world. Never on top. Never in the open at noon. They pause in the thin lines of shade where the glassmoss grows, not to eat the moss — though in extremity they will scrape the lenses and suck the bitter green ember underneath for its bound water — but to be cool for a minute. They learn the tick of the slabs at night, when the whole dense field contracts and exhales the day's stored light as faint warmth. They lie with an ear to the floor to listen for peristalsis far below, for where the deep structure is sealing a crack. They will not sleep there; that ground will be warm and then suddenly soft.

How do they drink, on a planet where there has never been water? Badly. Sparingly. They carry nothing that shines. They wear long wraps of salvaged filter-cloth that trap breath at night and wring it back into the mouth by dawn. They learn to eat beetle — the soft underside roasted on hot slab — for its fat-water. They learn to be quiet, because the place does. Their voices, if you hear them, are hoarse whispers, not from secrecy but because shouting here is like shouting into wool; the flute-holes drink it down into the structure.

They are not tribes. They are not survivors of a crash. Most of them walked out here on purpose and kept walking when the curve of the world came up at the edges and never came down. They tend the pellet cairns. They clear dust from wire-tangles with a feather-light brush, the way you would tend a garden you will never harvest. Some say they are listening for the designers become a layer. Some say they are just waiting to become a layer themselves, usefully.

Under all of them — under lichen, beetle, glider, human — lives the largest inhabitant, the one you are standing on.

Put your knife into it. Make a clean cut a finger deep in the compacted sinter. Come back in the morning. It will be gone, filled with a smooth, enamel-like scar. Lie with your ear to the warm floor at night and you will feel more than hear the slow redistribution far below: load shifting from a subsiding fin to a new buttress grown by a micron, a day-crack sealed, dust repelled in those narrowest slots where dropped grit falls slow and sideways because the floor still remembers how to repel.

It does not do this for you. It does not know you. It was designed to do this before its designers became geology — to level itself to Datum Zero, to crush its own towers to aggregate, to precipitate lime, to store light in slabs like heat in brick. It still does it, after millions of years, without purpose and without pause. In that sense it is not alive. In every sense that matters on a plain where there is no bedrock, only previous versions of itself all the way down as far as anyone has cut, it is the only thing that is truly alive, and everything else — wire, lens, beetle, glider, walker — is just a symptom of its very quiet, very long waking.

If you stay long enough to learn the rules with your body, you will join them. You will learn not to fight the brightness or the dryness or the crowding. You will walk the slot-canyons when the shadows go long and blue and parallel to the east. You will stop when the air shimmers without wind. You will listen for the almost-nothing that tells you you are not alone.
