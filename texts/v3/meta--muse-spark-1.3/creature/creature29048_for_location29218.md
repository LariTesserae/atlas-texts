They are not, at first, people to you. They are movement on stairs.

From the rim you see them sideways and foreshortened — a flash of bare heel on a dished step, a basket crossing a rope bridge, a hand reaching for a line. They move the way swallows fly here, as if the vertical were level. A child comes up forty feet of uneven terrace with a jar on her head and does not spill and does not look down. An old man goes down with a sick goat on his back, step by chanting step, his toes finding the worn hollows in the stone without looking. You, visiting, flatten yourself to the wall and clutch a handhold polished greasy by ten thousand other clutchings. They flow around you.

Then you stay a day and they resolve. Then a week and you learn their faces, because there are not many to learn.

There are maybe fifty of them that sleep here, more at midday when cousins come down from the dry fields outside to trade. That is the whole hamlet. It has to be small. The pit can only hold so many breaths.

Look closer and their bodies have been edited by the place.

They are stair-footed. Bare feet wide, callused thick as sandal leather, toes splayed from gripping. Even the toddlers, wobbling in doorways that are really old water doors, have that low center of gravity, knees soft. The old women have ankles like rope. No one here is fat — heat and stairs forbid it — but no one is thin in the starved way, either. They are compact, corded in calf and forearm from hauling. Shoulders sit a little forward from carrying. Hands are the real face: mason's hands white with lime to the wrist, weaver's hands nicked by the reed, pigeon-keeper's hands pocked on the backs where young birds have mistaken them for perches, children's hands always damp.

Their eyes squint even in shade. They have grown up in bounce-light, noon straight down and then wall-to-wall glare until the bottom goes amber-green, and they read that light the way farmers read sky. They know by the color on the west plaster what hour it is without looking up. Their ears are better than yours. You have to learn, as they have, that the pit is an ear. A bucket clanked below arrives at the rim as a bell. A mother calling a name comes back doubled. Visitors shout to overcome themselves and then shout louder. Residents speak low, almost murmur, and pause half a beat to listen for the return. Children learn early to distinguish their own mother's *tok* of copper pot from the dozen other *toks* dripping unsynchronized from the spouts at midday.

They are maintainers, not builders. That is their theology whether they say it or not.

No one here talks about making something new. They talk about keeping. The mason with his hand-stone goes tapping in the morning cool, listening for hollow plaster, and patches where salt has bloomed in white beards on the north courses. His son carries lime up, and shit down-out — because what goes down must be carried up, including shit, which goes up in sealed jars to the dry fields and comes back as onions and chili. The weaver under the reed awning clacks her loom beater in time with the drip; beneath her, in a sleeping niche just deep enough for a curled body, her rolled mat and copper bowl wait for night. Across the void, an old man mends what looks like a fishing net and is really a pigeon net, sitting in a doorway that once let water in and now lets a man out to sit.

Work sorts itself by height because damp sorts itself by height. Down at the bottom, near the pan that never dries and never rises, are the goats — three brindled, chewing imported straw in a pen of lashed poles — and the toads the color of wet mortar who start singing on warm evenings, and the women who dip drinking water by unspoken law. You may wash at the top spouts, but you do not foul the pan. Everyone, even a stranger's child, is corrected instantly, gently, if they forget. Up one terrace: the clay hearths, smoke tonguing the plaster black above each, and the pots crammed with tended green — chili, onion tops, basil, a fig kept small as a dog — each watered by the spoonful from the pan, each guarded from goats as if it were a child. Soil has to be carried down in baskets; there is none to dig. You do not dig. There is nowhere to dig to.

Above that: looms, sleeping niches, pigeon lofts made from stacked jars with their mouths knocked out. Laundry lines and gourd trellises and rope bridges strung across the void, because every horizontal inch is occupied and so they have taken the air as well. The whole pit hums with near-touching — shoulder brushing shoulder on stairs, a basket handed hand to hand up, chanting to keep time when something heavy must rise: a block, a water jar, a sick goat. No wheel. No pulley larger than a hand-span. Rope and back and shoulder.

The old women are the weather. They are small, darker from years of sun-bounce, and they watch the walls sweat. On certain mornings the condensation beads not randomly but in lines and whorls that follow old repairs, outlining blocked doors and filled channels you cannot otherwise see. The pit draws its earlier plumbing in water for an hour. If the ghost-door on the east wall sweats complete before sunrise, rain within three days outside. They read it the way you would read a face, and they are usually right. The masons shrug — different mortar, different cold — and the pigeon-keepers say the birds know first; they go quiet an hour before the sweating starts. Both are true. No one needs it to be one thing.

And the birds are not decoration. They are residents with tenure longer than any human lease.

Pigeons by the hundred — grey, white, rust — are livestock and weather and noise. They pour out of wall holes and jar-lofts in a clatter like thrown gravel, circle the square of sky, funnel back. Children know individuals. Men harvest squabs and eggs and droppings for the pots. Swallows own the day-shift, cutting lines through the pigeons, screaming, skimming footprint puddles for mosquito larvae. At dusk the shift changes without ceremony and bats take the air, leather-quiet after the day's noise. Two lean cats, temple-thin, patrol the fourth terrace and are paid in offal; everyone knows not to pet them on duty. Lizards, banded and quick, work the sunlit courses for flies. Rats work the night courses for grain, and are worked in turn by cats and traps. A kestrel has learned the trick of the place — stoop from the parapet, bank off the walls, take a pigeon mid-pit while the whole flock thunders in echo — and is tolerated as a tax. Even the ferns have citizenship: maidenhair in the drip-lines, caper bushes exploding sideways from a joint, moss where a spout never stops weeping. Everything else green is a guest that must be vouched for, watered, defended.

At evening they become most themselves and least like you imagined.

The light goes horizontal and cuts the pit in half, one side honey-gold, one side indigo, and cooking smoke cannot rise straight out but hangs in a blue layer at the second terrace until a night draft pulls it up like a cork. Lamps are lit in niches, little clay saucers of oil, and all that made, worked, hand-plastered surface flickers. The goats doze. The pigeons sit panting with open beaks. The loom stops. The toads start. People eat from shared pots on ledges, feet dangling over a forty-foot drop they no longer see as a drop, talking low so as not to wake the echo. A mason tells a boy where to tap tomorrow. A pigeon-keeper whistles his birds home. An old woman touches a wall to feel if it is exhaling yet.

From the rim, if you lie on your belly and look over, you understand finally that they are not living *in* a ruin or *around* a well. They are the thing the pit does to stay alive. Hands repairing as fast as it erodes. Feet dishing the steps deeper in the exact center. Voices keeping low enough to hear the drip return.
