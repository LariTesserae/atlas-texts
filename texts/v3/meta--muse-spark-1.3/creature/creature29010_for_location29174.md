At first you think it is people.

Because a shutter slides back and there are eyes. Because a red hand comes out with a flake of flint and trims the moss. Because children sit with their legs over a terrace and watch you. It looks like a village that happens to be overgrown.

Stay longer. Let the stillness work on you. The people do not separate out.

They are small — not dwarfish, but made compact by the place. Low doorways, shoulder-wide alleys, ladders that sway if you climb too fast have pruned any largeness out of them over generations. Broad in the knee and elbow from crouching. Pale in a way the moor above never allows, a white that has gone slightly green at the edges, as if the moss has bled into them. Cheeks and hands and noses always red, chapped to the point of shining, but they do not shiver that you can see. Their breath hangs longer than yours, white plumes that do not dissipate in the still air, that join the damp and bead again on the moss. They breathe the town and drink it back.

Their eyes are the most changed part. Narrowed permanently against the trapped light. The white stone throws noon back at itself until you have to squint, and they live inside that glare all day. They have deep fans at the corners, lids kept at half-mast. They look at you sideways, through lashes. But in shade, under a slab, in the blue hour when the bowl goes indigo, their pupils open huge and black. They see the tick-drop fall before you hear it.

For they hear differently. You come in loud — boots wrapped in nothing, breath ragged, heart thudding. The moss eats it. The cold deadens it. They have leaned all the way into that deadening until it is a medium. A ladder creaks like a knuckle and three heads turn at once. A bunting opens its beak on the rim and makes no sound you can carry, but a child on a roof-lawn lifts a finger to mark it. They have learned to work stone on stone without striking. They lift, they do not drop. They set, they do not hammer. When they must use voice, they do not throw it. They put mouth to ear, close enough that the warmth of the breath is part of the word, and exhale meaning. Mostly they do not need to.

They gesture. Not a formal sign language, nothing so invented. Just the economy of people who have lived elbow to elbow for longer than anyone remembers: two fingers flicked — *move aside, let me pass on this board*. Palm flat to own chest — *stay, do not step there, the sphagnum is fruiting*. Chin tipped to a trough — *it needs stirring*. A hand held out cupped, then turned over and emptied — *it is kept, it is enough*.

What they do, all day, is keep.

You watch the man with the slipped block. He does not build. He kneels, works his fingers into the joint until green comes away in tufts, packs fresh, wet sphagnum in behind with his thumbs, then lifts the same limestone block — it has sat there a hundred years, its underside dark and soft — and seats it back in the exact hollow it left. He runs a palm over it to test damp. He nods. That is a morning's work.

You watch the clipper. Every roof is a lawn mown to the stone. Pillow moss will swallow a house in a season if left. She goes along the parapet on her knees with a flint flake, harvesting the day's new growth to the line of the stones beneath, tucking the cuttings into a pouch at her waist. Nothing is wasted. That green will chink a wall, or line a shoe, or go into a trough to filter seep.

You watch the water child. There is almost no open water here, only beads and drips and finger-depths in log troughs under slabs. She carries a cupped bark scoop from one black trough to another and back again, and again, all afternoon. Not to move it. To keep it from skinning over with ice. To keep it open. The troughs must never freeze solid and never go dry. She stirs them with her own small warmth.

You watch the old woman. On her back, in a net of sinew, a stack of flat gray cakes — lichen scraped from the outer rim where the wind keeps it cropped, pounded, wetted with seep, pressed and dried on sun-warmed stone. That is bread here. That and seeds combed from the roof-lawns, and the eggs the ptarmigan sometimes leave and sometimes do not. There is no fire. The smoke-holes are old architecture, a memory of a choice. No smoke ever rises now. Fire would dry, would noise, would change the air. They eat cold and stay cold and keep the cold pooled around them like a blanket.

They are not alone in the keeping. That is the next thing to unlearn — that people live here and animals visit.

The voles live here. Fat as fists, brown-gold, moving openly down the middle of walkboards, between boots, over thresholds. Unafraid because no one chases. They mow the seed-heads, they turn the thatch, their droppings feed the moss. The shrews live here, flickering white-toothed along wall-tops, eating the spiders that eat the midges. The spiders live here, freckle-small, stringing lines from fern to fern. The woodlice live here, pale and slow, turning the dead leaf back into soil in the joints. Moths the color of lichen dust the walls at dusk. Black beetles work the peat chinking. Above the rim churns a weather of redpolls and snow buntings, dropping down one by one to pick, to shit seed, to leave. A ptarmigan hen leads her chicks across a bone-ladder bridge in single file every morning like a liturgy, and the children make room without being told.

And under and over it all, the green itself lives here — not as decoration but as citizen, as majority.

Pillow moss, feather moss, sphagnum swollen with held breath. Ferns uncurling from every crevice, liverworts slick as organs on the north faces, dwarf willow pruned by a hundred years of pinching into bonsai knots that hold the parapets together with roots. Map-gray lichen, sulfur-yellow lichen, black script lichen writing its illegible text across every white face. It upholsters. It silences. It sweats. Every cushion exhales condensation. Every stone, even looking dry, feels damp to the palm. Something has licked the walls — you can see the faint tongued paths where voles and people both take morning water straight from the stone.

That is who lives in the bowl: a single crowded body made of stone and leaf and flesh held at exactly full.

No one is born extra. No terrace expands. The birds come and go but the number stays. The ice forms and melts and forms. The moss is clipped to the line daily. The same block is set back in the same seat. The water is carried there and back to stay where it is.

They are not villagers. They are the town keeping itself alive — hands and paws and fronds and feet, all tending, all tended, breathing white into cold bright air that never moves.
