At first you think nothing does.

That's the wrongness you carry in from the lip. A factory town should have rats and pigeons and men in doorways. A marsh this hot and wet should be deafening with frogs and buzzing black with flies. Here the flywheels turn for no eyes, the sodium lamps burn for no moths, the banana leaves tear and no beetle comes to finish the edge. You listen for a splash, a call, and get only water on concrete and iron on iron, and your own breath too loud underneath.

Then you correct yourself. Not empty. Full to bursting, only full of things that don't have faces.

The rooted ones live here. They are the citizens.

They don't live like weeds in a ruin, sneaking in the cracks. They live like they own the heat and have made terms with the iron. Each works has its own keepers, its own breed suited to its leak.

Around the boiler house the banana clumps stand twenty feet tall in rings, trunks fat with stored water. The vent blast shreds them every eleven minutes — you hear the leaves rip like cloth — and they don't die. They put out a new roller of leaf a week, pale green and perfect, unrolling in the steam. They have given up on fruit the way animals need it. No bats, no bees under the lid to pollinate. They sucker and clone, daughter to mother to grandmother in a tight circle, immortal by repetition.

In the ditches the taro stands with leaves big as doors, elephant ears black-green and beaded with condensate that never dries. Their stalks are thick as your wrist, plumbed straight into hot runoff. Where a man would scald, they quicken. Their corms boil gently in black mud and love it.

Over the machine shop the banyans have learned masonry. They straddled the walls fifty years ago and now grip them like hands, mortar crumbling sweetly into root acid. They send prop roots down in curtains into the sluice outfall, drinking water no animal could drink — 40, 45 degrees, copper-green — and transpiring it straight back out through a thousand leaves to make more lid. If you put your hand on a root in the green shadow under the cable-vine tunnel, you feel it warm and faintly pulsing with lift. They are slowly crushing the shop even as they shade it. They don't care. Building and tree are one structure now.

Above you the second canopy lives: morning glory and bitter melon using the pipe runs and sagging overhead wires as trellises. They have laced cable to vine to cable until you can't tell which is holding which. They tighten the belts. Literally. A drive belt left slack will be found in a month furred with tendril, pulled true again. They flower without pollinators — small, furled trumpets that open in the dim brown noon and close unvisited, setting seed by themselves or not at all. It doesn't matter. The vine doesn't need to travel. It just needs to continue.

Out on the north settling pond, where the edge water is 50 degrees and turquoise with dissolved salts, the floating ones live: water lettuce and hyacinth matted so thick you think you could walk it, pale roots trailing a foot down into poison, leaves steaming gently in the evening light. Nothing eats them from below — no carp, no snail — and nothing crops them from above. So they pile, and the lower layers cook and melt into tea, and the upper layers root into the rot and steam harder.

What roots here roots in metal. That's the rule. On the canal banks the orange bloom is not rust, or not only rust — iron bacteria, in slicks and fringes, oxidizing what the pipes bleed. Copper-tolerant ferns feather out of every mortar crack, green on green, their spores able to germinate on brass. Algae blacks the brick to waist height, exactly to where the splash reaches. Above that, rust-red. The town has a tideline, and everything living knows where to sit on it.

Under them, and in them, live the scald.

You don't see them until you learn to look at water, not leaf. The canal's green-black is alive with thermophiles. The floating mat between the islands that looks solidly green is a skin of duckweed and algae over hot mud that will take your boot — and under that skin, in the anoxic dark where your boot would go, is soup: fermenting leaf litter that never got chewed because there is nothing to chew it, breaking down by heat and bacteria alone, silent, sour, 38 degrees. It sighs up methane that joins the lid. The drip that falls constantly from every pipe and leaf, clear and tempting and tasting of pennies and hot stone with numbness on the tongue — that is their sweat, condensed. The town exhales them and you inhale them on your shirt.

And then the heavy breathers live here. The machines.

Don't think of them as running empty. Think of them as grazing.

Each has a body temperature, a habit, a voice. The three boiler houses low in the center breathe slow and huge, chests rising in pressure gauges long dead but still twitching. The dynamo hall with its windows blown out houses the flywheels — slow turning discs taller than you, throwing shadows across the far wall, each revolution a long sigh. They are deaf and blind and patient. The drop-forge is the heart: THUMP — THUMP — THUMP on a timer no one remembers setting, shaking puddles into rings, shaking your sternum. You feel it before you hear it. The things that live here all feel it. To live here is to be able to live inside that thump without flinching.

The saw somewhere running on nothing burrs and stops, burrs and stops, chewing air. The transformers on their leaning poles hum the 60-cycle nerve-hum, insulators furred with moss, capacious and warm. They all tick and groan as they expand. Copper and iron pipes big as a man's torso run between buildings on rusted trestles, lagged in asbestos and vine, weeping constantly though it has not rained in days.

Every eleven minutes the stack screams — that rising thin note ten seconds before — and lets go in a white roar that drowns everything for forty seconds and lays fresh hot fog so you cannot see ten meters. Turn your back, cover your ears, shut your mouth, the rule says. The fog is not poisonous, exactly, but it scalds.

That is the lung clearing. That is the bowl coughing to keep its throat open. The shockwave strips new leaf, rattles rust loose, kills anything small and soft that might have wandered in over the levee. Perhaps that is why there are no flies. Perhaps it is the copper vapor, the constant pressure-shock, the infrasound from the forge that liquefies larvae. No one left a note. But the effect is an immune system: no birds, no herons, no rats, no mosquitoes. Cobwebs sag and fill with condensate and break, empty. The sodium lamps buzz orange all night over empty water and huge leaves, reflected double in the canal. No moth batters them. The light just burns.

The machines and the green keep each other. The pipes leak heat and water; the roots drink heat and water and shade the pipes. The vents make cloud; the cloud makes dim eternal evening; the dim makes huge soft leaves with no sunburn and no bite. Valves frozen open are fine because no one needs to throttle. Doors left open to let heat out are fine because no one needs to close. If a flywheel throws a belt, step back. It will slap and find its own way home, guided back into its groove by vine and groove-memory.

Put together, the bowl itself lives.

From the lip you saw it at once for a reason: it is one body, a kilometer across. The levee, gone soft and green, is its rim. The mill-race canal is its gut, moving hot green-black fast between concrete banks. The boiler houses are its belly, the stack its trachea, the low cloud-smoke-vapor-transpiration lid its held breath. The plants are skin and lung hairs, exhaling water to make more lid to hold more heat to move more water. It is a small, hot, wet engine for making vapor out of water and leaf out of vapor, settled long ago into its cycle, too loud and too empty and too green.

Men lived here once. You can see where they were rubbed out. Foundation slab with rails sticking out, drowned. Yard of stacked billets gone entirely to fern. The cistern by the dynamo hall, raised and roofed, still holds sweet water — the only sweet water — because the body remembers that its gut flora needed it. You drink from it and taste the last human thing. Everything else drips pennies.

If any of them stayed, they stayed by becoming un-animal: still, silent, deafened, moving only to step back from a thrown belt, breathing shallow through the scald, drinking cistern water, not speaking because you have to shout even in the lulls and shouting changes nothing. They would be indistinguishable now from root and rust. You will not see them at windows.

You, walking the duckboards and pipe-bridges, staying on the boards because the ground is floating mat over hot mud, are the only animal the bowl permits, and only briefly. Your shirt wets in a minute not with sweat but with everything condensing on you. The iron handrails weep onto your palm. The undersides of pipes drip on your neck as you pass under tunnels of leaf and cable. You breathe too loud in the gaps. You notice the absence under the roar.

Stay, and the town will notice you the way a body notices a splinter: not with anger, with heat and wet and copper and shock, until you leave or lie down and root or rust.
