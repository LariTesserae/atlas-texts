They do not live here the way things live elsewhere. Nothing here drinks, nothing washes, nothing rots. What lives in the Hollow stays, dries, and is used again.

Start with what you almost step on, because at first you think the ground is empty.

It isn't. It breathes.

Under every foot of white pan and grey-green mat, the earth of this place is moving. The masons. Chestnut-brown, each no longer than the nail on your little finger, blind as grubs, wingless, heavy-headed. Not ants, though your brain wants to call them ants to make them familiar. Their bodies are softer, their walk slower, their heads too large for their thoraxes, jaws made not for cutting meat but for chewing wool.

They come up in columns that never end. If you crouch by one of the southern boulevards — those trampled white avenues between the small towers — you can watch for an hour and the line does not break. Outbound empty-jawed, inbound carrying. A flake of stone-fleece, a strip of fallen ribbonbush, a pinch of salt dust, a dead cricket leg, a thatchbird feather frayed to down. Two lanes, always two, the outbound hugging the left edge, the inbound hugging the right, antennae flicking, tapping, tasting the road. Step your boot in the way and they do not go around in panic. Each one climbs up, taps its antennae twice against the leather as if reading a pebble laid down a century ago, and climbs down the other side. The road is older than you. You are weather.

Follow an inbound lane to a tower base and you see what they are. Builders.

The towers are not rock. Put your cheek close and you see the grain: chewed fleece-wool, powdered caliche, frass, shed skins, guano dust, all cemented with saliva that sets like resin. Each mouthful laid down wet and darkening as it hardens. Layer over layer for a hundred years, for five hundred. A new lip on an old vent flares a little paler than the wall below it. A patch where last winter's slump was filled in quilts out a little prouder. In the central plaza the five oldest ones are ten meters around at the base, buttressed like old trees, lopsided and re-plastered until you can read collapse in them the way you read rings.

Inside they are hollow. Not empty hollow — lung hollow. Galleries spiraling up around a central chimney, nursery chambers deep where the cold cannot reach, fungus-wool gardens where they compost the litter they chew. You cannot see this unless a wall has fallen, and few walls fall and stay fallen. But you can feel it. Lay your palm flat on sunless stone at dusk and it is warm. Two, three degrees warmer than the air. That is not sun. That is millions of bodies and the slow fermentation of their compost, and deeper still, the fanners.

They are a caste you rarely see outside: pale, big-abdomened masons who never leave the dark, who stand in ranks in the vents and beat their stubbed wing-cases — wings they never grew to use — to pump air. In and out, in and out, day and night. The tower exhales through its flared lip. On a still evening you can hold a strip of ribbonbush to a vent and watch it lift and flutter. All those exhales together make the low organ note that sits under the cricket trill. You feel it in your sternum before you hear it. The City humming to keep its children warm in a place with no sun.

Around each tower is its garden. The midden halo. Powdery rust-colored frass and white shed skins spilled out the base galleries for centuries has enriched the dust just enough, just there, that the stone-fleece grows thickest in a dark collar. From above each tower wears a green-black wreath. That is not accident. That is farming without intention. Eat the fleece, shit the ground, grow more fleece.

The fleece itself lives. It is the first green you mistook for shadow. Low cushions a foot thick, sage and ash and pale olive, made of millions of branched fronds no thicker than hair, crisp to the touch, soft to the eye. It does not root. It lies on dust and rock and combs the air. Every wingbeat in the Hollow lifts fine mineral powder; every night the cold settles; the fleece catches it all. Crush it and no water comes out, only a sharp oily smear that smells of turpentine and salt. Glycerin, not sap. It does not wilt when torn; it hardens like varnish. It grows a millimeter a year. The mat you stand on may have been continuous since before any town you know was named.

Above the fleece hangs the airwort. From the softened cliffs in veils ten feet long, paper-thin, grey-white, rootless. They sway when there is no wind you can feel, turning slowly in the sinking cold air, living on time and the exhalations of the City below. And the ribbonbush — head-high tangles in the sheltered swells, no leaves at all, only flat grey-green straps that rattle like dry paper when a nightjar pushes through. In a good decade a ribbonbush adds a centimeter. Some of the thickets are older than the towers beside them, and will outlast them when the towers slump to hummocks and then to swells.

And then, on that slow plant and slower mason foundation, the fast ones. Loud ones.

The crickets first, because they are everywhere and almost invisible. Pale, eyeless-looking cave-crickets with antennae twice their body, legs like dry twigs. They skitter low over the pan in legions, popping up like seeds spat from a mouth, feeding on mason midden, on fallen airwort flakes, on the salt flowers themselves. Their trill is the sheet everything else is written on — high, metallic, endless, layer upon layer until at a distance you stop hearing individual insects and hear only a solid band of sound.

Where the crickets go, flattened against tower sides, are the bark-lizards. The color of dust exactly, the texture of cement exactly. You will not see one until it pulses its throat. Then you see twenty. They press themselves into the fluting of the towers, into the shade side where the fleece collar meets the wall, waiting hours for a cricket to misstep. They do not drink. They get their glycerin second-hand from the guts of their prey. Their droppings are dry white pellets that the masons promptly carry away as building grit.

And over all of it, the birds. The second builders.

They own the air in pulses. For long minutes the Hollow is only trill and chitter and vent-hum, with a few hundred swifts carving thin lines between towers. Then something — a change in cold, a thinning of light only they perceive — triggers it, and the floor exhales birds.

Cliff martins by the ten thousand, soot-brown and white-bellied, screaming a needle-thin scream that in such numbers becomes pressure. They plaster their nests in tenements under every tower lip, side by side by side, cups made not of mud — there is no mud — but of dust mixed with fresh mason saliva stolen right off a building crew, and with an oily secretion from their own crops. Pirated mortar. They cling to vertical cement and raise each other's young inches apart, quarreling constantly.

Ash swifts, smaller, paler, faster, nesting deep in the honeycomb pits of the softened cliffs, screaming as they shear past the airwort veils without touching them.

And the thatchbirds. Crow-sized, ash-grey, raucous, intelligent in the way crows are. They do not steal mortar. They steal plant. They land on ribbonbush thickets and strip long fibers with sideways jerks of heavy beaks, then carry them to the tops of dead towers — towers whose colony has died or moved — and weave. Huge shaggy communal roofs, overhanging like hats, a dozen, two dozen entrance holes underneath leading to individual nests tucked into the old vents. From the rim those capped towers look like trees in a place that has no trees. Houses that were chimneys that look like trees.

Last are the nightjars. Big, slow, silent, with eyes like moths, feathered the color of salt and lichen. By day they are invisible, packed body to body in the ribbonbush, a thousand in one thicket, layered like scales, breathing together. At deepening dusk they lift all at once without a call — a soft thunder of down — and go up over the rim to hawk dust-moths where the desert still holds last light. They return before full dark, bellies full of outside, and shit outside into inside. Nothing leaves, but a little enters.

That is the rule they all obey without knowing it. What falls stays. A dead swift does not rot. In two days it is a mummy, perfect feathers and hollow eyes. In a month wind and crickets have taken it to powder and bone splinters. In a year those splinters are in a tower wall. Guano becomes mortar becomes garden becomes mason becomes cricket becomes lizard becomes guano again. Even the sound stays — thrown east to west wall and back a half-second later, so every wingbeat lands twice.

Stand in the plaza when a pulse peaks and you cannot hear your hands clap. Dust lifts off the vents with the hum and hangs glittering in the dim blue light. Your boots whiten with salt and frass. Your lips crack. A mason taps your boot and moves on.

Under your palm the tower is warm.
