No one lives here, if you mean it the way you mean it in a town.

No one stands up. No one looks around. No one has a door to close.

Stay a little longer, until your boots have gone soft and your breath has stopped sounding strange to you, and that answer changes. Then it reverses.

Everyone lives here. Everything is living here, so completely that there is no place left to *live on* — only to live *in*, and *as*.

At first you think it must be moss. Just moss. A single green patience spread flat. Then you push your hand in to the wrist and feel how wrong that word is. It is not a thing. It is a crowd.

The ones who live here longest are the low ones. The ones who never learned, or unlearned, how to stand.

First, the builders. The cushion mosses and the sphagnums, hummocked up big as armchairs. From above they look soft. They are not. Put your weight on them and they take you and give back, slowly, like a lung. Each cushion is a city of ten thousand upright stems, dead at the base and living at the tip, growing a millimeter a year at the top while turning to black butter at the bottom. They do not root in ground. They make ground. Old leaves thatch down, waterlogged and airless, and do not rot. They pickle. The sphagnum heads are acid — they sour the water around them to pH like vinegar, so that whatever falls in, leaf or fly or willow twig, stays half-whole for a hundred, a thousand years. A builder that is also a vat. A lung that is also a tomb.

Between them, plastered tight where nothing else will stick, the liverworts. Black-green, slick as oilskin. They are the film crew. When frost splits a cushion open, when a bank slumps and shows black peat, they are there within days, filming over the wound in a living skin, holding damp in, holding the edge until the slower mosses can come back. You have to get on your knees to see them at all. From standing height they are just the darker shade between greens.

And the lichens — the reindeer lichens, grey and brittle, branching like miniature antlers, dry to the crack when you touch them. They are not one thing but two, a fungus married to an alga, living so slowly they make the moss look hurried. They do not drink from the sponge. They drink from air. Condensation beads on their branches and they take it, bead by bead. They crumble under a boot and do not forgive. Twenty years to grow back the width of your palm.

Then the old ones.

The dwarf willow. Ankle-high and a hundred years old. You could walk past a hundred of them thinking they were weeds. Kneel, part the moss around one, and you find a trunk thick as your wrist, lying horizontal, crawling, bark furrowed and hard as pipe, scarred by a century of freeze and thaw. Its branches lift up only an inch or two, two small oval leaves to a twig, turning yellow-red for the two-week autumn it gets. It does not try to rise. It tries to persist. Its roots go sideways through thatch, finding the pockets, holding the mat together like stitching. Some of them are being nursed to death — the moss that sheltered the seedling now rising up over the stem, smothering it, bleaching its twigs white so they stick up like wires. Nurse turned shroud. That is how old it gets here. Even help is slow suffocation.

The sedges do the plumbing. Tussocks of stiff blades, roots pumping air down into the airless peat so they don't drown, stems swelling with taken-up water until they splay outward in a star. Star moss does its part with bristles, holding dew upright. Every hair of every leaf is a condenser, combing water out of thin air and not letting go.

Because none of them live alone. That is the rule you feel in your soles before you understand it. Everything touches. There is no bare larger than your palm that stays bare. If you lift one, you lift five. Willow root through moss thatch through liverwort film through lichen crust. Pull, and the whole upholstery puckers. What lives here is not a list of species. It is a mat that has learned to be an animal.

It breathes. Very slowly, frost-jacked up an inch in the morning and slumped back by afternoon. It drinks sideways, not downhill, passing water stem to stem instead of letting it run. It eats light and rain and its own dead, pickling the dead so they pile up instead of disappearing, raising its own floor higher year by year. A cold factory with no hands, where the workers are also the product and the building.

Underneath all that, smaller still, the ones you will never see without kneeling and then kneeling further in with a lens: the springtails flicking blind in the thatch, white as grains of salt. The mites, red-brown, armored. Rotifers and tardigrades in the water film around each moss leaf, drying to a husk when it freezes and swelling back to life when it thaws, dying and un-dying a dozen times a summer. Fungal threads white as nerve, lacing the peat, digesting what even the acid leaves. Without them the refinery would clog. They are the night shift that never clocks out.

Animals, as you count animals — those are rumors. Tenants. Visitors the mat tolerates.

The vole who never shows himself, only his tunnel-hum traveling under your boots, eating sedge bases, lining a nest with willow down. The white ptarmigan a hundred meters off, so still she is lichen until she blinks, picking buds and grit, her feet feathered for this sponge. The lone crane fly hanging in cold air, legs dangling, laying eggs that will become leatherjackets in the peat. They do not live *as* the place lives. They move through it, light and quick and warm-blooded or brief, and then they are gone, and the eyes of black water close over where they were.

Stand in the middle at midday and you understand the proportion at last. What lives here is mostly plant become geography — hummocks for hills, hollows for valleys, thatch for bedrock, living leaves for roof. It does not hurry because hurry would lift it off the ground it is making. It does not make sound because sound would be waste. It just keeps at its slow throughput, pressing and pickling and mending, inching forward under your feet even while you stand there watching for movement.

You came looking for inhabitants. You are standing inside one.
