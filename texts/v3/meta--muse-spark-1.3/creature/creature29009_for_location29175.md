They are not, at first, anyone.

They are motion. A boiling on the floor of the forest that you mistook for water shivering. A seethe of backs.

Stay. Let your eye stop trying to see big.

The boil resolves.

Most of them are white. The color of skim milk, of mushroom flesh, of something that has never needed color. Rice-grain long, smaller than rice-grain, soft-bodied, blind or nearly blind. Springtails. If you could hold one — you can't, at this scale you would crush it with breath — you would see the gut shining yellow-green through the belly wall where it has eaten pollen and fungus, you would see the short antennae going constantly, flick-flick-flick, tasting. They have a tube on their belly that drinks water straight out of wet air, and a folded tail-spring tucked under them like a pocketknife. When they are startled they are simply gone. *Pop.* Air, and an empty place where a body was.

Among them, slower, harder: the amber ones. Mites. Like walking seeds, chestnut and honey-colored, armored head to rear in plates that shine with the water-skin. They do not hurry. They cannot afford to. Every step is deliberate, eight legs finding purchase on slick moss-leaf. Where the springtails flow around obstacles, the mites chew through them. You can see their jaws working even from here — that distant sanding sound. Ten thousand minute rasps adding up to a weather.

Under them, in them: the blind pulsers. Beetle larvae, white grubs the length of a fingernail clipping, eyeless, ribbed, moving by peristalsis through the litter. They do not build. They till. They go through fallen leaf and frass and dead ancestor and turn it to pulp behind them. And in the thinnest film of water at the foot of the pillars, where peat-tea stands black and reflective, something threads without legs at all — nematodes, clear worms no thicker than a hair, swimming by whipping, living entirely inside wetness.

This is the town. Not one kind, but a pact.

The white ones and the amber ones are the builders. Watch a single street — that glossy worn trail in the algal slime between two moss-trunks, polished by a hundred thousand passages. A column comes home along it, each porter with a yellow pollen grain clutched in its jaws or rolled before it like a boulder, larger than its head. Antennae touch antennae as they pass. That touch is everything: *food this way, danger that way, repair needed above.* Their speech is not sound. It is smell laid down on the road, cut-grass and mushroom and, when a porter is crushed, a sharp almond of alarm that empties the road for a minute before filling it again.

Up the flank of the pillar, the work never stops because the foundation never stops. The moss they live on is alive. It grows at the tip, a millimeter a day, lifting whatever they lashed to it. So an old balcony of pressed frass and shed skins and chewed fiber — papery, tough, dried in the cold — slumps and tears as its pillar shifts. Two leaves higher, a crew is already chewing a new one out. One braces a splinter of spruce bark as a shovel. One draws silk — from its own mouth, from its own rear — and rolls it between forelegs into rope. One daubs fresh moss pulp into the joint. The pulp keeps living. It seals green. The house heals itself into the tree.

They build with the dead. There is no elsewhere to put them. A springtail dies where it falls — cold, a fungus, a misstep under a drip — and within minutes its sisters are on it. Not mourning. Butchering. Soft belly for food. Hard chitin for wall. If you look close at the platform edges, at the stoppered mouths of those translucent store-sacs packed with black spores and yellow pollen, you will see them faced with pale, translucent plates: head capsules, leg segments, back shields. Ancestors as bricks. The walls glint.

And among the builders, hunters.

She is impossible to miss once you learn to see her. On the shadow side of the pillar, huge — to them a wolf, to you a poppy seed — a velvet mite freezes. Rust-red, furred all over in dense hairs that hold a bead of water off her body, so she glows in the rot-glow. Her front legs lift and taste the air. She does not see the column below. She smells its vibration. When she runs, she runs impossibly fast for this cold place, a red blur between worlds.

In a pulp tunnel mouth, he waits. A false-scorpion, the size of a sesame crumb, amber claws held open wider than his body. No tail, no sting — only those claws with venom in the tips, and silk glands in his jaws with which he has spun a tiny dry chamber to molt in. He backs out only for the kill. Below them both, in the water film, the white flatworm glides. Blind, ear-headed, utterly silent, feeling for the thrash of a nematode.

It is not cruel. It is crowded. Grazer and fungus and predator happen in the same square inch because there is no other square inch warm and wet enough. A needle of ice lengthens in the peat with a tick you almost hear and topples a pollen sac; within minutes a crew is there, chewing it upright, re-lashing with fungal cord. A god-footstep passes overhead — a shrew, a vole, your own knee shifting on the sphagnum — and the whole dome compresses, bark sighs, a drip is shaken loose. No one stops. Stillness is death. To stop moving in this cold is to freeze to the leaf.

Because winter is coming and they know it in their bodies. They do not think it. Their blood thickens with glycerol, with antifreeze. The store-sacs swell. The chewing slows at the edges of night but never ceases — fire is impossible here, everything too wet, too cold to burn, so there is only work to keep warm. Light, when it leaks blue around the bark edge, is not for seeing. It hurts. It dries. They shy from it, press deeper into the black peat air where smell is sight, where the tremor of a drip — *plik* — through stem and water tells them more than any eye could: leaf to peat, peat to root, sky still holding.

Put your face closer until your breath fogs and rains back onto your lip. You are weather to them. Your exhale is a warm front that condenses on their forest and falls as rain. Your cheek on the peat is a season.

They do not look back at you. They have no eyes for you. A column of white backs moves pollen home between vast trunks. A mite polishes the road with its belly. A larva pulses through the dark, turning yesterday into tomorrow.

The drip on the bark lip swells, holds the faint green foxfire for a moment like a lens, and lets go.

*Plik.*

The town glistens, and goes on.
