Nothing lives here. That's the first thing it tells you.

And then you stay still long enough that your wrists go cool and your breathing slows without your permission, and you understand that nothing living here is itself a kind of resident. An absence kept so clean, so even, for so long, takes on weight. It develops habits.

What lives here is the Kept.

You don't see it at first because you are looking for a body. A fish-shape, a scuttle under a stone, something that darts and grazes. There is none of that. The water is too worked for that. The faint copper and ozone on your lips tell you why — this is water that has passed through filters and fields, water that is mixed top to bottom until it has no warm skin and no cold sink, water that is turned over and over in a slow, machined circuit. Nothing with a mouth can make a living off that. The chain stops after the first link on purpose.

So what lives here stopped trying to be an animal a long time ago.

It is spread thin. That's the rule of this basin — much space between things — so it learned to live spaced out.

In one place it is four strands. Pale green weed, no thicker than thread, bearded sparsely off a sloping stone. Bleached by low food and even light. They sway almost imperceptibly, not in wind but in the circulation. If you watch a mote in the light you see the path they sway to: out from center, around the rim, back to center, over and over. The proper motion. The strands don't reach. They don't grope. They just hold their place in the circuit and let the water bring them what little there is. They have been on that exact stone for years. They will be there in ten years. They do not grow to fill. They do not die back.

Ten feet away, it is a stain. A flat film of olive-drab algae clinging to rock so tight it looks like discoloration. No thickness. No meadow. Just a mouth pressed flat to stone, drinking light. It does not get eaten, because there is nothing here that eats. So it is never cropped, never torn. It just persists, millimeter by millimeter, century-slow.

In the low spots it is rust that isn't rust. A dusting of iron precipitated out of the water itself, tan silt blushed red. That is its bone. Its shed skin. The mineral the machines leave behind and it learns to wear.

If you put your hand down and fan the silt, it clouds and then settles back in exactly the same pattern within an hour, as if it remembers. That's because it does. The floor — rounded cobble and fine silt and bedrock worn smooth, long past arguing with erosion — is its bed, and it has lain in it so long the bed has taken its shape.

It has no front and no back. No eyes, because the light here has no direction, only green-white evenness from all sides, mid-morning forever. No echo, because sounds don't echo. No weather.

What it has instead is hearing of a different kind.

Put your ears half under and you feel it before you hear it: the low hum, 60-cycle steady, felt in the sternum. Big engines turning far above the rim where you will never see them. Pumps you will never find, because there is nothing built here — no wall, no culvert mouth, no concrete, no dam face. Only natural stone and silt. The machines live outside the body.

But the water knows them. And so does it.

Every so often the hum deepens for exactly three seconds and the pressure in your ears changes by a hair and releases. Intake. Discharge. Intake. Discharge. That is its heart beating, outside itself. That is its breath. It times itself to that. It does not breathe water in and out the way a fish does. It lets itself be breathed — drawn up and out by the slow circuit, held at this level, at this clarity, at this chemistry, all the time.

Occasionally a single bead of oil, rainbow-thin, passes across the surface and is gone. It does not flinch. It has learned that the distant world leaks a little and then corrects itself.

Once in a long while a leaf falls in from above and circles for days without sinking or rotting, preserved by the cool and the mineral balance. The Kept keeps it company. It does not decay it — there is nothing here to decay with — and it does not push it away. It lets it circle in the mote-path until the slow circuit finally draws it up and out. That is the closest thing it has to a visitor. That is the closest thing it has to speech.

If you stay, your own motion will begin to feel wrong to you — too abrupt, too animal. You will find yourself slowing to the weed's motion, to the mote's motion, which is almost none. The place rewards stillness. It is in equilibrium and it intends to stay there.

That intention is the creature.

Not dead. Maintained. A sample kept forever under glass between low stone walls, below the water table, below the grade of the land, down where there is nowhere else to go but up. Cool enough to pull the heat straight out of you. Clear enough to see edge to edge. Old enough that everything agrees.

It does not want. It keeps.

And as long as the far engines turn, it will go on keeping itself: four threads, one stain, a dust of iron, a memory of silt, a body made of paused water, listening through its whole thin skin to a hum it will never see.
