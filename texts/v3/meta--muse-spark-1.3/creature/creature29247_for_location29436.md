They are not, at first, what you think they are.

From the rim, coming down the worn steps, you take them for builders. That is the hammering, the scaffolds, the dust. Men with stone mauls, chips flying, women spreading clay. And then, lower, when you see the channels and the jars and the children running, you revise: water-carriers. Then lower still, when you see how much is being unbuilt — a wall coming down here to go up a hundred paces over, timber propped, hide buckets left in a line — you think: scavengers. Nomads who happen to be indoors.

None of those are wrong, but they are all too sharp. Nothing here stays sharp.

The people are low and rounded like everything else that lasts.

Physically, first: they squint and shiver at once, as you do. Permanent squint. The light is total and the pan throws it back up, so their eyes are set deep, brow-creased even in the young, lashes white with lime dust. Their skin is pale underneath and sun-prickled on top — forearms and cheekbones and the backs of hands reddened, almost blistered, while the rest of them is cold. Their feet are the strangest. Thick, splayed, almost insensible. They walk bare or in soft hide slippers on stone that is drawing cold straight up through the soles, on fitted slab causeways that ring faintly, and they do not flinch. Children stand ankle-deep in sky-mirror pans after rain and laugh without sound.

They dress for two climates at once, because there are two. A light headcloth, undyed linen or bleached hide, wound tight against glare, tail left to cover the nape. And over everything a heavy mantle of dun wool, goat wool, smelling of damp stone and smoke and animal. It is too hot in the sun and exactly right in shadow. You see them shrug it up, shrug it down, a hundred times a day as they cross from glare into building-shadow and back, prickle to gooseflesh to prickle. Underneath, loose trousers, loose tunics, all patched, all lime-whitened at the knees and elbows. Their hands are white to the wrist, as if dusted in flour, nails cracked, knuckles split from lime and salt.

They do not speak. Not as a rule. Not as a vow.

You will hear a cough, a soft clonk of a goat's clay bell, the dull tap of hammer through felt, a jar set down. If you shout, the basin takes it. The porous pan, the slumped aprons of dust, the wide flat — they swallow echo. A yell goes three paces and dies. So they stopped.

What they have instead is hands and stone.

Haggling is fingers held up, palm turned, a touch on the goods, a nod. A child running wants passage: palm raised flat, held a second. That carries further than a name called. Two taps of a loose stone on a wall means *come here, hands needed*. That sound — stone on stone — is the one sound the hollow respects. It travels along the masonry, through the soles. Everyone feels it. A scaffold collapses and someone taps-taps and ten people swarm. A channel clogs and taps go down its length like a pulse and twenty hands clear it.

They move like the pigeons wheel. That is the second thing you learn.

There is no foreman shouting, no line, no bell. For an ancient flattened place it seethes — anthill, bazaar — but leaderless-seeming. Five houses around a pit act as one body. You watch it: a woman lifts her head to the west where the sky bruises, she raises her palm, the children turn with their jars without being told, the men leave the dressing-floor, someone caps a cistern with woven branches and stones. Rain comes violently out of clear sky. Channels run milky. For half a day everyone is water work. Then they sink back to stone work. No orders. They have lived here long enough — layer on layer, new wall on melted wall on discoloration — that the work knows them.

The house-groups are the units. Five houses around a pit, a long wall with niches, a tower no higher than three men already leaning and propped with timber. That is a family, though not only blood. Who shares a cistern shares everything. They sleep in thick-walled rooms, low, round-cornered, dim and cool at noon when the light is unbearable outside and shadows disappear and the whole hollow glows. For an hour the turbulence pauses. They lie on hide pallets in that damp-stone-goat-smoke smell and listen to condensation tick into jars. Then a cloud shadow crosses the rim, the glare breaks, and the whole hive pours back out, silent and fast.

Their children do not go to school. They run water. That is school. Endless relay from channel to house and back, small jars sloshing, bare feet learning every slab, every seep, every dark line where stone sweats in wall-shadow. By six they know: stay out of channels when the west bruises, water comes fast and shallow and strong enough to take goats. Drink only from covered pits, open pans are bitter. Don't build sharp. Tap twice. Keep moving.

Keep moving because the old ones learned and taught: nothing here stays. The slabs sweat, salt blooms pink and ochre and sulfur-white and falls, dust drifts and uncovers a foundation, a wall melts, a seep dries and another opens a hundred paces off. What looks like ruins is material in waiting. What looks like a new house is an old house moved. So they are not attached to place in the way plain-dwellers are. They are attached to *cold*. To water pockets. They follow them. The city is sparse not because it is empty but because it is in transit around its own floor.

What do they eat, with no green? At first you keep looking, as the eye does, and failing.

They eat what stays. Goats, small and shaggy and dun, that eat what no one else will — rope fray, hide scraps, the black fungal slick no thicker than paint in the dampest cracks, salt crust. And the people in turn eat goat: milk, curd kept cool in the thick rooms, blood drawn sparingly, meat rarely, on days when a wall is raised. They eat pigeon: not the flock — never the flock all at once — but squab and eggs taken by rule from certain niches in certain towers, leaving the rest. The towers are not watchtowers. They are roosts, tended, repaired even as they lean. The white pigeons, thousands, erupting in silent sheets, wheeling as a single decision-making body, are not livestock. They are fellow citizens. Their guano feeds the black slick. Their wings are the hush-hush overhead, a low pressure. The people orient by them; when the flock lifts without sound you can hear from twenty paces, everyone looks up.

They eat fish, blind and pale, flickering far below in black cistern water. Someone put them there generations ago and now they are in every pit. The fish eat mosquito larvae and each other and keep the drinking water living. To take one is a serious thing, a winter thing. Mostly they are left to turn slowly in the dark.

And they eat trade. Bazaar-energy is real. Goods laid on mats by the causeways: salt cakes scraped from shaded sides, nitre, dried curd, pigeon down, goat hair, rope, fired clay bells and jars, hammer-dressed blocks. What comes down from the plain — grain, timber, wool, metal used so sparingly you only see it as copper-green stains where a bracket holds — is paid for in water-rights and stone-work and salt. They do not farm. They maintain. Caravans, runners from above, come for the cool and the water held in stone through dry months. The basin is wide enough to hold weather. The people are the ones who know how to hold it.

The animals are not ornaments. They are the rest of the town.

The long-legged runners — between lizard and bird — stalk the empty pavements fifty, a hundred paces across, on stilt legs, picking at salt. They freeze at noon, legs casting no shadow, perfect statues until shadow returns. They clean. The beetles black as varnish gather at dawn on east faces to drink dew off their own backs — they are clocks; when you see them massing, you set out your jars. The goats trot and chew and clonk softly and find seeps before people do; a goat lingering at a wall base means dig there. The pigeons find updrafts. The fish tell if water is good: if they rise, do not drink.

And under all of them, the slick. That black paint in cracks, the only growth besides mineral bloom. They do not speak of it much. Women scrape it sparingly with copper blades, boil it. Old men will tell you, tapping the stone, that it tastes of iron and keeps the bowels moving. Whether that is true or a joke made in gesture, hard to tell.

If there is religion here, it is noon.

When shadows disappear and runners freeze and pigeons sit and light bounces up until you cannot tell sky from floor, they go inside. It is not sleep exactly. It is a yielding. To the cold coming up through soles, to the stone holding cold the way old bones do. Sun on shoulders outside, cold from below — at noon, inside, only cold remains. They lie still. Fish turn. Water ticks. The ancient floor, patient under all this brief frantic life, holds. Then they rise and pour out again.

They will not stay as you know staying. Ask where someone lives and they will point to a pit, a wall, a tower — all three of which may be moved by next season. Ask who leads and they will touch their own chest, then yours, then the stone. Ask their name and they will show you their hands.

They are the thing that lives by not insisting. Low, round, thick. Moving fast and saying nothing, in a white hollow that eats sound and gives back water, tick by tick.
