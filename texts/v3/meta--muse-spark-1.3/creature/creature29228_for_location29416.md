What lives here does not arrive. It accretes.

At first you think: nothing. That is what the valley wants you to think. Lush beyond reason and empty. No eye to catch you. No wing, no fin, no bite. You stand chest-deep in soup-warm water that should be flickering with minnows and it is empty of mouths. Flowers open white trumpets to the rain and nothing enters them. Pods burst and nothing flees.

Then you stay still long enough for your boots to start buzzing through the metal plate underneath, and you feel it. The throb. Irregular. Extra chambers. And you understand nothing here is empty. It is just that nothing here is animal.

What lives here is the works become habitat.

Not plants *on* machines. Plants *with* machines, *as* machines, until you stop being able to separate vine from wire, root from bearing, leaf-vein from copper pipe. A new kingdom learned very fast on very young rock.

Start with the skin, because that is what you walk on until it lets you through.

The mat. Water lettuce, bladderwort, salvinia, duckweed knitted into rafts thick enough to dull the rain-sound. From above, bright drowned green. From below, a hanging forest in reverse — pale root beards six feet down, swaying in the street-current, filtering. There are no fish to eat the rot, so the mat learned to eat it. It eats fallen petals, pollen slick, oil sheen, orange peels of fresh rust, its own dead. The bladderworts, who in a saner swamp would be trapping water fleas, here trap nothing living. They trap bubbles. Steam pearls, gas belches, hot eddies off a pump outflow. Each tiny bladder pops with a pressure change, digests a mouthful of broth-water rich in iron and phosphorous. The whole raft breathes in that cannon-boom you heard in the middle distance: gas builds under it, lifts it, it rips, it vents, it knits closed behind like flesh. If you stand on it too long, it will knit over your boot.

Under the mat, in the bottle-green gloom, live the stilts.

They look like mangroves until you wade into them and put a hand out and find cold copper. Red root-mangroves and white wire-mangroves growing side by side, indistinguishable in habit. Prop roots arch down from six feet up, some wood, some 2-inch pipe furred in moss, some a braid of both where a vine has spiraled tight around a conduit and the conduit has sweated into the vine. They hold the banks of the water-alleys that used to be streets. Their seedlings are spears. They drop point-down, stick in slag-mud, split rebar with a slow hydraulic push. You can hear them growing if you put your ear close on a quiet — which never comes — moment: creak, tick, pop. Their leaves are waxy, black-green, big as doors on the giant taros between them. Door-taros. One leaf per plant, held up on a stalk thick as your wrist, shedding rain in sheets off a drip tip. They don't bother much with sun. There isn't enough. The cloud is permanent, noon is amber dusk. So they eat the other light.

That is when you notice the edges glow.

When the transformer huts surge — and they surge every few minutes, crackling blue, drooping cables fizzing where they kiss the flood — the leaf margins brighten faintly green-gold, as if lit from inside. Electrotropes. The roots seek current the way other roots seek water. A fig root will grow *toward* a hum, through concrete, through a bearing housing, and not stop the shaft. It polishes around it. You can see the groove, shiny, wet, the root shouldering the spinning steel without being cut. Current flows root to root as easily as wire to wire. The whole stilt-root labyrinth in a half-acre is one wet electrode, tasting voltage in the flood, passing the signal pipe to pipe. That's why you feel it in your teeth before you hear the pitch change. The jungle is deciding to surge, and your fillings hear it first.

Higher up, where air is almost air, live the stranglers and climbers.

The white trumpets on the guy-wires. A morning-glory that learned to climb lightning. Vines thick as your thigh go up every vertical: chimney, billboard frame, bent rebar, power pole. They flower extravagantly, stupidly, a hundred white horns to no pollinator — and then the rain fills each horn, and the flower nods, spills, rights itself, a slow pump. They are not for bees. They are gutters, cisterns, cooling fins. Their tendrils test everything still. Set your pack on that rare dry slab and in twenty minutes a blind white tip will be tapping the straps, tasting salt and nylon. In an hour you will need a knife. And if you leave the knife, it too will be tested.

The figs are slower and final. A strangler fig finds a pump-house, a boiler house, a generator on its pad, and hugs it. Roots go in the blown door, out the cracked flue, around the flywheel with clearance to spare. Leaves roof it. In a month the machine is the heartwood of the tree. And the heart still beats. Impossible fireboxes still lit orange *underwater*, sending steam columns straight up through the canopy to punch holes in the rain-wall. A fig-swallowed generator hums — you can put your palm on the bark and feel the 60-cycle shudder. The tree keeps it alive because the tree needs it warm. Seeds germinate faster in the hot-slick outflow. Seedlings crowd the discharge channel where water goes almost hot, broth-thick with oil and fallen petals no animal will ever sip. Warmth is light here.

And so each engine is an animal that plants keep.

Because down inside, scattered from above, up close they are not scattered. They are spaced. Like territories. Every half-acre a heart.

A diesel pump station sucks brown water in one side and vomits it hot out the other for no reason except circulation — which is reason enough when water, rain, sweat, sap, steam, condensate are all one body. It is a heart moving blood that is also air that is also drink. A line of compressors along old Main Street chuffing in sequence is a ribcage. Boiler houses breathing steam, relief valves lifting at random with clank-and-hiss, steam whistles starting backward — those are lungs clearing. Pipes, eight-inch iron arteries running just under the surface, surfacing to leap a roof, diving again, sweating, hammering, are gut and nerve both. Valves turn by themselves because root-knots have grown through the handwheels and swell and shrink with wetness, tightening, loosening. Gauges tremble because pressure is speech. A whistle you hear may have blown an hour ago on the far side, relayed pipe to pipe, root to root.

Nothing has a brain. Everything has a pulse.

The young rock makes this possible. Black basalt and poured slag still edged, still glassy, concrete slabs stood on end with white unworn fractures, fresh rust flaking in bright peels. Sharp, mineral, uneaten. Pioneer things love it. Razor ferns split around bristling rebar, using the unrusted tips as stakes, sharpening their own spore edges on slag glass. Velvet moss and liverwort slick every vertical inch within days of exposure. On your left a new island pushes up steaming — slag and root-mat heaved by gas or heat — and while you watch, pale sprouts crown it, darken as you stare. Blind, fast, hungry for iron. On your right a wall sighs and slumps because vines swelled in the mortar. Destruction and colonization are the same gesture.

They move at animal speed, just plant-slow made fast by heat. Leaves fall before they fall — you will see the ripple in a still pool a full breath before the leaf lets go above it, because the air is so saturated the intention arrives in the water first. Pipe shadows lag three feet behind pipes when transformers surge, because the charge holds the shadow a second too long. Growth is mandatory and fast. Stillness is death, which means being grown over.

At dimming — there is no dusk, only a dimming of the dim — they all talk at once. Electric huts strobe blue through mist, leaf edges answer gold-green, steam turns pink-grey, metal contracts with rifle-crack reports as cooling air tightens the water. Rain on ten thousand drum-leaves, water slapping water, diesel throb in your sternum, boiler scream, pods detonating, trunks splitting bark, lilies unfolding with a wet rip you have to open your mouth to equalize for. Your ears fill. Your jawbone carries it.

None of it is for you. No frog sings for the seedling nosing up through the crack beside your hand on the rooftop where you try to sleep. No hand will pick it. Tar paper warm under you, sharp aggregate biting through, moss already finding the seams of your groundsheet. Below, the street-river runs brown-green carrying leaves, flowers, a spinning door, an intact window with vines for curtains. A pump two roofs over hammers, misses, hammers again.

The pale shoot trembles in the vibration. It opens its first leaf to the dim. It tastes rain, rust, oil, current, your sweat.

It does not care that you are watching. It was already living here before you decided what living was.
