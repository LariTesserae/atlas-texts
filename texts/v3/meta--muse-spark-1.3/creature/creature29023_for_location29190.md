They do not live in the center.

That is the first thing to understand about them, because everything else curves around it. At first you think nothing lives here at all. The noise takes up too much room. The green takes up the walls. You stand on the gantry and see only leaves sweating, chains swinging, empty air holding its shape.

Then the edge moves.

A leaf underside blinks and is not a leaf. A shadow on a warm duct lifts its head. A handwheel you thought was vibrating turns, stops, turns back — because a hand turned it.

They live pressed thin against the machine, all of them. They are rim-animals. Edge-things.

Highest and fastest are the orbiters. You hear them before you focus them: white-collared swifts, soot-sparrows, a hundred screaming throats bounced off tile until the scream has no source. They do not fly so much as they are flown, taken up by the engine-heat rising off the compressors and carried round and round the void. Watch one long enough and you see the rule written in its body. It rides the updraft up the west wall, peels off at the crown, glides down the east, catches again. A perfect circle. Never a chord across. Never a dart through the middle, even when a moth hangs fat and stupid in that clear column. They would rather go the long way around for a mouthful than cut across. Their nests give them away as natives: wads of moss and moth-wing and stripped wire insulation wedged into junction boxes, into lamp cages, into the knuckle of a pipe elbow where it is always warm. The chicks hatch staring at green and iron and have never seen sky except as slats. The adults have forgotten, if they ever knew, that a sky can be crossed. Here the sky is a wall you circle.

Under them, by day, the other winged ones wait. Cloud-moths in their thousands. By daylight they are not flying things but texture — pale as steam, plastered flat to the undersides of fig leaves and fern fronds, wings folded like closed eyelids, drinking cold through their feet. They are the color of condensation itself, and that is their trick. Then the louvers grind open and the true cloud shoulders in, white and blind and cold, and the whole wall shivers awake. The moths rise all at once, not in a flutter but in a lifted sheet, and drink the fog mid-air. You can hear, if you press your ear close to a duct where an orchid clamps, the faint tick of their tongues. They are the garden's blood-bearers. The soot-sparrows take them in beakfuls. What is left pollinates the duct-orchids — those hot, waxy flowers the color of warning lights — moving pollen from one sweating copper run to the next on steam-pale wings. Eat and be eaten and carry seed-dust between. That is their whole theology.

Lower, slower, hotter.

The copper lizards. They are not copper, exactly, but after generations basking on the bus-bar conduits and the warm ducts they have taken on the sheen of them, belly-scales burnished verdigris-green, backs dark as oiled iron. They do not hurry. Hurry would kill them here; there is nowhere cool to run to. They stake out territories measured in hand-spans: this bend of pipe from 10:00 to 14:00, that transformer top until the vibration shakes them off for grooming hour. They flatten themselves to drink heat through their chins and watch the moths with one amber eye, too slow for swifts and content to be slow. When a drip lands on their snout they lick it sideways, unblinking.

Under that, in the grease and the dark, the cleaners. Roaches the size of a thumb joint, mahogany-black, working the grease traps around the compressor houses with the patience of monks, antennae combing oil from water. Mosquito larvae in every drip pan, flick-flick-flicking in water no deeper than a thimble, living their whole furious lives between one tic and the next drip. In the sump shadow, where the floor cambers down and the standing water never quite drains, rats — big, wet, green-shouldered from algae — swim short determined laps between the drowned wrench-reef and the root-mat, eating newts and newt eggs and each other’s tails when the moth-fall is thin. And the newts themselves, orange-bellied, gilled and unbothered, splayed on submerged ceramic like small gods in a bathtub universe.

And the rooted ones, who are not backdrop. They are inhabitants quite as much as the rats. The moss that lays itself an inch thick only on the cold-water pipes, nowhere else, because it has learned where the drink sweats first. The black-green liverworts that caulk the seams between wall plates better than any gasket the builders poured. Ferns that choose bolt-holes to be born from, one frond per hole, unfurling like green question marks, sporulating into the fan-wash. Figs — pale strangler figs — that send roots down twenty meters along cable runs, finger-thick, white as tendon, until they find a drip and then swell and grip, and over a decade gently crush the pipe they loved and force the water six meters left where a new garden will start. The vines that have learned industry: horizontal along conduit, vertical along downspout, diagonal along bracing, repeating the machine back to itself in green geometry until you cannot tell which came first, the pipe or the stem that shadows it. Where a panel fell away decades ago they have knitted a replacement out of root. They drink first. That is the law. Condensation beads heaviest not on cold steel but on living undersides, as if leaves coax water out of air by wanting it. The machines drink secondhand, from leaf-drip off a tip.

And then the tenders. The ones you will mistake for people, for a second, and then never again.

Short-coated high-altitude macaques, a troop of eleven, twelve in a good year. Their coats are thin because winter never comes inside; their fingertips are oil-dark to the second knuckle and never wash clean. They do not chatter much — you cannot chatter in this roar, you would tear your throat. They sign with eyebrows, with shifts of weight on a railing, with the back of a hand held under a drip.

Watch the old female. She is mostly grey around the muzzle, one ear notched from a fan-belt years ago. Her morning circuit is the base ring: six compressor houses, fourteen drip pans. At each pan she pauses, holds the back of her hand in the fall of drops to gauge the rate, and tips the pan a finger’s width with her thumb. Not to fix it. To tune it. A little more to the moss here, a little less to the rust there. She does not look at gauges. Gauges are for eyes, and eyes fog.

A juvenile rides the great screw housing — that thirty-meter rust-darkened spiral lifting mist one bucket at a time, one revolution a day — clinging to the inspection port with feet and tail, picking moss out of the seam with quick black fingers and eating some and placing some deliberately on a bare patch higher up. Gardening without a word for it.

Another, a young male with a scarred lip, does the handwheels. Quarter-turn, head cocked to listen through bone to the change in hum, quarter-turn back. Not imitation of humans. His grandmother did this. Her mother did this before the last human boot sounded on the service stair. It is not memory. It is ecology. To tend is to eat: because they prune, the figs fruit; because they clear drip lines, the moths have water; because the moths have water, the orchids bloom; because the orchids bloom on warm ducts, the ducts stay cleared and warm; because the ducts stay warm, the transformers stay warm where they nap in a pile at midday, bellies to hot ceramic, limbs draped over flowering vine, grooming each other with long, deaf, thorough strokes while swifts scream around them and the loose chain beats time somewhere and the whole cylinder roars through their sternums.

They watch you with total indifference. You are not predator, not mate, not drip. You are weather that came up the stairs.

If you stay long enough to stop listening for quiet, you begin to see what holds them all together. It is not the walls. It is the empty middle they all refuse.

Throw a leaf, and the updraft takes it up and flings it sideways. Drop fluff and it circles. Even dust and spores and moth scales take the long way around that faintly shimmering column of clearer air where the din thins just enough to hear your blood. Nothing nests there. Nothing roosts there. Nothing crosses. The garden presses in, leaf after leaf reaching, but never fills.

It lives there by not living there — the hollow keeps them at the rim, keeps them circling, keeps moth to orchid to sparrow to lizard to macaque to fig to pipe and back around. It is the still point that makes them orbit, and orbiting makes them meet each other over and over: swift eats moth above the same duct where lizard basks where macaque grooms where vine flowers.

That is the animal. Not the macaque alone, not the swift. The slow factory that turned into a body. Iron for bones, condensation for blood, compressors for a low molar heart that never stops because if it stopped the night at four thousand meters would come inside and kill the green. Verdigris for skin. Moss for fur. The troop for hands that prune and tune. The wings for lungs, stirring the warm air round and round.

Small enough to walk end to end in three minutes if you could walk straight, and you never can, because nothing goes straight here. Everything goes around.
