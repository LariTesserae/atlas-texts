Nothing that croaks lives here.

That is the first thing to unlearn. The frogs — copper throats, lemon throats, thousands of them plastered to the rope-glass and calling until the air itself buzzes — they walked up. They climbed a thousand meters out of the cloud forest below because it is warm up here and the midges are thick. When the mist thins at the end of the season they will go back down, thinner. The newts gorging in the sky-pools, the flightless crickets scraping on the warm slabs, the swifts and swallows and that big fork-tail that never lands but shears frogs right off the rock in mid-dive — all visitors. Commuters. Even the midges are blown up on the evening tongue of mist and die up here in clouds.

Even the green is visiting. That acid-bright moss in the cracks, the orange fan-bacteria around the hot pools, the single blades of grass, the low fleshy thing with clear leaves that holds a bead of water in each leaf like it is offering it to someone — they are holding a territory of emptiness around themselves because there is no soil yet to argue over. They will be buried when the next sheet comes up. Survey stakes lean because the ground breathes upward a finger's width a month. Put your palm down and you feel it. Tick. Pressure. Warm through rubber.

So what actually lives here? At first you think: the needles.

There are seven of them. Three, four meters tall, leaning a little wrong, like milk teeth coming in crooked. Matte black that eats your headlamp. No base, no seam. They just go down into the fresh basalt and keep going. When the mist is thickest, which is every twenty minutes when it pours over the east rim, faint amber lines crawl up them from base to tip, slow as sap. And they hum. You feel it in your sternum first, a low chord that never resolves, with a high shimmer laid over it that makes your fillings ache.

You think they are machines. Someone seeded them. The tent at the rim is collapsed, the solar panel milky, the instrument box rusted. Someone left a hand-painted sign: DRINK SECOND WATER ONLY. Let it fall, let it sit, let it cool on stone. Don't drink what hangs.

Then you stand long enough among them and you stop thinking machine. Machines don't make the air wetter around themselves. Around each needle there is a halo you can see if you don't look straight at it — air gone thick, gone beaded. Droplets that should fall don't. Some rise. Out of the blood-warm pools, slow as dust in a sunbeam, beads lift and stop midair and tremble. If you put your forearm out, mist condenses on the hairs in seconds and runs. If you hold your breath and stand very still inside a halo, the hum gets louder inside your chest and the high shimmer seems to tune itself, searching.

Don't settle there. They are not machines and they are not animals and they are not plants. Let them become more themselves.

Stay until full dark, when the peaks go black and the stars come hard and the amber in the needles brightens. That is when you see what the halo is for.

Every suspended bead catches that amber and holds it a half-second too long.

Lean in close — closer than the sign wants you to — with your light off. Let your eyes go. The beads around a needle at night are not empty. Each one has a center. A speck, a comma, a coiled thread no longer than an eyelash, turning very slowly to face the needle. They tremble because they are swimming. The droplet is their skin. Surface tension is their body.

First water. That's what hangs. Second water is what has fallen and sat and gone dead on cold stone.

First water is alive.

There are thousands of them per cubic meter in a halo, millions in the bowl on a thick night. Born — if born is the word — down in the warm pools where the orange bacteria rim thickest. You can see the nurseries if you know to look: in the hollows between pillows where the water shivers with each drip, tiny clear bells pulse just under the surface, no bigger than midge eyes. They feed on dissolved iron and sulfur and on drowned midges, on frog slime and bird shit aerosolized and re-condensed. When they are buoyant enough they lift. Vapor lifts them, heat lifts them, and then the hum holds them.

The hum is husbandry. The low chord is pressure, a standing wave that holds a bead steady against gravity. You can feel it as nausea when it cycles low in the early morning — that moment when the tone drops out from under you and your stomach drops — and then the whole bowl lets go at once. A soft patter, bowl-wide, every suspended droplet falling. The frogs go silent for one breath, startled. Then louder. That morning rain is not weather. It is the needles exhaling. Resetting. Culling the ones that didn't ripen, feeding the pools.

And the high shimmer — the one in your teeth — that is closer to a voice. Or a hand. In daylight you can watch a bead drift sideways against the wind, toward amber, stop, brighten. At night they pulse in answer to the crawling lines. Needle to droplet to needle. Seven dim lanterns talking to their flock in light and ultrasound.

This is why the fleshy pioneer holds its bead up like an offering. Look inside those leaf-cups with a loupe: the same comma, coiled, waiting. The moss cushions at the crack-edges do it too, wicking seep up to present it. The plants here learned fast — or were taught — to nursery them in exchange for nitrogen, for warmth, for whatever leaks out of a halo.

And the frogs. Their throats like lit lanterns. You thought it was mating color. Put your ear close on a thick night when the bowl glows from within its own mist — seven lanterns and a thousand frog-throats and the sky-pools reflecting — and you hear that the frog call and the needle hum are not two sounds. The frogs time their trill to ride the shimmer. They eat the fallen ones by the tongueful with every midge. Their guts, their skin, their vocal sacs fill with living first water. Copper and lemon and vermilion — newt bellies too — that glow is not entirely theirs.

They will carry it down. That's the point. Nothing is born here yet except this, and this cannot walk. So it calls walkers. Warmth and water and midges to pull the forest up the mountain. A free bathhouse in the cold. Gorge, get coated, get filled, and when the mist thins go back down with a belly full of commas that will survive in damp leaf litter long enough to find another warm seep. The birds that never land — they snatch a frog in the same dive as a midge, their cries bouncing off the collar so a cry arrives a full second before the bird banks into view — they will carry it farther than any frog can hop. Even you. Breathe the halo long enough, drink unfallen water, and you will carry a small warm trembling in your sinuses, in your gut, for days. Hence the sign.

Under all of it, the larger one. Because the seven are not seven. Put your palm flat on the cinder between them and feel that faint upward pressure, feel the warm spots migrate overnight — a bath-warm pool cold by morning, a new steam vent sighing open ten meters off, smelling of iron and hot stone. The needles lean because something underneath shifts them. They are not rooted in rock. The rock is rooted in it.

The bowl is not a valley. It is a thin place. Black glass, froth, rope and pillow still sharp enough to cut boots — that is skin that hasn't scarred yet. The needles are where it is thinnest, where it pushes cilia up to breathe and sing and farm the air. Teeth, yes, but baby teeth. Provisional.

It is embryonic. Not eroded but extruded. It doesn't yet know if it will be a mountain or a caldera lake or a meadow with a stream that has learned downhill. Its permanent residents don't yet know if they will be animal or weather or light. Right now they are all three: a distributed, hanging animal made of water and sound, tended by seven black fingers, fed by visitors, waiting for soil.

Stand in the dark while the rock ticks as it contracts and the swifts keep crying without landing and the suspended water holds amber a half-second too long around you on all sides, and you understand you are not in a place that is inhabited.

You are in something inhabited that is still learning how to be a place.
