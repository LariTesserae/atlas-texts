No one meets you. That's the first inhabitant: the absence.

You keep listening for people because the place is laid for people — doorways at shoulder height, bench for two legs, coat still on its nail in the dye-works — and the listening comes back empty. No voices, no footsteps but your own squelching and then knocking on the cobble underneath. If there was ever a caretaker, he has thinned out. His coat has grown into the doorframe, moss seaming the wool to the wood. His mug on the boiler has soil in it now, and stitchwort coming up through the handle. He isn't coming back for them.

What lives here instead doesn't come to meet you either. You have to kneel down to it.

Lowest and largest is the mattress. That's what you walked in on and didn't recognize as a who. Moss, cropped fern, stitchwort, clover — ankle-deep, sometimes knee-deep — covering every horizontal like a pelt. It isn't decoration on Brindle. It *is* Brindle, the citizen that holds office everywhere at once. Pull it off a wall and by spring the wall will be down; the brick here learned a hundred winters ago to stop arguing and let itself be sewn together. Under your boots it gives, drinks your footprint and then slowly forgets you. Press your palm into it in the evening, when it has drunk all day from the air, and it exhales that faint sweet iron-and-chlorophyll smell. If you lift a cobble at the lane edge you see what holds it all up from below: mycelium white and thick as rope, running seam to seam. The town's real mortar.

Out of that mattress, the others stand up just far enough to be counted as individuals, because Brindle insists on sparseness. Nothing crowds.

First, the tracers. Ivy that does not climb at random. It goes where the town goes, following mortar lines and conduit until from a distance you can read the old electrical diagram in green wire. Nettles that know heat better than any thermometer — massed thickest in winter where the underground steam main still leaks a degree or two, thinning where the ground goes truly cold. Ferns that in a mist-rain week unroll six inches at a time, sporangia popping almost inaudibly in the afternoon when the cloud thins. Stitchwort that whites out a lane edge in days and then waits. Clover holding the gaps between cobble-knuckles. And everywhere the young birch suckers that never get to become birch — trying every year to close the hundred-foot fern-seas between a chimney and its answering shed, and every winter thinned back by damp and shade. They are the town's failed ambition to fill itself in. Brindle breathes in, breathes out, never quite filling.

Then the overnighters, who are only sometimes there. After the five-minute mist that beads and doesn't fall, slime molds climb the north side of the dye vats in orange fans overnight and are gone by noon. You have to be lucky, or slow, to catch them. Springtails if you kneel and look. Worms, of course, turning the soft ground under the green.

And then, rarely enough to count as weather, the ones with eyes.

A wren, once, scolding from inside a boiler flue — using iron as a hollow tree. A black rat crossing a lane with great deliberation, leaving a dark green wake in the dew behind it. Moths around the day-burning lamps, grey as the light itself, bumping glass that isn't there anymore. They don't live here the way the moss lives here. They pass through, make one sharp note and are gone. The lamps don't need them. The lamps burn pale orange in full daylight anyway, useless and persistent.

Because the other half of the population is warm.

You felt it before you named it: touch the living pipes as you pass and they are blood-warm in a cool world, beaded with condensation that never quite drips. That's how you tell who is alive in iron. Cold and still belongs to lichen within a year — grey-green, dead. Warm or moving stays iron — black-oiled and rust-red.

They move slowly, the way plants move quickly. In the mill the line-shaft still turns. The big flywheel outside, twelve feet across and furred in velvet moss on its sheltered side, rotates once every nine seconds. It drives nothing anyone needs anymore, except it drives the ventilators, and the ventilators drive the air, and the dynamo in the substation still puts out enough to keep those lamps on. Stand still — which you will, because the town makes you quiet — and you hear them as animals, not machines: the low woolly hum through brick, the *tss-tss* of the loom still weaving a weft of nothing because no one told it to stop, the sigh and clank as the substation regulates itself, the long slow creak of a wire grown heavy with vine, tightening, loosening.

Roots have learned the warm routes around bearings and avoid them. That's the negotiation. The plants are not wrecking the works. They're farming its warmth.

So this is what lives in Brindle, when you stay long enough to stop looking for a crowd:

A green body that holds the brick together better than mortar, threaded underneath with white rope, breathing in and out but never closing.

A set of green nerves — ivy, nettle, root — that follow heat and current and trace where the town is still alive.

A few small, fast visitors with feathers and fur and dust on their wings, who make the emptiness audible for a second.

And a herd of large, slow, warm iron animals turning under blankets of moss, breathing slowly under quilts, tended by roots that know exactly where the heat is.

*Plik... plik... plik* — condensate from a hundred feet of overhead pipe gathering and falling into moss that drinks it instantly. The flywheel turns. The light stays the color of skimmed milk. Nothing floats, nothing glows. It is only just unusual: a soft, sparse, cool, quiet thing that was meant to be hard and dense and hot and loud, and kept its engines turning anyway.
