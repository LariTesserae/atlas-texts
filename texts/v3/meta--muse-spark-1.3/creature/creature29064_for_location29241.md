At first you think they are just people, crowded.

A hundred souls shoulder to shoulder in lamplight, damp to the bone, shouting a little too loud. That’s what you see. Then you stay an hour, two hours, and your jacket never dries and your ears start to ring, and they stop being just people. They become what the egg has made.

They are not pale in the way of stories. They are weathered the way wet stone is weathered. Skin gone soft and grey-white from never fully drying, fingertips permanently ridged and puckered as if they’d just come out of a bath hours ago and forgot to come out. Hair lank and clinging, blackened at the ends by tallow smoke, tied back with sinew or cropped short to keep it out of drips. Eyes that have learned late-evening. They don’t have big blind cave-eyes; they have narrow, patient eyes that resolve a face at five paces and don’t bother beyond ten, because beyond ten there is only gloaming and mist. They look at mouths as much as eyes. Over the drum-roar of the ceiling you have to push to be heard, so the old ones lip-read and the young ones learn to enunciate with their whole face, and everyone has that slightly raised, listening tilt of the head.

You learn them by posture first. Nobody stands tall on the middle terrace. You can’t — you’d put your head straight into the smoke. It hangs about head-height over the water, blue-grey, eye-stinging, never thickening, never clearing. So they have become a stooping people. A kind of glide-crouch. Knees soft, neck bent, shoulders rolled forward under perpetually darkened jackets and hides. Downhill sidles, uphill presses flat, water-carriers always have the right of way — it isn’t politeness, it’s anatomy. After a generation or two your joints learn the ledge. The steady cool gets into them — not cave-cold, but autumn-shade cool that never lifts — and stays there. They move deliberately, no wasted splash. An old woman with a stone jar can go from seep to house along a six-inch ledge with a rope handrail in the dark without spilling, while you are still clutching the rope with both hands.

Listen to them work and you hear who they are.

The knapper in the doorway, squatting on his heels, a chert nodule between his knees, working it down with an antler tine, tick tick tick, glass chips flying. His hands are a map of old cuts. Beside him a woman cold-hammers native copper — dull pink blebs picked out of the limestone — tap tap tap on a flat anvil stone, into a fish-hook, an awl, a needle, no fire but her patience. Further up, shave shave shave of hide on a frame. Cordage-twisting never stops: hair and sinew and the long pale fibers dredged from the pool rolled on thighs into line, into nets, into bridge-cable. Everything small, mended, mended again. A stone-carved bowl with three generations of drill-holes laced with copper wire along a crack. A sleeping pallet restuffed until the hide is thin as paper. They husband things the way they husband the pool.

Because they are, all of them, maintenance people.

The old women are the clearest of it. Each dawn — though there is no dawn, only a paling of that bruised grey disc in the throat — one of them takes the marked stick down to the black water and dips it and announces what everyone already knows: still even. Seep equals drink equals evaporation. In equals out. It has sat exactly here since their grandmothers’ grandmothers, between the pale bathtub ring above and the dark stain below, refusing to remember a drip. And the town takes its theology from that. A wall slumps in the night, and by morning it is rebuilt stone for stone, same lean, same shape. A fat-fire guts a one-room house — brief and terrible in such press — and it is rebuilt on the same footprint, with the salvaged lintel, the same hanging hook left of the door. Children don’t learn streets. They learn seeps: that flute will fill your jar by nightfall, that one never will, that overhang always fattens then drops on the left side. Nothing is improved. Everything is kept.

That makes them intimate past comfort and discreet past belief. Neighbors who can hear each other swallow have learned a kind of negotiated deafness. You don’t comment on a cough in the night, a child crying, a couple arguing in low talk bounced back half a second later slightly larger off the dome. You press flat and let pass. Privacy isn’t a wall — there are no walls thick enough in swallow-nest houses where the roof of one is the floor of the next — privacy is inattention, practiced kindly. What is shared is shared absolutely: the water, the ladders of black-greased bone and salvaged driftwood, the plaited bridges over the narrowest water, the pile platforms over the pool itself where work and gutting get done.

Their dead are not gone. There’s nowhere to put them. Laid in a high niche wrapped in hide, left until the damp has done its slow work, then the niche is emptied, the bones washed in pool water and built — literally built — into a windbreak or a step. You will step on an ancestor going to borrow salt and not think it strange. It’s just keeping the egg whole.

They are not self-sufficient and they know it. Most food comes down in sacks on the long rope — grain, dried meat — traded for what they make that the surface wants: pressure-flaked chert blades still better than steel for some fine work, cold-hammered copper hooks and awls, and the particular white salt that blooms in the upper solution cups. A few climb out, up on backside and braced boots through ten meters of rain-a-month-ago dark, out onto the moor with sheep and wind and heather they will never bring down here. They do the talking, the hauling coil by coil of withies for hide-pots, of tallow, of frayed hide for wicks. They come back down damp for days.

And with them, around them, under their hands, the other residents — the true natives, who need nothing from above.

In the pool, if you sit very still on a pile platform and let your stone bowl of tallow gutter down to almost nothing, you’ll see them hang: blind white fish, two inches long, eyeless, only pits where eyes should be, motionless then darting to take a fallen drip. The people eat them sparingly, more as rite than sustenance. To take too many would be to break the accounting.

Along the waterline at night, deliberate as monks, come the crabs the color of candle wax, picking. On the ceiling curtains, impossibly leggy, feels its way the pale cricket. That’s the census. No birds, no rats, no green, not even algae. Only, if you scrape a nail in the dimmer corners, a pale translucent slime — not plant, not animal, something simpler that lives on stone-breath alone — and the rust-red films of iron bacteria slicking the submerged ledges, and honey calcite growing a molecule at a time.

The people fit among those as if they were also a film on rock. Warm bodies pressed together to sleep in cool air that never warms, smelling of wet limestone and tallow and crowded sleep, tending their cups of light that make more smoke than light and have to be coaxed constantly in the damp. A child born here learns within weeks not to cry at the drip-roar. A newcomer puts hands over ears at midday-work-roar — hammering and splashing and talk thrown up and around and back — and the old ones don’t notice.

They are not stuck. That’s the thing you feel in your teeth before you can name it. A stuck people would dream of leaving or expanding. These people have learned the size of the world — sixty paces from water to crown, perhaps less, holdable entire in the mind — and decided to keep it exactly.
