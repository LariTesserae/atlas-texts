At first you don't see them separate from it.

That's the thing. You come down expecting people in a forest and what you get is movement in the green that resolves, slowly, into people. A hand where you thought it was a branch. A face under a leaf-eave where you thought it was shadow. A child's legs dangling off a platform twenty feet up, kicking, bare and mud-caked to the knee, swinging in time to the drip.

Then more of them, and more, until you realize the village is crawling with them in the way a log is crawling with ants — not infested, just fully occupied. They live thick here.

They are short, most of them. Not dwarfish, just compacted by generations of low ceilings and low branches and crouching under leaf-roofs. Broad through the chest and shoulders — you need bellows up here in the thin air, and you need arms. Everyone climbs. Old women with grey plaits tucked up under bark-cloth caps go up ladders hand-over-hand with baskets on their backs like it's level ground. Toddlers learn the rope-bridges before they can talk in sentences, and they cross them low and quick, hands on the side-vines, feet sure on the twin poles gone slick and black with use. Their feet are the first thing you notice up close: wide, splayed, hard as rootwood on the soles, toes that grip. Shoes are a dry-weather idea. Here it's bare feet or soft bark sandals lashed on for the duckboards, taken off again to climb.

Their skin is pale under the weather. Under canopy you don't tan, you freckle and then the freckles wash out in the damp. Cheeks stay red with cold, noses run, knuckles are chapped and split and then healed over with pitch and fat. Everything about them is a little waterlogged. Hair, black or rust-brown, hangs lank no matter how it's tied — topknots, side-knots, long plaits full of caught leaves and moss spores. You can tell a weaver by the green under her nails that never scrubs out. You can tell a pitch-man by the black in the creases of his hands. Children are perpetually sniffling, wiping on sleeves, sleeves perpetually damp. Old people move stiff in the morning until the midday almost-warmth loosens them, and they all have the low, wet cough from sleeping under smoke that never quite leaves the crown. They cough and keep working.

They dress for wet that never ends. Undyed wool felted thick and then fulled again until it will shed — for a while — a direct drip. Tunics and leggings and big shapeless cloaks of bark-fiber over wool, dark brown and grey as trunks, except where someone has felted in a bit of madder or a child's cap dyed bright red so you can spot him in the canopy. Nothing ever fully dries. Hems are heavy. You learn to wring your cloak before you go inside someone's platform, and to hang it on the designated peg under the eave where forty other cloaks are already steaming faintly. Nakedness underneath doesn't mean much; bodies are practical things when you live stacked like honeycomb and bathe in the rills together.

Because they are stacked. You can't understand them without the closeness. Thirty families in shouting distance means no one has a private sound. You hear your neighbors piss in the morning trough, you hear the baby next platform over dreaming, you hear who is arguing about kindling and who made up after. So they have developed a kind of cheerful, quarrelsome permeability. Privacy is a cloak pulled over the head, a turned back on a bridge, an agreement not to comment. Property is what you can keep dry and covered. Everything else circulates.

Watch for ten minutes on the root-plait plaza and you see the circulation: the girl with the basket of fat white grubs dug from a deadfall, trading handful by handful for dry splits of pine kept three seasons under a leaf-roof — dry being wealth here, true currency, because anyone can get wet wood. The two boys hauling a new pole, shouting *mind! mind!* and everyone leaning out of the way without breaking conversation. The old man on his back under the vine bridge, re-lashing with nettle cord, his mouth full of cord, while traffic steps over his hands and a goat watches from above with huge interest and tries to eat his beard. The mother gutting minnows — tiny black torpedoes from the outflow pool — on a flat stone, flicking heads to the cats and the jays who have learned exactly how far to sit. The woman mending wall, and this stays with you: she doesn't patch it, she *plants* it. She takes a green hazel withy, weaves it in and out of the living wattle, ties it with bark strip, bends the tip to where she wants next spring's growth to go. The wall has leaves. The wall will heal around her work.

That is their religion, if you want a word for it. You do not cut a pillar here. Everyone will tell you that, child to stranger, as a fact like water runs downhill. If a trunk is where a post should be, you leave it and lash to it. If a trunk is where a door should be, you move the door. It isn't reverence performed on holy days; it's carpentry. Their adzes are stone, their awls are bone, their cord is flax and nettle and pitch — you can fell a sapling, you can coppice willow, but you don't take a load-bearing elder of the bowl. To do so would be stupid before it would be wicked; the whole lashed-on, tied-up, hooked-over village hangs from those ancients. But it has become wicked too, over the generations. You hear them talk to the big trunks the way you talk to an old landlord — grumbling, affectionate, familiar. *Ah, she's weeping again, give her another channel.* *He's dropped a limb on my roof-leaf, I'll have to train a new one over.*

And they are not alone in the landlord's house. That is the second thing you learn after the closeness: the people are only the loudest animals.

They make room as a principle. The chickens — scrawny, loud, wet-feathered — have right of way on the duckboards. You step over, not on. The pigeons that nest in the hollow dead snags are half-tame and wholly expected; a boy will climb thirty feet to take half the squabs and leave half, counting on his fingers, because that's the arrangement. The bees in the log hives by the taro terraces are spoken to in the morning. *Stay, work, the beans are flowering.* The red squirrels use the ropeways and scold humans who are slow. The frogs — thumb-sized, fist-sized, black and green and orange-bellied — are everywhere water stands, which is everywhere, and no one dreams of moving them from the washing rills; you wash around the tadpoles. Leeches get picked off ankles with a practiced thumb-flick and a curse, not horror. Mice and voles in the grain baskets are trapped, eaten, complaint about, accepted.

Even the monkeys. Every afternoon the small grey troop comes down from the high crown, ten or twelve strong, to work the upper village. They steal. Of course they steal — a cold bean cake left a second too long, a pigeon egg. The dogs go mad. The children shriek with delight and throw sticks badly on purpose. The adults shout, wave, and leave a few cress tops on the far roof-leaf, which is tribute and bribery and also, if you're honest, affection. An old female with a torn ear will sit on a roof-ridge not six feet from a grandmother shelling beans and groom herself, and the grandmother will groom beans, and they will scold each other in parallel monologue. You make room. Keeping anything out in a place this alive is wasted work.

What holds them together, more than kin — though everyone is cousin to everyone by three routes — is maintenance. Nothing here stays built. Living walls outgrow their lashings, lashings rot, leaf-roofs get shredded by monkeys and wind and need re-training, rills clog with leaves and need clearing with a sod-knife, duckboards sink and need re-laying, terraces slump and need re-mucking. So there is always work, and work is always public, and public work needs feeding. A mending becomes a meal. A re-tying becomes a trade. You help haul because tomorrow you will need hauling. The woman who knows pitch, the man who knows bees, the family that keeps the driest kindling store on the uphill side, the girl with the sure hand for setting a broken squirrel-tail or a broken finger — same thing — they are wealthy in the way that matters. Not in pots — everyone has the same coarse grey pots — but in owed favors, remembered.

If you stand long enough on the plaza where the three paths meet over the main rill, feeling the faint thrum of water under the interlaced roots, you start to pick out individuals from the weave: the red-capped child who is everywhere at once, forbidden to climb the dead snag and climbing it anyway; his mother calling him in three short yips that sound exactly like the jays until you learn the difference; the stone-maul man with one milky eye, *tok tok tok* splitting kindling that will never be quite dry enough; the bean-shelling grandmother with taro mud to her elbows; the pigeon boy whistling through his teeth to call them home. They argue, they laugh wetly, they barter, they drip.

They do not conquer this place. You see that in their bodies, in their clothes, in the way they leave the pillars standing and step over chickens and wash around tadpoles. They ride it. Lashed on, growing, rotting a little at the edges, re-tied, re-woven, damp to the bone and entirely at home in the breathing bowl.
