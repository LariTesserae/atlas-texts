Who lives here lives up and down at once. That is the first thing their bodies know.

At first you think: people. Several thousand of them, that is all. Men waist-deep passing stone hand to hand. Women pounding lake-hemp, thigh-rolling cordage. Children shrieking flattened shrieks across canal-streets. Elders mending nets that never dry.

Then you stay a day, and the image loosens. Then you stay longer and what lives here becomes more itself.

Their skin lives here first. It never quite dries. In the eternal drip from the lofts, in the bath-warm cradle of the lake that holds them too easily up, the outer layer softens and stays soft — fingertips gone permanently ridged and pruned, palms pale and spongelike, cuts sealing slow and white. People’s hands look older than they are and younger than they are at once: wrinkled as fruit in water, uncalloused except where it counts. Because the second thing that lives here is feet.

Soless that have learned young rock. No sandals survive the glass. The aa on the rim, the pillow-lobes below, the causeways just awash — all of it is embryonic, unworn, cutting. So the children who learn to swim before they walk well learn a second walking after: a slow, flat-footed set-down on new basalt, soles thickening to hide. By seven a child can cross a slag zigzag without flinching that would slice a lowlander open. You can hear them coming by the slap and the careful drag. Up top, the air is too thin for running anyway. No one runs here. Lungs are for saving.

That is the third thing: breath.

Everyone here is, by lowland standards, a diver. Not by sport. By necessity. Food grows down. The past is down. The ribbon-weed forests twenty feet tall in the shallows must be thinned and trained and harvested with eyes open in bottle-green gloom. The hanging gardens — mussel-shell and bone lines seeded with edible weed, snail-ropes fat with farmed snails — must be tended arm’s length under. The drowned rooms, a generation below, must be visited to pry loose a good post, to leave an offering. So chests are large, breaths are slow. You see a girl of nine slide off a living frond-walkway without splash, disappear into tea-dark, and not come up for a count you have to stop counting. When she comes up she brings a fist of tubers and she does not gasp. Gasping is thin-air behavior. Here they surface like eels surface, with just the nose and mouth breaking the meniscus, exhaling steam into steam.

Their eyes have learned the light. The sun is a rumor, midday is evening, ten paces is far. So no one looks far. Navigation lives in palm and ankle and memory. They feel the too-buoyant push of the water — that wrong held-up-ness, as if the lake were slightly reluctant to let you go — and read by it where the warm upwellings are. Downwarm, they say, for the vents where you must not stand long, where the floor throbs through the sole. Upsun, for the kilns on the high eastern rim where fire is allowed to live. They do not say north. North would require a horizon and there is only vapor lid glowing amber-green. Their pupils stay a little wide always. In the dim the whites look greener. Old gatherers develop a milky film they claim helps them see under; whether it does or not, they go down blind and come up certain.

Socially, they are reef.

No family lives alone because nothing can live alone in water that rises a fingernail a year. The platforms are lashed together, two and three high, roof against garden against neighbor’s gourd-raft. A child climbs from sleeping mat to roof-fern to aunt’s lily-bed without touching open water. Sleep is shallow and communal by design: lofts just above the condensation drip, everyone within hearing of water lapping the posts below. That lap is the night-watch. If the tone changes the lake has risen. Every generation lays another course of hand-piled black stone on the causeways and abandons the lowest rooms to eels and lilies. You swim down and see them intact, green-furred: old doorways, drowned hearths, a toy coracle still tied. Nothing is thrown away because there is nowhere away to throw it to. Everything compacts.

Even the dead stay.

That is who also lives here, and you learn not to flinch at it. Drop an adze and it falls too slowly, as if considering. The dead, weighted properly with stones, still often return after three days, buoyed absurdly up through the warm density. So they are not given to the deep. They are housed. Set into the causeways with lime and shell, skull to stone, ribs to rubble, they become structure. The zigzag paths you walk just awash are pale in places, knuckled with bone — femur, patella, the dome of a cranium mossed over, barnacled with snail eggs. Children play knucklebones on grandmothers. No one finds this morbid. It is engineering. In a place with no soil except what people have made, with no quarry except the still-ticking floor, the ancestors are the only seasoned stone.

Work divides the living the way water stratifies.

The Builders, mostly men, mostly young, stand waist-deep in lines in the rain that never stops, passing pillow-stones that still tick with cooling heat. A meter a year a causeway grows. Their shoulders are broad, their lower legs always wrinkled white, their backs mapped with fern-spore and lily-pollen.

The Gardeners, mostly women, mostly incessant, tend the up and the down at once: thatch-roofs that are always damp and always sprouting orchids and mosses, and below them the weed-lines and snail-ropes and sapling fish-traps staked into still-soft lava. They have shell scrapers tied to wrists with hemp, copper slivers — those few treasured traded blades sharpened to nothing — kept in oilskin at the throat. Their forearms are fine-cut with ribbon-weed. They ferment in gourds sunk in lake-heat, they pound fiber with grooved stones until the sound flattens across water. They know which flowers live a single day — the white waxy shields of the giant lilies — and pick them before noon-dusk.

The Hearth-Keepers, old, keep fire like an animal. Fire lives only on the high rim kilns and on clay platforms covered against drip, tended like shrines. They carry coals in pots across swaying vine bridges, they know clay that will not crack in steam. Most food is eaten raw or warmed or fermented, but the idea of fire must be kept alive.

And the Rememberers, oldest, who cannot dive anymore but can sit in steam and tell you where the ford was before the water rose, which underwater fence belongs to which cousin three platforms over, where not to put your foot downwarm. Because sight fails after ten paces, memory is infrastructure. The town extends past the immediate not by sight but by recitation.

With them, cheek by jowl, live the other citizens — the ones who were here first, or who never left.

The glass eels, almost transparent, slide between stakes in schools that catch the phosphorescence at night-dimplevel; the children imitate them, linking ankles, moving without ripple.

The blind white newts with feathery gills crowd the hottest seeps where humans must not linger, waving their plumes in heat-shimmer. People watch them for warnings. If the newts move out, the floor there is about to crack and sigh.

The fat snails on the hanging lines, dumb and abundant, farmed like mushrooms. They are the main meat that does not have to be caught, scraped off ropes with shell, boiled in vent water in baskets.

The algae itself, which is not one thing but a bloom that keeps its own faint glow, smeared on oars and ankles at night, clinging to thighs. It lives on them as much as in the lake. Scrub it off in the morning and by evening it has returned.

The ribbon-weed and the lilies and the thermal ferns — huge, dusty-green, unfurling where the water is hottest — which are not decoration but architecture. Fronds laid flat to make living walkways that tremble under weight. Lily pads as wide as shields with spined undersides strong enough to hold a toddler. Roots dangling directly into lake to drink rot and growth simultaneously. A fallen house-post sprouts before it collapses, and no one hurries to prop it because the sprout will hold.

And over all of it, rarely seen, the deep turtle. Huge and algae-backed, barnacled into a small island, older than the town. It surfaces once in a while in open water between causeways, exhales thin-air breath with a sound like a wet kiln sighing, looks — or seems to look — and goes down for a month. The children are taught not to chase it. The elders leave the choicest snail-meat on a stake where it surfaced. Whether it eats or not is less important than that someone remembers to leave it.

No birds call. No insects cloud. No herds ever came up the switchbacks through thinning air to this held bowl. The town has not yet been told to fauna. So humans have become the pollinators, the seed-carriers, the disturbance and the fertilizer. Pollen dusts their shoulders. Seeds catch in their hair that never dries. Their shit, carefully managed in basket-latrines over deep water, feeds the weed forests. Their dead hold up the roads.

High and drowned. Young and crowded. Warm, dim, patient.

What lives here is not a people in a lake. It is a reef that learned to breathe thin air on top while staying blood-warm underneath — part gardener, part eel, part ancestor-stone, part turtle-dream — a city learning how to float before the mountain has learned how to be stone, and succeeding, for now, by holding very still and holding each other up.
