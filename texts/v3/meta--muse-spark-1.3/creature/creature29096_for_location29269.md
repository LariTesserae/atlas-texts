At first, nothing lives here.

That's what your body tells you, sitting in the warm dark with water beading on your eyebrows. Nothing could. It's too small, too dark, too finished and unfinished at once. No food. No light to make food from. No flow to bring food in. Just stone sweating, and air too full to breathe properly, and your own heart too loud.

Stay longer. Let your sweat stop feeling like yours.

Something lives here because it has learned to need almost nothing.

The first one you meet you never see. You meet it with the pads of your fingers.

The rock is soapy.

Not everywhere. In patches, where the wall leans closest and the weep is steadiest, the raw basalt has a skin on it. Thin to nothing, tasteless, odorless, colorless if you could see it. Your fingertips slip just a fraction where they should catch on new mineral rash. That slip is alive.

It is a film. A bacterial felt, a few cells thick, spread like breath on glass. It does not photosynthesize — there has never been a photon here to eat. It eats stone. It drinks the iron and warm dust in the seep, the faint sulfur and silica dissolved out of the young tuff, and it breathes out a little acid that softens the rock under your thumb. That softness you thought was youth, that dent you could make — that is partly it at work, teaching the unfinished stone how to become a cave, molecule by molecule.

It grows so slowly it might as well not grow. In the outside world a colony like this would bloom and die in a day. Here, in constant warm wet dark, with no competition and no disturbance, it just holds. It does not expand. It does not retreat. It thickens by a winter, thins by a summer you will never feel. Put your cheek to it and it is cooler than the rock around it, infinitesimally cooler, because it is wetting itself, keeping itself. Weep, drip, evaporate, condense, weep again — it has inserted itself into that closed breath and become one of its lungs.

If you licked it — and thirsty herders have — it would taste of nothing. Only wet rock. That is its genius. To be indistinguishable from the place.

The second ones are not residents. They are visitors who never left.

You found them with your forehead: the rootlets. From that blade-slit in the roof where cold air leaks but light somehow does not, they hang down — three, five, maybe seven of them, thin as sewing thread, blind-white. They belong to the cushion plants clamped to the scree above, those tight, hard alpine things that endure wind by becoming wind-shaped. Down here their roots have gone wrong. Or right.

They groped down through porous stone tasting for deep water and found warm air instead. So they stopped. A finger's length, two fingers, and then a blunt tip, hesitating. They do not grow longer in a human lifetime. They do not rot, because there is nothing here that rots in the usual way. They hang and hold a bead of water at the tip like a held breath, like they are offering it or waiting to take it back.

They have no green. No leaf, no chlorophyll, no use for sun down here. Only the intention of a plant, prolonged. The film gently colonizes them where they die back by a cell, and tiny hairs slough off and fall. That falling is important. That is manna.

Because the third one lives on almost that alone.

You will probably never feel it unless you sit for an hour without moving, knees aching, breath coming back to you off stone inches from your face, and even then you will doubt yourself.

A tickle. A crumb moving across the back of your hand where your hand rests in the warm skin of water.

It is the place's only animal. A springtail, blind, colorless, no bigger than the salt crystal on your lip. If you could bring light — which you must not — it would be translucent-white, like a tiny grain of cooked rice with overlong antennae, feeling its way ahead of itself at all times because there is no other way to go. No eyes; eyes were expensive and useless and were edited out generations ago. No pigment; pigment was vanity.

It walks, mostly. Millimeters per hour. It does not need to hurry because nothing here happens quickly and nothing hunts it. On its belly it carries the folded spring that gives its kind their name, a furcula tucked like a jackknife, but here under the low ceiling where you cannot even stand, jumping is absurd. It almost never jumps. Perhaps once in its life, when your shifting sends a slosh, it flicks — a white speck disappearing into dark that was already absolute — and lands a body-length away and freezes for a long time.

It lives on the film. It grazes with mouthparts too small to see, scraping bacteria and a fallen root hair now and then, taking in warm mineral water through its soft cuticle as much as by drinking. Its metabolism is turned down to pilot light. It can go months without molting. It lays eggs singly, rarely, in a damp pimple of tuff, a pearl you would mistake for a water bead. One egg is enough. Two would be gluttony. The population of the whole pocket might be dozens. Might be less. They do not meet often. They do not need to.

Listen for it and you hear nothing. Plink — to your left, a drop falls from nub to pool. Long nothing. Plink — to your right, answering, but not in rhythm. The springtail makes no sound at all in that interval. It is quieter than the tick and settle of stone cooling by fractions.

These three — film, root, grazer — are not separate. That is what takes time to understand. Up outside, life is separate things competing. Down here, in four paces by three, everything touching everything else, life is a single slow condition.

The film softens stone and holds water. The root tastes the warm dark and leaks sugar and sheds skin. The springtail trims the film so it does not smother itself and carries spores and cells on its hairs from wall to wall, a pollinator of dark. The water lifts them all as vapor and sets them back down as condensation on eyebrows, on root tips, on stone bowls. Your sweat, while you sit, joins it. Your exhale joins it. For the hours you are here, you are briefly part of the animal too.

And then there is what you felt before you named any of them: the reluctance to change.

Drops fall but the puddle does not deepen. Evaporation lifts but the air does not dry. The nubs grow by a molecule a year. The dipper neither floats away nor sinks further. Even sound is held close.

That is the oldest resident. Not a creature. A state.

The pocket itself is embryonic — broken yesterday in geologic time, stalactites only blunt nipples a fingernail tall, edges still crisp enough to skin a knuckle. It has not yet learned how to be a cave. And what lives in it is teaching it, very slowly, how to be alive without becoming something else.

It waits, drop by drop, warm, dark, wet, crowded, unfinished. It does not want to grow up. It wants to continue.

When you lift the heavy stone bowl and drink, warm and faintly mineral, faintly of stone-soup and time, the drop that replaces what you drank is already swelling overhead. The film will close over your fingerprint. The springtail, somewhere to your left where you cannot see, will resume walking. The rootlets will go on hesitating.

Nothing happens here, beautifully, continuously. That is who lives here.
