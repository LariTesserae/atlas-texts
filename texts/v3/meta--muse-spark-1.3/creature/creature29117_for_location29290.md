At first you think no one lives here.

You crouch in the lee of the root wall and stare until your eyes adjust, and for a long minute there is only the wall sweating, the drip ticking every eleven seconds, soil sighing down. Then the floor shivers. A green chip detaches itself from the litter and walks.

Then another. Then a hundred.

You have to drop lower to see them right, cheek almost to the humus, six inches up, until scale inverts and what was litter becomes landscape. Then they resolve — not as dots but as persons, rust-red and long-legged, each with a sail hoisted over her head three times her body, jogging in a double lane. Outbound empty-jawed, inbound laden. Their antennae flicker constantly, tasting the air for the humidity change you cannot feel. When the cloud crosses the canopy and the light dims a fraction, the whole column halts at once, sails trembling, and waits.

These are the town. Or they are the town's hands.

A leafcutter is not one animal. Up close she is all elbows and blades: mandibles like shears, body burnished the color of the excavated clay, legs absurdly long for hauling. Alone she is frantic, fallible, she gets stuck under her leaf in a ditch and cuts it smaller. Together they are a single slow mind spread over thirty meters of forest floor. Minims no bigger than a crumb ride the leaves like shepherds, cleaning them. Medias — the carriers you see — do the cutting and hauling. Somewhere on the trail a major lumbers through, head huge as two workers, jaws agape, built only to crush. She does not carry. She is a door that walks.

Follow an inbound carrier home and she leads you to the citadel. From your height it was a pocked mound a meter across. From her height it is a walled hill of red-yellow pellets, each pellet carried up grain by grain from below, each entrance an archway worn smooth by a million feet. Inside it drops. Down past the guard majors clacking at the lip, down where the air turns still and sweet-sour, are the true residents, who never see light.

The fungus lives here. It is the reason for the town. In vaulted chambers as big as your fist, tended like orchards, it grows in grey-white sponges of chewed leaf, riddled with hyphae. This is what the leaves are for — not food, but compost. The ants chew the green sails to pulp, lick them clean of poison, inoculate them, prune them. The fungus fruits into swollen tips, staphylae, rich as yolks, and that is what the ants eat. If you could peel the mound open you would see chamber after chamber of it glowing faintly pale in the dark, each patrolled by gardeners no bigger than a sesame seed who weed out alien molds with antibiotic spit from glands on their chests. On their backs many wear a powder of bacteria, white as chalk dust, a living pharmacy.

Deeper still, in the moistest, most held-breath chamber, she lives. The queen. She is not like them. She is the size of your thumb joint, wing-scars still visible on her massive thorax, abdomen swollen to a glistening amber grub. She does not walk. She does not see. She lays. Egg after egg, white and sticky, while a retinue licks her, feeds her fungus-yolk, carries the eggs away to nurseries where larvae like blind grains of rice hang in cradles of mycelium. She has been laying for years, since before the fig fell, when the colony was somewhere else under a buttress. When this wall slumps, she will shrink her laying, the workers will carry her sisters' brood grain by grain through new tunnels, and the town will move.

Because it is a town, it leaks wealth, and where wealth leaks, others gather.

First the thieves. Along the edge of the highway, in the shadow of a leaf-bridge, runs a second, fainter highway. The thief ants are the color of honey, eyes tiny, bodies no longer than an eyelash is wide. They do not cut. They follow the big columns to find the entrances, slip inside unchallenged because they smell almost right, and steal fungus and brood. If you watch long enough you see a leafcutter halt, confused, antennae whipping, while a thief rides the underside of her leaf, invisible.

Then the hunters of the margins. He is no bigger than your thumbnail, bronze back flecked black, belly ink. A poison frog. He does not hop so much as reposition, one deliberate step, throat still, eyes fixed on the column. He is not interested in the column. He is interested in the straggler, the carrier who fell and is wrestling her sail in a leaf-cup puddle, the minim wandering. His tongue flicks — too fast to see — and the ant is gone. He will take four, five, then sit in the tea-brown seep at the foot of the pit, hind legs splayed, tadpole cousins wriggling around his toes in water that will be gone tomorrow. His skin tastes of the ants he eats; that is his poison, borrowed and stored. A second frog answers from under the fallen trunk, low, a single plucked note muffled by moss.

Above them, unmoving to the point of invisibility, waits the gecko. She is wet-bark mottled, toes splayed on the vertical earth, tail curled around a root thick as your thigh. Only her throat moves, pulsing ten times, stopping, pulsing again. She watches the highway below not for ants — too small, too formic — but for what the ants flush: a blundering rove beetle bulldozing straight through the column, scattering carriers like ninepins; a cockroach nymph; a froglet. When she goes she goes in a blink, no rustle, and is bark again.

In the one shaft of light that finds the pit, the gnats hang. Twenty, thirty fungus gnats, each a trembling speck, holding station in the column of brightness, rising and falling together as if breathed. They live only days. They will lay in the bracket fungi stitching the fallen trunk — white fans, orange shelves, black fingers pushing out of softening bark — and their larvae will eat that rot and become gnats and hang again. Around them, springtails catapult through the litter, silver-grey, feeding on spores; mites ride the leafcutters like burrs, drinking from their joints.

High up, where the root plate curls over and makes a dry lip that never gets the drip, the wrens live. They are the largest residents, and even they weigh less than two coins. Chestnut brown, tails cocked straight up, pair-bonded. Their nest is a dome plastered against the earth, mud and moss and root-hair, with a side door no wider than your thumb. They come and go without sound, a flicker in and a flicker out, beaks stuffed with green caterpillar, with a spider, with a mouthful of thief ants gleaned from a highway. Inside, blind chicks gape in warm dark, surrounded by the constant eleven-second drip below. When one adult sings, the raw earth swallows it — a soft trill as if sung through cloth — and the carriers below do not even pause.

Even the wall itself is alive. The moss knows where the water lives and swells dark green around each cup and seep. Filmy ferns, one cell thick, unfurl translucent along severed roots. Seedlings push straight out sideways from the vertical soil, two fat cotyledons defying gravity, racing to make a leaf before the wall slumps. The fungi are already digesting the giant: white mycelium lacing the underside of the trunk, turning wood to soil to fern again. In the cupped curl of a buttress three tablespoons of rainwater hold a whole zoo — mosquito larvae hanging head-down, stippled commas, filtering, racing to pupate and fly before the cup evaporates; rotifers and tardigrades coiled in the moss, waiting out dryness as cysts.

None of this will last. That is the contract. The wall will slump within two seasons, the trunk will soften and sink until you can push your hand through it, the seep will move six inches left and the highways will be re-surveyed overnight, old galleries collapsing, new shafts opened after rain, waste pits — those exhausted leaf pulps dumped outside and already furred with their own gardens — becoming seedbeds for philodendron. The queen will be carried, the frogs will hop ten meters to the next pit, the wrens will abandon the dome. The town is built to be left, grain by grain.

From ten meters away there is nothing here. From inside, cheek to the earth, it is rush hour in a city that smells of crushed leaf and iron, silent except for breathing and dripping and soil falling, and everyone is carrying something larger than themselves home.
