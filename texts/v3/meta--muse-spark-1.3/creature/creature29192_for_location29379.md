What lives there is not, first, people.

First is motion on the walls. You think the rock is crawling until your eyes learn the scale.

They come out of the cracks in pours — not swallows, exactly, something higher and harder than swallows, dun-backed and white-bellied, built for air that isn't there. Thousands at a time. They fold out of a seam no wider than your hand, ribbon out across the slot, fold back in somewhere else, and never make a sound you can keep. In thin air, crying costs too much. So they wheel mute, feeding on what the town exhales: the ash-moths, the midges that breed in grease, the hot dust itself.

Below them, everything that can sit, sits and watches.

On every ledge a human foot hasn't claimed, the rock is furred. Pikas and rock-rats, undistinguishable at first except by size — round, woolly, grey-brown, fat beyond all reason for a place with no green. They live shoulder to shoulder in the frost-split seams, black bead eyes all turned inward toward the heat. They do not forage outward. There is nothing outward. They forage inward, on spillage: grain dust from the sacks, fat drippings from the drying racks, crumbs flicked from the bazaar planks, the dead moths that rain daily from the mill houses. In winter below, these animals would sleep. Here there is no winter and no summer, only the permanent cool and the permanent leak of warmth, so they don't sleep. They just get fatter, slower, woollier, generation on generation, until the old ones can barely wedge into their holes.

Lower, on the dump terraces where slag and bone and broken crucible are tipped to consolidate the walls, the finches boil. Mountain finches, snow finches, red-beaked choughs with glossy black coats — they hop, they do not fly if they can help it, flying costs — between shards, hammering at marrow, at frozen fat, at anything salted. They too have stopped calling. A chough that called up here would lose its voice in a week. They flick and flutter and steal in silence.

In the mill houses it is always night and always warm, and so there are things that have forgotten the sky entirely. Clouds of ash-moths, pale grey as rock flour, live and die around a single caged bulb. They hatch in the felt insulation wrapped around the machine feet — the felt put there to muffle sound — they batter the bulb for one long frantic night, they mate in the air, they fall into the oil pans. Their whole world is a three-meter radius of heat. The men scoop the dead out by the handful every shift and toss them to the rats, and by next shift there are more.

And the dogs. Feral, but not wild. Barrel-chested, short-muzzled, silent as the birds. Lungs like bellows. They learned generations ago that the ground is for people, and so they took the air. They trot the high cables and the handrails of bridges with the loose confidence of cats, four feet on a two-inch wire a hundred meters over the forges, never barking. Barking up here is suicide — the pocket picks up a sharp sound and throws it back and forth until it drives you out of your head. The dogs figured it out before the men did. They communicate by shoulder-bumps and breath. They eat afterbirths, scraps, dead pikas. They sleep curled in the warm ash under the winch-houses, in piles, breathing together.

Everything lives off the leak. Heat, crumbs, grease, breath-moisture. The town is the meadow. The town is the waterhole with no water.

Then, out of the steam and lamp-glare, the people unstick themselves from the walls.

You expect them to be tall mountaineers, lean and weathered. They aren't. High air over generations — and even over a single lifetime, if you stay — makes its own shape. Short, deep. Barrel ribs like the dogs. Necks thick. Faces dark red-brown, not from sun but from blood forever close to the surface, trying to catch what little oxygen there is. Lips cracked to the quick in an hour if you don't grease them, noses that bleed without drama and are wiped on sleeves, eyes narrowed and wet against dust and glare. Their hands are the most alive part: scarred, oil-stained, fingertips split from cold metal and then healed thick. They move slowly when they can and frantically when they must, but they never waste a motion. A wasted motion is a wasted breath, and breath is currency.

Because they can't breathe. None of them can, not really. Even the born-here, even the old porters whose chests boom like drums, breathe in short, labored pulls. Newcomers spend the first month with a hammering headache behind the eyes, vomiting quietly behind sheds. Most turn back down. Those who stay learn the gait: bent forward, sack or drum or child or timber high on the back, short steps, mouth shut, breathe through the nose, do not talk on stairs. The children learn it by age four, darting across planks with armfuls of coal-bricks, mouths clamped shut, eyes bright.

Work is vertical.

The porters — men and women both, indistinguishable under soot and wool and grease — run doubled-over up staircases that are almost ladders, ore sacks leaking red dust down their legs. Where the ore comes from is everywhere: they pick the pocket walls themselves, widening the bubble crumb by crumb, sorting as they climb. A rockfall is not disaster, it's delivery. When that sharp crack comes from above, everyone looks up at once — birds, rats, dogs, people, all necks tilt — steps sideways one pace, and lets a refrigerator-sized block smash a shed to matchsticks. Then the builders are already there, pulling timbers, re-nailing in a slightly different configuration to fit the new shape of the wall. Houses are hammered stone footed straight into cliff, then tin and black timber and boiler-plate second stories, then bridges, then rooms hung under bridges. Nothing is finished. Nothing is meant to be.

At the bottom, in the blue-black where midday sun never reaches, the smiths and millers tend the heat. White sheets pulled from rollers, furnace doors blinking orange, welding flashes blue-white that leave purple ghosts. They work stripped to the waist in air that would freeze a stranger, skin goose-pimpled and gleaming with sweat that evaporates before it can drip. Above them, halfway up the west wall on planks three wide under sagging canvas, the traders do the other work: hands flashing, scales swinging, barter without shouting. A pinch of this powder for that handful of dried meat. A coil of copper wire for a rag wrung damp. They talk in fingers and eyebrows. Shouts die ten feet out anyway, and what carries bounces.

And the water people. You don't see them at first because water is invisible here. No cistern, no pipe, no stream. Every roof gutter ends in a rag. Every chimney collar wears a tin beard of ice where steam and breath have frozen overnight. Morning duty is wringing. Old women, old men, kids too small to carry ore — they go round with wooden buckets, unwinding rags, twisting them with both hands, watching a thin brownish trickle collect. That's drinking water. That's washing water. The rest — the real water — comes up on backs in sealed drums from a glacier two days down, drop by precious drop, carried by relays who do nothing else, whose shoulders are grooved permanent. They never spill. To spill is worse than to steal.

They eat what can be carried up or dried hard: barley meal, salt meat on racks, rendered fat by the spoonful for the cold and the climb, tea boiled once and then chewed leaf and all because you don't throw away hot water. They save everything. Bone is fuel and tool. Slag is building stone. Dead moths are chicken feed, if you can call the choughs chickens. Piss is saved for tanning and tempering. Breath is saved — they even farm that.

They sleep in shifts, in rooms stacked like drawers, three high to a mattress platform, doors hung with rags to keep sound and heat in. They line their boots with felt. They wrap machine feet with straw and rubber. They converted the big diesels to electric hum because the knock would reflect and multiply until no one could sleep. The forges sigh. The winches hum. The whole place vibrates low through the soles of your feet from the generator pit — a subsonic thrum you feel in your molars — but your ears hear almost nothing. Just close things: rope creak next to your ear, a buckle clink, someone's labored breathing beside you, tick-tick-tick of cooling metal.

Stay a day and you stop seeing individuals.

The birds, when they wheel, all bank at the same instant, as if the air itself tilted. The lamps, when the generator surges, do not flicker out of sync but breathe together, dimming and brightening as one lung. Falling rock always falls inward, toward the center, never outward off the mountain, as if the pocket were pulling. The old porters shrug and tap their chests. Stay low when the wind sucks, they say. Never build out, only in. Never shout. Never waste a breath.

Because what really lives there is not a thousand people and ten thousand animals and a hundred machines. It's one thing, wedged, crammed, layered to suffocation in a stone lung a hundred meters deep.

A hive that learned to be quiet. A warm, amber-lit animal made of hands and paws and wings and pulleys and furnace mouths, all feeding off each other's leak, all breathing together in the blue dusk while a mile below them the clouds burn white in a sun they never get.
