There is no one here.

If by someone you mean someone who opens a door in the morning. Who planted the towers and means to come back to them.

The towers are empty. Put your ear to that pearlescent dark and you hear them ring — stairwells with no stairs, rooms that are ninety percent tensioned air, elevator shafts where only air rises. Built for giants with a kilometer of headroom who never arrived. Who may never have existed except as a specification.

What lives here instead is smaller, wetter, louder. And it does not live *in* the city. It lives *off* it. On it. Because of it. In spite of it.

Start with what hangs.

Because everything that lives here hangs, first. The ground is still a wound — black glass, rope and shard, too young to be soil, too sharp to be kind. So life refused the ground. It took to the air.

From every tower fin, from every cable strung thin as a thought between towers, the Veil pours down. That is the first citizen: a curtain forty meters long and never touching earth. Black-green moss and copper moss braided together, drinking nothing but tower-sweat. The towers run a few degrees cold — that is the only mercy here — and the warm basin air kisses them and gives up its water. All day a perpetual sweating. Rivulets braid and drip. And where they drip, the moss thickens, swells, holds the drop a second longer before letting go.

On the moss live things that live on things. Epiphytes on epiphytes until the word loses meaning. Orchids big as dinner plates, translucent, their veins lit from inside with stored amber dusk-light. They open only in the stunned second of silence after the city exhales. Ferns that unfurl overnight, huge and delicate, and collapse by afternoon into black lace to be eaten by their own seedlings. Vines that do not climb — there is nothing to climb toward — but dangle and interweave in midair, vine catching vine until they make nets, hammocks, snares of green. In those nets the air itself collects: spores, shed skin, droppings, pollen, dead wings. The air here is soil. Breathe and you taste chlorophyll and iron on your teeth. That is what they are eating. Each other, endlessly filtered through damp.

And in the Veil, mouths.

You hear them before you can separate them. The Choir.

Lowest: the frog-throats. They are not frogs. They are fists of wet muscle sunk deep in the moss curtains, throat-sacs pulsing orange and warning-orange, eyes like coals. Each one pumps out a bass note you feel in your molars. Alone it would be comic. In ten thousand it becomes weather, a pressure on the chest.

Over that: the glass chorus. Six-winged and glassy, not cicadas but doing the work of cicadas — stuttering, sawing, clicking in such numbers that individuality burns off and becomes a metallic sheen over everything, a high roar at the edge of pain. They cling to orchid undersides, shivering their wings to shake condensation onto their mouthparts. When the city hum steps up a quarter-tone and the curtains tremble, they stop — one stunned heartbeat of absolute quiet — then roar back twice as loud.

Through that roar move the Ribbons. Pale gray moth-bats, hand-wide, moving not as individuals but as long tearing ribbons between towers, hundreds to a ribbon. Their wings make a dry rain sound, papery, against the wet bass. They drink drip-tips on the wing, they strain spores from the air with combed feet, they give birth live in flight to a single damp pup that clings instantly to belly fur. Watch a ribbon turn: it does not turn like birds. It pours, like water thrown between towers.

And inside the silence inside the ribbon — that is where the Knives live.

Higher up, riding the heat convection that rises off the cracked basalt, the kite-hunters. Three-meter wingspans, so thin you see dusk through them. They are almost all wing and eye. They do not flap so much as fold and drop. You will know them not by seeing them but by the moving patch of silence in the moth-bat ribbon where a kite is inside that silence, screaming thinly as it falls through. They nest nowhere. They lay a single leathery egg in a passing tangle of vine-net and never return; the hatchling eats the net, then eats its siblings, then steps off into air it already knows how to read. They hunt by shadow, which is why you must never shelter under the curtains. The curtains are full of eyes, and eyes draw knives.

Down. Look down, into the cracks where heat still sighs out sweet and metallic. That is the other country. The Cracklings.

Quick lizard-metal scuttles, copper flanks flashing. They are not lizards, not machines, but something hurried into being between the two: scales that ring faintly when they move, bellies that glow dully with stored heat. They live in the hand-wide fissures, darting between green shoots forcing up impossibly from hot stone, eating fallen glass-chorus dead, eating each other's tails, laying eggs like hot beads that hatch in hours. They drink nothing — they lick condensation off black glass before it wicks away.

And wherever tower-drip has found a fused hollow and made a pan — a clear shallow lens a meter wide — a whole parliament lives an inch deep. Tadpole-things flickering, mosquito larvae writhing in endless S, a floating garden of duckweed and fallen orchid petals. A world that exists for a day, until evaporation or a hot wind takes it. In that day it eats itself completely and still manages to sing: at night the pans click faintly, larvae to pupae to brief furious adults that rise in a column to join the Veil.

Under all of it, keeping it wet, keeping it cold, the Keepers you never quite see.

The hum in your ankles. The engines deep in the basalt that still maintain this place. Every few minutes the hum steps up and the towers exhale — a fresh sheet of condensation bursting outward — and all the hanging curtains tremble.

And the motes. Hold out your hand near a wound — where a kite has struck a fin and broken that pearlescent edge — and they will come. Too small, too purposeful to be dust. Bright. They swarm the break, weaving, extruding fresh razor edge over an hour until the tower is whole. If they land on you they will taste your sweat and leave, uninterested. You are not infrastructure. You are not damage. You are weather that sweats salt.

Sometimes near the big tower bases, where the maintenance field is strongest, you will see mist rise in slow spirals instead of settling, dust hang motionless in columns, a pebble fall too slowly as if considering. Sometimes a tower's upper third will stutter, double, go transparent to a sky that is not this sky, then resolve with a soft chime. Birds that fly through come out reversed for a few wingbeats, then correct, unperturbed.

None of them mind. Frog-throat, glass chorus, ribbon, knife, crackling, mote — none of them know who built the towers or why the sky is dimmed to amber dusk by that faint hexagonal shimmer overhead. They do not need to know. The builders made a city spaced for giants, vast space between presences, streets wide enough for processions that never come.

Life arrived anyway — hungrily, brilliantly — and claimed the sweat, the air, the hanging nowhere.

It does not need you. It barely notices you, except as another cold surface to condense on.
