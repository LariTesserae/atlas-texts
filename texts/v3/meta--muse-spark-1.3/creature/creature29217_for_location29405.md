Nothing lives here. That's what you think, standing at the rim.

Eleven trees in three hundred paces of glare and wet stone. Empty space between them. Heat. Drip. A pan so still it looks abandoned.

Then you crouch, and the place comes apart into living layers, and it doesn't stop coming apart.

What lives here lives *low* and *wet* and *fast*, and above all it lives *quietly*.

Start with the water, because the water is the floor. Every dimple that holds tea-colored water an inch deep is not a puddle. It is a city.

First the muscle: tadpoles. Finger-long, speckled gold-black, so thick in the shaded side of a pool that the water looks solid. They don't scatter like proper tadpoles. They turn together, a single slow flank, mouths down, grazing algae as it blooms by the hour in the white light. In a day a pool can go from clear to green soup to clear again because of them.

Around the rim of that same pool, the keepers of the rim: thumbnail frogs the color of wet granite. You will not hear them until you learn what hearing means here. They sit throat-puffing in total silence — a pale sac inflating, deflating — and if you put your ear to the mud you get only the faintest *tick, tick*, like two fingernails touched underwater. They have given up song. Song is death here. Instead they signal with throat and foot. Males flash the yellow underside of a toe. Females answer by rippling the meniscus with a hind leg. Their eggs are laid not in rafts but singly under algae, each one tucked where a crab won't bulldoze it.

Because the bulldozers are always working: striped snails big as marbles, amber and black spirals, plowing slow furrows through rust-red algal felt at the pool edges. Behind them, water beetles row without stopping, black commas stitching back and forth, eating the weak tadpoles, being eaten in turn.

Nothing in the pool is alone for long. A fallen elephant-ear fruit hits with a soft *plop* and within four minutes it is covered — snails outside, tadpoles worrying the skin, a copper crab coming sideways from two pools over with claws up to claim the seeds.

The copper crabs are the commuters. Bright, varnished copper-red, low-slung, moving in a sideways procession along invisible highways in the mud from pool to pool. They drink by dipping their mouthparts, they hunt tadpoles in the shallows, they are hunted. When a shadow passes they freeze and become stones. When it leaves they resume as if a switch flipped. They molt only in one place — and this is where the wrong trees come in.

Three or four slender impossible things stand in the pan. They are not alive, but everything alive organizes around them as if they were the oldest animals.

The leaning needle near the center, thirty feet of matte grey tapering to nothing, holds a perfect circle of dry mud around its base when everywhere else squelches. Put your hand near it and your arm-hairs lift. The air tastes metallic. Nothing grows there. And that is why it is precious.

In a pan where there is nowhere dry to be, the dry circle is nursery and workshop. White moths — thousands of them that live pressed like scales under the great leaves — come here at noon to dry their wings after the shower. They lift in a slow puff, settle on the warm dry mud, shiver. Tiny striped rodents, long-nosed errand-runners that rustle endlessly in the tufts, cache seeds here where they won't rot. The rails, secretive black birds with red bills that never call, lay their speckled eggs just at the edge of the dry, where snails won't reach. And the copper crabs come here soft and white after molting to harden, sitting motionless in the static field, vulnerable and untouchable, because even predators won't step into the hum.

The second thing: the low arch half-sunk in a pool, two bent fingers meeting over water. Under it rain does not fall. Droplets hang in the air, a glittering curtain trembling, parting when a dragonfly passes through.

That curtain is inhabited. Not visited — inhabited. Midges lay in it. The great pillars of midges you see hanging over each pool in the bright light — humming columns that make no hum — are feeding on it and breeding in it. They drink from droplets that never land. Tiny, almost transparent tree-froglets, no bigger than flies, live their whole first week clinging to moss under the arch and leaping into the suspended water to feed on trapped pollen, never touching the pool below where tadpoles would eat them. The blue dragonflies know. Huge, silent, bottle-glass blue, they hawk the curtain's edge, picking midges so cleanly the droplets barely tremble. When they mate they do it on the wing without a wingbeat you can hear, locked together like needles.

Up in the eleven trees, life stacks vertically to escape the crowded ground. Each tree is an island with the same structure: deep black-green collar of ferns and moss at the base where turtles stack on weeping rock to bask, elephant-ear leaves above polished and dripping steadily from their tips — *plik, plik* into pools — and under every leaf a ceiling of white moths. Vines thick as your arm hang and re-root, making ladders. Skinks like quick copper stitches run them. The striped rodents run them too, at night, taking fruit before it drops.

The large soft-footed come only when you are still long enough to become weather. First one ash-grey neck, then seven, then twenty: lanky wading birds stepping from behind a trunk, picking through shallows with such care they barely ripple. They do not call to each other. They flash. A flick of white underwing, a tilt of beak — a whole silent vocabulary. Close behind them, the drinkers: a herd of mouse-brown deer-things no higher than your knee, wet-nosed, ears constantly flicking, drinking in shifts while one watches. They step only on stone, never in mud where a footstep would suck. Even their swallowing is quiet.

Under all of it, the rock itself is alive. The pink-grey granite loaves sweat beads that join into dark trails. Those trails are gardens of black and rust-red algae, of lichen that tastes iron. In solution cups no wider than your palm, whole universes: ostracods, mosquito wrigglers, a single tadpole grown large and cannibal because it hatched early. Press your boot and clear warm water rises around it — the pan breathing up through pores. If you tap the stones near one pylon they ring faintly like tuned glass, though granite should thud. The frogs know this. On still nights — if you could call any time still here — they drum through the stone with their throats pressed to it, sending clicks through rock instead of air. The whole bowl hums, below hearing.

And over all of it, the reason for the hush.

Something hunts by sound.

You will not see it clearly. Perhaps twice a day a shadow crosses the sun though there is no cloud — too fast, too straight, gone before you look up. The locals in the high scrub will not sleep in the pan. They say the cloud that comes down every afternoon though the sky is clear elsewhere is not weather, but the old machines still condensing, still wringing water out of air to keep their room wet. What they don't say, but what every frog and bird and crab knows in its body, is that something else was kept too, or invited. A listener.

It is large, and it is silent the way owls are silent, but more so. No wingbeat. Only attention. A croak, a chirp, a splash out of rhythm — and it is there. So life here has made a covenant: move fast, breed fast, eat fast, and make almost no sound. Tadpole to dragonfly to bird to crab to heron-plume to mud again in weeks, not years. Algae blooming by the hour. Pools shrinking by evaporation and refilling from rock-sweat and from the thickening curtain under the arch, until mid-afternoon when the light goes white-hot, mist rises to your knees, then your chest, smelling of mud and green and iron, and without wind or thunder it rains — only here, only inside the rim, straight down for twenty minutes while the hills stay sunlit.

Then it stops. Steam. Brutal clean light. Drip, drip from the great leaves. The frogs lift their faces. The wading birds stand still and let it wash them. The moths puff up again. The crabs resume their sideways march.

That is who lives here: a multitude that learned to whisper so well the whisper became a way of drinking, mating, warning through light and tremor and stone. And among them, patient as weather, the old grey needles that are not alive and yet still make rain, still hold water in the air, still ring the rock — keystones no one remembers setting, now tended by snails and moss and crabs as if they were elders.

Do not try to be loud. You are just another warm animal come to drink in the bright hush, stepping carefully between pools so as not to crush the overwhelming, silent multitude underfoot — while high above, listening, something you will never quite see circles, waiting for one of you to forget the rule.
