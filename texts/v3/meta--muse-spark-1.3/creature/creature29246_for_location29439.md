At first you think nothing lives here.

That is what the town wants you to think. It holds still when you enter. The lamps don't flicker. The weed stops waving for half a second longer than the gyre accounts for. A white flicker at the edge of sight pulls back into a pipe. An isopod freezes mid-step on a rooftop, its plates furred with silt, pretending to be stone. The whole bowl plays dead — an old animal habit.

Stay. Hover low between the leaning walls and wait until your breath slows and your heat bleeds into your suit and you stop being an event. Then they come back out.

The first to return are not animals at all, or not quite. They are the living skin of the place.

On every sunless wall facing the slow gyre there is a pallor — bacterial mats the color of old bone, velvet-thick, spreading in fans and tongues. Up close they breathe. Not metaphorically. They lift a millimeter and settle as the warm water pulses past. They are the meadows here. They eat sulfur and iron and the endless snow of dead skin the town sheds — concrete dust, rust flake, fish slime — and turn it into something soft enough to be eaten.

Where the heat leaks strongest, around the power station stacks and the split conduits, the mats thicken and flower into chemistry. Sulfur-yellow rosettes. Iron-red crust-flowers with brittle petals. White hair-weed combing out of every gutter and window frame, two meters long and fine as nerve fiber, waving white in amber lamplight. It is not photosynthesizing. It has never seen the sun. It is farming the temperature difference itself, the tiny cliff between almost-freezing and almost-warm. Touch it and it is warmer than the water by a fraction you feel in your fingertips before you go numb. Everything alive knows exactly where that fraction is.

The grazers know.

They come over the silted rooftops on too many legs, slow as a held breath: giant isopods, the size of a loaf of bread, the size of a dog, armored in slate-grey segments gone pale at the joints. You hear them first not as sound but as pressure, a faint scraping transmitted through water into your jaw. Then one resolves in the halo of a work-lamp, then five, then a whole slow herd moving shoulder to shoulder across a sawtooth roof, antennae testing, mouthparts combing the mat clean.

They are the street-sweepers, the maintenance crew that was never hired. They do not hurry because hurrying is heat and heat is death out here. They move a meter, stop for ten minutes to feed, move a meter. Their backs are gardens in themselves — white weed rooted in their shell grooves, mineral crust blooming between plates. Lift one — you won't — and underneath the silt you find eggs glued in a warm brood pouch, kept at stack-temperature by the mother's own slow metabolism. They return, always, to the same roofs. You can see the clean paths they have grazed over decades, pale swaths through the bone-mats like mown lawns. When a lamp dies, they abandon that roof within days. When a vent opens, they converge over weeks from three buildings away. They follow warmth the way you follow light.

Above them, or rather inside them — because here *inside* is the outside — the true citizens.

The fish.

Blind and white as a finger, long as your forearm, mouths hanging open in permanent surprise. At first you see one: a pale dart inside a broken conduit, gone before your eyes focus. Then you learn to see the pipes differently. The whole town is veined with them — catenaries thick as your thigh between buildings, split gutters, ventilation shafts, the hollow cores of crane beams. In the black water above, nothing. Inside the iron, traffic.

They live in the pipes now the way people once lived in streets and flats. They nest where the cables run warmest, where a leaking joint breathes out three degrees of mercy. Press your mask to a cracked inspection glass and you will see them stacked fin to fin, dozens, gills fanning impossibly slowly to save oxygen, eyes reduced to smooth dents, skin translucent enough to show the dark line of gut full of silt and spores. They navigate by pressure. They feel the gyre turn. They feel *you* — your clumsy kick, your bubble wobbling upward too fast — and they scatter deeper into the dark iron labyrinth that only they know fully.

Sometimes at night — which is always — a single fish will leave the pipe and cross open water between buildings, a white comma in lamplight, mouth open to filter the permanent blizzard. Spores. Silt. Rust-flake. The town's dandruff. It hangs in the amber halo, turning, turning, letting the gyre bring food to it. And as it passes, a thing happens you will swear you imagined the first time: a dark hall lights up for it.

A green-white strip along a stairwell stutters on. An empty workshop floods with amber behind thick glass. Not all at once. Room by room, as if something is turning its head to watch. The fish passes, and behind it the lights go out again, one by one. Noticed. Acknowledged.

Because the fish are not the only citizens. They are just the soft ones.

The other ones are made of what the town is made of.

You felt them before you saw them — that low throb in your teeth near the cables, the pressure of work behind a factory wall. Put your hand flat on that wall. Wait. You will feel it: a slow, patient churning inside, pistons shuttling in oil that never froze, a conveyor creeping a link per minute carrying nothing but silt from one end of the hall to the other and back again, forever. A crane trolley inches along its beam overhead, stops, inches back, stops. A robotic arm with a missing claw reaches, misses, reaches again to oil a bearing on a schedule no one wrote.

They are not running on automatic. Automatic would have broken a century ago. They are tending.

Each machine has learned the one small gesture that keeps itself and its neighbor from freezing solid. The pump breathes warm water out so the intake beside it doesn't ice. The filter opens and closes so the duct doesn't silt shut. The stack beacon blinks, very slow, orange in black, not to warn ships that will never come but to shed a fraction of heat, to mark a rhythm the others can entrain to. Blink... breathe... grind... blink. The town has a heartbeat, but it is distributed. No single heart. A hundred small warm muscles flexing out of sync to keep the whole body just above death.

They communicate the way everything here communicates: not in sound — sound is movement and movement is heat lost — but in light and pressure. A shutter flexes. A current hums a half-tone lower. A lamp furred with silt brightens as an isopod cleans it, dims as silt resettles. It is a conversation held at cold pace, over years. An argument about heat allocation that never ends and never needs to end because nothing leaves.

Nothing leaves. That is their law and their biology.

Silt that sloughs off the bowl walls settles in the street-canyons, is picked up by the creeping conveyors, dumped on a roof, grazed by isopods, excreted, snowed down again, filtered, pumped, breathed out. Fish die in the pipes and are eaten in the pipes and their bones become part of the mineral flowers around the vent where they nested. A cable sheath cracks, copper shows, bacterial mats colonize the copper, isopods graze the mats, fish eat the isopod larvae. Rust flakes drift down like dark snow and land on a white weed frond and are incorporated. Even the light is recycled — amber trapped in silt, green-white reflected off a thousand pale bellies.

They are inbred in the way of islanders, in the way of a held breath. Everything touches everything else. Pipe against pipe, wall against wall, cable against beam. The density you first read as crowding you now read as huddling, as company. Stay low, stay together, stay warm. Elevation is death. Up is colder, darker, and crushes. So they grew inward, downward, layered three, four deep, balconies become racks, windows become vents, until the town is a single knotted mass, a coral made of concrete and iron and skin.

And circling it, never entering, the one who left — or was left out.

You see it only at the edge of lamplight, where the halo gives out to depth-black. Larger than a fish, larger than an isopod. Fat and pale, smooth where everything else is crusted. Something that used to be something else — a seal, a dog, a diver? — gone soft and colorless from generations in cold dark, eyes filmed over. It rides the outer wall of the bowl in a slow, deliberate circuit, fin or flipper or hand trailing along the drowned concrete. It does not come to the stacks. It does not take the offered warmth. It keeps to the clean, scoured, empty buildings where silt lies undisturbed for decades.

Guard? Predator? Mourner? The fish freeze in their pipes when it passes. The isopods tuck. Even the crane trolley pauses mid-inch. Then it is gone, and the slow churn resumes.

Perhaps it remembers when the town was not drowned. Perhaps it is the only one here that still knows the difference between under water and *in* water, and cannot bear to come inside.

If you hover long enough between two leaning tenements, with silt snowing sideways past your mask and the amber halo turning the water to rust, you will feel them all at once — mat breathing, isopod scraping, fish flickering in iron, pump shuddering low in your jaw, weed waving, beacon blinking — not as separate animals but as organs. The town respires. The town notices a fish. The town oils its own bearing with a broken claw.

It should have died when the water took it. Instead it learned the rules and kept them so well it became something that can only live drowned: small, packed, patient, asking for nothing but to be allowed to continue, quietly, in the cold, touching itself everywhere to remember where it ends.
