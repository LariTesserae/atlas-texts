They are not birds. That is the first shape your mind offers and discards.

Not insects, either, though insect is closer. Not snow, though from a distance on the white pavement with the bleached sun overhead that is exactly what they look like — a snow that decided to stay up.

Stay still long enough for your eyes to stop hunting for movement and they come into focus, one layer at a time.

The nearest one, at the level of your chest, perhaps an arm's length off. Hand-sized. The body is the size of the last joint of your thumb, curled hard on itself like a boiled shrimp, like a fetus, like a question mark. Alabaster, but not solid. A thin shell with light moving under it, not a glow — an interference, oil on water, mother-of-pearl shifting very slowly when you tilt your head. No mouth that you can find. No legs. No antennae. Only the curl, segmented so finely the segments look like fingerprints.

And the wings. Six of them, held out absolutely flat, as if pressed between glass. Paper-thin, veined like leaves, like dragonfly wings, dry as paper to the touch and cool as stone. They do not beat. They do not tremble. They hold. From wingtip to wingtip the span is your spread hand. Each wing is perfectly clear except for the veins, and through it you can see — if you stare too long — not the ground behind it, but a paler sky, and another plain below that sky.

At either end of the curled body, a bead. Black, glossy, unblinking. Two eyes, and therefore no front and no back. No direction to face. It sees both ways along its own curve. Neither bead follows you.

That is one. There is another a body-length beyond it, wingtip almost touching the first. And another beyond that. And above, and below, knee-high to higher than you can focus until your eyes water. A lattice. A shoal in air. They keep their distance with a precision that feels decided, though nothing here decides anything anymore.

From knee height up they hang. In the shallow pans on the ground they lie. Same animal, other posture: curled shoulder to shoulder, edge overlapping edge like scales, like scree, filling the low places to brimming. If you kneel — and you will kneel, because your legs will want to and because the silence will make you want to be lower — you see they are not dead. The opalescence moves under the shell. Very slowly. Slower than sap. They are not sleeping the way animals sleep. They are suspended. Paused. A whole fauna that ate the last of whatever there was to eat, drank the last of whatever there was to drink, and then instead of dying, stopped.

There is nothing for them to eat. That is not poetry, it is accounting. A black star of lichen here, a rust cushion the size of a thumbnail there, acres of salt pavement between. No flies. No carrion. No water. They do not breathe that you can see. The shell does not pulse. Once in a very long while, if you have stood still for an hour that is not an hour because the sun never moves, you hear it: *tik... tik... tik*, scattered somewhere above, faint as cooling ceramic. The infinitesimal contraction of chitin in the cold. That is the only sound they make.

They hold the place still. Or the place holds them still. It amounts to the same.

Toss a pebble and it arcs and stops a fraction too soon, as if the air considered it. Breathe out and your breath hangs a moment too long before it thins. Dust hangs with them. Distance goes wrong among them — a pallid a mile away looks exactly the same size as one at your face, and shadows fall five degrees off from where the sun demands, and if you walk straight counting paces you sometimes come back closer than you should, as if space had folded softly between their wings. They are numerous beyond counting — millions near, hundreds of millions to the white rim — and in that number they have become weather, and architecture, and time.

Change bruises them. Shout, and the shout does not echo; it stays as a pressure behind the ears, and the nearest wings develop a faint brown spot, like a bruise on fruit, that never fades. A mile east there is a black scar where someone once tried fire, and the pallids above it are permanently yellowed. They do not heal. They record.

Very rarely, over years, one settles. The lattice lets go of a single husk and it comes down light as ash. Empty shell, dry veins, eyes gone dull. Those, and only those, may be gathered.

Which is why the woman wears wings.

You will mistake her for a stone at first. She moves so slowly that movement itself stops registering. She is small, salt-white, powdered to the eyebrows with exhaled dust. Her cloak is layered husks sewn edge to edge with gut, hundreds of them, so that when she crouches she disappears into shimmer. Under it, sewn hides. In her hand: a flint flake still sharp from a knapping you can see a few paces off, a splinter of wing-rib for a needle, a hammerstone pocked smooth by generations of hands before hers. A bone awl at her waist. A scraper made from a human shoulder blade, polished ivory-yellow by use — because there are no other large bones up here, there have never been, except ours.

She does not farm. There is nothing to farm. She does not hunt — to kill one would be meaningless, and the meat is dry salt. She tends.

She walks the lines between cairns with a step that does not stir dust, rights a toppled marker with two fingers, brushes salt from the ground-sleepers with a feather, whispers without consonants: pure breath, shaped by tongue and teeth, *hhhh, sssh, hhhh*. A language worn down to wind, so as not to bruise. Her people have been here since before the plain stopped eroding. They make nothing new. The beehive hut of dry stone in the swale with its waist-high door is roofed with husk-membrane stretched over ribs. Translucent, pale. Light comes through it the color of milk. Inside: nothing that was not there. A sleeping hollow lined with cloaks. The rule-stones by the door, scratched by her grandmother's grandmother:

*Do not touch the hanging ones.*
*Do not speak above breath.*
*Do not leave anything that was not there.*

She has learned to live like them to live among them. Breathe shallow. Want little. Move only when necessary. Go down, when water is needed, to the wet rock days below where the air thickens and stinks of life, drink, climb back up empty-handed, resume mid-step between two cairns. Her heart, if you laid your ear to her chest, would be slow as the ticking. She is not quite alive the way you are alive. She is enduring.

The smallest residents endure too, in the slab-joints: the black enamel stars of lichen that grow a millimeter a century, the rust-red cushions that crumble to spice if your boot strays — so you learn to watch your boots — the grey beard hanging under a lee stone, trembling without wind. They are not food for the pallids. Nothing is food for the pallids. They are simply fellow witnesses, going at their own unhurried speed toward the same equilibrium.

That is what lives here, at the top that is not a top: a sky made dense with paused life, a ground made dense with sleeping life, and a few thin human figures, white with salt, moving between them like blood cells that have learned not to disturb the body.
