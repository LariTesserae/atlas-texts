At first you think nothing can.

That's what the room tells you on your knees. Too cold, too loud, too dry, too new. No dirt, no rock, no water. The filters would have eaten it. The frost would have kept it. The roar would have shaken it loose.

Then your headlamp catches the angle where the floor meets the wall — where the diamond-tread panel doesn't quite meet the galvanized skirting — and you see a dark line that isn't shadow. It's grit. A spoonful of it, gray-tan, pinned in the seam by static and vibration. Cliff dust. Ground mountain, sucked up a thousand meters through the louvers one particle at a time.

And in the grit: green.

Not the green of a houseplant. A duller, grayer green, the color of something that gave up on being green a long time ago but hasn't been allowed to turn brown.

You have to get low, cheek almost to the ringing steel, to see them properly because the fan is trying to tear your attention sideways. There are maybe a dozen. Maybe eleven. You lose count when the floor buzzes up through your teeth.

They are rosettes. That is the first true shape that emerges.

Fingernail-sized. Tight. Alpine cushion-form, the kind that on the cliffs below hugs rock to survive wind. Saxifrage, almost certainly — opposite leaves packed like shingles, spoon-tipped, waxy even in death — and beside them, in two of the seams, something else: a pale grass that only ever managed two blades, thin as thread, bleached straw-white at the tip.

They came in as seed. That much is obvious. A gust off the face, a cloud of almost-weightless things, drawn into the inhale of AHU-3 and fired through the louver teeth. Most would have slammed into the filter bank and stayed there. These few slipped a gasket, fell out of the main draw into the dead eddy along the floor angle, and lodged.

There must have been an hour — one hour, at commissioning or during a summer shutdown when warm outside air sighed in and condensed — when there was liquid. A film of melt on a screw head, a breath of damp in the grit. Enough. They cracked, they put out a white root hair, they unfolded two cotyledons to that flat 4000K office daylight that never turns off.

Then the cold took them back. Minus twenty. Near-zero humidity. The draw resumed at 1,780 rpm and never stopped.

So this is what lives here: not growing things. Held things.

Freeze-dried mummies. That's not a metaphor, it's the preservation state. The water sublimed out of their cells before ice crystals could shred them. No rot, because rot needs water and bacteria and time, and none of those are permitted here. No decay, no melt, no rust. The frost rims them — a perfect white outline on every leaf margin, crystals that never melt and never grow — and stays. The green stays green. The paint stays satin. The welds stay blue. The cold is a museum case.

If you hold your gloved fingertip a millimeter above one without touching — you learn not to touch, the edges up here will slice a glove — you feel it before you see it. A shiver. A high, almost invisible tremor in each tiny leaf.

They are all leaning.

Not to the LED battens. In any other room, any starved seedling would throw itself at the light. Here, in this flat shadowless white, light is irrelevant. It never varies, so it gives no direction. Direction here is given by air and iron.

Every rosette, every two-bladed grass, lists a few degrees sideways, uniformly, along the axis of the draw. Petioles stiffened mid-tremble. Leaf tips combed, faintly, toward the supply fan — or away from it, you can't quite tell in the tearing wind, only that the vector is shared. They have taken the shape of the vibration field.

Because the room does not vibrate in one way. The floor rings with the fan roar, a chest-high pressure wall. The screws buzz with the belt's frayed sawtooth flutter. But the leaves themselves — small, light, tensioned like drumheads — have found the pure tone underneath: the transformer cabinet's 120-hertz iron hum, felt in the molars, unchanging since the breaker was first closed.

One hundred and twenty times a second, the magnetic core stretches and relaxes. One hundred and twenty times a second, each saxifrage leaf answers. A garden tuned to mains frequency.

Put your ear — not that you can hear anything discrete in that waterfall engine — but put your bone near them, your knuckle to the floor panel beside them, and you understand their ecology. There are no pollinators here. Too loud for wings, too cold for legs. Nothing eats them. Nothing will. There is no soil food web in belt-wear felt and freeze-dried skin cells. There is only grit, light that never warms, air that never stills, and that endless sympathetic shiver that keeps frost from quite settling on their upper surfaces, shaking it to the rims.

They do not photosynthesize, not really. Not frozen. They do not respire. They do not grow a micron wider through the season. What they do is persist in posture — hold the rosette open, hold the two blades up in a V, hold green against gray — while being played, continuously, like the tines of a very slow, very cold music box.

That is their life. Not cycle, but sustain. A dozen small bodies that learned, in one damp hour, how to be a plant, and then learned, in the months after, how to be an instrument.

When you back out on your knees, eyes watering from the thin-air burn, they stay behind the hatch in their bright, shrieking stasis: eleven or twelve gray-green knots in a seam of mountain dust, leaning into a wind that will never stop, buzzing inaudibly with a hum they did not evolve for and now cannot live — or die — without.
