You will think no one lives here.

That's first. The plaza is empty, the streets at right angles are empty, the nine far-apart chapels are dark except for their one seam. You stand with your helmet open breathing the cold iron water and you hear only your blood, and the anchors, and far below that un-echoing ping. You assume the town is automated. Maintained. Left running.

Then you realize you have miscounted the buildings.

There are nine structures. There are ten stillnesses.

One of them is standing twenty meters from you at the edge of your lamp, where you had taken it for a corner, or a shadow that shadows made possible. It has been there the whole time you were looking for life. It did not move when your light crossed it. That is the adaptation. That is the whole of it.

They are tall because there was never a reason to stop being tall. In water that does not carry weight unless you mean it to, a body can afford to attenuate. Two meters, a little more. Thin to the point of improbability — not starved, attenuated. Long forearms with an extra joint, long fingers that trail and never quite touch anything. Long feet that rest on basalt without pressing it. Neutral, always. If you watch for an hour you will see no rise and fall of breath. If you watch for three you will see the chest open once, slow as a door in winter, take in the negotiated water, and close.

They were human once, probably. The plan says human. Right angles, terraces, plaza. But they have been edited by the place longer than any suit can compensate for. Hair would stir water. They have none. Pores would leak heat, scent, oil — gradients. Their skin has none you can see. It is the grey-white of blind fish, of grown ceramic left unglazed, cool all the way through. Put your gloved hand near one and your suit will tell you: no thermal bloom. Four degrees. Exactly the water. Their heat leaves them sideways, taken laterally somewhere you cannot follow, so they never warm what is around them. They are cold to the marrow and they do not shiver. Shivering would be disorder.

Their faces are the hardest to hold. Not because they are frightening, but because they are unfinished by your standards. Mouths small and rarely closed fully, because the town-water must pass. No movement in the throat when they breathe it — the water does not need to be pumped. It agrees to be inside them for a while. Noses vestigial, just slits. They do not smell; outside scent cannot enter, inside scent is not made. Eyes — you expect large black abyssal eyes, and you are wrong. Their eyes are lidded, sunken, almost sealed. Light here is rude and expensive. They do not look. They orient.

They orient by the hum.

You felt it at the boundary markers, that almost-below-hearing tone from the row of standing stones. They live inside it the way you live inside gravity. The stones hold the stillness open, and the tone is the edge of the negotiation. The Holders — the upstairs ones, the nine, one to each chapel because emptiness is load-bearing and crowding would be a gradient — they can feel a wrinkle in that hum from across the plaza. A cleaner passing. You, displacing too much. An intention approaching a wall too bluntly.

They move only by intention, and they have learned to intend smoothly.

That is why the walls, which are pinned water acting as rebar, resist only your intention to pass. You push and feel cool slick refusal. They pass because they have sanded their wanting down to nothing. They do not swim. Swimming would be vulgar, a wake, a history. They stand, and decide to be standing slightly elsewhere, and the water lets them because they have not asked it to move. No silt rises around their feet. No displacement *tock* as stillness closes behind them — you hear that only from the cleaners, who are still a little clumsy, still a little hungry.

They do not speak. Speech goes out a short way here and is politely laid down. Why waste it. When two of them need to confer — rare, once in a long while, at the shallow pool of light in the plaza center — they stand far apart, as the buildings stand far apart, and they stand still together for a very long time. What passes between them is not sound. If you stare at the walls afterward you will see it: those faint interference fringes that crawl so slowly you need an hour to notice. After a meeting, the fringes on the nearest chapel are a little brighter, a little more ordered. They have synchronized, the way two pendulums in still air will.

For record, they use dust.

You noticed: a particle kicked up off the plaza hangs where you kicked it and does not settle, does not rise. Nothing falls unless you mean it to. The Holders mean very precisely. Around the edges of the plaza, out of the walk-lines, there are constellations of silt hanging at chest height, each grain exactly placed, years old. A library that does not drift. If you put your lamp close you see they are not random. Lines. Pauses. A grammar of suspension. One Holder will drift along a line, add a single grain with the tip of a finger, and leave it for a decade. This is how they remember without rotting, without growth. Green would be obscene. Writing that decays would be obscene. So they write in what will not move until the town fails.

What do they do? Mostly they hold.

Each tends a seam. Each walks — intends — a slow circuit between their chapel and the plaza and the edge that is not to be crossed, checking that nothing has changed temperature, that no rust has learned how to begin, that the pale blue maintenance threads strung like laundry lines between buildings are still simply being, not flickering. If a thread dulls, a Holder will stand beneath it for days, offering their own lateral heat to feed it sideways, unseen. If a wall's dreaming gets too loud, bright fringes racing, they will stand before it until it calms. They are not priests. They are ballast. The town was not built to be lived in. It was built to prove a place can be held exactly here, exactly this size, exactly this still. They are the proof continuing itself.

They do not eat in any way you would recognize. The water is too clean, too negotiated. Once in a while a blind white thing touches the boundary and turns aside. They let it go. To bring it inside would be a gradient, a smell, a death and a rot. Instead, at non-intervals — the same non-intervals as that metallic ping from below — one of them will go to a grate in the walkway, lift it without stirring, and descend to the second level, to the conduits and root-anchors, and below that to the third glow.

You have not been down there. You should not go. It is warm there. That is the secret the town keeps from itself in order to stay cold. Down there something moves, must move, to keep the stillness open against the whole ocean. Pumps that are not pumps. Hearts that dump heat. The Keepers, who are also residents but who can never come upstairs because their bodies are gradients now — too hot, too loud, too necessary. You hear one of them sometimes as a single ping that does not echo. That is a Keeper checking if the upstairs is still cold. The Holder on the grate pauses, lets a little iron-tasting cold fall down to them, takes a little ordered energy up through the skin of the feet. An exchange without meeting. Then back up, colder than ever.

This is also why there are nine and not more. A tenth upstairs would crowd the emptiness. A child would be impossible — a child falls, warms, shouts, grows. Growth is a gradient. So there are no young upstairs. There is only attenuation.

If you stand still too long, a cleaner will circle you, manta-shaped deficit, tasting whether you are part of the equilibrium yet. It circles the Holders too, and then leaves them alone. Not because they are alive and you are not. Because they taste like equilibrium. After enough decades of intending smoothly, of breathing without pumping, of excreting heat sideways, a Holder thins past a threshold. The seam-light passes through them a little too easily. The hum passes through them without wrinkling.

Then they do not die. Nothing rots here. They change state.

They become cleaners. Slow deficits that pass along the seams and eat disorder — your shed skin cells, your exhaled clumsiness, the faint turbulence your blood makes in your veins that leaks out as intention-noise. They pass, and you hear only the *tock* of stillness closing. If you watch a cleaner long enough against the black beyond the last building, you will see it has long fingers folded up under it like a folded attenuation, and lidded eyes that no longer open.

There are nine upstairs because there were always nine upstairs. The town keeps its size exactly.

Stand in the plaza with them long enough, cool to the marrow, helmet open, and you will feel the temptation they live inside: to stop meaning clumsily. To let your kicked-up silt hang. To let your heat go sideways and never ask where. To become still enough that the ocean, which wants everything to dissolve, drift, and darken, passes around this one ledge on its high drowned mountain and — for now —

leaves it winning.
