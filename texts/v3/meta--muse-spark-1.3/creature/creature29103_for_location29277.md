At first you think nothing lives there.

You put your cheek to the hot gravel and squint into the slit and for a second it's just dark. Warm dark that smells wrong for noon — held air, chewy air, stone and ozone and old oil. Then your eyes adjust and the dark starts to move.

It doesn't move in one place. It moves everywhere at once, in layers, because there is no room between presences.

The back is the wettest and the oldest-feeling, though the whole pocket is young. A cold main comes low across the back wall, sweating hard in the heat. The wall behind it is slicked green-black, algae never more than a thumbnail thick, glistening. It looks like wet paint until you see it breathe, very slowly, with tiny bubbles and grazes.

Working that edge is the centipede. Fat brown house centipede, legs too long for her body, moving like a dropped stitch pulled sideways. She doesn't run. She picks. She lifts one feathery leg after another along the algae rind, feeling for the soft spots, for springtails and mite eggs and the just-hatched. She is all antennae and recoil.

In front of her, under the lip of the pipe insulation, is the pillbug rind. Hundreds of them, shoulder to shoulder, slate-gray and dusty-rose, antennae waving in unison like wheat in a draft you can't feel. When the slatted light from the grate hits them right, they glitter. They carry copper shavings on their backs — lifted from a twist of wire dropped from above — whether by accident or because over months the ones that glittered got eaten less. No one could say. They just move, faintly lit, a living floor that tightens when you breathe on it.

Over the pillbugs runs the river.

Argentine ants, glossy and rust-dark, single file both ways along a fallen zip-tie that bridges a tilt in the concrete. They carry lint, skin, bread-crumb dust, a flake of dead moth wing bigger than any of them. A section of their highway is roofed — they have propped up a curled label, paper side out, and run underneath it where the drips can't knock them off. Further up, on the damp lagging of the pipe itself, is their garden: a felt of gray mold they tend and clip and harvest in lines. You can see the harvested patches, paler, and the lines of ants leaving with gray bundles in their jaws.

Nothing flies here, almost. So the spiders have adapted.

Cellar spiders hang so thick under the grate that their webs touch. Long pale daddies with bodies like rice grains, legs like wire shadows, they share vibrations across a shawl strung from live conduit to rust-pan edge. When dust sifts down through the light bars, the whole shawl shivers. What they catch is not flies but lint from the laundry vents — blue and white fibers drifting down endlessly. They wrap it anyway, out of habit or hunger or hope, and the ceiling is lined with small gray mummies, prayer flags in a row, swaying when the building breathes.

Below them, the crickets. Mottled field crickets, the color of the gravel outside, with hind legs like bent wire. In any square inch you can count five things moving and two of them will be crickets, freezing, twitching, freezing. They come to the drip to drink.

The drip is the clock. *Plik... plik... plik...* The pipe freckles, beads fatten and merge, and with the 60-cycle hum coming through the wall they sometimes don't fall straight but walk sideways a trembling millimeter before they let go into the long rust-pan underneath. The pan is flaking in razors and curls, fresh rust standing up sharp because the whole place was born last winter when the slab heaved. Around the splash zone the floor is growing pale mineral needles, months-old stalagmites still translucent at the tips. If you touch them they come away like salt. Clustered where the concrete leaches lime are the snails — tiny, amber, shells thin as nail — rasping in a slow circle.

And the green. There is almost none and it doesn't need more. To the left where daylight stripes in, a single fern wedged in a mortar crack, three fronds pale and dusty but alive, holding its spores close. To the right, in the driest inch, a tomato seedling some lunch dropped, gone leggy and white, leaning hard toward the light bars, two true leaves spread like hands. They are not competing. There isn't space to compete. They are just taking what falls: light bars and lime and drip-mist.

Above all of it, tucked in the warm hollow where the conduit enters, the mice.

A nest of deer mice, big-eyed even in sleep, bedded in dryer lint — blue fluff and white fluff packed soft — mixed with shredded work-glove leather. You can still see the glove: one stiff gray finger curled like a tunnel, now a hallway they run through. They cache crumbs under a bottle cap. They gnaw the paint chip. They know the building. Every seventeen minutes or so the big compressor on the other side kicks with a deep *whung* you feel in your molars, dust sifts, the web-shawl shudders and every spider freezes. The mice have learned exactly how long the shudder lasts. They freeze too, noses up, and then go back to grooming a beat before anyone else dares.

And in the dampest corner where the pan overflows into a saucer of concrete, he waits.

There is only room for one toad.

A Gulf Coast toad the color of wet gravel, throat pulsing almost imperceptibly, eyes half-lidded against the glare bars. He is too big for this place and that is why he rules it. He does not croak. The space is too small for a croak; it would deafen everyone. At dusk, by the clockwork of the grate light going orange, he eats the crickets that come to drink. One tongue-flick, unhurried. Then stillness again. The earwigs that patrol the dark side give him a wide berth, pincers up, glossy brown, cleaning their antennae with fastidious passes. They know.

This is the rule: water comes from the air, not the sky. Food comes from above, not below. Everything that falls in stays in and becomes architecture. The bottle cap is a cistern roof. The copper wire is a mine and a camouflage shop. The desiccated moth is a warehouse being dismantled leg by leg.

It is mostly quiet. That is what stays with you. The loudest thing is water learning to be water. Under that, if you hold your breath with your face in the hot dust, a faint dry whispering — chitin on concrete, thousands of feet repositioning. A mouse toenail on metal. The tick-tick-tick of the failed sump float trying to lift and falling back.

Then the wall inhales. The motor catches. *Whung.* Dust falls through the light. Every living thing pauses together — pillbug, ant, spider, cricket, centipede, snail, mouse, toad, fern leaning toward light — one held breath in warm dark.

Then resumes, faster.
