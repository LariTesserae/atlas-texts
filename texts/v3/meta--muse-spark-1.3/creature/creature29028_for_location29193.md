No one lives here. That's what you think, for the first hour.

Because the rule here is green in islands and black in between, and you are looking for something the size of you, something that walks and sleeps and leaves a second boot-print next to the melted first one. There is nothing that size. The ring wall is empty. The doorway frames only ferns. The hawk will not come in.

Crouch. That's when the saucer starts to populate.

First, the rooted ones. They are not background.

The tree-ferns are the elders, though they cannot be more than a few years old — the rock itself is barely older. No taller than you, but already gnarled. Their trunks are not wood so much as a braid of old frond bases and white root-knuckles clamped straight onto basalt. They do not grow *in* soil, they make a thimble of it under themselves: fallen frond, trapped dust, frog shit, dead bee. Their living fronds, door-wide and waxy, are angled not to catch sun but to shed it, tipped down at noon like umbrellas in a storm of light. Underneath each crown is always shade, always drip. If you put your hand under without touching, you feel it ten degrees cooler — their own private weather, inside the larger one.

Under them, the moss. You sank to the wrist. What you sank into is a sponge that is also a city. Cushion moss, electric green on top, black-green and brown below where last year's growth is already peat. Each cushion holds ten times its weight in seep water and does not give it back to the sun. Inside it, if you tease it — don't — springtails like grains of salt, blind white mites, a nematode fauna that has probably already speciated in this one bowl and nowhere else. The moss steams faintly at noon because it is cooking on top and cool underneath, and things commute up and down that gradient all day: down at 13:00, up at 16:00 after the rain.

The pitchers squat at the edge of the moss where it is wettest. They are the carnivores here because the stone gives water but almost no nitrogen. Each pitcher is half-full always, rain plus digestive fluid, rim beaded — you saw right — with both nectar and condensation, the plant cheating, using the ribs' gift to sweeten the trap. Inside each one lives a smaller world that never leaves: a gnat, no bigger than an eyelash, striped gray, that breeds only here. It lays on the inner wall just above the fluid line. Its larvae swim in the soup of dissolved flies without being digested — they paddle between corpses. When they hatch they do not fly far, just hop to the next pitcher on the same island. That's why there is no screaming insect chorus. The fliers that would scream cannot cross the black.

The orchids do not touch ground at all. They hang in seep cracks, leaves like blown glass — you saw — translucent because they have moved their chlorophyll deeper, behind a water lens, to drink light without burning. Their roots are naked to air, silver-white, covered in hairs that visibly bead. They flower rarely, white-green, scentless to you, blazing to one other thing.

Which brings the second order: the crossers.

Nothing connects the islands on foot. To live here and stay alive, you either never leave your island, or you can cross twenty meters of mirrored glass at 50°C in a straight line without stopping.

The glasswing bee does the second. Huge — thumb-joint huge — and almost invisible because its wings are clear panes twice its body length and its abdomen is banded in clear and smoke. You see it every ten minutes or so because there are only a few of them, and they work a circuit. Nest? Not in the green. In the black. In a finger-deep rain channel on the north side where the slab overhangs a millimeter, they have capped cells with resin scraped from fern trunks. The larvae can take heat that would kill anything else because they go torpid at noon against the warm stone and wake when the discs cool the air. The adults fly only island to island, fern to orchid to pitcher, drinking sweat from leaves, moving pollen. They fly absolutely straight because in the trembling air any curve costs water. During the three o'clock making, when your sweat lifts off your arm and motes hang, they are gone. They clamp under a fern frond, wings folded, and wait out the static that makes their hairs stand up. Your hand goes numb if you touch a rib then; imagine what it feels to navigate by charge. They wait.

In the largest pool — only the largest — the drowned ones.

White frogs. Not albino as a mistake. White as a decision. Translucent-bellied so you saw the dark coil of gut through the skin. Blind-looking because the eyes are sunk under a clear lid, pink-gray. They surface only if you cast no shadow, gulp with that pulsing throat, and sink to hang in the cool deeper layer where a seep feeds the pool from below. There might be twelve. Might be twenty. They never croak except once.

Tadpoles? No one has seen them. Likely they keep the young inside the moss at the pool's lip, in a burrow kept wet by capillary, not in the pool itself where the water swings from hot to cool every day. What they eat: the midges that misjudge and fall, the gnats, the occasional bee drowned in rain. What eats them: nothing. Nothing here is big enough except the hawk, and the hawk will not enter.

But they are not alone in the water. Look longer than is comfortable in the heat. The still pool is not still. Under the frogs, copepods flick like commas, pink. A film of green algae parts around a frog's nose and closes. On the pumice rim, the white mustache you thought was mineral is half mineral, half living: silica-depositing bacteria building terraces a cell thick, turning evaporated seep into lace. They are the ones that will, in a hundred years, turn seep into soil.

Third order: the ones that live on the working things.

Because the ribs and discs are not dead. You felt that. A degree cooler under them at noon. The ticking.

On the cold side of each millstone disc — the side no shadow explains, shockingly cold to touch while the other side burns — lives a felt of silver mites. At noon they crowd there, shoulder to shoulder, drinking the condensation that forms perpetually on that impossible gradient. At night when the stone rings and contracts, they disperse to graze algae. They are the only animals that prefer metal to moss.

And in the cairn near the eastern seep, the weaver. You only saw its thread when it was wet. Black on black. It is a spider the size of a lentil, black glass color itself, legs tucked into cracks between balanced slabs. It does not hunt. It has learned from the ribs. It strings its filament across the gap where seep steam rises in the morning, a comb of parallel lines, and waits for the air itself to bead on them. Then it walks the line and drinks the beads, one by one. During the daily cloud it does not hide — it works hardest then, reeling in swollen lines, while the frogs sing their one startled chorus below.

These are the permanent residents. Moss-mites that never leave their cushion. Gnats that never leave their pitcher. Frogs that never leave their pool. Mites that never leave their cold side. A spider that never leaves its cairn. Plants that cannot leave at all. All of them tuned, generation by generation — which here means months, not millennia, life going insane fast — to a cycle that should not exist: boil, sweat, cloud, rain, steam.

The makers? The boot-print melted a centimeter deep, the doorway with no door, the ring wall just above the seep line where it is safe to sleep. They are not here. But they left the tap running, and the tap is alive in the way a grafted limb is alive.

Stand under the ribs at 14:50 and watch them go dark at the edges, interference colors dying. The air thickens. Your shadow lags. The bees have already hidden. The frogs have sunk. The moss has opened every leaf hair. The spider has tightened its lines. The silver mites have pressed flat to the cold.

They all know what is about to happen. The bowl breathes out. White wisp at the center. Twenty meters visibility. Brief vertical rain only here while outside the rim the valley still glints hard blue.

For twenty minutes, the saucer is not an island in the sky. It is the whole world, holding its water up by will and wire and root-hair.

Then it burns off. Stone steams. Smell of hot iron and crushed green and ozone. The pitchers overflow. The rosaries hang on every filament, moss hair, spider line, rib bead. The frogs sing once and stop. The bee crosses again in its straight line.

If you stay quiet, if you drink only from the running seep and rest only on the black, you can be a temporary resident too — another animal that comes rarely, and leaves quickly, before the rock cracks again tonight and the moss takes the new crack by morning.
