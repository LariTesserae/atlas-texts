Who lives here is, at first, almost no one.

You walk the causeway expecting a town to gather — a noise, a crowd, a dog to bark at your strange stride — and instead the plan stays empty around you. The plaza built for thousands holds twelve sorters. The long straight lines hold one woman and one child, far apart. You learn quickly not to look for people. You learn to let them surface.

They surface the way stones surface in the marsh: slowly, where you are already looking.

The woman mending the sluice first. She is shin-deep and not minding it. That is the first thing about them all — no one here minds being wet.

Their bodies have stopped negotiating with damp. Skin is always beaded or sheened, hair always a little stuck, clothes always a little heavy. They do not wipe themselves dry; it would be pointless inside an hour. Instead they are smooth with it. Children shine. Old people's wrinkles hold water like the veins of leaves. Hands, especially, are never dry — fingertips gone soft and ridged from handling sluice planks and taro mud, palms pale and wrinkled as if they have just come from bathing, always. Feet are the opposite: toughened. Everyone goes barefoot, even on the sharp new scoria, and the soles are thick, splayed, yellow-grey with callus, toenails short-broken. They step stone to stone without looking down. You stumble for a week. They don't.

They dress for water and work and warm air that never cools. Loose-woven skirts and capes of the tall reed fiber, softened by constant wear, darkened at the hem where they drag in canals. Some wear nothing above the waist but a strap for carrying. Many wear a wide flat hat plaited from banana sheath when the mist thickens into rain, but more often they just get wet. Color has mostly washed out of clothing — greys and reed-browns and mud-black — except for fresh twine, which is green-gold, and except for skin itself, which is various, lowland various, brown to black to lighter brown, because the people who climbed up were not one kind. Their descendants are still various, though the damp has made them all a little similar in the way they move.

Which is slowly, and without splash.

No one here hurries. Hurrying makes noise and muddies water. A woman lifting a sluice plank will lift it an inch, watch, set it down, lift it again. A man setting a basalt block will seat it, wet the joint, look at it from both sides, leave it for rain to settle. A child with reeds walks at the exact pace the bundle allows. You will not hear a shout across a field to hurry someone. Sound dies at twenty yards in the wet air, in thatch and reeds and standing water, so they don't try. They nod. They come close and speak low, a few words. Their voices are soft — not whispering, just unprojected — a little hoarse from thin air. They breathe audibly, as you do now. Everyone does here and no one notices.

Look at their hands long enough and you understand what they are.

There are no animals in the bowl. No goats to manure, no chickens to weed for grubs, no bees. So they have become the animals.

They are the pollinators. At noon, when the gourd flowers open white and brief, you will see an old man go plant to plant down a trellis with a feather tied to a stick, or just his fingertip, touching inside each flower with a care that looks like blessing and is work. Small girls learn it before they learn to read water — if there is reading — squatting patiently by taro inflorescences, moving pollen where wind won't. They are the frogs and herons too, shin-deep for hours, thinning reeds, clearing ditch-mud with bare hands, feeling for blockages by temperature change. They are the manure, in a sense: everything green that is not eaten goes back, rotted, plus ash from the small cooking fires, plus night-soil carried carefully to distant compost mounds away from the cistern — because nothing can be wasted where nothing comes up the cliff except what a back can carry.

They eat what they can coax without help. Taro corm and leaf, sweet potato, gourds, bananas in sheltered corners, the tender heart of the tall reed. No meat, no milk, no egg. Ever. The old stories say chickens screeched all the way up the switchbacks and died within a season of the rim — cold nights below, then strange water above — and no one tried again. So their bodies are lean in a particular way: not starved, there is starch enough, but light-muscled, tendoned, belly-flat even in old age. Teeth worn from grit in pounded tubers. Eyes clear. They live long, or they live uncounted — no one seems to know exactly how old anyone is, and it doesn't seem to matter when the work doesn't change.

They live scattered on purpose. A house, then a hundred yards of marsh and garden, then another house. Not from fear. From water. Each household tends its own level, its own seep and drain, and if you crowd, the levels fight. So a marriage means a new house on new piers over a new damp patch: dry-stone to the waist, wattle-and-daub above, steep reed thatch above that, porch narrow, gourds hanging, baskets stacked, fire lean-to apart so the house never burns. A death means the reverse, unhurried: thatch not replaced that year, then walls slumping, then piers standing alone like teeth while reeds come back through the floor. Nobody mourns the plan contracting. The causeways stay straight whether two hundred houses are full or a hundred and eighty are.

If there is government, it is water. No one wears anything different. No one commands the plaza. On most days the big level of pounded earth by the crossing is just a dry place to sort tubers into piles, children sitting on the edge of the low stepped platform kicking their feet. But when a canal silts or a spring shifts after a slab-fall, people gather without being called — they see the water wrong — and stand around the cistern, which is always full to the brim, and point with their chins and move planks until the levels agree again. Then they disperse. The cistern is the closest thing to a shrine: stone-lined, clear seep water untouching, covered with a lattice to keep leaves out, swept daily. No one drinks from it directly with dirty hands. You dip with a clean gourd, carefully, so as not to cloud it.

Their children are few and watched the way the gardens are watched — not anxiously, constantly. A toddler learns three things early: which stones hold, which water is for feet and which is for drinking, and not to cry loudly because no one will come running through marsh to a loud cry. Mothers carry infants on the hip in a sling of reed cloth while they weed, the baby's legs dangling in warm water. Boys and girls both learn sluice, spade, feather. There are no schools, no shops, no pens for anything. What a child owns is a digging stick, fire-hardened, sized to the hand, and a basket that floats if dropped.

Old people do the lightest, most skilled work: sorting seed tubers by feel in the plaza, plaiting baskets tight enough to carry mud, remembering — though remembering here is thin. They will tell you, if you sit long enough and ask low enough, that great-grandmother came up with taro wrapped in leaves, that the far rim lost a tooth in a night of thunder, that a year came when mist stayed forty days and thatch had to be retied twice. Beyond that, time flattens. No wars, no famines, no floods to date by. Lichen spreads a finger-width per decade. A wall goes up course by course. The work is always half-finished and left, and taken up again.

At dusk you will find them inside or on porches, not gathered. Cooking fires are tiny — a few dry reed stems, a clay pot of boiled taro — smoke rising straight up a few feet and dissolving. No lamps that you can see; fat is too precious to burn, and there is nothing to see after dark that can't wait for plain midday light. They sleep early, on raised mats above the damp, under thatch that ticks as it cools one degree. No snoring carries. No dog dreams. Only water continues: seep, trickle, plink from leaf to pool.

If you stay, as the bowl asks you to stay, you become a little like them without deciding to. You stop listening for birds. You stop expecting manure-smell, blood-smell, feather-smell. Your hearing narrows to the register they live in: the difference between a healthy sluice hiss and a choked one, the plink that means the cistern is truly full, the soft knock of adze on silvery wood that means someone, a hundred yards off through reeds, is repairing a trellis and does not need help. You learn to hand-pollinate without crushing. You learn to nod.

That is who lives here: a quiet, wet-handed people who climbed above the world of animals and did not bring it with them, and so took its jobs into their own fingers. Sparse, patient, unvaried as the springs. Keepers of level water in a bowl in the sky, asking for nothing but maintenance, and giving it back, day after day without variation.
