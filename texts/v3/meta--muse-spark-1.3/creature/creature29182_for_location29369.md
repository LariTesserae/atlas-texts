On the Table you will think at first that nothing lives here. The towns look empty at noon.

Then your eyes adjust to the glare, and the empty resolves.

A figure on a flat roof, perfectly still under a dust veil. Two more in the shadow of a wind-wall, sitting shoulder to shoulder because you have to sit close here to be heard. A child lying belly-down on warm slag, watching. No one hurries to greet you. Hurrying is for people who still have air to waste.

The people of the Table are not a different species, not yet. But a thousand years sunk in troughs has made them something lowlanders would not recognize as kin on sight.

Breathe beside them and you hear it. Every breath doesn't quite finish — yours or theirs. Their chests are broad, barrelled from childhood, ribs flared like a sifter's plates. Their resting pulse is slow, forty, thirty-five. They move the way the place runs: in deliberation. A mason lifts, sets, steps back to breathe. Lifts again. Conversation has long pauses built into it, not from thought but from lung. Newcomers off the slag-train try to talk fast and go grey around the mouth and have to sit. The old hands smile behind their veils and wait. They have time. A firing cycle is eleven days. A kiln has been warm fourteen years. What is a minute?

Their faces give them away. Eyes narrowed to slits even behind smoked glass, crow's feet etched white with salt. Lips permanently cracked and healed and cracked, darkened with oil. Skin cured like leather by sun thrown up from below as well as down — the pan throws it back at you, so an old Tablers chin and nostrils are sunburnt too, in places lowlanders never burn. They wear veils not for cold, not for custom, but for sun and salt: long bleached cloth wound twice around nose and mouth, leaving only the eyes. Underneath, the mouths are pale. They sweat and it vanishes before it beads, leaving salt rime in eyebrows, in the seams of clothes. They smell faintly metallic, like broken varnish. If you stay, you will too.

They do not shout. The thin air keeps your voice for yourself. So they have learned closeness. To speak, they step in until shoulders touch, veil to veil, and speak low. Among engine crews who work in the felt-hum that sits in the teeth, they speak even less — a system of touches on forearm, two fingers for slack, palm flat for hold. Across distance they don't bother with voice at all. Every town has its lamp code on the cable-masts, arc lamps burning all night too bright in thin air, blinked with shutters. Children learn lamp-talk before letters. Lovers will sit for an hour on opposite roofs flashing tin mirrors at each other, patient as sails.

They are maintenance people. There is nothing else to be here.

Below them is carved rock a thousand years old, doorways rounded by wind to soft mouths. Above that salt-brick and mud-brick, lime-washed blinding white, blind-walled to dust. Above that sheet iron and pipe on stone piers marching to horizon. The newer houses sit on timber sleds and iron rollers because the Table breathes — salt and gypsum heaving centimeters over decades — and will crack anything that insists on staying still. The older houses have given up staying still. Their mortar is thicker than their brick from centuries of crack and re-point, crack and re-point.

Nothing is thrown away. There is nowhere to throw it. A boiler husk becomes a wall. Truck tires stripped long ago for seals leave chassis to become herd shelters. A walking excavator kneels in dust outside every town, bucket down as if grazing, and it is grazing — sifters rub their plates bright against its steel shins, furnace-moths winter in its shadow, children play knucklebones in its track.

What do they eat, where nothing green grows and no cistern has held water in living memory? Mostly what the rail brings. The single track dead-straight across the salt flat dissolves in shimmer, and once a week or once a month the train condenses out of it, black and slow, bringing lowland grain, dried beans, oil in drums, and — more precious — water in riveted tank cars that are guarded like temples. Between trains they live tight. Breath is caught in condenser cloths at night. Piss is distilled. Gypsum is baked in the long kilns not only for plaster but for the ghost of water locked in its crystal, coaxed out drop by drop the way the pale cistern-lizards do it by chewing. Meat is rare and ritual: a culled sifter, sixty years old and blind with dust, shared across a whole trough in thin strips dried on hot iron. Moth-cakes — ground furnace-moths toasted with salt — are everyday food and newcomers pretend not to mind. Pipe-rat, lizard eggs. Everything organic the humans shed is eaten by something, and then, eventually, by them again.

Their work is tending. Not farming as lowlanders mean it, but farming heat and wind and stone.

The varnish-tenders are the oldest office. That dark varnish on sunward stones, brown-black-orange matte as leather, lithic bacteria and desert varnish — that is the only field. It grows back slower than you live. So the tenders walk circuits that take years, remembering which boulder was scraped, which ridge left fallow for the sifters. *Do not scrape a stone clean unless you need to.* Children are taught this with a slap as serious as a prayer. To break crust with your boot and smell that faint metallic smell is to smell bread baking.

And the sifters themselves. You thought them indifferent. They are, until you learn their speed.

Low, barrel-bodied lithovores the size of dogs and goats, armored in overlapping gypsum plates the color of dirty ivory, moving in loose herds of twenty or fifty across the pans. They plow with blunt snouts, rasping up varnish and salt, leaving behind neat pellets of clean crystal glittering like droppings of glass. That glass is harvested — salt to brick, crystal to trade. In afternoon heat they dig shallow scrapes and pile, twenty bodies high, plates ticking as they cool, dusty eyelids blinking in unison. They live sixty years. They know their trough. A herd-woman — usually an old woman too breath-short for kiln work, accompanied by silent grandchildren — will walk with the same herd for decades. She does not drive them. She walks at the speed this place actually runs at, the speed of stone learning to breathe, and they allow her to pick up the pellets behind them, to pull beetles from a sore foot, to sit among the pile at noon while the dry snow of gypsum patters down hissing on hot iron and vanishes. When one dies it mummifies where it falls, plates and snout intact for years, until wind and the thumb-big carrion beetles undo it grain by grain in a single night's cleaning. The herd stands around while it happens. The woman stands with them.

Above, the sails own the air the way sifters own the ground.

Three meters across, manta-shaped, translucent skin over cartilage so thin the sun shines through amber. For hours they circle without a wingbeat, mouths agape, straining dust and furnace ash — the towns feed the dust, the dust feeds them. At dusk they drop lower and you see the faint blue tracery along wing-edges: St. Elmo's static they use to sense each other and the storm fronts building silent over the far scarp. That prickling on the back of your neck before sheet-lightning flickers cloud to cloud with no thunder — they felt it three days ago. Sail-watchers, boys with ruined eyes from staring at sun, lie on roofs and read them like barometers. When sails go high and tight, dust-front coming. When they scatter, electric storm. When they spiral down to the slag heaps at the edge of town where ground stays warm all night, to lay their leathery eggs in the warmth — then it will be calm, and the kiln vents can be opened. The slag-wives guard those heaps, shooing away pipe-rats but leaving one clutch per sail, because a town without sails is a town blind.

Around any heat and light after dark: the furnace-moths. Copper-winged, hand-sized, swarming kiln vents and arc lamps in such numbers they sound like dry leaves shaken in a bag — one of the few loud sounds here. By day they cling under eaves in overlapping scales like rust. They feed on oil films, on skin flakes, on anything human. The engine-men leave a lamp on all night just for them, though it wastes power, because the pipe-rats come for the moths and the flat white cistern-lizards come for the rats, and the whole chain keeps vents clear of dust better than any filter. A clogged filter stops a fan. A stopped fan stops a mine. So the moths are swept, ground, eaten, tolerated, cursed, thanked.

And the small quick ones underfoot, too fast for the Table and therefore beloved: pipe-rats with naked pink heat-dumping tails nesting warm in cable insulation, chewing just enough to be a nuisance and not enough to kill; cistern-lizards, pale as salt, drinking nothing, making their water from the gypsum they chew; beetles that do the undertaking.

All abundant. All patient. Even death is slow, except when it is beetles.

Stay long enough and you stop seeing people and animals and machines as separate. A dead excavator is herd shelter. A living herd is a mine. A moth swarm is a filter. Ash is seed. Slag is womb. The towns sunk out of wind into troughs, layered rock and brick and rusting iron, humming low day and night through thick black cables, smoke rising perfectly vertical till two thousand meters — they are not settlements on a landscape. They are part of the crust's slow metabolism, growths of lime and iron where stone learned, briefly, to tend itself.

When the dry snow falls at noon, stand still as you were taught. Let the sifters pass. The woman with them will nod once behind her veil, not from friendliness but from recognition: another slow animal, learning not to shout, learning to walk to what looks close, learning not to dig for water.
