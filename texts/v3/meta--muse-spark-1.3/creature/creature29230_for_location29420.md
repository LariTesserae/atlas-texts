There is no one here.

That's the first thing you have to let go of. No tenants, no keepers, no small quick bodies in the thatch. No birds to cut the steam, no insects to stitch it, no frogs in the cisterns, no rats in the ribs. If you stand listening for an animal you will wait until the sweat runs into your eyes.

What lives here is the place.

Not in it. As it.

Start with the Towers, because they are the most like individuals. Nine, eleven of them at any given count, each a quarter-mile from the next, each the size of a hill-town keep, each standing alone in its own private weather. Don't think of them as trees that happen to look like buildings. Think of them as buildings that happen to be alive, and young — insultingly young.

Everything about them is new and keen. Fronds unroll with a waxy, almost plastic freshness. Thorns come out pale green and harden to black by afternoon. Their buttress roots, wide as houses, meet at angles so crisp they look folded, not grown. Bark peels off in papery curls that haven't had time to rot. Leaves bigger than doors overlap like shingles, each channeled down its spine to throw water clear.

They sweat. They have to. In this bath-hot air perched above the clouds, a still surface rots in hours. So the Towers weep continuously from every tip, every hanging root, every thinned window-membrane. The broad shallow stairs inside them sweat to keep from softening. The leaf-drums boom when rain sheets across them. The cistern-membranes slosh. They are not cooling themselves the way an animal cools itself. They are plumbing themselves. Leaf is roof and gutter. Stem is pipe. Hanging root is pump. The whole towering body is a fountain that decided to stand up.

And they are not still. If you watch for ten minutes you will stop thinking of them as structures at all.

A vine thick as your wrist will quest out in front of you, blind head swinging, then lock on across a gap and leaf out behind itself while the tip is still moving — you can hear fiber sliding on fiber, a slow green creak. Somewhere else a flower the size of a bathtub gorges on water until it can't hold and bursts with a wet pop, flinging helicopter seed-pods that root where they land on the wet mat within the hour, throwing up bright red feelers. Somewhere else a wall fails: an over-watered chamber softens, sags, tears with a long ripping sigh and falls in a steaming heap.

That is where the second inhabitant shows itself. The Mat.

The valley floor you walk on, ten feet thick of root-felt, warm as skin, springy, seeping water up around your boot with every step, is not ground. It is mouth and stomach. It cambers itself almost imperceptibly to shed water toward the living gutters at the rim, but it keeps enough to digest. Watch the fallen wall: within minutes white root-hairs rise out of the floor like frost and begin eating their sister. Fallen matter doesn't litter here. Your own footprints will fill with fine green hair within a day. Nothing stays dead. The Mat breathes everything back in.

The Towers and the Mat are not separate. They are organs with different jobs. The Towers lift and shed and hold huge, deliberate volumes of nothing — cool dim ribbed halls, moss floors so even they feel swept, niches holding rainwater like basins, oval sphincter-doors that dilate when your wet body approaches and close behind you. A city for no citizens, but not a ruin. Ruins are dead. This emptiness is balanced on purpose. Each Tower holds air, steam, echo. Shout, and the ribs return it to you three seconds later, leaf-filtered.

Between them, the transients live and die fast. The bridges. A Tower will spend enormous energy to throw a living aqueduct fifty yards through hot sideways rain to its neighbor — a great hollow vine sagging with its load, humming as water runs inside it — hold it for two days while water and noon-pollen shuttle across, and then let it rot through and drop it spinning into the cloud sea when the wind shifts. Grow fast, grow sharp, abandon without grief. That is the morality here.

Because reproduction is drowning. At noon the Towers exhale together, vast pale clouds of pollen that hang in the saturated air and then rain out with the afternoon downpour. Pollination by storm. Fertilization by drowning. No need for wings or tongues.

They sense you, after a fashion. Not as a person. As weather and weight and water. The door smells your humidity. The Mat under you warms to your weight. If you stand still long enough, curious blind tendrils will come up to test the edges of your boots — not hostile, not kind. Vegetal curiosity. They are checking if you, too, might be substrate. If you will stand still long enough to be plumbed for water, grown over, made into another brief, sharp, dripping room.

That is what lives here in the high hot saddle above the clouds: a single, sparse, steaming arcology that never stops making itself. Young, wet, loud with wood and water working, empty of everything but itself, and busy — at bazaar speed, at anthill speed — being its own builder, its own building, its own eater, its own child.
