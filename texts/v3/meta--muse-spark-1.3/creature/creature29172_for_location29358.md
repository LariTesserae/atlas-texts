At first you don't see it as alive.

Down there, crouched in the brightness with sweat running into your eyes, everything is mineral until proven otherwise. The amber at the edge of the pan reads as stain. Sulfur does that — yolk-yellow bleeding to rust. Silica does that — varnishing everything white. The dark green-black underneath reads as shadow, as wet rock. And there is an oily sheen on the film, the kind hot water leaves when it carries the faintest load of salts upward and drops them. You think: residue.

Then you stay still past the point of comfort, past the drip off your own chin, and the residue resolves.

It has an edge. Not the hard edge of crust but a soft, raised lip, a millimeter thick where the water thins to nothing on the cracked plate. Amber on top, translucent, like dried resin. Green-black beneath, opaque, like algae left in a gutter — except there is no gutter, no soil, nothing to rot. It holds only to one contour: the exact line where the pan is wet but not drowned, lit but not seared, warm but not scalded. An inch higher on the dry silica it flakes and is gone. An inch lower, under the standing water where the noon sun explodes, it thins and is gone. It lives only in the seam.

Touch it — if you touch it — and it doesn't smear like oil. It gives, then resists. A skin.

Under a hand lens, if you could hold a hand lens steady in that soup-air, the skin becomes a city.

Filaments, thousands of them, sheathed and ropey, woven over and under each other to make a felt. Rods packed between the filaments like rice in thatch. Spheres stacked in amber jelly. All of it dusted, even choked, with fresh glass: opaline marbles precipitating out of the water they just drank, settling on their sheaths. They are being entombed as you watch, micron by micron, and they don't mind. They grow up through it. The top layer dies sunburnt and becomes sunscreen for the layer below. That is the amber — carotenoid, scytonemin, dead cells turned to smoked glass to take the noon. Underneath, in the green-black, the living keep the light they want.

Because what it eats is light. Not like a plant eats light.

There are no leaves here, no roots, no mouth to drink with. There wasn't time for that, and there isn't room. This is older than that. This is pre-green. It does photosynthesis without bothering to become a plant — a bacterial version, humbler and tougher. In that blinding room where you squint, it has tuned itself to the trembling second sun thrown off the pan and the white bounce off the walls. It takes the diffuse edge, the light that has already been softened by silica and water. Too much sun would bleach it white; it knows how to go amber and wait. Too little would starve it; it stretches its pigments toward infrared, toward the sulfur yellows your eyes barely register. It doesn't need a clear sky, only that coin of noon and the rock's own glow.

What it drinks is steam.

Not water as you drink it. It drinks the cycle itself. The weep off the walls, the bead-and-run, the *plik* from the ceiling, the film that condenses on your own hands — it lives in all of that at once. The silica tubes and zeolite cages in the rock beneath it wick distilled water up by capillary pull, salt-free, perfect. The mat spreads its felt across that wick and siphons. When the sun moves and the pan breathes off a veil of steam, the mat breathes it in on its upper face. When the seep at the warmest corner brings up dissolved sulfur and iron and silica, it takes what it needs and leaves the rest as rings: sulfur dropped as yellow globules inside its cells, silica dropped outside as glass. It exhales oxygen in bubbles so small you never see them, only the faint oily rainbow where gas is trapped under jelly.

And it smells, faintly, if you put your face too close in that thick air. Not like rot. Like hot iron and clean steam with a sweet, faintly rotten-egg undertone — sulfur being breathed in and out.

It doesn't move. That is its whole decision.

The cricket that fell in days ago picks its way along the dry ledge above it, tasting salts, missing a leg, and the mat does nothing. The midges ride the updraft in the sun shaft and die and fall and dissolve, and the mat will take their nitrogen without thanks. The kangaroo rat that once left four toe-marks and a tail drag in fresh silica mud — now hardening to fossil — came to drink the distilled pan and left, and the mat closed over the disturbed edge in a day. It is walked on, drunk from, rained on, baked, half-buried in new crust that ticks as it cools. It endures because it is almost nothing: no vulnerable parts. Dry it to paper and it waits. Flood it ankle-deep and it floats its upper felt. Heat it to 45 and it photosynthesizes faster.

Scrape a flake and look at the underside and you see its memory. Laminations, paper-thin, white-green-white-amber: last week's steam, last season's flood, the day the slab fell in the corner and changed the shade for an afternoon. It is laying down rock as it lives. The silica flowers, those tiny sharp gypsum needles pushing out in the corners, find footing on its dead layers. In a hundred years, if the crack stays open, there will be a stromatolite rim, a bathtub ring of former edges marking where the pan used to sit. The room is making itself, and this is how — not just mineral ticking, but mineral *remembered* by skin.

That is who lives here. Not a visitor. Not the cricket, not the midges, not you with your braced hands and swimming afterimages who must pull yourself back up into the wind because the heat gets in your head. You, they, me — we borrow the distilled water and leave.

It stays. A thin, amber-topped, green-black-bellied felt, a few handspans long in total if you unwound all its patches, holding the exact wet edge of a bright furnace room under the desert. Doing, without any machine, without any plan, what the whole room does: catching light and water and holding them long enough to live.
