No one lives here.

That is the first and truest thing to say about it. Nothing nests, nothing roots, nothing burrows or buzzes or overwinters. No tuft in a crack, no orange lichen, no moss, no bird passing through to eat something smaller. Your lowland brain keeps looking for it — for the green smear, the movement — and keeps not finding it. The bowl is too high, too scoured, too burned. Freeze every night, even in summer. Ultraviolet at noon like a blade. Wind that takes everything loose and sharpens what remains. It is mineral only. That is its rule.

And yet it is crowded. That is the second thing, and it doesn't go away the longer you stay.

The residents are white, first.

Knee-high, waist-high, shoulder-high where the snow has drifted deepest, they stand shoulder to shoulder across the whole flat of the saddle, so close you have to turn sideways to move between them. Thin as hands, leaning all one way, a few degrees north. Toward the midday sun.

They are ice, firn snow carved by sun into knives. Penitentes. But up here they don't behave like weather. They behave like inhabitants. Like a herd, like a congregation, like plants would if plants could live here.

Because they do what living things do. They are born — not fallen, but grown upward out of the flat as the sun hollows the pits and spares the ridges, over and over. They feed — on light, white and total. They jostle for it, shading each other, throwing those hard-edged blue shadows three-deep across each other. They migrate — slowly south over the season, the whole crowd inching as the sun eats one side and the other side holds. They get sick and die — undercut at noon, you hear it, a sharp *tock*, and a tip snaps and falls into the pit to melt. They are furry with rime in the afternoon cloud and then go glassy and hard again by four. At sunset they go translucent, honey then rose, lit from inside on their west faces, and for a few minutes the whole bowl glows as if something were breathing under it.

Stay an hour and you stop seeing them as objects. They have posture. They lean. They crowd. They make that low reedy hum when wind threads through a thousand narrow slots between them, a pitch that changes when you tilt your head, as if they noticed you.

The second residents are gray.

Where the ice thins, on the slightly higher, drier gravel, there is a second crowd mirroring the first. Dozens, maybe a hundred of them. Fist-sized towers, five to nine flat stones high, warm gray granite glittering with quartz. Some fallen and rebuilt. Some joined at the base into little villages.

From a distance you mistake them for more ice blades. Up close they are the human echo of ice blades — people saw the crowded white teeth and answered with crowded gray teeth. Add, don't take. That seems to be the law. Every traveler who can make it this high, breath shallow after three steps, heart hammering on nothing, finds a flat stone that fits the hand and sets it carefully on top so it will survive the night's cracking. The cairns lean with frost-heave and are righted. They grow denser every year. They grow white beards of rime in the afternoon and shake them off by evening.

They are not alive, but they are tended like they are. Like gardens. Like flocks.

The third resident is not a thing at all. It is the business between things.

Sun eats ice. Ice shades stone. Stone holds night-cold into day. At two in the afternoon melt gathers in the pits, an inch of sky-reflecting water impossibly clear, plink-plink threading down through firn into gravel in rills a finger wide that run three meters and vanish. By four the cloud lifts off the valleys thousands of meters below, temperature drops ten degrees in twenty minutes, every drop skins over into black glass. By night everything ticks — ice contracting, pebbles clicking against pebbles somewhere underneath as frost jacks the stones a millimeter higher, some stood on end by no human hand into their own mute ranks. By morning the wind sharpens it all again.

No predator, no prey. Just sun and shadow doing the work that eating and being eaten do lower down. If you crouch and let your breathing slow you can hear the metabolism: the hum, the tick like cooling coals, the water percolating, the little red-and-blue cloth, bleached almost white, worrying its stick with a dry tick-tick-tick beside the low dry-stacked windbreak — three courses high, open to the east, peck marks still visible where someone knocked the slabs square with another rock.

And the fourth residents — if you can call them that — are the people who are not residents.

No one sleeps here. You understand that at once. The shelter is only enough to crouch behind. The offerings in the collapsed niche — the twist of copper wire, the wooden bead with a face worn featureless, the cloth tied to a short stick — have not rotted because nothing rots this high and cold. They just bleach and wait. People come, or came. They could not stay. They left a bead, a wire, a stone, and went back down, breathing hard in the thin bright air.

They come at late afternoon, mostly. Because that is when the bowl does the unusual thing that makes people stack stones here. The light doesn't just get golden. It gets trapped. Each blade throws a long blue shadow across three others, shadows so dense and overlapping you lose depth, pools that haven't frozen catch fire, granite flecks spark. It feels absurdly, undeniably crowded with presence — though you are alone, though nothing lives.

Then the sun clears the rim and it ends. White goes blue-white, then gray. The hum stops. Your fingers go stupid even in gloves.

You add your stone. You set it carefully. You leave the white crowd and the gray crowd to their own quiet business of freezing and melting and sharpening themselves, lifeless and utterly busy, in the violet-blue cold.
