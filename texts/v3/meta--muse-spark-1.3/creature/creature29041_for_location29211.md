You will think at first that nothing lives here.

That is what it wants you to think. Not from cunning. From completeness. The green is so entire, leaf on leaf on leaf with no gap to slip a finger through, that there is no place left for an animal to be. You walk on root as thick as your thigh and assume you are walking on ground. You duck under a vine as thick as your body and assume it is a thing. You hear only the cicada winding and unwinding its one rusted spring far back in the tree-fern crowns to the north, and the *plink... plink...* of leaf-drip into the warm stone pans, and you think: plant place. Empty of anyone.

Lie down a while. In one of the domes, if you can find the dry middle.

That is the first hint. In a bowl where everything sweats — where the underside of every leaf is beaded, where your shirt never unsticks, where the rock under the roots sweats — there are four or five low domes of living vine, bent and re-bent and fused with no cut end, and in the middle of the moss floor it is dry. Truly dry. Dust-dry. The only dry in the valley.

Something needed dry.

Not much dry. Just a body-sized patch. Just enough to keep from melting.

Because here, anything that lies wet and still for long is taken. You have seen it: a dead leaf — rare — lets go, falls three inches, is caught by another leaf and by morning is laced through with white fungal thread and by noon is gone, melted back into the mat. No drifts of yellow. No rot piles. No seedlings pushing up because there is no room and no need. Branch rubbing branch has fused bark-to-bark, fern trunk to fig root, root to root, until the whole bowl is one valley-wide held thing. It digests the dead immediately to stay exactly as it is.

To live here and stay *yourself*, separate, you have to stay dry at the center.

They do.

You won't see them move. That is the second hint, and it takes longer. You will have walked past them two or three times before you understand that the square-shouldered bulge in the strangler fig is not quite a boulder. That the low moss hummock beside the stone pan is breathing, very slowly, in time with the thickening air.

They are low. Broad. Knee-high at most when they fold — which is almost always. There is no other shape to be in a place three or four canopies deep where tree-ferns meet overhead to make a second sky. They go on all fours but not like animals; they spread, they settle. Their skin, where you can see it under the coat, is cool-pale and damp-smooth, like the flesh of a root elbow just peeled, like the inside of a bromeliad cup before the black tea water fills it. Over it they wear the bowl: moss grown thick across shoulders and back, tiny white orchids rooting directly in the fracture lines of their knuckles the way they root in the sheer south wall, a lace of liverwort at the throat. Not camouflage. Accumulation. If you lie still long enough here, things grow on you. They have lain still for a very long time.

They have no eyes to speak of. Not blind, just shut. In held noon, under green-filtered light that never lengthens shadows, there is nothing to look for. They find their way by wetness. By the difference between beaded and dripping. By sound — that fat drop from leaf tip to leaf tip to pool that falls near your ear like a small bell. They turn their faces, eyeless and calm, toward *plink... plink...* and go.

And they go so slowly you forgave yourself for missing them.

This is how a vine dome is made without tools, without cut ends, without rope or metal. You do not bend it. You lean against a young creeper where it drops from the rim, in the hot trapped air, and you stay. A day. A season — though there are no seasons, only noon. Your cool weight makes it sweat, makes it turn fractionally toward the dry warmth of your moss back. It thickens around your shape. Another creeper crosses it and, rubbing in the heat-tick-creak of afternoon that never ends, fuses bark-to-bark. You shift a few inches in sleep and the whole green elbow follows. Over years, it arches. Over longer, it closes. What looks built was slept into being.

That is what they do in the center maze where the figs have grown together into hollow domes: sleep, and by sleeping hold the shape open. While they sleep curled on the dry moss heart, the white fungal threads that melt everything else cannot take them. Dry is a decision. *I am still distinct*, the dry says. *Not yet mat.*

When they wake — if waking is the word for the slow inflation that moves across them — they tend.

Not gardening as you know it. No planting, no weeding. Maintenance of stillness. That is the rule of the bowl and they are its keepers.

One will cross the fern terraces, an inch at a time, trailing its belly moss, and pause where pale unsorted dust has sifted into a crack between new pink-white limestone blocks. The blocks must not shift. The dust must not wash, must not become soil. With a soft forelimb, boneless-seeming, it presses the dust back down and breathes on it — a cool, mineral breath — until rootlets tighten over it. Elsewhere, another gathers fresh rock fragments that have spalled off the raw south wall. One by one, in its mouth — toothless, green-gummed — it carries them to the cairn inside the second dome and sets them down. Always fresh, never weathered, because nothing is allowed to weather here. Lichen is already taking the undersides before it finishes, because it works that slowly. It will turn each stone again tomorrow that is also today.

Another tends water. This is not a swamp; water lives only in hints and must be kept hinted. It moves from bromeliad to bromeliad on the central figs, dipping its long, furred knuckles into the black cups to stir the wriggling larvae, to drink without emptying. It licks condensation from root elbows. It sips leaf-drip from the shallow stone pans, warm as blood and filmed with pollen, leaving always a few inches. Enough to bead again. Drip, gather, fall, rise. It does not want flow. Flow would carve. Flow would break the rim and end the bowl. So it keeps the cycle small, mouth-sized, body-sized.

That is all. It is enough to fill a life that has no age because nothing here has age.

You will not see their faces, really. You will see, if you are lucky or patient, a skink like a dropped jewel dart across a sunlit root — a visitor, too quick, too bright to belong — and freeze beside what you thought was root, and then you will see the root has a slow pulse. You will see the moss on its back lift a millimeter as trapped hot air presses down. You will hear, very close, the papery rustle you thought was a dead leaf letting go, and realize it was a tenders' sigh, sweet and green-rotted, as it settled deeper into the mat without merging.

High above, a swift cuts across the circle of white sky and does not descend. It knows. To descend is to be included.

At midday — which is always — if you lie in the dome and look up through layer after layer of translucent green to white, beaded with water that never quite falls on you, crossed here and there by a raw unweathered edge of stone the green has politely gone around, you may feel the dome itself tighten fractionally in heat. A vine creaks. A drop lands near your ear.

Beside you, in the dry, something cool shifts its weight and keeps you dry too, for a little while, so you will not be absorbed yet.

Nothing proceeds. That is their work.
