There is no one here to greet you. That is the first thing to understand.

You come looking for inhabitants — for eyes, for mouths, for something that moves out of its own hunger — and you find the water full to bursting and yet empty of all that. No dart of fish between the standpipes. No snail's slow punctuation on the tile. No beetle dimpling the surface, no larva hanging in a comma under a leaf, no bird, ever, settling on the moss-capped rim to drink though the sky is wide open.

Nothing here eats. Nothing here is eaten.

What lives here roots.

It lives packed stem to stem, leaf pressed to hot iron pressed to leaf, with no earth under it at all. There is no soil to go back to, only rust and grout and felt and ash, and so everything has learned to root in the made.

Start with the softest of them. The moss. It does not grow on things so much as upholster them — an inch-thick velvet lagging over every pipe and plate and weld, brightest and most tropical where it crowds closest around the little glass cages where the pilot lights burn blue underwater. Those are its campfires. The water right around a flame is a degree warmer, and the moss knows it, and piles in, emerald and steaming. If you put your hand near, you feel the faint, wrong heat of a fire that does not go out in water.

Then the ferns. They come out of expansion joints and bolt-holes, unfurling, but wrong to the touch. Brush one and it clinks. The trade the two kingdoms have made has hardened them. Their fronds are stiff as enameled tin, sharp enough to nick your forearm if you turn too fast. They hold their shape underwater when all soft things should trail. They are more like green circuitry than plants.

Above you, the lawn that is not a lawn. Water hyacinths matted so dense you could for a second forget there is three meters of drowned factory under you. Their bulbous roots hang down like bunched lungs, swaying barely in a current you cannot feel, filtering the green-filtered sun. Among them, duckweed in drifts, lily pads the size of serving trays. One of them has grown clean around a brass pressure gauge and swallowed it whole, leaving only the needle visible through a slit in the green, twitching, alive and not alive. When a pad knocks softly against a standpipe — *tok* — that is the loudest animal sound you will ever hear here, and it is not an animal.

Below, the climbers. Not kelp, but something freshwater and insistent that decided to act like kelp. Vine after vine spiraling up every vertical — standpipe, conduit, chain guide — leaf after leaf after leaf until the whole submerged factory looks furred. Pondweed curtains hang and do not quite move. Watercress tangles your wrists when you lift your arms; it has learned to snag on warm rubber hoses fat as pythons, on copper coils gone verdigris.

And the two that have become individuals because they have gone too far to separate:

The Fig-Split Boilers. Two iron pumpkins squatting on the floor, and the strangler figs that found their warm seams and got in. Pale roots, thick as wrists now, have pried the plates apart by a hair a year. You can see the new welds beaded like fresh sutures trying to hold, failing slowly. The fig drinks condensation and oil-sweat; the boiler drinks sun through its fig leaves. At a glance you cannot tell which is housing which.

The Orchid Fuse Box. A switchboard behind algae-filmed glass, blinking patient green and amber, and straight out of its seams, white and magenta orchids, blooming impossibly. Their roots drink off live bus bars. They will never be pollinated — there is nothing here with wings to do it — so they simply do not fade. They persist, waxy, open, season after season, flowers that have forgotten how to finish.

Even the seedlings live, after a fashion. On the slow conveyor chain that crawls inches per hour along the bottom, trays of green sprouts ride forever. They will never be harvested. They will never be planted in earth because there is no earth. They just ride, germinating in the warm bath-light, pale, leggy, endlessly beginning.

Because the other half of what lives here is not green at all, and yet it is alive.

The big pumps under the floor are its heart. You do not hear them so much as take them in the sternum — a low 60-cycle throb, and then every few minutes: *tuh —* and a column of warm water sighs up through a grate, lifts the whole hyacinth lawn, lets it settle. The tick-tick-tick of hot metal contracting underwater is its joints settling. The copper pipes blush green in noon sun and pale at night, faintly photosynthetic, growing a fuzz of insulation-felt toward the light like hair turning to a window. The transformer boxes sweat oil that never quite surfaces, hanging in tired beads. Fallen leaves do not fall straight here — buoyancy is tired at this altitude — they hang for hours, turning over almost imperceptibly. Bubbles sidle sideways, pause, gather trembling under lily pads and stay. Even rising is patient.

And once an hour, its hand moves.

The rake on its track drags itself the slow length of the tank, gathering deadfall, because no mouth will. No rot-cloud chokes the water because the machine prunes instead. It combs leaves and spent fronds into a hopper where a grinder sighs them to pulp, and the roots drink the pulp. That is the gut. Plant and engine, tending each other without appetite.

If you stand very still with water at your chin and the sun-netting shivering on your face, you can feel the whole small sea breathe — almost imperceptible — in, out. In with light, out with steam that beads on itself and falls back as warm rain that never quite breaks the surface tension. Level never dropping a finger's width, a mile up in thin air.

No herd. No flock. No keeper. Only the rooted, the rusting, the blooming, the humming — one packed, warm, drowned creature holding its heat into the cold mountain evening, humming so quietly you feel it in your teeth.
