Who lives here did not conquer it. They negotiated with it, a long time ago, and have kept the terms ever since.

You see them first as slowness along the causeways. A figure in a heavy undyed wool cloak, hood up though it is midday. An old woman with a basket of turnips held close to her hip. A boy leading a pale goat by a twisted wool rope, not pulling, just walking so the goat walks with him. A man crouched at a terrace wall, turning stones over in his hands until he finds the one that was always meant to fit. They do not hurry. In this thin air you cannot hurry for long. Your heart hammers if you try; theirs do not, anymore.

They are built for the shelf.

Short, broad-chested, wide-footed on clinker. Hands broad and cut-knuckled, fingertips permanently roughened from stone and hide and barley awns. Faces weathered smooth rather than lined — the cold preserves — cheeks reddened high up, eyes narrowed against the white even light. Hair left long and greased with lanolin against the dew, men and women both, often with a little copper pin at the temple, hammered thin and dull green at the edges. That is the only metal you will see on them. They smell the way the town smells: damp wool, woodsmoke, cut green, warm animal.

They move the way water moves here: only where it belongs, and only so much.

You will not hear them coming. Their boots are soft-hide over felt, soles worn to the shape of slate. Their voices, when they use them, are low and close. There is no shouting across a plaza because there is no need to be heard across a plaza. If something needs saying at distance, they walk over and say it. Most of what needs saying is said with the hands anyway — a tap of the hoe on stone meaning *here, damp*, a brush of palm on a wall meaning *loose, mend*. The children learn early to keep their sounds small. Not from scolding. From listening. On the shelf a loud sound has nowhere to go. The thin air takes it at once, and the rock gives nothing back. So they stopped making them.

They are not few, and they are not many. That is the point.

Count the houses and you will overcount the people, because between every two houses is a garden, or a swept yard, or bare rock deliberately left alone. A household is not just the roof. It is the roof and the moss-hollow it tends and the length of rill it keeps clear and the cistern-barrel under its eave and the six swallows under that eave and the two goats that sleep against its south wall because that wall holds the day's heat longest. The dog curled chin-on-paws is not a stray. He belongs to that negotiation. He watches sheep he will never chase. He was bred — or bred himself, over uncounted unchanging generations — to not need to bark.

Nothing here is owned tight, and nothing is wild. The sheep move between garden walls on paths they have made as much as people have. Small, dark, patient, their wool dense and oily. They do not bleat because there is no distance to call across and no wolf to warn of. The goats, pale as gravel, pick their way on the upper rocks and come down at evening on their own. The marmots fatten on terrace edges and sit up to watch you pass, unafraid because no one has startled them in longer than they can remember. Rosy finches, choughs, the mottled ptarmigan — they nest in roofbeams and wall-caps as if roofs were cliffs that happened to be warm. Under the moss, beetles. In the sun-cracks, lizards. Over the cisterns at dusk, a brief cloud of insects, exactly enough, no more.

And under and around all of them, the green citizens, who are perhaps the truest residents.

Moss deep as your fist, emerald and rust. Ferns wedged in every wall foot. Barley and beans and turnips with leaves broad and beaded with morning dew. Silver-backed alpine herbs. Lichen, handspan-wide on the north side of the meeting house, that has been handspan-wide for as long as anyone can hold in mind. None of it spreads. That is the work. Every Keeper spends part of every day on their knees at the edge where green meets stone, pinching back what would creep, tucking back what would spill, keeping the velvet patch exactly velvet and exactly there. Not from fear. From manners. In a place where water lives only in pockets — the seep under the north ridge that fills three linked cisterns and no more, the basalt pans that cup sky-milk after night, the dew that must be caught in barrels set precisely under eaves — green is a promise to dampness. You do not make that promise where you cannot keep it.

Their hands know dampness the way you know faces.

This hollow always. That one never. This wall foot weeps till noon. That pan dries by mid-morning, leave it. The boy with the goat knows without being told where to lead it to drink and where not to waste steps. The old woman knows which turnips to pull and which to leave so the row looks tomorrow exactly as it looked today, lush but not overgrown. Harvest here is not taking. It is thinning to stasis. Eat, and let regrow to the same. Mend, and let wear to the same. Sweep the causeway, and let dust settle to the same.

That is why nothing is being finished. The low outer wall around the gardens looks unfinished to your eye because you expect walls to close. Theirs was never meant to close. It marks: this far, and no farther. Beyond it, grass and bare rock go on being grass and bare rock. The longhouse big enough for a hundred rarely holds a hundred at once. It holds twenty mending nets, ten shelling beans, children asleep in wool piles while rain-that-is-not-rain patters on sod. The communal ovens fire one at a time — that single distant woodsmoke you smelled — never all, because one is enough to keep.

They build as their grandparents built, not from reverence but from equilibrium. Adze on beam. Chisel on doorstone. Hide and twisted wool for lashing. Dull copper for pins. No forge, no slag, no straight sawn line, because straight sawn lines want more straight sawn lines. A post-and-lintel house, roof weighted with sod and thin split shingles, stone feet under the granary to keep it dry — once you have solved staying dry and staying warm and staying fed on a cold high shelf, you stop solving. You maintain the solution.

Ask them how long they have been here and they will misunderstand the question. Time here is not deep. It is wide and shallow, like the pans. Morning dew, noon dry, evening chill. Turnip season, barley season, lamb season that is never quite lamb season because the flock is never larger nor smaller. A shutter hangs half-open. A day later it hangs half-open in exactly the same way, not because no one touches it but because someone closes it against night wind and opens it to morning light in exactly the same gesture, every day. If you left for a lifetime and returned, the same barrel would be under the same eave catching the same drip, because barrels rot elsewhere, but here the cold and the care keep the timber from rotting, and when at last a stave does soften, it is replaced with a stave cut and adzed to the same shape, and the drip goes on.

People are born here. People die here. Rarely. Exactly often enough.

There are children, but never a crowd of them. There are old, but the old do not wither so much as weather smooth. The boy leading the goat will one day mend a wall. The man mending a wall once led a goat. The old woman carrying turnips has carried turnips in that basket along that causeway for longer than your visit can measure, and when she cannot, another will, with the same basket. They do not mourn loudly or celebrate loudly. Both would be a spike in a flat line. They mark continuance quietly: a new lashing, a swept path, a shared oven loaf broken in the longhouse without counting pieces because there are always enough pieces.

If there is a god on the shelf, it is Staying.

Not staying still as in stuck. Staying as in *staying with*. Staying with the seep that gives only so much. Staying with the moss that asks only so much. Staying with the sheep and the swallows and the beetles and the neighbor whose house does not crowd yours. Staying with young sharp rock and refusing to wear it round. They could terrace farther down the fall. They do not. They could dam the seep to get more. They do not. More would be a change, and change would be a debt, and the shelf is too high and too thin and too precisely damp to carry debt.

So they keep. That is the verb they live inside.

They keep the rill clear. They keep the wall kept. They keep the green cut like a knife at the stone edge. They keep the quiet so complete you can hear wool brush on wood. They keep each other, loosely, the way lichen holds stone — present everywhere, dense nowhere — until the town and the flock and the moss and the people are not separate things but one wide, low, breathing equilibrium, freshly made and never made new again, entirely decided to remain itself.
