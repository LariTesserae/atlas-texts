They live shoulder to shoulder, because there is nowhere else to live.

At first you think it is only the birds. That is what the bowl wants you to think. From the col the white on the cliffs reads as snow, and then it moves.

Cliff-doves, thousands of them, and the fat snow-auklets wedged between. They are not pretty up close. They are workmanlike: slate-grey and white, beaks gone translucent with cold like fingernails, feet a raw gum-pink that should not survive the ice but does. They sit so tight their breasts touch. They jostle without rancor, a slow feathered tide — lean in, lean out, shuffle a half-step when a neighbor lands. When a whole ledge lifts at once there is no call, no coo. They have given that up. It is only air through feathers, a papery whirr inches from your face, and then the soft hail of them landing again on another ledge, on a roof, on your shoulder if you stand still long enough. You are only the nearest warm ledge.

Their droppings whitewash everything. It feeds everything. That is the bottom of the circle.

In the pans below them the water seethes with what the droppings feed. Red ice-worms, as thick as bootlaces, knotted together in black water under a skin of ice you have to break with a knuckle. They come up for the guano and the drowned midges. The midges themselves are in clouds so dense they bend the vicious light — pale, almost transparent things that live for a day and die on the updraft and plaster the wet stone. The doves eat them by the beakful. The blind white loaches, eyeless as thumbs, nose through the milky rock-flour silt for worm eggs and algae. And along every seep-line where the water is richest, the snow-algae spreads like spilled paint, blood-red and sulphur-orange. It looks artificial until you see the midges grazing it, and the worms grazing what the midges leave.

Then the mammals. Then the people, who are mammals in the same way.

The goats first, because you hear them before you place them — not a bleat, but a hoarse little cough, a whisper-bleat. They have learned quiet too. They are no higher than your knee, shaggy to the ground with hair matted with ice and droppings, yellow teeth bared to scrape hair-lichen off stone. They belong to no one and everyone; they wander the peat sponge, the narrow slab streets, they fall into cisterns and have to be hauled out by two men swearing in hand-sign. Their milk is thin and iron-tasting. Their meat is saved for the dead of winter. Mostly they are kept for warmth — at night they breathe under the bench — and for dung, which is the only fire that will catch up here.

White voles run the walls like stitches. Long-tailed, pink-eyed, fast as drips. They steal crumbs of lichen-cake, bird down, goat afterbirth. The children do not chase them. They set little bone traps and eat them too.

And then, nested in among birds and goats as if they were another colonial species — the people.

Three hundred of them, perhaps, in a space built for thirty. You mistook their houses for rock on the way down and that was not a mistake. They are rock, rearranged. Short, broad-chested people, built low for carrying slate up ladders on their backs. Their lungs are huge in narrow ribs; you can hear it in the shallow, careful way they breathe. Their eyes are permanently narrowed to slits against the glare, webbed with fine lines from squinting, lashes white with frost by mid-morning. Their hands are the realest part of them: split-knuckled, swollen, wooden with cold even inside mittens of bird-skin and goat hair, but flickering impossibly fast. Because they do not shout. They cannot. Throats bleed if you shout in this air.

So they sign. A whole market conducted ankle-deep in icy meltwater in the thaw-pan plaza without a raised voice. *Egg. Careful, thin ice. Move, please. Two handfuls of worms for a palm of salt.* An old woman climbs past you with a slate on her back, nods with her chin. A child runs with a basket of red worms still writhing, stops to trade three for a mouthful of hot broth drunk through cloth. Two men waist-deep in a cistern pass up bucket after bucket of milky water, bail it, channel it under flat slabs to keep the street from becoming a stream. Above them a man re-lays a wall that slumped overnight — the whole town is built on ice-cored moraine and leans, drains, reappears under someone else's floor — he does not hammer. No one hammers after sun-up. Stone is set, not struck. He fits, lifts, fits again.

They wear what the bowl gives. Parkas sewn from dove skins with the feathers turned in, so that at night when you cram shoulder to shoulder in a low beehive hut you cannot tell where thatch ends and feathers begin, because the doves nest in the houses too, rustling above your head, dropping warmth and lice and the occasional egg into your lap. Goat-wool leggings soaked to the knee. Antler-hafted knives worn to slivers — a traded blade is a grandfather, saved for decades. Bone hooks for loaches. Sinew nets for doves. Hand-looms clicking softly, knucklebones clicking softly. Everything mended.

They eat the circle. Eggs taken one from every three so the ledge does not empty. Old birds too fat to fly far, snared and stripped. Worms boiled to a red broth. Loaches sucked whole. Lichen cakes dried on racks that jut from every wall until the racks touch. Goat, rarely. And they return to it — scraps for the gulls and voles, droppings for the pans, breath for the frost.

They move constantly because if they stop the bowl buries them. Bail, carry stone, prop, re-lay, trade. It looks frantic from above, an anthill kicked open. Up close it is gentle and economical. No wasted motion. No wasted sound. Only the small close noises they allow: the tick of drips from a thousand arm-thick icicles, the soft shatter when one gives way, the suck of boots in peat, the low groan of settling rock under your feet, your own blood loud in your ears.

At night they pack in — eight, ten to a hut — goats under the bench, doves in the thatch, bodies sharing heat because dung fire gives little. And then, as the walls warm, you hear what the old ones warned you about. A faint murmuring off the stones. Drip, tick, sigh. The day's lost sentences, they say, frozen outside in the bright cold and only now thawing. Whether you believe it or not, you find yourself whispering less and less, signing more, until a dove landing soft as hail on your shoulder feels no stranger than a neighbor's hand.
