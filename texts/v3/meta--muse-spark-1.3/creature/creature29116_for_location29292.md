At first you don't see anyone.

You see pattern, and then the pattern is wrong. Moss does not arrange itself flank to flank in neat black lozenges. Peat does not pulse. That knuckle of what you took for wet bark opens a jet eye and closes it again, too slow to be a blink.

Then you stop trying to see distance at all, you crouch down where your knees will dent and slowly exhale back, and close gets closer — and it is crowded.

They live piled.

Not scattered, not each on its own stone. Piled is the way of it here. In every moss hollow, in every cup between roots, the black alpine salamanders lie with their sides touching. Fat, slow, glossed with seep, dozens to the square foot. One breathes and the one pressed against it has to shift to make room for the breath. Their skins are the wettest things here, wetter than the leaves, wetter than the stone cups, beaded and re-beaded with hanging water that never quite runs off. They do not move away. When you breathe too close a whole hollow sighs and resettles, a rearrangement of flanks, and then stops again.

Between and under them, the others. Newts the color of wet bark coiled around each other, tail over back over belly, for a warmth that isn't warmth. Toads no bigger than your thumbnail knuckle, throats going — in, out, in, out — but making no call. That is how they live here: throat pulsing with no croak coming. It should be deafening, this many amphibians in a garden-sized bowl. It is almost silent.

In the water-cups they live thickest of all. Each pocked hollow in the old rounded rock holds its dark mirror, and in the mirror, tadpoles. Black commas so dense the water looks solid until something makes it shiver all at once, a whole cup flinching. They graze and hang and do not grow out of proportion. Nothing drains away so nothing is washed away. The cup feeds the channel feeds the cup. Near-surface, under-surface, in-surface. They live inside the cycle, not downstream of it.

On the walls they live slower. Snails with shells so thin they are almost clear — you can see the dark smudge of the body through them — moving a millimeter an hour up the weeping rock, leaving silver trails that never dry because the air itself is wet. You hear them, if you hold still long enough for your own blood to quiet: not the move, the extension. The softest *sill* as the foot lets go.

Over them, they live walking. Midges that do not swarm hang low in the still air, landing and lifting a finger's width. Moths — pale, high-altitude moths whose wings got too wet generations ago to bother with flying — walk, absurd and patient, over the backs of salamanders, over fern fronds no longer than your thumb, over toad heads. Antennae waving. A beetle crosses a toad's head and the toad does not snap. It pulses its throat and waits for the feet to pass. A worm surfaces between two salamanders, pink in black, and none of them turn. In the moss, springtails pop like sparks when your breath touches.

They live touching. There is no bare ground — that is the first way — so there is no empty place to be alone. Moss on rock on moss on peat on root, cushions two, three deep, swallowing sound, and on and in every layer a body. Lichen crusts the upper faces where the drip is lightest, sage and ash-white and sulfur-pale, and under the overhang where it is darkest the white threads of fungi lace everything together, holding the sponge of rotted leaves and liverwort and black peat in a mycelial net. From those threads rise tiny translucent caps, wet as eyeballs. They too are lived on, lived under. Everything is upholstered and everything upholstered is occupied.

And because no one calls, they live by breath. When you kneel long enough your own breathing slows down to meet it, you feel it: hundreds of small sides going in and out, a low collective wet breathing like wind in a distant pine, but it isn't wind. The air here does not move. The cloud sits. They warm it in patches just by being so many together, despite the high-shadow cool that makes your breath faintly visible and your fingertips go slow. Musky and sweet, animal under the smell of wet stone and crushed green. You can smell where a hollow is most crowded before you see it.

They live without building, but not without using.

By the largest pool, on a flat anvil stone polished hollow by ten thousand years of drips, lie empty snail shells all broken on the same edge, in the same way. While you watch, the dark, almost flightless thing like a thrush hops down, picks up a living clear-shell in its beak, and knocks it left-right-left against the stone until it cracks. It eats and leaves the hammered shells for the next. In the pools, the caddis larvae live in mobile houses they drag as they graze — perfect tubes of sand grains and spruce needles and tiny fragments of shell, built to the body, abandoned when outgrown. Under a lip of rock, the shrew lives impossibly dense-furred, nose constantly working, and it has wedged a sharp flake of quartz in a crack to wedge open beetles; the flake is stained, worn on one side from little not-hands guiding. Nothing made, nothing kept. Hand-tools in the moment, then dropped. That is how long they have lived here without leaving a monument.

Which is the strangest part of how they live: they stay.

A drip falls from the overhang into the same cup it has always fallen into. The cup is not getting deeper. The moss at its lip is not getting beaten back. The salamander in the hollow below it has not moved, except to breathe. The moth walking across the fern has been walking across the fern. You watch cold seep into your knees through the sponge-ground, water rising up around your fingers without you pressing hard, and you realize no succession is happening here. No colonizing, no dying back, no flowing downhill. They live weathered into a final shape and then held — packed, quiet, wet, breathing together in the dark up high where the world folded into a small saturated room and decided not to leave.
