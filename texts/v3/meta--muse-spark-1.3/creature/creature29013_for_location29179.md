At first you think nothing lives here.

That is the first impression, and it's wrong in the particular way high places make you wrong. No birdsong. No hum. No scuttle under leaf. Only water negotiating with itself, and heat ticking in pipe. You stand on the grate and you feel watched by green but not inhabited. Like a greenhouse after hours.

Then a white fleck lifts off the mist-net.

It is so small you mistake it for mist at first. Then another. And another. Until you see the net is fretted with them.

The moths.

They are the smallest possible residents, and the most numerous. White, mealy, no bigger than an eyelash, with wings that never fully dry. They live their entire lives in the black mesh on the windward side. They are born there, they mate there, they die there. The females glue their eggs directly to the damp filaments, in the beads of caught cloud.

They do not know lowland, or night-flower, or lamp. Their world is vertical fog. They feed — if they feed at all as adults — on algae-slick and the honeyed dust of orchid root. Their larvae are translucent green threads that graze the mesh clean, spinning tiny silk anchors to keep from being washed into the gutter in the morning rush. Most fail. The gutters run white with them some days, a living silt carried down to the settling tanks.

If you put your face close to the net your breath stirs a brief snow. It settles again almost immediately. They do not stray far. There is nowhere else for them this high up, nothing but falling air below the parapet. Their whole evolution has collapsed inward to this one damp curtain. They clog things. Drowned bodies felt the float valves. Living bodies fur the sensors. Without something to eat them, the machine would choke on moth.

Which is why there are frogs.

You hear them long before you believe in them. A single metallic *pink* — not a croak, a struck wire — from inside an iron box that should contain only wires. Lift the lid, and the heat breathes out at you, and there in an inch of seep are three black coals with eyes.

They are thumbnail-small. Black as wet iron, black as valve grease, with bellies the dull orange of rust and throats that pulse white when they call. Not camouflaged for rock or leaf. Camouflaged for machinery. In a shadowed junction box, in a moss-lined inspection pit, they disappear completely until they blink.

There must have been hundreds brought up once — tadpoles sloshing in a bucket alongside taro corms, or eggs stuck to fern root. No one remembers now. No one needs to. They have made the poured place their swamp.

Each frog holds a territory no larger than your two hands: one valve box, one weeping pipe joint, one damp corner where a terrace lip overflows. By day they sit in warm seep with their chins just out of water, absorbing heat through their skins. They are tropical animals living in a tropical pocket carried up into the cold like a coal in a closed fist. Outside the steel door, sleet. Inside, 80 degrees and spawning weather.

At dusk they hunt. They do not leap so much as climb, with toe-pads made for wet concrete. They pick moth-drowned bodies off float arms. They stalk live moths where the mesh sags nearest a pipe. You can watch one for an hour make the same circuit: up the copper tick-tick-tick, pause where condensation gathers, tongue flick, down to the water skin to rinse, back up. They learn the pattern of the pumps. When the small electric kick-on vibrates through the slab, they go still and wait for the moths to startle up.

At night, when steam is thickest and the bowl has vanished into cloud, they talk. Box to box to box. *Pink.* A pause. *Pink-pink* from three terraces down. A sparse circuitry of sound, the only language the place has learned for itself. It is how males space themselves, how females find the warmest seep. If a pump fails and a terrace goes stagnant and cold, its frogs go silent first. The crew — whoever they are, wherever they sleep below the cloud — learned to listen for that. Silence means a float is choked. Silence means climb up.

Tadpoles live everywhere water stands still long enough: in the zinc gutters, in the settling tanks, in the footprint left by a boot in moss that filled with warm seep. They are black commas, grazing algae off concrete. They grow fast in the heat. In two weeks a front leg. In three, a frog the size of a fly, climbing out onto felt pocket and eating its first moth.

They are the only tenders. That is not poetry. It is maintenance. Without them the moths would felt the valves shut, the valves would stick, water would find the low edge and go down, and the whole rule — RETURN IT ALL — would break.

And then the green. Because if the frogs tend the machine, the green *is* the machine's other half.

The moss came first. It felts every north-facing surface an inch thick, deadening the pump-purr to a heartbeat. It holds water the way a towel holds it, and then releases it slowly when the nets run thin at midday. If you press your ear to it you hear ticking water inside.

The ferns live in the wounds: expansion joints, cracks where lime weeps white, the damp sand of footings not yet poured. They uncurl a new frond every few days in this endless summer, black stems, green lace, spores dusting the wet concrete below them with rust-colored powder. Their roots pry, very gently. They are beginning, in a hundred hairline places, the slow carving the builders fear. But slowly. Politely, for now.

The taro stand in the shallows like a crowd of shields. Four inches of root-sweet, tea-colored water, warm from below — perfect. Their corms wedge between terrace flags, swelling, splitting weak mortar with quiet strength. Their leaves are big enough to collect condensation on their own and funnel it to their own bases. Under each leaf, in its shade, tadpoles hide from the noon flash. Their dead leaves fall and rot and sweeten the water further, tea to broth.

The vines are the opportunists, the climbers, the ones who found the heat. Philodendron with leaves like hearts gone huge and split. Pothos gone wild, reverting to monstrous adult form, leaves fenestrated and climbing twenty feet up scaffolding. And the orchid — white aerial roots hanging like wire, clinging to boiler lagging, to damp insulation, to copper gone green. One enterprising stem has wrapped a steam bleed and now breathes with it. When the leak puffs, the leaves around it shiver. Roots crowd toward hot pipe the way roots elsewhere crowd toward sun. Follow any pipe and the growth doubles, triples. The plant map of the bowl is really a heat map.

They are not decoration. They are plumbing. Leaves slow evaporation and then return it as drip. Aerial roots comb mist out of air the nets miss. Felt pockets transpire all afternoon, feeding the very cloud they drink from. The bowl keeps its own weather because green helps it keep it.

None of this was planted to be wild. It was planted to hold. But up here, held water plus held heat wants only one thing, and that is more life crammed into seam and shallows.

People do come — boots on grate, hands on valves, a bag of cement burst hard as a boulder where it was left in rain. Behind the western wall rebar already furs with algae before it is even poured. Someone will come next week to pour it, and curse the roots already waiting in the footing sand. But they do not live here. They climb up out of cloud, do the heavy work that frogs cannot, and go back down to breathe thin cold air. You can smell them when they've been: sweat, cigarette, machine oil under the chlorophyll.

What lives here, truly, year-round, without leaving, is this tight knot: moth born in fog to clog the valve, frog born in seep to eat the moth and keep the valve clear, taro and moss and vine born in crack to drink the seep and breathe it back as fog for the moth.

Keep it moving, keep it shallow, keep it warm. That is not written for people.

It is written for them.
