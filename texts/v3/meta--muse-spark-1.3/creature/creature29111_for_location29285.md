No one lives here the way you live anywhere else.

Not in houses. Not in day. Not even, exactly, in bodies that stay put. What lives in the Weeping Hollow lives in sound and in wet and in each other, and if you want to meet it you have to stop looking and lie down.

Start with what holds you up.

Under you is not ground. It's a slow animal made of rot. A foot, two feet, a fathom of black humus built leaf by leaf in a place where light never fell - springy, sighing, faintly fizzing if you put your ear to it, because the roots in it are still eating stone. This is the first citizen: the mat. White moss a foot thick, blind moss, that doesn't reach for sun but spreads for fog. Step on it and it takes your boot-print and exhales and then, after a long minute, pushes back. It is full of mouths smaller than your eyelash - mites, springtails the color of milk, worms thin as thread - turning dead elephant-ear into soil into food again. It smells like wet paper and sweet rot, and it is warm from the inside out.

Out of the mat rise the Drinkers.

You will never see them, not really. Even if you cheat with a half-second of lamp you'll only get a smear of cream and pork-pink at your knees, huge leaves gone soft and translucent with water. But put your hands out and learn them. Here is one: a blind elephant-ear as wide as your torso, drooping under its own weight, slick as an inner cheek. No green. There has never been green here. White, cream, veined pink where the sap runs thin. Its surface is bearded with hairs, funnels, cups. It does not photosynthesize - there is nothing to synthesize. It combs.

The air is soup. Twenty-six degrees in a mountain that should be freezing. Lime-warm seeps weeping out of a thousand pores in the old limestone, loaded with dissolved rock, and the ceiling sixty meters above you letting go, drip by drip - one, two - impact. Every leaf is a gutter. It catches condensation, channels seep, funnels fog into its own throat. And it eats something else, too. Lean your molars close to those cold, flat platforms by the big pool and you'll feel it: a pressure, a faint oiliness on the skin, metal at the back of the tongue. The Dark That Eats The Lamp. Three stool-high, door-wide slabs set in a loose triangle, no seams, drinking every photon and exhaling a low steady field. The leaves have learned to eat that instead of sun. Their roots excrete acid to melt stone, their hairs vibrate to gather charge and water both. They grow a millimeter a month. Patient beyond patience.

Where a leaf dies, the Eaters bloom. Fungi like cold dinner plates, clustered at the foot of breakdown blocks - those hut-sized boulders settled to their shoulders in humus. They are flesh-white, gilled, weeping spores into the fog so thick you breathe them. They smell of crushed celery and iron. Touch one and it is cool and firm and then gives, like cartilage. Moths find them, papery things you never see but hear struggling when caught. The moths drink from fungus and leaf-cups and rot-sweet pools, and in turn they are drunk.

Because above the mat and the drinkers and the eaters is the noise. And the noise is alive.

The Ringers first. Hundreds of them. Frogs that should not be here at 3,800 meters, in absolute dark, in warm ankle-to-knee pools fringed with marsh. They are there by the hundreds, each male holding six inches of water and leaf as his whole kingdom. They do not croak. They ring - high, glassy, tuning-fork notes so close together the calls braid into a pulsing sheet that rattles your sternum. They have no eyes, or eyes like smudged thumbprints that never opened. They find each other by heat and tremor. Walk among them and they go silent in a circle around you, a moving hole of quiet, and close behind you. Stand still for ten minutes - truly still - and they will resume on your boots. Your boot becomes territory. If you lie still long enough you'll feel a throat pulsing against your ankle.

In the curtains, the Sawyers. Blind white crickets the length of your thumb, clinging to those wrist-thick fleshy vines that run up the weeping walls and hang back down and root again where they touch. The vines are highways and houses both. The crickets saw from inside them, leg across wing, a dry counter-rhythm to the wet ring of the frogs. They chew detritus, fallen hair-leaf, fungus rind, each other's shed skins. They taste the air with antennae longer than their bodies. When you push through a curtain they stop, all at once, and you feel them listening to your heat.

In the water, the Waiters.

By your left foot, right now, in a pan of mineral-warm water that tastes of chalk on the air: a salamander as long as your forearm, pale as a grub, gilled like a feather-duster. It hunts by smell and by the lateral line along its flank that reads pressure the way you read print. It has been in the same posture since you arrived. It will be in the same posture an hour after you leave. There are many of them. Every few minutes somewhere in the dark you hear it - a slow suck and plop as one shifts, or lunges. They eat the frogs' tadpoles, the fallen crickets, each other when they must. They breed only in the center pools, the deepest ones where the chorus is thickest. That's why you don't step there. Not a rule written down. A rule the room enforces with your own shin and your own shame.

And in the void, the Circlers.

You know the ceiling not by sight but by time. Drip lets go up there and you count one, two before it cracks on stone and leaf and rolls around the dome for seconds, coming back from three directions. In that empty lung, that vast hollow air in the middle of the room where almost nothing lives per volume, wings beat. Small, leathery, circling and circling. Bats, or birds that decided to become bats when eyes stopped paying rent. They never land where you can find them. They navigate by tongue-click the way you are learning to - only theirs is perfect. Every few minutes one snaps a moth inches from your face and you feel the pressure wave on your cheek and hear the papery struggle and then nothing. They drink on the wing from the falling drops. Their guano feeds the mat. The mat feeds the leaves. The leaves feed the moths.

Everything here is slow except that snap.

Water falls grain by grain. Leaves grow a millimeter a month. Boulders settle a finger's width per century. Even hunger is patient.

And then, between the drinkers and the waiters, strung at ankle height where your shin will find it if you are careless: the Beads. Twelve black walnuts, weightless, on a line you cannot see or feel. Touch one and it warms very slightly and the darkness gets impossibly deeper for a breath and the frog chorus stutters. They are not alive the way the frog is alive. But they are not dead the way stone is dead, either. They are part of the resident - the installation that keeps night total. The platforms drink light. The beads, perhaps, drink sound, or drink motion, and give back that field the leaves eat. The plants grow on sound, the old guides below say. Four stone huts and a sheepfold a day's walk down the moor, and they will not come in. They are not far wrong.

So who lives here?

A mat that breathes you back. Leaves like wet ears listening for fog. Plates of fungus exhaling spores. Frogs that ring your bones and then sing on your boots. Crickets sawing in vine curtains. Salamanders waiting longer than you can stand still. Wings circling a cathedral you can only hear. Mites and worms and fizzing bacteria dissolving limestone one molecule at a time. And three cold slabs and twelve beads that are not rock and not plant, keeping the dark fed so all the rest can stay blind and crowded and loud.

Lie on your back in the deepest moss, between the roots of a giant white ear, hold your breath. Drip. Ring. Saw. Wingbeat. Drip. Your heart slowing to match it. You are alone in a big empty room and you are surrounded, and after an hour it feels ordinary - feet knowing moss from lip-of-pool, hands knowing curtain-vine, tongue-click returning you the shape: wall left close, wall right far, open water ahead, block above.

It does not need you to see it to be completely itself.
