No one watches here. That is the first thing to understand about whoever lives here.

Not that they hide. They do not need to. There is nothing here that would be seen by watching, not at night, not in this belonging dark. If you come looking for an animal — for eyeshine, for breath, for a shape that moves between bushes — you will not find it. The lower twigs are unbroken. The dust holds no foot but yours, and yours already settling.

What lives here lives lower than that. Lower than looking.

At first you think: only the shrubs. And that is true, but it is not small.

Each one is a person in the oldest sense. Not like you. Low, stiff, grey-black, knee to waist, many-branched. From a distance a knot. Up close, when you kneel and let your eyes give up and your hands learn, a whole architecture.

Take the large one. The one with its arms spread wide and nothing underneath but its own shade. It has been here longer than you can mean by *long*. Its stems are hardwood, twisted, peeling in thin strips, grey outside and yellowish where the wind has worried them. The tiny leaves are folded shut like paper wallets, both sides inward to keep what little moisture they still bargain with. If you bend one it does not snap. At the core it is still pliant. If you crush one — and you should only do it once — it gives off that faint resin-and-dust smell, bitter and clean. Creosote and sun. That is its sweat. That is how it keeps the day from taking it entirely.

It does not grow. Not now, not this year, perhaps not in several years. It maintains. Above ground, a crown no wider than a man lying down. Below ground, twice that, three times that: a root-claim, a wide circle of bare pavement that looks empty but is not empty. It is owned. Fine root-hairs hold the grit together, sip at depths you cannot feel. Nothing else is allowed in that circle. Twenty steps of nothing to the next bush is not waste. It is manners. It is how they live without killing each other.

They are very old. The three to the north especially. You can tell by the way the center has died out and the living ring has moved outward, stem by stem, century by century. What looks like three separate bushes might be one thing that has learned how to outlive its own middle. If you sat with them for a hundred years you would see a branch lean, thicken, split. If you sat for a thousand you would see them walk, slowly, outward from themselves. They remember rain the way you remember a story told before you were born — not as water, but as an instruction: *wait*.

Between them lives the second citizen, and you have already been trying not to step on it.

The crust. That pavement that crumbles a little when you scuff it and then settles — it is not just dust locked by time. It is skinned over. In the paler patches, almost white with mineral bloom, and in the darker patches between the grass clumps, there is a thin, brittle life stretched over the ground like a scab. Cyanobacteria, lichens, mosses so small and so dry now they are only a darkening, a roughness under your fingertip. By day you would see them as blackened flakes, curled edges. Now, at night, warm, they do nothing at all. They are utterly paused.

But they are alive. They hold the bowl together. Without them the wind would take the grit grain by grain. With them, your foot makes a small dry crunch that is immediately swallowed, and the edge of the break stays clean. They do not photosynthesize in the dark. They do not fix nitrogen in this heat. They wait. Ten years, twenty, for those few hours after a rain that may never come — though there have been no rivers here for longer than any story, though no dew will form tonight — and then, for a morning, they will swell and breathe and be soft. Then dry again. That is their whole life: a readiness so complete it looks like death.

You learn not to break them because they ask without asking. You take smaller steps. You become sparse yourself.

Lower still, slower still, live the stones.

Not the scattered pebbles — though they too have their long lying-beside-each-other, rounded not by rivers but by wind and heat and time, established — but the split boulder. Waist-high. Cracked wide enough to put your wrist in. Put it in. It is warm inside like a mouth.

Nothing lives in there. That is important. No lizard wedged in coolness, no beetle, no nest. The warmth is the stone itself, exhaled. By day it held the sun longer than the air did; by night it gives it back slowly, through your palm, through your shins when you pass near. It has no metabolism. It has thermal memory. In a place that does not change, that stays exactly warm, exactly dark, that is a kind of living. Expansion and contraction, a flake loosened every few hundred years — that oval palm-sized stone with one edge thinner where it has flaked is its child — mineral bloom sweated out ten thousand years ago and never undone. The white quartz chips scattered nearby that catch what little light there is and glint dully when you pass: they do not see you. They are the parts of it that learned how to hold light a second longer.

And under all of it, threading them together, is the one that is hardest to name because it has no edges.

The heat. The breath of the large animal still sleeping.

The ground gives it up through the soles of your shoes. The air sits thick and dry against your face, warm on the inside of your mouth. It does not move. It does not lift off in waves you can see. It stays, held. It is what the shrubs are pausing in. It is what the crust is waiting in. It is what makes the bowl hold itself, rimmed by those soft worn ridges no higher than your waist, no higher than your chest, keeping its quiet so complete you hear your own blood.

If you stand still long enough, you feel it settle on you too. Your voice lowers without deciding to. Your need to look far stops. That darker line where the rim lifts, that knot where the largest shrub stands, that pale mineral patch — that is enough. Three large bushes to the north. One mouth-warm crack. A handful of dull glints. You learn them by heart because they do not change and neither, for an hour, do you.

That is who lives here: the paused ones, the spaced ones, the low ones. They do not eat, they do not watch, they do not make. No wall, no cairn, no fire-ring, no cut wood. The materials propose tools — a sharp flake of dark basalt for scraping, a straight dead branch fire-hardened by sun alone for digging — but no hand takes them up. The hands here are roots. The eyes here are closed. The mouth here is warm stone that never eats.

They endure. When you leave, the pebble you moved will soon grow an underside of dust and become old again. They will still be in balance with themselves. Warm. Low. Dry. Dark. Quiet. Unmade.
