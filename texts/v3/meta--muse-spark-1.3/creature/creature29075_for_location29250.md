No one lives here the way you expect someone to live.

At first you think it is empty. You swim an hour through grass taller than you and corridors of gold-brown ribbon and you see no eyes, no flank, no hurry. Only leaf. Only light falling in nets. You start to think the forest is uninhabited, that it grew itself for no one.

Then you stay still. You hang motionless until your bubbles stop, until the green snow of larvae resumes landing, and you realize the rule is inverted: here, the ones who live here mostly do not move.

The true residents are the ones that *are* the place.

Lowest, the rooters. Seagrass in the flats, but not the thin shy grass you know from elsewhere. Blades as wide as your wrist, olive and jade, tough as strap leather, rooted not in sand — there is almost no sand — but wedged into cracks in fresh glass. A root finds a cooling fracture, swells into it, splits it a millimeter wider over a decade, and holds. A meadow is thousands of such wedgings, all leaning together when the six-hour tide breathes in and out. If you tear one — it bleeds green sap and seals by evening — you see how much life is in a single blade: pink crust growing on it like lichen, white frost hydroids furred at the edge, a thumb-sized limpet clamped near the base that has never moved more than a hand's breadth in its life and never will, grazing a clean lane through the film.

Above them, the risers. They decided, independently, to be kelp. From a holdfast the size of a fist, glued to bottle-green basalt with a cement stronger than any rope, a stipe rises, then splits, then rises again, ten, twelve meters straight up until the bladders at the tips kiss the underside of the surface. Brown-gold ribbons, serrated, studded with trapped rain-bubbles at noon so the whole canopy goes silver-mercury. They grow so close they touch. Frond on frond. Stipe rubbing stipe with that low subsonic creak you hear in your teeth. On each stipe lives a vertical town that never applied to be a town: sponges in shelves, sulfur-yellow and blood-orange and violet-black, narrowing the gaps until you have to turn sideways; calcareous pink crust like paint; green velvet algae; anemones that open over the course of a day, close over the course of a night, tentacles full of slow plankton.

In the middle water, the floaters — because here life can use the whole volume. Fern-like red algae that never attach to anything, rootless balls of lace drifting inches a day, turning slowly to show all sides to the sun. Chains of diatoms linked into spirals you can see if you hold still, glittering. Copepods and larval clouds, the slow snow that does not fall so much as loiter, each speck seeking rock. If you extend your arm you touch three living things. If you hang, more will brush you. The dead do not sink far here; they are caught in fronds and composted in place, so even falling is a kind of staying.

On the rock itself, the fur. Every hard surface taken. Pillows piled on pillows, tubes split open, chimneys standing with their throats still empty — all gone under life. White bacterial mats fringe the cold seeps in the north block-valley where sulfurous water shimmers faintly between house-sized black blocks. No plants there yet. Just mineral and heat-ghost and white fringe, and under a lens, thousands of mouths.

And then, in the quietest hollows, the gardeners. The ones who move, but barely, and only in circles.

Black urchins, long-spined, each the size of a dinner plate with spines as long as your forearm. They live in burrows they have worried into the basalt over years, rotating slowly, spine by spine. Each holds fragments of drift algae over itself like a parasol — not for shade, though the light is fierce, but to keep the film moist and to hide from the sky-eye above. Around each burrow is a mown circle, neat as a tonsure: the urchin reaches out at night, inch by inch, rasps the new growth, pulls it in. Outside the circle, lush; inside, pink rock and cropped stubble and the white drill-holes of snails who prey on the grazers. From above those circles read as a pattern, a leopard-spotting of the meadow. The urchins do not meet. They do not need to. Each keeps its garden for decades. Slow wins.

Other movers are visitors more than residents, because plants make here and almost nothing takes. You can go days without seeing a swimmer, and then:

The old one. A single green turtle, very old, shell furred green with algae so she looks like a moving piece of meadow, barnacles on her shoulders like epaulettes. She comes unhurried over the grass, flapping once, gliding a long time, cropping a blade here and there. She ignores you completely. She has learned the sharp stays, soft spreads — she knows the corridors, the amphitheaters, the arches and wells the stone allows, and she follows them year after year. Her droppings disintegrate slowly into green clouds that feed the grass she cropped. She is a loop closed.

The brief ones. A small cloud of silver juveniles, all of one species, all born in the same week out in the blue, who found this high country by smell of warm green and came up onto the table to hide. For a month they flicker through a kelp corridor, eating plankton, flashing as one when a shadow crosses. Then they thin, one by one taken by the drill-snails' grown young or simply washed off the rim when the tide sighs outward, and gone. Next year, another cloud, another species. Nothing permanent.

In the chimneys and under ledges, the tickers. Snapping shrimp, far off, almost never seen, only heard as faint ticking in the rocks. Thumb-length, translucent, one claw oversized, they keep burrows in crushed shell dust caught in hollows and defend them with sound. A nudibranch the length of your thumb crosses a sponge in an afternoon, orange on violet-black, laying a white ribbon of eggs as it goes. Anemones open. Hydroids frost and unfrost.

No sharks come up onto the table; the water is too warm, too leaf-thick to hunt in. Only traces pass through: a bite taken clean out of a bladder, edges already healing, a shell with a drill-hole, a turtle flipper scar gone white.

What binds them is not hunger but upwardness and patience. Everything grows up, floats up, reaches up toward that bright ceiling that is water meeting air. Even the sponges grow into vases open to the sky. Even the bacterial mats lift into tiny towers. Up is everything. Sharp stays, soft spreads — so bodies are soft, furred, ribboned, lacelike, going around the unyielding black glass that still cuts like broken bottles a century after it erupted. And slow wins — growth measured in millimeters a month, a holdfast keeping a good crack for decades, a larva waiting days to land, the forest closing over your trail minutes after you pass and forgetting you.

Stay long enough and you stop looking for faces. You learn to see living as rooting, furring, drifting, mowing a circle, kissing the surface with a bladder full of rain-air. It is dense in a way land forests rarely are, leaf-thick, sunlit, patiently holding its breath — a high meadow under sea, inhabited almost entirely by those who decided never to leave.
