They live low. That is the second fact, after high.

Everything that has learned to stay here has learned to crouch, to mound, to curl. Nothing stands up to be seen. To stand up is to be shredded.

First — the lush.

You have to unlearn green to see them right. From a distance on the pavement they look like spills, dark oil-spots on the mineral glare, blue-black with a bruise of orange in them. Only when you are kneeling, with your face inches away because that is the only distance where sight works in this leaking light, do they resolve.

The base of it all is the mattress-moss. It does not grow up so much as accumulate, a slow piling of cottony, silica-furred shoots a foot, sometimes two feet thick, springy under the knee and shockingly soft to lie on. Put your spine to it and the organ-tone comes up through it filtered, numbed — a hum you feel as a loosening at the base of the skull. Each filament is bearded in glass hair, dry as spun sugar, and each hair is a length tuned to tremble. Touch a clump and your fingertip goes blurred. The whole mound is a bass trap. It drinks the deep note that lives in your molars. Where the tone pools — in a hollow, at the foot of a sheer black wall where the sound doubles back on itself — the mattress swells highest, domed like a held breath.

Out of the mattress rise the bells.

Trumpet-lichens, big as a fist, big as two fists near the huts. Amber, blood-orange, a pale smoking yellow at the rim. They all point up, mouths open to the nowhere sky. They are not decoration. They are ears, and mouths. You can watch them work: the whole bell shivering visibly, rim blurring with the bass, a fine dry powder sifting off them when the note peaks. Each size is a tuning. The small ones near an overhang's acoustic shadow are stunted, closed, mute — and beyond that shadow line, where the hum returns, they flare open again in a sharp line, as if cut with a knife. That is how you can see sound here. The plants draw its map.

Above and between them, the thatch. Broad papery leaves, veined like open hands, overlapping into low roofs, shingle over shingle. They are dark to almost black-green — not for light, there is no light worth chasing, that paler smear in the east never clears the teeth — but for resonance. Their edges are split, fringed, frayed by their own trembling into finer and finer fingers, because a frayed edge catches more vibration than a clean one. They lean. Not toward the smear that is the sun. They lean into loudness, toward the focus between two ridges where the tearing overtone overhead comes down strongest. They rustle like paper being constantly shuffled by invisible hands. Crush one and there is no wet, no sap, only a dry crunch and a puff of scent like hot stone and old incense. They do not rot. Nothing rots without water and without animals. When the mountain's pitch drops a quarter-tone — and it does, over a week, over years, as the range creeps another hair's breadth upward — the thatch dies back on one side to pale, dry skeletons that keep the exact shape of life, and swells on the other. Every living clump wears a halo of its own ghosts, white thatch around dark thatch around orange bells. A history of loudness, kept in paper.

They are uneaten. Perfect except where sound has torn them. That is the eeriest thing at first, until you understand: there are no animals. No flies stitching above the bells. No worms turning under the mattress. No birds. Your boots on the pavement are obscenely loud because nothing else has feet. The lush performs for no witness but the rock. Until you come, and the bells near the path shiver a little harder with your steps — they hear you coming long before they are seen, feeling you through the stone.

And then — the people who learned to be low with them.

They are not there when you look. That is their first skill. The huts are empty, or the people inside are very still and very accustomed to not being found. Low beehives of dry-stacked black slab, no mortar, just weight and patience, each barely large enough to curl inside on a folded mattress of dead thatch, door turned deliberately away from the focus of the tone. A day's walk apart, sometimes two, strung between gardens along lines of cairns — waist-high, man-high piles of sharp new stone — that do not mark the shortest path but the bearable path, where the roar does not quite meet itself and a thin-blooded brain can keep thinking.

You will know them first by tending, not by faces.

The gardens nearest a hut are different. The fringed leaves have been trimmed clean with a knapped blade of volcanic glass — you will find one left on a flat rock as an offering — so the fringe does not grow so fine it tangles and tears. The dead white thatch has been cleared back, woven. The bells have been thinned, because left thick they ring each other until they shatter into amber shards; you will find circles of those upright shards where someone sat to do the work. Coils of leaf-fiber rope. A cup made from a giant trumpet, hardened and lacquered with something not water-based, to hold seeds, or dust, or sound itself.

They eat — if eating is the word — the dry lush. It crunches like frost without cold. They wear it. They sleep on it. They weave thatch into capes that buzz faintly in the bass and shed the mineral dust that hangs for minutes in the thin air. Their tech is all hand-tech: pecked, ground, tied, wedged. No wood to burn. No metal but the sound of metal tearing a mile overhead. Hides scraped and sewn with gut, though what animal gave hide here you learn not to ask aloud when the rock is listening. They must have carried them up from below, from where water still remembers its shape, and never carried them down. Charcoal handprints on a sheltered wall, tally marks, a wavy line that might be the tone made visible. Counting the slow shift.

What have they become, living where water never came? Dry, like the leaves. Thin-blooded, shallow-breathed. Noses that bleed black dust that crusts instantly. Skin gone papery and furred with fine dust ingrained against the dryness. They do not shout — shouting is shredded instantly. They speak with hands, with elbows nudged in code, with close foreheads pressed bone to bone so the pre-echo that arrives before any sound here can be used instead of suffered. Inside the hut, with the door away from the focus, the roar dulls just enough to sleep in short, cold naps with palms over ears, feeling the note in the sternum rather than hearing it. They have learned to move slowly, on the mountain's duration. To wait out a seam. To migrate a garden over years as the pitch drops.

Are they permanent? No one stays long on the extreme height, not without fraying. Perhaps they come up for a season that is not a season, tend, harvest, leave the glass knife for the next. Perhaps some stay and become stiller and stiller until they are indistinguishable from a mound of thatch, another halo of ghosts. The huts do not tell.

There is a third resident, though it is not whoever and not whatever. It is the place itself, still being born.

The mountain humming to itself as it grows. Stone under tension that has learned to sing before it learned to fall apart. That is why the cliffs glitter with fresh fractures a hundred years old and undulled, why talus has barely begun to fall, why the scree slopes are short and abortive. It has not finished deciding to be rock yet.

And with it, the things that are almost living because the note makes them move: the dust devil of dry mineral powder that unscrews itself with no wind you can feel and walks, over minutes, from one garden to another as if carrying a message too slow to ever arrive. The upward-falling trembling shadows of the moss mounds leaning toward the pale smear. The cairn you left an hour ago waiting ahead of you again, slightly to the left and paler, as if you are walking through drafts of the same place.

Lie on a mattress — it will numb your spine — and stare up at the seams in the sky, those long straight faults where the grey is a fraction darker, and wait. Sometimes the tone catches, skips like a needle. For three heartbeats there is something else underneath: a silence so absolute it feels wet. The bells stop. The thatch holds its breath. The tenders, wherever they are curled and still, hold theirs. You understand then that this whole broad tilted table, this valley the size of a small country cupped between ranges still being born, is not on top of the world at all, but inside a held note. Pre-erosion. Pre-river. Pre-animal. Pre-rain.

Then the seam passes. The roar floods back into your teeth. The leaves resume. The cold settles back onto your neck.

What lives here lives on that flooding. Lush on noise. Dry and exuberant and low. And tended, tenderly, by hands that learned, with only stacked rock and woven bells, to live in the loudest quiet there is.
