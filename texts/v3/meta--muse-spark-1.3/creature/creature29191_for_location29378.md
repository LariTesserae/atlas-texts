At first you think it is the floor that is alive.

Not anything on it — it. The carpet itself shifts. Put your boot down and the ground re-sorts around the leather, flows and re-knits. A claw clicks against your ankle, tests, withdraws. Something with too many legs pours over your toe without breaking stride. You are not being attacked. You are being included, briefly, as terrain.

But none of these are the ones who live here.

The crabs do not live here. Red-backed, purple-clawed, shell to shell in the thousands, they are only passing through on their long patience inland from the estuary miles off. They shoulder through fern and over log, they bump and click faintly like fingernails tapping, and by morning this wave will be elsewhere. Pilgrims. They drink from a root-hollow and move on.

The millipedes do not live here either, not the way a house is lived in. The black-red ribbons as long as your forearm that pour over the fallen logs, leg-wave running down their bodies like wind through grass — they are travelers too, eating their way slowly forward through rot.

The beetles that make the drone in your teeth do not live here. They commute. Heavy-bodied, blind in the true dark, they idle from tree to tree elsewhere in the black, rising and falling. You hear wings, not voices. Most of what is numerous here has no voice at all.

Closer.

Kneel, if you can stand to be climbed on. Let your eyes stay useless. The highway finds you by touch and smell — a half-meter of mud worn glass-smooth by millions of feet, running slopewise into darkness both ways without end.

Down one lane comes the green conveyor. Up the other goes the empty return.

Each ant coming down carries overhead a sail of fresh-cut leaf, many times her own weight, held high like a parasol. Green oil smell where the edge is torn. She does not hurry. She does not stop. When a crab leg blocks her, she climbs over it. When a fern arches, she goes under. When your boot blocks her, the line bends an inch to the left around you and continues, unbroken. Endless.

You think: the ants live here.

Not yet. Not these.

What you are seeing are fingers. Hands. Mouthparts detached from a body and sent out walking. The worker with the leaf is blind to what the leaf is for. She will never eat it. She will carry it for hours across the slope, down a hole no wider than your thumb, down into earth that is darker than this night and damper and warmer, and give it away.

Follow it, if you could shrink small enough to follow. Down past the egg-soft smooth stones, past root-mat and leaf-mold deep enough to sink a hand into, down where the *tok* of water from leaf to leaf no longer reaches. The air gets hotter, not cooler. Close. Fungal. Iron and mud and crushed green.

It opens.

Chambers, vaulted out of red-brown clay, a town under the valley. And in the chambers, the gardens.

That is what lives here.

Low, pale, spongy masses, veined and beaded with moisture, breathing faintly in the stillness — fungus. Not the cold smolder you saw on fallen logs outside. This kind grows nowhere else in the forest except here, in the dark they make for it. It cannot survive a minute of daylight, a breath of dry air. So they have built it a lung to live in.

Around it swarm the gardeners, smaller sisters of the leaf-bearers, amber and tireless. They chew the green sails to pulp, they plant the pulp, they weed it, they lick it clean of mold, they feed on its swollen tips and feed it to the larvae curled white in side-cells like grains of rice. The whole highway, the whole endless green conveyor out there in the night, exists only to feed this soft, blind, stationary thing that never moves and never sees the fireflies pulsing acres wide over the treetops.

And deeper still, in the warmest, dampest heart where the ventilation draws cool air in at the base and exhales hot air out the top, she lies.

The queen.

She was once winged, once flying. Years ago, on some other hot breathless night between rains, she flew, mated, fell, tore off her own wings and dug down. She has not seen light since. She will never go up again.

Now she is as long as your thumb joint, distended, pale amber, soft-bodied in a city of hard clay. Her abdomen pulses, slow, slow. An egg every few seconds, all night, every night. Workers crowd her, turning the eggs, carrying them away, feeding her predigested fungus from mouth to mouth. She ticks faintly. She rustles. Put your palm to the termite kiln nearby — chest-high, sun-and-saliva-fired hard — and you feel the same heat coming up through your skin: warmer than the midnight air. A furnace-room sound. That is not sun stored. That is bodies. Millions of bodies and gardens generating their own climate because outside, even here, is not close enough, not damp enough, not steady enough.

That is the rule, and she keeps it perfectly: live close. Share the leaf, share the drop of water, share the dark. There is no room for emptiness, so nothing is empty.

Her daughters pave the forest floor with their feet. Her breath, vented through spires, feeds the moss on the bark above. Her cast-off leaves feed the millipedes. Her dead feed the beetles. Even your breath, when you breathe out warm spores and iron mud into the still air, is immediately taken — by a leaf, drinking the dark, by a pool the size of a palm in a buttress root, filmed with pollen, shrinking by a millimeter as the mud drinks it.

She does not know the crabs are migrating over her roof. She does not know the fireflies are discharging in sequence ridge after ridge to the horizon. She does not need to.

She is settled. Like the basin. Low, and rounded, and weathered down to balance. Breathing, very slowly, in and out.
