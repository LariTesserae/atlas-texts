The people up there don't look settled at first. They look stranded.

You see them in motion before you see them at rest — a figure in yellow oilskins braced on the bow of a flat-bottomed boat, shouting over the diesel, one hand on a cage line, the other waving you off a culvert. Another one knee-deep in black water dragging peat bricks up onto a causeway, trousers soaked to the thigh and not caring. They are always damp. That is the first thing. Not wet the way you get wet in a shower and then dry, but wet the way an otter is wet. Hair stuck flat under wool hats, cuffs dark with water that never wrings out, boots that squelch even indoors. Their hands are the giveaway — red-knuckled, clumsy with cold, skinned at the knuckles from rope and net and rusted winch wire, nails black with peat. Their faces are wind-burned the whole year round.

Live up there long enough and you learn to spot the trades by stain and smell.

The net men and cage men smell of fish guts and diesel and live in a permanent shout. Broad-backed, bearded or stubbled, arms strong from hauling. They stand all day in sloshing boats with water to their ankles, lifting trout and char flashing silver-dark out of the black rings, sorting, gutting on the spot while gulls scream inches from their ears for the offal. They throw without looking. The gulls catch without missing.

The turbine men and pump men are quieter, paler — shed men. They live by ear. They can hear a bearing going out under the thrum of the house, they can tell by the pitch of the sill roar outside whether the loch is up a foot or down. They smell of hot oil and peat-smoke, they keep rags in their pockets, they drink tea black and scalding in the concrete houses straddling the narrows while water thunders under the floorboards.

The peat men, the causeway men, the ferrymen — they are legs more than anything. Thighs like posts from stepping quake to rock to pile to rocking boat. They know by eye which green will hold and which green will take you to the knee. They test everything with a pole before a boot. They move like deer through the sedge, slow and deliberate, and then sudden and loud on a deck.

And the women in the smokehouse and the gutting shed and the net loft, with aprons stiff with scales and fingers fast with twine, hair tied up under scarves gone grey with mist, arms reddened to the elbow in brine. The kids who learn to bail before they learn to read, who are set to mind the mooring lines and chase gulls off the drying racks with sticks, who sleep in bothies with the stove roaring and the loch slapping the piles underneath them and think that is what quiet is.

None of them try to be dry. That ambition leaves you in the first winter. They wear wool that stays warm when soaked, oilskin that sheds the worst of it, two pairs of socks and the knowledge neither will dry. They rest the way the place tells them to — not looking for a dry hill, because there isn't one, but perching. On a jetty edge with feet dangling over chop. On an upturned fuel drum in a shed doorway. In a boat with the engine idling underneath them like a heartbeat. They nap in snatches between tides made by men, because the water goes up a foot when it rains in the night and down a foot when they open the turbines below, and a boat left tied tight will heel in mud by evening. So someone is always awake, always listening for rope-strain and hull-scrape and the change in the sill note.

They are loud because they have to be, to be heard over engines and water and wind. Waving you in, waving you off, shouting part numbers and depths and warnings. But underneath they are attuned in a way lowlanders aren't. They watch cloud like farmers watch cattle — that flat grey-white midday that lies, that sudden lift when you can see three lochs deep in every direction and the next shed's smoke two miles off, and then the wall coming over the swell. Ten minutes from glass to white horses. They smell it first. They have the winch covered before you feel the first drops.

They are not alone up there, and they know they are not in charge.

The true old residents tolerate them. The char and trout, black shadows rolling in every calm to take midges that get in your mouth and eyes. The eels sliding through the ditches like living oil. The frogs that in thaw make every pool throb. The otters who work the narrows more expertly than any man, at dawn, and leave fish heads on the jetties as a kind of rent receipt. The red deer who stand knee-deep in sedge with cotton grass hung from their antlers, utterly unbothered by boats passing twenty yards off, because they were wading here before there were boats. The hares and voles and shrews holding the high hummocks.

And above, the ones who really own it. The raft-ducks — greylags and pintail and teal — that blacken a whole bay when they lift. The curlew and golden plover piping thin off the moor. The gulls and terns who follow every gutting for miles, who will take food uncovered for a second. The ravens who tumble in the updraft off the turbine house roof, waiting with terrible patience for lambing week afterbirth and gutting week guts. And the midges, the caddis, the dragonflies, the diving beetles — the air itself up there is alive and biting. Calm is just the interval when the midges find you, the men say, and they cover their necks without stopping work.

Even the plants act like animals. The sphagnum building itself up in lime and rust and acid yellow hummocks, the cotton grass nodding everywhere, the weed beds — pondweed, milfoil, stonewort — rising and falling with the seiche so thick you think you could walk on them. The floating bogs that calve off after rain and go drifting with their little knee-high birch and willow and duck nests on them like islands. The men just pole around them, or tow them back, or let them go. You don't argue with land that moves.

At night they leave the bare bulbs burning on the piers, strings of them all night in the mist, because a dark landing in cloud-water is a lost boat. From a distance the plateau glitters wet and yellow in patches — shed here, pump station there, workboat moving between cages with its lamp swinging — while below the cloud the water keeps slapping the piles and the halyards keep clanking against the masts and the sills keep roaring and everybody, human and otter and duck and midge, just keeps working the shallows.
