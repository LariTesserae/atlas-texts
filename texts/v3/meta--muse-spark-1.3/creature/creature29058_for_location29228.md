At first you think nothing lives here.

That is the trick the valley plays. You stand in a plaza big enough to land a ship on, stone still sharp under your boots, green pressing in from the edges, a tower half a kilometer off leaning its skirt of hanging vines, and you hear only drip and your own breath. No crowd. No traffic. No birds. For a city, it feels post-everything, as if it was built and then politely abandoned.

Then you learn how to see them.

Start with the traces, because they are everywhere once you stop looking for crowds. A basin at a street corner filled to the exact lip, the water clear. A pale path worn lighter across dark stone where feet have learned to walk when the hum comes. A dew-net restrung, its harp-lines beaded and humming faint with water weight. A doorway in a tower base wiped clean of moss at hand-height, the smoky wall there more solid than elsewhere — kept real by frequent witnessing.

Someone was just here. Someone is almost still here.

The people are sparse on purpose, the way the towers are sparse. A basin a day's walk across holds what another city would fit in three blocks. You can be alone in the middle of it for hours, and that aloneness is not loneliness to them. It is the manners of the place.

You meet one the way you meet weather — gradually.

She is tall, because everyone here is a little stretched by warmth and low light, unhurried in a way that first reads as tiredness. Skin gone pale not from sickness but from years under the veil, with a damp sheen that never quite dries. Clothes never quite dry here, so they stopped fighting it: loose dark wick-cloth that hangs wet at the shoulders and wicks the air-river off the skin, bare feet or soft soles because you need to feel the hum come up through the stone before you hear it. Hair plastered at the hairline. Eyes — you notice the eyes — pupils wide and staying wide, amber-flecked, built for brown-gold noon. They squint and blink and lift their faces like everyone else when the eye opens overhead and true sun falls through for a minute, shocked and grateful.

They are quiet. That is not shyness, it is architecture. The walls eat sound, the veil eats sky-noise, a raised voice goes nowhere and comes back strange. So they speak low, breath-first, little puffs of words that fog slightly even in the warmth. Much of what they say isn't said at all. To ride a thread you step into heat-shimmer and think where, clearly, without insisting. A whole population that has learned to think clearly. Children are taught it before letters: hold the destination lightly, and if the thread won't take you, don't argue. It means the tower is dreaming. You'll end up two blocks sideways, damp and disoriented, if you push.

Stand near one long enough and the green will tell you she is warm. That is how you first understand their place in the ecology. The sail-leaves turn toward movement and heat because there is no sun worth following. Walk past a wall of them with a resident and watch: one by one, slowly, the huge dusk-green plates pivot to face the person. If she stands still, the tendrils come out to test the air in her direction, tasting breath-moisture, skin-salt. The plants are not passive decoration. They are watching the only suns that walk.

And the people let themselves be watched. They pause. They lend attention.

That is the work here. The upper floors of the towers are that smoky translucent mass that is solid when you lean against it and goes slightly to mist when you step away, as if it only bothers to be real when witnessed. So someone has to witness. The residents do it as a matter of course — a hand left a moment longer on a wall, eyes resting on a corner while waiting for rain, sleeping high up where their breathing keeps the rooms held. The old ones will tell you the buildings drink, too. The corner basins are not decorative. Capillary stone pulls water up into the walls. Leave a basin empty and by morning there will be a crack, sharp as a fresh fracture, like a mouth. So everyone carries a little water. Dipping from a leaf-axil, tipping a gourd into a basin as they pass. A city of people watering their houses the way elsewhere people water plants.

In return, the towers tune for them. Every few hours the low chord rises through the feet, through the teeth — all the towers tuning together — and everyone without discussion drifts to the pale paths, to the overhangs, because the warm heavy rain is coming. The veil sweating out the basin's own exhalation. It knocks sideways if you're caught. The stone channels run fast for ten minutes. Then it stops, and the puddles mirror amber, and the moss drinks, and life resumes. The people shake off like large animals, wring their wick-cloth, and go back to tending.

What do they eat in a place with almost no sun and almost no animals? What the dim will give: shade greens, elephant-ear hearts, curb fungi pale as knuckles that push up overnight and are harvested by morning before they go spongy, epiphyte fruit, pocket-water filtered through ceramic. Tower gardens in the translucent upper light where white waxy flowers open in an hour to glow a little — built to lure pollinators that mostly don't exist. So the people pollinate. Hands dusted. Shoulders brushed. They do not pick the white flowers, not because it's forbidden but because cut flowers dim, and then the blind moths will follow you for hours. At night those moths come anyway, big as hands, dusted grey, bumping softly against the floating lamps and against the faint glow on a person's skin after garden work. The residents move through them gently, unsurprised to be mistaken for flowers.

The other animals leave them mostly alone, and they return the courtesy. Once a day a pale lizard flattens on warm stone, throat pulsing, and a child will crouch to watch it rather than touch it. High up near the veil something glides tower to tower on long silent wings — animal or feral maintenance drone, no one will settle it — and when it calls, that falling glass note, everyone pauses mid-step. Not fear. Recognition. Another resident, rarer than they are.

Because the truth is, people are only the middle layer of what lives here.

Below and around them lives the green that behaves like an animal. Mosses deciding in real time what will be ground and what will be wall, softening fresh fracture with velvet. Vines learning to climb unreal walls. Sail-leaves recoiling from the eye of daylight when the veil thins and a hundred-meter spotlight drifts across the basin, moss steaming, people blinking up — and then the leaves unfurl again, turning back to the nearest warm body, kinder than the sun.

Above and among them live the towers themselves. It took you a while to admit it, but you felt it the first time a thread refused you. Each tower has a temperament. One dreams long afternoons and misdelivers riders. One balances and will not be ridden at all, humming slightly off-chord. One keeps its skirt of vines trimmed clean, printing itself back clean as fast as the green grows over it; another has surrendered its lower twenty stories to hanging green and drips constantly. They drink. They tune. They remember different suns — that is why at dusk their shadows fall in different directions, and why your own shadow, under the drifting cold suns of the floating lamps, lingers a second too long after you move. Light here keeps a memory. So do they.

And over all of it lives the veil. Kilometers-wide panels shifting translucent to opaque, breathing very slowly, sweating on a cycle that is almost but not quite natural. The engineers from before maintain it, or it maintains itself. The old people disagree. Everyone agrees it is alive in the way weather is alive. It keeps the light dim and the air warm underneath like a hand over a seed tray. Sometimes it opens an eye and looks in.

To live here is to be part of that negotiation while the cuts are still sharp. No old softness to hide in. Erosion hasn't forgiven anything yet. The residents know they are not finishing a city. They are teaching a young ecosystem how to be inhabited, and letting it teach them back: stay on the pale paths, leave water for the walls, don't insist at a closed thread, let the leaves face you. Move slowly enough that things can grow while you watch, if you know how to watch — a runner across a plaza overnight, a flower in an hour.

If you stay until the brown-gold hour, when condensation beads on every edge and the air is thickest, you'll see one of them standing alone in the vast kept emptiness between towers, shoulders damp, breathing audible, sail-leaves turning one by one toward the sound. Not lost. Not waiting. Just being the warm thing the valley orients around for a moment, while the towers hum up through the stone and the rain gathers overhead to fall.
