No one lives here. That's what you think first.

There are visitors, plenty of them. Herons standing like borrowed posts. Gulls dropping in to stab and lift. Turnstones flipping shell. Even the rat in the pumphouse doorway is only watching, only pausing on its way somewhere drier. The mussels live here the way bricks live in a wall — they are here, edge to edge, but they don't *keep* the place.

Then you crouch long enough for the cold to get into your knees and you start to see keeping.

Because this place should have stopped. The diesel in the pumphouse the size of a wardrobe should have seized decades ago. The belt should have rotted. The discharge pipe should have choked. Instead the collar of cream travertine around its base is two inches thick and smooth, built drip by drip, and wiped clear where the drip would block the mouth. The bacterial mats grow in perfect rings around the warm seeps — yellow inside orange inside black, like contour lines — and the outer ring is grazed short, cropped, weeded, while the inner is left to thicken. If you toss a pebble it will in an hour be back where it belongs by weight, silt to center, shell to edge, as if a slow hand sorted while you blinked. Boot prints preserved in mineral are not all old. One, by the number 7, is still damp at the toe.

Something low tends this bowl.

You find it first by not finding it. Under the half-collapsed catwalk, where the legs have grown thick with mussels until they look like stalactites, there is a shape that is not leg and not tire and not pipe. At noon in the dirty amber cone of the sodium lamp it is just more texture — ochre and rust-red and mustard and oil-black. The colors of the place. No green. Nothing on it photosynthesizes and admits it.

Stay still until your shoulders dampen through, until the midges find the warmth at your collar, until the herons decide you are another post, and it resolves.

It is low. Like the place is low. It does not stand. It spreads.

Four feet long when it is folded, five when it extends to reach the flywheel. Broad across the back, flattened by years of staying under things — under catwalk, under pipe flanges, under the eaves where condensation forms first on the underside. Its back is the bowl floor brought up: a carapace not of shell but of accreted skin. Cream calcite laid down in smooth aprons over the shoulders, hard as tooth, soft-looking as icing. In the hollows between, bacterial velvet grows living — blood-orange and sulphur-yellow and dark chocolate, a millimeter thick, wrinkling where water tugs it. Barnacles, white as teeth, have settled on the calcite where it stays wettest. Small black mussels beard together in the crease behind its neck. Touch it — and you will, because it will come to test your laces — and your palm comes away orange. This is its pasture, and it wears the pasture.

Its underside is pale. That is the shock. Turn a slab and crabs scatter; it lifts, once, to shift from one warm seep to the next, and underneath it is the color of shrimp, of amphipods, unmineralized, damp and new. Legs, if you can call them that — arms gone thick and short from crouching, knees calloused to pads, fingers swollen and split at the tips from picking mussels, but still fingers, still with nails, and under the nails orange silt. Its face is mostly brow and cheek, eyes set deep against drip, small and dark and tide-adjusted to the perpetual late-afternoon grey-blue. It blinks sideways, gull-slow. Its whiskers — hair, actually, matted — are always wet. It breathes out and its breath stays a little too long, joining the beading in the air.

It weeps, the way the walls weep. Droplets grow on the cold calcite of its back and fall and grow again. On cold days the water rises out of the pool and condenses from below, and first of all things it beads under its chin, under its elbows, under the pipe it sleeps curled around.

It hears in hum. The transformer box on its sagging poles makes a high thin note you feel in your teeth more than hear, and it has that note. Hold your breath near it and you can hear it answering, very faint, in the fillings of its molars, in the iron in its blood. When the engine chuffs — *chuff... chuff-chuff... chuff* — the whole pumphouse trembles faintly, and its flank trembles faintly in time. When the pump gulps and the level drops four inches, exposing a fresh band of mussel and mat, it moves down with the crabs, unhurried. When the pump rests, shuddering and sighing, and the cold marsh water slides grey back over orange, it rests. Twice a day when the real tide overtops the inlet and the whole bowl goes brackish and cold and everything closes up tight, it closes up tight — pulls its pale underside in, seals its eyes, lets the mussels on its neck snap shut, and waits as stone waits.

It eats what the bowl eats. That is to say, it eats the eaters. It grazes only the outer ring of mat with a flat tongue, leaving the yellow heart to seed. It pries mussels with a thumbnail grown long for that purpose and sucks them cold. It takes bloodworms from silt with a quick sip that leaves a constellation of pits. It does not chase eels — the wrist-thick ones in the center channel slide for cover when it comes — but it drinks where they have nosed, and sometimes in deep cold it will hold an eel still against the warmest seep until the eel goes torpid, and then bite once behind the head. Its smell is iron and eggs. Its dung is shell fragments and orange mud, deposited always at the wrack line where amphipods boil and finish it in minutes.

What it does, more than eat, is keep.

Each slow circuit it wipes the discharge mouth clear of travertine fingers. It smears the flywheel shaft with a grease of eel fat and bacterial slime, keeping the impossible engine turning. It picks wrack from the belt. It nudges tires and duckboards back into place as foundations for new mussel settlement — mussel on concrete on pipe on tire on mussel again, layered, knee to elbow to shell. It sorts. Silt to center where bloodworms need it. Shell to edge where crabs hide. It does not waste sound. The fog mutes, and it has learned not to. Its movement is crab-tick and silt-sigh and *plink... plink... plink-plink* as water drops off it back into the pool.

The others have made their agreement with it.

Crabs graze its back for loose mat and do not pinch the pale underside. Midges column over it where it is warmest. The rat in the pumphouse doorway sleeps curled in the hollow behind its knee in winter, two heartbeats, one fast, one slow as the engine. The herons fish around it. The eels, who know every vibration, do not fear it until the water goes very cold.

It is not alone, exactly. Once, there were more — you can see the other hollows under the corrugated sheets, the other smoothed places on the terraces where a broad back rested. Now the rings are one keeper's width. The rest have settled fully, become foundation, pipe-fossils in new stone. In another fifty years it will be a natural formation with a person inside it like a fossil, the travertine closing over the shoulders, the barnacles meeting across the spine.

For now, if you stand long enough, your own boots begin to be investigated. A crab tests your laces first. Then it comes, low, without lifting its belly clear of the thin sheet moving across the terrace, finding the old expansion joints and running in them like veins. It does not look up. It smells the rubber, the marsh road grass still on your cuffs — green, a smell it no longer carries. It touches your toe with a split fingertip, cool, gritty with calcite. Decides you are neither food nor engine nor stone that needs sorting. A post, briefly.

Then it turns, heavy and slick, and goes back down toward water that is everywhere and it is not raining, back to the drip-counting dark under the catwalk, to its slow heart going *chuff* with the other heart in the brick house, to eating and being eaten without any need for green at all.
