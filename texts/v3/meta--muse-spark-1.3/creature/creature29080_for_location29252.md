Nothing lives here.

That's what you think for the first hour. Then for the first day.

The green you saw from far off resolves, when you get close, into something that doesn't quite resolve. It refuses to be one thing.

Start with the ones that hold the place together. The domes.

Each one is not a plant the way you know plants. It is a city of plants, or a single plant pretending to be a city. Tens of thousands of shoots, packed so tight the wind can't get between them. From a meter away it looks soft — a shaggy nest, a heap of moss left by some giant animal. Put your hand in and it isn't soft. It's dense and springy and complicated. Outer leaves silvered, furred with hollow glass hair. Middle leaves leathery, sealed shut like waxed envelopes. Inner leaves down in the dark, teal and oil-green and almost black, slick with gold.

They are warm. That is the first wrong thing you learn about them. At noon, when the air cuts your lips and your breath is ripped away, the surface of a dome is faintly warm to the touch. At night, when the stars come hard and the salt underfoot rings like iron, push your arm elbow-deep into the thatch and it is summer in there. Animal-warm. Humming — not with sound, but with slow chemistry.

They are not drinking. They have nothing to drink. Break a leaf and no water comes. Only scent, so strong it makes your eyes water: turpentine and lemon peel and hot resin. A clear gold oil that coats your fingers and does not evaporate, does not freeze. That oil is their blood and their fat and their child. They make it out of almost nothing: out of light piped down through those glass hairs, out of dust caught in those hairs and digested leaf by leaf, out of the cold itself. At night their salts sweat — just a beading, a molecular sigh — enough to coax two or three molecules of vapor from that thin air, then by dawn they seal again. They trade acid for stone. Underneath, where you cannot see, they have no roots. They have anchors, glassy threads fused straight into the chalk polygon they sit on, slowly dissolving it. Phosphorus for acid. Iron for acid. Year by year they eat their own island and turn it into oil.

They are old. A dome six meters across, waist-high, has been there longer than any human house. It grows outward a millimeter, a centimeter a year, dying in the center and living at the rim until center and rim are indistinguishable. It poisons its own surroundings just enough. Around each dome there is a bare halo where nothing else roots — the ground dosed with that lemon-turpentine oil until no young shoot can take hold too close. That's why they sit hundreds of paces apart, a loose grid of solitudes to the horizon. Not planted. Negotiated.

If you lie still beside one in mid-afternoon, when the dust pillars start to walk between them, you will see it breathe. Ten thousand sealed mouths open at once. A shimmer lifts off the dome, a heat-haze that is not heat: oil-coated spores, buoyant as dust, rising to join the gold veil that dims the sun for an hour. No rain follows. They fall back as tiny lights onto the polygons. Most land on stone and die. A few land downwind of an old dome, in its dust-shadow, where sloughed leaves and combed dust have softened the poison. That is where the young lean — always downwind of the old, catching what sloughs off. Nurse and child, though neither would admit it.

Between them live the lesser negotiations.

The flats: rosettes of black-green blades laid absolutely flush to the mineral skin, so flat you step on them before you see them. They have given up on height entirely to escape the ten-thousand-year wind. Their leaves are sandpaper on top, mirror-smooth beneath, holding a thin pocket of still, warmer air against the ground. They live for decades as a dinner plate, then in one afternoon put up a single short flower-stalk no higher than your thumb, dust-pollinated, and die.

The lace: fields of it in the lee of the whaleback ridges, a hand high, brittle as spun sugar, white-pink coral lattices that shatter if you put a boot wrong. Each lattice is a colony that took thirty, fifty years to grow that high, catching fog that never comes and dust that always does. Step on it and it explodes into shards that the wind carries. Those shards are alive. Where they lodge, they start again.

And the sails, very rare. You can walk for days and see only two or three. A single stalk, two meters straight up out of nothing, impossibly thin and tough as tendon, topped with a fan of translucent amber membrane veined like an insect wing. It never flutters. In gusts that tear your breath, it turns *into* the wind, edge-on, with a slow creak you feel in your teeth more than hear. It is not sun-tracking. It is dust-tracking. The membrane is sticky on a microscopic scale, electrostatically charged. It combs the passing air for the gold spores, for organic flecks, for iron dust, and then, at dusk, it folds — you can hear the ticking if you are close — and digests what it caught, dripping the surplus down its stalk to feed its own anchor. It is a plant behaving like an animal behaving like a weather vane.

Then the animals, if you can call them that, because they have solved the same problem in the opposite direction: instead of making oil from stone, steal oil already made.

The sipper comes late. For days you see only its writing: a single line of three-toed prints, impossibly straight, going from one dome to the next and then stopping, as if the maker stepped off the world. Then in the slant light you catch it far off, and you mistake it for a yardang at first, or a standing stone. Tall and narrow as a folded umbrella, the color of chalk and salt-pan, long legs that move with absurd, insulting slowness. It does not hurry because hurry is water, and it has none to spend.

Up close it is almost all legs and neck and a narrow head made for burying. It leans over a dome, parts the silvered outer leaves with a beak-like muzzle, plunges in up to the eyes, and sucks. You can hear it if the wind drops: a deep, slow drawing. It drinks the gold oil directly. The dome lets it. Around the wound the leaves weep extra resin and close within minutes, sealing the bite. The sipper lifts its head shining, oil-muzzled, and looks at you without fear. Fear would cost heat, cost moisture. You are not worth it. It walks on, straight-line, to the next island, digesting oil into oil, never urinating, never panting, exhaling only through night-cooled nostrils that recapture every molecule. Where it sleeps, the salt needles lift the stones a finger's width away from it year by year, as if even the ground is repelled by its concentrated life.

Above it, much higher, the strainer.

You will not see it until you learn to watch thermals, not sky. A manta of dust and membrane, three meters across, utterly silent, riding a dust pillar upward in slow spirals with its vast mouth open. Its wings are not feathered but veined amber, like the sails, only huge and flexible. Its body is mostly gut and filter — combs of glass hair inside the mouth that strip the gold spores and dust-motes from the air as it glides. It eats the weather the domes make. It does not call, does not flap unless it must. Flapping is expensive in thin air. It tilts, it banks, it lets the rising column do the work. At dusk it folds its wings around itself like a sail folding and drops, impossibly fast and quiet, to lodge in the lee of a ridge until dawn gives it another pillar. Its droppings, dry pellets of concentrated mineral, are the richest fertilizer on the plain — and the young domes lean toward where it roosts.

All of it sealed, all of it patient. Press your ear to a large dome at dusk and you hear the ticking as ten thousand inner leaves fold against the coming freeze. Stand under a sail and you hear the creak as it turns edge-on again. Otherwise only the high faint whistle of wind broken on glass hairs, and your own boots crunching too loud on pumice.

They do not know you. They have never needed to.
