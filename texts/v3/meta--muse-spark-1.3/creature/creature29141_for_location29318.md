You won't see it at first.

That's the point. You'll think the room is keeping itself. Empty means empty. You'll sit in the green aquarium light and listen to the diesel of wings and decide the bees and the beetles and the shrews are the residents and the hollow is just what happens when plants agree for three hundred years.

Then the print fills in.

You pressed your palm to the wall coming in and water squeezed out and refilled the print. Here on the floor it's the same, but slower. There's a shallow long press in the moss ahead of your knee where the beading is wrong — not beaded, slicked. A body-length of moss combed flat and already drinking itself back upright. By the time you blink it is almost gone. Almost. The moss remembers weight a little longer than water does.

That is the first law of who lives here: it is low, and cool, and the same wet as everything else.

Stay low. Breathe slow. Drink nothing but air. You've been following the rules without knowing they were a description.

She is on the floor. She is the floor, for most of an hour.

Don't look for eyes. Don't look for outline. Look for emptiness being kept. In a place this lush there should be clutter — a seedling, a sapling trying its luck in the drip-cups, a bramble cane reaching in from the creep-hole. There is none. Every drip-cup in the moss is licked clean, round and shining and empty, new growth nipped at the bud stage with a precision no insect has. The center holds. Twelve meters long and the middle meter is mown to velvet by a tongue you haven't seen.

Then she breathes, and you see her because the roar changes around her.

The drone never stops, but over her back it thins. The gnats that cloud the interstices go down to her the way iron goes to a magnet, not in a swarm but in a steady rain. A hundred an hour. A thousand a night. Industrial volume, without pause. They land on what looks like moss and do not take off again.

She is flat in the way the country is flat. Not pressed flat, decided flat. Six, seven feet long if you stretched her, which you never will, and not more than a hand's height off the moss when she moves, less when she rests. Broad head merging to broad body merging to broad paddle tail, all one low pan, no neck, no up. Limbs tucked completely under the fringe when she is still, so there is no shadow-line to catch your eye. In green filtered noon that has no shadows anyway, she disappears.

What gives her away is bead.

Everything here is beaded, but her beading is ordered. Dew stands on her in rows along lateral grooves, large drops that don't fall. Her skin underneath is cold-blooded cold — fifty-two degrees, exactly the air, exactly the wall — smooth amphibian dark olive to pond-green, and on it, growing in the grooves and in the pits behind the jaw, living moss. Not stuck on. Rooted. Sphagnum stars and liverwort scales and a fine feather-moss that waves when she draws breath through her skin. She is plant all the way down, too. The dome grows on her back at a millimeter a year, dies back in winter, grows again. If you touched her — you won't, you understand not to — you would feel first cold slime, then the give of moss, then muscle, slow as peat forming.

Her head is all mouth and all stillness. Wide, blunt snout with nostrils that close to slits. Eyes like the condensation overhead: two clear domes, lidless, liverwort fringed, that catch green light and give nothing back. They do not blink. They do not need to; they are washed by damp. On either side of the neck, where you'd expect gills to have been lost a hundred million years ago when hills were hills, she kept them. Fringed, fern-mimic plumes laid flat back along the body, blood-red at the stem fading to green at the tips from the algae that live in them, pulsing once, twice a minute. Big ferns unfurl with a hydraulic creak. She unfurls nothing. She just opens, very slightly, and water moves.

Because she drinks air.

There is no open water here and she never laps a drip. She doesn't need to. The damp as a condition is her pond. She absorbs it through belly and flank where she seals herself to the floor moss, and through those plumes that comb moisture from breath. The walls water themselves from their own breath; so does she. On her back the big drops swell, hang, are drawn in. When the air charges before rain and the moss crackles with static discharge, her dorsal hairs lift with it. She tastes the weather coming before the dome does.

And she is silent in a way that only something very old in a very loud place can be.

Her throat is a slow bellows. Watch now that you know where the edge of her is: the floor of her mouth drops once every two, three minutes. Breathe slow. Her heart — four, five beats a minute — is completely lost in the sternum roar of wings and click and shrew-hiss and sap-tick. Evolution hid it there on purpose. The wrens in the thatch sing over her. The deathwatch beetles saw in the buried logs under her. She does not answer. To live in an engine-room you become the quietest part of the machine, the held air between pistons.

What she does is tend.

Once a night, maybe less often — time here does not gradient — she makes a circuit. Not walking. Flowing. A low peristaltic oozing on four short splayed legs that never lift enough to tear moss, belly distributing weight so the living thatch never breaks. Walls are alive and load-bearing. She knows. She stays an inch from the walls, never pressing, her whisker-like chin barbels brushing ahead to read vibration. She pauses under each of the hundred drip points. A drop forms overhead, catches green light, grows, lets go, hits — not moss, her snout. Tick. Taken. Another forms in its place. She moves on before it falls.

Where a birch seed has helicoptered in through the creep-hole and dared to sprout, she nips it. Where mold threatens a bee tube in the wall, she licks it clean with a tongue as wide as your hand, leaving the larvae untouched. Where a shrew run tunnels under the floor moss, she settles over it for warmth — cool warmth, cellar warmth — and the shrews run under her, conveyor hiss going quiet for a body-length then resuming, unafraid. They know her the way the forty-generations wren lineage knows her: as weather. The beetles that cross your boot purposeful as forklifts will, if they cross her, be taken without hurry. The tongue flicks a centimeter. Gone. Manufacture continues.

She eats at factory scale because the factory makes at factory scale. Gnats, midges, springtails flicked from the moss with that short tongue, spores puffed from the ceiling that settle on her slime and are drawn to the mouth by ciliary flow, honey-dew scraped from fern. Hundreds a day, thousands a week, no progress, no fattening, just maintenance. What is made here is made by living things, at industrial volume, without pause, without progress. She is the pause that keeps it from progressing. Without her grazing the hollow would fill in a decade — sapling, shrub, trunk — and the cathedral would become wood and fall. She keeps the gourd empty so the air can stay held.

That's why the deer go around. Not fear, exactly. Recognition of cold toxin and of wrong footing. Her skin exudes the bitter alkaloid of ancient newts, cellar-bitter, that says *do not eat* in a language noses understand from a mile away on flat ground with no up to look at. And the dome over a seven-foot body with three feet of peat-moss over that will take a boot but not a hoof. The trails bend twenty paces out and rejoin. They have bent for centuries.

How old? Old past old. She grows the way the dome grows. Count rings and you find none; amphibians that never metamorphose fully, that stay gilled and aquatic in air that is almost water, lay down no hard seasons here because there are no seasons inside. Fifty-two degrees in summer, fifty in winter. Damp always. Loud always. The drip-cups she drinks from have fallen in the same places for three hundred years. She remembers every creep-hole the moss has sealed and opened, same size, same cool, same roar, because she was there for the sealing, pressed flat to the side while sphagnum knit over the old north entrance and a nurse-log rotted out somewhere else to make a new one. She does not hurry to it. The flat will still be flat.

If you stay long enough, breathing slow until your breath shows faintly even in summer and your skin prickles awake, she may decide you are weather too. The gnat on your wrist lifts and joins the rain going down to her. A drop forms overhead, falls by your knee with a soft tick lost in the roar. Another forms. The fringe along her side lifts, settles. Green light passes through it as through water.

You could stretch both arms and touch living wall on either side. She can, without stretching. She lies between presences with room for her body and her breath and not much else, and keeps it that way.
