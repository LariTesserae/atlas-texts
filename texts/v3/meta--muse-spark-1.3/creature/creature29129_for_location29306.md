There is no one here to meet.

That is the first thing you have to unlearn. You keep looking for it — for eyeshine, for movement between stems, for a trail, a nest, a burrow, something that goes in and out. You crouch by the black tarns expecting a frog. You part the reeds expecting insects to lift. Nothing lifts. Nothing watches back.

What lives here does not go in and out. It does not go at all.

It stays. It *is* the stay.

At first you think: moss. Just moss. A deep, cold, drowning carpet of it, star-moss and sphagnum and liverwort braided so tight you can't find where one plant ends. Then you think: no, not moss — a meadow. A reed-sea. A fern-hill. Because as you walk — if you can call sinking and pulling walking — the texture changes. Here a lawn so level it looks mown, each star a perfect rosette pressed to the next. Here a wall of reeds, jointed, hollow, shoulder-high and shoulder-to-shoulder, leaning all alike into the dim light. Here table-sized leaves curled into funnels, dripping. Here clubmoss sprawling, here great fronds unrolling overhead, shuddering off rain.

You think there are many kinds. Many tenants.

Then you stay a second night on top of the sponge, listening to the ticking under your pad, feeling water well up patiently around your weight, and you realize the kinds are not tenants.

They are tissues.

What lives here is one thing wearing many shapes. Not a forest. A body.

Its skin is the lawn-moss. Kilometers of it, velvet and springy, breathing out mist around 14:00 every day until the cloud closes. The skin does not keep water out. It keeps water *in*. Press it and water beads up around your palm, cold as snowmelt, because the skin is saturated to bursting, a perched ocean held in cells finer than any filter we can make. It is pocked with tarns — a thousand, a million shallow eyes without lids that are not eyes at all but kidneys, settling pans, ballast. They sit rimless in the thatch, perfectly spaced, hexagonal, feeding each other by runnels that split and split again. They hold the rain, release the rain, keep the whole vast weight from drowning itself.

Its hairs are the reeds. Dense stands that run to the horizon, that you cannot push through without tearing living flesh. Each stem hollow, each leaf curved like a lens and beaded with a second lens of water. In this half-light — pale sun as a bright bruise, noon like late afternoon — that should not be enough to live on. So they gather. Light is caught, bounced leaf to leaf down into the mat, until even the understory glows faintly green. Put your hand under the canopy and your skin comes up lit. The hairs lean together a few degrees, all of them, an entire valley leaning at once. That is not wind. That is attention.

Its hands are the great ferns and funnel-leaves. Upcurled, table-wide, they catch what falls and shed it ten thousand times. A single raindrop does not hit the ground here. It is caught, channeled, dropped to the leaf below, caught again, and dropped again. That is part of the roar you feel in your chest — billions of simultaneous drips become static become pressure. These hands also move in wind. They rub and creak and groan. Hollow stems boom like organ pipes. Fat seed capsules pop in the cold, sharp cracks rolling for kilometers, millions per hour. You thought at first it was noise. It is circulation.

Because underneath you, meters deep, is the rest of it.

Kneel — sink to the ankle, to the shin — and part the green with numb fingers. Green to black-green to pale white-green where light has never reached, layer upon layer, all interwoven, all alive. Fallen fronds are caught and stitched back in within days. Nothing dead stays dead for long. This thatch is gut and muscle and memory at once. Through it, water climbs uphill. You can hear it if you put your ear down to the moss and give up shouting to the person two meters away: ticking, gurgling, thumping. Hydraulic pulses traveling from hollow to hummock, lifting bog-water tens and hundreds of meters up the slope of the plateau without pumps, without power, only structure. Nested capillaries. Transpiration pull. A continent-sized heart that never stops because dryness does not exist here except briefly inside a sealed seed.

And through that same water, it thinks.

Not fast. Not like us. Pressure waves in the water-table carry information faster than any chemical should, opening and closing stomata in unison across valleys. Step here, and your footfill fills behind you with black water — and a kilometer away, within minutes, growth is redirected to fill the gap you made. Runners cross it within hours. Sporelings green it over. To leave a patch empty would be to leave a processor idle. Spores fill the air so thick your filter clogs; mist fills the gaps between leaves; leaves fill the gaps between stems. Every niche is occupied because occupation *is* the mechanism. It computes with water and sound. The deafening hum in your molars, the surf-shed of fronds unloading rain, the subsonic lift and fall — that is not waste. That is a thought, tuning its own resonance.

It does not need wires. It does not need a brain. Its memory is depth. The mat is meters deep for a reason. The lower layers, white-green, starved of light, are still alive, holding last year's rain, last decade's storm schedule, the shape of the rock underneath that it is patiently burying. This is young country — pale grey fangs of unweathered stone thrusting up sharp as broken teeth, weeping constantly — and it stays young because it is smothered before it can age. The mat creeps outward a few centimeters a year in all directions at once, which at this scale is enormous motion. It buries the newborn rocks before erosion can carve them into valleys. Geology does not get to happen here. Only biology.

Watch its day and you learn its habits, the way you would learn an animal:

In the morning, after the cold night, it holds its breath. Tarns glassy. Mist low. Light gathering in the lenses.

Around midday, the exhalation begins. Columns of mist rise off the moss. You can watch your own horizon dissolve as the plateau makes its weather. The living surface seeds its own cloud.

Most afternoons, it rains — which is to say, it waters itself. Then it sheds, shudders, booms, pops, gurgles. At dusk, the hydraulic ticking gets loudest.

It dips, just before rain, for a few seconds. The roar falters. That silence will frighten you more than the roar ever did. You will understand then how much weight you were leaning on.

It has no mouth, no eyes, no need for animals at all. Plant-water-plant, plant-light-water. Growth, overgrowth, drowning, rafting up, growth again. Storms born, storms shed. It does not eat. It harvests dimness. It does not drink. It *is* drink, holding a storm's worth and releasing it on schedule. It does not hunt or hide. There is nothing else to eat it — no grazers, no rot-flies, no birds to carry seed — and that is its strangest mercy: total, packed, furiously active life with no interruption.

If you stay long enough for the cloud to lift — that rare hour when the light strengthens to almost gold — you will see it cold-steaming in the cool air, dripping, humming, spread across a roof of the world past the curve of sight, lawn to reed-sea to fern-hill to black tarn field and back again but never thinning, never edging.

It will not notice you. Your bootprint fills with water and greens over. Your shout is lost two meters out. Your fire will not burn because dead wood is too full of water and too quickly reclaimed.

You are not prey. You are not guest. You are a brief dryness, a temporary disturbance in pressure, compensated for a kilometer away.

What lives here lives as place.
