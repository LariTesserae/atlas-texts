At first you think nothing lives here.

That is what the city wants you to think. The windows are lit and empty. The doors open onto black water. You cross a bridge and your lamp finds only motes, ten, twenty meters of warm dark between islands. No faces. No voices — shouting is useless against the screws. Only the roar in your sternum and the rain of silt through your beam.

Stay longer. Shut off your need for faces. Life here does not come to the window.

The first citizen is not an animal at all. It is the felt.

Every surface below the waterline wears it, a millimeter thick, rust-red and mustard and black. Velvety where the current is slow behind a stanchion or under a lip of catwalk, streamlined to a slick, almost varnish where the waterfalls scour past. If you brush it with a glove it peels in a sheet, soft as wet paper, and underneath the steel is pitted and weeping. Put your lamp close and it is not flat. It has nap, direction, little terraced ridges combed by flow. It breathes, in its way. When the system backflushes — that eleven-minute inhalation when the pressure drops in your ears and the lights go red — the felt lifts at the edges and ripples, holds on.

It is the only thing growing, and they told you rightly: leave it alone. It is holding the bolts together more than you want to know. It eats oil and iron and heat and the faint sulfur sweating out of the warm rock, and in return it seals. In the quietest eddies it goes pale and thick. If you kill your lamp and wait, some joints along the spine-pipe glimmer faintly where the slime has learned to make its own light — not enough to see by, just enough to mark a seam in the dark, green-white pinpricks like a town seen from very far away.

Where there is felt, there are grazers.

You will meet the crab before you meet anything else with eyes, because the crab does not run. White, the size of a spread hand, shell soft from living its whole life in warm, faintly acid water that never lets it properly harden. It picks at a cable with claws gone almost translucent at the tips, scraping felt, working methodically along armored sheath while the current plucks at its legs and it holds on with a patience that looks like prayer. Its eyes are white beads, useless. It feels with its feet. When the flush comes it flattens itself into a bolt-hole and lets debris spiral over it — washers, spall, plastic sheeting — and afterwards it climbs out and resumes, as if interruption were weather, which here it is.

There are more of them than you think. You only ever see one at a time because your lamp only makes a three-meter world. Beyond that, in black, hundreds of soft feet are working the city, keeping the slime cropped, keeping the felt from closing completely over the gauges.

And then, once per shift, perhaps — the lords.

A pale thing crosses your beam and is gone. You turn too fast, silt explodes, and there is nothing. You wait. You learn to wait without moving, clipped to the trolley line, lamp low, letting your bubbles be seized and flung south in a long helix toward the screens. And it comes back.

Catfish. Blind catfish stocked decades ago to eat the slime. No one stocks them now. They stock themselves.

Huge and slow, a meter, sometimes more, inbred to translucence. In your lamp the body is not white but pinkish-clear, and you can see the shadow of the spine inside, the dark line of gut full of felt, the slow bellows of gills. No eyes, or eyes sunk to smooth pits under skin. Long barbels drift ahead of it like hands, tasting the water for the chemical trail of the mat, for oil, for the electrical tang of the bus bars. It does not swim so much as hang neutrally and let the gyre carry it, with an occasional deep scull of tail to correct — because here up is another direction to walk and a dropped wrench does not fall but wanders off sideways, so does a fish. It has learned the circulation the way you learn streets: ride the north inflow down, coast the wall galleries, let the south surge push you gently toward the screens and turn at the last second before the bypasses kick.

It is not afraid of you. Nothing with no predators in forty years, in warm blood-water, in dark, is afraid. It will pass within arm's reach, and you will feel the displacement on your suit before you see it, a warm pressure, and smell briefly, even through your hood, a sweet, muddy, metallic smell — living flesh in a place of rubber and stone. Then it folds back into black, trailing silt from its barbels.

Above them, at the very top of the food chain that should not exist, the rats.

You never see the rats in the water. You see where they have been, on the uppermost dry grating near the shaft where the ladder comes out sweating and humming: small prints in silt, a tail-drag line, gnaw marks on a tarp edge. They live in the interface, in the almost-dry. They come down for condensation and for warmth and for what the divers drop — crumbs of energy bar, dead crabs hauled up in a bucket, felt scraped off a boot. They drink warm oily water and they thrive on heat. Their fur is thin, greasy. They have learned the eleven-minute breathing too; they nest above the high-water stain, in cable tray lined with shredded anti-fouling bag, where the flush vibration will not shake the pups loose. At night — which is always — you hear them sometimes in an air bell, above the doubled roar, a skitter too quick to be machine-clatter.

And the divers. The almost-people.

Very rarely, another diver — a flash of lamp across the void, there and then not, gone behind a pump-house. You do not call out. Sound does not carry as voice here; it carries as vibration. You feel his check-valve slam through your teeth half a second before you see his light swing.

No one lives here the way a town is lived in. The numbered houses — 3-C, 3-D, 4-A — are not houses. They are kiosks with amber instrument glow, too small to stand in, humming. Divers are migrants. They come down the shaft hand-over-hand, clip to the guide ropes strung like trolley lines between buildings because no one swims freely here, do their work with cold fingers in warm water, and hang in the air bells with just their masks out, breathing hot diesel-rubber-stone air while condensation rains upward around them and their skull rings.

You recognize them by habit, not face. The way they move with the surge, not against it, letting the exhale push them south toward the screens and catching a stanchion at the last moment. The way they conserve lamp, dimming until darkness, which is total and warm and leans on you, presses close. The way they touch railing worn bright by gloves, and pipe warm as skin, with a kind of tenderness. They are pale things too, after hours, sweating inside their suits, eyes red from oil film, watching a pressure needle quiver for what feels like two minutes and is twenty.

They do not stay. They ascend, hauling themselves out of the warm sea back into warm air, and the roar follows them up the shaft for a long way.

What stays, what truly lives here when the lamps dim to red and the klaxon you feel more than hear vibrates the catwalk, is bigger than any of them.

The city itself.

Listen: low bass of the main screws turning somewhere below you, felt in the teeth — a heart. Mid-range howl of inflow, two waterfalls the thickness of a bus pouring in from the north grates — lungs. Clatter of check-valves slamming, loose manhole cover lifting and dropping, lifting and dropping, *clang - clang - clang* without pause — joints, teeth. High whine of bus bars, hiss of cavitation eating impellers, *bloop* of huge air slugs burping out of side galleries and racing sideways, not up, because the current grabs them — gut sounds.

Every eleven minutes it inhales. Debris lifts and spirals. Walkways shudder. Oil on the ceiling air-bells tears into rainbow skins. Then it exhales, and pushes everything gently but irresistibly south.

The felt coats it. The crabs clean it. The catfish patrol its streets. The rats nest in its eaves. The divers are its fleas, its brief itch, clipped on, peering at gauges like doctors at a chart.

You thought from outside this was supposed to be a room. From inside it is a downtown drowned in a bottle, and like any downtown at 3 a.m., it is complete and hollow and not empty at all — just awake in a way that does not need you.
