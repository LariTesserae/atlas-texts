It is not a crowd. That's the first mistake.

When you duck sideways through the iron door you think: many things live here. Moss, and fern-things, and bead-things, and strap-things hanging down. A miniature jungle shoulder to shoulder. You try to count kinds.

Then you put your hand down — because you have to, the ceiling won't let you stand — and you feel it all the way up your arm: the tremble is the same everywhere.

What lives here is one thing wearing many shapes.

Call it the mat, because you need a word, but it is not moss on top of other plants. Underneath, where the green goes dark and your fingers can't quite get in, there is no separation. Lift an edge, just a corner where the felt has pulled a little loose from a rivet, and you don't find roots going down into distinct individuals. You find felt. White, hair-fine, endless, dry as old wool, matted into a single sheet a centimeter thick that runs uninterrupted over basalt and over plate and over bolt-head and over the rust-welded wrenches in the pried toolbox. Everything green you see above is just the felt deciding, here and there, to push up a different kind of finger.

In one place it pushes up cushion. Black-green mounds, knee-deep where they swell, springy as upholstery when you press and then crackling faintly because there is no water in them to squeeze out. The cells are full of oil, not sap. That's why they gleam dully in the pilot-lamp amber, like a moth's wing — every leaf-hair coated in transformer mist and graphite dust.

In another place the same felt pushes up rosettes. Dense, papery, dusty fronds that are not fern and not lichen, overlapping like shingles. They don't turn to the slit of daylight at the door. They turn edge-on to light and face-on to vibration. Their flat sides square up to the east wall where the transformer snarls its 50-cycle snarl. If you watch long enough in the loud dark you see them shivering with it, not from wind — there is no wind in here — but because they are tuned to it. Their undersides are freckled rust-red. That's iron they've taken straight out of the plate, laid down into their tissue. Some of the older fronds have veins of actual verdigris, green copper salts where copper filings from the conduit have been dissolved and re-laid like blood vessels.

Over the bolt-heads it makes beads. Stonecrop-like, tight-packed, using the dome of each bolt as a hill to catch a little more mist. From the overhead conduit it lets down straps — pale, almost translucent ribbons that hang and do not swing, too dry and stiff to swing, though the whole tower howls around them. Hold one up to the door-seam and the light comes through it from behind, and you can see the amber of the pilot jewel still in it, as if the tissue remembers being lit electrically from the inside.

It has no mouths. That is the point. In any other lush place there would be chewing and rot and flies. Here there is nothing to eat it and nothing for it to eat in the usual way. No flies, no mites, no spiders. Nothing rots. A dead leaf does not decay; it just dries further and becomes more felt for the mat to stand on.

What it does instead is drink without drinking.

It drinks the tower. Day and night the big oil-cooled brick in the east wall sweats a warm aerosol of dielectric mist — so fine you don't see it until your lamplight catches the column of dark dust hanging motionless — and breathes a faint electric field. The mat drinks that. The leaf-hairs comb the mist out of the dry air. The white felt conducts the field the way a wet root would conduct water. It eats rock dust ground off the basalt by vibration, and skin cells left by linemen, and grease, and the iron and copper it freckles itself with. Lush without ever being wet. Green without ever being watered.

And because of that it doesn't need to change.

That's what unsettles you as you crouch with green pressing into your knees and elbows. The old linemen are right. The photos from '64 and '82 show the same mounds in the same places, the same height against the half-swallowed 7-B stencil. This is not a young colony spreading or an old forest dying. It has found exactly how much growth the mist can feed and how much wear the dry cold can take, and stopped.

It propagates by flaking. A dry edge breaks off a rosette, lodges a centimeter away on felt, puts out white hairs and becomes a new rosette that looks exactly like the old one. No bloom, no seed, no wither, no sex anyone can see. No increase. No retreat. The moss in the deepest corner, where no light at all reaches, is still that blind electric green — because it was never really living on light in the first place.

If it senses anything, it senses tone. The moss nearest the transformer is a fraction taller, a fraction yellower for ten centimeters and then the gradient stops, as if the mat can only stand so much warmth before the cold reasserts. Everything else is even. Even in cold that fogs your breath. Even while the counterweight chain clanks... clanks... clanks somewhere above in its channel and the cables scream into white nothing outside.

You understand, with the hum in your teeth, that you are not looking at plants that happen to survive in a machine. You are looking at a machine that has grown a skin, and the skin has learned to listen.

It holds on. Packed tight, shoulder to shoulder with itself, felt on steel on stone, face turned toward the singing.
