No one lives here the way you live somewhere.

At first, when you drop in and hang motionless and let the silt settle, that's what you think: that this place is empty and the animals are just visiting. The water is too loud, too iron, too square. Then your eyes adjust to the jaundiced lamp-light and the green grate-light, and the hall resolves — not as empty with things in it, but as crowded.

They are not on top of the building. They are in its habits.

Start with what holds everything up.

The mussels. Black, knobby, numberless. They pave every horizontal iron surface until iron is only a rumor underneath. On the plinths, on the pipe flanges, on the cable trays sagging under their own concretions, on the crane rail where no mussel should be able to hold against vibration and yet they do. Thickest on the flywheel — that slow beard that turns once every twenty seconds and has thrown the wheel out of true, so the whole hall breathes with its wobble.

If you put your face close — and you have to get close, within the six feet the lamps give you — a mussel bed is not a mat. It is a city with streets. Shells gape and close, gape and close, siphoning the slow gyre the flywheel stirs. Between them, barnacles like white volcanoes, rust scale, silt caught and re-caught. When the relief valve blows and hot water belches for the grates, they all clamp at once. You can hear it if you listen past the drone: a faint, acre-wide click. Then, minute by minute, they open again.

They are the livestock and the barn both. Without them there would be no floor to stand on, biologically speaking. Their shit and pseudofeces rain down to make that soft gray ooze with the powdered concrete and tile grit. Their spat feeds everything else.

In the grout between the bottle-green and off-white tiles, the frost.

White anemones, fistfuls of them, packed so tight in the checkerboard seams they look like ice forming along the lines. Each one no bigger than your thumbnail, a crown of short tentacles working ceaselessly. They don't move. They don't need to. The hall brings food to them — mussel spat drifting in the orbit, copepods knocked loose when the sump pump clunks on and the current reverses. When a shadow passes they retract to pimples. When the fry school wheels overhead they extend a fraction more, hoping for what falls.

Stay still and you will see a crab pick its way among them without triggering a single sting. That is learned traffic.

The crabs should not be here. Shore crabs, green and knuckled, miles — in generations — from any shore. Someone's larvae came in on a flood decades ago and never left. Now they own the floor.

They move sideways through the ooze in that dotted, deliberate pick, leaving twin trails that the gyre erases by evening. They are the cleaners and the polishers. The top of Switch Cabinet Three — that warm, flat island where the transformer still leaks a degree of heat into the water — is wiped bald of mussels. Not by divers. By crab feet, day after day, year after year, commuting across it because it's warm and high and safe to rest and pick at food. If you hover there you feel it too: a thin envelope, 13 degrees instead of 12, that makes your fingers ache less. The crabs know. On a cold morning they pile there, antennae waving in the transformer hum, waiting for their joints to loosen.

They freeze when the valve blows. Utterly. Legs mid-step. That is how you know they are old residents: the young freeze late. These freeze a half-second before the *whoomph*, feeling it in their legs through the tiles before it happens.

Above them, in the pipes, the tenants.

Eels live in the flanges. Every vertical pipe thick as a tree trunk that rises to vanish into the gloom has mouths — joints, valve bodies, broken gauge taps — and in each mouth, if you wait, an eye and a jawline resolves. Then a headribbons out, two feet of muscle the color of wet concrete, holding station in the hiss of half-closed valves.

They do not swim. They *inhabit*. A big female in Engine Two's feed pipe has lived in the same flanged mouth so long her body has polished the rust around it smooth. She hunts by letting the hall hunt for her. When the silver schools scatter at the valve blast, she strikes — a sideways snap so fast your lamp catches only the after-vortex — and withdraws with a fry flickering in her jaws. Between blasts she just hangs, gills pulsing slow in the 11-degree cool, watching you watch her. If you come too close she doesn't flee deeper. She withdraws an inch. This is her doorway. She will not be evicted.

There are half a dozen like her. Each pipe has its temperament. The ones near the warm-water vent are fatter, lazier. The ones near the doorway with its door dogged open are lean and quick, used to the surge of river water when the level outside rises.

The silver between the machines — that is the congregation.

Schools of small fish, each holding its own station in the emptiness because emptiness is safest. Roach and perch fry, estuarine sticklebacks, sprats if the water has turned salt — it hardly matters. What matters is they have divided the hall the way the machines divided it.

Engine One school hangs east of the crane rail, tight and nervous, flashing all at once when they turn across the lamp. Engine Three school, out in the blue-green dark beyond good visibility, is looser, older fish, holding in the grate-light columns full of motes. Engine Four school are babies, this year's, no longer than your fingernail, clinging to the filament algae streamers on the upper pipe edges where light falls strongest.

They move as one body because they have to listen as one body. The hall is loud — drone and hiss and tick-tick-tick of loose chain and fizz of rust pinhole and your own bubbles bursting loudly under the ceiling in that trapped silver lake. A single fish can't hear a pike coming. A hundred fish can feel it in the lateral line before it moves. When they turn, their sides catch the jaundiced orange for an instant and the whole school becomes a signal mirror. That's not beauty. That's communication.

And then the one they are signaling about.

The pike holds under the crane rail and does not move. As long as your arm, mottled green-brown-gray that matches blistered primer-gray and rust-brown perfectly, gills working slow and steady, eyes fixed forward. Utterly still except for that. You could swim past three times and think she is a pipe shadow.

She is the warden. She cruises the perimeter only at the shift changes — when the flywheel's gyre yields to the sump pump's draw, when the light shaft moves a few feet across the floor, when noon for ten minutes drops that solid pillar straight down to the checkerboard and the fry gather in it like dust in a cathedral window. Then she goes. Not fast at first. A drift, a correction, a drift. Then a dart that empties the water. The whole ecology spasms — fry scatter to the switch cabinets, eels strike, crabs freeze, anemones retract — and then, minute by minute, it re-knits, and she is back under the rail, gills moving, digesting.

Near the warm-water vent, where the relief discharges, live the pensioners.

Two, sometimes three fat codlings, blind-eyed — cataracts milky white from living too long in dark and sudden heat. They don't hunt. They wait where the water shudders hot for a few seconds every few minutes and brings stunned fry tumbling down. They hover nose into the current, pectorals fanning just enough to hold, mouths opening and closing with bovine calm. Their skin is loose, scarred by barnacle scrapes, furred at the fins with the same dark freshwater sponge that crusts the corners. They have found the one place in the hall that is always a little summer, and they will never leave it. When the valve blows hard enough to rattle loose sheet metal, they ride it like old men in a hot bath, eyes closed.

Under all of them: the fringe that makes it possible. Sparse streamers of green filament algae on the upper edges where grate-light falls strongest, swaying like poor banners. Pale tufts in tile cracks. Dark crusts of sponge filtering what mussels miss. No meadow. Just enough green to say light gets in, barely, and to anchor the babies.

If you stay long enough the loudness sorts itself and you understand: nothing here is lost. The mussels found horizontal iron and made reef. The anemones found grout and made frost. The crabs found a warm cabinet and made a hearth. The eels found flanged mouths and made burrows. The fry found empty water between giants and made chapels. The pike found a rail shadow and made an ambush. The codlings found a leak and made a spa.

Very old, very small, very full. Running on in the dark below the world, without you, on the building's terms.
