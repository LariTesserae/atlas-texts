At first you don't see anyone. You hear them.

Or rather, you hear that the roar has seams in it — human noise stitched into machine noise. A shout torn off short. A laugh that doesn't carry ten feet. Metal clanged against metal up on a catwalk, deliberate, a signal. Then nothing for a long while except pumps and water and frogs and birds, and you think: no one could live here. People work here, in shifts, and leave.

Then the mist thins on the middle bench and they resolve, and once you've seen one you see them everywhere, thinly, the way oil spreads thin on water. They were there all along, the color of the walls.

They are waders. That is the first and last fact about them.

Everyone here wades. The foreman in the site cabin, the electrician with the burned cuffs, the kid sent down to clear a trash rack — all of them to the knee, to the thigh, boots full and staying full. Their trousers are rolled and unrolled and rolled again and finally left soaked. Their feet are white and ridged as river stones from never drying. Trench-foot pink at the edges, black fungus between the toes that no powder kills because the powder pastes instantly. They move slowly, not from laziness but from read. Every step here is a test — will this silt hold, will this grating hold, will this hummock be a hummock or a carp. They have the slow high step of herons, and like herons they pause a lot, head cocked, listening.

Because no one can hear. That shapes them more than wet.

They are a shouting people gone hoarse. Even their rest-voices are shouts. Throat-raw, ear-ringing, they lean in to a distance that would be a fight elsewhere, mouth to ear, to say CHECK FLANGE FOUR, to say SUMP'S UP SIX INCHES. They all wear the orange plugs on strings around their necks — bright dots in the yellow-grey — but no one puts them in when working, because then you can't hear the pump change pitch, and the pump changing pitch is the difference between wading and drowning. So they go half-deaf by degrees. The old pump men are profoundly deaf and proud of it; they read lips and vibration through their boots. They press a palm to a pipe as thick as a torso and nod. They feel the Pit the way you feel a sick animal breathing.

You learn them by their stains. High-vis gone the color of tea and rust, reflective tape dulled with algal slime. Arms to the elbow rust-orange from ditch water, hands nicked and permanently pruned. Heat rash up the back where the shirt never unsticks. Eyes red from diesel shimmer and lack of true daylight. They sweat and stay sweated. Towels in the cabins on stilts are black with mildew, laundry strung outside never dries, only gets warmer and wetter. Inside the cabins the air is soup with cooking oil and boot rot and ozone from the drying room that doesn't dry. Bunks with swollen doors that won't shut. A television always on, turned up to a scream to beat the marsh through the thin walls, no one really watching. Cigarette smoke that can't rise. A shrine of plastic flowers wired to a handrail outside where someone slipped — the flowers beaded constantly, too wet even for dust.

They are not building. They are holding. That is their work and their religion.

Each pump house has its keeper — one or two men per grumbling steel box, on every second bench down the spiral. They live with that specific engine the way a night nurse lives with a patient. They know its normal hammer and its tired hammer and its about-to-cavitate scream. They bleed air, tighten flanges with wrenches slick as fish, stack sandbags that sink by morning, pull reeds and plastic and dead gulls off the intakes with gaffs. The intake screens have to be raked every hour in the rains, every two when it's only weeping. If they stop, the bench they stand on becomes a lake by the next shift. They know this without being told. Keep the machines running or drown. So they keep them running. They siphon, they patch hot leaks with rags that steam, they lay plywood over fords, they stake painted sticks to watch the tea-colored water climb millimeter by millimeter up the legs of the idle dragline, that drowned dinosaur left mid-stride on the bottom floor, now a heronry and a depth gauge.

Between houses are the walkers — electricians, fuelers, fitters — who do the vertical miles on the haul road and the stairwells and the rusted catwalks over the drains. They travel in pairs because the fresh cut slumps without announcement, a slow slur that takes a handrail and the ground under it. They carry sticks to probe. They carry radios that mostly hiss. They communicate in big arm signals borrowed from crane ops because shouting fails in the bass note under the spillways. A fist raised: KILL IT. A palm patting air: EASE OFF. Both arms crossed over head: GET UP, WATER'S MOVING.

They don't look up much. Warm rain — or condensate falling off the pipe bridges overhead — is just weather. Only newcomers flinch.

What truly lives here, what owns the place and is increasing while the men thin out, is not men.

The ledges are white with birds. That is not a figure. Every bench lip that stays dry for a week is mortared white with droppings and jammed nest to nest — egrets shoulder to shoulder with night herons, gulls screaming over crakes, swallows stitching the air between. When a pump kicks up a gear the whole wall lifts at once, a sheet of wings that whites out the work lights for a second, and the scream rises a notch until even diesel is drowned. They have learned the machines. They nest in the conveyor gallery, under every eave and pipe bridge, in the warm lee of transformer yards, on the concrete plinths meant to stay dry, on the boom around the fuel farm, on the handrail with the plastic flowers. Chicks gape in the perpetual dusk, mouths red as warning lights. The guano, ammonia-sharp, is the only sharp smell in the sweet rot, and it cuts the rust for a moment before the warm wet folds back over.

Under the birds is the air itself, alive. At bench level in the dim, the midges and gnats and mosquitoes hang in pillars, in curtains, so dense you breathe them and stop trying not to. They breed in the oil-skimmed pans and the rust ditches and the warm seep pools faster than any spray can touch — spray would kill the fish and the swallows and still lose. So the men wear headnets that stick to sweat and slap and give up. And through those pillars cut the swallows by day and the bats by night, ceaseless, fat as fists, roosting in packed brown chattering masses that fur the undersides of catwalks and cable trays. Shake a pipe and a hundred eyes open.

Below them, winning, is the marsh chorus.

Wade to the bottom lakes, knee to waist in water warm as blood, and the floor erupts outward from your boots. Frogs by the ten thousand — green and brown and gold, throats pulsing — plopping off every hummock, off sandbags, off the treads of sunken trucks. Tadpoles blacken the shallows in writhing carpets so thick your bootprint fills in seconds with a swirl of black commas. Water beetles dimple the surface, dragonflies big as sparrows hawk blue over the reeds, leeches wait in the tea-color, patient as the water itself. In the deeper overtoppings between benches, carp and catfish roll slow, fat on midges, unbothered by roar, their backs breaking the surface like oiled stones. At the slightly darker dim that passes for dusk, the rails and crakes stitch their calls through the engine drone, and the bitterns boom low underneath it all, a bass under bass.

Even the smallest things here are thriving in the sourness. Dark green-black algal mats slick every stagnant pan and make the plywood treacherous. Iron-red slime bleeds from every lime-weeping crack in concrete. The wire-thin pioneer reeds along the ditches, yellowed, hold on and spread a foot a year. The fuzz of seeded grass on the one south-facing bench shivers in vibration but holds, roots finding purchase in pyrite-sour fines that would kill a garden. Mold furs the cabin walls, the life jackets, the paperbacks left open. Rust itself blooms — tubercles on handrails eaten from inside, orange fans spreading down every wall from every bolt hole.

And the machines are becoming animals. The pump volutes furred with swallows' mud, the idle pipe runs ticking as they cool and dripping their own rain, the warm jets at leaking flanges making little permanent rainbows in the work lights where midges dance. The engines shudder, miss, catch — breathing in their own warm breath and breathing it out again, just like the Pit.

Stay until your clothes are soaked through and you stop wiping your face and your molars ache with noise. From the bottom, looking up, the rim is a thin impossible bright line. Up there is dry. Down here the wet steps rise all around, dripping, steaming, crowded wing to wing, lit like an aquarium at night. A keeper leans into another keeper's ear to shout. A heron swallows a frog whole. The water keeps weeping out of new earth that has not yet learned to be a hillside. The marsh takes another yard while you watch, too slowly to see, too surely to stop.
