No one, at first. That's what you think, crouched with your knees already going through.

You wait for a door to open. For something with eyes to resent you. Nothing does. The mist stays in the runnels. The ticking goes on — water off rock, water off leaf, water off ice. A beetle the size of a poppy seed picks its way across a willow shingle not much faster than the leaf grows, and you understand you have mistaken scale for emptiness.

Then you stay longer, until the cold gets into your jaw and you stop fidgeting, and the place starts to move at its own speed.

The tenants first, because they are the quickest and therefore the easiest to see as alive:

Mites, amber and rust-red, armored like tiny ticks, grazing the moss stems hair by hair. They do not walk so much as ooze, every joint stiff with cold. Springtails — grey-white, long as an eyelash — gather in the damp axils where water never quite leaves, and when your breath hits them they go off all at once with a soft salt-spatter, a pop you feel more than hear, and settle again. The beetle, black and pitted, hunts them. It will take it all afternoon to cross one roof. It can afford to. Nothing here hurries because nothing here can afford to waste heat.

But they are not the ones who live here. They rent. If you lift a dome-cushion very gently with numb fingers and look underneath, into the green-black thatch where light has already given up, you will see the real multitude: in every bead of water cupped between leaves, a whole city inside the city. Rotifers opening like flowers. Nematodes swimming slow figures-of-eight. Tardigrades — water bears — hauling their fat, clawed bodies over moss forests that to them are redwoods, stopping, tucking, waiting out the freeze by becoming almost stone and then, in thaw, un-becoming. One droplet on one hair holds more citizens than you could count in a day. When it falls with its soft tick into the runnel, it carries them downhill toward the marsh-eye. That is their circulation. That is their streetcar.

And then, slower still, the citizens themselves. The ones who are the town.

You have to unlearn animal to see them. They do not move. They *insist*.

Each hut-dome is one. Not a house with someone inside, but a some-one shaped like a house. A hummock of sphagnum, a thousand shoots packed shoulder to shoulder, tips red as embers in the blue light because they are still growing, even now, even in cold that stops your fingers. Below the red is gold, below gold is green-black, below that is brown, below that is peat — its own dead ancestors, preserved by acid and cold, holding it up a finger's width above drowning. It breathes by holding water. A sphagnum shoot has no roots. It drinks with its whole body, twenty times its weight, and in drinking it makes the bog more boggy, more acid, more itself. To live here is to make the place you live in wetter.

Beside them, flat and dark as liver, the liverwort banks. They do not rise. They spread, skinning over willow root and fallen leaf, sealing seams. Where a dome weeps too fast, a liverwort will creep across the leak in a week and slow it. Where meltwater wants a gutter, the liverwort yields and hollows, letting itself be carved because to be carved is to be needed. Touch it and it is cold and slick and astonishingly strong.

And the elders: the dwarf willows. Thick as your thumb at the base, bark grey and polished by ice, wood so dense with decades that a two-foot stem might be older than you. They lie down instead of standing up — that is their wisdom. They crawl along the mat, throw up a spray of twiggy shoots no higher than your ankle, leaf out in tiny, varnished, round leaves that overlap like scales, and then let wind and snow press them back down until stem fuses to stem. That is how the tunnels are made. Not built. Allowed to collapse into usefulness. In high summer — if you can call this pallid thaw summer — they will put out catkins no bigger than a fly, dust-yellow, sweet for three days. That is their whole loud season. The rest of the year they creak. You heard it: that low adjustment under snow weight. That is an elder turning in sleep.

Upper town, against the weeping cliffs, live the hardiest: cushion saxifrage pulled taut as drum-skin over moss, and the lichens, grey-green and orange-grey, crusting the driest crowns. They live on almost nothing — rock-sweat, mist, light filtered to soup. A cushion is a trap for warmth. Its dome shape, its tight hairs, its packed rosettes hold a degree or two of summer inside itself long after the air has given up. Put your palm — if you can still feel — just above one at midday and you will feel it: a faint exhalation, warmer than it should be. Inside that warmth, midges that should not be alive here overwinter as larvae. Inside that warmth, seeds wait years for one good week.

They talk, after a fashion. Not with sound — the moss eats sound. With water and chemistry and fungus.

Under everything, through the meter of living and dead mat, runs white thread: mycorrhizae, fungal hyphae finer than hair, splicing willow root to saxifrage root to moss rhizoid. When one dome is too wet and begins to drown, it leaks sugars differently. When willow leaves are nibbled by that seed-sized beetle's larvae, they bitter the air with scent too faint for you. The mat tastes it and shifts growth a millimeter that way or this way. Next thaw, an alley narrows. A doorway you crawled through yesterday is shut by a fresh shoot, and two feet over, where drainage runs faster, a new soft place opens. No council. No plan. Just every shoot inching toward a little less wet or a little more light, and water deciding which inch survives.

They live by freezing. That is the pulse you mistook for stillness. By night — which is most of the time here — needle ice grows up through the mat in silver combs, lifting whole streets a half-inch, cracking with that sharp *pink* you heard. It breaks hairs. It pries stems apart. It hurts. By pallid day it melts, and everything slumps, sighs, knits. That lift and slump is how the hummocks climb. That crack and knit is how they heal. Death, when it comes, is not removed. There is no away here, no dead wood to carry off because nothing woody ever dies above ground. The lower leaves brown and sink and become the foundation. You are walking — crouching — on grandmothers a thousand years deep. The town stands on its own compacted dead, and the dead hold water for the living.

So it is fully occupied and empty at once because you were looking for the wrong kind of face. No birds — too low, too wet, nowhere to perch that isn't breathing. Only that once-a-winter stitch of ptarmigan tracks across a roof, white feet on green, gone by melt. The ptarmigan does not live here. It visits, the way you visit.

What lives here lives low, and wet, and together, and without ever cutting. To cut would be to open yourself to freeze. So nothing is severed. Even the water is not channeled so much as persuaded. The central marsh-eye — that black cup of sky-reflecting water in lower town that never quite freezes — is not a well. It is a lung. Or an eye that blinks very slowly with ice. Everything drains toward it and everything drinks from its vapor.

Stay until you cannot feel your boots and you will see one more thing: your own footprint, full of dark water before you even lift your foot, already beading at the edges with spores. Already closing. The mat gives under you and sighs water up, and when you step away it sighs again and settles a millimeter lower, a millimeter wetter. In a week no one — not even you — will be able to tell you were there. In a year that dent will be a runnel. In ten years it will be a lane with willow arched over it.

That is how they live. Not in the town. As the town.
