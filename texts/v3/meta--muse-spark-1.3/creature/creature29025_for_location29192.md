At first you think nothing lives here.

That is what the place wants you to think. It is so loud, so scoured, so new, that life would be an insult to it. Open warm water over black glass, nothing, nothing, then one more moss island. An hour can pass with only pumice knocking against your shins like teeth.

Then you stay still enough — which is hard, because the surge worries at you and the roar gets into your molars — and the spacing reveals itself. Life here is not rare because it is failing. It is rare because it is spaced. Embarrassed to take up room, yes, but also correctly parceled. There is only so much holding-on to go around.

Start small, because small is what lasted.

On every leeward edge, the black-green beards. Algae, but not the soft pond kind. This is rope-algae, flattened by a current that never stops into streamers tough as horsehair. If you lift a strand — and you have to brace to do it — you see why it lives: it does not resist. It combs. And combed into it, clinging with impossible delicacy while tons of water stand upright around them, are the true citizens.

Shrimp. Translucent as contact lenses, long as an eyelash, then as a fingernail. Dozens to a beard. They pulse, not swim. Their whole bodies are ears. In a place where shouting is eaten before it leaves your mouth, where a sharp noise calls a wind that knocks you sideways, they have learned the first law: make no sharp noise. They move in the troughs of the roar. When the deep *whoom* comes up from a pit, they flatten a half-second *before* it — they heard the future crash, the way you did, and believed it. They ride the hairline streams that run uphill to the center mound, letting the wrong water carry them up without kicking. If you watch long enough you see their commute: out to the tips of the algae to filter the black milk of ground glass and ash, back to the root to hide when fresh rock spalls off with that glassy *tink* under water. They are clear because there is nothing to hide behind. They are many because something has to eat the light that gets filtered down to olive-grey and turn it into motion.

Where there are shrimp, there are keepers of stillness.

Under the rock ledge, the crab. The color of ash, the size of your palm. You thought it was a stone until you realized no stone in this young place has learned to be that perfectly still. The torrent passes *over* it. Pumice rattles past. Silt clouds and clears. It does not blink — it has no lids to blink — it does not brace. It lets the mountain hum through it. This is its adaptation, and it is also its prayer: in a place that answers loudness with loudness, become the quietest thing. Its shell is pitted with vesicles like the lava itself, so that when it tucks, it disappears. It has been here longer than the ledge it lives under. The ledge has fallen and been remade three times this morning; the crab simply walked out from under the fall and walked back under the new fall. It eats what the surge leaves stunned: a shrimp that pulsed at the wrong time, a bit of bleached leaf that will never rot. Nothing rots here, so the crab is the closest thing to rot. A slow, deliberate curator of leftovers in a place too young to know decay.

And then, all at once, the visitors from below.

The pits. Tea-dark water knee to chest deep, then in circles a green-black that drinks the dim light and smells of iron. You put the warped shepherd's staff in — someone left it leaning on the slab ring — and it goes down and down and does not hit. Dropped stones do not plunk; they sound like a door closing far away, and minutes later rattle up around your ankles, slightly warmer.

Something lives down there and comes up to roll.

The fish. Blind, white, long as your forearm. No eyes because eyes are a rumor where light gives up and things glow faintly at their edges instead. It flickers up from a pit in the second between surges when your reflection does not match — you lift your hand a half-second after it does — and for a moment you are not sure if you saw the fish or the reflection's arm. It rolls in the shallows, flank flashing pearl, lateral line trembling to read the thousand throats swallowing off the saddle's lip. It is tasting the bass in its bones the way you feel it in your molars. Then it is gone, back down the throat that does not end. It does not live *in* the saddle. It lives *through* it. The saddle is the shallow breath at the top of a very deep lung. The *whoom* when gas burps up and sends a ring expanding that climbs the rain-wall and vanishes — that is the lung exhaling. The fish rides the exhale up, looks at the twilight at midday, and rides the inhale down.

Around all this, holding on: the ones who figured out how to be trees where no tree should be.

Five, maybe six. Stunted, umbrella-crowned, trunks bent double away from the prevailing blast, bark swollen thick with stored water. That last part puzzled you until you stood chest-deep for an hour: why store water in a drowning? Because this water is not for drinking. It is bath-warm, mineral-sweet, full of ground glass. It scours. So the trees drink only what they catch — waxy, cupped leaves that shed the deluge with a sound like distant applause, rattling constantly — and keep it in their trunks like camels. Their roots are fists in cracks. Not reaching down for nutrients — there is no soil, only slurry — but gripping so the mountain cannot decide to unmake them when it sheds a fin and pushes up a new one raw and steaming. Between them, the breathing mats: olive moss that inflates and deflates with the surge. They are lungs laid flat. They photosynthesize what little olive light filters through the standing wall, and they hold the ground together for a minute at a time.

None of these land. Nothing lands here for long — you saw the storm-petrel shear through the water-wall, scream, and go on, too bewildered to even try. And yet.

Someone stacked stones.

A low ring of basalt slabs near the center, enclosing nothing but deeper water. A cairn on the highest tooth, rebuilt and knocked down and rebuilt, stones slick, balanced impossibly against the wind. Three steps cut east, leading down into white cloud and stopping mid-air. A grass knot, a river stone from impossibly far below, a strip of cloth that whips continuously and never tears — because nothing rots, nothing tears, everything is kept bleached and clean.

The stackers do not live here now. But their keeping lives. That is the other ecology. In a place where everything that happens happens again, slightly louder — surge, burp, rockfall rehearsing itself — a careful act also rehearses itself. The cairn falls and is rebuilt though no hand rebuilds it. The cloth whips and never tears. The ring holds deeper water though it should drain.

Which brings you to what actually lives here, underneath the census.

The mountain. Young, still deciding what shape to be. It lives the way an infant lives: humming constantly, shedding skin, burping, making and unmaking without malice. Its breath is the bass through water. Its hiccups are rifle-cracks under your feet as rock splits open. Its drool is the black milk of ash that clouds and clears impossibly fast, sucked sideways. You are standing inside a body that has not learned to be soft yet.

And kept circling inside that body, replenished from somewhere that is not sky though it pours off every edge and never drops or rises: the Drowning itself. The water that mounds a hand's breadth higher in the center though it should drain. The streams that run uphill to feed it. The drips that fall *up* when the big *whoom* sounds. The roar that arrives before its surge. The saddle that has only one side when you walk it. The reflection that moves first.

It lives. Not like shrimp live. Like a held room lives. A small, high, loud-beyond-thought ocean perched on a knife above the clouds, in a cylinder three hundred paces across, that refuses to leave.

It eats sound and excretes stillness. It eats sharp rock and excretes smooth glass. It eats time and excretes echo, slightly louder.

And for a little while, if you stand chest-deep in it, breathing water-air, pruning and steaming at once, letting your ears pop and your shouting die and your skin be scoured clean — you live there too. Not as visitor. As kept thing. As one more translucent pulsing presence spaced correctly from the crab and the fish and the fist-rooted trees, held in the one place where the drowning was stopped from covering the world and made, instead, to stand up like a wall and stay.

Terrible, and inexplicably, shelter.
