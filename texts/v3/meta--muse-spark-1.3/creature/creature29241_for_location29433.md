They don't emerge as individuals first. They emerge as density.

From out on the pan you don't see people, you see thickening — the stain getting lumpy, getting restless at the edges. Then movement inside the stain. Then noise that is movement. By the time you are close enough to make out a face, you have already understood the first truth about whoever lives here: there are too many of them to be alone, ever.

They are wet people. That is the second thing, before sex or age or kin.

Skin that never quite dries. Hair plastered, dark with sweat and steam. Legs to the knee caked in blue-grey clay that cracks when it half-dries and is immediately re-wet. Broad, splayed feet, sandal-lost half the time, toenails thick with silt. Children are the color of the puddles they sit in. Old people shine — foreheads, cheeks, forearms gleaming in that directionless amber light, wet as the stones of the weeping lip. You learn to read exertion not by flush but by how the water beads and runs. Everyone glistens. Everyone smells faintly of smoke and sour milk and warm mud.

Let them come closer. Don't settle on one yet.

There is a man waist-deep in a borrow pit, handing up slabs. There is a woman on a walkway with a basket on her hip, shouting over a pig and not breaking stride. There are two girls on a roof, legs dangling, sorting minnows into pots while a dog sleeps against their backs. There is an old man in a doorway picking at hair-twine with fingers that look like roots, mouth moving constantly because you have to mouth-shout to mend and talk at once. There are toddlers with bellies round as pots, naked and unbothered, perched on walls where goats also perch, eye-level with each other. If you try to hold one face, the crowd replaces it with three more.

Because they live shoulder to shoulder, their bodies have learned to be public. They lean. They press. They carry together — a mud slab needs four hands, a split log needs six, a buffalo that has settled too deep needs shouting and ropes and ten backs. Their hands are the most finished part of them. Palms polished hard as the digging sticks they hold. Fingers thick, nails broken short, clay worked deep under the nail beds so it never comes out. You see the hands in the town because the town is hands repeated: finger-smoothed brick, chinked clay pressed in by thumbs, lashings of sinew and hair-cord retied a hundred times until the knot remembers the fingers.

Their voices have coarsened to fit the roar. No one here speaks in a normal tone because there is no normal tone that survives two feet away. Women shout in a chest-deep bellow to call ducks. Men shout-laugh. Children learn to wail at full volume and keep wailing — one baby somewhere never stops, not from distress but because distress is ambient and must be ridden like water. Even their songs are thick, not loud. Somewhere in the mat a hand-drum thuds and a line of voices chants against the frog-bellow, not over it. Their throats are hoarse by age six. Their ears must be half-deaf by age twenty, but they hear by vibration — feet on plank, splash-shift in a wallow, the particular grunt of their own pig.

And they are not alone in the mat. Not nearly.

If you count humans you miss most of the residents.

The pigs are citizens. Mud-colored, bristle-backed, low and clever, rooting the edges of platforms, nosing under walkways, watched constantly because they watch food constantly. Each family has its pigs and knows their squeals. The goats are upstairs citizens, balancing impossibly on wall-tops and drying racks, braying that peevish saw-blade bray that cuts through even frogs, eating what little is inedible to others — hide scraps, ash, the fungal felt off a damp corner. The dogs are warm lumps in the ooze, ribs rising, ears twitching in sleep, waking all at once to bark in relay — one quarter starts, three answer — then settling again into steam. They are not pets. They are perimeter.

Then the water-stock. The buffalo stand in their wallows like islands that breathe. Massive, black, nostrils flaring, hides steaming in the warm air, half-submerged and utterly still except for tail-flick and ear-flick against the midges. Children climb on them. Herons stand on them. Their dung is gathered wet and patted into cakes for the low fires, which is why the smoke never lifts. Around their legs move the wedges of ducks and geese, gabbling, herded by shouting boys with sticks through ankle-deep water, white and grey and brown, frantic and orderly at once. Long-legged wading birds — white, slate, rust — stalk the tea-colored clay pits as if they owned the lease, spearing tadpoles and minnows, squabbling with high screams, utterly unafraid of hands. They will snatch a fish off a drying rack if you turn.

Under the walkways, sidling, clicking: crabs. In the thatch-lines of hides, quick and sleek: rats. Not hidden. They run openly along cords and eaves because there is too much food to defend perfectly and everyone knows it. Keep your animals close and your food closer — that is not paranoia, it is choreography. A roof at any moment holds what a roof must hold: drying fish split and salt-crusted, a sleeping dog, clay pots cooling, two children, and a heron waiting for scraps. A square yard of puddle holds tadpoles wriggling, minnows darting, beetles rowing, frog adults with throats pulsing, and the reflected snout of a pig come to drink.

And the frogs. You have to let them become what they are, not background.

They are not decoration. They are the majority. Thousands per acre, thumb-sized and fist-sized, speckled, black, ochre, olive, throats going in every puddle, on every tussock, against your ankles when you stand still. Their roar is not a chorus. It is a climate — deep, rattling, full-throated, millions overlapping into a single unbroken band that lives in your jawbone and sternum. The people have stopped hearing it as sound. It is the floor they shout over. It tells them water is still at the surface, still good. When you press your thumb in the lee of a wall and the print fills instantly, a frog is usually already there, watching the fill.

Above them, the air-citizens. Pillars of midges and mosquitoes hang over open water, humming just above the frog band, shifting, dense enough to see as smoke. You breathe them. Everyone breathes them, waves them without thinking, has learned the particular slap and scratch. Through them cut the swallows — or things that have decided to be swallows here — flickering sheets, scything, eating on the wing, never landing in the mud because they don't have to.

And smallest, where plant should be: the ones that took plant's place. Pale fungal felt skinning churned mud. Olive algal slick in undisturbed corners. White salt crusts where weep-water wicks up. Sour-sweet bloom in damp corners under hides. Mold on sinew, on baskets, on everything left still for a day. The town's feet keep most ground scuffed back to raw, but wherever feet miss for a little while, life films over. The people scrape it, burn it, live with it in their lungs.

What holds them together is not a plan but maintenance. They patch as fast as slump. They tamp clay ribs that squelch. They relash bone frames when sinew slips. They dig clay and the pit fills with tea-colored groundwater and becomes a wash-pit and a heron pit. They save everything: shell becomes tool becomes temper in pots, bone becomes post becomes handle, ash becomes lye becomes plaster. Nothing thrown where it can't be built with. An old woman's hair becomes twine becomes net becomes fish becomes bones becomes posts. The buffalo's wallow becomes warmth becomes dung becomes fire becomes smoke that lies low and stings faintly and keeps insects — slightly — down.

They drink from the cisterns under the weeping lip, where water beads pure out of young sandstone. They wash in the pits. They let buffalo have the wallows. They stay on walkways if they can. They do not look for wood, for shade, for green. Shade is hide and mud-slab and another body leaning close.

Stand long enough inside the mat and the individuals dissolve again into what they truly are: a herd and a flock and a swarm that includes people. Young rock, old hands. Loud equilibrium, holding against the wet.
