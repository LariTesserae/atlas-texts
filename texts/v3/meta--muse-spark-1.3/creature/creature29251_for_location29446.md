They are not empty-makers. They are fillers.

At first you don't see anyone. That's the trick of this place. The light is too flat and too full, and the ground is too new and too sharp to look lived-in. You think: nothing could live here. No water. No shade. Just cracked brick-earth and low scrub locked stem-tight.

Then the scrub moves without wind.

That is the first resident — not one animal, but a thickness. The pelt itself is alive. Thigh-high and interlocked, fleshy and silver-backed, leaf against leaf with no gap to waste. Saltbush, ice-plant, resin-bush, and cousins with no names yet. Their leaves are fat as a thumb-pad, waxy, grey-green and olive-gold, swollen tight in the morning from the only drink there is: fog. They do not root for water because there is none to find. They drink the air at dawn when the cold plain breathes out white for an hour, and every hair and spine beads, and then they turn their silver undersides outward and seal shut by ten and hold that sipall day. Push through them and they are not soft. The stems are tough, dry-oiled, resin-smelling, built to be chewed and to survive chewing.

Under them and on them lives the second thickness.

On the leaves sit scale insects, round as glass beads, motionless, mouths plumbed into the leaf, turning sap into honeydew. Under the leaves run the ants in black rivers — not wandering, but on cleared highways they have swept grain by grain, thousands wide, moving with that almost inaudible crinkle of mandibles all clipping together until it becomes a hush. They farm the beads. They guard them. They milk them. If you watch one stem long enough you see the whole economy: sucker, guard, carrier, going on in miniature while the leaf sways.

And then out of that low sea stand the builders. The ones who made the vertical.

They are small enough you have to kneel to see one properly, and numerous enough you will never see them all. Pale, soft-bodied, blind workers the length of a rice grain, with dark heads and jaws like nippers. Soldiers with huge rust-red heads that plug tunnels with their own faces. Up inside, down deep where the clay stays cool and damp even at noon, the queens — vast, pulsing, egg-pumping grubs as long as your thumb, who will never see light and will never stop laying. One queen per tower-city, laying for twenty years.

You don't see the queen. You see what she thinks: towers.

Millions of them across the curve of the world, knee-high knuckles near your feet becoming shoulder-high further out becoming house-high on the horizon, all burnt-orange, fluted, pocked, leaning a degree downwind. Each grain carried up in a jaw in the damp of morning, cemented with spit and dung, dried to brick by ten. A living mountain still wet at the tip. Inside it is not solid — it is lung and gut and farm. Shafts for air to rise and fall and keep the nest at one temperature. Chambers for nurseries. Chambers for fungus gardens where chewed leaf-pulp blooms into white mold that the termites eat, the only crop that grows in the dark.

A colony is not an animal. It is a place that repairs itself. A hornbill lands heavy on a flank — big black-and-white bulk, clumsy on that fine point — hammers a hole, tongues out workers, swallows, lifts off. Before the dust from its wings has settled, soldiers have plugged the breach with their heads and workers are plastering from the inside. Tick of falling grit on the outside is the sound of masons at work. A column reroutes around the scar. The city heals without pausing.

Because there is never pause. Everything under construction is also someone's home. A fresh mound is nursery. An old mound, abandoned when its queen dies, becomes garden — succulents rooting in its flanks, grasses capping its top green, geckos laying soft eggs in its empty vents, spiders taking the high ground. Then the garden is eaten flat by foragers and tamped into avenue, swept dust wide enough for columns to pass. Then a young queen lands after the rains that never quite rain, sheds her wings ten feet to the left, digs in, and an avenue shifts to go around her. The streets move.

Around them, in the avenues and side streets and tamped plazas around the great buttressed cathedrals, moves the commuter crowd.

Mongoose families, single-file, noses down, turning pebbles with quick paws, churring low. Ground squirrels upright as sentries then flat as rugs as they dart crack to crack. Long-legged bustards stepping through with absurd dignity, pausing, spearing a grasshopper. Skinks flicking in and out of the boot-deep polygonal cracks in the raw earth — silver flashes that end in a soft *pfft* of dust. Trapdoor spiders who never commute at all: they have pegged their hinged doors in the swept dust between towers and wait with their hands on the trip-lines, feeling the city walk over them.

And above, the air-tenants. Kestrels hung motionless on the heat, eyes down. Shrikes darting tower-top to tower-top, carrying grasshoppers to thorn bushes to impale them on spikes for later — the thorn trees here are few and dead and so festooned with weaver nests they look thatched, hanging fruit-loud with chattering birds. None of them sing out. They click and churr, desert-quiet, because the white-gold light seems to eat sound. Even the driver ants crossing a main street — a red river carrying green leaf-crescents overhead like sails — make only a papery rustle as they go under the scrub leaves.

If you stand still for five minutes they forget you and resume. The repair crews boil. The foragers stream. A lizard moves a pebble and it ticks down into a canyon.

Late in the day, when each tower lays a long blue shadow across the olive pelt, you can see what they really are: not insects and lizards and birds separately, but one crowded body, stem-tight and tower-tall, making soil out of spit and wax and hunger. The sharp brick-dust under your boots is already being split by root acids, softened into the first whisper of real earth that in a thousand years will round all this sharpness down. The towers will become hills.

For now they are still new. Still raw. Still being built grain by grain, as far as the world goes.
