At first you think nothing lives here.

That's because you are listening for people. For doors, for voices, for boots on wet slabs hurrying to a shift change. Lowtown is laid out for that sound and the sound isn't coming. The doors are swollen shut. The condensation is on the inside of the windows. The caretaker's bicycle leans against the pump house with soft tires and a basket full of rainwater, and no one comes to move it.

Stay a little longer. Down here, you will.

What lives here does not hurry to meet you. It lets you be too loud for a while — your footsteps slapping and then swallowed — until you learn to stand still and let the bowl sort you out from weather.

The first citizen you notice is not the one you keep seeing.

The heron.

Grey, the exact grey of the weeping limestone just before it goes black with wet. He is in every view and there is only ever one of him at a time. He stands in the inch-deep mirror between the houses, one leg folded, neck folded, absolutely still in a way that makes the pocking rain around him look fussy. You look away at the flywheel making its two revolutions a minute — time enough to forget a link and remember it again — and when you look back he is in the next mirror over, twenty feet away. You did not hear a wingbeat. The pit remembers sound a moment too long, the cooing marbles around late, but it gave you nothing for him.

He is not fishing the way herons fish elsewhere. There is no stab, no hurry. He waits until the eels decide. He is the foreman who never clocks in. Whether there is one heron who has learned to move like water through stone, or a relay of identical grey birds holding the same post in shifts so the post is never empty, no one down here will tell you. There is no one to tell you. But there is always a heron, and the mirrors stay clean around him.

Below him, the shift workers.

The eels. They are not picturesque. They are lengths of wet rope, black-olive and yellow-bellied, thick as your wrist in the canal and thin as bootlaces in the culverts. In the straight stone canal with its six inches of clear cold flow they lie on the silt and do not seem to move until you see the silt itself breathing. In the dark they knot. Before the hour turns, before the turbines shudder on for their hourly churn, they gather at the outflow grates in a slow, muscular braid, heads all one way, bodies sliding over bodies without friction. The old men would have called them vermin. The old men wanted barges. The eels want exactly what Lowtown now offers: cold, low oxygen made tolerable by slowness, no hurry, a pulse of warm water from the culverts once an hour to bring food down.

Watch one turn in shallow water — that wet slap, too loud, then gone. They clean the gutters better than any gang ever did. They eat the weevils that eat the grain that spilled decades ago and is still spilling, endlessly, in damp. They eat the duckweed and the drowned moths. When the rain comes at four, needling straight down for twenty minutes, pocking all the mirrors at once, they rise a half-inch higher in the water and shiver.

Above them, the tenants who took the houses.

Pigeons. Hundreds. You hear them before you sort them — their cooing goes around the bowl and comes back late, as if thrown and caught. They own the crane girders, the louvres gone green with algae, the north side only of every roof. No one wrote down why north. It is just the rule, like the machines must not stop and nothing may dry. They sit fluffed against cellar-cool, damp to the bone and unbothered, their greys and blues deepened by wet to something almost oily. They drop guano that the rain never quite washes off, that the lime learns to seal over. That smell in your coat when you climb out — oil and guano and wet limestone — is mostly them. They are not afraid. Rats trot below them and they do not even tuck their feet.

The rats trot the curb lines with terrible confidence. Not scurrying. Trotting, single-file, in the exact center of the curb where the stone stays driest, which is still not dry. They are fat, sleek, almost handsome, gone dark chocolate from generations in shadow. Their whiskers bead with droplets. They know the timetable of the Dripworks better than any caretaker: when the chain drive ticks, when the droplet travels along the sagging cable and falls — plink, a second later — they pause. They have routes. From the corner shop — TEA FLOUR PARAFFIN still legible — to the gaps where buildings were politely removed. To the grain. Always to the grain. Their tails leave thin S-curves in the inch-deep film that the next rain erases at four.

In summer, the air under the rim belongs to the swifts. They cut it, screaming once — a ripping cry — and then the stone hushes it. They never land down here. They can't; there is no dry ledge to take off from again. They live on the wing in the pale dusty column at noon that doesn't reach the corners, eating the moths that should be sleeping. At dusk, by the sodium oranges and the one flickering white over the pump house, the moths take over.

They come in slow snowfall. Big, dun, furred things, wings heavy with damp so they cannot flutter fast. They spiral around the lamps and do not burn. That is the strangest mercy here: the lamps are cool enough, wet enough, filmed with lime, that a moth can bump and rest and bump again. They settle on the warm riveted iron of the mill and dry their wings a degree, then lift again. The translucent celery-weed in the gutters leans toward the lamps at night to catch what they drop.

After the daily rain, the pavement moves at ankle height.

Slugs as long as your forearm, leopard-spotted, amber, black-keeled, cross between the houses leaving silver sutures that glint for hours under lamplight. They eat the moss epaulettes on every north-facing ledge only to have the moss grow back by morning. They eat the hart's-tongue ferns unrolling from the chapel wall only where the fern can afford it. They are slow even for slugs — the cold slows them — and that is why they are so large. Nothing hurries them. No salt ever comes down the stair cut into rock. No thrush. Only the rats, who watch them with professional disinterest, and the heron, who will not waste a strike on such slow meat when eels are on shift.

Under all of this, in the pump house basement where the water is knee-deep and warm from the culverts, hang the blind ones.

White fish the size of your thumb, eyeless, translucent enough to see the pulse go through them. They do not school. They hang in the dark, each a little apart, facing into the seep, mouths opening and closing on nothing you can see. If you put your hand in — cold to the elbow after the warmth — they do not scatter. They quiver and stay. They have lived so long without light that light would be a rumor. They live on heat and iron bacteria and the vibration of the dynamo humming low enough you feel it in your sternum. They are the only thing here that never sees the cloud circle counterclockwise, never sees the top of it like skin on cold milk from the rim.

And the caretaker?

There might be one still, somewhere. Or the idea of one kept alive by habit. His bicycle holds rain. His pump house ticks as boilers cool and warm and cool again. The flywheel turns. The lime advances a millimeter a year over a doorway on Pump Row, a white lip already halfway to closing a door no one opens. The water-clock stain on the mill wall rises an inch and falls an inch over twelve hours though there is no tide this far inland.

Perhaps he dissolved. Perhaps he learned the final Lowtown trade: to become slow enough never to wear out. To tend by not intervening. The water comes in exactly as fast as water is pumped out. Heat enough to make a cloud. Animals enough to keep the gutters clean.

The pit inhales. The pit exhales. It does not need people to keep happening.

You climb out colder and damper than you went in. Behind you the lamps stay on in the dim. The flywheel turns. The eels gather at the grate. It starts to rain, only down there, only for twenty minutes, only for itself — and for them, who do not look up.
