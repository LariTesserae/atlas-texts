Nothing with eyes lives here.

That's the first thing you have to unlearn. You push through the curtain, frond over your face and frond under your chin, and your animal body keeps waiting for the flinch — for something to dart, to blink, to mind you. It never comes. No back uncoils. No claw stills. The feather-weed trembles in your lamp but not because you came. It was trembling already, an hour before you came, and will tremble an hour after.

What lives here does not notice you that way. It notices by touch, and very slowly.

Start close, because close is all there is. Six meters and the haze takes the rest.

The fur lives. That is the stupid, obvious thing your fingers find first when you steady yourself on a slab and expect stone and get give. The wine-dark and bottle-green velvet that coats every high surface is not a stain. Push a finger in to the knuckle and you are in a forest in miniature, dense, spongy, cold as a tongue. Encrusting algae, layer on layer on layer, a hundred years of almost-growth pressed into a mat. It lives by drinking the faint greening from above and the stone-dust from below. It tastes the stone as it covers it. Lime and crushed tile, pinkish underneath, softening to clay — the fur likes that. It sends acids thinner than breath into the pocks left by stone pounders and loosens a grain, holds a grain. It does not kill the block. It keeps it. Stitches it down.

Lower, where even the big kelp gets tired and stops, the fur keeps going. It is the one that never gives up. In the black between platforms, where your lamp makes a pale ball and nothing beyond, put your hand out and you will still touch fur. It is furred all the way down.

Out of the fur rise the Fingers.

They are low, fleshy, stupid to look at and impossible to stop looking at once you learn their pace. A field of them on a courtyard floor under a meter of silt-felt, each no taller than your hand, swaying a degree to port and back over the course of an hour as the great tide turns over. They are not swaying because they are pushed. There is no surge you can feel. They are swaying because they are turning themselves, very slightly, to face the sleeping-breath current that brings leaf-tea from one ward of the city to the next. Twice a day they lean east. Twice a day they lean west. That is their hunt. That is their migration. Dissolved things touch them and stick and are taken in. Stone-dust. Spore-skin. The faint iron of a copper chisel-stroke dissolved ten thousand years ago.

Above them, where the current is a little stronger on the lips of the canals, live the Pale Ones.

Feather-weed. In your light they go translucent, white-green, each frond divided and divided again until it is almost not there, just a shiver in the water. They tremble although there is nothing to tremble them. Look long enough — and down here looking long enough means hanging still until your fingers go stupid — and you see it is not trembling. It is combing. Cilia too small to see move the never-settling, never-clearing soup — spores and shed skin and fine mineral wool — across their branches and into their mouthless mouths. They are lungs hung out in the water. They breathe what the kelp sloughs off.

And above them, everything, the Holders.

Black kelp. Not black, really — dark red-brown and olive so dark it reads as black until your lamp gets close and then it bleeds color. This is what lives here the way trees live elsewhere, only water holds everything up so they never had to learn to stand. Stipes as thick as your wrist, ridged, многолетние, years laid down in rings you can feel with your thumb through gloves. Thirty-meter fronds trailing horizontally, layer upon layer, ceiling over the sunken streets. Where it is still they pile into peat-banks, rotting only very slowly in the cold, half-living, half-remembered leaf. Where the canal-lips pinch the tide they stream like hair.

Each one began in a joint. That is important. A spore — and the water is thick with them, they hang for hours, days, they never settle — found a crack between two enormous fitted slabs, between courses stepping down and down, and put down a holdfast. The holdfast is the real animal, if you want an animal. Strong as hands. White-yellow tendrils that go into the stone-pocks, into the rope-drill holes, into the oozing mortar, and swell and grip and never let go. A holdfast does not move for a hundred years. It thickens. It stitches slab to slab. When a wall finally gives after ten thousand years — and they do — it does not crash. It exhales and leans, and the holdfasts on it are already holding its neighbor, so it comes to rest and within a season its fallen face is furred over, already stitched down. The kelp are holding the city together now. That is what they do. They are masons, after the masons.

They do not see. They do not hear your breathing too loud in your hood. What they feel is pressure, and cold, and lean.

Pressure: they know how far they are from the rumor of surface because the water squeezes them, a hand more or less heavy. The high causeways where the slow current combs the silt clean — there they are short and brutal, streamers cropped by moving water. In the courtyards a meter deep in felted silt, in the roofed rooms still full to the doorway with slow green-black growth, in the drowned thatch hanging from a lintel that still spans — there they are long and piled and patient, because the water is kinder, heavier, stiller.

Cold: one or two degrees above freezing, always. It makes you stupid. It makes them wise. Growth measured in millimeters. Lengthen a cell, then another. Wait out the dark. There is no summer to wait for. Light will not increase. Warmth will not come. So they have unlearned hurry. A frond brushes your cheek and stays there. It does not recoil. It will be there when you kick gently upward to look out — careful not to stir the silt that will hang for hours if you do — over platform, canal, wall, courtyard, stair, forest, forest, forest until dark takes it.

Lean: the creak you hear, stipe against stipe as the water masses lean, is them talking in the only language that matters here. Too much lean means the slab beneath shifted another millimeter, somewhere out in the dark miles with a soft thud you feel more than hear. The forest adjusts. The ceiling re-weaves. Leaf over leaf over leaf, then an inch of water, then stone again.

That is who lives here now. Not a herd, not a shoal, not a people. A single, spread-out, eyeless body that learned to eat direction-light and stone-soup and itself, and to hold on.

It preserves without meaning to. That is just what cold, patient holding does. A woven basket left on a step is still a basket under its coat, because the fur grew over it before anything could unmake it and sealed it in green. A coil of rope turned to iron-hard fiber. A sandal print filled with hardened silt in a brickyard the size of a province, print after print going nowhere you can follow before the forest closes over — kept, not as a relic, but as a floor. Another floor. Tile. Brick set on edge in herringbone. Another floor beneath that. The makers paved their world many times over. The living fur is paving it again.

If you stay long enough for your lamp to dim and your breath to come back slower, you understand the rule is not cruel. It is just complete.

Nothing here is eaten. Nothing here eats in the way of mouths. Nothing watches. So the growth keeps what it touches, and touches everything, and goes on, cold and dark and green-black and quiet, growing over itself forever.
