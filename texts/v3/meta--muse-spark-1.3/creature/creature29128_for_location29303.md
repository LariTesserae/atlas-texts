What lives here is green. That is the first shock, and you have to unlearn it to see them right.

They are not plants that survived here. They are what happens when green stops needing what green has always needed.

Each island you saw from the rise is one of them — not a grove in the sense of many individuals crowded together, but a single held thing, forty meters across, dense to the point of sealed. From the outside it looks like jungle. From the inside it stops looking like jungle at all.

Begin with the uprights. They stand where trunks should be, a dozen or twenty to an island, swollen at the foot like boilers or cisterns, iron-red blackened with heat. What would be bark flakes off in hard enameled plates, curled at the edge, clinking faintly when they fall. Where one has split — from pressure, from age that isn't age — there is no wood inside. There is work inside: vertical pipes and chambers packed with pale fibrous wadding, all sodden with a thick amber oil that does not evaporate in the dry air. It catches the light and holds it. Touch it and it stays warm on your finger and smells of hot varnish and kerosene and something crushed and green without water in it.

That oil is their blood, and it does not circulate, it pressurizes. If you kneel at the base in the unnatural quiet you can hear it: a very low tick-tick-tick of metal-cool contraction, a sigh as a valve opens somewhere in the root mass and closes again. Stems visibly swell and then ease. The whole upright breathes without air.

Above, the canopy is three, four leaves deep, and the leaves are wrong in the way that lets them live. Thick as pottery, waxy to ceramic, deep oiled emerald veined bright with copper. They do not sweat. No pores open to a sky with no water to give. They hold heat instead — press one and it is warm from within, warmer than the sun on its surface, and it leaves no wet print, only that machine-smear. The largest have folded themselves into funnels, condensers, and you can hear them ping faintly as they cool and warm and cool again. Their undersides are dusted sulfur-yellow.

That dust is their children, and their pollen, and their product all at once.

Every so often, with no wind to call it, a frond will shiver along its copper veins and give a short silent puff. A cloud of yellow powder exhales and falls straight down. No bees, no wind-borne drift, no animal to carry it. Gravity is enough in a closed system. It settles onto cracked basalt and oil-wet leaf litter inside the circle and begins again as a low sprout, crowding for light under its parents. That is why the edge is a knife-line: enameled leaf-fall underfoot on one side, sterile cinder-glass on the other. They do not colonize. They intensify.

Between the uprights run the ropers — vines thick as cable, not climbing toward light but lashing trunk to trunk, holding the pressure network together, sharing oil and load. Below, the roots do the eating.

They do not go down. There is nothing down there to find. They go outward and shallow, thigh-thick at the shoulder and tapering to questing rootlets, wedging into cooling cracks, prying the newborn rock apart. Where they touch stone they sweat acid-oils that pit the black glass white and then crumble it to flour. The flour is taken up. Rock becomes body. Light becomes the work to do it. Fallen leaves never rot — there is nothing damp to rot them, no gnat, no mold — they crisp where they fall, crack into flakes, and the oiled rootlets crawl over them and dissolve them chemically back into the system. Nothing leaves. That is how lushness is possible with no soil and no rain: perfect thrift.

High in the canopy, production continues without hurry. The pods come on in runs — hundreds identical, then thousands — round, seamless, riveted-looking though nothing was riveted, each swollen with the same amber fuel and, coiled inside like a spring, an embryo waiting only for a crack to open beneath it. Not a seed waiting for rain. A seed waiting for fracture.

There are seven of these engines visible from where you stand, and more, presumably, beyond the pale horizon. Each alone. Each complete. No runners advance across the red-black flat between them. No spore blows over. The gaps stay reverently empty, sunlit, still.

And none of them grows old. That is the last thing you learn when you stay and listen to the low thrum coming up through the soles of your boots. No growth spurt, no die-off, no young grove and old grove. They idle. Leaf pings, spore puffs, root ticks, pressure builds and vents deep under the plateau. Stone and sun in, self out, island by island, humming so constantly you mistook it at first for silence.

No one built them. Nothing eats them. Nothing drinks with them. They are the only citizens of the tableland: green factories without builders, patient, oiled, copper-veined, manufacturing themselves forever out of light and broken glass.
