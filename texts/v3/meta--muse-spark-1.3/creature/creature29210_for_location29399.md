They are hard to see at first.

Not because they hide. Because they don't stand out. In that low, stone-colored sprawl, against stone-colored walls, among stone-colored, smoke-colored animals, a person is just another shaggy, slow-moving bulk. You look for people and you see herd. Flank, shoulder, back, breath-plume. Then one of the bulks straightens a little, lifts a face from a grazer's udder, and you realize you have been watching a woman milking for a full minute without separating her from the animals around her.

That is the first rule of them: they live flank to flank, and they look like what they live among.

Seen closer, in a courtyard at midday when the frost lets go and everyone is out working, they resolve.

Short. Not stunted, but built low to the ground the way their houses are built low against wind. Broad through the chest — men, women, children even, barrel-chested in a way that makes their arms seem to hang a little out from their sides. You hear it before you understand it: their breathing is deeper, slower, with a slight catch on the inhale that never quite leaves. Their bodies have learned to ask for less and take more from it. Big lungs in big ribcages. Blood thick with red. Cheeks that are always a little dark, even in children — wind-burned to a polished mahogany brown-red, cracked at the corners of the mouth, greased with fat against the cold and glare.

Their eyes are narrowed, not from suspicion but from a lifetime of white light. Lids heavy, lashes short and broken by frost, the skin at the outer corners creased early into fans from squinting. The eyes themselves, when you catch them, are dark and wet and very steady. They don't glance. Glancing is fast. They look and hold.

Hair is black, coarse, cut short under caps because long hair freezes and lice overwinter in it. Old men and some old women wear it in a single greased tail to show they have lived long enough to afford the trouble. Skin underneath the outer wraps is startlingly pale, almost translucent at the temples where the veins show blue — the parts of them that never see sun.

Hands give them away as builders. Even a child's hands are broad-knuckled, nails broken short, palms callused from stone and rope and hide and cold teats. No one here has soft hands. An infant will grip a fistful of long grazer hair and not let go when lifted.

They dress as an extension of the herd. Innermost, hide with the hair turned in against the skin — grazer calfskin for babies, old soft goat for the elderly. Then felt, layer on layer of matted grazer underwool, grey-brown, stinking faintly sweet of lanolin and smoke and milk. Then outermost, a wind-shell of scraped hide, seamed with sinew and patched until the patches have patches. Everything laced, not buttoned — bone toggles, hide thongs that can be worked with numb fingers. Feet are wrapped in hide stuffed with dry moss and pika-hay, then lashed up the calf. On the head, a deep felt cap with earflaps that tie under the chin, often with a raven feather or a goat-bell tied in for a child. When the wind gets up they pull a fold of felt up over nose and mouth and breathe through it, warming the air before it hits the lungs. You see hundreds of them like that on a drover avenue, muffled, humped, indistinguishable from the animals except that they walk on two legs, slowly.

And slowness is not a condition. It is who they are.

No one hurries. A boy of seven leading a two-ton grazer by a nose-rope walks at the grazer's pace — one deliberate foot, then another, breath timed to steps. To run is to drop gasping to your knees with your lungs clawing and your vision going black at the edges. Every child learns that before they learn speech. So they have made a virtue, a morality, of slowness. The respected person is not the quick but the even. Who moves without waste. Who can carry a full bowl of meltwater across a courtyard in wind without spilling. Who can stack stone for six hours without stopping, stone-set-on-stone, breath even. Impatience is vulgar here. Shouting is worse than vulgar — it is a waste of breath that others have to breathe.

So they speak low, and little. Their voices are hoarse from thin cold air, pitched down to carry without rising. A mother calls her child not with a yell but with a two-note hum that travels under the wind. Old men converse in short phrases spaced by long breaths. Much is done by touch. In the markets — those dense, steaming knots of people and beasts shoulder to shoulder in walled squares — you will watch two traders sit flank to flank under a shared felt blanket, hands hidden, fingers pressing. One press for one measure of barley, a squeeze for a milking goat, a long hold for agreement. It is quieter, and warmer, than shouting numbers into wind.

Their whole life is maintenance of warmth.

By day they are always among animals, hands constantly busy, because idleness is cold. Girls and women milk before dawn and at noon, kneeling into the hot lee of the barrel body, forehead pressed to flank to feel the let-down, fingers quick and gentle despite numbness. Boys comb out the long underwool with bone combs, collecting it in hide bags for felters. Men check hooves, trim with chipped stone, feel for heat that means infection. Old people, who cannot crouch, sit in doorways in the sun with a grazer calf across their feet and twist sinew cord, or grind barley between two stones one handful at a time, or mend felt with bone needles. Children are tasked with dung — following the herds with a flat basket, picking the fresh pats, patting them into cakes and setting them to dry in geometric hives against south walls. It is a child's first useful work, and they do it solemnly, because everyone knows winter fuel is summer dung.

At night they sleep as they live: in a pile.

When dusk drops blue and the smoke flattens overhead, each household pen circles. The big grazers lie down first in a ring, legs folded, calves pulled into the center where breath collects warmest. The black goats wedge on top and between, finding impossibly small warm slots. The long ribby dogs — silent, almost voiceless animals that rarely bark because barking wastes heat — crawl in last against human legs, shivering until they still. And people crawl in among them. A whole family, sometimes twenty bodies counting cousins and a widowed aunt leaned-against for another winter, under shared felts weighted with stones, sharing breath. You can hear it if you pass a doorway after dark: a low, communal tide of breathing, human and animal in and out together, the stones around them giving back the day's sun. They do not dream of privacy. Privacy would be freezing to death alone.

They are born into this warmth and they die out of it.

Babies arrive in winter, when people are indoors most and there are hands to hold them. A birth happens in the sleeping circle, among bodies, with a milking grazer nearby for heat and so the newborn's first air — that thin, hurting air — is breathed in air already breathed and warmed by others. The afterbirth is given to the dogs. The child is wrapped in old, impossibly soft felt washed in meltwater and urine, and laid to sleep on a warm dog's belly.

From then on the child's world expands in rings: first the sleeping pile, then the courtyard pen where forty bodies press flank to flank, then the footpath to the cistern where they learn to break ice with a heel without spilling, then the drover avenue where at seven or eight they are trusted to lead a yearling alone across what would be three streets elsewhere, walking for an hour and not thinking it far.

Old age, when it comes — and it comes early by lowland counting, forty-five, fifty is venerable — is a gradual sinking toward the ground. Joints thicken. The chest-barrel grows more pronounced. People get shorter as they stoop to save breath. They sit more, in sun-traps behind windbreaks, mending, remembering lineages of animals better than lineages of people. They can recite a grazer cow's mothers back seven generations. Human ancestors matter less than herd ancestors, because the herd is what continues.

And the dead do not stay.

Human or grazer or goat or dog, when breath stops, the body is carried — slowly, with many rests — outside the city to the high platforms: flat slabs raised on stone pillars above the reach of dogs. There it is laid open to sky. This is not cruelty. This is return. Within hours the ravens come, heavy black birds that live on every roof-ridge, croaking once to claim. Then the smaller birds. The wind does the rest, freeze-drying what is left. After weeks, kin return to collect the clean bones. Long leg-bones become roof beams, ribs become sled runners and sickle handles, scapulae become shovels, skulls are set into walls as wind-wards. Nothing is wasted because nothing can be replaced quickly. A child's first comb is often cut from the rib of the grazer whose milk fed her as an infant. They know whose bone they carry.

If you ask them — slowly, over shared breath, after sitting with them through a mending — what they are, they will not say a tribe name. They will gesture to the circle: animals in the middle, people among them, walls around, smoke above. *We who keep each other warm.* That is the whole of it.

Hundreds of thousands of them, spread to the horizon in fat-lamp flicker, all breathing together, all moving at a herd's pace, all dressed in hair, all smelling of milk and dung-smoke and warm wool. Outnumbered by their animals and not minding in the least. Because they do not think of themselves as owners of the herd.

They think, in the wordless way of people who touch more than they talk, that they *are* the herd — the upright, dexterous, fire-tending part of it, the part that builds windbreaks and remembers water channels and can mend a wall by hand, so that the four-legged, milk-giving, wool-growing, dung-fueling part can live, so that all of them together can make the thin air, for a moment at midday, almost thick enough to live in.
