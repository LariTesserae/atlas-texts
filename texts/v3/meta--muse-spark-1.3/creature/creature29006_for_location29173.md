At first you think nothing lives here. That's what the volume tells you.

Swim out ten kicks from any wall and there is only poured distance falling away under you, green-grey going to slate, your breath loud in your hood, your gloves empty. No weed to brush your knee. No fish to startle. Just concrete dust hanging in a work-light cone and the tick of cooling concrete coming up through the water into your teeth. It feels sterile the way a new parking garage feels sterile.

Then your lamp catches the edge you just left, and it is furred.

It starts with the skin. Everything sunlit underwater has it — a velvet green biofilm, thin as paint, slick under a glove if you touch a handrail on Bench 4. It is not pretty. It is the first life, the one that can eat light and concrete leachate and do nothing else. It softens nothing, but it makes everything else possible. In the cracks of expansion joints, where a little silt has wedged, ditch grass roots, pale and stunted, five or six blades shivering. Where a rock bolt head sticks out a half-inch, a cold-water kelp has taken hold. There are not forests of them. There are individuals. One stipe here, no longer than your forearm, white-tipped where the surge has frayed it, waving stupidly and stubbornly in the intake current. One there, twenty feet down the wall. Each one alone, each one winning.

Where the film is thickest, the mussels are.

They are the true citizens of the Terraced Sea. Blue-black, sharp as the rock they sit on, they came first — on larval drift in the diverted rivers, on gull feet, on a maintenance barge hull — and they found paradise: miles of fresh, uncolonized, 90-degree corners, sunlit, oxygen-rich, predator-poor, 12 degrees year round. They crust every edge from the waterline down to where light gives out, mat on mat, byssus drilled into shotcrete, prying it apart grain by grain. Underwater you hear them before you understand them: a faint crackle, like fat frying far away, thousands of shells opening and closing. Put your hand on a wall corner on Bench 6 and it is not rock anymore. It gives slightly. It is alive and cutting.

In the mussel beds live the hunters.

The eels. Long, grey-green, cool-water eels as thick as your wrist and, deeper down, as thick as your thigh. They den in the drain galleries — the square black mouths every hundred meters along the walls where seep was meant to bleed out — and in the drowned infrastructure: in lockers in the Bench 14 lunchroom, in the cabs of the ore cars, in coiled fire hose. By day you see only tails, a slow S disappearing. At dusk they come out onto the benches to hunt in packs of three and four, cruising the painted centerlines six feet off the bottom, nose down, picking off perch and mussel-crabs and anything stupid. They are not afraid of divers. They have never learned to be. One will stop and look at your light and you will see your own reflection fisheyed in its eye, and then it will keep going along its beat, the beat it has walked every dusk since the water rose.

Above them, in the open water that should be empty, the silver.

Silversides, in schools so large they are weather. Tens of thousands moving as one slow muscle through the void over a drowned airfield. You hear them first: a sudden rain-click, thousands of tails turning at once. Then the light changes. Then they are around you, parting without hurry, each fish three inches of bright quicksilver, all facing into the gyre current. When a work-light cone or a break in cloud catches them they turn together and the whole volume flashes — a bench, an airfield, a bay flashes silver and goes dark again. Perch hang under them, smaller groups, cannier, hugging structure. They love signs. A reflective road sign — BENCH 14 — 30 KM/H — CURVE — still bolted to a wall under twenty feet of water, and behind it in its lee, out of the intake pull, eight perch hovering nose to current, gills working slow in the cold. They spook when your bubbles hit them and re-form a minute later in exactly the same place.

And above them, the commuters.

Cormorants, black and snake-necked, and the gulls and terns that nest dry. They live on the rim — on the jibs of the gantry cranes that still stand up top, on transformer gantries, on the guardrail posts, whitewash streaking safety yellow — and they work below. Morning, after the mist burns off the upper water, they drop in. A cormorant can take the cold that would kill you in minutes. It stabs down a hundred feet into jade water, folds, and spears through a school edge, surfacing with a silverside crosswise, throat working. Their cries up top are thin and lost. Down below, submerged, you hear only the boom of their entries and then nothing, because they are built for this pit in a way nothing else is: they love vertical water over flat concrete, they love a fish with nowhere to hide except distance.

Deep, where sun is only a white ache, there is other white.

Sonar says the bottom is flat — final poured pad three kilometers down, sump pits, the chairs for rebar waiting for a pour that will never come. No diver goes there. The ROV lights do. And there, in total cold and in the cones of lights that were meant to illuminate crushers, are anemones. White, frost-like, thousands of them, each on a steel chair tip, tentacles open to a current that never stops, feeding on concrete dust and mussel larvae raining down from a mile above. They do not move. They are the opposite of the silversides. They have time.

And then — still — the people. Because the plant kept running.

They crust the rim the way mussels crust the waterline. The pump minders, two per shift, in a Portakabin with a kettle that never cools, watching levels on screens that never change much, listening to the low octave thrum through their boots. The light crew. The sparkies in drysuits with hoods up even in summer because the water is 12 degrees and steals thought. They go down in the cage elevator that still runs on its wall rails, lunchbox between knees, to change a sodium flood on Bench 9 while perch nip at their fins and eels watch from the gallery mouths. They work fast; fingers go stupid in minutes. Their talk is short, practical, shouted over waterfall roar: *hold the cone, pass the shackle, up.* At night from the rim you can see their work glowing faint under water, the whole spiral lit like a city through ice, and if you stand long enough you will see a pair of headlamps moving slow along a submerged bench — them, walking a road that is still a road, reflective paint flashing under their lamps, doing a torque check no one will ever audit.

Also the bird man, the lone counter who comes Tuesdays to count nests on Crane 3. Also the kids from the transformer yards who fish with drop lines off the concrete lip for eels they rarely catch and swear are as long as a truck. Also you, for a second, on the rim.

No one belongs here the way mussels do. Even the eels still smell faintly of river. The people get out, climb back up into wind and warm air off the factories, stamp feeling back into hands. The cormorants shake and fly up to dry steel to preen. Only the film stays, and the mussels, and deep down the white frost that has never breathed air.

But for all that — for all the poured sharpness, the drill marks still fresh, the rust drips only inches long — they are staying. A flake of shotcrete falls and there is bright grey underneath, and by spring there is green velvet on it again. A rockfall exposes stone that still smells of explosives, and by fall there are mussel spat on it, no bigger than pinheads, holding on against the seiche that sloshes bench to bench. The sea is young. It hasn't learned to round a corner. Life here has learned something else instead: how to love an edge.
