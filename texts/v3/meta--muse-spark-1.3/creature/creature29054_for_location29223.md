No one lives here, if you mean people. No one stays.

What lives here lives low and waits out the heat.

At first it's just a ticking in the shallows. Then the shallows resolve.

The puddles are not water. They are crabs. Thousands of them come up with the seep from the marsh and spread across the concrete in a slow lateral tide — fiddlers with one claw huge as a fist, white and ochre, waving it as they go, and smaller brown shore crabs, flat and quick. You hear them before you see them right: a faint dry clicking, claw on concrete, leg on concrete, shell on shell. They climb out of wheel ruts, out of potholes, out of the flooded rail bed where the sleepers lie sodden. They pick at the iridescent skin on the water, eating drowned midges, eating gull shit, eating anything soft enough. They must keep moving because they dry from the top down. If you stand still they go around your boots.

Where the crabs go, the white birds follow.

Gulls — big herring gulls, grey-backed, yellow-eyed — stand one-legged in two inches of water, then suddenly stab. They swallow a crab whole, head back, throat working. White egrets work slower, high-stepping through the laydown yards that have become lakes, necks coiled and then thrown like a spear. Down on the canal wall there are herons, blue-grey, standing absolutely motionless as a shut valve for an hour, for longer. They are not waiting like the dogs wait. They have removed themselves from the heat entirely.

Up off the ground, along every base of every wall, the rats run.

Not one rat. A system of rats. Brown, slick-backed, heavy-bodied from pigeon eggs and garbage, they have worn runways along the concrete — along pipes, along the foot of corrugated siding, from culvert mouth to dumpster to pallet stack. They pour and stop and pour again, noses twitching, whiskers bright with water. In the heat of midday they flatten in shade and pant with their mouths open, but they do not go far. The culverts are their burrows now. The hollow warehouses are their fields. If a board is lifted, underneath is a boil of cockroaches, chestnut and black, antennae waving, scattering from light that isn't even bright. The rats eat those too. They eat fallen squabs, they eat mummified trash, they eat crab legs torn off by gulls. They drink only from drips — from an awning edge, from a seam in a tank — never from the oily ground film if they can help it.

Above the rats, the air in the sheds is feathered.

Pigeons. Hundreds to a building. Thousands in the district. They own the beams, the rafters, the tops of cold stacks, the inside dark. Blue-grey and dun and white-flashed, cooing low in the gloom, shuffling, preening, dust sifting down from their wings in shafts of dim yellow light. Their droppings have whitewashed the oil stains below into pale islands. Eggs roll off beams and crack on concrete, and that is what keeps the rats fat. The weak fall and flutter and are taken. The rest tuck their heads in the midday steam and doze and tick and murmur, a whole soft roof of living sound under the hiss of evaporation off hot metal roofs.

Between the pallets slip the cats.

Thin, ribbed, brindled and black and orange-white, feral now for generations. They don't meow. They watch with huge eyes gone amber in the dimness and move only in a dash — from shadow to shadow, belly low. They know the rat runways better than the rats. They take pigeons too, the ones that drop too low. They lick condensation off cold pipes, off the underside of trucks left to rot. They have kittens somewhere back in the dark volume, blind and mewling in a nest of rotted pallet fiber.

And out on the four-lane, in the open, three dogs work.

Lean as the cats are lean, yellow and black-muzzled, tongues lolling. They trot with noses down, scattering rats in a burst, snapping at pigeons, then losing interest because it is too hot to chase. At this hour they have given up and lie panting in the narrow shade of a tank farm wall, flanks heaving, eyes half-closed but ears up. They drink from the sky too — from a gutter pour that has not stopped since morning rain — lapping at the fresh sheet before it hits the brine-fouled ground.

Over everything small and still, the insects hang.

Columns of mosquitoes and midges shiver above stagnant water, above a dead rat, above a gull pellet. Flies in blue-green clusters work carrion in a ditch — a pigeon, a crab husk, something unrecognizable. The whine comes and goes in your ears. If you breathe through your mouth you taste them, iron and rot.

On the canal edge, where salt comes back in, there is a black band that is alive. Mussels and barnacles crusted thick on concrete, opening and closing with the seep, filtering what the tide brings. And over the hot lots, high in that huge low sky, two or three kestrels hang — wings blurred, tails fanned, hovering on the heat rising off concrete, watching for whatever moves too slowly below. A rat that dares the open. A crab too far from water.

No one feeds them. That stopped years ago.

They feed each other. That is what lives here: a whole town made of meat eating meat on top of concrete, drinking rain as it falls, moving as little as possible until the light dims and the evening rain comes again.
