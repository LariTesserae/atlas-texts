There is no one here.

That is the first thing you have to unlearn. You keep looking for him — the stoker, the watchman, the barefoot caretaker who must be feeding the hopper and oiling the flywheel and changing the bulbs. Something must be raking. Something must be stoking. The engine is turning. The lamps are pulsing. The water is going up to come back down.

No one is raking. No one is stoking.

Stay a little longer and you stop looking for the man and start looking for the animal. A rat in the bread-soft brick. A frog in the tea-colored seep. A bird, anything, to make the noise make sense. Because it is loud here, warehouse-full-of-clocks loud, thump and hiss and plink, and loud means living.

But there are no animals. Not even the right insects. Only those few white moths, blind and silent, bumping from lamp to lamp like lost punctuation. The water is perfect and empty. The air is warm and empty. The green has the place completely to itself.

That is when you start to see it correctly. Not as an overgrown station. Not as plants *on* machines.

As one thing, wearing both.

What lives here is low and wide and sessile. A bowl-sized body, a hundred paces across, that never gets up. You are walking on its skin when you walk on that sighing thatch — wrist-thick rhizome braided over rhizome, moss over that, seedlings in the moss — that seals over your bootprint with tea-colored water when you lift your foot. If you stand still, it notices. Tendrils come out to test the edge of your sole. Not hungry, exactly. Curious. Tasting for salt and iron and wear.

Its blood is water and sap. The seep that breathes an inch below the surface, welling up when you squeeze. The four thin waterfalls falling forever from the split tank of the water tower, lifted back up by a pump that never stops. Split a stem near the dynamo shed and it bleeds metallic green — copper leached from the windings, carried now in the vascular fibers. The vines drink rust straight off the weeping iron and leave pale green streaks. The rocks weep. The concrete weeps. It drinks all of it.

Its breath is steam. Slow, in the molars first, then the ears: the ten-foot flywheel making its revolution once, twice a minute. Thump-thump. With each turn the piston sighs and the split pipe vents sideways and washes a whole wall of leaves white for a second. The leaves lean *into* the scald. Around the boiler they have learned the shape of heat and arch clear in a perfect halo, blackened tips already pushing red shoots back into the hot air. The big trumpet vines open only then, only for that pulse of distilled water.

Its gut is the firebox. Fallen biomass — leaf litter, deadfall, whole curtains of vine — grows *toward* the heat chute as if it were sun, and the roots themselves stuff the hopper. Fire makes shaft. Shaft drives the shredder that chews more biomass. That is its digestion, a season long: leaf to ash to leaf in one slow gradient. Growth and decay and rust all happening at the same patient speed.

And its nerves — its nerves are copper.

The dynamo still makes power and you feel it in your chest before you hear it, that 50-cycle drone under everything. The cables sag low under moss and ferns and drip steadily from their bellies, but at the ceramic bells the damp air crackles blue. At dusk all the leaves nearest the live wires turn their copper-veined undersides outward, together, to catch the field. The switching pylon leaning ten degrees off wears a crown of white orchids that hum faintly when the wind moves the wires. Deep in the canopy the flat belt from the flywheel disappears up into green, rubbing against a living pulley that has grown thick around it, groaning as it turns.

It thinks, if you can call it that, with light and pulse. The caretakers' work-lamps are still strung, and the plants have adopted them. One gourd has grown completely around a bulb, translucent when it lights — and it does light, every time the flywheel hits top-dead-center. On. Off. On. Off. Once, twice a minute. A heartbeat you can see. The blind moths blunder between those pulses. They are about the only thing it tolerates inside itself besides green and iron, perhaps because they are almost not animals anymore — no mouths to eat, no ears to hear the drone, just soft white carriers for the insulator-flowers.

Ah, the flowers. That is how you know it is dreaming about the machines. It makes blossoms shaped exactly like insulators, like fuses, like curled shavings of iron. A fig has rooted *inside* a transformer casing and fruits out through the ventilation slots, each fig warm to the touch like a small heart. It is not mimicking to lure insects. There is no one to fool. It is mimicking because after long enough, industry became its morphology. Photosynthesis by other means.

It does not hurry. It does not forgive or punish. Cut a path in the morning and by evening the edges will have sweated shut with new shoots, not out of malice but because closing is what this place does. It is a room that seals. A fruit holding its seeds — boiler house, dynamo shed, catwalk to nowhere, line of tiles leading into mud — not using them, not worshipping them. Having grown around the idea of industry until the machines became organs.

If you sleep here — and the bathhouse warmth invites it, and the drone lulls — you will wake damp to the bone, condensation having rained off leaf and pipe and wire all night in arrhythmic weather that has nothing to do with sky. There will be leaf-prints on your skin. Iron and green on your tongue. The lamps still pulsing slow. The water still falling upward and then down.

You will have been, for a night, held inside it. Tasted, and found mostly water, mostly salt, and closed over.

That is what lives here. Not a keeper. Not a beast. A slow, warm, electrified mass that learned to burn its own shed leaves to lift its own water to grow more leaves to burn.
