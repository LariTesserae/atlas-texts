At first you think no one lives there. Or no one could, for long.

From the bank it's too smoke-smudged, too low, too crowded with roofs to sort into people. Then your foot finds the first plank — shoulder-wide, slick, giving a little under weight — and the town resolves not into faces but into hands.

Hands always moving. Bailing, shimming, splitting, scraping. Everyone's fingers are ridged and pale and permanently wrinkled at the tips, as if they've just been pulled from a long soak, because they have. Nails split. Resin black under them that won't wash out. Knuckles chapped from cold water. Even the children have old hands.

That is what the bar makes of bodies. Short, because doorways settle lower every spring and you learn not to stand all the way up indoors. Stooped, because the plank runs are low between leaning second stories and you carry buckets that slop if you straighten. Pale in a way that has nothing to do with winter — a damp pallor, like mushrooms, like things kept under boards. Their hair never quite dries. It hangs in ropes and smells of peat smoke. Their wool and hide clothes stiffen and go limp by the hour. By noon the shoulders steam faintly. By dusk they're heavy again.

They touch all the time. There is no room not to.

On those plank runs you don't pass someone, you trade places with them rib to rib, hand on their elbow, their hand on your hip to keep you both out of the mud. Privacy never developed here. Babies are handed up through trapdoors, across lanes from thatch to thatch, without looking up to see who takes them. Old people sleep in shifts in the warm spot by the hearth someone else just left. Talk stays low because a normal voice hits three walls and comes back embarrassed. When someone laughs loud enough to carry across three roofs they stop themselves mid-breath, hand over mouth. You learn to knock with cloth-wrapped mallets. You learn to step where the board is already worn dark.

They are all shimmers and wedgers, by trade if not by name.

Someone is always bailing the bilge-pits under the floors where the runnels finger through — scoop, lift, toss to the channel, scoop again. Someone is always wedging a corner post that has sunk overnight, tapping in a fresh slate flake, then another. Doors are planed in autumn knowing they'll jam by spring. Walls are daubed with fingerprints still in them because why smooth away the only part that fit? They let their houses lean on each other like drunks holding each other up. They lean too. If you watch an old bar-woman walk you see the tilt the house taught her: one shoulder lower, hip cocked to the camber of the planks, feet finding the nails by feel.

They eat eel and minnow and mussel and duck egg when a duck can be spared. They drink their own roofs — rain traveling gutter to gutter to cistern — because the two river arms are not for drinking. The fishermen will tell you that without telling you why. They set their traps exactly, precisely on the seam at the tail of the bar where clearer water and milkier water run side by side for a hundred yards and refuse each other. Eels live under the town, fat in the dark, shifting against the floorboards on warm nights. Everyone hears them. No one lifts a board to look.

The other residents you count on one walk:

The grey heron downstream most mornings, motionless, ignored with great respect. It is the only thing allowed to stand still.

The ducks in the thatch, tolerated because they eat slugs, scolding when you climb too close.

Two or three bats at dusk, stitching roof to roof to take the column of midges.

The old brown dog, once a day, making his slow circuit of the planks, sniffing each post base, each bucket, each child's knee, then lying down again in the one dryish spot that moves with the sun. He belongs to no house because all houses are his house.

And the children with their too-careful buckets, told three things early: spill nothing, sweep spilled water back into the channel, and don't stare into puddles.

That last one you only understand if you stay till evening.

By late afternoon after the thin veil of rain, the puddles skin over with sky and go still. Step away from one and glance back. Your reflection takes half a breath longer to leave than you do. It stays, looking up, after you have gone.

Everyone knows. No adult looks long. They step over, eyes flicking away by habit, the way you learn not to put weight on a fresh shim. Children are told not to stare because children will stare, and get stared back at, and come home quiet and water-eyed and wanting to go back out to check if it's still there.

The old do stare. At dusk you'll see them, if you're not from here and don't know to look away — an old man crouched by a lane puddle, not bailing, not working, just letting his face hang over the water while the light thins to brightness and then thickens again. Letting the other one linger. There is a patience in it that isn't grief. The bar is only a few generations old. The flood that laid it was a slip upstream, a damming and a bursting. There hasn't been time for rot to sweeten the ground, or for a graveyard to take. There is nowhere deep to put the dead in a place where your palm pressed to mud wells up cold around your fingers. So they put them shallow, in other ways.

Stay later and you see what crosses.

After shutters of hide and wicker close and the small hearth fires are coaxed up — never lavish, never enough dry wood — the smoke that wouldn't rise by day crawls sideways between roofs, blue in the dark. Planks cool and tick. The river gets louder. Someone coughs. Someone is always bailing, because the water never stops coming up.

And the eels move.

They will not cross the seam in water. The two arms that look the same will not have each other. So on moonless nights the eels travel overland, from one water to the other, straight through town. In the morning there are wet tracks glinting across planks to prove it, S-curves over worn boards, between walls, sometimes straight through a house left open for them — trapdoor lifted, a wet plank laid down as bridge. No one blocks a doorway at night fully. You leave a gap. You sweep the track back into the channel in the morning without comment.

People who have lived here long enough move a little like that themselves. Low to the ground. At home in the dark runnels. Able to live pressed tight. Able to be in two waters without mixing them.

Because that is the real population of the bar, in the end. Not sixty or eighty roofs worth of people. Double.

There are the day ones, damp and resin-nailed, re-laying planks every week as the ground settles, mending copper thin as leaf, keeping the heron undisturbed and the ducks forgiven. And there are the ones who stay half a breath behind in every puddle, in every beaded foundation boulder, in the breath that hangs even in summer. The unfinished ones. Like the sharp-edged cobbles that haven't learned to be round yet, like roots hanging out of the cut bank like unfinished thoughts, they haven't finished leaving.

To build straight here would be to misunderstand. To dry out fully would be to die. To be one, alone, un-touching, would be to fall off the narrow tongue of gravel back into the talkative current.

So they hold on the way the houses hold on — by crowding, by leaning, by shimming each other up night after night. A wet, cold, crowded, unfinished people, keeping a scrap of new land in the middle of moving water by the sheer stubbornness of staying packed tight enough that the water has to come up through them, and finding, when it does, that they are still there.
