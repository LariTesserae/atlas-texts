At first you think no one lives here.

The terraces climb and climb and there's no movement on them except the movement that's always here — the shiver of leaf, the sway of rope, the hanging dust turning in light. A city for a thousand with no crowd in it. Then a hand flashes, far up near the crown. Then another. Then you learn to see them against the felt.

Seven, when you come.

They are dry people. That's the first true thing about them, before lean or quiet or kind. Dry the way the pod is dry. Skin pulled close over long muscle and bone, oiled until it shines faintly amber in the honey-light — bladder-oil, pressed from the swollen leaves, rubbed in every morning to keep what little moisture the body has from being wrung out of it. Hair cropped to stubble so it won't trap the sugar-dust that sifts eternally off the gardens. Throats wrapped, even in the heat, in thin cloth wound twice around, not for warmth but to feel the roar less in the soft of the neck. Bare feet, soles thick as husk-matting, silent. They move the way people move who have lived a long time inside a drum: without hurry, without wasted vibration, eyes always working.

You won't hear them. Inside, they don't speak. To speak inside would be to shout and still not be heard, and to waste the wet of the mouth. So their hands never stop.

It is not pantomime. It is language, rapid and fluent and full of slang, flicked at a distance across the void — *bridge loose on west two, check knot, moth-scale on high moss, comb the south felt before evening pitch* — whole conversations passed terrace to terrace while the body keeps working. Fingers stained with resin, knuckles cracked, but dexterous to an obscene degree. Babies here, if they stay, learn to sign before they learn to walk the narrow streets without looking down.

At night they go out. That's when you hear what they are.

When the outside air goes slack and cold on the salt pan and the three flute-holes stop pulling wind, the pod quiets to a murmur, a sleeping chest. Then the seven duck out through the seam sideways, one by one, spitting carefully outward and wiping their mouths, and sit in a circle on the white dust that is cool for the only hour of the day. And they talk. In hoarse whispers. Voices rusted from disuse, cracking, laughing at the crack. They talk about knots and moss and where the shell is thinning, the same things they signed all day, just to hear it in air. To remember what their voices sound like. To remember they have them.

In the day they are maintenance made flesh. There is no other work.

One will be up on a bridge with a bone awl between her teeth, cutting away a frayed lashing before the endless tremor saws all the way through rope, re-seating thornwood and re-tying with a knot you couldn't untie with teeth. The vibration takes everything apart eventually; they put it back. Another sweeps the dust-gutters with a bundle of stiff fern — not water-gutters, never water, but gutters for the fine talc-salt that would otherwise drift and deaden a resonant corner. Another combs. Always someone combing. Fingers or a carved bone comb drawn slowly through silver felt-leaf and grey feather-fern-that-is-not-fern, parting the wool so each tiny tympana-leaf can shiver free. If the felt mats, it goes quiet. Quiet is death here. They know where the loud lives and they tend it the way other peoples tend fire.

They chew resin for moisture — a hot-peppery bead that makes the mouth water in self-defense — and cook sparingly with bladder-oil over tiny smokeless fires whose smoke would coat the leaves. They weave on simple looms strung between beams, matting to replace the streets themselves as feet and sound wear them thin. Basalt flake, fire-hardened stick, bone pin. Nothing that would ring false. Everything hand-marked. And one, usually the oldest, checks the shell where noon sun has worn it thin as paper, daubing mud and chaff from inside, patiently patching the sky.

They will tell you, by signs, matter-of-fact, tapping two fingers to the sternum where you feel the drone: *we don't live here alone.*

Because the true population is not the seven. The true population is hanging ten feet deep off every edge.

The gardens. The felt. The lush that should not be.

Silver felt-leaf thick as winter coats near the floor where the low organ hum pools strongest. Grey-green feather plumes trembling higher up. Amber veils of air-moss hanging in curtains you have to push through with your shoulders, soft and clattering like beaten tin. Bladder-worts swollen tight not with water but with scented oil that beads clear and hot when you crush one. Paper-bells up near the crown where the wind-whistle shears thin. Tear the moss and it parts like wool. Crush a leaf and it does not bleed green.

These are phonotrophs. They do not want your water — water would rot the fiber, silence the felt, invite the wrong green, and the tenders will take your waterskin from you at the seam if you forget. What they want is the note.

Look close and you see it: fine white root-hairs reaching *sideways*, not down, creeping along beam and lashing toward a bridge-anchor that hums, toward a node in the wall where the drone doubles on itself. Leaves stiffened to shiver. The whole endless high dry rattle — millions of papery edges shaking — shakes loose from the plants a fine sugar-dust that hangs in the light columns and coats everything, skin and matting and tongue, sweet on the back of the teeth. The felt reabsorbs it. Nothing wasted. Different patches tuned to different pitches, the whole hollow voiced like a choir, silver moss for bass, paper-bells for treble.

They are livestock and citizen and crop at once. Combed, harvested for oil and resin and half the moth-eggs, and also listened to. A tender will press an ear, or a throat, or the back of a hand to a tuft to feel if it's singing right.

And under it all, the meat — what little meat can afford to compete with plants for sound.

Mites in the floor dust, invisible until you rub and see the talc move wrong. A single pale lizard, blind-white almost, that lives under the floor planks. They consider it a citizen. They step around its warming spot, they sign its name — a flick of tongue-finger — and leave it the cool dark it needs. It eats mites and shed sugar-scale and lives longer than any of them can remember.

Once a season, the visitor. A great dust-moth finds a breathing hole in the night and forces itself in, wings wider than a child is tall, beating the honey-air to glowing scales. It lays in the resinous thatch and leaves before dawn. The tenders take half the eggs and leave half. A tithe, both ways. Protein and reverence.

And then the one who lives here who is not flesh at all.

The center. Where nothing is built. Where you are not supposed to linger.

The tenders cross the sagging bridges over that void quickly, eyes up, hands still for once. Because the center belongs to the note. The low organ-drone from the three holes, the thermal turning over and over — hot rising along the sunward wall, cool falling along the shade — the seed-down riding it endlessly, never landing. In late afternoon when light deepens to copper and outside air thickens, it deepens too until your skull buzzes pleasantly, horribly, until the gardens lift all at once like an animal raising its hackles. That's when you understand: the note lives here. It was here before the nests for hundreds, before the alleys that turn to let the drone pass, before the seven. The town is fretting built around it. The garden is the player. The people are just the hands that keep the instrument in tune.

Seven dry whisperers, a wool of singing leaves, a white lizard, mites, half a brood of moths, and a sound so large it needs a city to house it.
