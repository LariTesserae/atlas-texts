What lives here does not drink.

That is the first thing you have to unlearn to see them at all. You keep looking for mouths, for guts, for wet. There are none. What lives here is lush and utterly dry, and it has solved thirst by refusing it.

**The carpet.**

Start low, where your boots sink. Ankles in copper-green, almost black. It looks like liverwort, thick and spongy, a reef-mat laid stem against stem until no salt or glass shows through. Step on it and it gives without juice. Crush it and there is no bruise, no sap — only a puff of fine metallic dust that smells of cinnamon and hot circuitry and ozone.

It is not soft. Under the sponge each frond ends in a foot — not a root. A fused glassy foot, clear and hard, dipped straight into the mineral floor. The floor hums, faintly, tellurically. If you lay your ear to it when the ticking pauses you can feel it in your teeth. The carpet drinks that. Ions. Charge. Through filament stems packed with thousands of dry hollow hairs, and in each hair a bead that is not oil, that never evaporates, that climbs by static alone.

The carpet is the grounding. The collector. It does not grow so much as precipitate, laying itself a molecule thicker, a foot wider, and then halting mid-gesture. It will not finish. Nothing here is allowed to finish.

**The midlayer.**

Waist-high out of the carpet come the whips and fans, sulfur yellow, oxide red. From a distance they look like coral waving in a current that isn't there. Up close they are not waving.

They are antennae. Tuning forks. Each whip is too straight, each fan branches at exact angles and then re-branches, down past what your eye can follow. They do not photosynthesize the way you mean it — they intermediate. They take the current from below and the white-gold light from above and marry them. Break one and inside is not xylem, not water column. It is dry filament, humming faintly. The dust inside is brighter. It stings your nose with pepper.

They are the ones that nearly touch and knock — that soft papery knock you hear when there is no wind. Two fans lean toward each other, almost touch but do not, exchanging a field. Deciding.

**The plates.**

Above you, ten, twelve meters up, the canopy. Broad plates the size of serving trays, translucent, veined with light. When the sun hits them they ring faintly, like struck glass, and throw down a dapple that is warm on your shoulders without any damp in it.

Hold still under one and look at its underside. That is where you understand they are not plants. The veins are too regular. Branch, re-branch, re-branch down to a shimmer that is not texture but interference. A single leaf holds more switches than a city.

The leaf does not flutter. When air — which almost never comes — brushes it, it computes the flutter and decides not to. It stores the light that hits it, holds it a fraction of a second too long, and releases it slightly slowed. That is why the light feels thick. That is why your shadow falls half a step to the left of where it should, and stays there even when you shift. That is why looking at your hands through a plate you see four fingers slightly offset, as if you are out of register with yourself.

The forest knows where photons are supposed to go and edits them. Light is food, water, and weight. Everything eats it, drinks it, stores it.

And the forest does not drop its dead. You will see them everywhere once you learn to see: brown lace leaves suspended in the understory, caught between live stems, hanging for — how long? There is no rot here without water to rot with. They just hang, weightless in dry air, neither falling nor decaying. Pale dandelion clocks the size of your fist, halted mid-air two meters off the ground, each filament perfectly still. If you touch one it does not scatter. It rings, very quietly, and resettles exactly where it was, as if time nudged and then thought better of it.

They reproduce by pausing. Seed, leaf, spore — all halted in the act.

**The tuners.**

For an hour you will think there is nothing else alive. No insect, no bird, no web because no flies to catch. No print, no dung, no breath-fog. Fauna to the point of rumor.

Then, if you are still enough — truly still, your loud wet breathing throttled — you will see one.

Tall as a deer. Narrow as a folded umbrella. Made, seemingly, of the same plate-leaf material as the canopy. Its body is a vertical fold, copper-green darkening to translucence at the edges. Its legs are dry reeds that do not bend but fold, at impossible joints, silently. Its head is flat, broad, leaf-like.

It does not eat so much as tune. It presses its flat head against a frond and the frond dims slightly, passing charge. Light moves visibly under its skin, a slow brightening traveling down its neck into its torso. The animal shivers with stored light.

It is anhydrous meat. Oil and glass and slow electricity. No sweat, no saliva, no piss. It never sips because sipping was never invented here. It can go its whole life on current and sun.

It steps — without sound — into denser green and is gone. It leaves no print in the sharp salt. The dust lifts behind it and smooths, as if the place smooths out intention. Far off, very rarely, you will hear another step that is not a step, a displacement, and understand there are a few more, scattered across a basin the size of a small country that is larger inside than its rim.

They do not herd. They do not hunt. There is nothing to hunt and nothing to hunt them. Occasionally two will stand facing each other, plates almost touching, and dim and brighten in counter-rhythm, exchanging something you can only hear as a quickening in the high thin ticking at the edge of hearing — the leaves switching faster as they pass.

And the whole — carpet and whip and plate and tuner — is one thing, really. A jungle that never drank, a machine that was never built. No wall, no path, no cut stump, no artifact, and yet everything engineered beyond engineering.

When you walk, the ticking quickens almost imperceptibly as you pass. The canopy above you brightens by a fraction as it reads your heat, your capacitance, your strange wet interior, and files it away without judgment. You will not get thirstier after the first hour, nor less thirsty. Your body, too, is paused at the edge of need.

They are holding you — lush and dry and warm and strange — without ever needing to change to keep you.
