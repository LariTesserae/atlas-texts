You have already stepped on one before you see one.

That's how it starts. Ankle-deep in skin-water, brown-green and bath-warm, you put your foot down on what should be black basalt felted with moss and it gives — not rock-give, but a slow, animal give. A yielding under the velvet. You jerk back. The water clouds, settles, clears. And there is still nothing there. Just moss. Just water mirroring nothing.

Then it breathes.

A shallow lifting, an inch, across an area wider than your chest. The moss itself rises and falls. The drip keeps coming down — plink, plink-plink — and under it you feel, through your soles, the faint warmth in the rock get a fraction warmer in one spot. That is the first way you know it. Not by sight. By heat that has an edge.

Kneel. Put your hands in. The water is so warm your hands disappear in it.

What you thought was bottom is back.

It is flat to the point of being a puddle with intention. Four, five feet long when full grown, not much thicker than your hand is wide, edges feathering out into the moss so you cannot say where animal stops and felt begins. Its belly is down in the sheet-water, spread over pillow basalt and cracked pahoehoe, taking the shape of whatever sharp nose it covers. Its top is barely out — a low, mossy dome, acid green and black-green, beaded continuously with condensation exactly like the rock beside it.

Because it gardens itself.

Acid-green velvet moss roots directly into its skin. Not on it — in it. The skin is porous as bone, pitted, constantly weeping, exactly like the young stone. Warm mineral water beads out of its pores, faintly iron and green-smelling, warmer than the air. In that seep, spores catch. Lichen first, orange as spilled paint, then moss, then fern seedlings no higher than your thumbnail, then — on the oldest ones — a thumb-sized orchid or a split-leaf sprouting from the shoulder while the animal is still walking. From below you would call it rock. From above you would call it ground.

It does not mind being ground. That is its trade.

Look closer and you see the dozens. On every fern frond in this basin sit frogs — thumbnail treefrogs in orange and lime and wet slate, throats pulsing — and on its back sit more. Ten, twenty, thirty of them, bodies touching, riding. They drink the seep from its skin. At dusk-that-is-not-dusk, when the dim deepens by a shade, their bellies glow faintly, cold lights, and so the dome sparkles when you disturb it. The animal does not flick them off. It needs them the way a leaf needs drip. They eat the midges that would otherwise clog its breathing plumes.

Ah — the plumes. That is the second strange thing you notice with your hands.

Along its spine, in two loose rows, rise white finger-things into the damp air. At first you think roots. Roots here grow upward as often as down, white finger-roots reaching out of basalt cracks into the steam to drink what never falls. These are not roots. They are gills turned inside-out for air. Feathery, branching, always wet, waving almost imperceptibly in air too thick to drift. The basin water is too warm to hold oxygen — those finger-long fish have to live mouth-up, drinking from the film — so it does not bother to breathe water at all. It breathes the tea you are breathing. It drinks the air with lifted lungs.

And it eats what the basin is always making and unmaking.

Leaves helicopter down constantly — broad plates, elephant-ears — but there is no litter. A leaf hits moss or water and within hours is lace: white mycelium, springtails, pillbugs the size of thumbs. The weeper — call it a mossback sow, call it a sumpter, it has no proper name because nothing stays still long enough to name — competes with that digestion by *being* faster.

Watch its front. There is no head, really, just a thicker lip, a blunt shovel of a mouth wider than your forearm, fringed with short, moss-colored barbels that taste iron. It moves inches per hour. You have to stake a fern to see it move at all. It bulldozes, vacuums. A slow ripple goes out in the ankle-water and a fallen fruit, a softened leaf with its lace of fungus, a ribbon of black tadpoles, a cloud of springtails — all go in with a soft, heavy *plop* exactly like a fruit giving up and falling. You have heard that sound a hundred times today. Half of them were not fruit.

It cannot afford to chew and leave. Anything chewed is taken by fungus here. So it does not chew. It swallows whole and keeps its mouth clean by molting it. Every few hours a translucent sheet sloughs off its back and belly — skin, moss rhizoids and all — and trails for a moment like bridal veil. Before it has drifted a foot the thumb-pillbugs are on it, the ants in their endless living chains pour over to cut it, the leeches find the soft underside. It feeds its cleaners with its own skin to keep from being eaten alive by the air itself. If you lift a flap you see pink new skin underneath already beaded, already being colonized.

Everything clings here because nothing can build. No mounds, no dams, no burrows that last the night — water too high, fungus too fast. So everything clings to the sow. Lizards cling to trunks; orchids cling to branches; on her, the whole understory clings in miniature. Leeches inch along her belly stems of gill. Moss-colored crabs ride in her wake to pick the tadpoles she misses. Vine-thin snakes hang motionless where vine should be *above* her regular path, waiting for her frogs to hop off. Small dark red-throated birds that never leave the basin do not nest so much as perch their eggs in the fork between two of her air-gills and sit, and then leave, and another sits. She walks with a clutch of another species wedged in her breathing organs and does not shake them. Shaking takes energy. Heat does not peak or break here. Energy is for staying wet.

She hears — if hearing is the word — through her belly. Lay your palm on a weeping boulder and you feel it: a low insect drone, millions of wings merging into a single hot wire, and above it the frogs keeping time, bell-tones in different keys, plink plink-plink. She lives inside that chord. Her own call is below it, too low for you to hear as sound. You feel it as an extra late beat when you stand still long enough for your heartbeat to sound late. A thump through basalt and skin-water. Fruit-plop low. Other sows a half-acre away lift their rims a fraction when it passes. That is courtship here. That is territory. A pressure wave in a puddle miles across.

Mating is temporary because everything is temporary, but the amount never drops. In the knee-deep clearings where lilies crowd leaf-to-leaf so tight you could walk on them, two sows will overlap rims at noon-that-is-always-noon. No display. They simply make a deeper place for an hour by lying together. Into the dorsal pools — shallow saucers in the moss of her back that hold bath-warm water even when she hauls out onto a basalt nose — she releases a string of clear eggs. He — or she, they are both in turn — covers them with a film of mucus. Then they separate and each carries a traveling pond on her back.

For a week the back is a nursery. Finger-long upmouth fish nose in from the sheet-water to steal. Tadpoles hatch in black ribbons and graze the moss on her spine. The riding frogs eat the gnats that come to lay. The pillbugs eat the dead eggs before fungus can take the living. When the tadpoles are legged enough to cling, they simply hop off onto the nearest fern frond at face height and join the thousands to an acre, throats pulsing, adding their bell-tone to the held chord. No mothering beyond carrying. No nest to defend because a nest would rot by morning.

And when she dies — which is soon, weeks, a month, growth is furious in this stasis — she does not leave a body.

You find the place where one was by the moss ghost. A slightly raised felt, sow-shaped, still beaded, with seedlings sprouting from a trunk that is already gone. Within minutes the white lace covers the soft underside. Within hours the ants have poured through in living chain and carried her cuttings away, green and pink. The crabs click sideways across. A red-throated bird lands, confused, where its egg-fork was. By evening — if evening means anything in green-black dusk that sweats — the sheet-water has closed over, opaque again, warm again, and another low dome an inch thick is already bulldozing in from the ferns, vacuuming leaves, weeping iron-green water out of stone-pores.

Nothing is being made here except more of itself, immediately, forever. The rock stays young and sharp because nothing washes. The water stays ankle-deep because there is nowhere lower to go. The sow stays because she is the basin's way of walking around for a little while — puddle, garden, mother, rot — before lying back down and letting the basin digest her as fast as it made her.
