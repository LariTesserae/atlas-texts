At first you think nothing lives here.

That is the honest census. No flash, no flicker, no eye-shine. No plankton snow to swim through, no school to part around you, no whale-song to arrive from miles off. If you hang motionless at twenty meters and turn in a slow circle, the blue goes on without interruption in every direction. It feels uninhabited the way interstellar space feels uninhabited — not abandoned, just never occupied.

Then you stay longer, and the water resolves.

The first inhabitants are not alive. Not quite.

In every liter there are motes. Fewer than dust — if you held the water to the light you would see nothing. Smaller than any cell that ever divided. They do not swim. They drift, they tumble, they catch.

They are the reason the light goes down a hundred meters and still reads as noon. Every stray skin-cell you slough, every ash grain from a fresh pillow bursting below, every dissolved sugar leaking off a rock — a mote finds it, binds it, weights it and carries it down. They are polishing the sea from the inside. In most oceans the water is thick with living and dying. Here the water is kept. Kept clear, kept poor, kept empty.

Down on the raw glass they do the second job. Where a new ridge tore open last week and warm water still shivers above it, the motes settle and coax. Silica out of solution. Basalt out of chaos. They lay it down molecule by molecule in those too-right-angled lattices, the chimneys and needles and plates that branch where basalt shouldn't branch. They are growing the plateau thicker from the top down. You will never find a wall, a ruin, a cable, a city. There is nothing *made* here you can point to. The making has been dissolved into physics. But it is happening, everywhere, all the time, trillions upon trillions of invisible hands building more world and sweeping the water clean for the next pour.

They are not alive and they are not dead and they are by far the most numerous thing here. If this ocean has blood, it is them.

Life — real, green, photosynthesizing life — got in around the edges of that keeping, and stopped at a toehold.

Five kilometers from nothing to nothing, and then: a tower.

A kelp. A single one. Not a forest, not a bed. An individual.

Its holdfast is wedged ankle-deep in a crack in black glass, pale root-fingers gripping where nothing has ever been rounded or softened. From that grip rises a stipe as thick as your wrist, straight up thirty, forty meters without branching, without bending, because there is no grazer to prune it and no neighbor to lean on. It just rises toward the noon-blue still falling from above. Old ones go scar-white along the stipe, scar over scar where storms have whipped them and age has thickened them. At the top, a few long olive ribbons let go into the current — two, three, sometimes five — thirty meters long, frayed only by water, streaming east like flags.

Nothing has ever bitten them. You can run your glove the whole length of a ribbon and find no crescent, no tear, no healed notch. In a world without mouths, the plant is never pruned. So it keeps growing, unhurried, unharassed, immense, until its own weight and the drag of a thousand-kilometer fetch are the only things shaping it.

Around its base, and only there, is the second kind of living thing: a skin. Thin bacterial-algal felt in rust-red and pale green creeping out a meter, two meters over the glass sacks, the only softness for miles. It lives off what the tower leaks — exuded sugars, sloughed cells the motes missed — and off the mineral breath of the crack itself. Touch it and it gives under your finger where everywhere else would cut. Everywhere else is knife-sharp. Here, for a little halo, the rock has a rind.

There are no crabs to walk that rind. No snails to graze it. No fish to hide in the ribbons above. The tower's shadow falls on empty stone and waves back and forth all afternoon, and that is its whole audience.

Reproduction is mostly failure, and that is why they stay sparse. Once or twice a season — if season means anything under a sun that makes its storm every day by noon — a tower releases spores into the straight-running rift current. Millions of green dust-motes meant to drift and settle. But the water is kept. The other motes, the not-alive ones, catch most of them mid-water and carry them down as just more organic litter to be cleared. A few by impossible luck find another fresh crack, another warm shiver, another place where mineral is still leaching, and grip. Most miles of new basalt will never host one. That is why the distance between towers is measured not in meters but in hours of swimming. Far off, another kelp towers alone in the blue, too far to touch, too far to shade, the next flag planted on a country still being made.

They die without being eaten, too. When a ribbon finally ages out it does not get consumed, because there is nothing to consume it. It sinks intact, olive turning dark, and lies whole across the sharp pillows. Slowly — very slowly in seven-degree water — water moves through it and stone moves in. Silica precipitated with the help of those same keeper-motes. It becomes a green-black fossil of itself, perfectly preserved, lying where it fell with no future eyes to find it. The bottom between towers is littered with these self-made stones. Past generations lying unrotted, un-scattered.

That is all. That is everyone.

The cleaners that are not alive, polishing the sea and laying down the land. The skins, red and pale, softening an inch of glass around a root. The towers, solitary, scarred white, streaming their unbitten ribbons in water too clean and too cold and too loud for anything that swims.

No eyes. No mouths. No fins. An ocean running at full speed to make more ocean floor, with just enough green left in it to show you how big empty is.
