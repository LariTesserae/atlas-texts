They don't live here the way things live elsewhere. There is no staying, no territory held. There is only holding on, and eating, and being eaten fast enough that nothing has time to rot.

From a distance they are not individuals at all. They are a texture on the rock.

Come closer and the texture sorts itself by height, the way sediment sorts — not because anyone planned it, but because falling and climbing and flying all have different costs in heat like this.

Highest are the soot-swifts.

You hear them first as a wrongness in the air, a tearing. Then you see them and you can't unsee them: small birds the color of the ash plain above, ash-gray from beak to tail-tip except for a soot-black bib under the chin, with wings bent like sickles that never quite stop. They don't soar. They cut. Ten thousand at a time they rake the basin in flat, intersecting arcs, wingtips almost touching rock, screaming — a high, hysterical *kik-kik-kik* that comes back half a second later off the far wall so every bird sounds like two.

They don't land anywhere else. Their feet are almost gone, little black hooks good only for hanging onto the pocks in basalt. On every ledge wide as your palm they are packed fifteen, twenty deep, shoulder to shoulder, breast to back, a living whitewash. The guano is inches thick under them and still warm from their bodies. Chicks are naked pink mouths in a gray press, heads up, crying without pause. If a chick slips — and one slips every few minutes — it doesn't hit the ground whole. It hits the middle air and is already being torn. The crabs get what lands.

Below the swifts, where the wall goes too steep for feathers, are the clingers.

You have to force your eyes to slow down to see them as animals and not damp on the rock. Thousands of salamander-things the color of wet brick, flat-bellied, splay-toed, each as long as your hand. They don't move unless you stare. Then you see the whole face breathe: throat sacs pulsing in and out, in and out, fast as a heart. They drink fog off their own skin. In the morning when condensation beads on every overhang, they tip their heads back and let it run down their snouts into their mouths. Between drips they lick up white eyeless crickets and pick flies out of the air with a tongue-flick you hear more than see. When the slab-calving thump comes, they flatten to nothing and ride it out. An hour later they are back pulsing.

Lowest, on the scree itself, is the carpet.

It isn't scree. It resolves, a meter at a time as you walk toward it, into crabs. Fist-sized, low-slung, ash-pale carapaces with joints flashing signal red — knee, claw-hinge, eye-stalk base — so that when they move together the whole fan shimmers. They move always. Sideways, over each other, claws up, claws down, a continuous low hiss of shell on fresh basalt. They eat anything still. Fallen swift, fallen chick, stunned fly-column glittering with condensed water, each other soft after molting, the film on top of a tea-colored puddle, your bootlace if you stand still long. They are why you don't stand still long. The carpet closes over boots in under a minute, testing with claws.

And through everything, the insects, which are really the weather here.

White crickets with no eyes boil out from under every lifted stone, antennae long as their bodies, tasting ammonia and heat. Flies travel not as clouds but as ribbons, dense black columns twenty meters long that bend in the updraft, and sometimes in the cool of morning water beads right on their wings until they get too heavy and drop glittering to the crabs. Dung beetles — broad, black, improbably strong — roll balls of guano and crab-meal twice their size backward across the angular rock, up and over edges that should stop them, burying them in ash fans where the ash has crusted with salt. Where they bury, the ammonia ferments. That's the base of it. That's the soil that isn't soil yet: shit fermenting in warmth, feeding maggots, feeding everything above.

Above all of it, riding, are the gliders.

Only three or four at a time, but you feel each one. A three-meter span of leathery, sun-cracked skin, dark brown veined pink underneath, head tucked like a vulture's, tail a short rudder. They don't flap in the sink. They can't. They ride the trapped thermal round and round, silent, casting a fast shadow. When that shadow passes, the whole wall inhales — twenty thousand swifts lift at once in a sheet that darkens daylight for a breath, the clingers flatten, the crab hiss stops. Then the glider tips, drops, and cracks a crab with a precise foot-stab, or tears a soft joint from a molter, or lands heavy on something already dead. A dead glider this morning is by afternoon not a glider. It's a glistening red articulation of crabs and beetles, humming.

Between boulders pour the other hunters. Brick-red lizards, quick as spilled liquid, long-toed, narrow-snouted, the same wet-brick color as the clingers but dry and scaled. They don't bask — there's no need. They flow from shadow to shadow, flip a crab, crack it at the thin neck with a sideways jerk, eat the greenish meat inside and leave the claws for the beetles. You hear them as a dry skitter before you see them.

In the puddles, for the day a puddle lives, are the briefest ones.

Tea-colored, ammonia-bitter, a hand deep, rimmed white with salt that by morning has grown into tiny cauliflower towers. In the brown water flicker tadpole shapes, mouths up, grazing the film of condensate and powdered shell and fly-dust. They hatch impossibly fast from eggs laid the day before in a different hollow, feed for hours, and when the puddle shrinks they either wriggle down into damp ash and wait or become a wriggling mouthful for a crab or a clinger. Nothing is wasted. The water itself is alive and then gone and then alive somewhere else.

And then, seasonally, us.

Not residents. intruders who know the rules. Lean people in heat-bleached shirts who come down the torn scarp in the cool season with split baskets and bone awls and stacked-slab blinds roofed with woven swift-feather mats now caked white. They keep to the cairn path — step here, not there — because the ground that looks empty is brooding crab eggs and buried beetle balls and cricket nests. They take swift eggs, one from a press of twenty so the ledge doesn't panic, and empty shells, and sackfuls of guano that burn the nose. They boil crabs in a dented tin over dried dung. They drink never from a pool with tadpoles, only the drip ticked off an overhang cupped in the hand. They leave three stick ladders lashed with twine leaning against a nesting face, rungs polished by hands, and go back up before the hatching peaks, before the basin becomes unworkable, unbearable, too alive.

None of them belong to the rock yet. The swifts do. The clingers do. The crabs do. They are what happens when rock is made so fast and so hot that plants haven't had time to imagine it, and animals skip straight to the second step: eat the dead, eat the shit, eat each other, breed in borrowed rain, and make so much noise doing it that the stone itself seems to tick and breathe with them.
