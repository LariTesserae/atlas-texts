At first you think nothing lives here.

That is what the Summit Sea wants you to think. You drift between trunks as thick as redwoods and you feel watched and unwatched at once, and you see nothing look back, and you decide: empty. A green cathedral with no congregation.

Then you stay a little longer. You hold out your hand and something lands on it and takes root. And you understand that emptiness was a failure of scale. Everything lives here. It just lives slow, and green, and touching.

The first residents are the broth itself.

The water is not water. It is a soup of exhaled sugar, torn leaf, spore and mucus-net and bacterial bloom, lantern-tipped seeds adrift by the million. If you could wring out a cubic meter of this dusk you would find ten thousand mouths in it, all microscopic, all trading sugar for silt for rot for root. The forest feeds them when the roots weep, they cloud, the sponges drink the cloud, the sponges die and become silt, the silt feeds the roots. It is a loop so tight and so warm you could miss it entirely. But it is the engine. Without it the great trunks would starve in their own shade.

Out of that engine, larger bodies condense.

You have already met the ones visitors call animals, because they move. The blind white ray that passes overhead once in a day, flapping without hurry. The school of needlefish no bigger than eyelashes, turning as one silver thought. The dog-sized slug grazing feather-moss with a garden growing on its back.

They are not animals in the way you mean. They are witnesses.

Look at the ray: milk-white, eyeless, its skin so thin you can see the green light through it. It does not hunt. There is nothing to hunt. It circles the tableland for years, sinking so slowly when it dies that the meadow will grow up through its ribs before it lands. While it lives, everything that happens below is recorded in the pattern of algae on its underside — a living map of upwellings and gyres and shed-leaf storms. The needlefish are the same, only faster and more brief. A school is a single eye that lasts a week and then dissolves into larvae. The slug is a walking meadow, and the plants on its back flower only while it moves, so the forest can tell where it has been by what blooms in its wake.

They are rare on purpose. The place can only stand a very few eyes moving around inside it. More would make noise, make hunger, make teeth. So there are few. Do not chase them. That is not a rule for your safety. It is census control.

The true people are slower, and you have probably already swum past a dozen without seeing them.

They were builders once. The doorway alone in the meadow, the seven fitted steps climbing to nothing, the humming ring, the drinking face worn smooth — those are theirs, from when they still needed doors and steps. Then they learned the trick of the place, which is also its temperature: stay warm and you are held. Shiver and you sink. Harden and you fall.

So they softened.

You find one only when you stop looking for a face and start looking for a thickening. A trunk that is a little too warm. A curtain of feather-moss that breathes out of rhythm with the rest. A patch of root-mat that gives underfoot like a slow lung.

The Held — divers have started calling them that, because no other word sticks — are no longer separate from the forest. A grown one is tall when it unfolds, two to three meters of translucent green-gold tissue, veined like a ribbonkelp blade. Ribs you can see light through. Hair that is not hair but a dozen living fronds, rooted in the scalp, lifting ten meters with the morning canopy sigh and settling at dusk. Skin that stopped being boundary long ago: press your palm to a forearm and you feel the faint sweet-salt weep of root-sugar, and within a minute spores have landed and taken root in the sweat of your hand and in the sweat of theirs. They are gardens that walk, briefly.

Their feet are the strangest part. They do not end. Below the ankle the flesh pales, threads, divides into holdfast tendrils that are always half-rooted even when they move. A Held crossing a meadow does not step so much as unroot and re-root, leaving a line of shallow breathing holes behind it that exhale those wrong silver strings that fall down instead of rising. Those holes keep breathing for days after. If you put your ear to one you hear, very faintly, tick-tick, and a heartbeat that is not yours and is not not yours.

They have faces, but they rarely use them. Eyes large, dark, made for perpetual late dusk, lidded with nictitating moss. Most keep them closed — like the carved face on the boulder, mouth open as if drinking. That carving is not a god. It is a portrait, and an instruction. In the hollows where light pools like water, a Held will kneel and scoop brightness in cupped hands and drink it. Not metaphorically. The pooled amber stays a little while before it leaks, and in their gut lives a symbiont bacteria that can eat light secondhand. They drink dawn the way you would drink broth.

They navigate the way you have already learned to, badly. They think *rise* and drift up. They think *fall* and sink toward the meadow. Their exhaled bubbles sometimes rise, sometimes hesitate, sometimes drop past their faces toward the ground, and they read those bubbles the way sailors read wind. A child Held learns before it learns to speak — and they almost never speak, sound does not travel right here — learns to empty its lungs and hang perfectly still between amber above and indigo-green below, listening to trunks rub and groan, learning which trunks are mothers and which are grandmothers.

Their work, if you can call it work, is manners.

If you find a staircase, climb it. Not because it leads anywhere, but because the forest notices manners. The Held climb. Seven steps up to nothing, turn, seven steps down, every day, on every staircase they can find. They weed the doorway lintels. They sit inside the stone rings while the current passes and hums through the precise balance, and they hum back, very low, until stone and throat are in tune. They oil the drinking faces with kelp mucus so the mouths stay open. No roads. No cities. Nothing that could not be made by hand and left. Maintenance of meaning, not of use.

When a warm upwelling comes from below, smelling faintly of iron, the whole forest leans toward it at once. The Held lean fastest. They gather, root-feet tangling into a temporary mat, frond-hair spread wide, and drink the iron. In that hour you can see them clearly, dozens, a pale grove inside the grove, swaying. They do not talk. They lean.

At dusk — though it is always dusk — the lantern-seeds ignite by the million. This is their hour, and yours to watch if you are lucky. The tiny cold yellow-green fires drift between trunks in veils. The Held open their eyes at last and release their own seeds from the gardens on their backs and shoulders and slug-like companions. Each seed is a memory: a taste of iron, the creak pattern of a particular canopy lift, the location of a good light-hollow. The seeds drift, land, take root, or are eaten into the broth. Nothing is written down here. It does not need to be. The past does not wash away; it accretes.

Because the dead do not rot. There is not enough teeth, not enough hunger. When a Held dies — which takes a very long time, decades of slower and slower unrooting — it does not fall. It settles. It chooses a spot in the meadow, threads its foot-tendrils deep, spreads its arms like fronds, and stops deciding to move. Moss covers it within weeks. Quillwort grows through the ribs. The translucent skin goes opaque, then green, then brown, then mat. Walk the root-mat in some places and you are walking on ten thousand years of forest and people, compressed, still faintly green inside. If you lie flat and press your cheek to that springy ground you can feel them, layer upon layer, holding the whole shallow sea up on the back of the world.

That is why they never try to find the surface. Some visitors swim up until the water gets brighter and warmer and emptier, until there is no forest, no ground, only light and the terrible feeling of being at the top of everything with nothing to hold. The Held turned back generations ago. The forest is kinder than the sky. Up there is only sun-rumor and rain striking a membrane that does not let you through. Down here is touch. Frond on frond on sponge on stone on root on hand.

If one notices you — truly notices, not just lets you drift by — it will do nothing spectacular. It will turn its closed eyes toward the sound of your too-loud heartbeat. It will extend a veined hand, palm up, and wait until something lands on it and takes root. Then it will extend that hand toward you, offering. Accept. Stay warm. Do not chase. Climb when you are asked to climb without being asked.

You will feel, for a moment, not wet. Held.
