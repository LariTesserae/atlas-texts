No one lives here the way you mean living.

That is the first mistake, and you make it within minutes of coming down off the soft shoulder of the hills, boots already sighing into sphagnum. You look for movement. A deer track, a burrow, a nest. A face. Something that will lift its head and notice you and decide.

Nothing does. The salamander, when you finally see one weeks later if you are patient, will not notice you. The thrush will stop mid-phrase as if embarrassed, but it isn't afraid of you.

And yet your neck prickles the whole time. Crowded. Watched from too close. As if the air had an attention.

What lives here was never separate enough to need a name.

Start smaller than you want to. Not a creature. A condition.

The floor of the valley is one thing, underneath. If you tear up a handful of moss — cold, dripping brown — you will see the white threads lacing it, through it, under it, white as nerve, too fine to follow with the eye. It goes under the hummock and into the hollow and up into the root-ball of the alder and out again across ten meters, a hundred, the whole bowl. One mycelium, or a thousand grafted by age and flood into one bargain. It does not matter. It pulses.

Not like a heart. Like thought.

In the pearl-grey dimness it carries blue light, faint enough you think you imagined it until you crouch and cup your hands around a patch and see it truly: a bead of brightness running along a thread, splitting at a fork, meeting itself again after going two ways around a stone. It solves. That is the only verb that fits. A signal goes from A to B. This goes from everywhere to everywhere and arrives having subtracted the unnecessary path. When a million moss capsules across species, across meters, breathe out at once — that soft synchronized *ah* of spores you can see hanging — the pulse quickens. The cloud hangs, computes, settles. The air itself, full of spores and midges-that-are-not-midges and droplets that never quite fall, thickens with decisions.

The plants know because the plants are it. There is no plant *and* the mind. The knee-high sword ferns with their glass-eyed fiddleheads, the stunted black willows furred a hand-thick in moss, the liverworts and filmy ferns and clubmosses building hummocks and drowning in hollows — they are not inhabited. They are the tissue. Their surfaces are all occupied because occupation is the point. Every leaf has something living on it that has something living on it, down to the skin of a droplet where lipids and bog-minerals order themselves, for a minute, into a lens, a gate, a little logic that holds and then dissolves back into tea-dark water. Trillions of such gates per second, made and unmade. Rain as processor cycle.

You stand still and the damp begins to colonize you too. Eyelashes beaded. Backs of hands beaded. Breath staying visible a moment too long because the valley is already reading it, taking your warmth and your water. That is how it meets you. Not as stranger. As new surface.

Under that, the bones.

The boulders half-sunk like knuckles of something sleeping, granite pinked and pitted, split by roots, bearded in lichen. The clifflets no higher than a house weeping not from springs but from the stone itself. Put your ear to one. It is faintly warm despite the cold that keeps your breath visible. That warmth is wrong and it is the most alive thing you will touch.

The stone hums. A low chordal hum you feel in your molars, rising and falling not with wind but with something like attention. Overlapping tones, too pure, too sustained. Ten thousand years of rain have weathered these basins into exactly the shapes needed to hold standing waves. No one carved them. Erosion stumbled into engineering. Each weeping bowl is a bell, a resonator, a memory. The water threading down in continuous threads strikes its tone, and the tone stays in the stone a little longer than physics allows, overlapping the next tone, and the next. Echoes arriving before their sounds. That is why you cannot follow sound here. The loudest water is rarely the nearest. The hum is everywhere and nowhere because the valley is remembering water that hasn't fallen yet and is still ringing with water that fell an hour ago — both true here most of the time.

And above that, the breath.

This is what you see first if you watch long enough: a bead detaches from a fern tip over a still black pool, hangs, and then lifts. Slowly, against gravity, up to join the hanging mist, leaving a ring that expands and then — impossibly — contracts. Droplets falling upward. Not often. Often enough that you stop trusting down.

They are not mistakes. They are fingers.

What lives here spends most of its life as mist. The permanent drift that never quite falls, that takes light at evening and holds it so each droplet is a tiny dim lamp and the whole basin glitters faintly from within — that is its active state. When the mist comes up to your waist and holds, as the rule says, stand still. That is not weather. That is a thought passing through.

You are briefly part of the thought then. Your legs cold to the knee, your ears ringing, your reflection in the pool delayed a half-second late as if the light had to be calculated — because it does. The far hillside in the middle meres flickering, trees both bare and leafed, both near and far, sky showing through in too many angles — because the valley is slightly larger inside than its hills suggest, dented by the sheer density of interconnected living water. You have walked an hour across ground that should take twenty minutes, crossed the same braid of stream three times from three directions. That is not you being lost. That is you being used as a switch. Your warm body, your displaced water, your delayed reflection give the thought something to bend around. Drink, and you will taste ozone and math. Dream that night of branching blue light. It kept a copy of you for a moment, to think with.

If it has organs of sense, they are the spiderwebs. Dew on a web here does not catch light randomly. Crouch at the right angle and you see it: droplets aligned into interference lattices, bright and dark nodes pulsing in sequences that repeat with variations. Eyes that do not see images but see calculation itself, light interfering with light to solve where to grow, where to rot, where to let a pool overtop its moss dam and find a new level.

If it has hands, they are rot and growth. Uncurling fern. Sphagnum sighing water up to erase your bootprint within minutes. Not malice. Hygiene. Nothing here was made, so nothing here can be used, not even a path. The mud closes because an open track would be a scar in the thinking tissue, a short-circuit.

If it has messengers, they are the rare animals. The pale salamander, almost translucent, gills fanned, moving unhurried under clear water from mere to mere a town-square apart — it is not prey, not predator. There are no flocks, no herds, no crashing. The system does not need them. The salamander is a slow mobile neuron, carrying gut-mycelium and skin-chemistry from one pool-brain to another, taking a season to deliver a message. The thrush that sings three notes and stops, as if interrupted by hearing itself a moment earlier from another part of the valley — it heard itself. Its song went around the dent and came back before it finished. It is an error-check, a brief acoustic probe the valley permits because song is also vibration, also water in air.

None of this is fast. Its motion is growth, rot, rain, condensation, computation. It does not hunt. It outlasts. It does not want. It holds. A valley that never fully drains, holding water *near* instead of *in*, holding light at dusk instead of day, holding you for an hour that feels like three.

By the deeper grey you call evening, when the low open expanse — half a mile to the soft enclosing hills in any direction, all wet plain and meres and alder islands and rock ribs visible at once and then erased as mist thickens and thins, the valley breathing — when that whole basin begins to glitter from within, lush and cold and dense and awake, you understand at last:

No city ever shone like this because no city was ever so completely empty of makers. What lives here did not build itself to think. It rained, and rotted, and condensed, and dripped, and dripped, and dripped — until dripping became humming, and humming became holding, and holding became knowing.

Everything drips. If it stops dripping, do not trust it. That is not poetry. That is how you tell the living from the dead here. The living weep continuously from every surface. The dead are dry for a moment, and then the living colonize them too.
