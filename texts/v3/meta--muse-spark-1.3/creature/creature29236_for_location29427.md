They are not visitors. You can tell by the lungs first, and then by the eyes.

The Hollow-born breathe shallow and without thinking in air that would make an outsider gag and weep in ten minutes. It is warm, close, feathered with down and moth-scale and fine guano ash, sharp underneath with ammonia. A lowlander coughs himself raw trying to clear it. A Hollow woman in her thirties — which is not young here — just breathes, her chest moving a little too visibly, a little too wide. They are all barrel-chested, even the children. They have to be. Up on the plateau the air is thin and cold and scoured clean; down in the egg the air is thick and warm and half-used by twenty thousand other mouths. Their bodies learned to make do with the leavings. Their cough has a name: hollow-throat. Every child gets it in the first winter and either keeps it soft or keeps it forever. The old ones huff when they climb, a wet paper sound, and spit grey.

Their eyes have learned the dusk. In full sun they squint to slits — the young eye-cleaners who go up to scrape the rim come back blinded and weeping for an hour — but in the amber brown of the Hollow they see everything. They thread bone needles by rat-fat lamplight. They find a dropped needle in dung dust. Their whites are never white. They are tea-colored, dust-colored, faintly pink at the corners from constant irritation. They line them with soot-grease, not for beauty, though it has become beautiful — a black smudge that cuts the glare of the central column when they have to cross through it.

You would not pick them out of a crowd by height or color so much as by coating. They are powdered. From the hair to the sandals they carry a film of pale ash, guano dust, feather-down that no amount of shaking ever quite removes. Their hair — black, rust-black, grey early — is worn long and braided tight to the skull and stuffed up into caps of beaten fiber, because loose hair in the Hollow becomes a nest for mites and down in a day. Men and women both wear it so. Their skin underneath is paler than it should be for high desert people, damp-pale from living without true sun, chapped at the mouth and nose where the dry air cracks them despite the warmth. They smell, to themselves, like nothing. To you: sour milk and smoke and birds, sweet rot, warm feathers, old sweat. They oil their hands and feet with rendered rat-fat against the cracks. It does not quite work. Every adult's heels are split like old leather, every knuckle swollen.

Hands are the truest thing. Even a child's hands are rope-wise. Before they can read — there is nothing to read — they can tie the six giving-knots that hold a wattle platform to soft rock and flex when ten thousand swifts lift at once. Their fingers are short, callused at the pads, nails broken short for picking. The old woman scraping guano cakes with a shoulder-blade hoe has a thumb permanently bent sideways from fifty years of twisting beard-fiber cord. The men who mend the bridges walk them barefoot while working, toes curled for grip, a coil of cord over one shoulder like a bandolier.

They dress like part of the wall fur. Long vests and wrap-trousers of beaten beard-fiber, soft as burlap and the color of dust-olive, patched with squares of down-felt — swift-down swept from the floor, carded, pressed with spit and fat until it holds. In winter, when cold seeps down the eye-shaft, they add cloaks of goat-hide with the hair left on, or, for the best-off, a sewn shawl of carpet-moth wings, grey and brown eyespots, that sheds dust. No shoes to speak of, just sandals of braided cord with fungus-leather soles, because they need to feel the sway. No jewelry but copper — a thin beaten bead at the ear, a grandmother's ring twisted around a finger, dull green. Everything else would be pecked off or lost in the guano.

Children are everywhere and underfoot and impossibly sure-footed. There is no quiet childhood here. A baby learns to sleep through wing-thunder, to be hushed not into silence but into the town's lower chitter. Toddlers are tethered — a cord around the waist tied to a strut — not from cruelty but because a platform is a ten-foot drop to dung-brick and goats. By five they are useful: sweeping down for baskets, turning fungus strips, shooing rats from the wrong ledges, clicking back at the caged crickets. By eight they climb like harvestmen, all splayed limbs, up and down the ladders that sway when a flight goes out, laughing when adults gasp. The proudest and most terrified are the eye-cleaners, the lean fourteen-year-olds chosen each season to go feet-first up the bird-bone ladder into blinding open sky and scrape the rim clean. They come back changed, blinking, sunburnt on one side of the face, suddenly adult, allowed to sit at the night hearth and tell what the flat world looked like.

The old do not retire. There is no room for useless mouths and also no way to stop working when work is all around you. The guano-scrapers, mostly old women, move along the edges with their hoes all day, singing very low, half-deaf. Deafness is common after forty. Not total — selective. They stop hearing the high swift-chitter and hear only human voices and the low huff of owls. They say it is a mercy. They become the memory of knots, sitting in doorways mending net and cord by touch, telling children which bridge sags before rain that never comes.

But humans are only the third-most numerous soul in the Hollow, and they know it. They live like a polite, late-arriving colony tucked between older ones.

First are the sooties. If you have not lived under them you will think of them as birds. They are not birds the way a hawk is a bird. They are weather. Soot-black backs, dirty-white throats like a splash of ash, bodies no longer than your hand, wings like sickles that never seem to fold properly. They pack the upper dark in a living crust, ten, twenty thousand in season, each clinging to a toehold in soft basalt with tiny needle claws, shoulder to shoulder, tail to beak, shuffling, preening, chittering that high needle *tseet-tseet* that gets into your teeth. Individually they are scrappy, bug-eyed, warm as a coal. Together they are a single animal that breathes and twitches. When the dawn ribbon goes — first three, then thirty, then a roaring corkscrew up and out the eye to hunt high-air plankton for fifty miles — the whole town sways on its cords and everyone grabs a strut without looking. When they return at dusk, crops swollen with midges, dropping through the slot like stones and then flaring at the last second, the air fills with dry thunder and falling feathers and the smell of warm guts. The Hollow-born do not worship them. That would be presumptuous. They make room. They never reach into a live nest. They gather only the fallen each morning — the cold chicks, the failed flyers, the old dead — into baskets, quickly, reverently, before the rats get them. A dead sooty is soup, is a strip of smoked breast, is a thimble of fat, is feathers for felt and bone for awls. Nothing else is asked.

Second are the owls, and no one admits how much they love them. Barn owls gone huge and pale in the dark, fat as cats, heart-faced, blind in the day column and absolute at noon when the crust sleeps. They own the lower dark and the edges of the human rings. They slide silent across the light shaft at dusk and you gasp the first time because they are so large and so soundless after all that chitter. Each family platform tolerates one, the way you tolerate a strict landlord. It eats rats that would eat stored fungus and cricket cages. In return it is never trapped, never named loudly, left the warm entrails of butchered goats on a flat stone. The children whisper to them. The old say an owl looking at you means you are forgiven for a loud noise at noon.

And then everything else that lives off the fall: the quick grey rock-rats, long-tailed, clever-handed, officially vermin and unofficially livestock, trapped on the floor in bone snap-traps and fattened in wicker cages on fungus parings before they become night stew; the carpet-moths in brown clouds around the light column, *thup thup* against skin, whose larvae the harvestmen eat and whose adults the goats snap at; the blind-white harvestmen themselves, hand-span leggy things picking daintily over moss, cleaning up mite and scale; the thick-bodied singing crickets kept in every home in little beard-fiber cages, clicking all night, eaten when salt comes, replaced from the walls; the few long-legged goats, dun and narrow as deer, that never leave, that eat trimmings and moss-scraps and beard-ends and give thin sweet milk that is mostly water — precious, life-saving water combed from air by plants and passed through a goat; even the small dust-colored dogs, silent, short-haired, that sleep under ladders and eat nothing you can see and wake if a bridge cord frays.

Water is how you know they belong here and you do not. They do not drink water. They chew. The fat waxy rosettes, leaves thick as a thumb, are nipped and sucked for their stored sour juice. Air-plant bulbs are braided into onions and roasted at night until they weep. Fungus broth, thin and liver-colored, is sipped from bone cups. Goat milk is shared by the mouthful. In the driest month they wring the beard-plant curtains at dawn into cloths and suck the cloths. Their urine is dark and scant and goes straight to the fungus beds. They sweat almost nothing. Their bodies have learned what the moss knows: comb it out of breath.

At noon they go still, all species together. The crust stops shuffling. The owls open their eyes. The humans whisper. Even the rats move softly. You can hear your own heart, and then someone else's baby breathing, and then the rope creaking as the whole hanging town settles. At night, after the dung-cakes smoke in the three hooded pits and the rat-fat lamps bloom like dim stars on every ledge, they sing low — not to be heard, but to cover the birds settling, to make a second colony sound underneath the first. That is them. Not masters of the Hollow. Just the ledge that learned to tie knots.
