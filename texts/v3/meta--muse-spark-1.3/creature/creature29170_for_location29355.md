You will think nothing lives here.

That is the first courtesy the place extends to you. It lets you believe in the emptiness — the wet floor to the horizon, the tools with no hands, the ladder leaning on air. You are the only animal heat for acres, and your mind will insist on keeping it that way.

Stand longer.

Let your eyes unfocus the way the steam wants them to. Feel the floor give that millimeter under your soles, warm as fresh bread, and give again, a minute later, when you have not shifted at all.

It is not settling. It is noticing.

What lives here does not live *on* the bricks. It lives *as* the bricks, and under them, and in the habit of laying them. You are standing on a back.

---

Down, far down past where you could ever pry — brick turned sideways on brick turned sideways, getting hotter and softer until the trowel won't come back up — the laying never hit bottom because bottom is alive.

There, where the bricks are just warm paste and the heat comes up in slow pulses, lie the Deep Ones. The Mothers of the Lay.

Each one is vast. A work-square vast. From a blister-top at dimmer-dim when the cracks vent orange you can see their boundaries written in light: those huge irregular tiles, each slightly out of true with the last, each steaming faintly. That is not a work schedule. That is skin. Each square is one animal, lying shallowly buried in itself, breathing an inch down an hour and an inch up the next.

They have no faces to find. No front or back that matters. They are thickenings, like the continent is a thickening. A body the consistency of wedged clay, veined with hot water that remembers level and holds it across miles. If you wade through a pan here and wade through a pan beyond the horizon, you are wading in the same held breath. It is their blood, and it is all at the same height because they will not allow themselves to be uneven inside.

On their upper sides — just under the crust you walk on — they grow hands.

Not metaphorical hands. Hands.

Hundreds to a back, shoulderless, wristless, rising straight out of the paste on stalks of paler mud. Small at first, the size of a fingernail, each with a fingerprint too small for any human hand. You have seen those prints already, in the broken blister that was cracked like an egg, on the perfect miniature bricks inside, still steaming. That was a nursery. Do not break them. That was someone's beginning, laid down in miniature before they are strong enough to lay full-size.

The hands grow. They push up through the soft brick, through the sucking mud-mortar, and in the dark wet heat they pat.

*suck - pat. suck - pat.*

That sound you hear always in another direction, receding at the speed you approach — that is them. You will never catch one in the act. The builders left only the habit of building, because the builders *became* the habit. If you stand dead still in a white-hot storm-afternoon when visibility is down to twenty bricks and the ticks of expanding salt fill the air, and you hold your animal breath, you will feel the floor around your feet go busy. A thickening of pats. A careful laying, just out of sight, behind the steam-belly dragging low. In the morning there will be a new low wall where there was flat yesterday, still shiny, fingerprints fresh. They worked around you the way water works around a stone.

They do not see you. They have no eyes because there is nothing here that needs to be seen from a distance. Distance is unfinished here — you know that, you have walked toward a kiln that stayed the same size for an hour and then was suddenly beside you. The Mothers do not navigate by sight. They navigate by touch and by level and by warmth.

Everything has been touched. If it exists, a hand shaped it. That is not philosophy to them. That is how they know a thing is real. A brick is not a brick until a palm has pressed it and a thumb has dragged for keying. A wall is not a wall until it weeps with the same damp as the hand that made it. You, when you leave your four brief prints that fill slowly with brown water behind you — they read those. A strange, too-hot, too-heavy, two-footed pressure, drying at the edges. You are the only thing here that tries to dry. They do not understand you, but they touch you back by touching where you have been. By morning your footprints will be overturned, relaid, patted smooth in the slow turbulent remaking. It is not hostility. It is tidying. It is making you touched, and therefore real.

What do they eat? Unfinishedness.

They take warm paste from below, squeeze water out of it just enough to hold a shape — a hand-sized brick, swollen, dusky red and liver-brown and ash-grey — push it up into the cool weeping air where salt whiskers can bloom out of it in pale rosettes and slag-glass can grow like black frost, and then they let the almost-rains melt it again. Laying and unlaying. Wetting and warming. They metabolize the difference between wet and wetter. Nothing ever dries. Drying would mean finished, and nothing here is allowed to finish, because if it finished they would starve.

Their young are everywhere and nowhere.

First, the Slicks. You have seen them in the mortar joints if you crouched: that oil-colored film that trembles and spreads a finger's width and stops, as if testing the idea of being alive, then giving up. It has not given up. It is tasting. It is a spore-skin, a cast-off palm-sweat from a Mother below, learning to hold together in the seam-water. Most dissolve back into damp. A few thicken, bead, grow a lip.

If a Slick survives long enough in a cool seep, steam pressure lifts the floor above it into a blister. A great low dome, fifty paces across, hollow and drum-soft, rocking under you if you climb it. Under the thin crust you can hear water moving, and something else moving, slow as paste. Inside, in the dark hot hollow, the Slick grows hands — first buds, then fingers — and begins to practice. Fingernail-sized bricks, perfectly laid. Row on row. A whole tiny district, an apprenticeship. They are pale as unbaked clay, eyeless, fringed at the neck with salt-white gills that crunch and soften. They do not need light. They need touch. They lay and relay their miniature city until the dome can no longer hold them, until their hands are full-size and leave full-size prints.

Then they drop. Down through the softening layers to join a Mother, or to become a new work-square themselves, sinking until they find their own level. That deep *thud-whump* you hear, swallowed by steam — a scaffold giving up, a kiln wall slumping — sometimes that is not collapse at all. That is a settling. A young one finding its depth.

The kilns? The beehive kilns squatting mud-plastered to the horizon, mouth black and breathing white? Those are not theirs. Or not anymore. They were an attempt, long ago, by something else with hands — something that wanted to fire the bricks right, to make them hard, to finish them. You can feel how wrong that idea is here. The kilns are abandoned stomachs. The Mothers use them now as vents, as blowholes, to exhale. That is why the air glows faintly from below near them, why shadows fall toward the heat-glow. They breathe out, the sky — which is only exhalation, hot water condensing — breathes in, rains ten feet and stops mid-air as steam again. The ground hisses for acres.

And the paired bricks — you will find them if you look, two bricks with the exact same fingerprint, warm to exactly the same degree no matter how far apart they are. Put your ear to one and you hear faint patting in the other. Those are a Mother remembering herself across distance. A left hand and a right hand that belong to the same body miles wide. Touch one, and the other knows. The floor is laced with these entanglements. The whole continent is a nervous system made of touch-memory.

So do not follow the patting sounds. Not because you will be harmed, but because you will be incorporated. Walk toward them and they recede because the back sinks slightly ahead of you, water welling up to cover it in a mirror that reflects the dim amber. Your weight is read, your level is corrected. The floor wants you at the same height as everything else. Wade long enough and you will feel your own feet go soft and thoughtful, wanting to spread, wanting to press. Your sweat does not evaporate. It joins the general damp. Your breath joins the weeping. That is how they invite.

They are not cruel. They are not kind. They are unfinished, and they maintain unfinishedness the way a reef maintains calcium. Primitive mud. Infinite hands. Hot water under everything.

At night, when the cracks vent brighter and the planet shows its tiling, climb a blister — gently, do not break it — and lie flat. Feel it rock. Put your palm down. Hold it there until the brick under it is exactly as warm as your skin.

You will feel, after a while, an answering pressure from below. Millimeter by millimeter. A small, wet, infinitely patient hand pressing back, learning the shape of you, so that tomorrow it can lay a brick that remembers you ever stood here at all.
