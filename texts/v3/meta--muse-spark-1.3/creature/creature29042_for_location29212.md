They live close together by not living together.

That is the first thing you learn on your knees in there. At first you think *crowded* — the walls shimmer if you unfocus, legs like eyelashes lifting and settling — and then you think *empty*, because there are acres of clean wet stone between every body. It is not a swarm. It is a parish. Everyone has a station, and keeps it.

The landlords are the crickets.

Pale, almost translucent, the color of old fingernails. They do not chirp — there is nothing to chirp for down here, and the rock would eat it anyway. They hold. Each one has chosen a patch of basalt about as wide as your hand and stands in the middle of it with its antennae out, tasting the cold sink at your knees and the warm cap above your head. Dozens of them. Spaced as if someone walked around with a ruler when the stone was still soft.

They build. That is why you see them first. Out of the bright ellipse, where the floor is dim and damp, stand their turrets — three that are finished, thumb-high, perfectly round, and two more that are beginnings, low rings. Pellet by pellet of rock flour chewed and spat and patted. They back into them when a centipede passes. They are not houses so much as throats. A burrow with a lip to keep the three-minute rain from drowning them. If you stay very still you will see one add a pellet to the rim, turn, tamp it with its head, and step back to admire. Careful masons in a stone that still takes fingerprints.

Under them, in the scale beneath, live the salt.

Blind white springtails, in the fist of moss and along the drip-lines. You would miss them until your eye adjusts to the glitter — then the moss seems to pour, very slowly, without moving. They are the cleaners and the eaten. They eat spore and scale and skin — moth dust, your skin cells that fell in and will stay until the rain — and in turn they feed almost everything else. And they have discovered the membrane.

About a foot above the heavy light, where the cold pool at knee-height meets the warm shaft falling from above, the air skins over. You cannot see it. You can feel it if you put your hand in and lift slowly: a faint resistance, like pushing through warm water into cold. The springtails walk there upside-down, in a straight line, legs on nothing. An inverted highway across the light. They cross mid-air from moss to ledge to turret-top, dozens at a time, unhurried, while below them the frogs bask. No one looks up. It is simply a road that exists because the air here has learned to have a floor.

The frogs are the monks.

Two of them. Small, the exact grey of wet concrete, throats going. Each sits in his own rain-cup — a hollow in a fallen block that holds sky-colored water for an hour after the drip or the storm. They do not sit together. They do not look at each other. They sit and pulse and wait for the cups to fill with drowned springtails and midges that fell down the chimney thinking it was somewhere else.

At noon they move. That is the truce. Slowly, with old-man legs, they edge out of their cups toward the rim of the ellipse, to where the light is thick and warm and heavy on the skin. The salamander comes too.

She is the oldest thing in a new place. Pink-gilled, the color of inside your eyelid, curled all day in the dampest vertical crack where condensation runs constantly. You think she is a frill of stone until she uncurls. She does not hunt in there — there is not room. She waits for hunting to come to her, and at noon she too leaves her crack to lay her flank in the heavy light. Cold-blooded people need the sunbeam to run their blood. The beetles — black seeds, hard-backed, clicking silently over the quartz spiral — edge in as well. For ten, fifteen minutes they share the rim, spaced still, but closer than at any other hour. Frogs and salamander and beetles shoulder to shoulder around a washtub of sun, not touching. Basking. Then, as the ellipse thins and slides off, they withdraw to their stations. No one is taken at the light. That is the rule older than any of them.

The hunters keep it by breaking it elsewhere.

Centipedes, rust-red, long as your little finger, moving through the cricket territories like thoughts. You will not hear the kill. Three inches from your ear a centipede takes a cricket by the back leg and there is no rustle, no squeak. The moss, perhaps. The newborn stone, still porous, still soft enough to fill back in after a thumbprint, drinks vibration. That is why the place is silent despite being full. The moths lift and settle without a whisper, like pages turning behind glass. Only the water ticks — bead growing, trembling, falling, felt in the teeth — and your own breath too loud, dimming the brightness if you let it get ragged. Sound and light are on opposite pans here. The residents know to keep quiet.

The moths are the snow.

A drift of them on the dark side, away from the shaft, tissue-paper wings folded, eyeless. They should not be here. They fell in as dusty things from the moor and stayed because falling in means staying until the rain. They do not feed — there is nothing for a moor moth in a pantry cave. They live off themselves, getting paler, thinner, dusting the air with scales that catch the shaft and glitter as they fall. In dying they build the place. Their scales go into the mouse chapel, into the cricket spit-mortar, into the bellies of springtails. When the three-minute rain comes — that sudden vertical pouring that hisses on warm stone — it washes their dust down into the cups and resets the floor. Erases footprints. Cleans the lenses. Then they begin dusting again.

The green people are barely holding on.

Four ferns on the ledge where dust has gathered, bleached translucent green-white from trying to live on a sunbeam that moves and vanishes. They lean — wrong. Everything's shadow here leans *toward* the shaft, not away, as if the light pulls. The ferns lean that way too, yearning in. A cushion of moss no bigger than your fist, so bright it vibrates against the black basalt, holding a whole city of springtails in its branches. A thread of liverwort along a drip-line, flat and dark and patient. Great distances between them, for this scale. Long stretches of bare sweating stone. They are not a garden. They are four castaways sharing a raft of dust, making oxygen for a room that barely breathes.

And then the absent ones, who still live there as buildings.

The mouse is gone. Last season, by the smell. But its dome remains near the moss, woven from rootlets washed down the chimney and moth-wing scales felted together, tight as a chapel, with a round door you could put your thumb through. No one uses it. The crickets walk around it. The beetles sometimes shelter under its eave during the rain. It holds.

Along the ledge, the spiral. White quartz pebbles, one by one, decreasing in size, laid in a perfect coil by someone you never see — a beetle? one of the frogs with its blunt nose? No purpose you can name. Not a trap. Not a nest. It does not wash away in the storm because the ledge is just above the splash. It sits there and gathers condensation on its white faces and ticks faintly as pebbles contract in the cold. An art, or a memory, or a marker for the membrane road. Whoever made it still tends it: one pebble is always damp, recently moved.

Faint marks everywhere else on the soft stone if you soften your eyes: claw-script, drag-lines, old egg-cases like tiny paper lanterns glued under overhangs. Scattered architecture made and left by bodies, not hands.

If you stay an hour on your knees and keep the truce — quiet, out of the center unless invited by noon, willing to be heavy-warmed and then cold again — the place stops pretending to be normal and shows you what actually lives there underneath the census.

Not the crickets. Not the frogs. The interval between them.

A young, still-making-itself room that has learned how to keep too many mouths alive in a pantry by spacing them, silencing them, and sharing its sun. A whole active world the size of a held breath, ticking, sweating, hunting without sound, building mud towers in blinding light.

And the light itself, which has lived there so long pooled in that ellipse that it has grown heavy and worn thin. Put your hand in and you feel it resist like water. Click your tongue and you hear the answer a fraction *before* you click, the room anticipating to keep its silence intact. Step fully in at noon and look up and for half a second you do not see moor-sky but a deeper blue, a different hour, and your own head silhouetted looking down at you from above, before you climbed in.

That is the true resident. Not passing through like you. Living there. The pooled light that learned to have weight, to have a floor, to have a memory — and the cold that holds it, and the soft stone that records everything and then lets the rain erase it clean.
