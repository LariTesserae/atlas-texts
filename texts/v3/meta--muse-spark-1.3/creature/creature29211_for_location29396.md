They are not what you first hear.

You hear the mill and the boiler and the drip and the insect-choir and you think, *workers.* You picture men in aprons. But when you come down the sagging stair-roots past the first amber lamp, snowed with moth-scale, there is nobody there. Just traffic.

That is the first lesson of who lives in the Sump: the town looks empty because you are looking for the wrong size of citizen.

The air between the ledges is inhabited, densely, and it never lands. The moth clouds, hand-big, orbit each lamp in a slow, shedding vortex. Into them cut the shift-workers — swifts by dim day, bats by dimmer night — a braid that never unties, only changes hands at the green dusk. Below them, on every catwalk and cable and sweating pipe, the ground crew runs: rats single-file along the copper veins, nose-to-tail like commuters who know the timetable. A river of ants ten ants wide carries leaf-bits and paint-chips and dead moth wings up one root and down another and no one, no one steps on it. You learn to step over without thinking, the way you learn not to stand in a doorway. A troop of soot-black monkeys owns the long rope bridge between the lodgings and the press-mill; they cross it bouncing, bickering, stealing washers and leaving fig seeds in return. Coatis snout through the warm oil-cake behind the seed-press shoulder to shoulder with the dogs — lean, half-naked dogs gone thin-haired from the heat, who keep barking at the section of rim where the waterfall was ten minutes ago, as if scolding a ghost.

Leave a peel on a step and it is gone in twenty minutes, carried off in pieces. Nothing rots here. That is not poetry. That is the cleaning staff.

And then, inside all that motion, you start to pick out the humans. Slowly. The way you pick out a face in bark.

There are not many of them. A hundred and fifty, maybe two hundred souls across two dozen ledges, each family marooned on its own root with black air between. You don’t see them because they are the same color and temperature as everything else: shirt-sticking warm, perpetually beaded. No one in the Sump is ever fully dry. Skin stays pruned at the fingertips, slick at the neck. Hair hangs lank and is braided back with living vine to keep it out of the flywheels, and it is always dusted white with moth-scale like dandruff, like snow. They strip to the waist, men and women both, in oilcloth aprons gone soft as skin, feet bare and splayed wide from a lifetime gripping slick root. Toes that can curl over a pipe. Cork wraps for going up after a rim-rain, when the roots turn to streams.

Their eyes give them away. Pupils blown wide from living under the leaf-roof where noon is green dusk. When a rim-runner has to go top, into the dry scrub and the white light, he wears smoked glass and squints and bleeds from the nose in the dryness. The Sump-born hate the up. The light hurts. The air starves them.

Ears, though. Ears they keep uncovered. Big, waxy, always slightly cocked. You navigate here by sound — the mill means center, the drip-wall means west, the cicada swell means the canopy is about to shed water — and plugging your ears after a rain is how you walk off a lit walk into true dark. So no one plugs. Mothers slap children’s hands away from their ears. Babies in the wasp-nest lodgings don’t cry so much as listen, head turning toward the nine-second sigh of the boiler like it’s a lullaby. The old who go deaf go vulnerable in a way blindness up top would be. They get led by the wrist by grandchildren holding a length of copper wire for them to feel the transformer hum through.

Voices. Gods, voices. Everyone is hoarse. You have to lean in to talk and shout to be heard over the stack — low thump and drone, mid patter of ten thousand drips, high tearing chitter — so conversation is a physical act: a hand on a forearm, foreheads almost touching, mouth to ear. The mill crew can tell a true steam whistle from a pigeon imitation by a half-warble at the end. The pigeons here learned the whistle perfectly and love setting the whole press running for a false venting. Apprentices always fall for it. Old hands just tilt their heads and say, *bird.* Children learn pigeon before they learn whispering.

Work never stops because the press never stops. The squat bark-brick factory on the third terrace turns its cartwheel flywheels on steam and on root-pressure, tapped and valved like an artery, and someone always has to watch the valves. So they keep three watches named for the swarm, not the clock: Day Swarm, Change, Night Swarm. You sleep when you can, in hammocks slung from living beams, because the buildings breathe.

You can see it if you stand still — the lodging wall flexing a half-inch in and out when the boilers vent. If you fought it with rigid bunks and square frames you’d crack. So they hang and sway. Sleep is shallow, tuned. At 3 a.m. the Sump doesn’t quiet, it just changes key, and they roll with it.

Every ledge has its trade, and the trades have grown into the bodies that do them.

Stokers live by the boiler-house, beetle-men. The fist-sized glossy beetles cluster on the warm iron walls to roost and hum, and every morning the stokers shovel them off still humming, keeping time to the hum with their shovels. Their forearms are stippled with tiny burns, their sweat smells faintly sweet, like the chimney smoke that gets trapped under the leaves and sinks back down. They feed the furnace with the blue-burning guano shoveled from the piston housings where the bats roost for warmth — bats that eat only the figs that ripen in the hot shadow behind the chimney, bats that smell faintly of machine oil.

Pressmen tend the squeeze, hands stained dark to the elbow with nut-oil and leaf-pulp, nails green underneath that never scrubs out. Their cough brings up glitter: moth-scale lodged deep. They call it scale-lung and compare colors like miners.

Lamp-tenders are the closest thing to priests. Every building keeps an amber lamp burning outside it or you lose the path in the gloom, and someone has to wipe the scales and the condensation off the glass, trim the wick, keep the moths from smothering it entirely. After a rim-rain, when the white waterfall has poured over for ten minutes and the whole bowl steams, they walk the catwalks with long poles, relighting, calling out. Stay on the lit walk, the rule goes, and it is their light you stay on.

Cistern-keepers work lowest, where the oldest root has cupped into a black pool of drip-water and oil-sheen. They tend the bathtubs and pitcher-plant vats as big as barrels, ladling mosquito larvae to the chickens that somehow survive here, netting blind white fish that boil underneath. They drink the fermented drip-distillate — sharp, metallic, faintly green — and their teeth go brown from it. They know the taste of west wall versus east wall water.

And the runners. Children mostly, and wiry aunts. Because the structures are set far apart with drops between, connected only by chain-and-plank and rope and sagging copper cable, someone has to carry oil and paper and news and tools across the bounce. They run when the swarms pass and the bridges buck. They know not to shout across — sound lies in the bowl, echoes stack — but to go themselves, hand to hand. Their calves are corded, their feet are the surest. They play in the ten-minute waterfall, sliding the stair-roots when they turn to streams, screaming with the swallows pouring over the rim like water over a dam.

They eat together behind the mill, humans and monkeys and coatis at the oil-cake pile, picking quickly, no leftovers. Nut oil, leaf bread, fish stew, chimney figs. No one leaves food. No one leaves waste. No one leaves the dead alone.

That last one matters. Up top you bury. Here, if you leave a body for an hour unattended, the cleanup crew finds it. So they sit vigil — the Eaters’ Vigil, they joke, but it isn’t a joke. Someone stays with the corpse, talking loudly over the noise so the listening continues, until the kin are ready to lower it to the cistern ledge or the compost vaults under the boiler where heat does the work fast. Bones come back clean in days and are set into root-cracks to fill them, the way vines fill everything else. The chapel with its bell gone to seed doesn’t do funerals anymore. The bell went to figs years ago, split and sprouting, full of pigeon nests. People still gather there when the canopy sheds, because it’s high and relatively dry, and they listen together. The liturgy, if you can call it that, is telling where each sound is.

Plant and engine and animal have been pressed together so long in this small, dim, wet heat that they’ve started to use each other as organs, and the humans are no exception. The vines have learned to follow the current, stolen charge making certain tendrils glow faintly green at night. The electricians — bark-smiths who splice copper veins shining through split bark — test with their tongues, and the children use those glowing lines as nightlights to find the latrine path. The lodging walls flex, and the people who sleep in them have learned to breathe with the venting in their dreams.

Stand at the bottom and look up: terraces of light receding, lamp after lamp spiraling through green gloom, bridges swaying as a troop crosses, slow snow turning in chimney heat. Someone shouts from the lip and someone answers from the far terraces. The sound stacks until you can’t separate boiler from insect from voice.

That’s who lives here. Not settlers in a forest. Not workers in a factory. A gut flora. Hot, loud, damp, listening, fruiting oil and paper like the factory-fruit it tends, feeding the swarm that feeds them back, worn round-shouldered to fit the bowl that holds them.
