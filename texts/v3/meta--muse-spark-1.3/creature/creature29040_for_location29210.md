At first you think nothing lives here.

That is what the noise does. It covers. The fans and the pumps and the transformer hum lay down so even and so loud that for a few seconds you read the room as empty — just iron and rock and amber light. No one could live in a held chord like that.

Then your eyes adjust to the salt on the bulbs and the movement separates out of the haze.

It is not one thing moving. It is the room itself, furred.

Start with the smallest, because they are the most numerous and the easiest to mistake for part of the machine. The housings of the piston pumps — those big, body-warm blocks that go *whump whump whump* without lifting anything — are not pale metal. They are pale with crickets.

Furnace-crickets. Thousands of them, packed flank to flank in every seam where heat leaks out. They have no eyes because there is nothing to see here that heat and vibration don't tell better. They are the color of old lard and salt, translucent at the joints, with antennae twice as long as their bodies that never stop combing the air for dust. They eat dust. They eat the orange scab of rust-lichen off the floor plates. They eat each other's shed legs and the wings the moths drop and each other, whole, when one stops singing. And they sing without pause. Not in bursts. Not in night and day. A continuous high metallic sawing, leg on wing, that rides on top of the pump-thump and never drops out. If you put your hand near a housing they go quiet for a half-second in a wave, then come back louder, and one will step onto your wrist because you are warm too, and warmth is ground to them.

Above them, the snow that falls up.

Lamp-moths. Clouds of them around every caged sodium lamp, so thick the bulb looks like it's boiling. Their wings are ash-gray and dry as paper — when they hit your face they don't flutter, they tick. They don't eat, not really. They hatched to orbit. Behind the pipes, if you stand still and let the big noise recede, you can hear the faint, separate tick-tick-tick of their egg-cases splitting open in the salt-fur, thousands of paper purses letting go at once. That is the replacement rate. Because they are always burning. Every minute a few touch too close to the crusted glass and crisp and fall, wings still beating for a second on the hot housing below. It doesn't matter. The ticking behind the pipes is keeping pace. The same cloud, forever. The same moth hitting the same bulb.

Below them, on the floor, the polishers.

Copper beetles, backs domed and hard as rivets, copper-green gone brown with oil-dust. They own the conveyor — that long rattling belt that carries fine white dust from one identical pile to the other, forever. They march up the side, get carried shuddering along the top, fall off the end into the dust pile, lie on their backs kicking, right themselves with a click you can feel in your teeth, and climb back on. They are not trying to go anywhere. They are eating. They eat rat droppings, dry pellets that crumble to salt. They eat dead crickets that fall from above. They eat the oil-film off the belt itself, tongues working the rubber until it shines bright where they walk — polished, but never thinner. Their shells, when they shed or die, are eaten in turn by the crickets. Nothing is wasted because there is nowhere for waste to go.

And in the warmest places, the mammals.

Rats. Naked-tailed, pink-nosed, eyes sealed shut under skin — blind as thumbs, because eyes would only dry out here. They live in the cable trays, where the black cables sag under their own weight and hum with current. They sleep there in piles, ten and fifteen together, breathing slow, pink sides heaving in the hot air, borrowing heat from the wires and giving it back. When they wake they move by whisker and by heat, noses lifting to find you by the salt drying on your upper lip. They gnaw insulation — not for food, but to keep their teeth filed — and then lick the exposed copper clean, and then lick salt-whiskers off the lee side of the pipes. White starbursts, brittle as glass, that shatter sweet on their tongues. They eat crickets, snatching them off pump housings with a quick, practiced bite. They leave the antennae. The beetles take the rest.

Around all of them, holding the edges, the things that learned how to be dry and green at the same time.

In every bolt-hole and weld seam, tufts of wireweed: not leaf, but black copper-stiff filaments that tremble constantly in the fan-wash, rootless, drinking nothing but vibration and dust. On the pipes, those salt-whiskers. On the floor plates, rust-lichen spreading in orange scabs, slow and crustose, eating the microscopic rain of chitin-dust and shed wings and powdered rat dung that never settles because the two big exhaust fans — each taller than a man, turning in opposite walls — lift it exactly as fast as it falls. The lichen eats the dust of them all, and the crickets eat the lichen.

That is who lives here. Not four species. One mouth.

A closed mouth eating itself, warm and dry and perfect. No one drinks. Oil is the only liquid and it stays inside its lines, black and endless. Sweat dries to salt on your lip before it can run. Births are dry — ticking egg-cases, splitting seed-tufts. Deaths are dry — a crisp on a bulb, a shell on a belt. The animals tend the machines because the machines are their climate: they clean contacts with their tongues, they nest in warm relays and keep them warm with their bodies, they keep the dust aloft by moving through it, they keep the current flowing by needing it.

You cannot move without brushing life. And after a minute in the chord you stop trying to separate them — cricket from pump, moth from lamp, rat from cable, lichen from iron, beetle from belt. The shadows pooling under the motors vibrate faintly, out of sync with anything, and you realize that vibration is feet. Thousands of feet.

They will still be here. That is the rule that holds them together more than warmth or salt. If you come back in fifty years, with dust still in your lungs and ringing still in your ears, the same warm air will be making the same loop around this small, dense, buried room, and the same cricket will be singing on the same pump housing, keeping time for a factory with no product that learned how to keep going without ever going anywhere — by growing a workforce that never clocks out.
