At first you think nothing lives here.

That is the first impression the room gives, and it is almost true. You sit on the warm block in the center and turn, rim to rim, and you count only stone and space. The grass does not move. The water does not move. No wing goes over. No back goes under. If there were eyes, you would have seen them blink by now.

Then you stay longer than a glance takes, and the place un-conceals, very slowly, not by movement but by staying.

What lives here lives the way the stone does: low, and wide, and in place.

The oldest and most numerous are the ones you mistook for color on the rock.

Lichen. Sage-gray and sulfur-yellow and bone-white, laid flat on every stable surface like paint that grew. Up close they are not flat at all but rimmed and cupped and dusted, pale maps with darker borders where one individual meets another and stops, by ancient agreement. They have eaten their foothold into the limestone with weak acid, pitted it into honeycomb under themselves so they cannot be lifted off without taking stone with them. They do not hurry. A millimeter a decade, the same rate as the wall settling on the north arc. They drink what you breathed in when you arrived — wet stone, evening spatter, morning condensation — and nothing else. They can go black-dry for weeks on the high spots, dust over them, cracked earth around them, and then soften and green faintly for an hour after rain before going still again. They are not in a hurry to die, either. Some of the rosettes on the fallen blocks are older than the tumble of the top stones from the wall. They will outlast the groove worn in the slab.

Between them, in the damp seams where the description thins into shadow, lives the felt.

Moss, black-green, saturated only on its underside. It does not rise, it mats — a low sponge pressed into cracks, into the lee of slabs, into the clay-dark line at the bottom of the smoothed troughs. If you lift a loose stone, which you should not, you would find it palest underneath, almost yellow, damp with the night's breath that never quite evaporated. With it, in only the dampest of those seams, the ferns. No taller than your hand, and most shorter. They do not unfurl here as ferns do elsewhere. They stay curled inward, fronds rolled to save water, waxy to the touch, dark-veined. Each one holds its own pocket of still air around it. They are not lush. They are exact. A single fern may own a crack two feet long and share it with no one else.

And then, spaced as if placed by a hand that hates crowding, the larger ones.

The bunchgrass. Coarse, stooling up from a tight crown, blades bleached white at the tips where the sun-smear has burned them, green only deep in the sheaf where shadow remains. Its roots go out shallow and far, ten times wider than the crown, drinking the brief warm spatter before it leaves the sand. Around each clump is a bare circle — clay and grit with no seedlings. Not accident. It has poisoned or starved or simply shaded out its own children to keep its water. When the faint air comes over the rim, it is this grass that brushes, low, dry, a sound you have to lean to hear. Nothing has chewed it. The tips are unbroken, frayed only by weather.

A few paces off, the shrub. Low, wind-pruned even though there is almost no wind, as if it remembers wind from elsewhere. Twigs gray and hard as wire, leaves small, thick, folded, dark olive on top and pale felt beneath. It keeps its leaves for years. It does not drop them to make a litter; there is no litter here. If you look under it you will find its own shadow and clean earth and perhaps a single curled dead leaf that has not rotted. It flowers — it must — but sparely, and without audience. No insects stitch the air to carry its pollen. It trusts the air itself, the rare stirring over the rim, or it trusts itself.

That is the rule for all of them: they live unvisited.

No mouth has taken a crescent from a leaf. No burrow has lifted the crust. No dropping has manured a tuft. In the clay at the bottom, where your own boot left the only track, there are no smaller prints filling slowly with seep beside it. In the palm-deep tea-colored pools in the slabs, no larva hangs, no skater dents the meniscus. The rings you see are from stone-drip, *tok*, and then wind. The water holds only itself and light and a faint skin of dust, and, invisibly, the slow brown leach of stone and leaf. Even in the trough hacked from a single block, where fallen leaves lie half-preserved in dark water, rot itself is hesitant, incomplete — a bacterial patience that never finishes the job, because it, too, lives on condensation and waiting.

So they live alone together, each in its circle of empty ground.

They do not compete by height. They compete by endurance. Who can stay greenest longest on a palm of water. Who can hold a crack through the dry weeks. Who can photosynthesize in unbleached-linen light, late-afternoon even at noon, without burning.

If there is a citizen of this room, it is not the grass or the shrub. It is the lichen-map, because it wants least. Stone, air held still, a smear of sun behind cloud, a spatter in the evening. That is enough for a century.

Sit long enough and you begin to count them not as plants but as presences. Ten grasses. Four shrubs. Countless lichens if you count each rosette as one, which you should. A dozen fern-cracks. Three moss-felts large as your hand and many small as a thumb. Each one known to itself, each one holding its measure of moisture like breath held without effort, each one exactly where dampness as an accent allows it to be.

Nothing is about to happen to them, and nothing needs to.
