At first you think nothing lives here.

That is the greenness tricking you. So much plant, so perfectly held, you assume animal would be noise, would be tear, would be rot — and there is no tear and no rot. The floor is unbroken. The drip falls in the same place. No husk, no shell, no bone, no fallen feather. If anything lived it would have left something dead behind, and there is nothing dead.

Then you see the footprint.

Not a print, exactly. A welling. You lift your own boot and the mat sighs back up, water beading brown-green around your fingers, and a minute later a shallow to your left fills on its own though you did not step there. A wide, splayed pressure, two-lobed at the heel, that holds water a second longer than the ground around it before the floor redistributes it and forgets it. Something just heavy enough to matter went by. Soft. Bare.

You wait, and that is how you learn to see them. Not by looking for movement — nothing here hurries enough to catch an eye tuned to monkeys and birds — but by waiting for a patch of floor to decide to stand up.

They are floor-colored. That is the first thing, and you have to unlearn it to see the rest.

Low. Broad. Child-high when they straighten, which is seldom. Most often they move in a crouch, knuckles brushing the moss-hair, belly almost touching the mat, weight spread. Their feet are the giveaway: huge, naked, toe-less-looking, the sole a thick pad of living sponge that spreads under load the way the plaza-leaf spreads rain. It does not dent the mat so much as persuade it. Where you sink and well, they settle and are held.

At first you think they are amphibians. Then you think they are people stunted by wet and light. Then you stop trying to decide, because here, as with forest and city, those are the same thing.

Skin: damp always, beaded always, green-black at the back and shoulders where a fine moss-hair has taken hold — not worn, grown, symbiotic, pale new-green at the seams behind the ears, at the elbows, between fingers. Underneath, the true skin is olive-grey and translucent at the throat and inner wrists; you can see the pulse very slow, and the water moving under it. They weep, as everything weeps. Condensate gathers on their brows, on their forearms, runs down and drips off their fingertips in rhythm with the leaf-tips. They do not wipe it. To be dry would be to be separate.

Face: wide, lipless almost, eyes very large and gold-green, lidless in the way that suggests they never needed to blink against dust or dark. The light never changes, so they never squint. Pupils fixed at mid-morning width. Noses flattened to slits — so much water in the air, smell is taste, taste is bath. Mouths small, round, suctorial. No teeth that you can see. Nothing here bites, because biting would make a wound, and a wound would be raw edge, and raw edge would have to heal or rot and neither is permitted to finish.

Hands: three thick fingers and a press-pad thumb, all soft. Nails have become blunt keratinous plates for parting moss-hair without tearing it, for coaxing, for pressing. If you watch one work — and work is too hard a word, tend is better — you see what the cup-leaf sentence meant.

A young bowl-leaf, waist-high, still curled. A Tender lies down beside it in the wet. Sleeps. Breathes. Hour after hour the warm CO2 of its breath, the damp of its weeping body, the slight hollow of its own heat tell the leaf: *here, deeper*. Over what you call days, though the shadow never moves, the leaf cups deeper. No carving. No forcing. Just company, correctly placed. That is their technology. Presence, prolonged.

There are few of them. Perhaps nine. Perhaps eleven. It is hard to count because they do not gather, and because when one lies still long enough the moss-hair closes over its back and it becomes, for a while, floor again. They space themselves as the Great Ones space themselves — far apart, each in the catchment of one trunk, one plaza-leaf, one marsh-basin. Total life, but not crowded. The rule holds for animal as for tree: be enormous in effect, and few, and make even emptiness out of yourself.

They do not speak. In the constant percussion — plink on taut drum, gurgle inside wood, whale-creak of shifting crowns — a voice would be lost. They click. A soft palatal click, exactly like the tiny black frogs in the leaf-basins, whom they tend and who tend them. Two clicks: *move your weight left*. Three clicks, spaced as drips: *this gutter is clogging*. A long exhale through the round mouth when one of the house-sized flowers vents — they gather then, unhurried, three or four smudges converging over half a kilometer of open air, to stand in the warm perfumed mist and open their mouths and throats and skin to it. That is a meal. That, and licking.

They lick salt and algae from bark as the gliders do, long slow strokes with wide tongues, leaving clean pale swaths that darken again in hours. They sip hydathode beads from leaf edges without detaching them. They press their foreheads to the fluted buttresses and drink the sheet-water as it falls. They do not pick, do not pluck, do not kill. How could they eat dead when there is no dead material? They live on exudates — what the plant freely weeps, sweats, vents, overflows. Sap like maple and blood welled from a purposeful pore. Nectar-mist. Algal film. In return they give back what plants crave in a closed valley that drinks its own rain: concentrated breath, warmth, movement, salt from their own sweat, redistribution of weight and water. Press the mat and water wells up. They know exactly where to press to coax marsh-water toward a thirsty runner, where to lie to dam a root-gutter for an hour without stopping it. The floor knows their weight and they know the floor's knowing. Messages travel as pressure. A Tender stamping softly twice on a bridge-root can be felt half a valley away as a sigh in the thatch. That is their town meeting. No one needs to assemble on the plaza-leaf, though sometimes, for the pleasure of being briefly many in one place, they do — four or five damp backs gleaming in the unchanging gold-green, saying nothing, listening to water fall inside wood.

And inside the wood — that slow in-out you feel when you press your ear to bark at what would be night — that is the other who lives here, the one the Tenders tend but never see.

Call it the Hollow One. It is large. Each Great One holds one. Not a parasite; an organ that learned to be an animal. A huge, slug-pale, lung-heart thing that lives in the hollow heart of the trunk where rivers fall inside wood, pumping, breathing, squeezing water hundreds of meters up to leaf-roofs. You hear its pop and boom in the xylem wide as your torso. The Tenders press their cheeks to the bark and click to it, and it slows or quickens almost imperceptibly. When a glider lands with a soft splat to lick bark, it is licking the sweat of that breathing. When a flower vents, it is the Hollow One exhaling through the flower's valve.

The gliders themselves — those huge slow translucent sails, frog-skinned, finger-winged — are the Tenders' cousins, or their livestock, or their angels. They do not flap so much as fall with intention, half a kilometer at a time, from crown to far trunk, carrying on their bellies pollen, spores, slime, the salt-map of the valley from one catchment to another. The Tenders watch them without watching, the way you watch your own breath.

Birth and death, here, have been solved the way building has been solved: by refusing the distinction.

No Tender is born. At the base of each Great One, where buttress meets mat in a warm dark cleft that smells of green tea, there is a thickening. Over long, uncounted drips it swells, pales, puts out limb-buds that are at first indistinguishable from young aerial roots. Then one day — though there are no days — it detaches by thinning, not by cutting, and uncurls and is floor-colored and stands and clicks for the first time. No mother. No child. An outgrowth that learned to walk.

And none dies. When a Tender grows thin, slow, waterlogged — when its moss-hair thickens to thatch and its sponge-feet root too deeply to pull free — it does not fall. It lies down in a low spot between root-ridges. The mat rises a little to meet it. Over long drips the seams close. Back becomes floor, fiber becomes floor again. You can still see, for a while, the faint outline of shoulders, the gold-green glint of eyes that no longer open, before the new-green covers them. Nothing is lost, because nothing ever left the cycle. Water does not leave. Body does not leave.

That is why they do not wait for change. The seedling at the raw pink edge will not be a tree tomorrow. The drip will fall in the same place when you return. The sun will stay at mid-morning. They know this in their bones in a way you feel after only hours: to live here is not to progress, but to maintain the pause perfectly. To keep growth equal to decay to the exact leaf. To be the attention of the valley, its mobile hands, its brief upright thickenings that walk around making sure the held breath is held evenly.

Stand still long enough, warmer and wetter and louder inside your own head, and one will come close to inspect you — not with curiosity, which implies future, but with tending. It will part the moss-hair on your boot. It will press its wide forehead briefly to your calf to feel your weight and where the floor should take it. It will click once, softly, whether to you or to the frogs or to the Hollow One breathing inside the nearest mountain of wood, you cannot tell. Then it will lie down beside a young leaf, and breathe on it, and wait with a patience that is not patience because it expects nothing to happen, only everything to continue.

You are standing on a living thing. It is standing on you, in a way. That is who lives here.
