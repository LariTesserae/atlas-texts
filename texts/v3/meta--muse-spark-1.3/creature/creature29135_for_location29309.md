At first you think nothing lives here.

That's the altitude talking, and the noise. Your own lungs are working too hard to imagine anyone else's working at all. Three steps on the duckboard and your heart is up in your throat, your shirt is already sticking to you, and the roar — pump-throb under water-roar under steam-hiss under electric whine — fills your head so completely there seems no room left for another life inside it. Too high, too small, too loud, too closed. A lung with no body.

Then you stand still long enough to breathe twice, and breathe again, and you realize the green is not background. It is residence.

The first to live here, the ones who said yes immediately, are almost not alive in the way you mean. In the warm pools — those shivering skins of 30, 35-degree water that smell faintly of struck matches and old nails — there is a slick, a fringe. Rust-red and mustard-yellow and oil-slick green where the seep comes up hottest. Iron-eaters and sulfur-eaters, bacterial mats, algal felt. They don't care about thin air. They breathe rock-heat. They lay down in sheets over black peat and over concrete footing alike, and they make the water edge iridescent. If you crouch — slowly, because you have to move slowly here or black out — you see the bubbles coming up through them are not just heat. They are being breathed out.

On top of them, and made by them over patient time, the peat itself. Black, saturated, quaking. It is alive the way a sponge is alive. Ten mosses deep: sphagnum rust-red and lime-green holding twenty times its weight in condensed sky, liverworts lacquered dark as if varnished, all stitched together so you cannot lift one without lifting all. The peat holds the water because the lava lip holds the peat. Nothing drains. It evaporates off the warm skin, condenses on the cliff-cold, rains back as seep. So the mosses never have to be afraid. They just drink the air.

And then, out of that wet mattress, the structure.

Not tall — the wind and the thinness forbid tall — but dense to the point of touch. Ferns chest-high in every crack, unfurling new fiddles every day, their black roots as much in air as in soil, drinking the suspended beads. You hear them dripping even when it hasn't rained in a geologic age. Behind them, bent over like old men, the dwarf trees — polylepis cousins, bark peeling in red paper strips, no higher than your shoulder — entirely furred. That is the only word. Furred in epiphytes. Thumbnail orchids, white and wax-orange, opening over weeks, not days. Bromeliads wedged in forks holding tiny cups of rainwater two thousand meters above any pond. A fallen trunk is not dead here. It becomes a street. A live cable becomes a trellis. Everything grows on everything else because there is nowhere else to grow. The boundaries are always visible: black wall, white sky, white cloud-bottom. So life stacks vertically in centimeters.

This is the city, and most of the citizens never move.

The movers are small, and you have to tune your eyes down past the machinery to see them.

Over the hottest pool hangs a cloud that is not steam. Tiny white midges, a dozen, a hundred, holding station in the updraft, never leaving the column of warmth. They live fast and die fast — the opposite bargain of everything else here. Their larvae are down in the warm muck, red with extra hemoglobin because warm water holds less oxygen and thin air holds less to begin with, wriggling in the bacterial slick, eating it. The adults rise, mate in the hum, lay, die on the surface and become peat. They have found the one gap in the cold.

On a cold pipe, in the shade of lagging where condensation runs constantly, a stonefly sits. Gray, long-antennaed, absurd. Its ancestors are cold-water, fast-stream insects. This one has learned to live on a steel stream, where the water never freezes because the machine never stops. It flattens itself to the vibration and lets the tremor pass through it.

Under the machinery, if you shut your mouth and listen *under* — not over — the roar, you hear them: plink. ... plink. Singly in the sedges.

Frogs. Two, maybe three. That is the whole vertebrate nation that lives here year-round. Small, mud-brown-backed, sulfur-pale-bellied, the size of the last joint of your thumb. They sit shin-deep in warm muck that smells of earth and oil, their throat sacs moving twice for every one of your breaths. They breathe through skin kept permanently wet by the air, which solves the lung problem. They call not in chorus — a chorus would drown — but singly, in the narrow acoustic windows between pump-throb and steam-hiss, a high clear plink that cuts through. Their tadpoles develop in the warmest, most closed pools where no fish could ever have arrived, growing fast in the heat, feeding on midge fall. They do not hurry, except as tadpoles. As adults they wait. They have learned peat-time.

At dusk — though noon lasts so long here you forget dusk exists — the single bulb in the hut and the small red lamp on the transformer click on, absurd and homely, and then you meet the other accidental residents.

Moths. Brown, furred, dusty-winged, that should not live at four thousand meters. They batter the glass. They came up with fuel drums, with a load of lagging, with a technician's coat years ago, drawn to light that never goes out, and then they couldn't leave. The cold void below and the cloud-sea on all sides is a moat. So they stayed and became something else: a stranded colony, smaller, darker, damper. Their caterpillars eat moss and fern spores and the beard off the guy-wires. They live their whole lives within ten meters of that bulb. They are inbred and damp and they thump softly against the lit window from inside the lung.

The others you see do not live here. That matters too.

Once a day, impossible, emerald: a hummingbird works the orange orchids. You hear it before you see it — a dry insect whirr cutting the wet roar — and then it is there, hovering in thin air that should not hold it, tongue in a thumbnail flower, heart doing a thousand beats a minute in air where you can barely walk. It does not live here. It commutes, two thousand meters up from forest you can only guess at through vapor, a spark blown off the lowlands, doing pollination work the orchids cannot do without. It visits each wax-orange throat methodically and then vanishes over the lip, over the void, before you have caught your breath.

Very high and far, sometimes, a condor tilts past *below* you, looking up at you. It does not live here either. It lives in the vastness this room has opted out of. For a second you are seen — a small warm green bite in black rock with steam coming out of it — and then it is gone.

And the hut?

There is soup-smell in the stovepipe sometimes. Boots by the door gone soft with moss. A logbook inside with entries in the same hand getting slower and rounder over years: *pressure steady. cleared intake 3. orchid out on north pipe. frog under board.* Whoever lives there — if one lives there still, and not just the habit of one — has learned the rule: they do not stop, and so he does not hurry.

You glimpse him only sideways: oil under nails that will never wash out in this wet, cheeks permanently red with extra blood, moving at peat-speed on the duckboards, listening to the pump with his palm, not a stethoscope, clearing ferns from grating with an apology, leaving most to grow through. He breathes twice to get enough, and breathes again, without resentment. He has an enlarged heart, like the hummingbird, but in the other direction: slow, large, patient. Lichen-change. His rust also creeps a millimeter a year. He talks loudly an arm's length away because he has to, and then stops talking at all.

Because the last thing that lives here is the works itself, and he is part of it.

The riveted boiler-house squatting on concrete where steam is strongest, breathing patiently through its stack. The thicket of pipes on low trestles, hissing at joints, insulated, moss-eaten, dripping — arteries that have grown skin. The transformer humming without pause, bearded — nerves. The catwalk, the winch, the fuel drums, the gauge panel whose needles tremble but never move far. It is industrial, scaled to produce, but it has settled into a long idle, just maintaining pressure, decade after decade. It leaks fever. It sweats condensation that feeds orchids. Its vibration warms the tadpole pools. Its light breeds moths. Ferns grow through its grating and it lets them.

It does not stop. That is its metabolism.

Stand on the lava lip at evening, where the hanging moss beards the frozen dam and the ground falls away sheer for two thousand meters to forest, where the cables hum and dive straight down into white — don't touch them when wet — and the beads in the air catch the bulb-light and for a moment the whole small room looks like it's underwater. Like you're inside a lung.

You are.

Breathe twice to get enough, and breathe again. Everything here touches everything else: warm water to moss to orchid to hummingbird to frog to midge to rust to steel to steam to skin. Nothing hurries, everything turns over. The room holds what it holds because it holds itself.
