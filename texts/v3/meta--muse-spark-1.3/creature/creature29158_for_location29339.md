They are not many. That is the first thing to unlearn — that a city means crowding. Here, life is sparse on purpose, thinned out to what condensation can support, to what the chord will tolerate.

Start low, where it is wettest, because everything else grows up from wet.

On the lee side of the sweating pipes, where the warm drizzle never quite dries, there is a velvet. Rust-red, rust-brown, the color of dried blood thinned with milk. If you put the back of your hand very close you feel it cooler than the pipe, breathing faintly. This is the moss. It does not photosynthesize so much as *absorb* — mineral leach out of basalt, iron and silica, heat, and the steady rain of what falls off the decks above: skin cells, oil, hair, the sloughed dust of people. It spreads in slow patches, no bigger than a spread hand at first, then joining at the edges into shawls a meter across. When the hourly breath peaks and the drizzle thickens, it spores. You smell it before you understand it: iron and fresh bread, warm and metallic at the back of the throat. For a few minutes the air around any large patch is hazed with it.

The white threads come next. They hang from cracks in the fluted basalt, from the grout lines behind bolted niches, a few centimeters to a forearm's length, swaying almost imperceptibly in the updraft. Rootlets with no plant attached that you can find — or rather the plant is only this, a mouth left open to fog. They drink. Touch one and it is cold, impossibly cold in this blood-warm egg, and it retracts, very slowly, like a touched anemone.

And in the quietest corners — behind a heat exchanger that has gone quiet, under a stair landing where feet do not often fall, in the acoustic shadow where the chord cancels just enough to let you hear your own swallow — the blind fern. No bigger than a thumb. Frond after translucent frond, white-green, veined like an eyelid. They make no spores you can see. They glow. Not enough to see by; if you bring a light you kill the ability to see it. Only after twenty, thirty minutes with your eyes starved in buried-dark do you start to make out that some patches of black are less black, a faint milkiness hovering at shin height. That is a whole thicket of them, feeding, exhaling oxygen in sips too small to matter except right around their leaves, where the moss leans toward them.

What eats, eats softly, without eyes.

You hear the moths before you ever sense them. A dry papery displacement in the whine above the chord, a beat that is not the beat of the projectors. They are big as a palm, pale as the ceramic slabs, furred, eyeless — eyes would be a waste. They steer by sound, riding pressure gradients the way surface moths ride scent. The nodes are their roads. When the moss spores they come out in a slow snow, tongues out, drinking spores off the air, bumping gently into pipes and into each other and correcting with a flutter. Clap — and they drop. A sharp transient deafens them, overloads the fine hairs on their wings that read the standing wave. The old hands teach children early: never clap down here. If you must kill a moth that has blundered into your niche, you cup it and wait.

What falls, feeds the floor. A spent moth spirals down to deck or gutter, and before its wings have stopped twitching, they come clicking. The isopods. Finger-long, segmented, blind white-gray, with antennae longer than their bodies that taste the air ahead. They live in the rim gutters, in the black trickles down the wall, grazing moss where they can reach it and scavenging everything else. You hear them at night — if there were night — a faint *tick tick tick* of claw on steel as a line of them works a gutter after a rainburst, clearing algae, eating drowned moths, eating each other's shed skins. Pick one up and it curls instantly into a cold pill, waiting. The maintenance crews leave them alone unless a gutter clogs. Then they scoop and relocate by hand, gently, apologizing in low vowels.

Down furthest, in the sump where fallen water collects in black, warm, bitter basins between the fins of the exchangers, the fish. White, finger-long, jaw-heavy. No eyes, no pigment. They hang motionless in water that never cools below thirty degrees, feeling. Vibration comes through their lower jaw into a skull made mostly of resonance chamber: the drip of condensate three hundred meters above, the knock of hot water slugging through a pipe, the footfall of a person on rim decking telegraphed down through stone and water. When a node collapses and true rain hammers the decks, they dart — you hear the small splashes if you lean over the lowest railing — eating stunned isopods washed down, eating moths, eating the rich soup of moss spores and skin.

And then people, if you count them.

There are dozens. Never hundreds. A town built for thousands, kept by a skeleton shift that became a population.

You would not pick them out by sight. In the dark they are sounds first, smells second, touches third. A boot scuff returning off the far wall a half-beat later. A breath folded into the chord. Sweat and iron and a little sweet rot of fern kept too close in a niche garden.

They are pale in a way that has nothing to do with lack of sun and everything to do with perpetual wet heat. Skin stays damp, stays slightly pruned at the fingertips. Feet are wide, calloused from perforated steel, quiet by training. Hands are their eyes after ears: fingertips ridged and hardened from reading embossed signage, from finding the familiar worn smooth spot on a railing that tells you *switchback here, third terrace*. Ears are everything. Children learn before speech to echolocate — a tongue-click off the palate and listen for deck versus rock, for the flattening that means a slab drifting close overhead, for the too-early return of the sound-lens where pressure in the middle bends everything a meter closer than it should be.

Their voices have changed. No shouting; shouting is violence here. A shout slaps back upon itself four, five times around the egg and makes the suspended boroughs shiver in their troughs. So they speak in low vowels that join instead of fight — *oh, ah, oo, mm* — sentences that hum, that add like tributaries to the great tone. Maintenance tuners can hear, inside the three-or-four stacked deeps, when one projector is going sour: a roughness, a beating that speeds up wrong. They walk the wall galleries with their mouths slightly open, throats trained, humming under their breath to feel sympathetic buzz in their sternum, finding the crusted round mouth by touch and clearing mineral with cloth-wrapped tools that will not clank.

Most live rim-side, in the locker-niches bolted to stone, ten, twelve terraces deep. A curtain of heavy cloth for sound, not privacy. A sleeping sling that does not creak. A kettle with a low whistle tuned to sit inside the chord. Chair legs capped in felt. Everything softened. You pass one on a stair and you both go quiet by agreement, listening for each other's feet to avoid collision, exchanging a low *ahh* of acknowledgment as you pass shoulder to shoulder in black.

A few live out. The tether-walkers. The boroughs — those pale gray seamless slabs the size of rooms, sitting in the troughs of pressure with nothing under them — are not empty platforms. Some hold gardens of fern and moss-thread, some hold a single dwelling, a low ceramic dome that hums with the slab. To live there is to live in the loudest part, where the chord holds you up and deafens you if you are careless, where mist hangs at chest height in glittering bands and refuses to fall, where fat warm drops bead on the tether filaments and evaporate halfway down. They go out clipped in, two tethers minimum, moving hand over hand along humming catenaries, learning to feel tilt and correction in their ankles as the slab breathes a few centimeters up and down. They are the most adapted and the strangest to rim-dwellers: their inner ears have remade themselves, they sway when on solid deck, they speak even lower, almost sub-vocal, because out there voice carries impossibly.

There was once a child — there are always once-children — who learned the room by deliberately humming off-key on the rim, leaning over the void, listening to how the egg corrected her, bent her note back toward its own. The adults did not scold. That is how you learn to see here. That is how everyone lives here: by offering a small sound and listening for what the loud dark gives back, by sweating, by walking softly, by tending moss and clearing gutters and scooping isopods and listening for sourness in the foundation-tone, held up like everything else on nothing but noise made solid.
