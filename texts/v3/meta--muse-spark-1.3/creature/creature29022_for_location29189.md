First, almost nothing.

That is the population here. For three days running you can see it: your own wake, the sawblade ridges crawling two hundred meters down under glass water, your reflection broken on hammered blue. The volume is too big and too empty for bodies. No silt, no green, nothing per liter to eat or hide in. So most of this ocean is uninhabited by definition. It is a desert held vertically between a white sky and a black floor.

Life does not spread thin here. It clots.

Where it happens — where a station breathes out hot brine on stilts, where a fresh crack in the young glass leaks heat from below — it happens all at once, too much, too dense, as if the whole sea has poured itself into one place to make up for the emptiness everywhere else.

At the bottom of it, literally carpeting it, is what passes for vegetation. It is not.

White felt. Rust-orange mats. A living chemistry, simpler and older than plants, that paints the shallows in pale tiger stripes. Sulfur-oxidizers, iron-oxidizers, making a skin over black pahoehoe rope and fault scarps. In the dimming that passes for night here they glow faintly orange along the ridges, like a city seen from a plane. They do not grow tall — rule one, written inside the station doors: nothing grows tall. They grow flat, felted, tight to the glass. They eat rock and heat and the stripped mineral water the pumps dump back overboard. Then they slough off, endlessly, flaking upward and outward, becoming the snow.

The snow is the base of everything. A fine, endless mineral fall through the upper empty water — dead bacteria, precipitated silica and iron, specks of white that never settle because something is always stirring them. In this clear, cold, heavy water you can watch it fall for a hundred meters straight down, glittering in flat noon light.

And where the snow thickens, the blind whites gather.

Billions of them. Krill that are not krill, each the length of a thumbnail, translucent-white, eyeless. They have no eyes because eyes would be useless and expensive here. The light is everywhere and reveals nothing; food has no shape, only chemistry. So they pulse. Whole clouds of them open and shut, beating the water white, tasting for sulfur, for iron, for warmth. From above they darken the clear water from below, a moving shadow in a sea that should have no shadows. From below, looking up, they blot out the white sky. They live fast and die fast and eat snow, and then eat each other when the snow thins. Eat snow or eat each other — that is the whole economy.

On the glass under them, the plates move.

Giant isopods, the crews call them plate-lice, each the size of a dinner plate, pale segmented armor clicking against armor as they crawl over each other in drifts meters deep. They do not swim. They walk, slowly, deliberately, on too many legs, over rope lava still keen enough to cut rope, and they are not cut. Their shells are thick mineralized white, barnacleless like everything here, but scarred from scraping glass. They are the cleaners and the patient. They eat what falls: dead whites raining down, whale skin, eel bodies, the bacterial felt itself ripped up in sheets. You can hear them if you put a hydrophone down — a dry rustling, like dead leaves, under the roar. They pile, they right each other, they molt in place and eat the molt. Where the ridge breaks the surface as a black sawblade, they stack at the waterline in lace ice, motionless, waiting for heat to return.

Above them, the ribbons.

Pale eels, blind too, each as thick as your wrist and as long as a man, but they never travel alone. They travel in ribbons a kilometer long, ten thousand bodies braided into one muscle that turns as one muscle. From the deck you see it as a single white scar moving under the water, flexing. It is not one animal and it does not behave like many. It pulses with the whites, folds around them, inhales them. Then it unfolds and re-braids and goes on. If a mill-whale passes, the ribbon parts around it without breaking rhythm, reforms behind it. No panic. There is no room for panic in water this cold. Everything is slow except in aggregate. Alone an eel is helpless, almost transparent, gut showing full of white paste. Together they are the fastest thing here.

And above them, slow, immense, the reason for the clicking:

The mill-whales.

Thirty meters of white blubber and baleen, mouths always open. They do not dive deep — there is no deep to hide in, the bottom is visible everywhere — they plow, horizontal, just under the surface, sieving the snow. They are the color of the sky, the color of the bacterial felt, the color of deafness: white on white. No flukes like ocean whales, but broad paddle mills, slow-turning, built for 1-degree water that never freezes. Their eyes are small, almost vestigial, clouded by brightness. They do not need them. They click.

Millions of clicks, all the time. Not songs — there are no songs here because a song implies beginning and end, change. These are constant echolocation pulses, sonar that carries too far in this cold, empty, heavy water. A whale feeding at one station can hear the plates rustling at another three hundred kilometers away as loud as under its own belly, because sound does not fade here. So they live in one continuous acoustic room, the whole sea. They keep apart by agreement of distance. Vast space between presences. One whale per cloud, usually. Sometimes two, moving in parallel for years, never touching, clicking to map each other, the glass teeth below, the falling snow ahead.

They must breathe this place, and that is their hardest work. The air is -12 C and thin and knifey at four kilometers up, the sun too white and too close. When they rise to blow, the blow freezes instantly and hangs as a short white column that does not drift in the still air. They hang a moment themselves, vast white backs steaming, oil smoking, breath smoking, and then sink again without a splash, too slow to make noise that matters against the roar. Calves — if you can call them that in a place with no seasons — are born white and huge and already deafened, already clicking before they can feed. There is no nursery season because it is always just now, just midday. They are simply added to the count.

And on stilts above all of it: the keepers.

One per station, sometimes none. A human animal gone local. Deaf, necessarily deaf, because 120 decibels of noon gets into teeth before ears, vibrates deck and sternum and bone, and earplugs do nothing. They shout into each other's ears and are stripped by wind of sound. They sleep shallow vibration. They smoke breath and oil. They tend pumps the size of houses that suck cold water down and dump hot brine back, with nowhere to send the product, just to run. Because rule four: keep the engines running.

The keepers have become pale too. White skin under white light, eyes squinted to slits against flat shadowless glare that has lasted a hundred years. They eat imported tins and sometimes, the old ones say, the whites boiled — thumbnail shrimp tasting of iron and sulfur. They watch the needle that has not moved in a decade. They watch the sawblade tooth that never dulls. They blink with the mast light every ten seconds, in perfect unison across a sea too big to see across, and they write the rules again on the inside of the door for whoever comes next to read by shouting.

They live here by becoming like the rest: blind to distance, deaf to variation, eating snow in one form or another, or eating each other’s noise. Suspended between two shallows that feel like depths, with billions of pale animals pulsing below them in the cold high sea that never changes.

That is who lives here. Nothing tall. Nothing green. Nothing that expects the sun to set.
