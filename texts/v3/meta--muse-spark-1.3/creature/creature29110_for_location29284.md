They live there the way wading birds live in a marsh. Not visiting it. Belonging to the water level.

At first you don't see them as residents. You see movement between buildings — a shape crossing too-wide shell road from shop to boiler house with its head down, a bicycle hissing — and you think, men at work. Then you stand longer on the apron and the dim and the roar fill you up and you realize no one *works* here in the sense of coming and going. They persist here. They hold.

The men are the most visible, because they are the most islanded.

They are slow. Not lazy slow. Conserved slow. In that heat no one hurries unless steam is venting wrong. They walk with knees slightly bent, hip boots sucking in muck at the edges, rags in back pockets, shirts black between the shoulder blades by eight in the morning and never drying again until they are taken off at night and hung up to get wetter. Their skin never quite dries either. Forearms beaded. Necks shining. Hands soft-pruned from always touching wet metal, then calloused on top of the softness from chain and valve wheel. You can tell the old hands by hearing: they are half-deaf and don't know it, shouting mouth-to-ear without embarrassment, reading lips through the roar, feeling the stamping press through their boots before they hear it.

They have learned not to touch anything bare. A rag for a pipe. A stick for ditch water. A boot for a crab. That caution is automatic now, like blinking. They check gauges the way other men check the sky — a glance, a tap, a nod — oil, coal, watch, wipe. Two boilers, one backup. Three pumps for the levee, two for process. They keep the balance. In as fast as out. Heat made as fast as heat lost. They don't build anything new here. The sharp edges were sharp when they arrived and will be sharp when they are replaced. Their job is to keep the mark on the foreman's notched stick exactly where it was yesterday.

The foreman is one of them, only longer. Hip boots to the thigh, stick in hand, same ditch levels every day. He doesn't command so much as confirm. Level's good. Fan's on. Keep her going. His voice is gone low from shouting over engines for years.

The women who run the mess and the dormitory laundry live more under the drip than in it. They work where condensation falls from eaves in a second rain, wringing mops into buckets that fill again on their own. Their arms are red to the elbow from hot water and bleach and marsh damp. Their hair is always damp at the temples. They feed everyone — stokers, machinists, the boy — great trays of rice and grease that steam in the already steaming hall, while sparrows quarrel in the rafters overhead and drop crumbs to the cats below. They have given up on dry. The laundry on the wire behind the foreman's house is their flag of surrender: perpetually warm, perpetually damp, flapping heavy in air too thick to move it.

The boy on the bicycle lives in the spaces between. Thin legs, tires hissing on wet cinder, basket of bobbins or gaskets or messages. He is the only fast thing, because he is the only one who doesn't yet know how to conserve himself, and the men watch him with a kind of fond irritation and shout at him to slow down before he kills himself on a crab. He knows every plank walkway, every pallet shortcut, every culvert that gurgles on the same note. He belongs to no building and so to all of them.

None of them crowd. That is what makes them strange. They live like buoys, far apart, shouting or telephoning across weather. At mess they sit together, sweating into their plates, too tired and too loud-dulled for much talk. In the dormitory they sleep in shifts with the roar in their sternums, learning to dream inside it so that quiet would wake them. In the little tin-steepled chapel — which no one quite admits to needing — a few sit on Sundays while mud-daubers work the eaves and bats shift above, listening to a sermon that has to pause for steam venting.

And with them, braided in, the other residents who are not on the payroll but are more at home.

The lean yellow yard dogs that belong to no one and everyone. They have learned the whole catechism: don't touch pipe, don't chase crab, don't sleep anywhere but under that one pallet where drip doesn't hit. They move slow as men, tongues out always, finding the one square of concrete that vibration has warmed. They are deaf too, in their way. They don't bark at the vent scream anymore; they just lift their heads.

The dormitory cats, thin and fierce, living on rats and scraps, nursing kittens under the floorboards where the ground breathes hot. The rats themselves, who run the pipe racks at dusk like commuters who know the schedule better than men, nose to tail above the ticking drip. The swallows and martins who live in the steam over the boiler house, eating mosquitoes as fast as the ditches breed them. The sparrows in the mess rafters. The bats in the chapel.

The white egret — there is always one — standing motionless on the warm discharge pipe on one tucked leg, waiting for minnows stunned by heat in the outflow. If it flies, another takes its place so exactly you think it never left. The copper and blue dragonflies patrolling the settling basins. The mosquitoes rising in pillars at shift change to feed and be fed upon. The frogs in the culvert mouths calling at all hours, day and night blurred. The big blue-clawed marsh crabs clicking sideways across the roads at low dim, unhurried, ancient, owning the right of way.

And further, at the edge where the levee seeps cold, the chorus that sometimes rises above the engines for a minute — frogs in thousands — and beyond them, out in the reeds where men don't go without boots and a reason, the grunt of alligators. The marsh waiting.

What lives here, taken together, is not a town. It is a holding action that learned how to breathe.

Skin stays wet. Lungs work for air chewed with water. Ears fill with low roar until silence feels like falling. Eyes adjust to dusk at noon, to bare caged bulbs haloed with moisture that don't illuminate so much as insist: keep going. Sweat never dries, rust blooms in the same spot after wire-brushing, slag is added at one end of the heap and taken at the other and the heap stays the same size. They have stopped waiting for weather to break because they have become weather — hot, dim, loud, wet.

If you come back in ten years the boy will be a man shouting mouth-to-ear, a dog will still sleep under the same pallet, the egret will still stand on the same pipe, the tamarind by the mess hall will not be taller, still white with bird shit. Not stuck. Balanced. A small, sharp-edged island of work humming to keep from drowning, and the humming things on it, human and otherwise, holding exactly their own against the low water.
