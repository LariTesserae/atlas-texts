It does not live there the way a crab lives in a pool.

At first you think nothing lives there. That is the fur talking. Then you think everything lives there, separately. That is closer, but still wrong because you are counting.

The shrimp is not it. The glass-colorless shrimp longer than your thumbnail, picking across a frond with antennae tasting decades ahead — he lives there, yes, in the way a louse lives in fur. He is a lodger. He combs. He prunes dead tips and eats the snow of bacteria and is so slow that you can watch him lift one leg and forget to watch him put it down. There are maybe three of him in the whole pocket. They never meet.

The eelpout is not it either. The pink-white eyeless fish that hangs in front of your mask and regards you with pits, then is elsewhere when you blink — she lives in the back crack and comes out only to check the weather of you. She is a visitor who never left. She eats a shrimp every few years when she must. Mostly she hangs and drinks the seep through her skin.

The one who lives there is the room.

Not metaphorically. The velvet is its skin.

Start with the skin because that is what touches you first. The bacterial mat, rust-red and sulfur-pale, is not a coating on stone, it is a mouth spread thin. A millimeter thick and miles deep in time. It drinks the iron bleed coming down from the cliff above, a drip so slow you would need to love it to perceive it, and it breathes out that faint amber when you press it. That amber is not panic. It is registration. *Something touched me here, keep it.* The light does not fade in the order it was made because the skin does not forget in order.

Out of that skin rise the hairs — the blind kelps, white-green and stiff because there is no current to lean in. They do not photosynthesize. There is nothing to photo. They are lungs turned inside out. Each frond stands and stiffens and holds its milk-light pulse, very slow, a minute per beat, and the tips bead that blue-green phlegm that detaches and hangs and does not rise. That is not decoration. That is how it sees in a place with no light. The beads stay where they are made, so the water becomes mapped in points. When you move too fast and the dark sparks where your skin displaces it, you are walking through its eyes.

Under the hairs are the stomachs. The sponges like ears, like lungs, like folded hands. They do the eating of what should not be eatable: a bubble fallen from your hair that sits shivering on the floor, the silt you stir that settles a finger-width left of physics, the dead feather of a worm crown. They open and close on the timescale of tides, a lungful per tide, that soft long sigh you hear as water exchanging through a seep too small to see. They are why up and down are only suggestions here. The pocket has learned to keep everything. Nothing rises to escape. Even air is made to fall and be eaten.

And the fingers — the tube-worms by the hundred in white calcium chimneys no taller than a thimble, each crown a perfect maroon-orange feather filtering the stillness. Alone each is nothing. Together they are hands.

Because they build.

You found the five little cairns in a loose arc, each three or four flat basalt chips capped with a white shell. You found the low arch no higher than your forearm, made of fitted tubes, the tiny colonnade, the wall that goes nowhere. Nothing with hands made them. That is true. Hands would be too fast.

It builds the way a reef builds: by staying. A worm larva settles on a chip because the bacterial velvet there tastes right. It grows its tube a fraction of a millimeter that year, leaning infinitesimally toward its neighbor because the neighbor's feeding current brings a little more. Over ten years the tubes lean into an arch because water moves best through an arch. Over thirty the arch is a doorway. No one planned it, and yet the place is more than tangle. It has arranged itself to save effort.

The cairns are different. Those are tended.

Watch — if you can hold your breath for a fourth time, if you can be still until your heartbeat goes distant — watch the shrimp. He does not walk randomly. He picks up a flake of black sand-flour on his mouthparts and drops it. He nudges a basalt chip with his head, a tenth of a millimeter. A chip falls from the upper wall once a decade, sharp as broken bottle. In a year of Sundays the shrimp find it. In another year they shove it. In ten years it is stacked. In thirty a sponge has filmed over it and sloughed off and filmed again, cementing it with living mortar. The white shell on top is not ornament. It is a garden marker: beneath that shell the mat grows thickest, sheltered from the faint fall of silt, and the shrimp graze there.

So who stacked it? No one. Everyone. The skin decided where food was best, the fingers grew toward flow, the combs moved stone toward skin. Over seep time, intent emerges without anyone intending.

That is why if you brush one crown the whole wall inhales — the fronds lean away in a slow wave that takes a full minute to travel three feet, then lean back. You have not startled a hundred animals. You have touched one thing in a hundred places. The density does not part for you; it seals around you because you are, for a lung-burning minute, included. A frond grows through a sponge grows through a worm tube grows through another frond and now, temporarily, grows through you. The bead of thick water that clings to your skin far longer than it should after you squeeze back out through the split — that is it keeping a sample. The dozen tiny cuts from the flour-sand you won't feel until later — that is it tasting you, too.

It is old in the way stone is young. The walls are fresh-broken basalt, glassy, unsoftened because nothing scours. Yesterday or a hundred years ago, no difference. It remembers the breaking. Before the split, it lived thin in the seep crack, a smear of red waiting. When the boulder cracked and the pocket filled with sea that never left, it bloomed. It has been thickening ever since, a millimeter a year on the kelps, a film per decade on the cairns. It will stay sharp for a thousand years because only the softest mouths wear it.

It does not sleep, but it has a slower state. When no visitor stirs silt, when the stone ticks cold, when bubbles do not fall, the milk-light dims to almost nothing and the crowns close. The whole flesh holds still so still that your closed eyes report the room larger than your open eyes do. That is accurate. When you stop demanding edges, it is larger. Darkness has more volume.

It does not think, but it keeps place. Where one organism ends and another begins you fail to find, because there is no end. Your hand reaching out gains a second edge, a faint after-image half an inch left, keeping pace — that is not your eyes failing. That is the water showing you how it holds position: everything here keeps a ghost of where it was, trails of amber that do not fade in order, fronds both in front of and behind stone. It lives in residue.

If you stay until your fingers go stupid, you will feel the etiquette settle on you, cold and thorough and not brutal: do not hurry. The food web is folded inward, patient, mostly microbial. Nothing is in a hurry to stop touching everything else.

And when you back out, squeezing sideways into the loud bright fast world of gulls and wind, it closes. Not behind you like a door. Like skin after a splinter. The curtains you parted seal. The wave completes its minute. The shrimp resumes his decade-long shove. The eelpout is elsewhere. The five cairns wait under their sponge film, the arch waits, the colonnade waits — not for you, not for anyone. For seep.

That is what lives there. A single pocket-flesh, furred and feathered and packed, blind and luminous, with no brain and perfect memory for touch. It does not need you to believe in its architecture. It will keep gardening stone in the dark, stacking and filtering and glowing faintly when pressed, long after your ears lose its particular quiet.
