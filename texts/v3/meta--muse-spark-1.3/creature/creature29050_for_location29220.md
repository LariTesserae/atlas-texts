Who lives here is not, at first, what you think.

From the levee you expect villagers. From the noise you expect mill-hands. What you find when you drop down into the bowl and have to turn sideways past the elephant ear is something slower and more intermixed than either — not a population so much as a maintenance fauna.

Start with the people, because they are the noisiest and the shortest-lived.

There are maybe a hundred and fifty of them, maybe two hundred if you count the babies and the company clerk who sleeps above the office and pretends he doesn't live here. Three, four generations deep now. Sixty years in the wet heat will do a thing to a body and they show it.

They are soft-footed and loud-voiced. They have to be. You learn to walk the sunk brick without looking — foot finding plank, plank finding brick, brick finding give — and you learn to shout a little through green that eats sound. Even the children shout. Their feet are wide and pale from never drying. Their soles are ridged from plank-splinters and their toe-webs are white with immersion fungus that never quite clears. Nobody wears shoes that lace. Rubber boots slit for ventilation, canvas sandals gone black, bare feet. Trousers cut off at the knee, shirts thin enough to wring out at noon and put back on. Everything they own smells faintly of hot oil and cane juice and mildew, because everything they own is never dry.

Their skin glistens. Not with sweat that comes and goes, but with the standing film of this place — condensation that forms on people the way it forms on pipes. Old women have a green tinge in the creases of their necks and elbows where algae takes in the salt. Men's forearms are stippled with small white scars from bagasse splinters and condensate spits. The pan-tenders have forearms gone shiny pink from years of sugar steam. The pumpmen have a permanent tremor in their hands that matches the engines, and oil under their nails that no amount of washing — and they wash constantly, in ditches, in sumps, under rain chains — will lift.

They do not hurry. That is the first adaptation. Hurrying makes heat you can't shed. They duck under vines without breaking stride, lift a curtain of morning glory with the back of a wrist, step over a ground ditch on a brick placed for that purpose forty years ago and never moved. A woman with a hoe moves twenty feet, throws, moves twenty feet. A boy goes up a pole with a machete between his teeth. An old man taps a gauge. All day the same loops, and they do them without resentment because resentment is also heat.

Listen to them and you hear how deeply the pumps are in them. They talk in pump-time. *Before shift. After the belts were changed. When No.2 was coughing.* Newcomers — there are almost none now, maybe a bride in, maybe a company fitter down for a year — are taught first not how to work but how to listen. To tell by the thump in the floorboards through their sleeping mats whether the lift is steady. The men in the pump house can hear a quarter-inch drop in the sump in their sleep. The women hanging laundry over the steam pipe can hear it too. If the note changes every head in the yard turns at once, the way animals turn to a strange sound. Then turns back.

That is their social order, really. Not company rank, though the company papers say so. Proximity to the heart. The drivers and oilers who live closest to the engines, in brick rooms that vibrate day and night, are the priests. Below them the crushers and pan-men, deaf in one ear from the mill. Below them the punt-poled cane bringers, the ash carters, the ditch women, the roof-boys. The children belong to everyone and to the fig tree. At lunch they swing on its aerial roots over the yard while the crusher groans, bare legs pumping, screaming to be heard over the belts. At evening they are called in not by name but by whistle — one blast for supper — because names flatten.

They eat what can stand the bowl. Morning rice, midday rice with boiled greens from the plots behind the cottages where the white cabbage moths work all day, evening rice with cane sugar stirred in and sometimes a moorhen egg if a boy has been quick in the hyacinth. They drink rainwater caught off the mowed roofs and boiled with fig leaves to take the iron taste out. They chew cane constantly. Their teeth are brown at the gumline. They are, on the whole, healthy in the way of people who have traded longevity for equilibrium — bad lungs from bagasse dust and spore and smoke, bad knees from stooping, bad skin in the wet season, but steady. They bury their dead up on the levee, above the waterline, in dry ground. It is the only thing they do up there.

But people are only the briefest layer.

If you stay long enough to stop looking for faces and start looking for persistence, you see who really holds the bowl.

The plants are residents, not weeds. That distinction the outsider makes doesn't exist here. The privet and fig that make the north cottage walls are planted, yes, but they are also inhabiting — their roots are in the brick footings, drinking the seep, holding the walls up as much as the brick holds them. The women who clip them square every month with shears talk to them the way you talk to a large, slow animal you live inside: chiding, familiar. *You've got through again.* Snip. The bamboo of the sleeping lofts still leafs out in April because it was never killed, only persuaded into a vertical shape. At night in April you can hear it growing, creaking against its lashings, and the people who sleep inside it say they dream greener that month.

Then the uninvited who have become permanent. Kudzu on the wires — cut back and back and always a handhold left, because to strip it bare would leave the wire naked to the sun and it would sag and snap. Morning glory on the kudzu, using the user. Water hyacinth in every ditch and sump, forked out steaming every morning and thrown on the compost, and by afternoon already closing over again, glossy, purple-flowered, unkillable. Moss and soot velvet on the mill brick that comes off on your palm if you touch it and comes off on their shoulders and backs where they lean. Maidenhair fern in every settled crack, purslane and pennywort between every herringbone brick, fern in every bolt-hole of the light poles, grass tufted on the crossarms. And over all, the strangler fig.

The fig is the oldest inhabitant after the water. It took the cracked chimney in '58 and did not let it fall. Roots like rebar down the flues, crown thirty feet over the yard. It drinks condensate, it drinks steam, it drinks transpiration. Its leaves make the big wet rustle when the warm air moves. Its aerial roots are swing-ropes, clotheslines, boundary markers. Its figs, tiny and inedible, drop and rot and feed the black mill beetles. In the evening when the bare bulbs come on under its canopy, moths orbit inside its shade and the light goes amber through wings and leaves. Nobody remembers who planted it. Nobody would cut it now even if the company ordered it. To cut the fig would be to cut the roof off the town.

And then — and the people here would laugh if you said it, and then nod — the machines live here too.

Not as tools. As large, warm animals that must be fed. The two low-pressure engines turn day and night, thump-thump, thump-thump, and the whole bowl rests on that rhythm the way a body rests on a heartbeat. They sweat oil, they shed heat, they breathe steam through weep holes. The dynamo housing is always warm, which is why the one-eared cat lives there, curled in a tight loaf, deaf to the whine, waking only to knead when the load changes. The belts slap and stretch and are dressed with tallow. The cane crusher groans and chews at intervals and then rests, groans and chews. The tailrace lifts its smooth glassy sheet up and over the dike without pause. The men feed the fireboxes — coal in the wet season, rice-husk and pressed cane trash in the dry — and carry away ash, and hang spare gaskets on nails like cured meat, and wipe and oil and listen. You do not turn such things off. You keep them alive, and they keep you unflooded. In a week to the knees, in a month to the windowsills. Everyone knows the exchange rate.

What animals remain have learned the terms.

No frogs. That is the strangest absence until you understand the oil film and the heat and the constant draw-down. The water looks alive and is sterile. Instead: mosquitoes in clouds at dusk, breeding in every can-chain drip and moss pillow, whining high over the engine thump, feeding impartially on people and cat alike. White cabbage moths flickering over the vegetable plots all day, laying, being picked off by hand. Black mill beetles clicking in the bagasse dust, living their whole lives in sweet rot. Once in a while a moorhen, thin-legged, picking through hyacinth at the south sluice where the punts come in, quick and nervous. And the egret.

The egret lives on the sluice gate for hours at a time, motionless as a valve. White against black-green brick. It does not fish — there is nothing to fish — it waits, as everyone here waits, for a change in level that brings something with it. When a punt poles through, it steps aside with insulted delicacy and steps back. The children do not throw stones at it anymore. It has been there longer than most of them.

By night they all settle into one shape.

Shift changes. The air cools one degree. Hoeing stops, vine-cutting stops, gauge-marking stops. The slate by the sump shows today's line, same as yesterday's line. Lights hum on. Steam from the open pans and breath from a million leaves rise together and sit in the bowl as a low cloud that will not leave till morning. Inside it, people eat, mend nets of vine, oil machetes, sleep in lofts to the thump through the floor, cats purring against dynamos, beetles ticking, leaves dripping after no rain onto moss pillows, condensate spitting every few seconds, water falling in small voices everywhere.

From above, from the levee road, you would not see any of them separately. Only the cup of light and mist, smelling of crushed cane and hot oil and wet earth, throbbing faintly. A small, crowded, living thing holding itself just below the water — people trimming, plants thickening, engines turning — each keeping the other from drowning or drying or stopping, in that order of patience.
