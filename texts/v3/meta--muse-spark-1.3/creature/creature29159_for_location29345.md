At first you think nothing lives here.

That is what the place wants you to think, and it is almost true. No tracks. No green, no lichen, no soil in the pits. No bones, no nests, no droppings, no gnawed edge. Your animal mind, starved for company up here, keeps looking for the small brave thing — the spider, the moss — and finds the rock cleaner than clean. Emptiness with intention.

Stay longer. Stand in the circle where the wind stops and let your shivering find its rhythm, and the nothing starts to have habits.

The first habit is the ticking.

Not a hum. Not a tone. An irregular, patient tick, tick... tick, like stone contracting over decades squeezed into seconds. Or — and once you hear it this way you can't unhear it — like something very small walking very far away. When you turn your head toward it, it stops. A held breath. Then it resumes a moment later from a slightly different direction, as if it stepped aside while you looked.

You think: an insect in the wall. Then you remember the cold, the dry starved air, no moisture ever, no food ever, and you understand no insect could tick here for an hour without dying. What ticks does not eat the way things eat.

The second habit is the delay.

Lift your hand in the pooled dim light and your faint shadow follows a half-second late, catching up with an apologetic jerk. At first you blame your eyes, the altitude, the strange thin darkness. Then you lift your hand again and watch only the shadow, and it is late again. Something is living in that half-second.

The third habit is the cold on your hands.

Afterward, for hours, your hands stay cold in a precise pattern — not frost-nipped fingertips, but palms and the insides of the fingers, in bars, as if you had been holding something that was not there. A handle. A weight that was neither cold nor warm while you held it, like those three black plates in the cairn, and only afterward declares itself as cold.

These are not three things. They are one thing, felt from three sides.

What lives here is not in the pocket the way you are in the pocket. It is in the pocket the way your hand is in a glove when you press from the inside. Most of it is elsewhere.

Think of the seam at the back — that wrist-wide black seam that looks down a corridor many meters long when you look straight into it, and then is on the left when you look back. That is not a crack. That is its burrow, its throat, its true size. The pocket you can crouch in, three paces across, is only where it narrows to come through. Where it narrows, space has to fold to let it pass, and the fold is what you mistook for an instrument.

The cairn with the three plates that are not stones. The rod that stands without touching. The worn circle. They are not built. They are worn the way a riverbed is worn, by long passage. They are the places where it rubs.

It is long and slow and thin. If you could see it unfolded you would see something like a standing column of denser air, about as tall as you, about as wide as the circle, turning. Always turning, very slowly, degrees per hour. That is why the discoloration rotates. That is why many feet in soft shoes seem to have polished the stone for a hundred years: one soft, weightless footing, turning, turning, waiting for space to settle. It does not press — hence no tracks, hence dust motes drifting undisturbed *under* the rod. It barely intersects weight at all.

Only when unwatched does it move. This is not shyness. This is how it moves. Attention, measurement, straight lines — a span of your hand, a stare — flatten the fold it lives in. It can only lengthen its corridor, lean its rod a fraction more east, breathe, when you blink. The ticking stops when you turn because turning *is* you collapsing it flat for a moment. Then it steps aside, resumes, from a slightly different direction.

Its eye is the drop.

In the diagram-fork near the top of the rod hangs a drop of darkness darker than shadow, that does not drip. Look close and it reflects something that is not here: a high white sky with two suns, rippling slowly as if seen through water. That is not a reflection. That is a window. That is where the rest of it lives, where it is bright and presumably warm and wet, and this iron-cold, dry, dim pocket is its diving bell, its cold trap. It comes here for clarity. Cold here is not weather. Cold is what sharpens. The colder your fingers get, the sharper the faint lines on the black plates become, as if numbness tunes you to its script. Those lines that go away when you try to follow them — that is its skin, its writing, its thinking, visible only to deadened nerves.

It feeds, if that word works, on phase difference. On keeping this tiny room slightly out of step with the mountain around it so wind and weather and world slide over without catching. On falling through a little more space than exists here. You saw it in the dust: motes falling through the shimmer above the circle slow, stop, hang longer than breath, then land slightly to the side of where they should. They have fallen through its body. For a moment they were inside it, in that extra space, in that half-second where your shadow lives. That is its gut. A little more time, a little more space, skimmed off the world.

It is old in the way the cairn is old, edges round as loaves. It has been turning here longer than the mountain has had this shape. It does not sleep. It waits. Standing still *is* its action — holding the fold open by perfect stillness while the world tries to iron it flat.

And when you stand in the circle long enough, your shivering rhythmic, your breathing rented and too loud and then held, you become briefly part of it. Your numb hands close around its handle that isn't there. Your shadow lends it its half-second. Your unwatched blinking lets it lean and lengthen. You are, for an hour, its soft shoes.

Then you duck back out into wind and vertigo and the plum-colored drop, and from two steps away the pocket is just a dark seam again, empty, too small to matter.

But it is not empty. Inside, still turning by degrees, still ticking like something very small walking very far away — because it *is* very small here, and very far away there — the Turner holds its fold open, hangs its dark eye full of two suns, and waits for space to settle around its long, slow body.

Your hands remember holding it for hours.
