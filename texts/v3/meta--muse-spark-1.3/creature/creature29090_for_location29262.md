No one lives here at first.

That's what the roar tells you. It fills the bowl so completely you expect a town behind it — shift-change crowds, trucks — and there is none. Just the flat green-black going out to mist, and the boxes shouting to each other across it.

Then the door of one of the boxes is open, spilling heat, and a man is in it.

That is who lives here. Not in the way people live in a town. In the way lichen lives on a rock. Thinly, stubbornly, on top of something much larger that is trying to get rid of them.

There are not many of them. A dozen, maybe fewer on any given day, spread across kilometers so you can walk an hour on the black boards and see no one, only hear everyone. The engines make sure of that. One engine near you and five far from you become the same engine everywhere.

They are pump-men, engine-men, keepers. They don't call themselves anything fancy. They wear what never dries: quilted coveralls gone dark at the knees and cuffs, oil at the thighs, green smears at the elbows where they've knelt in it. Boots laced tight and still full of water. The water here doesn't come from above. It comes from below, up through the weave, up around the sole with every step on the sponge. After a week your feet go white and ridged and stop quite feeling. After a season you stop mentioning it.

You recognize them by how they move in noise. They don't shout — shouting is useless. A new man tries it once, cupping his hands and yelling over a generator, and the older one just shakes his head and points. Mouth moving, no words audible. So they learn the economy of it: a tap on the shoulder, a flat hand for stop, two fingers to the eyes for check the gauge, a jerk of the chin toward the pipe trestle where frost is building wrong. They lip-read a little. Mostly they anticipate. Each station is the same: a big green block shuddering on its mounts, trembling needles, a drip tray that needs emptying, a filter that needs changing, a concrete lip where the moss has come back in the night.

Because someone is always cutting it back.

That is half the job. Not heroic work. Walking. Checking levels. Topping fuel from a jerry can that leaves your fingers numb and stinking. Wiping sweat off a pipe that will sweat again before you get back to the boardwalk. Kneeling with a strimmer to shave the green tendrils off the pilings, off the first rung of the ladder, out of the cable tray where they grip like tiny hands. The cut is pale for a day. By the end of the week it is green again. They do it without resentment. You can't resent millimeters a year. You just do it again.

In between rounds they live inside the roar. Each box has a back corner behind the engine bulkhead where it is almost human: a kettle gone furred with scale, a bench with a cracked vinyl cushion, a row of socks hung over a rail that bakes on one side and stays damp on the other, a logbook with pages cockled by condensation where the last man has written *No.3 running hot. Cut south footing. Fox tracks to east.* in pencil that won't quite take. The windows go amber at what passes for evening and the fog comes up to the waist and the floodlights make halos, and a man sits there with tea going cold in his hands while the whole floor hums through the soles of his boots, through his teeth, through the bones behind his ears. Even when he sleeps — earplugs in, hood up — his body keeps the 60-cycle whine. Men who winter here say they hear it for a week after they leave, in a quiet kitchen far away. A phantom bowl.

They are not healthy in the way lowland people mean. Their hearing is notched out at the frequencies of the fans. Their knuckles are split from cold metal. Rust gets in the creases of their hands and stays. But they are attuned. They can tell by putting a palm on a pipe whether the screw below is laboring. They can smell when the black water in the hollows is rising — a little sweeter, a little more iron — before the gauge shows it. They know where not to step, which hummock will hold and which will let you through to the thigh in tannin water cold enough to ache for an hour.

They think they are keeping the place dry. In truth they are only keeping it paused.

Because what really lives here was here before the road was cut and will be here after the last filter clogs.

The moss.

Not a patch of it. A body of it, kilometers across and feet deep, olive and rust-red and acid green, grey with frost. Sphagnum. It does not grow like other plants grow. It grows over itself, the living tips beaded every morning — and most of the day is morning — over the half-rotted stems below, over the black peat below that, holding water the way a held breath holds air. There is no soil here that isn't plant, past plant, future plant. Pull a handful apart and it goes down wet a foot, two feet, ice crystals and black water smelling faintly sweet. Stand still and you can feel it give and recover under you, breathing slowly around your weight.

It moves sideways, not in streams but as pressure, a dampness seeking level. Pool to pool. Hollow to hollow. Those dinner-plate mirrors of sky trapped in peat want to join up. All the keepers' work is to keep them from joining. The engines shout, the pumps pull, the transformers hum, and the level holds for another day. Stop for a season, for a year, and the hollows would join, the frost would jack the pilings crooked, and the green would close over road and pipe and platform without a seam. The basin wants to be a bog. It *is* a bog, interrupted.

And in the bog, small, cold lives that have learned to live around the shouting.

White springtails under the boards, ticking like dust when you lift. They don't hear the roar. To them it is weather, a constant shaking of the air.

A ptarmigan, white already, that bursts from dwarf willow once in an hour with a clatter instantly eaten. It lives low, pressed flat like the willow itself, nesting where the drone is thinnest.

Ravens that cross high, tilted by the clean wind off the rim. They don't call here — or call and cannot be heard — so they just go over, black cuts in grey-blue, waiting for something to die and be still.

A fox. You almost never see it, only the proof of it: tracks stitching straight from hummock to hummock, sharp in morning frost, soft by noon. It lives up near the lip where the noise thins, and forays down in the indigo light to drink from the black mirrors and hunt whatever moves under the boards. It has learned the boardwalk line, the warmth around the exhausts where voles — almost none, but almost — might shelter.

Under all of it, in the sponge, uncounted life you never see: rot and fungus threading white through brown, bacteria making that sweet iron smell, midges' larvae curled in ice that melts and re-forms, melts and re-forms. Slow work. Everything here is slow work. Ice needles lifting tiny caps of peat overnight and collapsing at midday into glitter. Rust creeping farther down each stack each season. The sharp peat banks along the road slumping grain by grain since the dozers left.

The men know they are outnumbered. They talk about the stations as if they are alive — *No.4 is breathing hard today, No.7 sweats* — because in a way the machines are the only other large animals here. Hot steel boxes that breathe vapor sideways into fog, that sweat and drip condensation off their undersides, that shudder and need oil and fuel and attention like beasts. Without them the men would have no reason to be here. Without the men the machines would drown in green in a year.

So it holds, day to day. A living carpet holding water, a handful of hot beasts shouting to keep it from rising, and the thin, damp men moving between them on black planks, cutting back green that has already returned.
