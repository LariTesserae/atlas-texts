No one tall lives here. That is the first thing the living have agreed upon.

At first you think no one lives here at all. You walk the gravel streets between the moss blocks and the town seems untenanted. The polygons are too perfect, the doors are missing. A beetle crossing is an event. You stand still and only water moves.

Then you kneel. That is the price of admission. Your knees go into cold wet and your face comes down to within a hand of the ground, and the empty city fills.

It fills downward, and inward, and slower.

The oldest citizens are not in the centers. They are on the walls.

On the curbs — those upright, lichen-grey slabs that rim every polygon — live the Painters. Map lichen, orange sun lichen, black crust lichen, grey leaf lichen. From standing height they are just color, a freckle on stone. From kneeling height they are continents. Each rosette is a hundred, five hundred, a thousand years old, eating light and eating stone at the same time, exhaling acid so weak you would need a century to taste it, and yet the pits in the rock under them prove it works.

They do not grow so much as persist. A millimeter a year in a good year. A millimeter a decade in a bad one. They have been here since before the polygons finished sorting themselves. They remember when that slab was buried and when frost jacked it upright. They rim the town like old flags left out until they became part of the wall. If you put your fingernail close you can see their edges lift, black cilia holding on against wind that would peel paint off steel. They are the only ones who can live directly on hunger — no soil, no shelter, only sun and fog and stone-dust — and they make soil out of stone for everyone else. They die into dust, and the dust washes into the centers.

In the centers live the Matrons.

They look, from standing, like green fists, like moss-roofed loaves, like houses with no doors. They are cushion plants — moss campion, stemless sandwort, saxifrages pulled tight into domes. Approach one and put your palm just above it without touching. You will feel it: a degree, two degrees warmer than the air that hurts your nose. Each dome is holding its own summer.

A cushion is not a plant the way a tree is a plant. It is a city-state. Hundreds, thousands of shoots packed so tight that wind cannot enter, water cannot leave. The outer leaves take the beating — frost, grit, ultraviolet — and die into a thatch, and the thatch becomes insulation, and inside, in the warm damp heart, new buds wait. One cushion the size of a bread loaf can be three hundred years old. Tap it and it is hard as wood, as muscle. Part its edge very gently and you smell cucumber and tannin and deep peat.

Inside the dome it is crowded. This is where the Matrons shelter everyone else. Seedlings of other species germinate only in their lee. Mites overwinter in their thatch. Springtails gather under their skirts where the black soil stays unfrozen an hour longer each day. The cushion does not mind. Or minds so slowly it does not matter. It grows outward a fraction of a millimeter a year, swallowing its own dead, making its own ground higher.

Between and under the Matrons, filling every polygon center ankle-deep, is the Forest.

That is what the moss is, though you have to be ant-sized to walk it properly. Haircap moss with stems like red wire and leaves like tiny spruce. Star moss. Peat moss swollen like pale sponges. Liverworts laid over them like dark green tongues. From above: a mattress that rebounds under your boot. From within, with your fingers parting it: trunks, canopy, understory, glades beaded with water that never fell as rain.

Everything here drinks fog. The Forest combs it. Each leaf tip condenses a bead at night, each bead runs down red stem to rhizoid to soil, and by midday the whole center is wringing wet. Lift a tuft and underneath is black, sour, alive-smelling humus only a few centimeters deep — the entire accumulated wealth of ten thousand years, never burned, never washed away, only frozen and thawed and frozen again.

And in the water — because water is not a river here, it is a breath — live the Bears.

You will not see them without a lens, but they are the most numerous animals in the town. Every moss leaf holds a temporary sea. Every melt pocket the size of your hand, every black wet gravel throat, every rime-drop melting at noon is briefly an ocean. And in it: tardigrades, barrel-bodied, eight-legged, lumbering through the film like slow cattle through high water. Rotifers, coronas of cilia spinning, mouths opening and closing. Nematodes threading white between soil grains. Green algae greening the undersides of translucent stones. Cyanobacteria in black jelly beads, fixing nitrogen out of thin air for the mosses to steal.

The Bears are masters of the daily unmaking. When the pocket dries by afternoon, when the contact-lens ice skins over at dusk, they do not die. They tun. They pull in legs, make a glass of their own sugars inside their cells, and wait — dry for a month, frozen for a year, dead to every test and then, with one drop of melt, unpacking themselves and walking again. In a single handful of moss-center there are thousands of them, going through wet and dry, alive and almost-not-alive, several times a day. They are the true citizens of a place where water exists for three hours and then is gone.

On the streets — the gravel troughs and gutters between polygons — live the Jumpers and the Armored Cattle.

Lift a slab, and only one slab a visit, and replace it exactly, and you will see them: springtails, white as salt, grey, slate-blue, popping like static when light hits them. They are not insects, not quite, six-legged and wingless and older than dinosaurs. They graze. On spores, on algae dust, on fungal hyphae, on the dead edges of lichen. They jump by a catapult tail folded under the belly; to them the gap between two gravel grains is a canyon.

With them, slower, almost too slow to see move: oribatid mites, reddish-brown, round as poppy seeds, armored like tiny tortoises. They chew lichen crust and moss litter grain by grain. One mite will spend its entire two-year life within a single polygon, never crossing a curb. And even smaller, quick as a prick: predatory mites, the only hunters resident here, blind, feeling their way with long front legs for a springtail egg. There is no pack, no flock, no roar. Predation here is the size of a sesame seed eating something the size of dust.

For an hour, on the one warm bright day in many, the town gets wings.

Stoneflies, midges. They hatch out of the hand-sized mirrors when melt lingers long enough for larvae to finish. They are black, frail, bad fliers. They rise, shiver in the sun, mate in the air above a moss dome while poppings of moss capsules go off below them like tiny fireworks, lay eggs back into water that will be gone by evening, and freeze. Their bodies litter the moss and become, within a day, food for mites and springtails and fungus. They do not live here so much as visit their own birthplace briefly and leave it fed.

And then the true visitors, the tall ones who break the law of low.

Once a day, perhaps, a white ptarmigan cuts low over the collar, wings whistling, feet feathered like snowshoes, and is gone. It does not nest in the bowl — too exposed, too wet — but it remembers the bowl the way travelers remember a well. It drops a few droppings, nitrogen-rich as gold here, and takes a beakful of cushion buds and grit for its crop.

Once, hare tracks pressed into soft moss, prints filled with clear water, going from one side of town to the other and out. It did not stop. Nothing big stops. A mountain hare, a fox on a bad year, a reindeer strayed high — they pass along the rim, look down at the green, and keep to the dry flat because the mattress would soak their feet. Their leavings — a hair, a pellet — are windfall. The springtails find them within hours.

That is all that moves at your scale. And yet if you lie down with your cheek near the moss you understand the town is not empty. It is just living at speeds and sizes you were taught to ignore.

Listen: capsules popping in the sun, thousands of lids flipping open to let spores sift a millimeter. Hear the stones tick as ice lets go in their pores, thunk as a needle of ice shoves a slab a millimeter higher — the Builder, still working. Frost is not alive, but here it acts like the only government the town has ever had, sorting soil to center and stones to edge, knocking down what grows too high, lifting footprints smooth by morning, keeping everyone low.

Smell it: damp wool and stone dust and green iron. Feel the cushion's breath on your palm. Watch light vibrate — orange against green almost buzzing.

Stay till fog spills back over the collar and the green darkens from emerald to black-green and closes. The Bears are already tuns. The Jumpers are already under the slabs. The Matrons are holding their two degrees against the night. The Painters are already rimed white.

You stand up. Your knees are wet. From standing height the city is empty again.

That is how it wants to be seen by the tall.
