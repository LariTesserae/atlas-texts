What lives here lives by heat and touch and hearing, not by sun.

Start with what you are standing on. You think it is ground. It is not. It is a citizen.

The moss — that black-green, iron-dark mat knee-deep and house-deep — is not one thing but a hurry of things, a weave. Frond-moss to comb the fog. Root-moss to grip the teeth of the basalt. Ember-moss that glows. Crouch and put your ear to it in a thaw and you hear it working: a faint fizz, frost needling up on new shoots even as old shoots freeze and burst and rot beneath. It grows while you watch. A vine thick as your wrist tightens around a rock flake and lifts it. Spores dust down onto fresh steam-bared stone seconds after a hanging curtain tears loose and falls, and by morning the hiss is muffled under green-black felt. It shears easily, springs back slowly. It holds heat the way a held breath holds heat.

It does not live alone. Its light is not its own. Along every warm seam — every crack where the young ground sighs steam that becomes dew that becomes drip — the mat is veined with a deep bacterial ember-green, pulsing very slowly, as if breathing. Bacteria, fungi, moss in compact: the bacteria eat sulfur and iron sighing up from below, and make light and sugar as a byproduct; the moss shelters them and drinks; the rot feeds them back. That is the secret compact that lets a continent of plant live in true night. It did not wait for the sun to return. It learned to eat stone-breath and make its own faint dawn.

Hanging off sheer rock where the moss cannot mat, the lichen-curtains live the same bargain on vertical terms — hundreds of meters of rope-vine and hair-fine frond and grey-green lichen, swaying, freezing, thawing, freezing. They are fog-nets. In the hours when the cloud-sea boils up over the plateau edge and combs through, they swell heavy with gallons, then let go in fat cold drops, leaf to leaf to moss to stone pocket. They tear under ice-weight, crash, expose steaming rock, and are recolonized before the rock cools. Further up, the cup-fungi live as cisterns: shallow cups that catch drip and freeze into cloudy lenses, holding water as ice until a thaw or a tongue comes.

And then the giants they feed.

You mistook them for hills at first. Pale and barkless and as wide as city blocks, rising out of the moss-mat and disappearing straight up into the black where cloud makes its own weather. They are trees only if you stretch the word until it breaks. They do not so much grow as build. Their trunks are pillars, their branches are cross-beams, arches, lattices miles across that groan like organs in the wind, creak and crack as fibers freeze, shed ice in ticking showers that fall and fall before smashing brittle far below. Their roots are the country: you walk for hours across springy tundra and then realize the ground has risen — you are walking up the shoulder of a root a mile between trunks.

Their leaves are not leaves. They are organs. Bladders small as your hand and big as a house hang thick in the dark, trembling with stored water, sloshing when the wind hits. Sails spread to comb fog sideways. Cisterns cupped upward to catch ice-shed. When the wind turns them you see their undersides freckled with that same ember-green, constellations drifting — bacterial colonies farmed in the leaf-skin for light, for lure, for chemistry. In the dark they seem to breathe, and they do: exhaling during brief thaws, inhaling fog, sweating condensation that falls as rain from below and sideways and from themselves. A whole brief sleeting shower can patter off a million bladder-leaves like distant applause. Boom, now and then: a bladder bursting, a trunk settling, rock contracting — rolling between the pillars, echoing back changed.

Where water is held, life clusters. Not in lakes or rivers — though now and then melt finds a sharp channel and runs black and fast for a kilometer before vanishing — but in pockets. Tip a bladder-leaf and drink. Lick a frozen cup. Find a hollow lined with ice that never quite melts. Everything here knows how to find a pocket, and how to become one.

The air belongs to the moth-birds.

You don't see them until they are on you, because there is so much empty night for them to be lost in. Then you hear wings, and slow snow comes.

They are pale, blind white, furred to the wingtip against cold that would kill feather. Wingspans wider than you are tall. Thousands at a time riding the warm updrafts along the seams, where steam rises and spores rise with it. They do not see; they listen and smell heat. Their wingbeat is a soft thunder that goes on and on. Their fur combs water out of the air and frosts, and they land heavy on bladder-leaves to drink and to scrape sugar-lichen with rasp tongues, to dangle and slosh. Calves of the herds below will stare up and whistle at the sound. When they roost they cling in shaggy drifts to the undersides of the lattice, ten thousand breathing bodies, shedding fur in clumps that drift down like larger snow. Their guano, their dead, their shed fur — all of it falls to feed the moss and the cups. Their young, blind and pink and squeaking, hatch in down-lined crevices near steam, where it is almost warm.

The moss belongs to the grazers.

Smell them before you see them: sweet rot and musk on the wind. Low, shaggy, barrel-bellied, six-legged — six because the ground gives and springs and is-toothed underneath, and four is not enough to not break a leg on a dike-fin in the dark. Their heads are shovels, blunt and wide, that shear frozen moss in swaths with a sound like tearing cloth. Inside their barrel bellies a furnace: fermentation vats that turn poor frost-moss into heat and fat, grumbling and steaming in the cold. They move in loose herds, grunting, calves whistling thinly to mothers, bulls rumbling low enough to feel in your boots. Their wool is long, matted, ice-beaded outside and dry-warm at the skin, shed in felted patches that snag on rock and vine. They know the seams the way you know a path home — they drift from warm crack to warm crack, from pocket to pocket, cropping, dropping dung that glows faintly for a night with bacteria before cooling, bedding down in moss hollows where steam keeps the frost off.

Where grazers go, the hunters go.

Lean, dark, almost silent. On fresh basalt you hear the click-click of their claws before anything else, deliberate, testing. Then nothing for a long time. Then the scream — sudden, shocking, bouncing from trunk to trunk until it seems to come from everywhere at once. They are built for night that never ends: narrow heads, huge flared ears that swivel independently, eyes that are mostly shine, whiskers long as arms to read vine and rock and wind. Their coats are close and black-blue, frost-shedding, no silhouette. They hunt not by sight but by triangulation — the grunt of a herd a mile off, the whistle of a calf, the thunder of wings meaning something fallen. They take a calf by the throat and are gone into dark before the herd has turned. They will take a lone walker, too, if hunger presses. You learn to listen for the absence of other noise when they are near.

And moving the way the herds move, from seam to seam, from pocket to pocket, are the hands.

They are people — or near enough that a dropped mitten fits a child's hand. Short, broad-chested, big-lunged for air thin enough to ache, moving slowly when they can, quickly when they must. In darkness you rarely see their faces: hoods rimed, eyelashes frozen, breath white, eyes large and dark and long-adjusted, catching ember-green more than starlight. Their craft hangs on their bodies: cloaks felted from grazer wool and lined with shed moth-fur, soft thunder made into insulation; boots wrapped against drip that freezes; stone scrapers at the belt, a needle of sharpened hunter-bone, a flask made from a small bladder-leaf stoppered with resin, sloshing at the hip.

They do not live here the way lowlanders live anywhere. There are no villages. No fields. Nothing that tries to hold this place, because it cannot be held — it rises millimeter by millimeter, cracks opening somewhere deep enough to feel in your teeth, curtains falling, seams shifting. Instead small bands — six, ten, a family and cousins — follow heat. A cairn of sharp basalt flakes marks a warm seam until moss tips it over. A low bothy woven from living vine, trained in a dome and left to keep growing, doorway still open, inside lined with shed moth-fur, a cold fire-pit of stone where bone-char glitters — they rarely burn; there is little to burn that the roof can spare, and heat is in the ground if you know where to lie. They drink by tipping leaves. They eat cropped moss-heart fermented in a bladder, moth-bird eggs taken sparingly from steam crevices, old shed fur boiled to soup, and meat when a hunter has made a kill and been driven off, or when a grazer calf is failing and will not see thaw. Everything is shared by touch in the dark.

Their great skill is coaxing. To tie a vine here so that in a week it makes a handrail. To prune a bladder there so it swells and leans and drips into a cup you left. To braid leaf-fiber between two root-ridges over a breath of void into a bridge sagging with ice, clearly made to be crossed in darkness by feel, foot memorizing sway. To train hanging moss into a weeping wall that waters you as you brush through, sleeves soaked. In the morning they leave it, and the moss takes it back. That is not failure. That is the agreement.

Children learn early to listen for water, to read the pulse of ember-green — faster means warmer, means nearer steam, means grazers, means be wary of hunters. They learn the organ-groan of the lattice means wind aloft, ice-shed soon, keep clear of open air. They learn the shovel-head grunt, the calf whistle, the wing-thunder, the claw-click, the scream and which echo is real. They learn to walk up a root-shoulder without thinking it is a hill.

At night — which is always — you will find them lying belly-down on the springy mat with an ear pressed to moss, listening to the fizz of growing, or standing still under a sail-leaf while condensation patters down, face upturned, mouth open. Very small, very awake, walking on the back of something enormous that does not know they are there, but keeps them warm anyway.
