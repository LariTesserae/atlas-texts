Nothing here lives *on* the marsh. Everything lives *in* it, and low, and close.

At first you think it's only the small things.

Because that is what you touch first. The air is a body. Walk ten feet and you go through a curtain of midges, then moths, then midges again — columns of them hanging over the pans, so dense they coat your lips and lashes and you have to breathe through your sleeve. You don't so much hear their wings as feel them, a pressure on the eardrums. Under them, the ground is paved.

Snails shoulder to shoulder on the levee mud. White crabs, bone-colored, moving sideways through each other's trails. Tadpoles so thick in the shallows the water looks solid and black until you disturb it and it ripples alive. Frogs — thousands of them — but they don't chorus. That's the wrong word. Here and then far off and then right under your boot, one call at a time, as if they are taking turns minding something. Eels in the ditches, fat as your wrist, leaving V-wakes that don't fade because the water never moves enough to erase them. Nightjars you never see. Herons you see only as heat coming loose from mud, that soft suck of feet, a wingbeat too close to your ear.

For a while you tell yourself: ah. A rich place. Teeming. Young land makes a lot of small life fast.

Then you count legs.

Seven, nine, then eight when you look again. A crab walking backward perfectly through the groove its own body just left. You hear a heron lift to your left and you see — sideways, by heat on your cheek — its shape lift to your right at the same instant. Both splashes. Only one set of rings.

The moth columns do it most openly. Most of the time they are just moths. Then, for no wind, for no reason, they all beat at once. One pulse. And for half a second the air above the water is not moths. It is what the moths were outlining. Too large. Too jointed. Knees and elbows where no knees should be, a span that goes from one pan to the next without crossing the middle. Then it is moths again, settling, stupid, bumping your face.

That is the first inhabitant to understand: the Double. Or the Behind. It doesn't live *beside* the small things. It wears them for a second at a time.

The second inhabitant is easier to touch. It hums.

The machines were left here, scattered — pump house, shed with no door, transformer on a plinth, stack half a mile off breathing sulfur heat you feel on your face, conveyor trestle marching out and just stopping over nothing with its belt still. No one tends them. They have not stopped.

A big flywheel in an open shed turns once a minute. Turns. Pauses. Turns. Oil weeps from its bearing into a pan, overflows into the mud, and that is where the eels gather thickest, mouths up. A pump out in the dark clunks, draws a long breath, pushes water from one ditch to another ditch. No reason. Transformers hum so low you feel it in your molars. Pilot lights burn red and green, pinpricks that illuminate nothing. They only mark.

At first you think *abandoned but running*. Stay longer and you realize they have gone animal.

Put your hand near a humming transformer and the hum stops. Not dies — moves. A moment later the next pole down the line picks it up, as if it shifted over to make room for you. The pump that was behind you is suddenly in front of you, still clunking at the same interval. The stack you walked toward for twenty minutes is no closer. They are shy the way herons are shy. They are patient to the point of eerie because they have learned the marsh's pace: one revolution, one breath, one drip from the weeping eaves. Metal weeps here like rock weeps. The catwalks drip. Cables sag, beaded, ticking as they cool. The machines have rooted. Slag has grown up around their feet. Snails lay eggs in the junction boxes. The eels know the oil pan by taste.

They are not haunted. They are inhabited — by themselves. They have become the large, slow fauna of a place with no tall vegetation to hide them.

And someone tends them.

No one lives here now, or no one admits to it. But a walkway swept clear of snails, in a place where snails cover everything in minutes. A kerosene lantern with its wick trimmed neat and never lit. A row of eel traps along a ditch, emptied and reset, the withies still wet from hands that were here just before you. On a concrete plinth, in a neat line: a bolt, a frog skull, a red pilot bulb still warm to the touch.

Not a resident. A keeper. Or several.

You never see them head-on. You learn to see sideways, and sideways you catch them as a difference in heat — a tallness where there should be flat, a place where the midges part. If you hear your own footstep a half-second *before* you make it, stop. That is them, or that is the marsh wearing them for a second. Wait until it catches up. They sweep. They trim. They do not light the lamp because the fixed red and green lights are safe and the white ones that drift low over the pans are not, and any new light might be mistaken.

They leave offerings because they have learned you have to feed both sides: iron for the machines, bone for the mud, light for whatever watches. The bulb still warm means it was just taken from a panel — means the machine allowed it. A negotiation.

What are they negotiating with?

Don't step where the water is warmest.

You learn this with your feet. Most of the water is warm — breathed-out warm that sits on the skin. But in places, in the low spots, in the pans that tremble without wind, it is *warmer*. Put your palm to the mud there and water wells up around your fingers smelling of iron and rot and hot oil, warmer than the air. That is where the mud is thinnest. That is where the eels are thickest, knotted, mouths open, not feeding but attending.

Something underneath breathes up.

The whole basin is young. You can feel it under your boots — slag ridges still sharp where they broke, levees still crisp-edged, rebar quills out of a collapsed culvert with only a skin of rust, formwork grain still in the low concrete walls. Pushed up, drained, dumped — new. Water is already taking it back. Every bootprint fills ankle-deep, and in the warmest places it fills *before* you lift your foot, with water that was not there a second ago.

In the weeping places — under the catwalk, at the culvert mouth — droplets detach and do not fall. They hang, trembling, catching red pilot glow, then slide sideways and vanish. Gravity hasn't quite set here yet, or something is sipping.

Lie still long enough and you feel it: a slow lift under you, a sigh a hundred yards away as a pressure valve lifts and settles though no gauge moves. The young land deciding its shape. The Breather. It is not a creature in the mud. It *is* the mud becoming a creature — old sea floor and new mud and poured concrete all condensing together, learning to inhale through eels and exhale through stacks.

The white lights are its lures, or its dreams. Don't follow a light that moves. The red and green stay. The white drift low, patient, stopping when you stop. They want you in the warmest water.

And the shed stenciled 7. You pass it with its caved roof. Half an hour later, circling a pan you cannot see across, you come to it again. Same 7. Your own bootprint on its step, still filling. That is not you getting lost. That is the place folding to keep you near the warmth, the way a tongue folds.

So who lives here?

The small multitude that paves the mud and crowds the air. The slow engines that learned to be animals. The shy keepers who sweep and reset traps and will never let you see them straight on. The Doubles that wear herons and crabs and moth-columns for half a second to look at you. The Whites that drift.

And under them all, wide as the basin, flat and warm and still deciding: the young thing itself, breathing up through thin mud, tasting your boots through eels, marking its presence with red and green eyes that do not illuminate, waiting out a night that does not end.
