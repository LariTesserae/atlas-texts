At first you go on thinking no one lives here.

That is the town's first lesson, and you learn it wrong. You walk the eleven minutes from lip to lip, you try doors, you find swept floors and cool darkness and no dust and no footprints, and you think: abandoned, but the machines left on. Condensers turning. Net tightening. A spalled corner foaming smooth overnight as if licked.

It isn't abandoned. It's occupied so completely there is no room for a body.

Stay past the second fog and you start to see who sweeps.

---

They are not fast. That is why you miss them.

The first ones are the walls themselves.

On every north-facing wall, where the damp holds an hour longer, the varnish lives. Ochre and gunmetal and bruised violet, in lobes and terraces, in tiny dammed rims no bigger than a fingernail that fill and spill and fill again each weeping. You were told it accretes. It does. Micron by micron, fog by fog, silica and iron laid down like varnish on a violin.

But accretion, watched correctly, is behavior.

Each film drinks. You can see it if you stay still enough during the weep: the matte surface goes satiny first at the edges, then the color deepens, the lobes swell almost imperceptibly. The whole wall darkens as it pulls water sideways, out of the air, out of the stone next to it. Touch it - don't - and the oils of your finger cap those pores for a season. That patch will stay pale and thirsty while the rest darkens. The wall will drink around you, and remember you as a dry spot.

They are colonial, the films. Not one wall, but all walls. Water here is communal by design because they made it so, or the builders made them so: the cisterns are all connected underneath, the gutters all lead down, and the damp moves through fused glass and foamed basalt by capillary will. A wall that drinks in the north shares south through the stone. That is why the rule: the wall drinks for all the walls. Hurt one and you thirst them all.

They are slow the way geology is slow, until fog-hour. Then for an hour, maybe two, they are fast. They swell, they breathe, they lay down a new layer you can almost hear ticking as it contracts. If you put your ear - don't put your ear - very close to a wet varnish terrace you hear a faint fizzing, a chemistry too eager to wait. They live one frantic hour a day and spend the other twenty-three remembering it in stone.

They do not see you. They taste your shadow as a coolness, your breath as a brief acid. They know you mainly as a interruption in drinking.

---

The second ones live only to die well.

In the hottest fissures by the drums, where the condensers piss waste heat into cracked scoria, the bloom comes overnight. White and lemon-yellow needles, feathers, ferns of sulfur and salt, impossibly delicate, branching against all weight. You go to sleep and the rock is bare crust. You wake and it is furred, garden-soft, glittering faintly in the brown dusk.

By mid-cycle the heat wilts them back. They slump, they sugar, they collapse to crust again.

It looks like failure. It isn't. The collapse *is* them.

Each bloom is a brief lung. While it stands it has enormous surface — ten thousand crystal tips to catch fog as it spills over the rim. The needles bead first, before the net does. They load with Almost-water, heavy, bending, and then as the day heats they dissolve into their own brine and wick it down into the fissure, sizzling without ever quite boiling away. Bloom, collapse, bloom. Inhale, exhale.

No single feather survives. The habit survives. The fissure remembers the shape of wetness, and rebuilds a slightly better lung each night to drink it. If you stay a tenday you will see the same crack grow more feathery, more efficient. The children who were never born would have called it a garden. It is a garden that gardens itself, with no gardener but heat.

---

The third one is underneath.

You hear it in the pipes. Thick as a torso, matte black, sweating. They climb out of fissures and converge on the low drums, and the drums knock as they cool. The gutters lead, the pipes gurgle, the cistern throat holds three inches of rust-red water that will be gone by next dusk.

Put your palm an inch above the glass plaza at mid-dusk and you feel heat rising in sheets. Put it flat on the plaza at fog-hour and you feel something else: a faint tremor, a peristalsis, as water moves below you from cistern to cistern, leveling itself.

There is a body under the plate. Not a creature — a circulation.

The whole leveled, fused floor is a skin over a shallow, hot, connected gut. Every roof slopes. Every alley is a watershed. Every gutter finds a mouth. What the net catches and the films don't drink and the blooms don't wick goes down, and down there it is shared, warmed by the rock, kept from evaporating, moved to where the stone is driest. The town drinks whole because it has one stomach.

You never see it. You hear it tick, tick, tick on basalt. You hear *plink* from net to gutter. You hear the low knock of drums contracting. Those are bowel sounds. The town is digesting fog.

If you dam a gutter for your own bowl you are not stealing water. You are giving the gut a cramp. It will route around you, and leave your corner dry thereafter.

---

And then there is the one in the center. The one the whole engine is keeping empty for.

Stand in the void court at fog-hour. Fifty paces to a side, scrupulously clean, four tall masts leaning slightly inward, humming so low you feel it in your teeth. Your shadow does not fall away from the sun. It falls toward the masts, all four ways at once, faint and smudged. Drop a pebble and it falls a fraction too slowly. Speak, and the hum answers half a tone higher, and your echo comes back *before* the sound has quite finished leaving your mouth.

Something lives in that anticipatory second.

It is not in the masts. The masts are just the teeth of a comb, tuning the air. What lives is the emptiness they comb.

The builders used the mountain like a lens — thin air, high heat, daily condensation, fresh sharp rock that still remembers being liquid — to make a pocket that is a little less insistent about gravity, a little looser about cause and effect. Heat rising, cool wind spilling over, fog condensing, stone ticking as it cools: all of it pumps that looseness, keeps the center cool enough, damp enough, quiet enough.

And in that kept quiet, weight thins. Sound leans forward. Shadows lean in.

The Keeper is light the way the films are slow. You never see it because it is clearest when there is nothing to see. On fog nights, if you do what you were told not to do — if you make noise in the court — you will feel it try to tune to you. A headache behind the eyes that lasts until the fog burns off. That is not punishment. That is attention. It heard a loud, heavy, oily, fast animal making future-noise in its nursery, and it leaned toward you the way a plant leans toward light, and its leaning hurt.

It feeds — if feeding is the word — on un-weight. On quiet. On the exact moment when hot breath hits cool wind and water hangs undecided between vapor and rain. It holds that moment with the four masts the way you would hold a soap bubble on a wire loop, and lives inside the skin of it.

That is why the houses are spaced absurdly far apart. That is why the plaza rings faintly under your boots. That is why the high wind shears off the rim and doesn't enter. Vastness between presences. Air left in. A city built for ten thousand that holds only you and the hum, because ten thousand loud heavy bodies would collapse the bubble — and because the bubble, once grown large enough, *is* a body.

The town is not a town with an inhabitant. The town is the organs, and the void is the organism.

---

You can live here. That is the last thing you learn, and the slowest.

Not as a citizen. There are no doors that will stay closed for you, no market, no well. There are only conditions.

Be as empty as the court. Don't fill the silence with calling out; no one will answer because answering would break the tuning. Move so your boots don't ring the glass. Learn the eleven-minute pace that doesn't stir the air.

Be as quiet as the stone. The stone is not silent — it ticks, it plinks, it hums in your teeth — but it never makes a sound before its time. Speak softly or not at all in the center, or you will be tuned, and it will ache.

Be as active as the stone. Repair without tools. Slope yourself toward the fog. Drink only what beads on your sleeve from the public throat, and let the rest run down. Let your oils stay off the walls. Let your shadow fall where it is pulled.

Do it long enough and the films will grow around your dry spot. The bloom in your fissure will learn your heat. The gut under the plaza will include your footsteps in its leveling. The Keeper will stop leaning toward you because you will have become, for an hour a day, light enough to overlook.

At first you thought the top was empty.

It is full — full of a life that needed emptiness so badly it built a city to keep it.
