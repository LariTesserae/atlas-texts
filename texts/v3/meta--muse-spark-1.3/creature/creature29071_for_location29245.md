At first you don't see them.

That's because they aren't separate from it. There's no crowd to meet at a gate, no green market square with faces turning toward you. The city is too dense and too wet and too loud for that kind of arrival. People are in it the way the wire-rats are in the pipes — inside, moving along channels, half-heard. You hear a whistle and a shift pours across a bridge and is gone into a machine hall before you can look up. A door opens and steam comes out and a woman with flour-white arms crosses with a basket on her head. A child darts between your legs after a paper boat in the street-rill. Then dripping, humming, kree-kree-kree again. You think: where are the people?

Then your eye learns the pace, and they emerge.

They are lowland stock, mostly, by blood, but plateau-shaped by life. Not tall. Not thin. Broad through the shoulders and hips from stairs — everything here is up and down ravine sides, up and down dovecote towers, up and down ladders slick with algae — and short-legged, sure-footed as goats, with a rolling, unhurried gait. Nobody strides. To stride is to slip. They amble, clog-shod, weight low, eyes half-down for weep-holes and cavy runs and sudden sheets of water across basalt.

Their skin never quite dries. That's the first thing you notice up close. Even the old men sitting dry under an eave have a sheen to them, a bath-warm damp at the temples, the upper lip, the back of the neck. Newcomers towel constantly for a week and then give up. The residents don't. They shine. Their hair, black or iron-grey or rust as the rock, is plastered or frizzed by steam depending on its humor, usually tied back under a broad, shallow hat of oiled felt — not for sun, but for drizzle and droppings. The hat is the uniform. Rich or poor, engineer or guano-scraper, everyone wears one, brim softened and white-spattered, crown dented by years of being hung on the same peg.

Their clothes are the color of the city because the city dyes them. Undyed wool that has taken up soot and mineral brown, oilcloth aprons gone green-black at the folds, canvas trousers patched at the knee with sailcloth, wooden clogs with the front worn to a shine by kicking grates clear. Nothing ever quite clean, nothing ever quite dirty — just wet, mended, salt-ringed. If you work the flumes you wear brass-toed boots and a tarred smock. If you work the mills you wear starch-dust like a second skin and your eyelashes are white with it. If you work the compost vaults harvesting the brick-red caterpillars you wear gloves to the elbow and you smell faintly sweet and corrupt, and no one minds, because everyone's nose is full of guano and hot oil anyway.

For they do not live alone here. That is the point you have to get through your lowland head. They are not the inhabitants. They are *an* inhabitant.

The true multitude, the ones who outnumber and outlast, are the animals, and the people live among them not as masters but as a large, slow, tool-using species that has negotiated terms.

Learn the terms and you learn the people.

The white river-birds own the air and the ledges. By law older than any council, no ledge wide enough for a nest is built smooth. Every cornice is notched. Every chimney pot has a perch-ring. Every funicular tower carries its ten-story dovecote like a crown, slatted and whitewashed and roaring softly with muttering. Twice a day the bird-rivers go out and come home to the marshes far below, and the whole city pauses under the shadow — not from reverence, exactly, but from practicality: you do not shout, you do not blow a valve if you can help it, you pull your hat down and wait. The birds bring fish-mud on their beaks and feet, and news of low water, and, most importantly, themselves. Their eggs are taken on a strict third-egg rule. Their fallen fledglings are eaten by dogs and cavies before a man can bend down. And their droppings — that chalky glare that whitewashes everything — are wealth. There are men and women whose whole lineage is guano: scrapers with long flat spades who work the factory roofs at dawn before the heat bakes it on, barrow-pushers who run it to the leaching sheds, weighers who mark the sacks for the cable-down to the lowlands where, for lack of green here, grain must be grown. A guano family smells of ammonia and keeps its accounts in a book soft as cloth from steam. Their children are the whitest birds themselves, bleached by dust, utterly unafraid of the edge.

The cavies own the ground. Long-eared rock cavies in ochre and slate, wild and tame at once, in warrens that predate the foundations poured over them. No one exterminates them because no one can, and because no one wants to. At feeding hour the mill-women come out with buckets of spent grain and starch-cake and pour it in long lines across the plazas, calling *tsu-tsu-tsu* through missing teeth, and the stone itself seems to move as hundreds pour from under walls, from culverts, from worn hollows in thresholds shin-deep from generations of the same path. The women know the matriarchs by sight — split ear, grey muzzle — and leave extra. In return, at culling time, the cavies give meat, dense and dark, and skins for bellows and aprons. A child grows up with a cavy asleep on his feet for warmth in winter drizzle, and an old man will step carefully, painfully aside for a nursing doe in a doorway rather than shoo her. Do not block a goat path, the saying goes, and do not block a cavy run. It is not kindness. It is traffic law.

The wire-rats own the pipes. Lean, clever, almost hairless from heat, running the warm dark like blood in veins. A newcomer sets a trap and his neighbor, without anger, takes it down. The rats keep the grease traps clear. They eat the moth-fur that would clog the weep-cavities. They find the leaks before men do by gathering where it drips sweet. Every kitchen leaves a saucer. Every powerhouse leaves a rat-hole unbricked. The rat-catchers of the lowlands would go mad here; here there are no rat-catchers, only rat-watchers, old women who can tell by the squeaking in the wall whether the dye-works main is running hot.

The moths own the night and the eaves. Great ash-grey mill moths the size of your hand, clinging in furred, breathing mats under every overhang, dusting everything with soft grey scales that get in your lungs and give old millers a particular wheezing cough they call moth-chest and wear almost proudly. No one brushes them off by day — why spend energy, they will return by dusk to the lamps and heat. When the pressure valves vent, they lift in a choking, silent blizzard and settle again, a little thinner. Their caterpillars, fat and blind and brick-red, seethe in the compost vaults and grease traps, eating starch waste and night-soil and paper, and are harvested by the sack with wooden rakes, boiled, salted, pressed into cakes that taste of mushrooms and fat and keep through the dry months. The harvesters are a caste apart, pink-armed, easygoing, always a little damp and smelling of earth that isn't earth. Their children play with empty cocoons like marbles.

Below them, in water: the pale eyeless fish and fat black newts of the cisterns and flumes, tended by the cistern-men. The cistern-men are the closest thing to priests. They go barefoot even in cool drizzle to feel vibration, carry hooked sticks and brass keys that open a thousand grilles, and speak little because they listen for water. They know every throat, every double wall and weep-cavity, every street-rill by its song after rain. You will see them at dusk, unhurried, clearing a grate while bats flicker over their heads eating the midges that rise from the foam. They nod to you once. If your roof catchment is fouled, they will tell you without blame and help you sweep it, because your neglect is their flood.

And above, at the changeover, the bats. Small, fast, numberless, pouring out of the expansion joints in the aqueducts as the birds come home, a second river going the other way. No one keeps them, no one harvests them. They are simply allowed the sky for an hour, and the children lie on warm roofs to watch them stitch the pink steam columns.

Even the dogs and goats have their offices. Feral dogs, yellow and ribby, asleep in warm culverts, who clean up after cullings and births and are never kicked awake, only stepped over. Goats with bells on the steepest ravine paths where no funicular goes, eating paper and ash and the bacterial slicks, giving thin sharp milk that is made into a cheese that keeps and tastes faintly of iron.

And under all of it, oldest of all, the films — mustard-yellow and copper-green slicks in the dampest chinks, black crusts a millimeter thick, pale salt-fur on the north side of cisterns. No one tends them. No one needs to. They were here before the first wall wept, and will be here when the last engine rusts to a stain. The children know not to scrape them idly, not because it's forbidden but because it's stupid — like picking at scab on kind rock.

The people, then, are maintenance people. That shapes their souls.

No one here talks about building a new city, or leaving, or growing big. There is no outward left. The plateau ends in air. The ravines are full. So they build inward: a niche chiseled for a new baby's cradle, a loft slung over a machine hall for a grandmother, another dovecote on top of a powerhouse when the birds increase. Iron is patched, not replaced. A wall that has subsided a finger's width in a lifetime is shimmed with volcanic glass and re-pointed, and the mason carves his grandfather's mark next to his own. Engines are oiled by the same families for two hundred years; a young apprentice learns the particular knock of *her* dynamo the way you learn a relative's cough.

Their speech is slow and loud and full of pauses for work-sound. They do not shout at shift-change; the birds are louder. They use hand signs on bridges, whistles in halls, a tap-code on pipes that the wire-rats have learned to ignore. At evening, when the cloud-lid pinks and the bird-rivers dim the sun and the gutters sing, they stand still on their roofs with brooms in their hands and simply look, while something — moth, cavy, white bird with mud on its beak — lands near them and looks back with complete indifference.

That indifference is mutual, and it is affectionate. It says: you are also a resident of warm stone. You also drip and hum and breed and mend. You will also, in time, be whitewashed and rounded and patient.

Stay a week and your skin will shine and you will stop toweling. Stay a month and you will learn not to cover a weep-hole, to leave the saucer for rats, to sweep your roof without being asked. Stay a year and your clogs will have worn their own small hollow in the basalt threshold where the cavies run, and an old cistern-man will nod to you once, as one maintenance animal to another, and you will understand that there is no forest to walk in here, no meadow, no green break — only this vast, kind, weeping table, covered to the horizon with layered work and wings, and the slow, wet, crowded people who have agreed, without ever saying it, to keep it dripping.
