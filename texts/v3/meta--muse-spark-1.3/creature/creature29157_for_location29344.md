At first you don't see animals at all.

You see texture. The warm pipe overhead looks upholstered, furred with something the color of wet paper. Then the upholstery breathes — not once, in and out, but in a faint, uncoordinated shiver across its whole surface, like wind going over wheat.

Then the wheat resolves into wings.

Moths. Thousands of them. High mountain winter moths, the dull grey-brown of soaked cardboard, shoulder to shoulder and then shoulder on shoulder, two and three deep on every surface that holds any heat at all. Thickest on the conduit bank overhead where the 400 volts leak a faint warmth through the steel. They pack in head-out, wings folded steep over their backs, antennae trembling almost imperceptibly, too cold to fly and too committed to leave. The ones nearest the caged bulb are dusted white with their own scales. They die there, stick for a while by the legs, drop into the webs below, and by the time you have blinked they have been replaced by others shuffling forward from the wall.

Under them, in the air you thought was empty, there is a second weather.

Midges — small, black, hump-backed — rising off the sump in a low cloud that never disperses and never lands. They breed in the black slime under the grate and live their whole lives in a cubic meter of wet cold, thousands of tiny wings making that high endless whine that finds the duct you came in through and sings in it. They don't bite. There is nothing warm-blooded enough, long enough, to be worth biting. They just rise, hang, fall back.

And where the midges are thickest, the hunters have learned to hang their traps.

House spiders, gone pale from how many generations since sun. They have strung grey sheet webs from pipe to valve stem to lamp cage, hammocks sagging under the weight of husks — moth wings, midge skins, legs. They sit upside-down in the middle, milk-white, legs tucked, waiting. In the corners where the pipes meet concrete, harvestmen have knotted themselves into beards, a hundred long legs braided into a single slow-writhing ball that loosens and tightens without ever coming apart. In the sodden mineral wool bulging from split lagging, silverfish ripple when your light moves — blind-quick flickers of pewter, eating glue and paper and mold.

Then, bigger than all of that put together, and so still you mistook them for part of the machine:

The doves.

Eighty, maybe a hundred. You can't count because they won't flush, they only shift. Rock doves crammed on every horizontal line the room offers — the cold feedwater main, the tracer line, the valve handwheels like iron artichokes, the lip of the drip pan, each other's backs. Puffed to twice their real size against a cold that never lifts, dirty white and blue-barred, the iridescent green at the neck gone grey with cement dust. Their feet are shockingly vivid, arterial red, wrapped around freezing steel. They watch you arrive on your knees with orange eyes and no alarm at all. Fear would cost heat.

They have learned the room the way a body learns a womb. All of them face inward, breast to the lukewarm tracer line, tails to the draft knifing in from the louver slit. They ride the shudder when a bird loses its footing — a sudden detonation of wings in a steel drum, thunder in a closet, a hundred claws ticking on metal — and then they settle, resettle, shoulder back to shoulder. A dove will squeeze out through the slit into that white terrible wind a kilometer above the clouds, and within minutes another squeezes in, or the same one returns, so the number on the pipes is always the same.

Tucked behind a flange where no nest should fit is the proof they mean to stay: a nest of plastic wire-ties, dead moth wings, and pulled mineral wool, and in it two squabs, almost translucent, pink skin showing through white pin down, gaping silently when you breathe near them. They do not peep. Peeping would be lost in the roar anyway.

Lower down, almost beneath notice, the other mammals.

Two mice. You will see them eventually if you stay past the point your fingertips go stupid. They move like slow thoughts along the top of the conduit, nose to tail in the moth furrow, pausing to gnaw the spiders' leavings. Gray, big-eyed, winter-coated absurdly thick for animals that have never been outside. They do not squeak. They drink condensed water off bolt heads.

And smaller still, what you will never see without a lens but is there in the dust on your hands: the mites in the dove dander that sifts constantly down, the pale fungus fruiting in pinhead dots on dead moth bodies and droppings, the black-green smear of algae and the thread of moss in a bolt-hole and the three fern sprouts no taller than your fingernail where the slit of daylight lands for an hour that never moves. White for lack of light, leaning forever toward the greenish bulb that has not flickered in eleven years. They have been that tall for as long as anyone remembers. Neither growing nor dying.

This is the loop they have closed:

Spiders eat midges. Mice eat the spiders' leavings and stunned midges. Doves pick the moths stunned by the light and snap at midges in the air without opening their puffed chests. Mites eat dove dander and feather dust. Fungus eats the dead — moth, midge, dropping, dander. Water in equals water out. Heat in from the cables equals heat lost through the skin to the storm. Nothing cycles because everything cycles so tightly it looks still.

Even their sound has joined the machine. They coo — not the soft park coo, but a hollow moan amplified by plate steel — and every few minutes they do it together, a low pulse that for half a second cancels a harmonic of the transformer hum so perfectly a pocket of true silence opens behind your eyes. If you stay long enough your own breathing joins it without your permission.

They are not visitors. They are residents of thickness, warm spots holding formation in a freezing, weeping, man-made womb bolted to the sky — puffed, shivering, resettling, waiting for nothing to happen, which is exactly what happens, continuously.
