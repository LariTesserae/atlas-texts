At first you think no one lives here.

That is what the noon wants you to think. The white is so total, the empty causeways so long, the plazas so clean and ringing, that any life feels like an afterimage. You walk and your footfalls die two steps behind you. A lizard does push-ups on a warm cobble and freezes. Nothing else moves. The towers are empty. The basins are empty. The work looks finished and left.

Then you learn to look where the green is.

Because no one lives in the white. People live the way frogs live — only where it drips and rots and piles.

You will be going down a long blind causeway with nothing on it, sweat joining the general weeping on your arms, and you will turn a corner around a fitted wall and walk straight into a wall of sound-that-is-not-sound: that thin high fizz, a single *pink* cut short, plink of water from leaf to stone. And there they are. Not many. Never many. That is the first law of bodies here.

A garden holds three, maybe four. No more will fit, though there is room.

An old woman on her knees in the mud trough between bricks, hands to the wrist in pounded clay and leaf compost. A boy up to his ankles in black cistern water, holding utterly still while dragonflies stitch the air three feet above his head. A girl with a coiled basket, picking papaya — one perfect, one collapsed to slime — setting the good one aside and leaving the rotten one exactly where it is. A toddler sitting in the cuff of ferns at the base of a post, staring at you with unblinking dark eyes, a thumbnail frog on his shoulder like a brooch, neither of them moving.

They are spare people. Barefoot, soles thickened to leather by hot lime and cobble. Skin sun-dark and always wet, not with sweat exactly but with the air itself. Hair cut short with chert, or bound back with fresh fiber that has not had time to fray and never will. A wrap of beaten palm, a loincloth, nothing that would hold heat. Their eyes are dark and gold-flecked from squinting into whitewash all their lives. Their hands are the most made thing about them — broad thumbs, plaster under the nails, adze-callus on the palm, fingertips worn smooth as river stones from smoothing.

They do not look up quickly. They do not startle. Sound does not travel, so they never learned to shout for each other. When the old woman wants the boy she does not call down the causeway. She waits until he passes. Everything here is passed, encountered, not summoned. If you speak loud your voice falls flat at three feet, embarrassed. You learn to stoop, to murmur. They murmur to each other, to the plants. Not prayer, exactly. Inventory.

*Hold this. Water here. Turn this leaf.*

The children will tell you, if you crouch and drink leaf-water with them, warm and sweet off a taro door, that there is no bottom. They say it the way children elsewhere say the sky is up — no mystery, no pride. Grandmother laid this brick. Under it, older hands laid that brick. Under that, wattle. Under that, something fitted before fingers. If you lift — and there are few loose bricks — you find damp, and brick, and water standing between. So they do not dig to find. They dig to lay.

That is what they do. That is all they do, and that is why at first you thought no one lived.

The old woman re-plastering a wall that does not need it. You watch her a long time because the light does not change and you have nothing else to measure by. She scoops pounded mud from a wooden bucket, pats it on, and with the side of her thumb smooths the same whorl of straw-fingerprint that is already there underneath. She is not repairing. Repair implies damage and damage implies time and time implies morning and evening. She is keeping. Her hand remembers what her grandmother's grandmother's hand did, and does it again at noon.

The boy replacing a lashing that is not worn. He saws with a flaked chert blade — still sharp from yesterday that has lasted a hundred yesterdays — cuts the fresh fiber, pulls the old one off though it is still stiff and pale, wraps the new one around the joist, knots it with teeth and fingers, snips the ends so they stick out stiff. It will look exactly the same as before. That is the point.

They all have early tools and they all look new. Polished stone adze with a handle polished by sweat to a dark shine. Wooden mallet. Fire-hardened digging stick. Bone needles for thatch, coiled baskets that weep water through their weave. No metal sings here. Nothing that could rust or ring or hold an edge longer than stone already does. Everything is hand-liftable, hand-tied, hand-set, because hands are what there are, sparse between gardens, moving quietly with buckets and scrapers along the low arches over black water that does not flow.

They do not advance. The vine does not advance down the causeway year by year, and neither do they. A courtyard that is jungle today will be jungle. A courtyard that is bare whitewash fifty paces down will stay bare, hot and clean and silent. They do not colonize the empty. They tend the full. One garden per knot of life. Much emptiness between things, on purpose.

And they are not alone in the tending, nor even the main tenders.

Where there is green, there is animal, crowded shoulder to shoulder, and the people make room inside it rather than clearing it. That is the second law. The frogs own the walls — emerald-backed, gold-eyed, throat pouches pulsing, so many you cannot put a hand down without covering one. The people put their hands down elsewhere. They have learned the map of frogs. The herons own the shallows, white and slate-gray, necks folded, standing motionless for a noon that never ends. The boy in the cistern does not chase them. The heron does not empty the canal of fish. The snakes, fat and slow, looped over warm bricks in the hotter shadows, are stepped over, not killed. Finches and sunbirds flick between banana leaves over the old woman's head and she does not look up. Mudskippers and small black crabs work the weeping edges at her knees. Clouds of sapphire and ember-orange dragonflies hang over the water she draws, never leaving their three feet of air.

It should roar, that much life in bath-heat. It fizzes. A thin high edge of hearing, and under it plink. Long silence. Plink. The people belong to that quiet. They do not chorus any more than the frogs do. Once in a while a child gives a single laugh and then stops, as if cut with a knife, and the thatch and water and hot air eat it.

If you stay — if an hour means anything when your shadow pools directly under you like spilled oil and does not creep — you begin to see what actually lives here is not the woman or the boy or the frog. It is the keeping itself, wearing them for a while.

Lean over a cistern with the girl to wash plaster from your hands and your face arrives a half-second late in the tea-black water, smiling when you are not. She does not flinch. She has seen her own late smile all her life. Walk the straight causeway from the white plaza to the stepped mound counting steps, walk it back and get a different number, and the toddler will tug your wrap and shake his head: don't count. The weeping rocks at the intersections — upright carved stones dripping steadily from their carved eyes and grooves into mossy basins that never overflow — they are not markers to the children. They are elders, doing what the old woman does: weeping without emptying, keeping level.

Growth and rot in place. Evaporation and condensation in place. Papaya perfect and papaya slime side by side. No argument being won.

The people know they are part of the floor. You learn to walk slowly between the sparse gardens, through the great quiet white, while frogs watch you from walls that will still be sharp when your bones are fitted somewhere below as part of the laying — and the old woman, without looking up from her smoothing, pats a place beside her in the trough, for your hands, at noon, because that is what you do.
