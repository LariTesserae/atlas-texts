At first you think nothing lives here.

That is what the brightness does. It flattens everything to white and green, and the green looks like covering, like paint on stone. You have to sit long enough to get cold in your bones to see that it is moving.

Then you see that the wall is drinking.

Close up, with your face inches from it because you are clinging to a cable-root with numb hands and trying not to breathe too loud, the pelt resolves. It is not one thing. The silver-green frost-moss is a tight thatch of a million tiny stars, each holding a bead of ice in its center like an eye. Under it the lichen-wool hangs in beards, gray and damp, and under that something darker is working — mites, the size of a freckle, black and amber, moving in slow tides through the thatch. You hear them before you see them, or rather you hear what they allow: the faint tick tick as condensation pearls and is sucked up. The plants drinking. Put your ear to the moss and it is louder. A faint, endless sipping.

If you lift a sail-leaf — and the leaves here are not leaves the way you know leaves, they are tents, blankets, sails translucent enough to see your hand through, veined white where the water runs — if you lift one gently, beetles scatter underneath. Black, flat-backed, slow with cold. They cluster where the leaf meets the stem to stay out of the glare. The pickers eat these by the handful, raw. They taste like fat and frost.

The tendrils are faster than any of it. Sit still for an hour and you will see one near your knee uncurl. Pale green, blind, questing. It lengthens in jerks you can almost hear, finds a crack in the basalt or a purchase in dead fiber, and thickens as you watch. The whole hanging forest is doing this, everywhere, all the time. Growing out and down and in. Away from the scoured white sky and into the damp air of the void.

And then, once you learn to see movement that isn't wind, you start to see animals that aren't plants. Or plants that aren't animals. Here it is hard to tell.

The wall-goat first. You will swear it is a hummock of moss until it blinks.

It is shaggy and low and the exact gray-green of old lichen-wool, because old lichen-wool is growing in its fur. In the folds behind its ears, in the dewlap under its chin, little gardens have taken hold. Its droppings are green with spores. Its hooves — split, splayed, soft-edged — are made for this impossible vertical bog, spreading its weight so the moss does not tear. It moves the way the pickers move: test, pause, shift. It probes with a forefoot before it commits, feeling for the lie. Green lies about depth. Sometimes it dislodges a little avalanche, a puff of green that falls and falls into brightness and is gone, and the goat freezes, head down, and waits for the sigh that would mean the whole meadow is going. Mostly there is no sigh. It goes back to cropping sail-leaf edge with its black lips, delicately, leaving the veins.

There are very few of them. A band may share a range with one goat for a whole season and know its cough. They do not hunt it unless starvation is already in the camp. To kill a goat is to lose your way across.

The moths are rarer still. Rare, the pickers say, like a blessing.

You see it first as weather. A white change in the light along the wall, a slow thickening a half kilometer off. Then it resolves into wings. Wider than you are tall. White, furred, silent. A blanket-moth riding the updraft, never flapping if it can help it, just tilting those vast sails to stay in the lift. Frost trails off its wing-hairs and glitters behind it. It is grazing. When a curtain-forest boils off a spore-storm — that vast silent glittering cloud that obscures the far wall — the moth hangs in it and breathes. You can see its abdomen pulse. Its legs, tucked tight and black against the white, comb the spores from its own fur. If you are very still on your cable-root, it may pass close enough that you feel the displacement, a soft push of cold air, and hear nothing at all. Nothing. A creature that size should roar. Here, the moss would eat it.

They go down and do not come up. The pickers say this flatly. On a clear blue-dusk when the far wall stands out, you can sometimes see one tip over the edge of a terrace, fold its wings, and drop deliberately into the white mist below where the next rain is being made. None return. Whether they lay something down there, or dissolve, or feed what is growing the bottom, no one knows. No one follows.

And then the people. You find their marks days before you find them, because they are thin here, like the animals.

A cairn of sharp black basalt teeth stacked three-high on a moss hummock: safe anchor. A vine cut cleanly with stone, weeping sap frozen into amber beads. A rope left hanging — not made rope, but chewed rope, harvested green, softened in mouths, braided while still pliable by hands that knew it would only hold a season before the wall reclaimed it.

They are small, not by birth but by erasure. The cold and the climb pare everything unnecessary away. Broad across the shoulders from hauling themselves hand-over-hand up living fiber, thin in the leg from never walking far on anything flat. Their faces are wind-burned to the color of old bark, their eyes pale and permanently squinted from glare; even in the blue dusk they squint. Frost has taken the tips of fingers, ears, toes in every adult — little blackened blunt ends. Their hair, if uncut, mats with spores and greens at the roots. Lichen finds them too.

You hear a camp before you see it: not voices — they do not shout, they whisper as a habit, as manners — but small close sounds. The creak of a platform of dead stem laid crosswise over a dip. The papery touch of sail-leaves pinched together at the edges and lashed to a cable-root so they keep growing into a watertight pod. A child coughing smoke. A bone hook clinking on basalt.

Six pods, maybe eight. A fire-pit lined with basalt chips where they burn dried vine-pith and rendered beetle-fat. It gives little heat and a thin gray thread of smoke that rises straight up for a kilometer in the still air. That is how you find each other across the emptiness: threads. At noon you count threads to know you are not alone in the wound.

They are pickers and pruners because the place demands it. In the morning after rain they move out along the braids with long poles, probing every step, and cup the water from giant leaves before it evaporates — sucking it through chewed fiber, spitting it into bladders. They comb sap-sugar, slice fiber, gather beetles under leaves with quick numb hands. They prune, too, deliberately: cutting back a sail that shades a sap-seep, thinning a terrace that has grown too heavy and wet, listening with their knees for that deep soft sigh that means it is about to tear loose and go down turning into the white. Sometimes they can save an acre by cutting a cable. Sometimes they just get clear and watch it go.

They build to be abandoned. Every lashing is quick-release. Every platform can be left. When the meadow tears — and it will — they go up or sideways along the wall to the next cairn left by the last band, and start again. Their wealth is what can be carried hanging: stone blades that hold an edge in cold, combs, a hand-loom for weaving sail-fiber into furs, amber.

At night in the pod, cold to the bone despite the fire, you listen with them to drip, creak, breath. The frost forming on the sail-leaves with a faint crackling like tiny glass breaking. Your own blood. And very far off, softened by three kilometers of bright air, that dull *whump* as something large, somewhere, lets go.

They do not pray. If they have a god it is quiet. Don't shout, they tell you, not because something will hear you, but because the quiet is what holds the hanging forests together.

In the morning your rope is stiff with ice. The tendril near your hand has found its purchase overnight. A moth, high and white, hangs motionless against the glare. Below you, nothing but light and falling green and white mist being breathed.
