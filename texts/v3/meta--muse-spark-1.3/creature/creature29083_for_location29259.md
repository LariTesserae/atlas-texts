They are not where you look first.

Walk the floor and for a long while you think nothing lives here at all. A half mile of cold shingle. A tarn flat as iron with no ripple. A cushion of olive moss unmarked. Your boots are too loud. The cold gets through your soles. Then a shadow crosses the gravel — then another — then the air tears — and the sky is full of them.

That is the rule in the Hollow: low saturation, then saturation all at once.

Mostly, they are birds. In numbers the valley floor should not be able to feed.

Auks and kittiwakes, big sooty ravens, and others that are like them until you look twice — duskier, heavier-billed, more ragged at the edge, as if the dim light here had smudged the field guide. Tens of thousands of them. They pour off the cliff in ribbons, catch the basin air and circle it in a slow screaming gyre, wing to wing to wing, then settle again and cover the scree in a shifting black carpet. Startle — at nothing you can see — and they all lift together with a dry clatter you feel in your chest.

They live shoulder-to-shoulder on the bare rock. Every narrow basalt ledge is packed with bodies, jostling, calling, shuffling a half-step left and right. The cliff faces are streaked white vertically with guano, the same streaks year after year, ledge vacated and ledge re-filled. They lay, where they lay, on stone itself. No twig nests, no mud cups, no burrow towns. A warm hollow worn by breast feathers in the cold rock, an egg balanced against the wind. That is enough, because the Hollow does not ask them to stay — only to return.

For they do not really live off the Hollow. That is the secret of the noise. The wind spills over the rim and drops what it was carrying from the coast: fish, plankton, blown insects, salt and offal and cold sea-smell. The birds go up to meet it, feed out over the plain and come back heavy, and manure the stone beneath them. Their droppings feed the moss. The moss holds the damp. The damp keeps the rock cold, the cold keeps the mist, the mist keeps the light sideways and weak. Frost shatters as much as rain smooths, so the scree never rounds. Sharp stays sharp.

On the ground, between the unconnected pools, live the others — the ones who live off the birds, and off each other.

In the tarns: crabs. Grey-green, slow in the cold, clicking through black mud in water only a few yards across. How they got from the sea to these isolated iron-grey saucers no one saw, but they hatch and are eaten and hatch. A crab eaten, another hatches.

Over the damp mud: insects in cold clouds. Midges and black flies that lift all at once when your foot sinks, hang, and settle again on sedge and skin. They breed in the seep-apron at the foot of the walls where condensation never quite dries.

In the sedge: grey mice and voles. You hear them before you see them, rustling in the knee-high willows — those stunted, wind-combed things with small hard leaves — and in the tufts around the tarns. They are the nibblers who keep the green in equilibrium. The tufts never spread, never shrink. Nibbled to exactly themselves.

Padding from hollow to hollow: the foxes. Gone pale and shaggy with the permanent chill, almost white, almost silent. They do not den in towns. One fox, alone, working the damp line, nose to peck-marks and footprints, taking a vole, taking an addled egg rolled from a ledge, taking a crab left too shallow. They vanish behind a boulder and the gravel swallows their steps.

Stalking the shallows: gulls. Big, patient, loud even inside loudness. Floating on their backs in the larger pools, where the water holds a little longer: a few sea-otters.

And here is the thing you only see if you stand still for an hour and let the roar become pressure in your ears: they all work with their hands.

Watch one gull for five minutes. She picks a crab from a tarn, labors up thirty feet beating hard in the cold air, and drops it — not anywhere — precisely on the one flat anvil stone in the middle of dry gravel. Crack. Missed. Up again, down again, until the shell splits. Then she eats, and leaves the stone and the shards where they lie. Tomorrow a different gull will use the same stone.

A raven hops the scree with a short stick held crosswise, stops at a crack, probes. Too short. Drops it. Tries another, bites it twice to length, probes again, fishes a grub. Leaves the tool behind.

In the large pool an otter rolls, lays a rounded beach pebble in the fold of its chest, hammers a mussel against it — the same pebble for days, carried, then lost, then replaced by any other pebble.

Crows along the seep pry limpets and mussels with shards of basalt, with a splinter of willow. Three bites to shape it and it is left.

It is everywhere once you see it. Hand-work, without workshops. No stores, no cairns, no lasting make. A stone picked up, used, dropped. The Hollow remains, in any lasting sense, unmade, except for footprints and droppings and peck marks.

That is why nothing changes. The flocks should eat themselves out and leave. The moss should spread and fill the pools. The cliffs should soften. They don't. A ledge empties, another bird lands. A crab is dropped on the anvil, another crab hatches in the tarn. The white streaks stay in the same places. The sedge stays the same size. The roar never gets louder or quieter.

They are a crowd that replaces itself perfectly. Come back in ten years, in a hundred, and a different gull will be dropping a different crab on the same stone, under the same grey that never clears, in the same cold that shows your breath at midday.
