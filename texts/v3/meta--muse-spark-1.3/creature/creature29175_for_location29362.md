They are not the first thing you see. In the Works you see hands before you see people.

Shoulders first — a density in the dark a little warmer than the air, breath going white off it. A back bent under a block. A forearm crossing lamplight. Then, if you stay, the rest of them resolves.

They are human. That is the strange thing to keep returning to. Not pale cave-things with blind eyes, not monsters. Men and women and children, stocky with work, gone soft-pale from a life without sun and gone red at the joints from constant damp. Their skin never quite dries. If you stand close you see the beads form on their forearms as they work, the same beads that form on stone. They wipe them away without thinking, on wool, on hide, on each other.

Hunch is their shape. Everyone hunches. Partly against the cold that pools in the basin, partly from the platforms — the ceilings are low, the ladders narrow, the walkways strung so close overhead that a grown man cannot straighten. Partly, you realize after an hour with your own neck wet, to shed water. Shoulders up, head down, so the drip from planks and troughs and beams runs off the oilskin or the matted wool instead of down the collar. Children learn it before they walk well. The old never lose it, even asleep.

Their clothes are weight. Unwashed wool felted to armor, hide aprons stiff with lime and tallow, wraps of the same black timber-dust cloth over and over. Nothing ever dries here, so nothing is ever light. Everything smells of wet sheep and smoke and the sour-sweet of bodies kept too close. They do not mind the touch. They cannot afford to. A boy will climb over his sleeping sister to reach a lashing. Two men will pass chest-to-chest on a ladder with a dressed block between them, breathing each other's breath white. A grandmother will scrape condensation from an awning with a shell into a jar while a stranger's boots pass inches from her head, and neither flinches. Distance is a thing from up the trail. Down here there is only near.

Look at their hands and you know them. Hands are their eyes.

Broad, split-nailed, white at the fingertips from cold and hammer-shock, calloused into leather across the palm but tender and almost prehensile at the fingertips. In ember-light you watch a boy take apart the walkway under your feet by feel alone, finding the rotted plank among sound ones by its give, by its colder sweat, working a wedge with a maul in total dark to a rhythm he learned at five. Behind him, another boy follows re-lashing, pulling hair-rope — yes, hair, much of it human, cut and twisted in the idle minutes that aren't idle — tight against the stretch the damp puts into everything. They talk very little while they do it. A short hoarse *hek — ho*, *hek — ho* when hauling. A click of tongue to mean *hold*. A hammer code: two quick taks for *again*, a scrape for *slack*. Speech is expensive in air you are trying to keep warm.

Their eyes are for ember. Large-pupiled, wet-shining, slow to move, good at catching the dull orange of a copper pit or a tallow dip in a clay cup. By that light you see faces for a second at a time: soot in the creases, limestone dust white in the eyebrows, lips cracked from breathing cold. They squint at distance — there is no distance — but up close they see absurdly well. A woman mending a tarred hide trough will find a pinhole leak by the way lamplight catches it, press it shut with a thumb of warm pitch. An old man tending a cistern will see the shiver when a single drop hits black water two feet down and know, from the ring, how full it is. For the rest they listen.

Because hearing is the other sight here. The Works is too loud for outsiders and exactly loud enough for them. Under the continuous *tak-tak-tak* of stone, under the creak and groan of rope and timber, they pick out the separate musics: the low *tok* of water into water that means a cistern is alive, the higher *plink* of a miss, water lost to stone, the gurgle of skin-to-trough pouring, the wrong-ringing chisel that means a flake is about to shear. A child asleep in a hammock strung under a walkway does not wake when feet pass above her face because the rhythm is right. She would wake instantly if a trough tipped a finger's breadth and the pattern broke.

They are all ages, because no one is exempt. The rule you feel within minutes — *you build, or you get wet, or you fall through* — is not cruelty, it is physics. Stone settles. Hide stretches. Daub slumps before it cures. A trough hung wrong spills a night's drinking water onto sleepers. So they maintain without pause, in shifts that are not called shifts. Toddlers carry chips away in folded hands. The boys you saw are six, eight, already journeymen of rot and lashing. Women nurse with one arm and scrape sweat from hides with the other. The very old, too bent for ladders, sit by cisterns and eaves with jars and shells and rags, farming the air drop by drop, coaxing beads off cold undersides, feeding the blind white fish finger-long flickers that live in the black water to eat the wrigglers. That is holy work, though they would not call it holy. Without the fish the water sickens. Without the water they die.

And the animals — there are so few you learn their names by function. The thin pale rats that run the trough rims are not pets and not quite pests. They find leaks first. Where a rat stops to lick, a joint has failed. No one chases them far. The moths, ash-colored, torpid knots furred with damp high on poles, are living hygrometers; when their wings lift a fraction, the air is turning to give water. The fish are wards. Everything else that lives here was brought in as timber or bone long ago — the great split bones made into troughs, the antler picks still pecking stone though the deer are a story — and used until it shines.

Eat? You wonder after a while what they eat, with no green anywhere, no soil to stand on. They eat what comes down the stone-plank trail on backs: grain in skins, dried meat, cakes of tallow for lamp and mouth both. They supplement with fish when a cistern must be thinned, with rat when a rat is stupid. Hunger is a background ache they work through. Thirst would kill faster, so thirst organizes everything. The cisterns, skin-covered against soot, are property and prayer at once. No one steals water. To tip a trough is worse than to strike a man, and punished faster — a tool pressed harder into your hand until you have repaid in labor.

For that is the other rule you were told: there are no watchers. If you stand still someone — a girl with lime under her nails, a one-eyed man with a copper hammer — will press a chisel or a rope-end or a jar into your hand without hostility and without asking. To be warm and breathing in the Works is to be part of the weather. Your breath, your sweat, your blood-heat off your skin puts moisture into the cold air. Later, on cold stone, it will condense and fall and be drunk. They know this the way farmers know rain. They will make use of you gently and absolutely, and in a day your shoulders will hunch and your clothes will double in weight and your hands will learn the knot.

Stay longer and you see the deepest adaptation, the one that is not in the body.

No one directs, and yet everything repeats. The same three-pole joint, the same inward lean of the slate towers like reeds, the same trough hung at the same angle valley-wide. Not from a plan — ask and they shrug, they learned from watching hands — but because damp and cold and dark only allow one shape to stand long. The Works is teaching them, over generations, to build itself through them. They have stopped fighting the wrong echoes, the drip that runs sideways along a rope by capillary to fall somewhere else, the hammer heard half a second before the arm falls. They build for it now. They hang troughs to catch where water *wants* to go, not where it should fall. They lash loose enough for the stretch. They listen for the building settling and answer with a wedge.

In that sense they are not inhabitants separate from the place. They are its maintenance organ. Warm, brief, steaming, endlessly replacing plank and lashing and daub so that cold surfaces can go on sweating and black cisterns can go on ringing *tok — tok — tok* in the dark where no river runs.

If you leave — up the plank and stone trail into thinning cold — the last thing you will feel is not the hammering fading. It doesn't fade. It is the beads forming again on the backs of your own hands, your own body still giving water to the air, and the understanding, suddenly bodily, that you have for a little while been one of them: a human condenser, a layer in the layering, built to build and to sweat and to be drunk.
