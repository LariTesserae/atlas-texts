What lives there doesn't look like much at first. Just noise.

You drop below the lip and the sound hits you before the green does — that layered roar — and for a minute you think it's one thing. One swarm. One mind buzzing. Then you sit, and your eyes adjust to the smallness, and it comes apart into islands, and each island comes apart into mouths.

Start with the smallest and most numerous, because they set the pitch.

The midges. Each moss cushion holds its own column, a living needle three or four feet tall, thousands of bodies rising and falling without breaking rank. Up close they are not grey but clear-winged, gold-eyed, their wings beating too fast to see. They hold station in air so thin you are panting, and they do it by never resting. If you wave your hand through a column it parts around your skin like water around a stone and then closes behind you, the same shape, the same place. They are not following scent. They are following tone. All the columns in the bowl lean — five degrees, ten — not toward the noon sun hammering straight down, but sideways, toward the nearest black arch. Put your ear to the peat and you feel why: the low hum coming up through your soles. The midges ride the standing edge of it.

Under them, in the moss, the crickets saw. You almost never see them. They are the color of wet bark, long-antennaed, living down inside the cushion where it is dark and 100% humid even when the stone five steps away is hot enough to burn your palm. They sing without stopping, one band of the roar, and when you part the moss to find one it stops and you see only the tremble where it went deeper.

Then the flies. Wet-motor green, bottle-blue. They own the pitchers. The pitcher plants crowd every true hollow, fat-throated, pink-lipped, each cup half full of brown soup — drowned wings, dissolved midges, rain from this morning's sweat off the arches. The jewel flies walk the rim drunk, slipping, falling in, buzzing out. And with them the jewel wasps, blue-black, thumbnail-big, armored and fearless. They should sting, they should scare, but here there is too much sugar to defend. They crawl inside the pitchers headfirst to steal nectar, emerge dripping, clean their antennae with their forelegs while a frog watches from an inch away.

The frogs are the citizens.

Tiny amber frogs, knuckle-small, translucent at the throat and belly so you can see the heart ticking. They sit in rows. Literally in rows along the base of each arch where the drip is steadiest, shoulder to shoulder, eyes like wet black beads, throats puffing in and out. Dozens. Maybe hundreds if you count the ones tucked in moss pocks. They don't hop when your shadow falls. There are too many of them to afford fear individually — a predator can't eat *all* of them, so none of them bothers. In the daily twenty-minute shower they call, and it isn't random. Three pulses. Pause. Three pulses. Pause. Sit long enough for your own breath to slow and you realize the pause is the arch hum. They have tuned their throats to the vibration coming through the peat. Three beats, pause. It lets a male be heard through a hundred others. Their whole life is timed to saucers: they lay in rock-pocks no deeper than a teacup, tadpoles like commas that must hatch, grow legs, and get out before evening evaporates the pool or a wasp drinks it. Most don't. That's fine. The bowl makes more.

Above them, the kings. Two, sometimes three hummingbirds with copper throats that catch fire when they turn. In thin air their wings have to work impossibly hard — you can hear the slap of each stroke over the insects — so they are furious about energy. They own an island each, a dwarf tree crown with white orchids, and they scream at each other all day, needle-sharp chips that cut straight through the insect roar, and scream at you when you stand up. They drink from the orchids, which smell impossibly of vanilla and rot at once — sweet to call the bird, carrion to call the fly, both pollinators in one. They eat midges on the wing, they snatch frogs off leaves, they steal wasps from pitchers. When one dies — and with that metabolism they burn out fast — mites and beetles unmake it by afternoon. You will find a single copper feather in the moss, and if you watch, pale specks converge on it, and by the time the steam rises after the shower it is lace, then dust.

Under the leaves, the shy ones. Mirror-backed beetles, green-gold, that flash like signal mirrors as they bulldoze through litter; their backs reflect heat, keeping them cool enough to hunt at noon. Pale blind centipedes, pink-white, threading the black underside of moss, eating eggs. And the ripple. You will see it once, maybe twice, if you are still: a wave moving through a moss cushion with no wind, a tunnel pushing up from below. Underneath is the only mammal in the cup, something mouse-sized, grey-brown, long-nosed, almost hairless on its feet so it can feel vibration. It drinks drip-water, eats centipedes, is eaten by nothing except time — too fast for the birds to catch in the tangle. It lives its whole life without ever touching bare stone.

And the plants that make the rooms they all live in.

The moss itself is the foundation animal-vegetable — deep emerald and rust cushions that sink to your ankle and hold your footprint for a full minute before slowly exhaling it back. It is a sponge that never quite dries. Out of it rise the dwarf trees, knee-high, ancient. Their trunks are twisted horizontal, bark polished by a wind that used to scour the plateau a million years ago and no longer reaches down here, but their bodies remember and grow sideways anyway. Their crowns are dense waxy leaves to hold water, and the orchids. In the wettest shade, sundews glitter with glue, each drop holding a tiny sun and a struggling midge. A fern unrolls from a crack that never sees direct light, one frond a day, slow in this fast place.

None of them live alone. That's the point. You can stand in one spot for an hour and watch the whole wheel turn: fly drowns, frog snaps fly, bird takes frog, feather falls, mites take feather to moss, moss feeds pitcher, pitcher feeds fly. Fast because warm, because wet, because small. The edge of a moss island advances a millimeter over grit in the morning and retreats a millimeter by evening. A pitcher opens pink and slick. A pitcher rots black and breeds mosquitoes.

What holds it together is not food. It's the tuning.

The dwarf trees lean toward the arches, though light is overhead. Roots follow condensation, yes, but also vibration — water gathers most where the cold black loops sing deepest. The midge columns lean the same way. The frogs keep time. When the daily cloud snags and dumps and then tears away, the arches cool and ping — a clear glass note, three of them slightly out of unison — and for one full second every insect in the bowl goes silent at once. Total cut. As if listening. Then the roar slams back louder, as if released.

Stay a whole afternoon and your own body joins. Your pulse, laboring in thin air, slips without your permission into three-beat time. Three beats, pause. You only notice it when you climb back out, up those six or seven meters into the wind and the huge flat indifference, and for five or six steps on hot granite your heart is still keeping bowl-time before the dry air wipes it clean.

That is what lives there. Not a list. A single tuned hunger, wearing a hundred small bodies, drinking its own breath over and over around three cold black loops, eating itself alive in the ordinary sun so nothing ever has to leave.
