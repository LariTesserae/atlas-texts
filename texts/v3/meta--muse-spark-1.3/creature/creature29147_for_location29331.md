They do not live here the way people live in other places.

At first you think no one does.

For minutes you see only stone and water and light doing their work, and the town looks like an instrument left out in the cold — strung, tuned, waiting. Then a door sighs somewhere above you and seals again before you can look up. A breath of warmer air crosses the cleft and is gone. A figure crosses the highest needle-bridge, dark against the sky-slit, moving with the unhurried economy of someone who has counted their breaths on stairs.

That is how you learn to see them: not as a crowd, but as brief corrections in the quiet.

They are few. The place was built for more — you can feel it in the long empty terraces, the dark level with its shuttered eyes, the platform that holds only a sealed container and a coil of cable, as if density were being saved, like water, for later. The ones who are here have thinned themselves to fit the crack. Perhaps sixty. Perhaps a hundred. You never see enough at once to count.

They are high people, cold people. Lean from the thin air, red-burnt at the cheek and lip where hoods don't cover. They move slowly because twenty steps winds you here and they know it. They do not hurry the stairs, they do not fill the air with talking. Voices, when you hear them, are low and close, almost murmured, because in the cleft there is no wind to shout against. Your breath fogs and hangs, and theirs does too — they live inside small, brief clouds that follow them from level to level.

From a distance they all look the same: dark insulated shells, matte as the pipes, hoods up, gloves on, boots soft-soled to not bruise the etched channels. Up close they come apart. The maintenance woman with pale, cracked knuckles who will put her bare hand flat on a walkway to show you — *see, neither warm nor cold* — while the rail beside it furs white. The two technicians crouched for an hour over an open panel, foreheads almost touching, watching amber light move, saying almost nothing, handing each other tools without looking. The old man on his terrace with a wide soft broom, sweeping seep back into the runnel, back into the runnel, an endless task he does not seem to want to end. Children — there are children, impossibly — who run full-tilt across a bridge that should bounce and doesn't, laughing in short, high-altitude bursts, because to them phase-lock is not physics, it is floor.

They are not farmers. Nothing green will grow here and they do not ask it to. They do not hunt — there is nothing to eat; the vulture knows that and only tilts over once a day to check. What comes to eat comes from below, on the silent lifter the size of a door that rises without rotor or push, bearing sealed containers, ice-cores in padded racks, folded cloth, medicine. They take what the lifter brings and leave the platform empty again. That is their manners: use, and clear.

What they *do*, all day, is tend.

Tend the drip, not stop it. Every child learns early to walk a half-step to the side of the drip-lines, to never block a channel with a boot or a bag, to read the tick and ping of midday melt as weather. The old man's sweeping is not cleaning, it is steering. The technicians are not repairing so much as retuning — listening for when a pod has drifted a hair off the stone's resonance after a night creak, coaxing it back a hand's breadth off the rock where it floats on cold shimmer. The maintenance woman talks about the mountain the way you talk about a large animal you share a house with: *if it shifts, we shift.* No bolts. No fight.

They have learned not to warm the air. That is the strangest intimacy. In any other cold town people would huddle around heat, make a glow, make a roar against the night. Here there are no heaters, no hum. The walkways keep themselves free of frost by some negotiation you stop trying to parse. A kettle left on a ledge stays just liquid without a cord. They wear the warmth on their bodies and give none to the sky. At night when it drops hard and every bead locks to glass, they go inside their faceted pods — windowless but for that one deep eye — and close the numbered doors, and the windows come on far apart, warm yellow, like camps that have agreed not to crowd each other. You can stand on the central bridge and feel how deliberately alone they are, together. Leave room for emptiness. That is not loneliness to them. It is respect.

And because they are so spaced, they notice small lives out of proportion.

The white cat on Level Three, for instance. The only cat. How it got up here no one can quite agree, or why it stays where no mouse or bird will ever come. It is thin, long-haired, perpetually slightly damp, with pink skin showing at the ears from cold. It cries thinly in the stairwells and someone always opens a door. It has the run of the warm paths, it sleeps on cable coils, it is photographed by every visitor and indulged by people who otherwise indulge nothing. To hear that cry in all that mineral silence — stone, water, sky — is to understand what they have kept themselves from: something soft, unnecessary, alive for no engineering reason.

The other residents are not human and no one pretends they are pets.

In the wall niches live the crabs — small silver automata, folded flat as lichens would be if lichens lived here. When a gutter chokes on ice — and it always chokes on ice, there are no leaves here to blame — one unfolds, patient, many-jointed, picks the channel clear with absurd delicacy, and folds back. They do not speak, they do not wander. Children crouch to watch them and do not touch. The lifter, too, has its moods. It parks itself at night against the wall and goes still, but by day it rises past you so gently your hood barely stirs, displacing air like a held breath let out. The people treat these things the way the old man treats his broom: extensions of the tending, not servants.

If there is a god here, it is water moving under ice.

You hear it best at night when everything else has stopped. Stand very still after the windows have come on and the stars have filled the slit — sharper up there than they have any right to be — and under the deep infrequent creak of young rock settling, new stone learning its weight, there is a faint continuous hiss: water still going, in the pipes, in the braided sleeves climbing uphill without pumps, in the basins linked by runnels, unwilling to stop. They listen for that. You see them pause on stairs to listen for that. Keep the water moving, keep the stone bare, keep the quiet.

That is why they stay in a crack you could walk past without seeing, when the valley below is easier, greener, lower. They are not stranded. They are holding.

A whole town, stacked in the walls, sparse as stars, clinging high and cold and dripping — alive precisely because it asks so little: not to own the stone, not to dry it or warm it or cover it, only to live a hand's breadth off it, and let it weep.
