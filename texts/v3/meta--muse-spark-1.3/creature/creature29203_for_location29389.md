The first thing you notice is you don't notice them.

Not at first. You come up onto the rim and look down and you think the town is empty — no smoke standing up, no movement big enough to read at a distance through fog. Only the amber dots, the wet glint, the moss swallowing the roofs. Then your eye adjusts to the scale of the place, which is low, and you see them everywhere, low like everything else.

They are short. Not dramatically — no one would call them a different people on the road below — but after a generation or two up here no one wastes height against the wind. They move with short steps, knees a little bent, shoulders forward, the walk of people who are always ducking an eave or stepping over a threshold that has risen while they slept. Broad in the chest. You hear it before you see it: a deeper breath, unhurried, that doesn't catch the way yours does. Children run without panting. Old men climb the stair-alley without stopping. Their lungs have learned the thinness.

Pale, in a way lowland paleness isn't. Not sick — washed. The light never hits them straight, only filtered through linen cloud, so no one tans, no one burns. Skin goes the color of underside fern, milk-white with green in it, and cheeks stay permanently high with cold blood. Hair darkens with damp and is rarely fully dry. Everyone smells faintly of what they work in — peat-smoke, iron-water, vats — and underneath, peat and wool lanolin and the sweet rot of moss that gets into cuffs and never comes out.

They dress for condensation. Not for rain, which comes and goes, but for permanent wet. Thick felted wool, undyed or dyed in the lichen colors that are the only bright things allowed: sage, sulphur, rust, a deep bruised purple from the dye-works that never washes out of forearms. Over that, tarred canvas aprons, oilskin shoulders, hoods that sit close to the head. Boots are soft, wide, laced high — not for marching but for squelch. You can tell a local by sound: not clack but *suck-squelch, suck-squelch* on the saturated path, unhurried. Strangers slap. Locals suction.

And they are quiet. That is not manners, it's physics. The moss eats shouting. So they stopped shouting generations ago.

Their voices are low and a little hoarse, kept at the level of dripping. A mother doesn't call a child across the pan; she clicks her tongue twice, or whistles through teeth, and the child hears it because children here learn to hear the way lowland children learn to look. Everyone listens. You see it in how they pause mid-work, head cocked, to place a sound: which eave is dripping fast, which gutter is about to overflow, whether that turbine thrum has dropped a half-tone, whether the cable bucket is coming loaded or empty by the pitch of its groan. Conversation happens in doorways, shoulder to shoulder, murmured, with long pauses where they just listen to water together. To raise your voice in the street would be like stripping moss off a roof — exposing something.

If you watch long enough, the town resolves into its trades, and the trades resolve into bodies.

The Catchers, first. Mostly women, mostly old, though every child does a turn. They tend the condensation-nets strung between poles on the saddles and rims — great harps of fine wire that sing faintly in wind. They walk them at dusk and before dawn, when fog is thickest, with a hooked pole to tap the frame so the beads let go, and a ear for the tinkle of drain into gutter. They know nets the way sailors know sails: which mesh ices first, which sags with dew-weight, which has begun beading on the underside, upward, wrong. They don't comment on that wrongness. They just turn the net a quarter-turn and tighten the lower stay, as their mothers did. Their hands are white and puckered permanent, fingertips soft as newt-belly from always being wet.

Then the Menders. Because nothing is made here, really. The rock is too old for ore, the moor too low for trees. Everything comes up the switchbacks — grain, timber, new wire, coal for supplement — and everything that breaks stays up here to be fixed. The foundry men are not pourers but re-rollers, re-spinners. The machine shops clank in rhythm all day: patch, re-bore, re-tooth, re-wind. They are big-handed, scarred at the knuckles with green rust — for iron here does not go orange but a pale verdigris under lichen air, and the cut you get from a green-rusted sheet leaves a green tattoo that never fades. You can read a mender's years in his hands: dots and lines of green. They are proud of it. A new tool shines for a day and then dulls to that same green-grey as the tors. They like it that way. Shine doesn't belong up here.

The Dyers and Wool people. Their vats stain the alley behind the dye-works impossible colors even in grey light — magenta, acid yellow, teal — and the people who work them are stained to match to the elbow. A girl with sulphur-yellow forearms, a man with one purple hand that looks gloved. They press lichen, scrape it with bone knives from slabs they then carefully replace, boil peat, card wool that comes up raw and goes down again as dense, water-shedding cloth. They talk least of all, because the vats need listening: a simmer, not a boil.

The Leat-keepers and Turbine men down by the lowest tarn. Sallow from never quite being dry, always with one trouser leg darker to the knee from wading. They train the brief rills into leats, clear moss from intakes with their bare hands, feel voltage in their teeth. They know when the plateau breathes — those nights when cloud thins to smeared stars and the amber bulbs dim without anyone touching a switch. They don't log it. They just ease the load, murmur to the apprentice, *she's exhaling*, and wait.

And the Threshold-carpenters. Perhaps the most important. Because the old rock flows. Over decades the houses settle, belly, sag. Lintels bow like wax, floors tilt toward the pan-center, doors jam. Every generation the doors are re-hung, the thresholds built up a finger's width. You learn the step-over without thinking. The carpenters are thin, patient men and women who spend their lives with levels they don't trust, shimming, jacking, listening to wood creak against stone. Children follow them to collect the curled shavings for tinder.

Children themselves — you hear them before you see them, which is rare here. A clear *ping — pong — ping* ringing across a pan on a windless moment. Certain slabs of the oldest stone ring like bells underfoot and sustain a second too long, and every child knows the map of them. They hop from one to another to make tunes while running errands with water-butts sloshing. They are moss-expert before they are lettered: which cushion is springy-dry enough to sit on, which liverwort stains, which fern-frond unrolls sweet enough to chew. They play quiet games. Hide-and-listen, not hide-and-seek.

Elders sit in the shared-wall warmth — for no house stands alone, they clump for wind, share walls and heat and cistern-water — and read weather in the light. They can tell by smell how wet the moss is three streets over, by the exact pearl of the cloud whether fog will lift by two or settle for the week. They keep the extra cup on the sill during fog. If you ask, they will say, annoyed, *just catch*. Not offering. Just catch. But they rinse the cup first, and set it level, and empty it into the butt with care in the morning, and never let a stranger's hand touch it. Something in them remembers that water here doesn't fall, it arrives.

The animals that live with them are not pets, not livestock. No herds could eat this low mat and no flock could stand the wind. They are co-tenants.

Stone-mice flicker grey in the walls, quick as eyelids, eating crumbs and peat-dust. No one traps them seriously; a house without mice is a damp house, a dead house. The pair of high crows tilts across once a day, calling thinly — the only thing that dares height. Everyone looks up. It's a habit, like checking a gauge.

Under the mill eaves live one or two swallows, soot-dark, who work the columns of midges that hang over the wettest moss when wind drops. That is the whole hunt, played out nightly in silence: midge rise, swallow cut, gone. People pause to watch it the way lowlanders watch sunset.

And in the black tarns, things nose: thumb-long, dark, ancient. Newts with orange bellies like pilot lights, or shrimp left from when this was seabed. Children lie on their bellies on the peat fringe, faces close, breathing ghosts onto black water, whispering to them. Adults gather tarn-water for the sill cups and for dye-mordant and never speak of what lives in it.

What really lives here, if you stay past the first day and your steps shorten and your hearing sharpens, is not any one of these. It's the mat itself. The green that climbs walls, softens rooflines, swallows sheds. The people have learned not to fight it but to be farmed by it. A roof stripped of moss drums and leaks; a wall scraped of lichen weeps and cracks. So they let it stay, trim it like hair, train ferns out of mortar but leave the mortar damp. Their houses breathe through moss. Their boots are always wet because the ground is always giving water back up around the knuckles when you press. When you press your hand in, cold wells up. When you lift it, it fills again. That is the terms.

At dusk, when amber bulbs come fully on and the pan glows in warm reticulation against pearl-grey, you can stand in that highest alley — ferns in the mortar, cable shadows striping wet cobbles, dripping in different keys down the street, turbine thrum in your soles — and see them for what they are: dense and low and clever, tucked into the final wrinkles of a tired mountain that got tired of being a mountain. Lush in the cold. Busy in the quiet. Drinking fog because there is nothing else to drink. Mending because there is nothing new to make. Listening because there is nothing loud enough to hear unless you listen.
