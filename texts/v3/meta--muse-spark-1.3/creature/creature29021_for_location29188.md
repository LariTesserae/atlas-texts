You think at first that nothing does.

That is what the room wants you to think. It gives you the swept street and the empty hearths and the long echo, and you read it as *left.* Your kind of living leaves noise — gnawed cakes, droppings, footprints still damp at the edge. Here there is none of that. Not a skitter, not a wing-tick. You crouch in the bottle-wort and you are the only wet thing for miles, dripping and cracking, and the place tolerates you the way stone tolerates rain.

Sit longer. Until the sweat dries off you and your breathing goes shallow because the air is too hot to take deep.

The street stays swept because something sweeps it.

You will not see them move. That is the first rule. Nothing here moves at your speed.

Watch instead the edge where the fronds lean over the packed earth but do not enter it. The leaves there are smaller, clipped. The wax is thicker, scarred and resealed. Little crescent bites — not teeth, never teeth — have been taken out of the ash-flag where it got too ambitious. A fallen husk-mat that was slumped across the way an hour ago is now — somehow — tucked to the side. You did not see it happen. You heard only what you have been hearing all along: tick, pop, creak, the sigh of the superheated draft in the slit.

Lower your eyes to the floor. Below the waist-high canopy, at ankle level in the ember-dark, there is a second topography.

They are loaf-sized. Fist-sized. Mortar-bowl-sized. At first you took them for stones, for fallen roof-clods, for parts of the houses come loose. They have that same hand-patted look — fingerprints, almost. Domes of layered wax, black-green to amber, lacquered an inch thick like the leaves, but overlapping in scales. No head. No tail. No face to find. No wet eye anywhere, because there is nothing wet in them to make an eye from. On top, a thatch of silica bristles, glassy needles that clink faintly when the draft moves. Underneath, a felted fringe that drags.

They are not animals. Cut one open — you won't — and you would find no blood, no gut, no muscle, only concentric wax and fiber and dust pressed to pottery-hardness, and at the core a dull ember-orange knot, the same stored heat you saw in the leaves, only denser. They are fruit that learned to walk. Detached buds. Hands.

The whole jungle is one thing, you begin to understand. The cinder-plume fern and the ash-flag and the bottle-wort are not separate species competing; down in the dust their roots have grafted long ago into a single felt, white-hot and thread-fine, running under the street, up through the wall-cracks, out through the hearth-stones. What lives here is that felt, that slow exchange. And these domes are the parts of it that can lift, for a little while, and go elsewhere.

They drink nothing. There is nothing to drink and so the want has been planed out of them over a thousand years. What they take is heat and mineral and time, the three abundances. By day the rock radiates and the wax softens a fraction; the bristles on their backs, those silica needles, catch infrared the way your leaves catch light, and funnel it down into the core-knot where day-heat is stored. By night — which here is always, that perpetual dusk — they run on it, glowing faintly from within, little lanterns going dimmer as they work. You can see it if you let your eyes go fully ember-adjusted: a pulse moving under the canopy as a draft passes, leaf to dome to leaf.

And what is the work? Maintenance. Nothing leaves, nothing needs — but everything settles, and settling must be tended.

A dome will spend a week crossing a hand's breadth of street. Its felt fringe secretes hot oil, the same oil a snapped leaf exhales to seal itself. Dust sticks to the oil. Ash sticks. Fallen spore-dust, shed husks, the crumble off a mud-brick. The dome drags it slowly to the edge and combs it off on the fronds with its bristles, where the plants trap it on waxy leaves and let it enrich and fall as richer ash. That is the sweeping. No broom. A body for a broom.

Another will park itself for a season against the foot of a terrace wall where the dust-drift presses. It roots there, temporarily. Its fringe hardens into guy-lines. It becomes a retaining stone, living, holding the wall until the seedlings on the other side have taken the load. Then it loosens, seals the scars with oil, and goes on.

Another will climb — so slowly the idea of climbing insults it — up the stones of a hearth where a plume has sprouted straight through the fire-blackened ring. It does not eat the plume. Nothing eats here. It noses around the base, laying down a collar of wax that guides the new roots to go *around* the stones and use them as trellis, not through them to topple them. That is the civility you noticed. It is not agreement. It is self-care. The house shades the root. The root needs shade. Why destroy shade?

They prune, too. With no mouth they prune by weight and heat. They lean against a too-bold frond for days until the wax at its joint softens and it bends back over the street-edge. The crescent bites you saw are heat-burns, pressed, sealed instantly.

If you stay perfectly still, one may come to you. Not curious — they have no organ for that. You are a sudden cool shadow, a damp weather-front. Your breath comes out and dries before it leaves you, but for half a second it is moisture, and moisture is a wrongness here older than the houses. The felt under the floor will feel the cool of your belly on the dust. The nearest domes will stop. The ticking of the leaves around you will change pitch, just slightly, as stomata you cannot see close. They will wait you out. They have waited out everything.

Their lives are long and circular. They bud in the dark under the largest bottle-worts, where the heat is steadiest — a swelling in the root-felt that hardens, waxes, bristles, and finally pinches off with a soft *tak!* no louder than a bursting spore-pod. For years they wander, sweeping, shoring, trellising. And when the core-knot has cycled enough heat through itself that the wax goes dull and cracked beyond resealing, they do not die so much as settle. They find a low spot against a foundation, against a granary stone, in a doorway threshold, and they stop. The fringe roots deeply. The dome flattens. Dust covers it. The plants send rootlets into it for its stored mineral. In a decade it is indistinguishable from a hand-patted mud-brick, fingerprints and all. The town grows, very slowly, out of its own tenders.

That is why the thresholds are worn into shallow bowls, why the corners are rounded. Footsteps began it, generations of small dry feet that left and did not return. Domes continue it.

You will have to back out on your belly to leave. You will be too loud, too wet, too fast. Behind you the blades of glare from the roof-holes will have shifted an inch. The ash-flags will groan in the breathing draft. A mature pod will burst — *tak!* — and throw its dry spores up to sift hissing down on lacquered leaves, uncarried, unneeded, falling straight back to the felt that made them.

And at ankle level, below seeing, the little ember-knots will go on with the street, keeping the way open for no one.
