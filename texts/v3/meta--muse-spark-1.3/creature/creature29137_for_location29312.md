You don't see them at first. Through the steam they are just more of the Pit — slower smudges between the hanging taro and the pipes. Then your eyes learn the half-light and they come forward, the way leaves do.

They are pale people made damp. That is the first true thing.

On the plain above, everyone is sun-cured, red-brown and split-lipped. Down here the sun only visits for an hour at noon, straight down like a spear, and nobody looks up into it. The Pit-born live in amber cage-light and furnace-orange and green shade, and their skin shows it: sallow, perpetually beaded, soft as boiled root from never fully drying. Their hair hangs lank. Their eyes are large in the gloom — pupils wide to catch the sideways tired light — and when they go up to the rim to trade they squint and weep in the white sky and put their hands up. They get nosebleeds up there, in the dry. Their lungs have learned to chew.

They don't wipe the sweat. Visitors do, constantly, fogging and mopping. A Marroweller just shines. Old women sit in doorways knitting with forearms glistening. Men on the pump terraces work stripped to the waist in shorts of mill-cloth, skin mapped with old heat rash and pale moss-burns, moving like sap. Children run barefoot and leave wet prints on warm wood that vanish in seconds. Even their clothes sweat — the undyed pulp-fiber shirts from Level Six, the dyed wraps from the dye-works, limp and clinging by midmorning, then hung at night on lines that never quite dry them, only warm them.

They are quiet people, because they have to be. That is the second thing that becomes itself the longer you stay.

Sound comes back off the wet walls and embarrasses you. Everyone learns this by age four. So they speak low, in short breaths, and much of what they say isn't said. On the roof-streets — which are only roofs, remember, tar-paper and thatch and slag-concrete sheets bridged with planks — people pass in cloth slippers with a soft *shush-shush* that doesn't carry. They nod. They touch a shoulder to ask right of way through a parlor. Because to go anywhere you must go *through* people. To reach the sump you walk through eleven parlors. It is literal. You lift a curtain, step around a sleeping grandmother, cross a kitchen where taro is boiling, nod thanks, go out a back hatch onto the next roof down. At every third house you leave your shoes, then pick up another pair, then leave them again. Nobody locks. Nobody knocks very loud. Privacy is not doors. Privacy is agreement not to notice.

The traders who ride the big cage-winch down in the morning are the loudest of them, and they are not loud. The platform takes all morning to descend — a winched garden the size of a market stall lowering one slow meter at a time — and the women ride it down knitting, calling out in low sing-song from their laps: shoots, pickles, oiled cloth, gourd-seed. Buyers on the terraces as it passes lean out and murmur and hand coin hand to hand. Nobody shouts for custom. The pumps set the tempo for everything: *keh — ... — keh — ... — keh* from the old Cornish engines lifting seep-water to the gardens, one stroke at a time. People time themselves to it. An old pump-keeper will tell you his resting pulse is the same as his pump's. He is not joking.

They are gardeners of iron. That is the third thing, and the strangest, and the truest.

Forty years is not long. The founders are still alive — blasters and truck drivers and camp cooks who poured in when the cut went bust, copper or coal, stories differ and they will argue it softly for an hour. They remember paint. Their children, the Pit-born, do not. The Pit-born prune where their parents painted.

Give a boy a handrail and he will tell you where the rust ends and the stem begins, but if you cut it open you will see he is wrong, because there is no line anymore. Leaves near the smelter come up copper-edged, stiff as tin. Moss is farmed like sheep. The boiler-tenders pack it thick around the housings so the machines hum instead of ring, and the vine-girls train morning-glory thick as rope along the hot pipes to cool them, pinching back new growth every day with nails gone green at the cuticle. Fig roots hold burst cisterns together better than mortar. Cutters-grass thatches every roof. Gourd buckets hang to shade windows. A cable left alone for a season comes back furred, root-hairs drunk on its grease, and nobody is surprised. They call it green-iron and they tend it as husbandry.

So their work looks like tending. The_LEVEL Six_ mill women pulp fiber in boiling leaf-smell and spread paper in steam. The dye-men stir vats the color of bruises. The wire-drawers work behind moss-baffles, humming, drawing green-furred copper thin as finger. The oil-press smells of hot nuts. The canning shed smells of vinegar and pickled shoots — almost all they eat, besides taro root big as a child's torso, gourds, pressed oil, an egg sometimes from the panting roof chickens kept in wire boxes. Meat is a memory and a story about a heron.

Everyone knows the heron. When the rain fell straight in once and hissed on the roofs and made waterfalls off every catwalk, a heron came down with it and stood on the sump rail for three days — that small scummed steaming eye of water at the bottom, no bigger than a courtyard — while the whole Pit brought it scraps and whispered. Then it lifted out. People still talk about it the way other towns talk about saints. *It stayed. It left.* That is theology enough for a place that rises and falls with the sump over months. When the bottom water rises, the lowest family carries their bedding up without argument and waits. When it falls, they sweep the silt and move back in. No deeds. No hurry. Rust blooms over a season. A vine takes a wall in a year and gives it back in ten.

The children are the water system. Every morning the cloud forms at the third terrace down and gets caught, unable to lift over the lip. It beads on washing lines and door-leaves. Small girls and boys are sent out with rags — serious, sleepy, bare-legged — to wipe down tarps and elephant ears and wring them into bottle-cap chains that tickle down into cisterns. They know which pipe sweats coldest, which seam weeps blackest but still drinkable after boiling. They play soundlessly in the vapor, appearing and gone. They have never seen a horizon except by climbing to the lip houses that jut like teeth and looking out at cracked laterite and thorn scrub and feeling their skin crack in minutes. Most don't like it up there. Too bright. Too dry. Too loud with wind.

At dusk they come out onto the roofs together. The cage-bulbs click on one by one down the spiral, amber in green. The sky-shaft above goes violet. Below, the steam glows from underneath where the geothermal seam breathes and furnace doors stand open. You will see them then plainest: hundreds of glistening faces, old blasters with drill-scars still in their knuckles, winch-knitters with yarn stuck to their forearms, moss-packers, cistern-sweepers, boiler-tenders with blind white lizards clinging to their warm backs, all barefoot on warm wood, all looking not at you but at the air lifting — that long hot plant-sweet exhalation when the whole packed hamlet breathes out once, lifts its hanging cloud a meter, and lets it settle.

People are the fauna here. That is what they say, half joke, when you ask about the pipe-rats, the panting chickens, the crow that circles the rim and thinks better of it. They mean it.
