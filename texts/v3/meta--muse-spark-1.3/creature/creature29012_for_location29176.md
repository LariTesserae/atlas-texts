They do not show themselves at first.

For the first ten minutes down there you think it is empty — just cinder and concrete and sweating pipe, and the red bulb making everything the color of dried blood. Then your eyes finish changing. The black resolves. What you took for shadow gets thickness. What you took for wet concrete gets a nap, a pile, a breathing edge.

It is green. In islands.

Bare ground between — long stretches of hot black cinder where nothing has taken, where the heat coming off the slabs shimmers and the mineral rings lie empty — and then, all at once, a thicket so dense you can't see into it. Not tall. Wide. Low and spilling. A place where the damp collects has decided to be alive, and only there.

The ones that own most of it are the ferns.

Not like any ferns you know, except at first glance. Then they become more themselves. Three meters if you stretch a frond straight, which you never can because they arch and collapse under their own weight, piling tip over stem over tip. The stems are thick as your wrist at the base, fleshy, pale, almost white where they have been covered by their own thatch. The fronds themselves are thin to the point of being unfinished — you can see the red bulb through the edges, the veins lit up like a hand held to a lamp. They never made sunscreen. There was no sun to need it against, only the pilot blue, the cherry seam, the red hum. So they stayed bleached and translucent, a green so washed it goes yellow, goes white at the margins.

They do not reach up. There is no up that means anything here. The sky is a cold slot twelve meters above and gives nothing. They reach sideways, toward warmth and wet. Every clump leans. You can read the pipes by the lean of the ferns. Where a steam line runs low along the concrete, the fronds comb over it, press their undersides to it. Lift one — it comes away with a sucking sound — and underneath is the rootwork: not in soil, because there is no soil, but in the wound itself. White rootlets by the thousand, fine as hair, have found the canvas lagging around the pipes and worried it open. They live in the wrap now, drinking the sweat straight off the steel as it beads. The lagging sags with them, uddered with roots.

They are whole. That is what holds you. Kneel long enough and you keep looking for the missing piece and never find it. No chewed crescents. No skeletonized patches. No galls, no mines, no webbing slung between pinnae. Each leaflet is perfect, unbitten from crozier to brown tip. Spores fall straight down in the still air and make a rust-colored dust on the frond below, and where they land on wet felt they germinate where they land. A baby fern no bigger than an eyelash, already perfect.

Under and between them are the mosses. Mattresses of them.

Where the ferns are architecture, the mosses are upholstery, filling every cleft where condensation gathers long enough to sit. Acid green in the dampest runnels. Lime where it is a little drier. And out toward the edges, a white-green that in the red dark seems to throw back a little more light than it receives — not a glow, exactly, but a pallor that stays with your eye after you look away. Put your weight on it and your hand sinks to the wrist. It is cold, even here. It holds water like a sponge and smells of deep earth and iron. Peel a corner back and underneath is last year's fern collapse: brown fronds gone to sodden felt, not eaten, not carried away, just melted layer on layer into a sour, black peat that feeds the mat from below. Nothing interrupted the rot. No mouth came.

Hanging above this, from the dead catwalk, are the trumpets.

You know them as pitcher plants, though they have forgotten that trade here. A dozen of them drape down on vines thick as extension cords, their pitchers swaying almost imperceptibly in the stirred air from the fan. Each trumpet is flawless — waxy, veined red on cream, the lid held open perfectly level, the rim unchipped. And each is full to the brim. Not with drowned flies. With water. The rain that is not rain. The sweat of the plant dripping off the catwalk, the breath of the boiler condensed on steel and run down the vine and collected, cup after cup. Tip one gently and it pours clear, smelling faintly of oil. Inside, a few drowned spores turning. A mosquito husk from Before, from when there were wings here, still floating after years. They have become cisterns. Meat-eaters with no meat, keeping water instead.

Furthest out, where the gradient fails — in the dark corners past the ferns and the moss, where the wall is merely warm to the touch and the red bulb doesn't reach — are the listeners.

Pale shelf fungi, stacked ear over ear on a fallen cinderblock and on the damp end-grain of a timber left leaning. White, gray-white, pinkish in the seam-light. Flesh thick, cool, beaded with droplets that never fall. They do not spread so much as accumulate, one shelf over the last, each a little larger, listening. No slugs have rasped them. No gnats have laid in their gills. Their edges are entire as cut paper. When the valve sighs — *hssss* — the droplets tremble on their rims and hold.

And closest in, where you almost don't want to put your hand, is the scald.

Around the foot of the live boiler, where seep hotter than you can hold comes up through the cracked plinth and runs in a black trickle to a pan that appears and vanishes, there is a skin. Mustard-yellow and rust-orange slime, black-green felt. Scald-moss and the bacteria beneath it, the only things that can take it that hot. It looks painted on. It quivers with bubbles too small to hear. This is the frontier — life laid right up against the almost-boiling, thinning out as the slab cools: orange to green to the first true moss cushion a meter away. Stand there and you can feel the bands on your own skin, on your shins and face. The heat sorting everything.

None of this eats. That is the rule that makes them what they are.

No pollinators, so the pitchers never set seed anymore; they just extend, vine a little longer each year. No herbivores, so the ferns never defend; they just unroll, a centimeter a week, extravagant and undefended. No scavengers, so death just settles. If you stay still your sweat joins it — beads on your elbows, falls to the moss, is taken without thanks.

And beneath it all, keeping the exception open, is the other resident. The one that is not green.

The living boiler. Three culverts on a plinth, one cold with croziers growing out of its open firebox door, one ticking itself to death, one low and patiently churning. You can lay your palm on its jacket and feel it: not hot, exactly, but unwilling to go cold. The pump lifting something hot from deeper still. The belt turning so slowly you have to watch a bolt for a minute to see it move, turning the fan that only stirs. The gauge trembling just above zero. It is not alive the way the ferns are alive. It is alive the way a heart kept beating in a room with no body is alive. Without it the walls would cool, the sweat would stop, the rings would dry white and stay white, the green islands would shrink to brown felt. It does not know it is a keeper. It just hasn't yet given up, and so they haven't yet had to.

That is who lives here: the unhurried, mouthless green arranged in rings around a tired heat, drinking its own breath, perfect because nothing has ever taken a bite.
