No one lives here.

That is what you think for the first ten minutes, while your sternum is still learning the roar. The room is too loud for a heartbeat, too cold for blood, too wet and too new and too high. Anything that could walk or fly or spin a web would have left by the ladder. And nothing has. No mouse droppings in the corner by the pallet. No spider in the cable tray. Not even a fly working the sump.

Then your eyes drop, because you have to work by feel and you start watching your boots, and you notice that living here does not mean walking around.

It means staying low.

Low to the crack, is how they do it. Down where the vibration is a little less murderous, down where the concrete meets itself and water never quite leaves.

First, the smear.

In the mortar line where the uphill wall meets the floor — that always-damp hypotenuse where the mountain's push becomes a sheet a millimeter deep — there is a black-green slick, matte, not slime but almost felt. Algae. Not the bright pond stuff. This is high, cold, starved stuff. It does not need soil. It roots, if you can call it rooting, in pore-water, in condensation. It drinks the weep. Every eleven minutes when the spill valve cycles and the floor kicks and the wall weeps harder, as if the mountain flinched, it gets a fresh feed. The rest of the time it just holds on while oil skins wrinkle past it toward the trench. If you wiped it with your gloved thumb it would come away like wet soot. By tomorrow noon it would be greening again at the edges. It does not grow up. It grows out, a coin at a time, along the line.

Second, the thumbprint.

In the lee of a conduit clamp on the uphill wall — just one clamp, where the drip off the cable tray is steady but not scouring — a cushion of moss no bigger than your thumbprint. This is the luxury address. Shelter from the sideways cloud that piles in the vent slit, shelter from boot-splash, but right under the tap.

Look close, when the brown-yellow bulkhead stops flickering for a second. It is not soft the way lowland moss is soft. It is tight, starved, hunkered. Hundreds of tiny tongues, dark green at the tips, brown-black at the base where they are always soaked, rhizoids sunk not into earth but into the roughness of poured concrete, into the grain left by the formwork. It shivers — everything here shivers with the loose chain and the 60-cycle snarl and the river-in-a-pipe three feet under the plate — but it does not let go. It catches what falls: a bead off a pipe, breath off your jacket, cloud itself. Your jacket darkens at the shoulders in a minute; it darkens too, and then lightens as the warm side of the transformer cabinet — the only warm thing in the room — flashes its bit of water back into air to condense again. It lives in that tiny weather. It has never been dry. It has never been still. It does not need to be.

Third, the rust-eaters.

In the trench itself, under the checker-plate where the proper stream runs two inches deep over steel, furiously cold and clear, there is a film where the light hits. Only for an hour a day does the sun come straight through the east slit and lay a blade across the submerged steel. There, orange as a fresh bruise, bacteria bloom.

Iron-oxidizers. They are eating the room while it is still young. Fresh weld beads like caterpillars on the bypass, knife-sharp coring from last spring, galvanized racks still glossy — all of it bleeding a molecular rust into that cold run, and they are there to take it. A velvet, a stain, a skin on the metal under water that does not wash away because it *is* the washing. They thrive on what would kill anything else: no light except that hour, no warmth, constant scour, the pump coughing overhead, float rising, contactor slamming *clack* through your boots, trench level dropping an inch then rising again. They ride it. They are the color of the process.

That is all. You could count the living patches on two hands and have fingers left. They do not keep company. The algae along its line, the moss under its drip, the orange film in its hour of sun — scattered, sparse, each alone in its microclimate of wet and cold and tremor. No soil between them. Only concrete sweating, ceiling pipes beading and falling, ticking rain indoors.

They do not hear the roar. They have no sinuses to buzz, no voice to lose shouting to the man next to you. The vibration that would drive anything with a heartbeat out is, to them, just weather. Another cycle. Drip, run, evaporate, condense. Pump on, pump off. Cloud to three feet visibility, then clear.

You will log your time, wipe the pen because the first ink ran, climb back down sixty rungs into the humming dark. They will stay.

High and hidden and fully made, the room will keep weeping and dripping and pumping to no audience — except them, low to the crack, becoming more themselves, green and black-green and orange, holding the wet concrete as if it were ground.
