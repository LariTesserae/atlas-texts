At first you think no one lives here.

That is the town's first manners. It shows you the signs and withholds the hands that made them. A walkway swept clear of grit, the fan-marks still damp. Wires labeled in careful, small handwriting — *NORTH LOOP / DO NOT CUT / she hums* — the ink feathered by condensation. Boots left outside a door that is only paint on a blank wall, laces loosened, leather dark with having just been worn. Windows lit amber from inside in flat daylight. Music, once, very faint, from three walls over and one floor down, stopping when you stop to listen.

You wait for someone to come out. No one does.

Then you understand the density is not empty. It is occupied the way a held breath is occupied. Every door, every lit rectangle, every humming box is a presence choosing not to resolve right now. If you stand long enough in one of those shoulder-wide gaps where the buildings failed to merge, you will feel it: the air gets slightly warmer on one side of your face. A breath-cloud thins just past your shoulder where no one is standing, three seconds after it should have. A rail you are about to touch is already warm from a hand, just not warm now.

They move the way the town moves. Slowly. And mostly out of the corner.

A shoulder turning a corner two layers up, a coat the exact grey of wet concrete, gone before the turn completes. A hand withdrawing from a curtain made of insulation foil — pale, pruned, middle finger stained black at the tip, as if dipped in that filament lace that heals cracks. A clink from inside a shed that cannot contain a kitchen, and the smell of tea, tannin and hot metal, venting from a seam no wider than a thumb.

Don't look directly. That is how you start to see them.

They are not tall. Or rather, they have learned not to use height outdoors. Outside, in the alleys and on the laced balconies, they pull themselves in — shoulders hunched, chins tucked, arms close — to pass sideways. They wear what the town wears: layers built onto layers. Wool over plastic over wool, cuffs taped, hoods stiff with frost. Everything slightly damp, always. Their eyebrows hold ice. Their boots make no sound because the film of water takes it. You will never hear one come up behind you. You will only realize your shadow has company, arriving a half-second later than you do, a little wider than it should be.

Indoors is different. Everyone who has glimpsed indoors agrees, without having compared notes.

The old woman in the wardrobe-shed is the one who will show you if you let her. She is not old the way people are old outside. Her face is smooth and waterlogged, like a stone kept in a stream. Her eyes are adjusted to that flat late-morning light that never changes; they have no squint in them. When she opens her door — and for her, the door opens quickly, out of courtesy, in only an afternoon — you see past her into a long kitchen, much longer than the shed, with windows on two sides looking onto different weather: sleet on the left, thin sun on the right. Steam coming off a pot. Three chairs. Two of them occupied by coats that are drying into the shape of people.

She offers tea. Everyone is offered tea once.

If you refuse, and you should refuse, she nods as if you have passed something, and closes the door hair by hair. If you accept, and some do — out of cold, out of politeness — you step over the high threshold and you feel the filing begin. That is the word the ones outside use, in the rare notes they leave for newcomers pinned under barrel lids: *filed*. To be put among the layers. Inside, your ears pop as if descended. Your watch stutters. Your breath stays in the air in front of you and does not thin, but joins the other breaths hanging there from morning, from last week. You sit. The tea is hot and tastes of rain that has waited a long time in a pipe. You become, hour by hour, more interior than exterior. Larger inside than the building that claims you. When you come back out to Copper Alley — and extraction is slow, a season, they say, if you are lucky — you come out slightly doubled. Like the cup left on the ledge. There are two of you for a morning, one slightly more transparent, and by evening only one again. But it is not quite clear which one remained.

This is how they are made. Nobody is born here. The potatoes on the third-level balcony are stunted, the moss grows like wire, the lichen never gets past crust. Nothing that needs fast warmth takes. The inhabitants accrete. Visitors who stayed for the door to open. Tenders who came to fix a conduit and learned to wait for their shadow before stepping off curbs and then forgot how not to wait. They came in single and they stayed and the town, which expands inward because the basalt ring holds, expanded inward in them too. Room inside room.

Up close — and you will only ever be up close once, by accident, shoulder to shoulder in a laundry court that becomes a chapel of pipes that becomes the same laundry court — you see what the waiting has done to the body.

Their skin sweats the way every surface here sweats. Not perspiration. Condensation. A fine film that never beads enough to drip. Touch one, if one lets you, and your fingertip comes away cold and clean, smelling faintly of iron and ozone. Their hair, when unhooded, lies flat and drips so slowly you have to watch for an hour to be sure a drop has swollen at the end. Their teeth hum. If you stand very close and both hold your breath, you can feel it travel between you, that low pressure in the molars, the pumps, the heating, the something else holding on.

Their eyes do not flicker. They have learned to look the way you watch a drip swell. Their movements are economical to the point of stillness. No one pushes. To push a three-day door would be rude to the process. So they lean, they accompany. A man — you think it is a man, in three coats and a hat made of tarp — will stand with his palm an inch from a seam for an hour, keeping it company while it widens. A child — if there are children, and there must have been once because someone has knotted a rope swing in a shaft too narrow to swing in, and the rope is worn smooth — a child will stand at a curb and wait, patiently, absurdly, for her shadow to catch up before she steps, and when it does she steps with it, perfectly timed, unhurried.

They speak rarely, and then in writing, or in maintenance. That is their language. To sweep is to say *I am still here, we are keeping this clear together*. To label a wire is to say *I traced this, it goes here now, it was blue and came out green*. To leave boots outside a painted door is to say *I am inside, do not knock, the inside is larger and I am far in*. To leave two cups and take one by evening is to say *I was doubled and I chose*.

At night — which is not night but a thinning of light into blue-grey — they tend the paradox as a utility, piped and metered like water. You will see them then if you are quiet, because the lamps in their hollow sockets brighten a fraction and throw their work into relief. One with a wooden spoon coaxing frost into spiraling patterns outward from the dark glass column, not making it, just gardening it. One on her knees in the gap where the ground is still being made, watching the black upwelling crack into hexagonal plates, already deciding where the next alley will be roofed with cables and tarps, what name she will paint by hand. One feeding the black filaments with a fingertip of blood, letting the lace taste and fill a frost-crack smoother than the original. They do not wear gloves. Metal burns to touch, but they have learned the exact length of touch that warms without sticking.

And the fox.

Everyone sees the fox and no one remarks on it because the fox is what they look like when they are still moving. Thin, winter-white, too long in the legs — all the stretching the town does inward, made animal for a moment. It crosses a footbridge three stories up and then a lower bridge without having descended because that is how they get around now: to make four lefts and return slightly above, to climb a ladder and arrive lower, colder, damper. To be filed is to learn to be in two places along the same pace. The fox leaves no prints on frost it should disturb because by the time the prints would form, it is already filed, already interior. It curls asleep in a pipe elbow and makes no cloud with its breath because it is keeping all its breaths inside, hanging them up in the long kitchen with the others.

The pigeons, after a fashion, two or three, perched upside-down under walkways, making no wing sound when they take off — those are the ones who stayed longest. Who got most dense. Who learned to live entirely in the underside, where pipes bead and hold, where condensation gathers and sometimes falls upward very slowly toward the overcast. They do not coo because there is nothing to say that sweeping has not said.

If you stay until the wire-moss stiffens and sings its glass-harp note that stops before you are sure you heard it, you will feel the town notice you noticing. A window that was lit goes dark, politely, to give you privacy. A drip from tarp to barrel — *tik... ... ... tik* — times itself to your breathing until you hold your breath without meaning to. The hum in your teeth rises slightly, inquisitive. Someone, rooms away, through walls that are thicker inside than the wall that holds them, puts a kettle on. You can smell it. Tea. Tannin and hot metal.

That is when you should go. Leave your breath hanging in the air behind you. It will stay longer than it should, slowly dissolving, joining the others. And they — the swept, the labeled, the filed, the damp and patient and interior-large, the ones who knit crack with black lace and wait three days with a door — they will keep it for a while, turn it over, taste it, and file it with the rest, before they turn back to the quiet work of keeping the impossible from freezing apart.
