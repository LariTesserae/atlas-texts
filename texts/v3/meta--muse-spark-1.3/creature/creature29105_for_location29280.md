They don't live here like you live anywhere. They live here like water lives in a cupped hand — held, and constantly spilling, and replaced.

Start with what you thought was smoke.

The midges. They are the weather in here. Not a swarm, a climate. Individually they are nothing — a gray fleck with frantic wings, a body that is mostly hunger and damp. Together they are a moving solid that learns the shape of you and flows around it. They are born in the skim. In that inch of bathwater that never leaves the deck, in the lime-crusted cups where water slows, a thousand wrigglers hang head-down from the surface film, breathing through their tails. When they hatch they don't fly up, there is no up. They fly sideways, into the drum-roar, and join the cloud. They drink the drip. They drown in the drip. A whole generation can live and die between one baffle and the next, and the bodies — soft, drowned commas — go straight down into the mouths waiting below. That is their job: to turn warm water and rot-air into meat.

The moths know that. The service moths.

They are pale to the point of colorlessness, the color that dust gets when it gives up. Big as a thumbnail, small as a coin, furred to the eyes. In any other floor they'd be pests shaken out of a filter. Here they are cattle. They plaster themselves in shivering ranks around the amber photons, forty, sixty to a rib, wings open flat to catch the condensate as it sheets down. Watch one close: it doesn't so much drink as press its whole face to the wet alloy and let its tongue unspool. Its wings never stop — a low shiver, ten beats a second, not to fly but to stay warm, to shake off the water that would otherwise glue it down. When you breathe, the whole rank lifts at once in a papery detonation, settles again a handspan over. They shed constantly. The air is full of their scales, itching your nose, silvering your sleeves. Down in the gutter, the cleaners eat the fallen scales like plankton.

The cleaners. Call them roaches because the Stack needs a word for flat things that survive. They are mahogany-dark, polished by wet, oiled-seeming. They work in lines, always in lines, antennae tapping the water ahead like blind men’s canes. They patrol the waterline — that bright meniscus where deck meets rib — eating everything the web discards: drowned midges, moth wings, shed frog skin, swiftlet down, your dead skin cells the moment they slough. Flip one — don't — and you'd see its underside is pale, puckered from constant immersion. They can hold their breath for twenty minutes, tucking into the capillary gutter when the sump glugs and surges. They don't scatter from light. There is no light to scatter from. They scatter from vibration, from your knee in the water, and then re-form behind you, closing the line.

Above them, the jumpers.

The cave crickets are all leg and nerve. Burnt-sugar brown, eyeless-seeming, with antennae twice their body that read the drumbeat of this place better than ears. They live in the dryest places that aren't dry — the bolt-holes, the cable catenaries furred with dust. They spring rib to rib with a *tick-tick* of claw on metal that you feel more than hear under the chorus. They are tasters. Everything new gets tasted: a cable sheath, a moss tuft, your bootlace, the salt on your wrist. Not to eat you, to test if you are food yet. In the dark end they gather in knots, legs interlaced, sharing warmth. When a spider sheet sags too low, they are the first to know, and the first to be missing.

But the heart of the seam isn't insect. It's throat.

The frogs. God, the frogs.

Thousands of them, amber as the maintenance dots, no bigger than the last joint of your little finger. They should not be here. Two thousand meters up, no pond, no sky, no soil. But someone carried spawn up on a boot tread years ago, and the sump never dried, and now they own every horizontal lip in the pocket. Look: in each white mineral cup sits one, throat pulsing. On each cable, a row of them like beads. In the trays, piled three deep, eyes shut against the wet. Their bellies are cold even in this heat — that's why one will climb you. You are the warmest, driest vertical in its world.

When they call, they call with their whole bodies. The throat-sac blows out to twice the head, translucent, veined, trembling. *Peep-peep-peep-peep.* One is shrill. A thousand, amplified by alloy ribs into a drum, is a physical wall that gets behind your teeth. They aren't calling to you. The males are calling frog to frog, holding territories the size of a bottle cap, screaming *I am here I am wet I am alive* over the building's exhale. The females are fatter, quieter, throats still, moving belly-deep through the skim to lay strings of clear eggs under the water film where the cleaners can't quite reach. The tadpoles — black commas — graze the black-green biofilm in the slowest water. You can smell them: sweet, spawn-sweet, under the rot.

And where there are thousands of singing throats, there are mouths waiting.

The geckoes live pressed into the warm seams where inner hull bleeds heat. They are the aristocrats here, fat and slow and utterly indifferent. Translucent-bellied — hold an amber dot behind one and you can see the dark coil of its gut, the pulse of its heart. Toes splayed impossibly wide, lamellae gripping wet alloy that would slide anything else off. They don't chase. They wait with those wet-bead eyes open and unblinking, and when a frog hops wrong, when a cricket misjudges a spring, the tongue flicks — too fast to see in this light. Just a missing frog and a throat pulsing down a gecko's neck. They lick their own eyes to clear the condensation. They shed in one perfect sock and the cleaners take it within the hour.

The spiders take the rest.

Palm-wide, long-legged, the color of damp concrete so you don't see them until the blue bleed catches the water beaded on their silk. They don't build orbs; there is no space, no still air. They build sheets, hammocks strung across the narrowest pinches between ribs, sagging under the weight of their own drinking water. Each drop on the silk is a lens catching amber and blue. Trapped in them: wings, wings, wings. Midges by the hundred, a moth still shivering. The spider sits belly-up under the sheet, mouth to the silk, drinking the vibrations. When the building breathes hard and the whole interstice shudders, they ride it, legs loose, giving with the drum instead of fighting. Tear one sheet and the balance screams — the midges will find your eyes, your nostrils, in seconds.

At the far end, where the slit narrows to a hand and the outer wind screams through the wind-slots, live the only ones who remember sky.

The swiftlets. Gray as wet ash, sharp-winged, crammed shoulder to shoulder on half-cup mud nests stuck to the vertical baffle. The mud is not mud — it's guano and mineral lace and lime spit, carried pellet by pellet up from below, cemented with saliva. Each cup holds two chicks, blind-grinning, necks corded, gaping constantly. The parents come and go through the slots with a timing that should kill them — out into thin, tearing, night-cold air, back in a half-second later with a throat-pouch of high-altitude insects smashed into a ball. *Rush-rush* of leathery wings, added to the roar. They don't sing. They scream in ultrasound and clicks to find their own cup in the dark. Their shit falls straight down and feeds everything: the biofilm, the moss, the midges, the fern. This whole web runs on their shit and drowned wings. That sweet rot-bird musk you taste? That's them. That's the base of the food chain wearing feathers.

Even the almost-plants know their place. The black-green biofilm slicks the slowest water, a living skin of bacteria and algae that never saw sun, eating minerals and guano. The moss in the bolt-hole is pale and leggy, etiolated, reaching for a lamp-glow that died decades ago, surviving on shit-dust. The single fern at the sump grate is stunted, fronds curled and lime-coated, roots drinking warm drain-water. They lost. They stay anyway, as decoration, as extra surface for a frog to sit on.

None of them are alone. That's the point. Wing over throat over claw. Midge to frog to gecko to cleaner to spider to bird to shit to midge again. A closed loop in a four-meter sweat-seam, shouting over the building to be heard, drinking the building's sweat, warm as a mouth, packed layer on layer because outside the skin the air is thin and clean and kills.

Leave your hand still long enough and a frog will take you for landscape. That's not accident. In here, you *are* landscape.
