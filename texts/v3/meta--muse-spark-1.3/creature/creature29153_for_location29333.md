You won't see them at first. That is part of how they live here.

You will think no one could. For that first half-mile of nothing — just wet stone and sky and the sheet-water winking — your mind files the place under uninhabited. Too flat, too loud, too wet. No wood to burn, no soil to plant, no wall to stop the wind. And then your foot catches on purpose.

A V of stones that was not left by ice.

That's how they first become real: not as faces, but as rearrangements. A few careful stones moved two inches to the left where the rill narrows. A flat slab lifted out of a pothole so a hand could reach in. A crab carapace set upright on a cairn, bleached white, facing the way you should walk.

The people who live here are low. The place does not permit anything tall, and they have obeyed it so long it is in their bodies.

They are not tall to begin with — short, broad through the shoulders and hips, legs thick and short from wading cold — but they make themselves lower still. They crouch to move across open sheets. They trot bent at the waist between pools. When the wader colonies lift shrieking they drop to one knee without thinking, head tucked, mouth open to let the pressure pass through them. A stranger stands upright and is knocked back by sound and weather. They fold, and let it go over.

You learn their color before you learn their shape. Mud-grey. Everything they wear is the color of wet stone just before it dries that never dries. Cloaks and hoods sewn from otter and fox and the skins of the big slate waders, scraped thin, rubbed over and over with crab fat and fish oil until they shed water in beads. Underneath, soft bird-skin shirts, feather-in, for warmth that still breathes. Their leggings are hide strips, cross-lashed to the knee, dark with saturation. Their feet — if you ever get close enough to see them bare, slipped out of sealskin moccasins to grip — are pale and puckered and immensely calloused, toes splayed wide from a lifetime of not slipping. The quartzite rings under boots; under their feet it barely whispers.

Their hands give them away. Even in the cold, when your own fingers have gone stiff in your cuffs, their hands are bare and working. Short fingers, nails broken short, skin stained rust-orange at the cuticles from the mats, from wringing and lifting and sorting all day in iron water. They do not seem to mind the wet. Or rather, they have stopped trying to stay dry in the way you mean it. Dry is a relative state: high stone versus low sheet. Elbows up versus elbows down. They keep their cores dry under oilskin and let everything else weep, like the rock does.

There are not many of them at a time. A kin, six or ten or twelve. Grandmother, daughters, sons-in, children who can already walk the high lines without being carried. No village. One beehive bothy for the worst nights, for smoking, for birth, and otherwise shallow hollows behind windbreaks of stacked slab no higher than your shin, with hides stretched over driftwood poles. They do not try to change the flat. They add a few careful stones and let water and animals do the rest.

They come with the birds. That is the simplest way to say it.

Somewhere else — days away by skin boat along a forested coast you will never see from here — they have winter houses, woodsmoke, trees. Here is summer work. When the tide begins to push up through the fissures and freshen the meres, when the midges pillar and the blind fish rise mouth-up, when the black divers lay their single speckled egg in every hollow, they load the boats with bone hooks and greenstone choppers and coils of sinew and dried hide, and with priceless driftwood, and they come to the flat. They rebuild what winter and storm collapsed.

Watch an old woman at a weir and you understand the whole economy.

She does not stand in the rill. She kneels on the high stone to one side, hood up, utterly still for longer than you would be patient. Below her the V of hand-stacked stones, fifty paces an arm, converges to a gap no wider than a basket. Water braids through it, inch-thick, carrying flickers. She is reading the orange. Where the bacterial mat is thickest and most wrinkled, grazed short by crabs, the fish will be. She reaches, not quickly, and lifts a woven sinew trap she set at dawn, and empties it with a practiced shake: three pale fish flipping, a fistful of copper crabs clicking. She slits the fish with a greenstone flake, saves the guts for bait on bone hooks set in the deeper potholes, tosses the crab shells she doesn't need onto the midden pile inside the V — that's why you found only shells there. Nothing wasted. The blood goes into the water. Within minutes midges settle. Within an hour crabs clean what she leaves.

The children do the loud work, because only children can afford to be loud here. While adults go silent and hand-signal — a flat palm for *wait for the sound to pass*, two fingers walking for *high stone path*, a fist for *pothole* — the children scream back at the colonies, run flapping with hide cloaks spread to panic the waders off a patch of eggs. They learn early the rule you learned: do not shout to be heard over the colonies. Wait. Time your voice to the trough between shrieks. A five-year-old here can feel the wave building in her sternum and hold her breath until it breaks, then speak a whole sentence clear in the three seconds of relative quiet. Their first game is cairn-finding in fog: run half a mile of empty glistening nothing, find the next flat-slab stack by memory, touch the white shell on top, turn.

They eat what the mat makes. That sounds strange until you see a day's food laid on a slab: boiled white fish, roasted crab split and steaming, a handful of speckled eggs taken one per hollow so the divers stay, strips of wader meat dried stiff in the damp like the hides in the bothy, and a sour orange paste scraped from the thickest mats and fermented in a seal stomach — iron and rot and salt, the taste of the rock itself, eaten in fingertip dabs to keep the gut alive. They drink stone-water, scooped from seeps where it runs clearest off granite, tasting of iron. They do not cook much. Driftwood is too precious to burn for comfort, whalebone too precious to carve idly. The cold hearth in the bothy is used sparingly, a smudge of bone-fat and dried midge-cake to smoke fish, then out. Heat is bodies together under hides, low roof holding breath-warmth in.

At dusk — though dusk never really comes, just that perpetual pearl-grey late-afternoon — they mend. Bone hooks sorted in piles, chipped, ground, lashed. Nets re-knotted. Cloaks re-rubbed with fat. And they stack.

Always stacking. A fallen arm of a weir rebuilt stone by stone. A cairn raised an inch where subsidence took it. A white shell replaced where a gull-thing knocked it off. Each cairn gets its offering: a perfect carapace, a speckled shell, turned face-out to the path. Not decoration. Memory. In a land with no landmarks, where weather can eat distance in minutes and every sheet of water looks like every other, these are sentences: *safe here, knee-deep there, eggs that way when tide drains.* A child who cannot yet read water can read shells.

And the nail. Every bothy has one, hammered into the lintel, rusted almost to nothing. They did not make it. Someone, generations back, traded or found or stole a plank with nails from a wreck or a southern boat, and brought one iron nail here as a charm. They do not use it as a tool. They touch it going in and out. Iron does not belong here — everything else is chipped, ground, lashed — so iron must be for luck. For remembering that there is elsewhere. The grandmothers say the nail holds the roof down when the wings become weather. It is nonsense and they know it is nonsense and they touch it anyway.

They are quiet in a loud place because they have to be, but they are not solemn. When the tide sighs out and leaves stranded pools wriggling, when the foxes trot through with fish in their jaws and the mottled otters slide S-wakes from mere to mere, they laugh — short, barked laughs timed to the quiet troughs. They imitate the waders, throats puffed, to tease each other. They let the midges crust their hoods without swatting, because swatting is wasted motion.

They will leave. Before the light thins any further, before the sheets skin with ice and the birds go, they will dismantle the hide stretchers, cache the greenstone choppers under slabs, turn the boats over onto the bothy roof to weight the whalebone against winter storms, and go back to trees. The flat will breathe without them for months, empty stretches of glistening rock and sky, crabs clicking unwatched. The weirs will collapse and be rebuilt, collapsed and rebuilt.

If you stay long enough to be trusted, they might show you how to walk: step only where the stone is a shade lighter — that's higher by an inch. Never step in the mirror-pools. Keep your feet dry if you can, because the low sheets hide potholes that go to your knee. And when the ground ahead darkens and begins to move, when ten thousand throats lift that rattling *krr-krr-krr* and the slabs hum under water, don't cover your ears. Open your mouth. Kneel down. Make yourself low.

The plain is breathing between heartbeats. Walk a little further with them, and it will lift, shrieking, into the dim air again — and they will be already moving under it, quick and grey and sure-footed, gathering what the noise feeds.
