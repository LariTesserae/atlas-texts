There is no king here. No single owner to meet at the bottom.

That's the first mistake you make, sitting with your back to the warm stone — you look for it. One shape. One pair of eyes. Because a room this complete ought to have someone it belongs to.

Instead, what lives here lives *shoulder to shoulder*. It lives three-deep.

You have to unlearn counting.

Start low, because they do. Stay low. Stay damp. Make no noise. Everyone here has agreed to that without ever meeting.

The floor-keepers first. They are the rot made patient.

The millipedes — forearm-long, gunmetal and rust — go through the knee-deep litter in procession, not hurrying. A hundred legs each moving like a breathed stitch. They eat dead leaf and make soil, they eat soil and make millipede, and when they die the fungi take them in a night and put up tiny white flags to mark the spot. You can hear, if you put your ear almost to the ground, the faint dry rustle of them, like pages being turned very far away.

After dark, when the light-coin above goes out, the crabs come out. Land crabs the color of wet brick, high-backed, one claw larger. Up on the plain they would clatter. Here their joints are muffled with slime from the drip-pans. They work the shallow black pans — no deeper than your palm — crouching, scooping, sifting fallen flowers and fermented fig for fly larvae. They do not fight. There isn't room to fight. They pass each other with their eye-stalks lowered, polite as monks.

Above them, the waiters.

She is the most permanent. The golden orb-weaver. Abdomen barred black and gold, legs like varnished twigs. Her wheel is two meters across, strung between two thigh-thick roots, and in the morning it is so thick with dew — dew that forms only on the downward faces here, beading under every thread — that it looks jeweled, a chandelier. She sits head-down in the center, utterly still. She does not need to move. The pit brings her food. Midges rise off the litter-heat, moths blunder, a gecko's shed skin lands and quivers. When something hits, she is there without seeming to travel. Wraps, bites, hangs it like a lantern. Then back to stillness. She has been in that exact hub for molts uncounted; the silk at the anchors is thick as string.

Not far from her, on an aroid leaf bigger than a doorway, the mantis. The color of dead leaf, edged with rot-brown, with one false eye-spot on its folded arm. It sways even when there is no wind — because there never is wind — sways to the rhythm of a leaf that would be moving if air moved. Theft of movement. It takes moths that come to lay on the aroid. Takes them so gently the leaf doesn't bounce.

And above the mantis, in the curtains, the snake. A vine snake, green as young fig, no thicker than your thumb, six feet of held breath hung among six feet of liana. You will think it is one more hanger until you see the tongue — twin red flicks, tasting your sweat from four feet away. It eats the geckos. It eats them without falling, without rustling, unhinging around a head while hanging by its tail. Then it hangs again, a little thicker in the middle, and digests for a week.

The geckos are the crowd. Translucent-bellied dtellas, dozens, hundreds shoulder to shoulder on the seep-walls where the rock weeps continuously dark green with liverwort. Throats pulsing. All facing out into the green dusk. They drink by pressing their chins to stone. They snap midges with a soft wet *tick* that dies three feet out because the moss — a foot deep, pillowy — swallows sound. If one is taken by the snake, the others do not scatter. Scattering makes noise. They press flatter.

And with them, in the dampest crevices, the snails. Hundreds. Amber and banded, the size of a thumbnail. All coiled left, every one, as if the pit had only taught one direction. At night and after drip they emerge and glide over algae, leaving silver braids that are gone by midday, evaporated upward to become tomorrow's dew. They are the slowest memory here. Shell over shell in the cracks, living over dead over living.

The air-drinkers live higher.

The midges, tiny white, rise and fall through the slanted column of light for the few hours it hangs. They never leave the shaft. They drift as if through honey, turning in it, breeding in it. The moths — huge, pale green, owl-eyed, the size of your hand — spend the day as bark. Pressed flat, wings folded, eyespots hidden. At the shift of light they lift without a sound you can hear and settle again a foot away. No wingbeat. The moss and the shape of the stone eat noise; they have learned not to offer any. They lay on the underside of leaves, where the dew will bead and keep the eggs wet without drowning them.

The orchids live on air too, midway down the bare cords of liana and curtain fig. White roots exposed, silver-green, drinking morning condensation straight out of vapor. No soil. They flower seldom, white and waxy, scentless by day, and at night they smell faintly of rot-sweet wine to call the moths.

The plants are not backdrop. They are residents with hungers.

The hangers at the rim: lianas dropping straight twenty meters, bare cord with leaves only at the tip, reaching down like they are trying to drink from the floor without leaving the sun. The curtain figs strangling, tightening grain by grain, splitting limestone, root-thighs coming down a hundred feet and exploding at the last moment into a broom of feeders. The begonias mid-wall, all turned inward toward the light shaft, waxy leaves angled like a crowd turning faces to a speaker, tracking the shaft as it crawls an inch a minute. The aroids unfurling white-stemmed seedlings so crowded they are etiolated from reaching, pushing up through fermenting fig fruit that smells of sick wine.

The builders are smallest and busiest.

Under every overhang, mud-daubers have potted rows of tiny clay jugs, each sealed with a dab. Inside each, a paralyzed spider and an egg. A nursery in miniature pottery. Along one root the size of a road, ants have built paper galleries with off-ramps and refuse piles, farming scale insects for honeydew, carrying leaf bits overhead like sails. In folded leaves lashed with silk, jumping spiders keep tight retreats, peering out with four forward eyes. Everything is working, all the time. Roots tighten. Fruit falls. Flies breed. Fungi eat everything that falls — ears and shelves and tiny orange cups on dung, on leaf, on a dead crab's carapace.

And then the ones who come and go, and the one who stayed.

At dusk — you will miss the moment if you blink — the air lifts. A slit in the lower wall you never noticed in daytime exhales. Hundreds of bats, small bent-winged, funnel up and out without a sound you can catch, spiraling up through the mouth to the plain where the cicadas are still screaming. They are gone all night. Before dawn they pour back, plastering the slit with guano that feeds the ferns below in a green rush. They are the pit's lungs, breathing out at night, in by morning.

High above, swifts cut the mouth in screaming loops. Their cries don't reach down here. Only now and then a feather spirals down through the light, turning and turning, and is claimed before it lands — by ant, by fungus, by crab.

And on the drier side of the floor, no wider than a platter, cleared leaf by leaf: his court.

The bowerbird. Satin, blue-black, yellow eye. Lost, or choosing to stay. He should be up in open woodland, calling, displaying under sun. Here there is only the coin of sun for hours. So he has made a smaller sun. White snail shells — emptied, bleached — set in a circle. Blue beetle elytra, iridescent. And in the center, polished daily with his beak until the edges are soft: one red plastic bottle cap. It must have fallen years ago. It is the only bright manufactured thing here. He tends it, rearranges the shells around it by millimeters, dances for no female, throat puffed, wings quivering silently on the damp litter. He drinks from dew on downward leaves. He eats fallen fig and the occasional stunned midge. He will not leave except by climbing toward the light, and he has decided not to climb.

That is what lives here, finally. Not one thing. An agreement.

A crowded, hot, half-dark animal made of many bodies that has learned to whisper collectively because sound goes into the moss and does not come back. Stay low where the dew forms. Stay damp where the stone weeps. Make no noise, because noise is waste and waste is food and food is scarce except what falls. Drink what condenses. Eat what falls.

If you sit long enough, breathing shallow to match the heat, sweat accumulating, not evaporating, you stop being a visitor and become one more warm surface for condensation to form on overnight. A midge lands on your forearm. A gecko watches you without blinking. The orb-weaver adjusts one thread to your breathing.

The pit, listening so intently to itself, briefly listens to you.
