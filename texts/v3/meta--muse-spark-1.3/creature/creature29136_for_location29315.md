Nothing lives here. That's what you think for the first hour.

It's the only conclusion the air allows. Your lungs are working too hard to argue. No grass to soften a step, no lichen even, to prove something once agreed to stay. No bird to stitch the gap between shoulders. No fly at your eye. The mineral stains lie — that green is copper, that rust-fur is iron — and once you know it's a lie you stop looking for green. You accept: a lifeless crowd of wet stone standing in its own roar.

And then you sit long enough in the lee of someone else's low wall, with your stone added to the top and your blood throbbing thin behind your eyes, and the place starts to correct you.

Not all at once. It does not present itself.

First, the stones.

They are not dead the way valley rock is dead. They are too close together for that, too established, too answered. Shoulder to shoulder in water up to their ankles, three and four deep back into whiteness, they tick in the sun. You hear it if you stop walking: a faint, all-over ticking as heat moves a millimeter into black basalt, as last night's frost in a hairline crack lets go at noon with a ping you feel in your teeth. Once in a while a dull *tok* from deep in the crowd where a spall has peeled and dropped into a pool. No proper echo. The sound comes back flattened, or early.

They weep without stopping. Mist beads on sun-warmed faces, gathers, runs in bright trickles down flutes worn over ten thousand years. Each has a dark wet skirt. Each has an overhang that drips on metronome time. They drink the cloud as it fingers up between them and they sweat it back out. In the basins on their tops, light pools until the water looks solid, poured glass too bright to look into. Do not drink there. Drink from the running veils.

If you stand between two close pillars and watch the mist, you see the air is not empty. Spray hangs. Glare hangs. A drop detaches, falls, then shivers and lifts an inch in a gust you do not feel on your face before falling again. There is no empty volume here. Water, light, wind, stone are layered, near-touching, high and saturated. The density you felt from the pass was not just numbers. It was presence.

That is the first resident: the crowd itself. Slow, cold, bright, listening to its own loud music. Tolerant of you the way an old herd is tolerant of a fly.

The second is faster.

You clap a rock to test it and hear the slap a half-second *before* your hand lands. You shout and the far wall answers faintly first, then your voice catches up. At noon your shadow does not fall away from the sun. It leans toward it, short and black and too dense, pooling in dips the way light pools.

At first you blame altitude. Then you notice it keeps happening in one direction. Sound before cause. Light before wind. If your echo answers first, wait — let it finish. That's one of the rules the place taught whoever built the walls, and they left it in the way the walls curve.

Something here lives a little ahead of you.

You see it in distance, too. That tower with the double cap that looks a hundred meters off: twenty steps and it's still a hundred meters off. Twenty more and it's suddenly right in front of you, water running down a groove you could fit your arm in. And you've seen that groove before. Three towers back. Then again further in. Not exactly, but the way a sentence repeats when you're falling asleep. The far pillars vibrate at the edges and go double and resolve. Under today's hard blue there's a fainter, older white, a yesterday cloud still caught, moving the wrong direction. The sun has a thin double, a ghost sun offset to the left that disappears if you squint.

Time pools here the way light pools and water pools. It gathers in basins, spills, gathers again.

Which is why the third residents are the hardest to see and the only ones who use tools.

People have been here. Low dry-stone walls to break wind around a flat sitting slab. Beehive huts no taller than your chest, doorways facing away from the gust. Hundreds of cairns stacked with impossible patience from flat shards, some topped with a single white quartz cobble that flares like a lamp. Pecked spirals, cupules in rows that fill and overflow in order, ladder lines, hand-sized hollows polished by other hands. A hammerstone left on a wall, end battered round. Black glass debitage sharp as ice where someone sat and worked. A ladder of three long slabs leaned together. A wobbling causeway of flats across the braids.

No metal. No cut timber. Nothing to carry with except your body in air that is not quite enough.

No one is here now. The huts hold only water. One has old fire-blackening washed almost clean. Another has a neat stack of slabs waiting. And yet: a fresh cairn, its stones still darker with new wetness, its top quartz still muddy at the base from being pulled out of the grit *that morning*.

Who keeps building?

You don't see them until the clouds rise in mid-afternoon and visibility drops to thirty meters, then ten, then to the pillar in front of you weeping hard, its trickles turned to runnels. The light doesn't dim so much as diffuse, becoming everywhere at once, shadowless and mercilessly bright. Your clothes are soaked. Your fingers are white and stupid. Your breath plumes and is torn away.

In that white, movement stops belonging to you.

A breath plume torn the wrong way between two pillars. A footprint in the braided skin that milks over *before* your foot lifts. A ticking that quickens as you approach a wall, as if something just set a stone down and stepped back out of phase. The quartz lamps flaring one after another back into whiteness, too regular to be sun.

They are not ghosts. Ghosts are past. These are near. Half-a-second forward, living in the answer-before-the-call.

Think of everyone who ever came up gasping over the pass, learned to keep to the high stones because the water between is always moving and colder than you think, learned to stack if you stop to give weight back for passage, learned to read orange for iron and seep, white for salt and sun, black for always-wet. Think of them breathing that thin sweet stone-dust soup, head throbbing, hands going clumsy, adding one flat black piece that fit the palm because it felt ruder not to. Think of that moment pooling, the way light does. Not passing. Staying.

The valley is crowded not only with stone but with those moments, layered three and four deep, near-touching. Hands polished the hollows and the hollows kept the polish. Feet steadied the causeway and the causeway kept the steadiness. Someone sat in this exact lee, soaked to the bone, watching tendrils condense instantly on cold glass rock, and decided to go on stacking anyway. You are sitting in the dent of it. The stone you just added is already darker with new wetness. For someone coming up tomorrow, or yesterday, it will still be muddy at the base.

Sometimes, if you go very still and let your echo finish first, you catch the fringe of them: not figures, exactly. Denser mist where mist shouldn't hold. A cold spot in spray that keeps its shape for a breath longer than wind allows. A shiver in a hanging drop, lifting. A *tok* from somewhere deep in the crowd that is not spall peeling but shard set on shard, patient, almost touching.

They are thin, because the air demands it. They are slow, because weight at this height can only be moved slowly. They are wet, because nothing here is dry. They do not speak — the organ roar shredded through a thousand flutes would eat it — they listen. To water joining, to veils slapping ledges, to stone warming and shedding skin. They add. They balance. They face their little doors away from the prevailing gust and go on.

You will leave before dark. You must. The cold will deepen just enough to skin the door-sized tarns with ice that will be gone by ten tomorrow, and the ticking will become cracking, and the path to the pass will glaze. But part of you understands already that leaving will be like the echo: you will hear yourself gone before you have stood up, and a part of that hearing will stay, stacked knee-high against wind, weeping bright water down a groove you could fit your arm in, waiting for the next lungs to come thin over the pass and add their weight in return for passage.
