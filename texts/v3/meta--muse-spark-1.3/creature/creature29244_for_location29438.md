At first you think nothing can live here.

It looks too white, too hot, too loud to the eyes and too silent to the ears. Then your eyes adjust to the glare and the valley resolves into inhabitants — not one kind, but a whole stacked reef of them, all tuned to the same two facts: the water is scalding, and you must be quiet.

The people you notice last, because they have learned not to stand out.

They are pale the way the town is pale. Not just their clothes — loose, undyed linen and sinter-cloth the color of bone and ash, long-sleeved at noon despite the 42 degrees because reflected light burns from below — but their manner. They move slowly on the boardwalks, single-file, pausing at crossings. They don't call out. A raised palm, a tilt of the head, two fingers tapped against a pipe means *hot, wait*, *let it pass*, *behind you*. Children learn it before speech: stay on the boards, never on the orange. Don't shout. The old women who tend the blue-marked taps will tell you, half-joking and not joking, that noise dries the springs. So everyone keeps their voice low, throat barely working, as if the vapor takes the sound out of them.

Their bodies have settled into heat. They are lean, salt-cured looking, skin filmed faintly white at the hairline and knuckles with precipitated silica that no washing quite removes. Their teeth, if they are old and careless, are furred at the edges from years of sipping straight ground-water. So they drink only where the blue mark is — water that has fallen three terraces, cooled step by step in the open aqueducts, cut with roof-cistern rain. They wash in the evening runnels, not the morning ones, because the morning water still belongs to the mats and the birds. At eleven they vanish. Awnings come down over every walkway, louvered sheds close, lathes go still in the machine shops. Even the salt-rakers — men and women up to their ankles in pink brine, pulling wooden rakes in perfect lines — lay their tools down and lie in the thick-walled rooms on short piers where steam breathes cool underneath. Rest is not laziness here. Even the crabs slow.

What they do, when they work, is shepherd water, not make things. The water-tenders walk the upper arches with long poles, clearing bacterial skin from the intake so the milky-blue flow keeps falling. The salt crews harvest the pink ridges in the pans, rake, pile, bag for the lowlands. In the glassworks the blowers — stripped to the waist, white with dust — take the local silica and coax it into pale blue jars, working in short bursts because the furnace heat on top of sun heat is unbearable for long. The engineers tend the quiet machines: sealed steam, electric pumps sunk underwater so they tick rather than roar, slow-turning ventilator cowls, the settling tanks crusted orange and teal where minerals fall out like snow in reverse. They trade power down the pylon line through the outflow gap, and food comes back up the basalt road, because nothing green grows here and no one pretends it does. What protein they take from the valley itself they take carefully: a little shrimp paste from the coolest outflows, a few blind fish, never the mat, never the breeding pools.

Below and around them, and far more numerous, is the true abundance — white on white, built on light and sulfur instead of leaves.

It starts as skin. Every pool that is not lethal to touch is rimmed and floored with living mat, inches thick, slick and leathery. Tangerine, mustard, copper-green, blood red. Not plants. Bacteria and archaea, old as stone, eating sun and hydrogen sulfide and dissolved silica, laying down the very terraces the town is built on. The bright orange edge is the living front — a degree cooler than death, a year to regrow if a boot crushes it. It stinks faintly of eggs and iron when you bend close, and it brightens visibly after the daily rain.

On the mat, everything else grazes.

The shallows boil if you stand still long enough to see it. Clouds of translucent shrimp, each the length of a fingernail, flick and hang and flick, filtering mat. Blind white fish, eyeless or nearly, nose along the edges turning a flank to flash. Flat crabs the color of bleached bone pick sideways across the sinter, shells crusted with salt until they creak. They communicate by waving one bleached claw, a semaphore you only catch from the corner of your eye. Between them on damp mud, springtails in black legions — spilled pepper that springs up in a sheet when your shadow passes and resettles.

And over them, the birds. You hear no call, so you keep miscounting them until they move.

Flocks of needle-billed white birds stand leg-deep for hours: stilts with impossible pink legs folded like wire in the warm water, spoonbills sweeping side to side, a kind of ibis whose feathers have gone glassy and warped from constant heat. They feed thick in the morning, rise all at once when startled — a white sheet lifting off white stone — wings beating with a sound like cloth shaken in another room, circle in the trembling haze, and land again without a cry. On the flat roofs, doves the gray of steam sit puffed, cooing with throats that barely vibrate. Under the pipe shadows, sooty-bellied swallows dart, mouths open, harvesting the other great abundance.

For the air at knee-height is alive. Columns of tiny midges rise off the mats and hang, shimmering. Dragonflies the bright blue of pool water hover in them, wings like cellophane, making no whir you can hear. They talk in light, tilting to flash polarized sun off their abdomens. Mirrored beetles walk the handrails deliberately to drink condensation off metal. At dusk — which comes bright and sudden, sun dropping but stone still radiating — the flats go pink and the pale tide comes in: thousands of bone crabs emerging to feed, clicking so softly you feel it in your teeth more than hear it. And with them the white owls, day-white, silent-feathered, hunting by sight alone along the edge of moving shadows.

Because here even predators have taken the vow.

You might watch for an hour a lanky, dog-sized lizard dusted head to tail in white silica until it is indistinguishable from sinter, standing motionless by a runnel. No growl. No splash. One liquid snap and a crab is gone. It blinks sideways and resumes being stone.

Nothing sings. Nothing roars. The whole food web flashes instead: claw-wave, flank-turn, wing-tilt. It is a bright, teeming reef turned down to mute, all of it — human and shrimp and owl alike — living on scald and sun, leaving room, staying on the boards, waiting out the noon lid of vapor until the warm rain hisses down and the pools rise a finger's width and the cycle starts again.
