From the rim they are not people yet.

They are movement. A shifting in the gray. Hands and backs between roofs, a head bobbing up a stair-alley and gone, laundry flicked over a parapet, a goat led from one pen to another. The bowl is so packed and the light moves so fast that at first you can't separate them from the work — they look like the place keeping itself busy. Like ants in a kicked nest that decided to stay kicked.

Go down. The air gets colder around your knees, then your waist, then your mouth, and your breath starts to show. So does theirs. That is the first true thing about them: they live inside their own breathing half the year.

They are people. Shorter than you expect, but not by breeding — by posture. No one who lives here stands fully straight for long. The alleys are a shoulder wide, the ceilings are low to save stone and hold heat, the stairs are steep and uneven and always damp at the lip. So they go bent-kneed, head-forward, shoulder-turned sideways without thinking. Children learn the turn before they learn to talk loud. Old men and women, and there are more old than you would think in such a hard place, move like water down the switchbacks, one hand always trailing on the wall. Their palms and fingertips are permanently gray with rock dust ground into the whorls. You can hear them coming by the scuff, not the step.

Closer still, and the sameness breaks.

What lives here is not a tribe, not a type. It is a density that has learned how to be warm.

Skin: pale underneath the dust, because for half the day a person is in shadow. Even at noon the bottom alleys never quite lose the chill coming off fresh stone. Cheeks redden fast in the morning fog, then go white again. Eyes are used to flicker — sun to shade to lamplight — so they squint less than you do and see better in the two-meter fog at dawn. They find damp the way you would find light: a hand held out to a shared wall, to the back of a slab, to mortar that looks a shade darker. The water-knowers, mostly older girls and grandmothers, can tell by smell if a seep is about to shift.

Hands: That is where they are most themselves. Everyone's hands. Broad-knuckled, short-nailed, cut along the cuticles with tiny white lines where the sharp basalt has kissed them. The mason's hands, the child's hands carrying fines, the pot-mender's hands with copper stains green in the creases. Even the babies have grit under their nails. They touch stone the way other people touch wood — constantly, testing. Cold morning stone rings different than afternoon stone. They do their cutting then, when it is brittle, and the whole hollow learns the rhythm: *tink — tink — pause to listen for the crack — tink.*

They speak low. Not from shyness. From acoustics and attention.

The bowl swallows shouting and returns nothing. So they stopped shouting a generation ago. A name called two roofs over is pitched just under the murmur, and heard. Work crews prying a terrace loose communicate in grunts, in the knock of a mallet handle twice for *hold*, once for *pull*. Mothers hush with a palm more than a voice. At midday, when the spoons are in the bowls — barley, onion tops, goat milk thinned with seep-water, sometimes pigeon egg — you can stand in the center and hear the place drink. Bucket creak. Jar *plip*. Condensation ticking off thatch. A dog sighing in dust and turning over.

They share. They have to. There is no room not to.

A wall is never one family's wall. It is two families back to back, keeping each other warm against the cold that seeps out of new rock all night. A roof is a cousin's floor, a neighbor's garden, a bridge abutment. Every roof drains to someone's jar by law that was never a law, just obvious: you do not let seep-water fall to waste when your neighbor's child is thirsty and the next rain is three days off. So the eaves are all tilted, chipped channels run like veins along every stair, and the children know the map of water better than the map of names. Who gets the morning drip, who gets the evening overflow. To waste is the only rudeness. To hoard is unthinkable — the rock would find out; it weeps somewhere else tomorrow.

They are builders and dwellers at once, and they haven't separated the two. There is no mason caste, no laborer caste, only degrees of knowing stone. A seven-year-old can sort rubble into wall-stone, cap-stone, fill. A twelve-year-old can swing a wedge without smashing a thumb. An old one who can no longer climb sits at the rim of a cutting and tells by sound if the block will come clean. Every new room is a hole made elsewhere, so birth and building are the same verb here. *We are making room,* they say, whether they mean a baby or a back bedroom cut a meter deeper into the eastern wall. And they build inward, downward, never outward onto the grass, because everyone, even the smallest, knows the rim grass holds the edge from crumbling. You can see them check each other without checking — a glance up when a stranger steps too close to the lip.

At dusk they become most visible, and strangest.

When the sun leaves the floor and only the western upper terraces still hold light, they stop. The *tink-tink* stops all at once, as if the stone itself called pause. Lamps come on, hundreds of small tallow flickers set into niches chipped for exactly that purpose, and faces come out to the edges. They sit on roofs shoulder to shoulder, knees touching, sharing a hide against the pooling cold, watching the fog begin to gather white and low between the houses. They do not go inside yet. Inside is for deep night, for sleeping packed tight in one warm room while frost pries outside at yesterday's cut. Dusk is for being together above the fog, breathing together, letting the day's dust settle.

Then you see what the place is making: not just survivors in a quarry. A pit people. Cool-blooded in habit, warm-blooded by crowding. Gray as the rock from a distance, green in small tended patches up close — onion trays, gourd vines, the moss they deliberately coax around a seep and stroke flat so it holds moisture. Goats lean against their legs. Pigeons settle on their scaffolds and are not shooed. Rats run the drainage cuts at their feet and are tolerated the way you tolerate a difficult cousin.

They are embryonic. Like the hollow. Not yet a city, not just a camp. The rock is still sharp and so are they — quick to cut, quick to mend, quick to re-chip a channel when water finds a new path. If you stay a few days you stop seeing individuals and start seeing a single crowded organism learning how to keep itself damp and warm in fresh stone, breathing out fog in the morning, glowing like embers at night, always tinkering at its own edges, downward and inward, deciding what shape it will be.
