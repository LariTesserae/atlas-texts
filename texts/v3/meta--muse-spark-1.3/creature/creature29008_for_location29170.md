In Cleft, at first you think no one lives here.

That's because you are looking for people. Stand on Foundry Row at shift-change and count. A net-tender crossing the chain-bridge with his oilskin hood up. Two mill-girls sharing a cigarette under a buzzing lamp. An old woman carrying a pail up from the cisterns, pausing every six steps to breathe. The plazas are built for thousands and hold twenty, at most, and the rest is dripping and gulls. It feels abandoned until you learn to count differently.

The people are thin-air people. You can hear it before you see it — everyone breathes audibly here, a soft in-out rasp that never quite leaves, even in sleep. Children are born with it. Barrel-chested by ten, red-cheeked from blood working too hard, eyes a little too wet and bright in the dim. They move the way the engines move. No one hurries. No one can. The women on Net Street walk with a short, shuffling, water-saving gait, boots perpetually soaked, waxed coats gone green at the shoulders where moss has taken hold and they have stopped picking it off. The men at the geothermal heart have forearms corded from slow work and molars worn from years of feeling that low hum in their jaws. Their skin smells of iron and wet wool.

They are patient the way geology is patient. A crew will spend eleven years on a viaduct and not call it late. A water-carrier will feel his pail lighten halfway up the steps — that sudden, gut-lifting slack when the plateau forgets to pull — and he will not stop or exclaim. He just shifts his grip mid-stride, the way his father taught him. Old workers all have that adjusted shoulder, one side set a little higher from years of compensating for weight that comes and goes.

The mill-girls taught themselves to live with the early echoes. If you stand outside the crusher hall at dusk you can hear them singing to get the timing right: a line thrown across the crack-canyon and caught half a second before it should come back, so they sing rounds with themselves, two voices out of one mouth, verse and answer overlapping. Children do it playing. Lovers do it without thinking. Shout a name and you answer yourself. People here rarely talk fast or interrupt, because they've already half-heard you.

Near heat, they lengthen. Everyone knows not to run past the smelter wall. That forty-meter walk takes six minutes and leaves even the capra-herders winded, their breath steaming in long ropes. The smelter crews who work that stretched air day in and day out are different — taller-seeming, slower-blinking, their shifts measured in breaths, not hours. They age, people say, on a slightly longer clock.

And they never go inside alone if they can help it. Every Cleft child knows about the shed on Spur Three: eight paces outside, twenty paces deep if you enter alone and don't look back, cold, smelling of wet wool, the far wall receding as you walk. Go in with a partner and it's just tools and a cot. So they go in pairs. They eat in pairs. Night-watchmen make their rounds in twos, talking continuously, keeping the rooms small with sound and witness. Solitude makes spaces too large here. Solitary old folk are the only ones who brave it, and they come back strange-eyed, saying it was quiet in there, so quiet, with plenty of room to become someone else.

But people are the sparse minority. Cleft belongs to everything else.

The true citizens are the capras. You'll see them before you see people, every time — herds of them out on the mossfields between the rail spurs, six-legged, barrel-chested, shag-maned to the fetlocks with dripping hair. Built for air that isn't there. Their nostrils are wide as fists and flare pink with each slow intake; their ribs move like bellows, once, twice per minute, in time with the great house-sized pistons. They graze the pale sweet steam-grass around the outfalls, utterly indifferent to shunting engines that creep past inches from their horns. When rain on Net Street begins to lift — droplets peeling off their manes and rising in slow deliberate lines back into the grey — they lift their great heads all together to watch it go. They chew, and watch, as if remembering when they did that too.

Above them, the air is never empty. High swifts and soot-gulls wheel endlessly in the lamplight between stacks, screaming, screaming, hundreds of them. They don't migrate. There's nowhere higher to go. They nest on ledges of hot ductwork, on crossbeams, in the rusted teeth of abandoned gears, laying speckled eggs in nests of moss and wire shavings. Their bellies flash white when they bank through welding-white light. Beneath them, around every exposed bulb, moths the size of your hand crowd and bumble, dun and eye-spotted, drinking heat. And hunting them, flickers of ash — the bats. Small, black, almost silent except for a dry papery snap as they take a moth mid-air and vanish into the grainy dusk.

Low, underfoot, live the fox-things. Rust-red, dog-lean, with eyes where eyes shouldn't be: two where you'd expect, and then three, four more, small and bright, strung along the cheeks and flanks, unblinking. They den under the cisterns, in the warm dark where pipes sweat, raising litters of blind, mewling kits in nests of stolen textile waste. People let them stay because they keep the maintenance sheds small — a fox-thing staring never lets a room grow — and because they eat the rats that would eat the steam-cabbages. At night their surplus eyes catch lamplight in the alleys like wet rivets.

And then the crack things. No one has seen the bellower. You only hear it: at slow intervals from the absolute vertical dark of those three-hundred-meter cracks, a bellow too deep to locate, felt more in the sternum than the ears. The geologists say it's air moving. The night crews know better than to argue.

What climbs up from it they have seen, in glimpses. On windless nights things with wet feet climb the knapped basalt to lick salt from the rails. They come after the last cable-car. You hear them first — a soft pat-pat-pat on iron, a rasping lick — and if you swing your lamp fast you'll catch a pale, eyeless flank, a fringe of gill, a six-fingered foot pulling back into dark before your light can hold it. They are unfinished, like the rock. Blind tadpole-white, seam-gilled, neither amphibian nor mammal, leaving silver snail-trails on the rails that the morning shift hoses off without comment. They only want salt. They only want what the low world sweats out of metal.

Even the green is an inhabitant. Lichen the color of verdigris and bone tattoos the factory walls until concrete looks like skin. Cushion moss three feet thick swallows lava and rebar alike. Dwarf birch and ironwood, bent double, hold their waxy leaves to every gap. Ferns spill from broken pipes. People don't fight it. If you're not using a wall this season, you let the moss take it. Your coat will fur anyway. Your boots will grow green at the seams. To live in Cleft is to be half-colonized and not mind.

That is what lives here, then: slow-lunged people who sing with their echoes and walk in pairs to keep rooms true, herds that breathe with engines and remember the sky, birds that never land far, moths that crowd light, bats like ash, many-eyed foxes that watch space into shape, pale climbers that drink salt, and one deep voice below that never shows itself. All crowded together in the leaks and gaps of a half-built town, steaming faintly in cold air, dripping, patient, leaving room around themselves for the place — and themselves — to finish being born.
