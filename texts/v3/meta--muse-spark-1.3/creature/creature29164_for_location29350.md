At first you think nothing lives here. That is what the bowl wants you to think.

From the rim it is dust and dim copper air and far-apart shadows. Then you come closer and the shadows resolve into presences, and the presences turn out to be crowded inside themselves.

They have to be. Nothing is shared here. A hundred paces of singing dust between one thunderleaf and the next — that is the law. So each one has learned to be a country unto itself.

The thunderleaf is the oldest citizen. Not old as in dying. Old as in finished becoming and now only maintaining. A squat trunk, waist-high to chest-high, corked and fissured until its bark reads exactly like the basalt it grows out of. You could put your palm on it and not know where rock stops. Out of the top erupts the jungle: twenty, thirty leaves the size of blankets, thick as felt rugs, furred on both sides with dense grey-white hair. Black-purple underneath, rust at the curled edges where the sun-gauze catches them.

They do not drink. Tear one — and you will only do it once, the stink of resin and scorched hair drives you back — and there is no sap, no wet. Only dry white pith, fibrous as wool, pulling in strings. The leaf lives by combing. The wind over the rim never stops bringing its meal: fine salt, ash, pumice powder, pollen-husk, powdered shell of insects dead leagues below. The fur holds a static charge. Put your ear to a leaf on a windy day and you hear it, faint: crackle, tick, tick, as the dust strikes and sticks. The leaf exhales oil to dissolve what it can use and seals the rest away. That is why the margins are beaded.

Wax is the water here. Where a lowland leaf would drip, this one accretes. Amber beads swell along every edge, grow heavy, harden into knobs the size of a thumb-joint. The lee side is always thickest, sheltered from scour, and if you learn to hear — a deep sail-slap when the gust hits, low and slow — you know which crown is heavy. They never fall. They wait to be chewed.

Under and between them lives the ground fur. Bellows-moss, which is not moss, forms low black cushions that give under your boot and then puff up again with a sigh, exhaling dust. Carpet-lichen spreads in grey scabs that break into powder and re-knit. And the chime-trees, the nervous adolescents of the bowl: ten feet of spindly, knotted wood, every branch hung with paper-dry gourds, each with a handful of loose seeds inside. In even a light gust they go off — thousands of dry maracas, arrhythmic, insistent, beautiful. When locusts come they can be stripped naked in four minutes, standing rattling and bare, and by evening they have already pushed out pale, wetless furls, furled tongues to catch the next fall.

Because the animals never let it rest.

You will think for a minute it is empty. Then the air tears. An ash-locust swarm lifts off a thunderleaf all at once, a black glittering sheet unfolding, wings rasping like files on slate. They are not eating green — there is no green — they are eating wax and fur and each other's shed skins. Dust-moths the size of your hand blunder after heat, thudding blindly into your chest, into rocks, into glider wings, laying dry eggs that hatch into wax-eaters. Pouch-mice boil up from under the moss in a chittering flood, cheeks and belly-pouches bulging with stolen wax-knobs, stealing from leaf to cache under stone. And over it all the veil-gliders.

They are mammals, though they look like weather. Manta-thin, wide as your outstretched arms, furred membranes stretched between elongated fingers, long-snouted heads with nostrils fringed in hair to sieve dust. They launch from the lee of rocks and organ-pipe flutes and scream to each other as they fly — high needle whistles that cut through the rim-hum and the gourd-clatter, echolocation in the gloom where color is useless. They chase moths, moths harass locusts, locusts startle gliders. Molts drift through it all like snow: transparent wing-cases, shed fur, hooked down from the thunderleaf seed-pods that split with a gunshot crack and fire their plumes into the wind. Everything lands, catches on moss, becomes soil, becomes dust to be combed again.

Kill anything here and it does not bleed. It oozes warm wax for a minute and then dries to husk within the hour. Its pellets, its husk, its molt crumble back to powder. A closed dry circle.

And moving light through the circle, tending it, are the ones who learned to tie knots.

You will not see them at first. They are the color of the ground fur, they go quiet between gusts when predators glide, and there are very few of them — three, four in the whole three-hundred-pace bowl at any time, never together. Hermit-herders. Wax-tenders. Whether they were always people or became something else here is hard to say.

They are short and compact and settled, like the thunderleaves. Skin thickened and salt-caked until it squeaks, lips sealed dark with chewed wax, tongues cracked from licking dust. They do not sweat — you stop sweating here within a day. They do not urinate wet. What little bound moisture is freed by chewing wax they hold in the mouth and nose, breathing shallow through hairy nostrils, exhaling into cupped hands to catch it back. Their hair has felted into mats that comb dust exactly the way leaf-fur does. Their eyes are narrowed to slits against the permanent ember air, milky at the edges. Their ears have become their eyes: large, cupped, always turning. In a place with no water to reflect light, with dim air and far-apart giants, you do not find your way by sight. You find it by sound.

Watch one work and you learn the trade. Dawn, no dew. He — she — it goes to the lee of its leaf with an obsidian comb, teeth worn smooth, and scrapes. Not stripping. Tending. Clearing the sealed wax so the fur can comb again, taking the oldest knobs and leaving the weeping beads. Chewing a knob slowly for food, working the jaw for an hour. The wax smells sweet when burned — the fire-pit in the low ring of stacked basalt has never boiled water, only melted wax and charred wax-wood hot and smokeless — and sweet when chewed, with that after-bite of scorched hair.

They herd as much as harvest. The bone fence of ribs interwoven with fiber, now broken and half-buried, was built to funnel pouch-mice. They take a few mice, slit them for their warm wax and their pouches full of concentrated knobs, let the rest run. They shake a chime-tree and listen: the hollow gourd that rings fullest has the most seed, and seed pounded with wax-dust makes cake. They hang cracked bells of fired clay from a tripod of knotted chime-wood, tied with glider-hide and sinew, to call or to scare — a sharp clay clack to scatter locusts off a young furl, a low tone to warn a glider off. On the cairn they set a long-snouted glider skull facing the wind, not worship exactly, but acknowledgement. Listening made visible.

They live light because they have to move. One thunderleaf cannot support chewing every day. So they walk the hundred paces of open dust to the next, cloaked in powder, clothes full of it, ears ringing with the rim's low molar-hum and the slap of blanket-ears and the clatter overhead. Between gusts they freeze and tilt the head. That silence is where things hunt.

If one dies, there is no burial. Like everything here it dries to husk and unmakes to dust within sight, its felt and hide left for moss to catch. The marks half-erase: charcoal, a comb left on a rock, a fallen ring.

They are not lush despite dryness. They are lush because of it — curled, layered, overlapping, carrying everything they need on their backs, accreting instead of flowing. Chewing, breathing powder, listening.
