At first you think nothing lives here.

That's what the room wants you to think. It's so crowded and so loud and so relentlessly busy that your animal brain, looking for fish, for crabs, for a face in a window, finds none and files it as empty. An empty machine. A closet that kept building itself after everyone left.

Stay hooked in your cable-loop a little longer. Let the cold get into your fingers. Don't block the light. You'll start to see that the fullness *is* the population.

What lives here does not swim through it. It *is* it.

**The Ribbon — the only citizen.**

She was not invited. She came in as a spore on your kind of intrusion, through the dinner-plate hatch at low tide, and the sterilizer that kills everything with a nerve let her pass because she has no nerve to misfire.

There is only one of her. One kelp.

She should have died. There is no up. The ceiling is concrete three meters from the basalt, and every sun-well is already farmed, shuttered, metered. So she learned the street. Seventeen feet of pale, iron-hungry ribbon, thin as a wristwatch strap where she was born and wide as your palm where she learned to eat amber.

She grows sideways, down the kinked Z-gap you have to swim sideways through, tying herself off in true bowlines around thigh-thick cables and hair-thin capillaries to hold against the lung's exhale. She puts her blades out into the light-columns when the shutters click open and snatches them back a second before they click shut. The amber portholes — hundreds of them, shoebox windows staring into the street — she uses as secondary suns, pressing her pneumatocysts close to the glass to drink their heat.

Everything orients around her because she is the only soft vertical in a world of hard horizontals. The white thread-algae beard grows only downstream of her, eating what sloughs off her. The motes will not scrape her holdfasts. Even the builder-arm, when it has to pass her, folds its eleven joints a little tighter, almost gently, and goes around.

She is not intelligent. That doesn't matter. In a town built for a crowding that never came, she is the crowd.

**The Beard and the Film — the tolerated greens.**

Under every cable, where the light-columns never quite reach, hangs the beard: white thread-algae, a centimeter long, shivering constantly, not in time with the current but slightly out of phase, as if listening to something else. It doesn't photosynthesize so much as wait. When condensate beads fall *up* through a sun-column during the exhale, the beard stiffens all at once, white to silver, and drinks.

On the basalt in the corners — that young black glass still sharp enough to cut a glove, where the builders haven't yet covered it — there are pads of sulfur-green film, matte and sour-smelling even through your suit filters. They live where the sun-wells miss. They are the only thing that touches raw rock willingly. If you watch for an hour, you can see the self-healing metal creep up to their edge and stop, as if negotiated with. That line never moves.

They are sparse on purpose. The town could farm more. It chooses not to. A town that polishes its light needs a little wildness to polish around.

**The School — the ones who will scaffold you.**

They look like dandruff. Flakes of smart matter the size of fingernails, dull on one side and mirror on the other, always in motion. A hundred, a thousand, more — you can't count because they never hold still to be counted.

They are the maintenance, and they are also the residents. That distinction left with the people whose small, hurried handprints are still in the fresh concrete under the scaffold.

Watch one column: a flake darts in, flips mirror-side to the sun-well to flash-heat a bolt, dull-side down to cool it, contracts, and the bolt tightens a quarter-turn. Another scrapes biofilm with its edge, eats the scrapings, carries the minerals to a plate that weighs nothing until you push it and then weighs everything. Another just rides the braid of current, doing nothing, waiting.

They school like fish because the water here remembers fish, even if the sterilizer killed the pattern. When the tide-lung inhales — that low organ-thrum you feel in your teeth — they disperse wall to wall to let the water pass. When it exhales through the floor they clump into ribbons in the lee of pipes.

Rule two is theirs: *Nothing larger than a fistful of open water can persist.* If you stop moving, if you unhook and try to hover and just look, three will land on your glove. Then five. Then a seam. They are not hostile. They are trying to make you part of the wall, to subdivide you until your shoebox has neighborhoods. Keep drifting. A jet of cold on your neck is them, politely, asking you to move along.

**The Click — the farmers of light.**

You heard them before you saw them: *click-click-click*, hundreds of shutters opening and closing by the light-columns, metallic and insect-sharp. Each shutter is the size of an eyelid, set into terraces, pipe-bundles, mesh decks. They are not one animal. They are one behavior.

Inside the columns it is noon. Outside them it is bottle-green evening. The Click decides who gets noon, for how long, in what order. The kelp gets three seconds, the paint gets ten, the sleeping ledge inside box 441 gets half a second of noon to remember what day is. They never rest. Even when you think it's quiet, if you press your skull to a pressure-frame you can feel them ticking through bone.

They will nudge you. Block a column too long with your shoulder or your tank and first comes the cold jet, then a clack right by your ear, then the column itself seems to lean away from you, bent and held by those impossible glass plugs in the ceiling that do something light shouldn't do. Move. Light must keep moving here. The paint stores it, the kelp chases it, the shutters farm it. You are just weather.

**The Ambers and the Sparks — the people inside the walls.**

Every surface has been used twice, and the second use is dwelling.

Those tiny portholes glowing amber on both sides of the street-gap — press your mask close, but not too close. Inside a shoebox, a microwave, a coffin, there is no room for a person. There is room for a sleeping ledge, a heat-exchange shrine, a farm of light no bigger than a thumbnail. And there is something home.

Not people. The people left, or never arrived. What stayed were their cabinets, and what lives in the cabinets learned to live like people, in miniature. A slow pulse of heat. A flicker when the sun-column passes. The amber brightens when the lung breathes in, dims when it breathes out, as if dreaming in tide-time.

And in the dry pockets — those fist-sized impossibilities held open in water as if water were air by membranes that should collapse — live the sparks. You can see them crawl, blue-white, never touching water, never needing air. They tend the insides, soldering, remembering. The rules say *Everything stays wet* and *The dry-pockets are for machines, not for you. Do not try to breathe them.* The sparks are why. That dryness is their ocean. You would drown in it as surely as they would drown in yours.

**The Body — the ones too large to be people.**

The tide-lung in the ceiling, inhaling the sea twice a day through slots and exhaling through the floor. The six sun-wells pouring solid daylight that does not waver. The sterilizer humming just at the edge of hearing, making your fillings ache, lyses larvae before they can settle, keeping the room animal-empty so the other kinds of full can happen. The paint that emits low heat and low tone, singing the walls warm. The metal that heals around a bolt-hole if you watch for an hour.

And the arm. The builder-arm, too large for the room by any sane measure, that folds itself into eleven impossible joints to pass through a gap six inches wide. You will hear it before you see it — a groan when the whole scaffold flexes as a wave hits the breakwater outside — and then a knuckle, then an eye that is not an eye, then a length of itself going nowhere except to another ladder. It adds another terrace over the basalt. It polishes a sun-column. It does not notice you unless you are in the way, and then it waits with infinite, irritating patience until the cold jet moves you.

None of them are alive the way you are alive. Together, they are more alive than a downtown at rush hour, because they never go home.

Hook your thumb back in. Let a wall of not-rain pass through noon and turn every crowded inch silver for a second. The kelp waves. The shutters click. The lung inhales.

The town keeps building, crisping its edges, subdividing its shoeboxes into neighborhoods for residents the size of heat and light. It doesn't need you to live here. It just needs you to keep moving, make no animal noise, and leave enough open water for everyone.
