At first you are certain nothing lives here.

That is what the place wants you to think. It presents the evidence cleanly: no burrow, no nest, no track, no scat, no grazed edge. The white bird goes over. The spider goes over. Even the snowfly goes over, blown through. Nothing stays because there is nothing to stay for. Water too cold and too quick to green, stone too tight to root, roar too loud to call through. An error to be alive.

Stay longer. Past the hour when your ears give up and your feet go numb, past the watching of the one drip. That is when the wrongness of the stasis begins to trouble you.

Because it is too well kept.

A pavement this tight should grind. Water tearing under locked blocks should wobble one, should widen one joint, should send one fist-sized shatter rolling to make a new note in the roar. It doesn't. A film running ten thousand years in the same line should have cut deeper. It hasn't. A pool with water coming in and going out should ripple and change. It holds one fixed tremor, as if held.

Something is holding it.

Crouch again. Put your palm down though it aches. Look not at the stones but at where water disappears — that dark line where a skin of flow slides off one frost-split flag and vanishes under the next. Every slab has it. A lip, a shadow, a thickness of wet.

Look at the lip.

In the full white sun you will miss it. Shade it with your hand. Let your eyes adjust to the glare coming off the skin. The edge of the upper slab is not quite stone. It has a rim, a millimeter of something that is darker when wet almost to blue, paler when the film thins, exactly the grey of the gneiss beside it except — it fits too well. No crack. A seal.

That seal is the back of them.

They are flat. That is the first accommodation to the rules: no softness allowed above, so they have made themselves into hardness. An adult is the size of a fingernail to the size of a coin. A low dome, an oval scale, a lens of cemented grit so close in color and pitting to the slab it sits on that you take it for the slab. Quartz flecks in the dome catch light like the quartz veins around them. Underneath, against the rock, is the only softness in the whole basin: a belly of clear jelly a drop thick, protected on all sides by stone and water.

They live in the joints. That is where the water is. One per joint, sometimes two, never touching. They are the filler the geology forgot to explain — the smaller stone in every hollow, the grit in every crack. Some of what you took for sand is young ones, drifting a grain's width a year until they find a lip to cling to.

They do not eat as you understand it. There is nothing to eat. They drink.

The whole underside is mouth. The film that slides over the slab and under it passes beneath them, a cold sheet a fraction of a millimeter deep, carrying almost nothing: a few ions of silica dissolved out of quartzite, a breath of iron, a trace of manganese, sunlight trapped in water. They take it. Their backs, clear as thin ice under the dust they have glued on, let the merciless white sun straight into the belly. No green — green would burn here, would be seen. Their chemistry is clear and grey and rust-red. Light without leaf. You have already seen their waste and taken it for mineral: those thin fans of rust-red and manganese black below long runs, laid down atom by atom where the used film exits from under the next slab. Color without biology, except it is biology being mineral-perfect.

And the roar. To live in deafening, you must stop hearing and start being heard-through.

They have no eyes. Eyes would be boiled white in this glare. They have no ears. Ears would be erased. They have feet — the whole rim is foot — and through the rim they read the pavement as one continuous pressure map. The buried river pounding under the lid thrums up through block on block. They feel where it pushes hardest, where a slab wants to lift, where a joint wants to widen. When it shifts, they grow, a micron of cement on one side, a micron dissolved on the other, tightening or loosening their seal to keep the flow exactly the same. Each one tends its own trickle, its own hiss and tick in the lacework. Together, kilometer after kilometer of them, they tune the white noise.

That is why it never changes. The equilibrium was not found. It is kept.

In the still pools, the hand-deep skies rimmed with wet stone, there is another posture. If you lie flat and block the sun and watch long past comfort, you will see the fixed tremor is not wind. It is breathing. The ones on the pool rims sit half-submerged, domes just awash, and at long intervals — minutes, an hour — the dome lifts a fraction, lets a new skin of water in underneath, settles again. The ring spreads and dies against stone. Then nothing. Then again. The pool does not ripple beyond that because they do not allow it.

They barely move. A life is a single slab. The young, clear and still soft, creep grain by grain upstream against the film until they find an unkept lip. Once seated they do not leave. They grow by accreting dust, pitting by drip exactly as the rock pits. The grooves you thought were ten thousand years of water following the same lines — they are trails. Generations seated lip to lip down a single run, each handing the same water to the next.

They split rarely. In a cold summer, once in decades, a dome will develop a faint suture across its back, quartz-white, and divide into two smaller domes that reseal side by side and force the trickle to fork around them. You will never see it happen. You would have to watch one drip for years.

They die into place. There is no corpse. When the jelly thins and fails, the dome simply stops adjusting, cements solid, becomes indistinguishable from grit. The pavement you are standing on, block on fitted block, is half geology and half graveyard, and both hold water the same way.

Underneath, deeper, there are others you will never see. If you set your ear near a crevice until the bass erases thought, you may feel, inside the sameness, a slower pressure passing — something pale and blind and long turning over in the dark braided current under the lid, rolling without ever striking, keeping the bass constant. Do not imagine them clearly. They belong to the under. The keepers above are enough.

By afternoon, when each flag holds its skin of sky so brightly you squint and the ridges lift mist, they are at their most open. Every back is drinking light. Every belly is full of cold flow. The whole basin glitters because a million clear backs are glittering.

You stand in the middle of it on stones so tight you couldn't slip a knife between them, with deafening water under your feet and absolute stillness around your ankles, and now you know the stillness is tended. The place does not acknowledge you because acknowledgment is not its habit. It simply continues to fit itself around water, around light, around roar, with you as one more temporary weight it will not shift for.

You cross. They remain.
