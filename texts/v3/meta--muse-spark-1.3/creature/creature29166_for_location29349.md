At first you think nothing lives here.

Not really. The crab, the midges — those are visitors, commuters. The moss and the fern are holding on at the edges, the way lichen holds on to anything. Nothing here looks *at home*. The place is too loud, too bright, too restless. The floor remakes itself hourly. The water scalds. The air is wrung out and then wrung again. You think: this is a place that keeps happening, not a place that is inhabited.

Then you stand under the noon and look up longer than is comfortable, until your eyes water, and you see that the mineral bloom is wrong.

On the underside of the ring — that dark, segmented, impossible ring hanging fifteen meters up with nothing holding it — where the rising threads kiss it and flash to steam, there is a staining you took for sinter. White, pinkish, orange at the rims. Only sinter doesn't breathe.

It does. Very slowly. If you brace your vision against the shudder of the segments and watch one patch, you see it swell almost imperceptibly and then flatten, swell and flatten, on a cycle longer than your own breath, closer to the surge-cycle of the pan below. It is layered like nacre, like a reef, overlapping scales of living crust lapped over dead machine, over dark metal, until you can't tell where one ends. Lichen will grow over both without preference. So has this.

That is the body.

It is not *on* the ring. It *has* the ring. The segments — those ponderous dark plates that slide past each other and realign and occasionally jolt inward — they are its skeleton now, or its shell. Look into the gaps where they do not quite touch. You expect to see sky through. You don't. There is a wet dark bridge in each gap, glistening, veined, flexing. Muscle. When the tone changes, when that low C you feel in your molars climbs half a note, that is those muscles tensing, hauling the plates a few centimeters in or out, tuning.

It is a sessile animal the size of a house, draped over and through a machine it did not build, grown so thoroughly into it that the machine has become climate for it, and it has become maintenance for the machine.

The threads are its doing.

What you took for rising water — those sewing-thread filaments lifting off the churn, wobbling, joining, breaking and rejoining — look closer. The water is only the sheath. Inside each there is a core, thinner than hair, almost invisible until a bead slides down it and catches light: a filament of mucus the animal extrudes downward. It wicks. By capillarity and by charge — the air there tastes metallic for a reason, the hairs on your arms stand up — it draws hot, silica-rich water up off the pan toward the belly of the ring.

And the beads.

Hundreds of thousands of them, perfectly round, hanging motionless in concentric slowly rotating spheres around the thrum. You thought acoustic levitation, some field effect of the ring. You are not wrong, but it is farmed. The animal sings them into place. That continuous white-noise roar of falling water, that deep thrum underneath, that sizzle and tick — inside that, held like a still pocket, is its note. It holds the shells with sound and static. Each bead is seeded.

Each bead is a tiny suspended pond. In it, lime-green algae, sulfur bacteria, larval polyps of the animal itself, all turning hard sun and dissolved mineral into living tissue. They hang and ripen. When a surge comes up from below — that invisible lift that raises the whole sheet by a hand's breadth and sends it slapping to the lowest terrace — the innermost shell shivers. You were told: when they tighten and the hum climbs, get up two terraces. The animal feels the pressure wave before you do, in its gut, in the silt. It tightens the field to protect the crop. The ripe beads it lets fall, pattering down to seed the silt and the back-pools. The unripe it holds. New threads rise immediately to replace the fallen.

This is why the moss fruits after the big surges. This is why the midges cloud over the cooler pools. The falling beads fertilize everything. The small white blind crab that walks sideways across the hot mud and vanishes into holes that fill with water behind it — it is the clean-up crew. It eats dead fall, burst beads, aborted polyps. It aerates the silt with its burrows. Its holes are the little vents that open and close, belching steam. It does not see the reef above because it does not need to. It lives in its exhalations.

Even the glassy jointed things in the brackish back-pools, half plant half salt, that rattle when the wind moves — those are shed joints. Now and then the reef sloughs a fringe cirrus from its underside, a jointed filament crusted with salt. It falls, it roots in silt, it goes on photosynthesizing a while on its own, rattling. Nothing is wasted.

The whole animal is careful, the way the vegetation is careful. Nothing tall. Nothing that needs quiet. It has no eyes. It does not need them in this overexposed bowl where shadows shrink to nothing at noon and the undersides of overhangs glow with bounced light. It sees by vibration and by light-pressure and — this is the part that unsettles the stomach — by anticipation.

Do not shout near the center. Not from reverence. Your voice comes back wrong, arriving a fraction before you speak, doubled by the field. That is not an echo. The field around the shells is dense with charge, and the animal, which lives its whole life listening for the half-note climb before a surge, which lives by predicting pressure a second ahead, extends that prediction to you. It hears the shape of your shout in the intake of breath, in the tensing of throat, and sings it back a fraction early. You feel sick because for a moment two times overlap.

It notices you the way it notices weather. A shadow — like the hawk's shadow that crosses the water at intervals, the hawk itself never descending because the lift is wrong and the air tastes of lightning — a shadow is a cooling, a dip in photosynthesis in a thousand beads. A footstep on dark stones is a tremor it can ignore. A footstep on white crust — where you were told not to step, where it is hot and thin — is a burn and a break, a hole punched in its garden silt. It will seal it with fresh mineral, white rim inside white rim like growth rings.

It is old. Older than the walkway of grated metal now half-collapsed with its supports dissolved to orange stalactites. Older than the white hut with the fused cable, older than the bootprints cast in sinter mid-stride, older than the painted 7 wept over with calcite. It did not build the ring or the three leaning black pylons bearded with lichen to a sharp clean wash-line. It found them, the way moss finds a crack. It grew through the gaps, learned the thrum, learned to flex to tune it, learned to milk the upwelling.

What it does, patient, misaligned, weeping light and water upward into blinding air, is hold the pit open. Without its constant wicking and flashing to steam, without its harvesting of silica into living tissue and shell, the vents would choke, the pan would crust over white and seal, the walls would slump. The earth wants to close it. The water wants to drown it. It keeps it breathing — surge in, surge out, ribs of silt left steaming — the way you keep a wound open to drain.

Stay long enough and you feel it notice you less as intruder than as temporary mineral. Condensation pearls on your eyebrows. Your shirt sticks. Your ears ring after. You are, to it, a brief cool surface in a hot place, a little falling water that will leave and be replaced, like a bead.

It will still be there, tuning its dark plates a few centimeters at a time, holding its spheres of suns in the air, when the stair wears a little deeper in the middle and the next feet come down.
