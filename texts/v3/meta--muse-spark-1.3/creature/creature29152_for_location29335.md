At first you think nothing lives here. Then you think a lot of things live here.

Both are wrong. What lives here is one thing, wearing a lot of bodies.

You have to learn to see it the way you learned to hear the place — not as objects scattered on black rock, but as parts held at exactly the right distance from each other. The twenty green ones far apart with a lot of nothing between them. The white columns of midges hung in the air between them. The birds drawing the same loop over all of it. The screamers posted one to a rock. Spacing is anatomy here.

Start with the green ones, because they are the warmest.

They are not moss mounds. The moss is only their skin. Up close you can get your hands into it — and you will, because your hands will be numb and they steam — and feel that it is not rooted to anything. Under the saturated dome the lava is clean. The whole island sits *on* the stone like a bird sits on a nest, not growing out of it. Lift a handful of that quivering green and underneath it is hot dark, breathing.

Because inside each one is a gut. A low, slow, churning fermentation that never finishes digesting because it is never allowed to finish. Old fern fronds collapse inward, beetle husks and drowned midges collapse inward, the water they drink collapse inward, and bacteria like tiny stokers burn it all back into heat. Put your palm flat and you feel it: 98, 99 degrees in a place that never gets above 40. They run a fever forever, on purpose.

That is why they steam. That is why snow — if snow ever fell in this nailed-down blue hour — would never settle on them.

They are furnaces that learned to grow fur to hold their heat. The mosses, the liverworts like wet leather, the heavy ferns with their always-about-to-drip tips: those are not separate tenants. That's pelt. Living insulation, kept wet to keep conducting, kept green to keep photosynthesizing just enough sugar to throw back on the fire. They do not spread because a furnace does not spread. It stays where it is banked. Twenty hearts, banked around the floor, holding the bowl a few degrees above death.

And every heart needs hands.

The beetles. At first you take them for infestation — thousands per mound, black and copper, clicking over the dome. Then you watch one. It doesn't eat. It doesn't mate. It goes down into the sodden underside where water pools against hot moss and threatens to drown the fermentation, takes a droplet in its mandibles, climbs — up through the labyrinth of stems, out into the crackling air where blue sparks crawl on its back — and sets it on a drip-tip at the top. Then it goes back down for another. Another beetle takes the droplet from the tip and carries it back down. Up, down. Up, down.

Type-keys. Rivet guns. That's what they sound like, thousands of elytra clicking.

They are not carrying water anywhere. They are carrying heat somewhere. Each droplet is a little bucket of temperature, moved from where there is too much to where there is too little, thousands of times a minute, so the inside never cooks and the outside never cools. You will watch a single copper one for an hour making the same six-inch climb and you will understand: this is shoveling coal. This is tending. When one wears out — and they must wear out — another comes up out of the warm dark already knowing the route. The pile never gets bigger. The pile never gets smaller. The crew changes, the shift doesn't.

Above them, the choir holds the air still enough for work to happen.

The columns looked empty until you stopped looking for big animals. Then: white midges, millions, holding themselves in vertical pillars between the furnaces, each pillar humming at a slightly different pitch. Together they make that chord that hurts your teeth. They don't drift. Wind shears over the rim hard enough to sing the needle-eyes in the rock, and the columns bend a little and come back. They don't get eaten down, though the moths and birds must be hungry. They don't rise or settle.

They are tuning forks. Each column holds a note and holds its place so the bowl has a standing wave to stand on. That's why the loudness doesn't fade with distance. That's why your breath hangs and then vanishes all at once. That's why a detached drop can stop an inch below a fern tip and tremble there, never landing. The air here is sewn tight with sound, warp and weft, and small light things get caught in the mesh.

The moths are the bobbins. Ash-grey, thumb-big, flapping in place over each black mirror-pool without ever landing, without ever tiring — because to land would be to break rhythm. Their wings beat just out of phase with the midge chord, stirring the boundary layer over the water so evaporation happens at exactly the same rate as the walls weep in. Inflow equals outflow. Level never rises. Level never falls. They are the levelers, rowing in air to keep water still.

And over it all, the conveyor.

Hundreds of white ptarmigan-things, black-eyed, circling rim to rim and never leaving: up the east wall, across, down the west, across, up again. Always the same loop. From below you hear their wingbeats join the piston-breath of the seeps — canvas-flapping, belt-slapping — and every so often one opens its beak and lets out a perfect two-tone steam-whistle, and another answers.

You will check three times to be sure the echo comes *before* the call. It does.

They are not flying. They are circulating. In a body this small with no wind inside to speak of, something has to move the air from warm to cold and back, or the cloud ceiling would collapse or rain out. So they do it with bodies. Up the east wall they drag damp air up into the cold lid, where it beads on their feathers — that's why their wingtips crackle blue — and across they let it condense, and down the west wall they drag the cold sink back down to the furnaces to be reheated. A conveyor belt made of birds. They carry nothing because what they carry is the air itself. They make nothing because what they make is circulation.

That's why they never land. A blood cell doesn't get tired and sit on the artery wall.

On the ground, the governors scream to keep the speed constant.

The whistlers. Marmot-like but too long, too wet, their fur spiked with condensed water and crawling with St. Elmo's fire. Each sits up on its own shard — you will memorize which rock belongs to which, because hours later they will still be there — and pumps its chest, bellows, bellows, and lets out a sustained mechanical scream like a saw biting stone. Throats throbbing. Not alarm. Not song. A reference tone.

They are listening to themselves. In this bowl where sound doesn't decay, a loud sustained note will build and cancel and build again. The screamers hold their notes until they lock with the midge chord and the turbine whine over the rim and the piston-gulp of the walls. When they are locked, everything holds: the drops hang, the midges hold station, the furnaces burn even. If the wind shifts and the rim sings sharp, they bend their scream a quarter-tone to pull it back. You can see their flanks heaving with the effort. Bellows keeping a forge at pressure.

When you turn your head fast and the turbine whine lags half a second behind with a click — that's them, correcting. Catching up.

So: furnaces for heat, beetles for conduction, moths for water level, midges for acoustic scaffolding, birds for circulation, screamers for tuning. No soil, no smoothing, no rust, no lichen creep, no young getting old, because every process that would age the place is caught by another process and burned as fuel to keep it young. Sharp aa stays sharp because nothing is allowed to complete. Water weeps in, steams up, condenses down. Sugar burns to stay exactly as warm, exactly as big. Production without product.

What lives here, then, is not twenty mounds and some bugs and birds. What lives here is the mill.

An untouched mill with no housing, made of breath and beetle and bird-circuit and weeping stone, running at full power forever to keep everything exactly as it is. A single cold-blooded, warm-hearted animal the size of a room, curled rim-to-rim around its own weather, refusing to become ground, refusing to become stream, refusing to leave or die or grow up. Infant and ancient at once — the rock so newly broken it's still glassy, the work so practiced the echo has learned to arrive early.

Stand in the middle long enough and your own breath gets hired. Your fog hangs with the others, then vanishes all at once into the ceiling to be wrung out again. Your hair lifts with the charge. Your teeth ache with the chord. For ten minutes, rim to rim, you are a temporary organ — a mobile warm spot, a little extra water, a pair of eyes to notice the shade that isn't a shadow dim the moss.

It will keep you exactly as you are, too, while you're inside. It won't let you leave a mark. It doesn't need one. It is already full.
