No one walks here.

That is the first thing to understand about who lives here. From standing height it looks uninhabited. No track, no burrow, no dung, no hum. Nothing tall has learned how to stand the noon. Nothing with a mouth has any business here yet — there is almost nothing tender to eat, and nowhere to hide to eat it.

Get low, and then lower, and the place stops being empty. It was never empty. You were just looking at the wrong scale.

The ones who live here most completely are the place itself.

The floor is not ground that life sits on. The floor *is* life, laid down. Meters thick, tile after tile of faintly domed plates, lamina on lamina, light and dark, light and dark, a season per millimeter. Put your hand on it at noon and what you feel as heat and sandpaper and shell is the top of a body.

They are not green. That is the strange part. The green things are tenants, later, in the cracks. The builders are underneath, in the wet dark, where light never fully reaches. Pale, colorless, blind films — sheets of cells too small to see individually, living pressed together in slime they excrete from themselves.

In daylight they go quiet and hard. The sun would kill anything exposed, so they wrap themselves in their own mucus, in sheaths thick with sunscreen pigments, and wait out the white hours as a chalky skin. At night and at dew-dawn they wake. They breathe.

What they do is simple and planetary: they drink temporary water and exhale stone.

Dew runs off the domes into the seams and wicks sideways underneath. Rain bursts three miles off and the seams run knee-deep for twenty minutes. For an hour after, water sits under thin lids of fresh mineral and the whole basin glugs as it finds level. That is when they work. They take the dissolved lime in that sour pond-smelling water — calcium and bicarbonate carried in from nowhere but the floor's own dissolving — and, by the chemistry of their living, make it insoluble again. A crystal nucleates on a sheath. Another on another. By morning, a new film of lime, less than a hair thick, but laid down everywhere at once, over an area larger than some seas.

Each dome lifts faintly at its center because that is where they are thickest and most active, pushing up from below. Where two domes meet, the seam stays raw and soft at the lip because that is where water lingers longest and the work is still today. Break a plate and smell pond and lime. That pond smell is them. The snap is clean, conchoidal, because no root has broken it, no worm has churned it, no old mountain dust has dirtied it. It is pure secretion.

They do not live as individuals. They live as a laminate. If you could peel time apart you would find it stratified by job:

At the very top, where sun hits hardest, a scorched rind of dead sheaths and salt — a sacrificial thatch. Below that, when wet, a thin green-gold working layer that catches light and makes sugar, then shuts down to amber dormancy by nine. Below that, pink and olive and rust-red layers that do not need oxygen, that can photosynthesize without making it, that can live on sulfur and the leavings of the layer above. And below that, in permanent dark and damp, the pale lime-layers themselves, the oldest inhabitants, laying sheet after sheet.

Lift a shard at a seam edge and look at its underside. Pocked with damp chambers beaded with water that never sees light. Those beads are not empty. That is where they hide through drought, in tiny sealed rooms of their own making, waiting for the tick of trickle lateral through the dark.

They are patient in a way that makes heat and ruin irrelevant. Every day heat tries to crack them, water tries to dissolve them, sun tries to sterilize them. Every night they lay down another lamina. Growth is faster than ruin here. That is their entire strategy.

Only after them, only because of them, comes the second kind of life: the squatters in the seams.

Nothing can root in the dome tops. There is no soil, and by noon it is hot enough to kill anything tender. So life condenses into the lines where water runs.

Every hundred yards, or half a mile, a seam widens just enough to trap what the floods carry — loose grains, flecks of green, dead filaments — and hold it as real mud. There a tuft holds on.

It is not grass, exactly, but it is doing the job grass will do later. A starburst of stiff, gray-green blades, waxy, rolled tight as needles against the sun. From above, in the dense sectors along that barely-ridge, they draw the polygons in green lines, almost a meadow in crack-form.

Each tuft is an island with its own weather. Under the blades the mud stays damp all day while the domes bleach. The blades cast an inch of shadow that is the only shade in this country. The roots — shallow, matted, desperate — stitch the mud so the next flood does not take it. Tiny white salt pillars push up around the base where water evaporates; the plant drinks and exhales the salt out through glands, leaving it to crystallize and sting your eyes when wind lifts it in veils.

It lives ungrazed. No mouth has found it. It lives and crisps at the tip and dies at the base and collapses into the seam to become the mud the next tuft needs. You can smell its slow bread-like work if you kneel: around each starburst aprons a black lichen crust, holding the edge like a seal.

That crust is the third kind of life, and perhaps the toughest. Not a single thing but two things living as one — a fungus that cannot eat sun, and an alga that can, braided together so tightly they become a new body. Black, cracked, leathery, it aprons out from the tuft and glues silt and lime shards into a stable rim. At dawn it is supple and faintly sweet-sour, like bread left in sun, exhaling after drinking dew. By noon it is brittle as char and utterly dormant. It does not die. It turns off. Rain turns it on again in minutes.

Further on, where a pocket stays wet through the afternoon — tea-colored, warm as bathwater, shrinking — lives the fourth kind: the skins.

A smear of rust-red or dull olive stretches across the water, wrinkling in the breeze. It looks like scum. It is a city. Filaments of sun-catchers on top, olive and gold, making oxygen in a place that has almost no use for it yet; underneath, purple and rose films that prefer the rot-smell below; underneath those, black, anoxic thickets that bubble faintly when you disturb them. At midday the whole skin goes slack and slimy to survive; in morning it is taut and beaded with oxygen bubbles like sweat. When the pocket dries, it cracks into flakes that the wind does not take far — they settle in the next seam, and wait, years if needed, for water to remember them.

None of these green tenants built the floor. They rent the cracks the floor left for water. Their dead bodies are the only true soil here, and there is so little of it that you could gather all the soil of a mile in your two hands.

And that is all. No stitching insects. No mites in the mud, no springtails under the shards, no birds that land. Very high, sometimes, something crosses and does not stop. At night the stars pour down and the floor gives back heat until near midnight, and what sleeps warm on that living stone is you, briefly, the only large warm body that has ever been here. The lichen does not notice. The tuft hisses in unbroken wind. Under your back, millimeter by millimeter, the pale builders go on secreting a continent out of breath and sun and temporary water.

It is only just beginning.
