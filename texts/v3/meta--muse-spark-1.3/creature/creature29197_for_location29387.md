At first you count only people. That's the mistake.

You stand ankle-deep in the center and you see a dozen tower-things and bridges between them and figures moving on the bridges with baskets on their heads, and you think: ah, villagers. A hamlet. Twelve minutes rim to rim.

Then you stay past the first hour and the count comes undone.

The humans here are the loudest, but they are not the most numerous, and they are not separate.

They are wet people. That is the first taxonomy. Skin always beaded, hair plastered or frizzed with steam, shirts long abandoned to damp or worn as a second skin. Bare feet wide and pale from constant immersion, toes splayed for moss-work. They move the way you move in a place that never dries — without flinch, without checking where to step, suck-squelch, suck-squelch, at home in it.

If you look close, they are greening at the edges. Chlorophyll freckles up forearms and cheeks and the backs of necks, especially in the pruners and bridge-tenders who work high in the light. Old aunties in the market porches have green under their nails that won't wash out because it isn't dirt, it's them. Babies sleep in leaf-cups and come out smelling faintly of crushed fig. Nobody finds this strange. It's just what happens when you trade breath for shade long enough.

Because these people don't live *in* the towers. They live *with* them.

Each tower is an inhabitant. Count them, too. Twelve, fifteen, depending on the day's growth and the last collapse. Thirty feet across at the flute, buttressed, hollow, humming at 50 hertz if you put your palm flat to the wall. They are urban and arboreal at once — market porch at the ankle, family lofts a level up with hammock-floors that tighten and slacken to catch your weight, workshops higher where the light is white, mist-collectors at the crown with door-sized cup-leaves tilted to drink the wind. They lean. They want. Stand still under one at midday and it will cantilever its great leaves toward you to cool you, and sip your exhale — your CO2, your sweat-salt — like gossip. If it doesn't know you, the leaf-windows stay clear and watchful. If it knows your household's sweat, they polarize dark. They remember.

And the humans surf them. That is their profession, all of them: surfing a house that will not stand still.

A man with green forearms prunes a doorway with a singing knife, and you watch the cut lip curl and seal itself even as the shavings sprout at his feet. A woman coaxes a bamboo bridge to a new anchor, whispering, tying copper-nano thread that glints, cooing it forward a handspan. Children do the real maintenance — they swing on vine-loops from crown to marsh, screaming, trailing seed-fluff, stripping fallen limbs for orchids before the fungi get them, picking jewel leeches off leaves and trading them for sweets at the stalls because the saliva is antiseptic and the vendors want them. An elder pounds starch in a hollow root, steady as rain, while a gourd-radio crackles weather-song beside her. Vendors don't just sell *on* the trees; they sell *with* permission from vendor-trees. You learn to ask.

When a crown collapses — and it will, a water-heavy limb shearing with a crack like a sail tearing, exploding down into beetles and parrots and white spray — no one mourns. Within the hour the same vendors have laid their mats out along the fallen trunk. The kids are already in the branches. Electric-blue mycelium is already filming the wound. Death here is just fast reassignment.

Under and over and through the people is the second town, denser by a thousandfold.

The frogs own the floor. Dozens of species, green-backed, translucent-bellied, bellowing from every puddle until the mat itself seems to croak. Some you don't see until they blink, because leaves are growing from their backs. You lift your boot and the suck-hole you left fills with brown water and in a minute there are tadpoles in it, humming in nursery leaf-cups.

The air-owners own the vertical. Cicadas sawing at a pitch that gets in your teeth. Bees big as thumbs barging flower to flower. Iridescent beetles clicking off bark. Flocks of tiny screaming parrots wheeling tower to tower, shitting seed, trailing fluff. Jewel lizards that slap across open water on their hind feet and freeze into leaf. Moths that land and open wings and *are* leaves — veined, photosynthesizing, only betrayed when they decide to leave.

And slower things. A sloth-like animal comes down a cable-vine so slowly that ferns have fruited in its fur, moss shaggy on its back, orchids in the crook of its arm. It blinks at you with great patient eyes and keeps coming. It has been coming all morning. Leeches like dropped jewels wait on the undersides of leaves, red and green and gold, and let the children pluck them.

Even the rot is alive and opinionated. Fallen wood goes turquoise, then orange, then white with mycelium that pulses faintly at night. Fruit cut open in the market exhales its own tiny weather — a sour-sweet fog that smells of lightning — engineered so long ago no one remembers if it was natural. The moss itself decides where you walk, offering pale inflated stepping-bladders if you step gently. Step hard, step rude, and you sink to the knee while the frogs scold you.

And then the smallest, strangest residents: the black glassy tetrahedrons, warm to the touch, drifting down like seeds that are not seeds. When they land they unfold into lattice for a new tendril to follow. They fell from orbit years ago, someone tells you, and just kept growing. Nobody maintains them. They maintain us. In the attics, old ones click as they cool at night like crickets made of glass.

By late afternoon when the towers exhale together — that visible sigh of oxygen and mist that rises, hits the low rim, and falls back as internal rain — you finally see them as one body. Lantern-fruits blinking on warm yellow in clusters, bridges full of dripping silhouettes going tower to tower, frogs louder and louder, ground breathing under your feet like lungs.

No one here is finished. No one here is dry. No one here is alone in their skin.

The people, the towers, the frogs with leaves on their backs, the moths with leaves for wings, the mossy sloth, the singing pipes that lift water forty feet on sound, the children who are also gardeners who are also roads for ants — they are all just different speeds of the same wet, green decision to keep growing through each other and call that living.
