You will not see them at first.

That is not because they hide. Hiding implies hurry, implies a sharp division between watcher and watched. They do neither. It is because you are looking for the wrong thing — for a figure cut out from ground, for a face, for eyes that meet yours. Here nothing is cut out from anything else.

Sit still long enough in one of the grown hollows, where the floor-moss is dry enough to sit on, and watch a fork in a tree that you were sure was empty. Watch until the drip from leaf to leaf has marked an hour. Then a part of the moss in that fork will sigh, and shift its weight, very slowly, from one arm to the other.

It was never moss.

---

What lives on the Roof lives the way the Roof lives: slowly, softly, wetly, on top of something else living.

The adults you will mistake for animals at first, because there is no word in your lowland head for a person who does not stand out. They hang. In the crotches where the black water gathers, in the forks where the mat has furred two hands thick over bark, they settle their bulk and do not move for days. Shaggy is the wrong word — they are thatched. A dense, hanging fell of hair, green-grey with living algae, beaded always with mist, so that their outline breaks and goes soft at the edge exactly the way a branch does. Under that fell, pale moths roost. Glass frogs sit in the damp of their armpits and pulse their throats in time with theirs, soundlessly. An orchid may take root in the small of a back and bloom there once in a decade, white in the gloom.

Unthatch one in your mind and you find no angles. They are round in every direction the Roof is round. A barrel chest that never quite empties, because breath is short up here and must be hoarded. Throat and chest moving always with that silent amphibian pulse you have seen in the crotch-pools — *in-out, in-out* — drinking air through skin as much as lung. No nose to speak of, only a velvet muzzle, blind-soft, that wrinkles when it smells water. Eyes: you will look for large night-eyes and be disappointed. Their eyes are small, dark beads, half-lost in fell, almost vestigial in a place with no shadows and no distance. They do not need to see far. Fifty paces is the end of the world.

What they have instead are hands and feet absurdly wide for their bodies. Unfurled, a foot is a living snowshoe, a fringed oval of splayed, webbed toes that spreads their weight over the sighing felt so they do not sink where you sink to the ankle. Their fingers — six to a hand, or five and a thumb that is really another finger, it is hard to count because they never hold still for counting — are long, boneless-seeming, tipped not with nail or claw — nothing sharp survives — but with soft, blunt pads like the ends of roots. With those they do everything. They do not grip so much as coax.

Watch one move and you will understand why you have heard only rustle without cover. It does not climb. It pours. An arm extends over the course of minutes, pads find a curtain of red-brown air-roots, test, settle. Weight follows like water finding level. The other arm follows. To cross from one crown to another without flapping, without falling, they go hand-over-hand through the hanging hair, so slow that the moss-midges settle on them in transit and are carried along.

When they must cross open mat — and they dislike it, for all their width they still leave those quickly-filling footprints — they go on all fours, knuckling softly, *chuff-chuff*, and the felt rebounds behind them.

They are about as tall as you if they stood, which they never do. They are heavier than they look, water-heavy. If you ever touch one — and you may, because curiosity runs both ways here — you will find the fell is warm from trapped bathhouse air, and underneath the skin gives like soaked peat. There is cartilage in them, but no hard bone near the surface to bruise you. Teeth? Lift the lip of a sleeping one and you will find only ridges, soft white gums ridged like a gourd. They cannot bite. They press, and suck, and sip.

---

For they do not hunt. Nothing on the Roof hunts in the way you mean, with chase and edge.

They garden, and they glean, and most of all they wait for water to bring them things.

Morning — the paler grey that means morning — finds them with mouths open to the mist, throats pulsing, gathering condensation on fell and licking it from each other's shoulders with long, flat tongues. They scrape algae from bark with those gum-ridges. They tend fungus in the hollows of trees the way you would tend a fire, feeding it leaf-fall and coaxing its pale shelves wider with touch. They sip from the tea-dark sheets that slide through moss channels, filtering through fringed lips. In the season when the bathtub bromeliads brim, they will lift a tadpole — not their own, just a frog's — in a cupped hand and drink around it, and set it back unharmed. To harm would be sharp.

They speak, but not in your medium. The mat ate voice long ago and they let it go.

When two meet on a narrow root-bridge, they do not call. They flash. Under the chin, shaved bare of fell by mutual grooming over years, is a patch of loose skin, a dewlap sac the size of a fist. At rest it is the color of wet bark. In talk it floods — blue to orange to a green-gold you have seen only once on a frog — a visual song, a pulse of color in the dim that carries fifty paces where a voice would die in five. Mothers flash to young. Mates flash slowly, for hours, until their rhythms sync. Alarm is white, sudden, and then they go still and become branch again.

At closer range, touch. They are always touching. A passing hand lingers on a back to read the garden there — what orchid bloomed, what moth laid, how wet the fell is, how long since grooming. Much news travels by lick and nuzzle of those velvet muzzles. And in water, they speak in ripples, like the big crotch-pool amphibians who are their cousins if not their kin. A fingertip trailed in a fork-pool in expanding rings says *I am here, I am calm, the water is good*. A double tap says *come*.

You will smell them before you understand them: not musk, not sweat — rain on warm stone, crushed fern, the faint iron of peat.

---

And the houses. Now you understand the houses.

No one builds here. Building implies cutting and cutting is sharp. They grow by bending.

A young one, barely out of its rooting time, will choose a clearing where the mat domes a little higher and find a circle of supple saplings, whip-thin stems. It bends one, gently, day after day, sleeping against it to hold it down, until the stem takes the curve. It comes back in the wet season and bends another. This is not its house. This will be its great-grandchild's house. For the nest-hollows you slept in are not made in a lifetime. They are coaxed over generations, each occupant adding a bend, a weave, a tuck of living stem into living stem, until the walls close overhead seamless as a bird's inverted nest. Inside the air stays a finger drier than outside. That is enough.

That is their writing, too. There is no carving here — lichen would eat it — but a bent stem remembers who bent it and when. An old hollow reads, to a muzzle and a hand, like a history: here a mother laid, here a fever passed, here the mist was heavy for three years and the roof was lowered. To abandon a hollow is not to leave it; it is to let the forest unmake it, root by root. That too is recorded.

And the eggs. Yes, they lay eggs — two, three at a time, soft-shelled as frogspawn, opalescent, laid not in water but in the dry moss of the hollow floor, where they can breathe damp air and not drown. The parent — sex is a slow, changeable thing with them, not worth your categories — curls around them for weeks without eating, keeping them at bathhouse warmth with its own body, flashing faint color over them in sleep. When they hatch, the young are not miniature hangers. They are swimmers.

This is the secret the Roof kept from you until you stopped looking for persons:

The tadpoles in the bathtub bromeliads. The blind, velvet-nosed rooters tunneling through peat. The slow, algae-furred hangers in the forks.

They are the same.

Hatched young are carried in cupped hands, one by one, to the high crotch-pools and slipped into black water. There they live a year, two years, gilled, leaf-tailed, grazing on detritus, communicating in ripples they will never forget. When they grow legs they drop — or are lifted — to the mat, and go down. Down into the felt, where eyes are useless and they go blind for a time, pale and velvety, tunneling, eating humus, aerating the sponge that mothers them all. You have heard them at night as a suck and shift under your sleeping mat. They mean no harm. They are children.

Years later — no one counts how many, leaves take years to fall here — a rooter will surface near the roots of its birth tree, blinking small dark eyes open for the first time, and climb, and grow its fell, and find algae, and be groomed, and take its place in a fork to hang for days and flash blue to orange.

So when they say you could walk for months and never step on anything dead, it is truer than you knew. You are stepping on nurseries.

---

The stones, then. Waist-high, leaning inward like tired conspirators, bearded in lichen past recognition.

They are the only sharp thing they ever allowed themselves, and even that they softened. When a very old one feels the settling come — that heaviness in the chest, that thirst no mist quenches — it does not go away to die. There is no away. It chooses a swell and lies down, and its kin lay stones around it, upright, leaning in. Not to mark. To shelter the spot from feet while the forest does its work. Over an afternoon, over a season, over years, the mat sighs up and over. Moss upon peat upon humus upon moss again. Fungi nurse the body for a decade until it is unmade. The fell becomes fell of the ground. A tadpole in a nearby pool may carry, in its tail, a cell that was once a finger.

Afterwards the stones remain a little while, growing their own beards, until root by root the forest softens them back into hill. No names. Names would be sharp. Only the bend of nearby saplings, coaxed a little wider to let light in on that rich spot, says: someone was grown over here. Remember by growing.

If a giant emergent falls — that deep boom you feel in your sternum once in a lifetime — they gather. Not quickly. Over days they converge, pouring hand-over-hand, flashing low and gold. They do not mourn. They tend. They guide the tea-dark water around the new hollow, they transplant bromeliads to its crown while it still lives on its side, they lay their young in its new pools. A fall is a country opening.

If you stay long enough, they will tend you too. Not as guest — they have no word for that — but as temporary warm spot. An old female — old is a smell, of deep peat and sweet rot — will approach while you sleep in the hollow and groom you, with those root-tip fingers, picking the lowland lice from your hair, licking salt-sweat from your neck, planting, perhaps without meaning to, a spore in your damp shirt that will bloom green-gold by the time you descend, if you ever descend.

Do not mistake their gentleness for weakness. It is discipline. To endure on the Roof, be slow, be soft, be quiet, and grow over whatever came before. They have outlasted every tool that was ever brought up here by digesting it. Your knife left out overnight will rust to a red stain by morning and be gone by wet season, nursed by fungi. Your voice will give out from disuse. Your stride will shorten. Your nails will soften until you can gouge soapstone with them.

Look out now from the highest swell, fifty paces of sight, green going to grey going to white. The rainhead is coming at the speed of thought in a dream. You have time to sit down.

Beside you, almost touching, a patch of moss breathes in-out, in-out, and flashes, very faintly, blue to orange.

It is saying, in the only language that survives where sound dies and stone softens:

*Stay. Settle. Soften. We will grow over you kindly.*
