They are not visitors. That's the first mistake.

At first you think: pigeons. Just pigeons, blown in because no one chases them off anymore. Feral spillover. You see them as spots on the pipes, a crust along the rails, and you almost read them as part of the building — like the rust lichen-circles, like the salt.

Then one turns its head, and two hundred turn.

What lives here is not a flock that happens to be here. It is a population that belongs the way lichen belongs to stone. It has fitted itself to the cold and the tremor and the chemistry until it runs without friction.

The bulk of them are pigeons — slate, checkered, rust-red, blue-barred — but not quite city pigeons anymore. The cold has fluffed them out. They are round, puffed low against the pipes to keep the engine-warmth under their bellies. Their feet, gripping the condensation-slick steel, are caked white with lime. Their breast feathers where they rub the handrails and valve-wheels day after day are polished smooth, darkened with oil. Their eyes are orange and unblinking in the sodium light. When they sit, they sit shoulder to shoulder without pecking, without shuffling for space. Weight distributed perfectly. Two hundred bodies on the long pipe-rack with an inch between each, and none falls though the pipe ticks and sweats under them.

Perched, they are almost entirely still. That is the second thing you learn. They do not preen much. They do not fight. They face east.

All of them.

You will walk the yard and check this because you won't believe it at first. The row on the pipe-rack: east. The line along the mill mullions where the glass is gone: east. The hundreds hunched on the catwalk rails: east, beaks slightly open, throats pulsing with the hum. They are not looking. The sky is white and has nothing to look at. They are listening. The thrum that you feel in your sternum is what holds them in place, the single held note from pump and mill and transformer. It comes from everywhere so they face the direction it once came from, or will come from. If a pigeon lands on a valve wheel and its weight shudders the wheel a quarter-turn and steam sighs out, it does not startle. It rides it. When it leaves, another takes its exact warm spot, faces east, and shudders the wheel back.

In the air they are a different tissue of the same animal.

When you step out they lift — not in panic, there is no hawk here, no man with a broom — but in circulation. The sky between the nine far-apart buildings fills with a low, thick river of wings. Thousands of wingbeats make their own wind that smells of cold feathers and hot oil and wet stone. It pushes against your face. You duck without meaning to.

They do not scatter. The starlings braid through them in dark strings, lifting and settling and lifting again, but the pigeons hold their routes: clockwise around the mill, counterclockwise around the pump house, figure-eighting forever in the yard that is the center. They never tangle. They slap past each other with inches to spare. Below, feathers sift down like slow snow and catch in the oil puddles and on your shoulders, and for every feather that lands there is already another in the air. The number in the sky never empties. The number on the rails never thins.

The screamers hold the ground of the air. Big herring gulls with pink legs stand knee-deep in the shallow oil-skinned puddles and do not drink. They stand. They scream the same scream at intervals so regular you stop hearing it as alarm. Backs to the wind, white heads stained grey at the wingtips with soot, eyes pale and furious and completely calm. They are the loudest part of the loudness, and they never move except to step out of a drip line and then step back in.

Below that, the ground crew. Rats trot the canal edge with complete indifference. Not scurrying. Trotting, in the open, along the green stain one inch above the water that never rises or falls. Wet brown, fat on something — fallen midges, dead moths, guano grubs, the inexhaustible sift of feathers and lice. They do not hide because nothing hunts them here. The gulls watch them and scream and do not strike. The rats polish the same runs day after day until the concrete is dark and smooth.

And around the lamps, even though it is daytime and the lamps have been left burning, the orbiters. Pale winter-moths, hundreds to a lamp, circling the sodium heads in a tireless ring, never landing, never singeing. If one drops, another takes the orbit. The hum of the lamps holds them as the big hum holds the pigeons. Under them, over the still canal, stand the columns of midges — thin, vertical, shivering pillars that do not drift. You can put your hand through one and it re-forms behind you.

None of them breeds into more. None of them dies into less. That is what stays with you if you stay, pinned at first by the noise and then erased by it. A chick might cheep somewhere under a girder in the white dripstone, but you never see a nest empty and you never see the perches crowd. A rat might slip in the canal, but the level never changes. They have worn themselves and their housings into a perfect fit, the way the engines have worn their housings.

They polish what people polished. Feathers where hands used to be on the rails. Small claws where boots used to trough the steps. They keep the belts turning empty, the hooks coming around empty, the fan moving the same cool air, because their movement is the work now.

Two weights on a scale that never tips: on one side the machines that never rev and never idle down, pumping water from the canal back into the canal; on the other side the warm, feathered mass that never leaves and never increases, facing east, listening, circulating.

Together they are the citizen.
