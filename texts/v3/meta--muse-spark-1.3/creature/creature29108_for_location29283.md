You will think at first that nothing does.

That is what the place wants you to think. The roar is so total, the wet is so total, that any life smaller than a shed is erased before you can register it. You see movement at the edge of vision and you turn and it is steam folding. You see a dark thickening in the fog between lamps and you blink and it is only fog. You learn not to look.

Stay longer. Past the hour when your own voice goes distant in your head. Past the time your boots fill and your shoulders go through. Stand in one of the long empties where there is only deck and sheet-water and glare, and stop trying to point to the source of the sound. Let your sternum do the hearing.

Then you will start to see who is already standing with you.

Low. That is the first thing. Everything here that lives lives low, down in the meter of air where the steam spreads sideways and becomes fog. Not upright like you. Upright is for things that want to be seen by lamps. They don't.

At distance they are misread as unevenness in the ground-haze — a slightly darker smear, a little more stillness where fog should roll. Closer, when a sodium stain catches them sideways, they resolve into backs.

Wet backs, the color of wet deck. Iron-gray mottled with rust-orange and the black-green of soaked moss. Broad and shallowly domed, beaded always with condensate, shedding a perpetual secondary rain off their flanks the way handrails do, the way chains do. You thought the shine on them was sheet-water. It is skin.

They are not large by shed-standards. Laid flat they would cover one of the curled deck plates. Perhaps a meter and a half long if they stretched, which they never do. They go hunched, folded, close to the iron. Four — no, more — low limbs tucked under, no knees to speak of, just blunt pads that make no tock. Where your boot makes *tock-suck, tock-suck* in every direction, they make nothing. They distribute. They have learned the deck the way water has learned it. They flow across it without breaking the film. That is why you never heard them approach.

Don't look for faces. That was your first mistake, the human one. There is a front end, lower than the back, that dips toward water. No eyes worth the name — under lamp-light you will see two clouded pits filmed over with the same oil that skins the ponds, rainbowed, blind. They do not need eyes where true light never reaches. There are no ears, either. The outer ear would be a cruelty here. The whole body is an ear. The whole sternum and belly pressed to vibrating iron, reading the pumps through the soles of themselves: thud — thud — thud — moving water from one low place to another. They navigate by that. By tremor and drip.

For they can hear the drips you only hear in the half-second when the relief valve slams shut.

Plink. Plink. Plink.

That is their country mapped. A thousand edges ticking onto iron, onto concrete, onto black grass, onto black water — to you, after the roar returns, it vanishes. To them it persists underneath, a high bright net laid over the low fundamental. Valve stem to puddle. Flange to puddle. Bolt to moss. They walk — if that slow deliberate oozing can be called walking — from tick to tick, head tipped to catch the fall on that filmed snout.

Watch one feed and you will understand the oil rainbows.

It finds a permanent puddle under a pipe trestle, one of those little flange-drip puddles with its own halo and its own fringe of moss. It settles its front end level with the surface until its mouth — not a mouth so much as a wide flat lip, pale underneath, almost translucent — just breaks tension. And it sips. Not the water. The skin of the water. The slow-turning film of oil and grease and cold condensate, with the drowned rust-bloom and the spores and the pollen of lichen floating in it. It draws that film in with infinite patience, leaving a clear black eye behind it in the puddle that slowly rainbows over again. Then it turns its cheek and rasps, with a tongue like a wet file, at the moss fringe. Not to eat the moss clean. To prune it. To keep the drip-line open.

That is what they do. They keep the drips open.

Follow one for a lamp-to-lamp migration and you see the rest. It will pause at a pipe foot where the constant vibration has shivered the standing water into concentric ripples that hold for hours without wind. You thought that was physics. Get close and you will see it is husbandry. The animal settles its pads around the foot, very still, and adds its own minute shiver — a tremor run through its belly — tuning the ripple, holding it. Why, you don't know, unless it is to keep the water oxygenated for whatever lives in the film, or to call. For in that held ripple other backs converge, very slowly, out of the fog.

They gather where it is warm. Not warm as you mean it. Warm as iron means it. In the slow breath from the throat of the blind cooling tower, in the lee of a boiler house with its doors gone and its fire banked and breathing, where condensate falls hot. There they pile, not touching quite, but near, backs steaming faintly, moss on moss. The moss grows on them, you realize, when you see them close in green pilot light. In the seams where their plates — for their skin is plated, in overlapping dull scales like rusted shingles — have heaved apart by millimeters over years, black-green sponge-moss has taken hold, and pale gray lichen follows their drip lines down their flanks. Wire-grass sprouts from a shoulder joint and shivers in fan-wash. They are mobile seams. They carry the punctuation of green with them across the empties.

They do not compete with the roar. Nothing here does. That is why the gulls and rats and dogs are gone. The tenders learned the other trick: go beneath it. They make no sound you can keep. If you crouch — cold seep in your knees — in that half-second of relative quiet when the valve slams, you may hear what is really their voice: not a cry, not a tick, but a soft wet seating, a sucker adjusting on iron. A faint scrape of file-tongue on rust. And sometimes, from a settling basin, a low spreading *shhh* as one slides into black water and goes fully flat, only nostril slits and back-moss above the surface, drinking cold through its belly.

The black water is their other home. The deeper stands — the settling basins, the old cooling ponds with collapsed rims — are nurseries, though nothing is born here the way you mean birth. No thrashing, no cry that would have to fight the crusher's boom. Instead: accretion. In the oil-skimmed stillness you will find, if your lamp holds, thin rust-colored flakes floating, each fringed with green. Shed scales. Each one carries a bud of moss and a bead of fat. They drift to the rim, lodge in concrete cancer where rebar shows like bone, and wait. A centimeter a year. A millimeter a year. Patient as green. One wet season the flake thickens, domes, beads condensate on its own. One day it shivers into a ripple pattern around itself and does not wash away.

Maintenance without maintainers, you thought. No. Maintenance *as* life.

They do not fix the machines. They do not need to understand pumps or manifolds or the empty crusher chewing nothing. They tend the consequence of machines: water that has nowhere to go, rust that blooms and flakes, concrete that spalls. They lick rust-bloom before it seals a drip. They clear expansion joints with their snouts so moss does not dam and lift a plate too fast. They crowd under the deck where it is jacked on rusted piers, in that dripping underside dark, and scrape the concrete's weep-channels open with their claws. They were not built. They accumulated, the way cinder accumulated under deck, the way ash-concrete accumulated under that. Another layer of making, only slower.

And the strangeness you felt is them, partly.

The reflection of a lamp that ripples and holds after the water calms — that is where one has just sipped and left its tuned membrane. The dark shapes that resolve into steam when you turn — sometimes they don't resolve because a back has gone still and let fog take it. Compasses spin lazily here not only for iron and water, but for the iron in them; their plates carry magnetite laid down flake by flake, and a close pack of them tugs the needle as they pass. Radios fill with crackle timed to the pumps because they move timed to the pumps, mass to mass, shifting the standing field.

They know you by your wrongness. By tock-suck, tock-suck. By upright. By breath smoking too fast and eyes hunting for horizon. They do not flee. Fleeing is loud. They go stiller, flatter, more deck. If you stand very still in fog between lamps and let sheet-water tremble under steam-rain, you will feel through your boots what they feel always: the place resuming itself. Steam screams again. Pumps thud. The empty crusher booms and comes back off the shed walls.

And all across the open dark, low to the iron, backs beaded with secondary rain lift slightly to meet it, drink the vibration in, and settle again to the endless, silent tending of the weep.
