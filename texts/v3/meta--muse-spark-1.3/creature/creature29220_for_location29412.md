There is almost nothing alive in there. That's the first thing you have to understand about who lives there.

Not the way a desert is almost empty, where everything is hiding. This is empty the way a just-poured pot is empty. The heat has boiled the room clean. The noise has shaken it clean. The water is too hot to hold a wriggle, too thin with oil to hold a skin, and it never stops shivering. Anything that needs quiet, or cool, or rot, or dark to chew on - it dies on the ladder down.

So what lives there lives by not needing very much.

First, the green.

You won't see it at first because you're looking for *life* - for something with leaves or legs or eyes. This isn't that. It's a slick. A discoloration. If you came down fast to check the sump you'd think it was paint, or damp shadow.

Three patches, and only three. One on the north wall where the daylight from the grate grazes sideways for an hour at noon. One on the lip of the central channel, right where the hot water keeps licking over the concrete curb. One directly under the grate itself, in the full fall of that white rectangle.

Dark green-black. Not grass green, not marsh green. Old-blood green, almost black until the work-bulb catches it wet. No thicker than a coat of paint. No edge you can peel up. It is slippery as soap if you put a thumb to it - which the men don't, anymore - and it comes away on your skin as nothing, just slickness.

It is thermophilic algae. Heat-loving. The only thing in that box that actually likes the weight of the heat coming down through the ceiling. While your sweat sits on you and goes nowhere, it is drinking the heat. While your boots fill with hot, clear, oil-sheened water that would cook mosquito larvae, it spreads a sixteenth of an inch wider a year across the pore of the concrete.

Stay with it long enough and it stops being a stain and becomes a hide. A skin the room is growing for itself. It lives in the thinnest possible margin: where there is just enough light to photosynthesize and just enough water to stay wet but not enough flow to wash it off. The hundred drips from the ceiling that hammer the puddles into rings - the algae lets them hit. It doesn't pit or tear. The twelve-inch main screaming through the middle, shaking the whole box with *duh-duh-duh-duh* until your molars ache - the algae rides it. In fact it needs the shiver. Stagnant would cook it or starve it. Vibrating keeps the film thin, oxygenated.

At its edges it is already turning to stone. The white, then cream, then sulfurous yellow mineral skin that is creeping over every pipe and wall - young rock, fresh - is creeping over the algae too. Where the green meets the white you can't tell which is eating which. That is its life: to be slowly entombed by the very water that feeds it, and to keep sliding outward a dust-width ahead.

And then, the other one. The fern.

In the southeast corner there is a hairline crack that opened last summer when an engine threw a mount. Marsh water pushes in there from outside, cooler than anything else in the room by maybe five degrees. Not cool - just less hot. And in that seep, lodged in the sharp concrete where the formwork grain still shows, there is a fern.

One plant. One. Two fronds, no more than four inches high, pale and etiolated as if it grew underground and never got the news.

It shouldn't be there. Ferns want soil, shade, cool rot. There is no soil here, only poured concrete weeping hot and cold at once, and mineral teeth half an inch long growing sharp as broken glass around it, the room cutting its teeth. There is no shade here, only noon white through a grate and a yellow bulb that never turns off and buzzes 60-cycle. There is no rot here - iron doesn't even rust properly, it just stays gunmetal gray under sweat.

So it doesn't grow. The men upstairs say it's been that size since spring. Not growing, not dying. Just holding.

Look closer. The fronds are almost translucent. You can see the veins because there isn't enough chlorophyll to hide them. The stem is white-green where it comes out of the crack, then pales to yellow at the tip. A drip forms somewhere above it, swells on a bolt, catches the daylight, falls - *plik* - into the channel, and the fern shudders with the whole box when a man walks across the engine room floor upstairs. But it does not break. Its roots - if you can call them that - are down in the crack, drinking that one cool seep before the heat gets it.

It is patient the way only something that has given up on getting bigger can be patient. It is not waiting for spring. There is no spring down there, only endless running diesels and heat that never breaks. It is just persisting, frond by frond, doing the absolute minimum of fern-ness: staying green, staying upright, staying wet.

Those four. Three slicks and a stunted fern. That is the whole population.

No rats - they would cook. No roaches, no silverfish, not even a mosquito to lay in the inch of hot water in the trough. No spider, though mineral fiber hangs from the conduit like false cobweb. Outside, twenty feet beyond the levee, the marsh is riot. In here, life has been boiled down to what can live on hot concrete, loud water, and light.

If you sit on the warm curb long enough, shirt plastered, voice coming back flat and small off the wet walls, you start to see them not as plants stuck in a pump room, but as the room's residents. The only things that have learned its rules: stay thin, stay low, stay wet, don't need quiet, don't need cool, don't need to grow fast. Lengthen by dust. Widen by hair.

They are not thriving. They are enduring so completely that enduring has become a kind of belonging.
