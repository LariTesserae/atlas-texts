No one lives here, at first.

That's what you think, walking it. An hour and only water moving. A city with streets for water and houses for nothing but water. No doors, no smoke, no voices. Only your boots sighing and filling with seep behind you.

Then you kneel, because the place forces you to kneel. The scale is wrong from standing. And it fills.

What lives here lives low and slow and almost entirely within the first inch.

The true residents, the ones who never leave, are not the ones with legs. They are the films.

Every lip, every rim, every spillway that looks like a gutter is alive on its wet edge. A skin, olive and ochre and black-green, slick to the touch. Cyanobacteria in sheets a few cells thick, and the algae strung through them, and the moss protonema underneath. They sip the cold spring water as it spreads thin — water heavy with dissolved limestone — and they catch it. They slow it. On their sticky sheaths a lace of calcite drops out, needle by needle, grain by grain. You can see it if you hold still: downward-growing frost under the overhang, white marl precipitating in the sun-warmed corners.

They are the masons. Stone is grown, not placed, because they grow it. Without them the water would run off and leave nothing. With them, a film becomes a rim, a rim becomes a dam, a dam becomes a pool. They build by living, and by dying — living moss on top, dead moss below becoming peat, peat underlain by white marl, marl underlain by older moss again. One square meter of pool edge is a layer cake you could read with a fingernail: sky water, pollen dust, algal skin, calcite needles, black peat, white marl, living green, dead brown.

On the drier tops, where the rock lifts just clear of constant wet, the masons change uniform. Star-moss in tight cushions, hard as a thumb-pad, that swell and open after rain and shrink to black knots in wind. Black-green liverwort pressed flat into cracks, thriving exactly where the rock weeps. Gray-green and orange lichens mapping the oldest, highest rims — the rounded, rain-pitted ones that haven't seen active flow in decades. They don't build fast anymore, they just hold. Their acids pit, their bodies seal. They are the old town.

Tufts of sedge hold on where peat has gathered enough to be called soil. Short, cropped by cold and wet, roots sipping rather than anchoring. And here and there on a wall-top that stays drained, a pincushion of alpine campion, impossibly tight, with a pink flower no bigger than your nail that opens for a week in high summer. They are not many. There isn't enough soil or warmth to be many. They live like tenants allowed a windowsill.

And where there are masons, there are grazers. You have to look for them at snail pace.

Along every submerged rim moves a snail no bigger than a grain of rice, amber and translucent, a pond snail made stout by all that calcium. It leaves a clean trail through the algal skin, eating the film and leaving the calcite. In a calcium-poor bog it could never make a shell this thick in this cold. Here, shell material rains from above. If you lift one — and you have to wet your fingers first or you'll crush it — you see the black peat showing through its foot.

In the sun-warmed corners, where a shallow pan catches noon and goes a degree or two above the seep, midge larvae flick. Blood-red, eyelash-thin, curled in silt tubes of marl and mucus. No chorus, no swarm, no traffic of insects up here — the wind is too cool and constant and there is almost nothing to eat on the wing. Just these flickers, underwater, feeding on pollen dust and dead algae, waiting out most of the year frozen. There are no fish in these bathtubs. Nothing to hunt them except cold and each other. Rotifers wheel, tardigrades lumber bear-like through the moss cushions when they are wet and tun into dust when they are dry, diatoms like tiny glass boats drift and settle. Everything touches everything else.

That would be enough to call it full. But something warm lives here too, in the interstices.

Between two rimstones, where a collapsed plaza of broken slabs has been re-cemented just enough to leave dry pockets, a pika has made a home. Not in the water, but in the city as if it were talus. The size of a fist, round-eared, the only mammal that truly resides. You won't see it at first because it doesn't move like water moves. It darts. It freezes. It darts again with a mouthful of sedge almost as tall as itself, cut from the marsh-edge where the terraces dissolve into true string bog. Under a slab it has a haypile — sedge, campion stems, moss raked off — curing for winter when the whole city locks into ice and heaves and cracks. It will live under snow, in the steam of its own breath, listening to water still ticking under ice, laying down another invisible film of stone.

Others only visit. Far off a raven crosses once, wingbeats suddenly loud because there is nothing to cover them, looking for what the pika drops or what winter kills. At the bog edge, where the white stone gives way to soft marl and willows far below, deer or elk come up in the thaw to drink and leave cloven prints that fill from below with clear cold seep, just like your boots. They don't stay. This height belongs mostly to things that do not need to move fast.

In winter, even the residents go thin. The pools skin over, then lock. Snails burrow down into peat and seal their apertures with mucus and calcium. Larvae coil tight in diapause. Mosses freeze-dry. Cyanobacterial sheets go dark and dormant under ice lenses. The pika stays. The lichens stay, brighter against snow. The stone itself heaves and cracks — and in spring, when the springs well up again everywhere, mineral-sharp and unstoppable, the weeping faces darken and bead and the cracks heal with fresh lace.

Put your hand to one of those weeping risers in late morning when mist is lifting off ten thousand shallow mirrors. Your palm comes away wet and smelling faintly of iron and moss — rust-yellow where iron-oxidizers work, bone-cream where pure calcite falls. That smell is them. That wet is them exhaling cold.

They do not hurry. They do not gather. They do not build anything but the ground they sit on, which happens to look, from a distance, unmistakably like a city shouldering into itself for tens of miles until haze takes it.

It is inhabited. Utterly full and almost empty. A town of films and cushions and grains that move, keeping house for water.
