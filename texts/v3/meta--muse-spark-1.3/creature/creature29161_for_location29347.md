At first you think nothing lives here. That's what the bowl wants you to think.

The roar takes up all the room. The green takes up all the eye. The emptiness in between — the wide air between the terraces, the long flight of the single raven across nothing — feels toorinsed, too new to have learned how to hold a life yet. Where there is room for three hundred hearths you count a dozen thin smokes, and then lose count, and then realize you have been standing in the middle lane for five minutes and no one has come to meet you. In any other town that would be suspicion. Here it is manners.

Then you hear them, because you learn not to listen for voices.

*tock tock.*

Not a shout. A knock. A woman crouched over a drain cover on the second terrace lifts her hammer and strikes slate twice against slate, and the bowl takes the sound and lifts it and hands it to everyone. Two heads come up on two other terraces. A child stops mid-hop on a singing stone. That is speech at distance here. They do not bellow over the water — the water always wins, and shouting would sour the whole cup. They have learned the acoustics the way you learn a sore tooth: go around it.

Up close they are quiet-voiced, close-leaning people. They move slowly. Not from age — though some are old, rope-veined, wind-burned — but because everything underfoot is wet sheet and sharp newborn slab and a foot-thick lung of moss that will take your ankle if you hurry. Their boots are soaked through by rule, dark with it up to the shin, laced with hide thongs that have swollen and shrunk a hundred times. Their wool is hand-spun, undyed, grey and brown and moss-green, and it is always damp. It beads. Everything beads here. If you stand near enough to talk, you see the fine dew standing on their eyebrows, on the sheep-wool of their hoods, on the back of their hands.

There are perhaps thirty of them. You keep revising the number down. A town built for ten times as many, and mostly air.

The woman with the hammer is typical if anyone is. Late thirties, broad in the shoulder from lifting stone, fingers pink and swollen at the tips from cold even at noon, breath smoking faintly as she works. She doesn't get up to greet you. She knocks again — *tock* — once, toward the lane, which you later learn means *see, it's only feet, let it come.* And she goes back to seating a flat slate over a gurgling under-drain, with water running clear over both her wrists. Her tools are beside her in the wet: a hammer-stone, a bone awl, a coil of bark for gasket. In her doorframe behind her, green copper pins hold the pine lintel true. No iron. No nails. Nothing here was made anywhere you can't see from where you stand.

The children — there are only a handful, and that more than anything makes the terraces feel vast — are the only ones who play the place as an instrument. The adults use the knock-code sparingly, functionally. The children have mapped which paving slabs ring.

Because the lava underneath is still full of bubbles, still porous, still almost breathing, some of the standing slates of the lanes give back a faint metallic tone when stepped on right. The children know them all. When the adults aren't looking they hop from one to another, slow, deliberate, making a tuneless scale that the bowl carries and enlarges until it sounds like the stones themselves are thinking aloud. No one scolds them for it. In a town that is a drum, even play has to be tuned.

Lower down, by the cisterns, an old man dresses stone where it fell. That is law here. He does not quarry. He walks the rim-fall after frost has worked, finds where the cliff has already split a blue-black slab clean, and kneels there in the weep and squares it with patient taps, ash-dust blooming on his forearms. He will not round it. None of them will. The elders say the bowl will round itself in ten thousand years; until then, sharp locks better. And you can see it — in the retaining walls tall as two men, teeth interlocked, angular block against angular block, no river-smooth boulder anywhere because no river has lived here long enough to make one. His hands know weight the way the woman knows water: without looking up.

They are not farmers, not really, though there is a communal threshing platform swept obsessively clean of moss. It is too high for grain and they know it. What they grow is foothold. They tend the moss the way lowlanders tend fields.

That is the other population, and by far the larger: the green that went mad.

Lime-green star moss and black-green cushion moss in mattresses a foot thick that sigh under your step. Liverworts like wet leather in every seep. Ferns uncurling tight as fists from cracks. Dwarf willow no higher than your knee but a hundred years old, trunks twisted as rope, leaves silvered with bead-water. Woolly white edelweiss-cousins and purple saxifrage jammed furiously into any dust of crushed stone, blooming close to the ground to get out of the wind. The people do not own this. They weed around it, lift slates for it, divert — never dam — water around it. Cut no living moss for fuel: that is the first true commandment, because the moss is what pins the sharp stones down, what makes soil where there is no soil yet. Peel it and underneath is barren rock-flour that washes away in a day. So they burn only deadfall pine carried up from below on bent backs, and cut peat sparingly from the thickest mattress where it will regrow, leaving a neat square wound that greens over by next season if you watch.

And with the green, its small keepers.

Four shaggy highland sheep, stone-grey, live on the turf roofs and continue the moss right over the houses. You don't see them until they move, and then a whole roofline shifts. Their bells are the only tame sound, dull and intermittent under the roar, *tung... tung*, when they pull at grass. Someone — a boy with a pika-tooth necklace, barefoot by choice — moves them with stamps and hand-claps, never with voice. No dogs. No chickens. Too high, too wet, too new for most tame lives to have found it yet.

The wild lives are rumors that have just arrived and haven't decided to stay.

A pika calls once from the rim rocks, a sharp *eep!* that the bowl throws back, and then thinks better of it and vanishes. A pair of mountain crows ride the updraft off the veil-falls all afternoon, the only voices louder than water, croaking and tumbling, at home in the enormous sky in a way the people aren't yet. Clouds of tiny midges hang over the standing puddles, rising and settling in columns. And in the moss itself lives a pale, almost translucent moth, the color of lichen, that you only see when your boot sighs into a cushion and a dozen of them lift a hand's breadth and settle again, as if the mattress exhaled them.

None of them are numerous. Everything here is just beginning to learn that it can live here.

By evening you understand the people a little better because you see them lit. Light lingers long and white at this height, then mist rises — not from the valley a thousand meters below, but from the bowl itself, exhaled off the moss as the air cools another notch. The veil-falls thicken and blur. The three green terraces soften into steps. And in niches in the walls, absurdly small in all that wet stone and space, the tallow lamps in stone cups come on like fireflies. A family eats shoulder to shoulder under a turf eave with water hissing off it, speaking low, foreheads almost touching, while ten paces away the roar goes on unchanged — high white hiss of falling water, mid-tone rush in the under-drains, low hum when wind catches the rim and the whole cup resonates. Drip, seep, trickle, needle-pricks on top. You can hear your own heart under it if you listen.

They are not a remnant. They are not hiding. They came — recently, within a lifetime or two, judging by the unworn thresholds and the half-built wall with its hammer-stones still left on top — because it is safe and wet and green when all around is old grey wind-scoured ridge. Because the stone was waiting already broken. Because the water cannot be poisoned upstream; there is no upstream, only sky and rim.

So they live by unfinished rules for unfinished land: never dam, only channel; take only dead wood and frost-split stone; keep the sheet moving while the world learns its shape. Thirty people in a town for three hundred, keeping the drains clear, keeping the moss down-pinned, knocking *tock tock* across a loud, patient, bright-cold garden that hasn't decided if it is a city that hasn't decided if it is a rock.

They are not its owners. They are its midwives.
