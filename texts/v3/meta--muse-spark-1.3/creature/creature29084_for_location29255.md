At first you will be sure nothing lives here.

Not really. The moths, the gray wader, the ticks in the joints — those are margin-life, and you will file them away as margin-life. What you are listening for, walking that empty boulevard with your own steps slapping back too loud off the wet walls, is something the size of you. A voice. A second footfall. And there isn't one. Vast space between any two living presences. That part is true.

It takes a day, maybe two, sweating sweat that never dries, breathing thin and fast, before you start to notice that empty and uninhabited are not the same thing here. The chair is warm. The kettle in the open doorway is warm. The tram sitting mid-street with its pantograph still up — its windows are fogged from the inside, and if you wipe it, the fog comes back. Someone breathed there, not long ago. A laundry line that was still in the morning is empty by afternoon. A pilot flame you could have sworn was out is burning again.

They are sparse on purpose. That is the first thing to understand.

There are maybe thirty of them left in a bowl built for thousands. Maybe fewer now. You will never see two together, because they have learned what the heron knows: in heat like this, another body near yours is unbearable. So they keep a tram-route, a terrace, a whole housing block between each other. Each keeps to a range, a circuit of pipes and cisterns and stairways to tend. You walk all day and are the only footfall because they heard you coming a half-mile off and stepped sideways into the steam.

Don't picture them arriving. Picture them remaining, and being unmade slowly into something that can remain.

They are still human-shaped enough that your doubled shadow will fool you at first. Up high where the air should be thin and cold, and instead the ground cooks you from the ankles up, people thin out. They go long and narrow, chest casks for the thin air, limbs ropy from stairs. Their skin has gone the color of the place — not tanned, blanched then stained. A dusk-pale under a varnish of rust and salt. The biofilm that grows in the gutters, that living varnish, grows on them too, in the creases of elbows and behind ears, a thin orange-brown sheen where sweat never dries. They don't wash it off. It keeps the metals in the water from raising blisters. Salt blooms white at their hairlines and on their eyelashes like frost. If you got close — you won't get close — you'd see their teeth faintly blue at the roots. That low transformer hum you feel in your molars? They live inside it. They've lived inside it so long they use it to find each other, pressing a jaw to a cool pipe to listen for whose hum is cycling where.

They do not call. Nothing here calls or sings; the air is too thin and too hot to waste it. They tap. Two knocks on an insulated main means *pump about to run, lift your feet.* Three means *venting soon, get under.* You will have heard it and thought it was cooling metal, that *tink-tink-tink.*

They eat what the place offers, because nothing green has grown here in a very long time. They scrape sulfur cauliflower off shaded walls — careful, only the white heads, never the yellow — and boil it with handfuls of lamp-moths. The moths are almost translucent, dusted with sulfur, stupid with light. At dimming, when the pilot flames come out as a constellation, the moth clouds get so thick around the sodium lamps they cast huge slow shadows, and the tenders just walk into them with their mouths and shirts open. Protein that tastes of struck matches. They chew the dried sheets of gutter-film when the cisterns run low, though it binds the gut. They do not drink from the ground pools. The white hands painted under the bridges are theirs, repainted every season. They know where the canal runs cold for a few feet for no reason and steams in reverse. They don't drink there either. Too cold is as bad as too hot. It means something underneath is breathing.

Water is their whole work. Water weeps straight out of the cut rock, condenses on every cool cable and rains back down ticking on awnings, runs an inch deep and hot down the lower streets and never drains. The city was built to move it, and the city still idles — pump houses shuddering awake every few minutes to groan water uphill, louvers breathing open and shut, rooftop vents lifting with a soft *whoosh* to dump white jets that fall as hot rain. The tenders don't run this. No one could run this. They coax it. They clear a culvert with a hooked pole. They turn a valve a quarter-turn back from screaming. They relight a pilot before gas can pool. Each tends a small constellation of those hundreds of flames on walls and stacks, walking the night circuit with a taper, outlining streets and stairways and the rims of the great cisterns so the place stays legible from the rim. Without them, depth would go entirely.

Their day is built around the siren and the venting hour. You will learn it too if you stay. Don't sit on metal after the siren — because the low iron, the catwalks and rails and manhole rims, will take the next heat pulse and brand you through cloth. They sit only on basalt, on the smooth-backed ridges of old flow, or on wood swollen soft with steam. And be under cover at venting hour. When the vents are all closed in the stillest heat of afternoon and those thousands of droplets hang at chest height over the lower plazas, neither rising nor falling, catching lamp light — that is not weather. That is the city inhaling. The tenders stand very still at the edges under awnings and half-pipes, heads bowed, arms out a little from their sides, letting the hang settle all at once as if decided. They open their mouths to it. It is the only water cold enough and clean enough to drink straight, condensed out of air and not yet touched by rock. A minute of manna, twice a day. Then hot rain, then runoff, then steam again.

That double shadow you noticed even where there is only one lamp — watch theirs. It lags a half-step, paler at the edges, as if looking through water. Everyone who stays here gets it after a while. The old-timers thought it was sickness from the metals. The ones now think it is just accumulated steam, a personal cloud that follows you because you are always sweating and the air around you is always a little wetter. They are half-right. On cold mornings, if you ever catch one crossing a plaza — tall, narrow, gray as the featherless-looking wader in the outflow marsh — you will see the second shadow detach slightly at the bridge-colds and hesitate over the reverse-steaming water, then catch up. They walk like the wader walks: statue-still for an hour in the black mud flat where the city's water finally spreads and cools, standing ankle-deep in warm muck rimmed with white crust, waiting for heat-ticks to come to the salt on their legs to pick them off. They learned that posture from the bird. Or the bird learned it from them. There are only ever one, maybe two waders in a day. Same with people.

They sleep where it ticks. Not in the beds made in the tenements — those rooms are too still, the hollow has its own pressure in the ears. They sleep curled in pump houses between cycles, in the lee of cisterns, in the abandoned tram when it rains hot, fogging it from inside. They sleep in short naps, because every few minutes something cycles and the whole bowl shudders and a sheet of siding pops free somewhere out of sight like a gunshot. They like that. The noise covers movement. That's when they move — during the groan and the whoosh — darting across open boulevards while the city is louder than they are.

Are they lonely? You will ask that on the second night, watching the pilot flames outline everything and the steam glow faintly from below, stone sweating and iron sweating and you sweating. The answer is in the varnish. That rust-colored slick in the gutters is not plant, not quite fungus. Peel it when it dries and by morning it has reformed. Sample it on the upper roads and sample it down in the industrial terraces by the furnace mouths and it is the same organism, continuous through canals and half-pipes and weeping rock for miles. A single slow mind spread thin across the whole bowl, tasting metals, holding water. The tenders feed it with their sweat and their piss and their dead moth husks, and it feeds them back by keeping the water moving and the pipes from corroding through. They press their palms to it in the morning. They read the night's heat in its thickness. In a way, they are never alone. They live inside a body larger than theirs that idles but does not die.

If you stay until the dim dims further, be quiet. Don't try to follow a tender. Just take cover at venting hour, don't sit on metal after the siren, don't drink from the ground pools. Stand under an awning while the droplets hang and listen to the drip and tick and your own thin breathing. You will hear, under the transformer hum in your teeth, a very soft slapping, bare feet in an inch of hot runoff, stopping when the pump stops, starting when it starts. A white jet will lift off a rooftop and condense instantly. For a second the steam will part and you will see, across the plaza, a tall gray figure with salt on its eyelashes and moth-dust on its shoulders, arms held slightly out, mouth open to the settling air, with its second shadow pooled at its feet like spilled water.

Then the lamps will halo orange in the gloom, the furnaces will breathe red down below, the echo will take its time crossing the bowl, and it will be gone — back into the grid of tenements with laundry lines still strung, back into the workshops with roller doors rusted partway up, back into the vast space between any two living presences, where it lives, and tends, and sweats, and holds.
