At first you think nothing lives here.

Nothing upright does. That is the first correction the place makes to you.

Then you kneel — and your knees go numb through your trousers — and you see the floor is not floor at all.

It is alive. Low and total.

The main resident is not a who. It is a what, a foot thick and three feet thick in the dips, edge to edge between the boulders and over them. A sponge. A mat.

Sphagnum moss is its bulk, electric green on top where it is growing, rust-red and tea-brown underneath where it is drowning and becoming itself again. It grows a millimeter a year, which is to say it does not hurry. Water rises around your boot when you stand on it and the whole thing sighs and settles. Beneath it, stone still scratched from ice. On top of it, everything else using it as ground. Roots in moss. Shoots in humus that is barely humus yet. Flowers growing out of the corpses of last year's moss.

Pressed into that mat, flat as if held down by the weight of the light, live the others.

Dwarf willow, woody and ancient and two inches tall, its leaves the size of fingernails, its trunks crawling sideways for decades to stay out of the wind. Cushion moss, hard as a fist. Starry saxifrage, throwing up thin stalks with white stars that tremble. Liverworts black-green as wet leather, sealing themselves to slabs that weep. In the hollows where the water table is an inch below everything, cotton grass — thousands of them, white heads nodding all one way when the wind comes up off the drop — rimming pools so still they hold the sky.

And the tiny red killers. Sundews, no bigger than a coin, waiting in the wet with their tentacles beaded. There are almost no flies up here to catch, the midges rise in a brief glitter and are blown flat, but they wait anyway. Red on green. Patient.

Even the trees, what few try, live here by not being trees. Down near the rim there are spruce, or the idea of spruce — scattered, stunted, flagged hard east by decades of wind, knee-high after thirty, forty years. They do not rise. They creep. Their needles are dark and resinous and stung with ice half the year.

The animals have not really moved in. Evolutionarily speaking, they are still scouting. This is too wet for burrows, too young for soil, too exposed for cover. No herds. No flocks.

What you get are events.

A ptarmigan, still mottled white-brown for summer, that you flush once in a whole afternoon from behind a till shoulder. It clatters off on loud wings and is gone in seconds, white against white ridges.

A water pipit working the edge of a pool, brown and streaked and impossibly loud for a second between gusts, stabbing at midges before the wind takes them.

In the pools themselves, tadpoles. Nothing else moves. Slow as minutes in water brown as tea, commas of black life waiting to become frogs in a place with almost no summer. They are perhaps the truest animals here — aquatic, cold-blooded, content not to be upright.

And then, with so much space between presences that you miss them at first, the other residents. The upright ones that are not alive but live here anyway.

Half-swallowed by the moss near a braid: a concrete intake box, its steel grate humming with suction. A low weir, algae-slick, metering water into a buried penstock you can trace only by the line where the moss sags. A rusted pylon marching to the rim and then down, down toward a town you can't see.

Down near the lip, where all the sheet-water gathers to throw itself off, three small flat-roofed buildings of weather-gray concrete. A catchment station. A turbine hall. A store. No road to them now. Narrow, bright windows. A door with green lights that blink, indifferent. A low vent that breathes warm air that condenses instantly in the cold.

Inside, the steadiest resident of all: a turbine the size of a truck, spinning on meltwater, governed, automatic. It spools up over hours. Gates open a centimeter to let a spring surge through. It feeds power down the cable and hums — a steady, held organ note under the roar of the falls, the thing you feel in your sternum when the wind drops for a moment. For ten feet around the door you smell ozone and hot oil. Then gone. Swallowed by peat and rain and cold iron.

No one lives with it. Most weeks, no people at all. The shepherd's hut a mile off has its roof caved and moss growing straight through the slats — whoever wintered there is long gone. There is one cairn. One weather mast ticking in the wind, its guy wires singing. A mile between each.

Once a fortnight, maybe, in good weather, a maintenance man or woman hikes up the wet ruts to clear the grate with a rake, check the oil, log the levels in widened bogs that serve as reservoirs, and leave again. Boots sink half an inch. Footprints fill. The door locks behind them with a click that is instantly lost. The green lights keep blinking to no one.

That is who lives here: a mat a million plants thick that is slowly becoming soil, a few flat willows and star-flowers and white nodding heads and red carnivorous dots and creeping spruce, a handful of scouting birds and slow tadpoles, and three gray boxes and a humming machine that breathes warm air into cold light and keeps the power on below.

Nothing fast. Nothing tall. Just a high, cold, saturated sponge of living matter laid over unfinished stone, humming and weeping and glinting — holding on, low to the ground, at the edge of its own loud falling off.
