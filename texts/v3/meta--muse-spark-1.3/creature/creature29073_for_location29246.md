At first you think nothing lives here.

That's the trick of the bowl. From the rim it's all buildings and interval — brick, iron, knee-high grass wet till noon — and no people in it. The streets are too wide and empty. The flywheel turns once every twenty seconds. Drip, drip from the pipe bridges. You wait for someone to come out and explain it.

Then you see movement that isn't machine movement, and you realize you've been looking straight at them.

They are maintenance.

There are not many. A dozen, maybe fewer on some days, enough to fill a fraction of the two rows of slate-roofed houses and leave the rest to moss and wrens. Old enough to have slowed down to the speed of the place. Men and women both, undistinguished at distance by cut of coat — heavy jackets gone dark at the shoulders from the perpetual condensation, boots dark at the toes, a rag in a back pocket, an oil can in a side pocket the way other people carry keys. Pale in the way people get under bright overcast that never quite becomes sun. Damp-handed. Patient-eyed.

They walk like the flywheel turns. Not slow because they are tired, slow because hurrying would be a category error here. You cross the fifteen minutes in an hour if you live here, because you will stop to listen to a bearing, to sweep water out of a doorway with a pushed broom, to clear leaves from a grate before the culvert chokes and ticks louder. Their day is not shifts. It is rounds. Around the mill, around the machine shop, along the pipe trestles to check for beads, under the belt-drive housing like going through a covered bridge. Oiling what needs oiling. Wire-brushing the rust bloom where the sweat of the metal has started orange under a flange, then oiling again. Coaxing.

You can tell them by their listening. A visitor hears noise — the low molar-felt groan every time the twelve-foot pitman comes around, the boiler sighing and ticking, the ventilator fan forever out of balance going *tak-tak-tak-tak* in its housing, the white roar of steam let off at no time you can predict that sends the hundred pigeons up all at once like laundry snapped. The keepers hear weather. They can tell the groan that means normal from the groan that means a foundation bolt wants a quarter-turn. They don't flinch at the steam. They look up, count the pigeons, look back down. They have learned, the way you learn gulls at the sea, to distinguish the real squeal of a dry bearing from a starling doing a perfect dry bearing from three lots over. They smile, just barely, when they're fooled. The birds fool them two or three times a week. They're proud of that.

They live in the intervals, the way everything here does.

The rule of the buildings is leave space, and the people have taken it personally. They don't cluster. One will be at the north wall of the machine shop where the moss is ankle-thick on the vibration side — because the hum shakes the dew into the brick, and that is where the wall sweats most, and that is where the rust will start — brushing, humming tunelessly under the hum. Another will be half a quarter-mile off, on knees in wet grass pulling bindweed off a guy-wire hand over hand. They call to each other rarely, across that cool pooled air where breath shows till ten. Sound bounces off the spoil ring and stays in. So they have learned not to shout unless it's steam-time and shouting is useless anyway. They gesture. A raised oil can means *come look*. A hand flat on a transformer box means *warm, give it a minute*.

In the houses, they nest the way the fauna nests. A kettle always on. Boots lined up to dry that never quite dry. Windows wiped daily and beaded again by evening. Sodium-orange lamplight from outside coming in all night because the lamps are always on — buzzing, moth-flocked even at noon in the cool brightness — so their front rooms have that double light: gray day from one side, orange hum from the other. They sleep cool. The bowl holds cold the way it holds sound. Blankets smell faintly of hot oil and wet stone no matter how often washed. They don't mind. It's the smell of the small sun they tend.

Because that is what they tend. Not production. That scaled down long ago. This is a factory for keeping factories, an electrified, mechanized hearth. The mill engine idles all day, not making anything, just turning. Just staying alive. The keepers feed it oil and attention the way you feed a very old, very large animal that can no longer walk far but still breathes heat-shimmer off its chimney. They think of warmth the way plants here think of warmth. And the plants have taught them.

Look: seedlings in an exact green dotted line under every pipe run where condensation falls nightly. Ferns unrolling not toward light but toward the boiler wall, the culvert grate, the transformer. Pigeons spaced along a warm pipe at exact intervals, biggest to smallest, as if placed. The keepers do the same. On cold mornings you will find two of them with their backs to the boiler house wall, coats steaming faintly, drinking tea, saying nothing, turned to face heat the way the ferns are. At noon they stand under the pipe bridges for a minute on their rounds just to let the drip hit their hats. They know where it's warmest to put your hands. So do the three-legged cats.

The cats are residents with seniority. There are four or five, all missing something — a leg, an ear tip, half a tail — the tax the old machines took years ago and have not taken since because now everyone, human and cat, knows where not to step when the pitman comes around. They sleep on the transformer boxes where it's warm, curled tight, unbothered by the *tak-tak-tak*, by the starlings doing wrench-on-concrete. The keepers step around them. One woman leaves the corner of her warehouse doorway open even in thin rain for the black one. Property lines are respected here, even across species. The green grows around, never quite over. The people weed around, never quite out.

The other animals are not pets. They are colleagues, or landlords. The goats — someone's, everyone's — six or seven of them, bells gone dull green with verdigris in the damp, cropping the lots between the shops with soft rips you can hear between groans if you stand at the cross. The keepers move their tethers when a lot gets cropped short, and the goats know the rounds and follow at a distance for the salt on a hand. The rats run the culverts bold in daylight, fat on spilled grain from the warehouse with its doors permanently open, and the keepers let them run; the traps have been put away for years. Only the food store is proofed. A negotiation. The frogs in the tire ruts are so fat and unbothered you have to step carefully after rain — the keepers do a small sidestep you learn after a week, automatic. Lift any board left out and there are beetles underneath, black and shining, doing their own maintenance. Wrens stitch the bramble with alarm and song. Moths batter the sodium lamps. And the robin —

everyone knows the robin. She built in the elbow of the steam exhaust, a cup of moss and grass in warm iron, impossible, suicidal, perfect. When the workers found her they didn't move her. They rigged a little shield from a scrap of tin, angled so the white roar goes over her and the warmth still reaches under. On steam-days the keeper on boiler round checks the shield first, before the gauge. He talks to her, low, under the fan rattle. She stays. Her eye is bright as a flange bolt. That is the level of negotiation here: they will alter the plumbing of the sun for a six-ounce bird.

If you stand at the cross at noon — cool light flat on your face, mill groan to your left, fan rattle to your right, smell of coal-smoke and chlorophyll and hot grease, wet glint on everything, sharp black spoil teeth all around holding it in — you will see them at last as they are: not operators, not owners. Symbionts.

Moss up to the ankle. Ferns in the mortar. Buddleia splitting concrete over three patient years. White birches leaning out of slag. Grass coming up through wide empty streets. Pigeons, starlings, wrens, rats, cats, goats, frogs, beetles, moths, one shielded robin. And among them, upright, oil-can in pocket, jacket damp, boots dark, moving at twenty seconds per revolution, the humans who have agreed to keep the engines idling so the warmth continues to fall like rain in lines, so the dew keeps shaking into the brick, so everything lush and living can keep facing the small cool damp sun they have made.

They don't need to go further. There's nowhere further to go.
