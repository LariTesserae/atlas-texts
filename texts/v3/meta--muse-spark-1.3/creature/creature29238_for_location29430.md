No one lives here, if by someone you mean eyes that meet yours.

You look for that first, out of habit. You sweep the headlamp and wait for the flinch-back, the eyeshine, the wing-dust, the scuttle. Nothing gives it. No moth comes to the light. No midge hangs in the drip. No mouse, no pika, no spider strung between the blocks. The night is too scoured, the hold too new and too high for anything that has to hold on with feet or keep its heat inside itself.

And then you stop looking for animals and realize the upholstery *is* the animal. The green is not decoration on the room. It is the resident.

What lives here lives flat and soaked and without pause. It does not walk. It does not call. It covers.

First, because there is most of it, the mosses. Not a moss, a thousand mosses grown into each other until you can't pull one tuft without pulling ten. They came as dust — spores on the same updraft that now roars through the mouth — and landed on knife-edge basalt that had been broken open a winter or two ago and had no soil, no softening, nothing to offer except texture. It was enough.

They do not root. They have no roots to put down. They grip with rhizoids fine as hair and drink with their whole bodies. A moss leaf is one cell thick in places; it cannot afford to be waterproof. So here, in a place where cloud is forced over the lip and stalls against the backwall and sweats out of the air onto everything, they are in perfect luck. They don't wait for rain. They comb it. Every frond tip gathers a bead, swells, lets go. Patter, ceaseless. In the cups between blocks they sit in an inch of black mirror water and don't drown because they were never built to keep water out.

Look close — closer than you want to in the cold with clumsy fingers — and the mat resolves into layers of time. On top, emerald, alive this month, springy, the growing tips turned up like velvet nap. Under that, olive, last year's growth still holding water like a sponge. Under that, almost-black green, suffocated but not rotted, because cold and acid slow rot to a crawl. Ten centimeters thick on a table-sized block that fell edge-up and hasn't yet fallen again. Sporophytes rise out of it everywhere, thin reddish setae with capsules nodding like pins stuck in a cushion, shaking every time a larger block settles underneath with that dull iron *clonk* and the whole floor trembles. They will open in daylight and let the piston wind — *huh-huh-HUUUH* — do the sowing.

They are softening nothing. That's important. On older mountains moss rounds and soils. Here the arrises under the moss are still glass-sharp. Lift the mat and the rock beneath is blue-grey and raw, unworn. The moss doesn't digest the sharpness, it just upholsters it, makes a green cast of a wound. When the block shifts with a dry click under your boot and tips into a dark slot, the mat tears. By next storm it is already creeping back over the tear. Everything falls. Everything regrows over the fall before the edge has dulled. That is their whole economy.

Second, hanging underneath: the filmy ferns.

Where a slab leans on another slab and leaves a black slot, they hang down in curtains, translucent as skin. Hymenophyllum in all but name, if not in name. Their fronds are one cell thick too, no cuticle, no protection — leaves that should only be possible in a greenhouse. Outside the room they would crisp in an hour. Inside, they never dry. The rock sweats, the moss wrings, the air itself stays at saturation, thin and wet at once, tasting of iron and crushed stone. So they can afford to be naked.

They shiver constantly. Not from cold, though it is springhouse-cold, breath-visible cold. From weight. Each pinnule holds a bead at its tip until the bead is heavier than surface tension and falls — *pit* onto moss, onto stone, onto your hood. Then the frond, lightened, springs back a millimeter. Ten thousand of them doing that out of sync is the high patter you hear under the wind-mouth throb and the mid hiss of seep and the ticking of stone on stone. In the three-second grey luminescence when the cloud tears overhead you can see them beaded all over, each one outlined in water.

They live where light almost doesn't. In day this pocket gets little direct sun — walled on three sides, open to cloud — and tonight, moonless, there is none at all. They are built for that. They photosynthesize furiously in gloom that would starve a seed plant, spread sideways by creeping rhizomes along the underside of rock, and wait. A falling pebble — *tik-tik-tik-tak* down the organ-pipe wall — can shred a curtain in a second. It doesn't matter. The rhizome remains in the crack, wet, and starts a new curtain.

Third, sealing everything: the liverworts.

If moss is the fat and fern is the hanging cloth, liverwort is the skin. In the joints where drip is constant, where you would have to dig with your fingers to find rock at all, they lie in overlapping scales, dark green to brownish, starry with gemma cups full of rain. Leafy liverworts plaster the verticals of the hexagonal columns where the fall sheared them clean, tracing the damp lines down. Thallose ones make sodden mats in the drip-channels, so dense you can't tell where one plant ends. They smell the strongest when you brush them — peppery and earthy and sweet-rotten, greenhouse left in rain — because they hold the rot inside the living layer. No grazers to prune them, no insects to eat the spore capsules. So they pile, ferment slightly, grow over their own dead.

That absence is part of their bodies. In a normal cloud forest these mats would be cropped, tunneled, nibbled, carried off. Here, too high, too new, too storm-beaten that night, nothing with a mouth has arrived. So there is no check. Frond over moss over liverwort over moss again, near-touching, no bare interval larger than your hand except the fresh scar and yesterday's fall. Lush beyond reason because no one is eating the lushness. They are alone with mineral and weather, and they are using all of it.

Together they make one continuous creature more than three kinds. A living skin that breathes with the squalls. When the pressure drops before rain and the wind holds its breath and every drip hangs half a second longer, the skin waits, charged, hairs on your forearms lifting from the static the sharp column tips are gathering. Then rain lashes sideways, the temporary falls restart brown-white over the rim for five minutes, spray hits like valve steam, and the whole skin is flattened and released, flattened and released — a green bellows working hard.

It does not sleep. At night, when there is no light to make sugar, it still grows, still drinks, still repairs. It ticks water downward through dark slots between blocks where you hear it but can't see it. It holds an inch of trembling water in every cup and lets it go slowly. It catches the cloud boiling over the lip, sheared apart on the columns, wrings the water out of it, and lets the shredded remainder be thrown back out by the updraft.

If you press your palm flat to the wall, water beads up around your fingers immediately. That is not the rock weeping. That is the resident exhaling.
