No one lives here the way you mean living.

You look at first for the signs — a wall to break the wind, a fire-blackened ring, a path worn down to mineral, a cairn. There is nothing like that. No straight line holds. Your eye keeps trying to find a roof and keeps finding only the Roof itself, and after a while you stop looking for houses and start seeing occupancy of a different kind.

Because the ground is occupied to the depth of your arm and then further down. Because the air is occupied to the height of your head and then further up. What lives here does not clear a place to live. It *is* the place.

At first they are only shapes in the ground-mist.

You are wading knee-deep in the evening cloud between tussocks and you think the swells ahead of you are moving. Not wind-moving. Grazing-moving. A darkness low and long unthreads itself from the white and becomes backs. Many backs. A hundred abreast.

They come more themselves as you stand still and let the cold get into your sleeves.

The grazers. Heavy, barrel-bodied, low-slung so their bellies almost comb the moss. Some walk on four, some on six — on this sponge it hardly matters how many pillars you have, only how wide you spread them. Their feet come down and do not sink. Toes splay like snowshoes, nails blunt and broad, ankles flexing to let the mat sigh up around them and sigh back. They are shaggy to the point of thatch. Long guard-hairs shed water in beads; under that, a dense wool holds air. And on the guard-hairs themselves, green. Old bulls go entirely grey-green over the shoulders where filamentous lichens have taken hold and now ride them like epiphytes on a slow tree. They do not scrape them off. Weight is not a problem here if you distribute it.

Their heads are low and blunt, lips prehensile and perpetually wet, teeth that meet with a soft, piston *clop-clop* you feel in your sternum more than hear. They move in a line and crop in a line. A hundred mouths take the top inch off the tussock fountains and step forward together. Behind them, the cropped crowns exhale a sharper green smell, and the dark dung falls steaming — and does not last. Within minutes it boils. That is the only word. Dung-beetles, black and chestnut and copper-green, erupt out of the moss in such numbers the pat seems to shiver and liquefy and then simply subside, drawn down tunnel by tunnel into the peat. By the time the grazer line has moved ten paces on, there is no pat. There is a slightly darker, slightly warmer green place that the moss has already closed over.

They do not look at you. Their eyes are small, lashed against drip, set high. Calves stay in the middle of the line, pressed flank to flank, steaming faintly. Everything about them says: slow is safe, low is warm, together is ground.

And so the wolves that live off them have learned to be slow too.

You see them later, on a long swell where the light is milky and even. Pale, almost white, long-legged to the point of stilted, narrow-chested for thin air. They trot. They never run here — nothing that weighs more than a moth runs on sponge. Their feet are splayed wider than the grazers', their pads fringed with stiff hair that sheds water with each step in a little flick. They move in a loose string, five, seven, heads low, noses working. They do not bark. The moss would swallow a bark. When they take an old grazer that has lagged, they do it lying down, jaws to throat, waiting for the great barrel heart to slow. They eat with their whole muzzles buried, white ruffs stained amber with peat-water and red with blood, and the steam rises off them in the cool until they look like part of the ground-cloud.

Above them, what waits.

You hear no wingbeat before the shadow crosses you. The vultures ride the upslope wind where the roof breaks and the cliffs weep. Huge, dark, condor-deep, but ruffed in white wool like the rosettes below — as if they grew their own cloud-collar. They turn in great silent wheels, ten, twenty, wingtips almost touching, never flapping. They are waiting for wolves to finish, for age to finish, for saturation to finish. When a carcass comes, they drop without sound and mantle it, and the flies come first in a low veil, then the beetles, and then, within a season, you will not find bone. You will find a small green hill, slightly warmer than its surroundings, already sprouting two or three young cabbage-on-stilts rosettes from its crown. Death here is just a brief hill.

But the most numerous of the large lives are not the eaters of grass or flesh. They are the black birds.

You smell the ibis rookery before you see it — not foul, just dense, marshy, ammoniac and feather-oily. Then the moss ahead resolves into black. Thousands to a hectare, shoulder to shoulder on low nests of plaited tussock, so tight the colony looks from a distance like another tarn, a black water. They are ibis-like only: long down-curved bills, glossy black plumage that beads water perfectly, short legs sunk to the hock in sponge. Parents stand and regurgitate into open, trembling beaks, chicks peeping with a faint wet sound that the moss drinks instantly. When a wolf trots too near, there is no panic-flight — flight costs too much up here. The outer birds simply stand, lift their bills in a bristling palisade, and settle again. Their guano whitens the moss between nests and feeds it. The colony moves a few meters each year, leaving behind a rich, trampled mat that erupts in rosettes.

And over all of it, in shifts at midday, the hatch.

You think at first the milky sun has dimmed. Then you see the air has thickened. Great pale moths, the size of your palm, the size of a sparrow, rise out of the tussock bases where their woolly larvae have fed for a year or two years on half-drowned litter. They rise in clouds so vast they turn noon to dusk for minutes. Their wings are felted, dusted, sound-absorbent; they pass overhead with a sound no louder than someone turning a page, millions of pages. They live a day, two days. They mate in the air, lay on the wind, die onto the moss where spiders are already waiting.

The spiders have strung the gaps between tussock heads with sheets so dense and beaded you mistake them for more fog. Each sheet is tended — an old female with a swollen amber abdomen and legs like wet twine sits head-down in the center, repairing, tightening, harvesting the stunned moths, wrapping them in wool-white silk until the sheet looks strung with pearls. She never leaves it. Her whole life is that yard of fog-net.

Below her, in tunnels an inch wide kept as clean as a factory floor, run the leaf-cutter lines. For kilometers they run, two-way traffic, each ant-like cutter — though they are not ants, they are something stockier, red-headed and cold-slow — carrying aloft a green sail-fragment many times its size. The highways dip under cushion plants, bridge rills on living moss arches, disappear into thatch and reappear. They do not hurry, but they never stop. The green goes down into the sponge to fungus gardens that glow faintly if you pull the mat apart, sour-sweet smelling.

And in the black tarns, what lives without ever seeing this.

Tea-dark water, perfectly still, rimmed with gold moss. Beneath, mats of algae that pulse faintly blue at dusk — a nerve-flicker under the surface — and hunting through that field, blind white catfish, long-whiskered, pink-gilled, navigating by the electric hum. They nose the peat-stones that rise after storms, light as cork, bobbing for days, picking off the drowned gnats.

Even smaller, and everywhere: mites on beetles on liverwort on moss on lichen on stone. Beetles occupying liverwort. Mites occupying beetles. Woolly hairs of the rosettes combing droplets and also housing tardigrades like slow bears in the water-beads.

The rosettes themselves — are they who lives here? Cabbages on stilts, trunks thick with old leaf-bases, crowns of thick woolly leaves holding last night's rain like cups. Each is a fog condenser, each a cistern and an island. Frogs the size of your thumbnail, black with gold bellies, live their whole lives in one cistern, tadpoles in the leaf-axils, never touching ground. Gnats cloud over them. The plant drinks cloud, overflows to sponge, exhales vapor in the afternoon to make its own low weather.

That is what lives here. Not a species. A packing. A slow, cold, wet industry of bodies fitting into bodies, mouths fitting to crops, feet fitting to sponge, all muffled in wool and water so the great work — filtering, distilling, weaving, digesting, condensing — proceeds at a hum below hearing.

Stay until pearl-evening, until your boots are full of cold seep and the tarns begin to flicker, and you will feel them settle around you: ten thousand ibis adjusting on touching nests, the grazer line bedding down with a collective sigh, wolves curling nose to tail on a dry dome, moths folding, spiders tightening their sheets against night frost. No roof but cloud. No wall but mist. Everything occupied, everything fitting, everything continuing at a millimeter a year and a continent wide.
