On a first pass you will say no one lives here.

The moss lives, of course, if you count that as living. The machines run, if you count that. And there are people, a few, moving between lit windows. But none of it looks like inhabitation. It looks like leftovers. Like a mill-town after the whistle when everyone has gone home, except no one ever blows the whistle and home is the mill.

Stay longer. Walk the week of nothing between lights. Let your boots fill and empty and fill. Let the throb get into your sternum and stay there. Then you start to see them properly — not as visitors, not as a skeleton crew, but as what the place has made to live in itself.

They are human, still. That is the first strangeness and the last.

Short, broad, quiet people. You notice the chest first: barrelled out by a lifetime of thin air, breathing a little too deliberately on the stairs, ribs showing through wet wool when they haul. The air hurts you for a day. It has hurt them into shape. Big lungs, slow hearts — you can see the pulse crawl in their throats when they stand still at a gauge, sixty, fifty beats a minute, unhurried as peat water.

Their skin never dries. No one's does here. Hands gone soft-white and seamed at the knuckles from perpetual wet, faces wind-burned a permanent raw pink under grey. Their hair is lank with drizzle. Their eyes, after years under that white, level, unfiltered noon-that-never-ends, have gone the color of the place: lichen-grey, shale-green, the pale yellow of frosted glass. They don't squint. There is nothing to squint at. They look best at close range — at moss, at a hairline crack in a penstock, at the needle of a dial that has not moved in living memory and must still be watched.

Because watching is what they do.

They are not operators. Nothing here needs operating. The turbines turn with the water that will never cut a gorge. The looms loom. The electrolysis sheds hum. The trains leave full and come back empty. A keeper will tell you this if you ask, without pride or bitterness: "She runs herself." Then he will go back to oiling a bearing that has no wear, chalking a tally for ingots that will be fed back into the furnace, cutting back the moss.

Ah, the moss. You will see them every Tuesday — though no one remembers why Tuesday — out with flat spades along the turbine halls, the culverts, the hot pipes, the vibrating foundations. Green felt creeps up brick, over sill, into the warmth, a finger's width a week. They cut it to the line. The line is always in the same place. An old woman in the northern ward showed me, laughing without sound in the roar: a faint white paint mark under the green, thirty years old, still exactly at the edge of the cut. "She remembers," she said, and meant both the moss and the mill.

Their clothes have remembered too. Oilskin over wool over felt, all of it blackened with peat oil and green at the seams where spores have taken hold and will not wash out. They don't try to wash it. A keeper's coat, hung by a boiler to dry, steams and drips and never dries, and smells — you learn the smell — of wet sheep long gone, of hot iron, of peat and ozone and clean rot. They move in it like divers, slow, economical. No wasted motion to make you sweat, no standing still long enough to chill. Working cold has taught them the gait: steady, ankle-deep, splayed-footed for mire, sure-footed on frost-split fin.

They are deaf, most of them, and don't know it. Not stone-deaf. Roar-deaf. Close up the shriek of steam and crash of mill-race takes the top off everything; far out the chord — wind over fins + water over lips + current in wires + fire in boilers — settles into the bone. They speak little, and when they do they lean forehead to forehead almost, or touch. A hand on an elbow means *come*. Two fingers on a pipe means *listen, this one is off by a hair*. At night in the barracks — long stone rooms above the dynamos where the floor is warm through your pallet — they talk with lights, with handlamps blinked from window to window across the mire, one unchanging note answered by the same note. No gossip travels that way. Only: *still running. still running. still running.*

They eat what the plateau can spare without being interrupted. There is no mouth here but theirs, and they have learned to be a small mouth. Mire-stew: sedge root, cotton-grass flour, vat-protein from the pulp works, a spoon of black moss-oil that tastes of iron and smoke. The same stew, in the same tin bowls, in every ward kitchen you will ever enter. They do not get hungrier or less hungry. They eat at the bell because the bell rings because the shift changes because the bell rings. Their teeth are ground flat early from grit in the flour. Their guts, the dispensary man told me, tapping his own broad belly, have slowed to the speed of the place. "A millimeter a year on top," he said, "a millimeter below." He meant peat. He also meant us.

There are no children shouting in the squares full of settling tanks. There are children — a few, pale, solemn, ankle-deep in sphagnum from the time they can walk, learning not to step on the starry saxifrage — but they do not multiply. The numbers hold. When the trains come in, sometimes a young face gets off with a duffel that is already damp. Sometimes an old face lies down out in the green between towns and does not get up, and the moss, which swallows boulders whole without rounding them, swallows him whole without hurrying. No grave, no marker that lasts. Press your boot into that spot a year later and water rises around the tread, smelling faintly of peat and iron. The roster in the chapel-transformer station still adds to the same total.

This is not resignation. You expect sadness in them and you do not find it. What you find, if you stand long enough in a cobbled square between five-story mills with every window lit warm yellow and no one in sight but the steam breathing white into low cloud, is a patience so complete it stops looking like a human virtue and starts looking like geology.

Because they are not separate from the other things that live here.

The second thing that lives here is the green itself, and it is one thing. Walk for weeks and you will see no edge to it, no change in kind, only the same cushion moss, sphagnum, liverwort in black-green pelts, dwarf willow no taller than your thumb, lichens the color of rust and bone and sulfur climbing the weeping cliffs before the scree has settled. It is clonal beyond comprehension. A single spore-drift, billions undisturbed with no insect to carry them, settling, growing, dying into peat with no mouth to interrupt it. The peat grows a millimeter a year on top and compresses a millimeter a year below. That is its breathing. If you lie down it steams faintly around you with your heat. It is learning the shape of hot culverts and vibrating foundations and human shoulders, and it trims itself to memory. It does not need eyes. The light never really travels. It does not need seasons. Frost feathers and melts, melts and feathers, on the lee side of every pipe. It only needs wet, and cold that never finishes, and time that does not cut back.

And the third thing that lives here is the towns.

Not in them. Them.

Each cluster — mill-town, factory ward, settlement of stone barracks and steel sheds and brick stacks with streets that run straight to the edge of the block and stop at air — is an animal of a sort the low world has no word for. Rooted on new stone, drinking straight off a hard lip, exhaling thick white steam that becomes cloud that snags on the fins and falls again as drizzle a mile downwind. Blood is water in leats and penstocks. Nerves are cables on pylons marching to the horizon, singing one unchanging note. Gut is furnace and pulp press and electrolysis shed, taking in ore and peat and cut moss-brick and piling ingots and bales and glass insulators in warehouses to be inventoried and fed back in. Production without progress. Digestion without hunger.

The keepers are its flora. Its lice, its mitochondria, its white blood cells with flat spades. They move through its warm halls cutting back its green skin to the same line, listening with their teeth to its low iron drone, feeling for the hair-off rhythm that means nothing is wrong because nothing is ever wrong in a new way. When you stand far out in the absolute hollow between lights, miles of dripping green under white sky, and feel that far, constant throb in your sternum, you are feeling towns talking to each other across mire on slender viaducts, low endless trains like blood cells, lamp-blinks like slow synapses: *still running. still running.*

They will take you in. They will give you dry — drier — socks, a bowl of stew, a pallet over the dynamo where your bones buzz all night. They will not ask where you came from or when you are leaving, because those questions assume erosion, or history, or hunger has begun. Here rock weeps but does not crumble. Rust forms and stops. Frost forms and melts. Clouds form over the same three ridges every afternoon.

If you stay long enough you feel it in your own body — breath fogging and clearing, boots wet and never wetter, never dry, cold keeping you alert without ever pushing you into shivering. You do not get older, exactly. You get damper, slower, greener at the seams. You learn to read water level by ankle, to hear trouble as a tooth-ache before an ear-ache, to cut moss to the white line and find it back to the line next Tuesday and feel, obscurely, comforted rather than mocked.

That is what lives on the roof of the world, after the uplift, before the wearing-down: a green that eats stone-water and light and makes peat-memory; a people that eat the green's leavings and tend the iron beasts that keep the water moving; and the beasts themselves, loud and wet and lit yellow in the white-grey brightness, idling their engines with infinite patience, waiting — not hoping, not fearing, just waiting, like moss — for whatever will finally be hungry enough to eat them.
