No one lives here the way you think of living.

At first there is no one to see. That is the point. Whoever lives here has learned how to be the same color and speed as stone.

Stay.

Let your eye unlearn the plain. Up there, life is wind and distance. Down here, life is interval. A great deal of lit air, and then - a pulse.

The first who shows himself is the one who can afford to be seen because he can vanish.

He is flat on the sun-slab, belly to yesterday's warmth. A wall lizard, grey-gold, dusted with darker pepper, throat going like a small heart under his chin. He does not bask, exactly. He *negotiates*. An inch forward into sun, an inch back into the thin line of shade, all morning long. His toes splay on pocked limestone. His eye, when it turns to you, is a bright bead with a black slit that does not blink so much as click shut sideways. He has been here since before you came down and will be here after you climb out. There are three, four of him - a large male who owns the biggest slab, a female the color of honey with a torn tail regrown stubby and pale, a juvenile no longer than your thumb who lives entirely in dashes between cracks. They do not gather. Each holds a territory measured in sun-hours.

They eat what the air brings down. They husk. If you crouch by the smooth pebble you will see the neat pile of spiny pods split open and emptied, and the dust around it printed over and over with the fine handwriting of their bellies and tails. They drink nothing. They lick dew off stone at dawn, tongue flicking the underside of a ledge where the beads hang and never fall.

Below them, lower and slower, live the multitudes you have to kneel to see.

The ants first. A river, black and unhurried, a single file following the umber tide-mark around the bowl where the stone darkens with damp. They do not march like an army. They meander like water finding its own level, touching antennae, turning aside for a grain of sand as if it were a boulder. They farm the seam. Where dew wicks into lichen they harvest, where a dead bee has fallen they dismantle it joint by joint over the course of a day.

Under the stones where it is still cool to the touch, the pillbugs live rolled tight as seeds. Grey, armored, ancient. Lift - gently - and they sit unmoving in the damp hollow they've polished with their bodies, waiting for you to put the roof back. They breathe with gills that should not work in a desert, and they work because here, two-thirds of the way down in the shade of an overhang, the air still remembers the night.

And the snails. You will not see them alive unless you come at dawn after a rainless month and know to look in the deepest north-facing crease. White-toothed, tiny, no bigger than a lentil, shell coiled tight and chalk-white to throw back the sun. They graze the black lichens at a pace that makes the fig look hasty. A whole life spent on a square foot of damp stone. They are the reason for the anvils.

Because someone eats them.

She comes down from the rim without sound, although you thought nothing could enter without you hearing. The crow. Not large, but iridescent when she turns - green and violet oil on black. Wary to the point of intelligence. She lands beside the flat-topped stone in the lee of the largest block, cocks her head at you, decides you are a kind of weather, and goes to work.

She knows this station. The top is pitted with small round dents from years of striking. Around it, the empty shells, all broken at the same whorl. She picks one up, hammers, extracts, eats. Then the twig. Stripped, one end frayed by her own beak. She carries it to a crevice you cannot see into, inserts it with a surgeon's delicacy, fishes, and brings up something pale and wriggling. Wipes her beak on the stone. Leaves the tool set down neatly on the edge for tomorrow. She does not live *in* the hollow - she roosts out on the plain somewhere - but she lives *by* it, the way you live by a well. Day after day, year after year, same anvil.

Above, not living here but belonging to it, the swifts. Five, seven, eleven. They never land. At noon they lock their wings and circle the column of warm air rising out of the bowl, round and round without a beat, suspended. You hear them once, very faintly, a scream like a needle on glass, then nothing. They are here for the others - the mason bees working the fig flowers all afternoon on a single bloom, furry and amber-dusted, the blue-black wasps prospecting holes in the fluted rock for a place to paralyze a spider and lay an egg. The hollow funnels insects upward in its heat, and the swifts reap.

And rooted, which is another kind of animal here:

The fig, wedged in a crack, dwarfed and gnarled, leaves dusty but alive, roots flattened to white ropes that crawl ten feet along the seam from weep to weep. It puts on a finger-length a year. It fruits sparingly, figs no bigger than marbles, but enough for bees and wasps and ants.

Tufts of needle grass bleached almost white, each blade curled tight to hold its own shade. The brittle shrub with lacquered leaves that click faintly in the updraft, holding its water behind varnish. Cushions of moss like green felt in the deepest shade, no bigger than your thumb, that dry to brown paper by noon and swell green again by next dawn without dying. The tiny black lichens that are really the lungs of the place, wicking the dew back into stone.

None of them touch. Yards of bare warm stone between each green island. They do not compete. They wait.

That is who lives here: waiters, borrowers, hammerers. Nothing that builds, nothing that stores. A stone held for a minute and set down. A stick shaped and left on an edge. A hollowed slab that rings like ceramic when you tap it - *tong* - and then swallows the sound. A drip that hangs for an hour under the amber calcite drapery and never falls, and in ten thousand almost-falls makes a millimeter of stone.

If you stay until the western wall throws its tongue of shade across the floor, you will see them all at once in the honey light, for a minute, before they go back to being stone-colored: lizard throat pulsing, ant river flowing, bee dusted gold in a fig flower, moss darkening as condensation begins, crow's twig waiting on the anvil for morning.
