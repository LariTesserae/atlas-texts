You won't meet them at first.

That's the trick of the spacing. You can stand out on the apron in your beading coat and think the Basin is empty — just machines talking to each other — and then, an island of orange light away, a door slams, small and late through the cold air, and a figure crosses from one pool of sodium to the next and is gone.

They live thin here. Not many. Not together.

The true locals aren't people. People go up. Even the double-shifters go up eventually, up the helix with their ears popping in reverse, to sleep where it isn't raining inside. No — the people who live here are the ones who have learned how to stay damp.

You'll see it first in their gear before you see it in their faces. Everyone wears the same damp uniform that was never issued: black thermal under orange hi-vis gone brown at the cuffs, Carhartts stiff with concrete dust and grease, boots with the tread packed with slag-grit, gloves left on pipes to steam. Hoods up under hardhats. Beards and eyelashes beaded. Breath that hangs. They don't take their coats off indoors because indoors is also 4C and dripping. In the Pump House break room there's a row of lockers with the doors rusted open and inside each one another identical damp coat, so a man can change from his wet coat into his other wet coat.

There are maybe thirty of them, all told, across three shifts, and you will rarely see more than six at once. The Basin is too big for crowding. They move like animals in a very large winter pasture, from shelter to shelter.

The Pump Men live closest to the heart. Two per shift, sometimes one and a radio. They live in the throb. You can tell them because they shout without meaning to and then go quiet and listen with their whole bodies tilted — head cocked for intake icing, that rising whine that means the canal screens are furring over. Their shack is inside the brick Pump House, plywood over the blown windows, a kettle that never leaves the hot plate, a logbook with water levels in pencil, and the high-water marks on the door they touch for luck going in. They mark time not by clock but by throttle-back — that thirty-second dip at shift change when the whole building shudders and settles. They drink tea with a skin of concrete dust on it. Their hands are always chapped raw from handling chain and screen in canal water that never gets above 2C.

Then the Shed 4 keepers. Three men and a woman for a line that was meant for forty. They don't operate the press so much as chaperone it. The line — uncoiler, press, stacker, bander, conveyor — runs itself, slamming every six seconds, and they walk it, oil can and stethoscope, listening for the bearing that is about to go, watching for the misfeed that will jam two tons of steel. Between rounds they live in the control pulpit, a glass box up over the line with two space heaters and a bank of dead monitors and one living one, green phosphor, that shows only PUMPS OK / PRESS OK. They play cards with swollen cards. They have a microwave that trips the breaker if the press fires at the same time, so they have learned to time their burritos between slams. The woman — Mara — has been here eleven years and can tell by the echo off the far terraces whether the lid dampers are open or freezing shut.

Then the Walkers. Electricians, primarily, and one ice man. They live in motion, because light only pools and you have to move between pools. You see them far off, headlamps bobbing in the blue shadow between masts, high-vis flashing orange-orange-out as they pass under. They check the flood masts with their pans of dead moths, they re-lamp the fluorescents that buzz over doors, they walk the perimeter canal with a bucket and a stopwatch to measure weeps — is that seam doing more than 5 L/min? Report it. They carry scaling bars for the ice beards on the north wall, and they live by the red line: DO NOT PARK UNDER ICEFALL. The ice man, old Kolya, talks to the ice while he works, cursing it in two languages as six-foot curtains calve and shatter on the concrete. He has a scar on his cheek from a thrown shard. He wears it like seniority.

They all share the same Basin habits, which is how you know they live here and you don't. They never set anything down in a scupper. They never step over a yellow walk-line ghost without checking for forklift — the driverless one, which they call Mule, which grazes its perfect rows out in the yard day and night, beeping softly to no one, headlights sweeping empty apron. They have learned to pause their conversation when it passes, the way you'd pause for a cow. They stay in light between shifts, huddled shoulder to shoulder under a mast, stamping, waiting for relief, because the dark between is -15C with wind and the wind finds every zipper. They can hear the difference between steam-trap hiss and methane sigh near Shaft 2, where they never strike a lighter. When the three blasts come — test on Fridays — their bodies turn uphill before their minds do. Not across. Up.

If you eat with them once, in the Shed 4 pulpit or the Pump House, you'll see how pale they are under the orange. Sodium pallor. Eyes red-rimmed from iron mist and 60Hz flicker. A cough they all have, small and dry, from breathing inside-rain for years. They speak quietly, because sound arrives late here and loudness betrays distance. They speak mostly about water. Water up, water down, weep rate, canal level, pump pitch, ice. KEEP WATER MOVING isn't a rule to them, it's a prayer. They know if the pumps stopped they'd have eighteen hours. Everyone knows the number.

Around them, and more permanent than them, live the others.

The crows live highest. A mated pair, huge and shaggy, puffed against basement cold, nesting on the stack catwalk in a nest of wire ties and insulation stolen from above. They are the only commuters who like it — up the ventilation shaft into real sky for fries and back down into the haze with beakfuls of white fluff. They shout from the stacks and the sound takes a full second to come back off the terraces, so they seem to answer themselves.

The rats live lowest, along the canal edges, sleek and fat on press-line grease, running the ten-foot ring like it was built for them. They drink the black thread-water off the walls and don't die.

The carp live most hidden. In the three settling tanks by the water treatment skid — green-black, steaming faintly — the blind white shadows turn, huge now, three feet long, eyes filmed over from years without real light. Dumped as fingerlings to eat algae. They ate, they grew, they stayed. They surface only when the aerators stutter, mouths opening slow in the cold air, then sink again. No one fishes them. It would feel like eating a coworker.

The cat lives between. Feral tabby, night-shift's cat, ribs hidden under winter fluff, whiskers always beaded with condensate. She has a bowl by the Shed 4 door — tuna tin, washed by inside-rain — and she makes her rounds from warm pipe to warm transformer, sleeping on whatever is humming. She does not meow; the Basin is too loud for meowing. She headbutts shins.

And under all of them, living slowest, the green things. Black-green moss in the expansion joints, tough as felt, rimed with frost, crushed daily by boots and Mule's tires and still coming back. Leggy lambsquarter and two dandelions in the lee of the warm pipes, pale and translucent, leaning desperately toward the sodium islands. And under that one perpetually dripping flange — everyone knows the one — a hanging garden of liverwort on bare concrete, lush as a wound, drinking rust and light. Nothing planted. Everything volunteer. Everything patient.

Stand with the Walkers at shift change in the middle of the empty apron, when the press pauses and the pumps dip, and you will feel what they have become. For thirty seconds the roar drops and there are only drips — thousands of them, plink on steel, splat on concrete, tick on ice — and every head, human and crow and cat, tilts up slightly to listen. Checking pitch. Checking weather. Checking if the place is still breathing.

Then the pumps ramp, the press slams, the inside-rain veils in, and they pull their hoods tighter and go back to keeping the water moving.
