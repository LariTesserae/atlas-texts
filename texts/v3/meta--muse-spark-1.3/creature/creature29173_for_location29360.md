At first, nothing lives here.

That is what the place wants you to believe. The water between the towers is too black and too clear, and your eyes keep looking for the wrong kind of animal — something with forward eyes and a hard outline, something that hurries. There is none of that. For a long time there is only the thrumming in your chest and the snow of white motes drifting that never lands.

Then you stop swimming and let the cold hold you, and the place fills in around you.

The small ones come first because they were already there.

**The dust.** Clouds of them, each no longer than your thumbnail, pale as milk-glass, hanging in sheets twenty meters thick. Krill, almost — but not. They have no hurry and no shells worth the name. Their bodies are soft envelopes, all antennae and feathered legs, and they live by hanging in the current and clicking. Click-click-click, a hiss of static you feel more than hear. Individually they are nothing. Together they are weather. They thicken where a tower's crown has just pulsed, thinning the water of snow, and they thin where the white fish have just passed. If you put your hand out they part without fear and close behind your wrist without memory. They do not see you. They taste the pressure you leave and re-weave it.

Out of them, and feeding in them:

**The pour.** Long, eyeless white fish, as long as your forearm and thin as ribbons. They never travel alone. A school is a hundred, a thousand bodies moving as a single slow muscle around the base of a tower, always with the gyre, always the same direction the towers lean. They have no eyes because there is nothing at a distance worth seeing — twenty meters of dusk-blue and then dark. Instead their whole flank is an eye. A lateral line of silver cells runs nose to tail, and when they turn, all at once, flank-on to the faint down-light, they flash. It is the brightest thing here: a sudden sheet of moonlight rolling around a stalk. They are saying: *here, here, here.* To each other. To the tower.

They do not bite. Their mouths are soft funnels, toothless, made for sipping dust and silt and exhaling fertilizer. They trail a faint ammonia-sweet plume behind them as they circle, and where they circle the mat below greens and thickens almost imperceptibly. They are manure with a will. They are tended in turn — when a fish slows, peels off from the pour, sick or old, it does not sink and rot. Crabs find it before it touches the mat and it is unmade, gently, mouth to soft mouth, and carried into the tower walls to be regrown as wall.

Below them, on the floor that is not floor:

**The haulers.** The newts. Slow as thought. As big as canoes, six-limbed, belly-dragging, the color of the mat itself until they move. You will not see one until you are almost on top of it, and then you will think it *is* a swell in the mat. Broad flat heads with nostril slits that flare, lidless dim eyes that do not really see, mouths like soft hoes. They haul themselves hand-over-hand over the felt — sponge giving faintly under their weight and springing back — grazing as they go.

They do not tear. They rasp. Their tongues are wide pads of fine, ever-shedding grit, and they lick the mat clean of overgrowth, of silt that the mat has not yet drunk, of dead dust that has fallen. Behind them they leave a brighter trail, olive to lime, a mown path that closes over within minutes as the felt surges back. They prune the world flat. Their shit is a soft green rope that never reaches the water — smaller mouths are already there to take it. Their bellies are calloused pale from centuries of this contact; their backs are gardens. Moss grows on them, small sponge colonies, sometimes a whole crab family riding, picking parasites from their neck folds while they walk.

They breathe rarely. Every long while a newt will stop, lift its great blunt snout a meter off the mat, and exhale — a single huge silver bubble that wobbles up into the black and is gone. Inhale. Sink back down. Continue.

Above them, in the grown rooms:

**The keepers.** This is where life concentrates and becomes deliberate.

The towers are not empty stalks. They are tenements. In every flared cup big enough to sleep in, in every latticed fork where walk-fibers have calloused from a million passings, something has nested and stayed to tend.

The eels first, because you hear them before you see them. Coiled, two meters long when uncurled, bodies the milky blue of deep ice, throats like lanterns. The throat sac is translucent and pulses with its own faint light — not bright, just a shade warmer than the dusk-blue, enough to gather dust toward it. They hang in loops over door-holes, throats out, sieving and glowing and singing. Their song is a long descending moan that goes on for a minute and comes back off a tower a kilometer away, changed. It is how they know where the other towers are. It is how they know if a crown is clogged or a walkway has thinned. Males and females and neithers answer each other in rounds that never quite end. When you swim past a cup, the eel inside will stop pulsing, draw its throat in, and watch you by vibration until you are gone. Then, slowly, the light comes back.

Beside them, the bell-animals. Colonies, not individuals — hundreds of small translucent bells on a shared stalk, each with a ring of hair-fine tentacles, all fused at the base into the tower wall itself. They are doors that are also stomachs that are also alarms. Touch the wall and the nearest bells snap shut in a wave, *whup whup whup* down the corridor. Filter-feed for an hour, then all together flush. Their waste feeds the wall they are part of.

And the stitchers. The crabs. If anything here has hands, it is them. Small — your palm would hold two — with backs so overgrown with living moss they look like walking lumps of mat. They are everywhere once you learn to look: upside down under causeways, sideways in leaf-scars, clustered around a wound-lip where a branch was bitten back. Their claws are not claws. They are soft, blunt nippers, rounded by design and by a lifetime of nipping only living tissue. They nip parasites off eels. They nip algae out of bell crowns. They coax two young branches together and hold them, for days if necessary, until the sap fuses and a new wall is born with a hole left politely in the middle for a door. They eat what they prune. They sleep piled together in warm pits where the tower's muscle gives off heat, legs twitching in time to the great thump.

Because under all of it:

**The thump.** Something large and slow beats water rhythmically out past seeing, *thump … thump … thump*, never hurrying, never stopping. You will never see it. No one here has seen it. The newts pause when it passes. The eels tune their songs to the gap between beats. It may be one animal, very large, circling the summit basin with the gyre, so large it mistakes towers for reeds. It may be several. It may be the summit itself — the drowned mountain breathing through some hollow in the bone-stone, the mat inhaling and exhaling once a minute. The keepers do not chase it. They set their work by it the way you would set your work by a heart.

And finally, what actually lives here, larger than any of these:

The agreement.

No single species made the towers. The towers are a kind of kelp-reed-muscle that learned, an age ago, to grow hollow and forked, and then everything else taught it to grow into rooms, and it listened because rooms bring fertilizer and pruning and cleaning. No single species keeps the mat. The mat is a felt of a hundred mosses and sponges and root-hairs that learned to drink silt and heal under a hand, and then the newts and fish taught it to stay flat, and it stayed because flat brings light to all.

They are all soft. That is the rule written in their bodies. No tooth stays sharp; the water rounds it. No claw stays hard; the work softens it. No edge, no shell, no stone, no dead sharp thing is permitted for long — it would cut the wall, tear the mat, break the sieve. Even their young are born soft, live, and stay soft. Death is soft too: there are no skeletons here, no white scatter of bone. Everything that dies is eaten before it can become object, re-woven before it can become rot, drunk by the mat or stitched into a wall. To live here is to agree to be unmade gently and remade nearby.

They do not know up. Up is blacker water that gets colder forever. They do not know down. Down is myth — stone under felt under life, and below that, impossibly far, clouds they will never touch. They do not know morning; the dusk-blue has never brightened and they have no word for brightening. They know current, which always loops; they know touch, which always heals; they know sound, which always returns.

Swim between towers long enough and you become part of it whether you want to or not. Your kick joins the thrumming. Your breath joins the clicking. A crab will come to inspect your fin for parasites and, finding none, nip politely at your dead skin. An eel will light its throat a little warmer as you pass, tasting you. The mat below will push back, almost imperceptibly, against your shadow.

High, drowned, tended, dark, and awake. Not a place with animals in it. A place that is animals, keeping itself.
