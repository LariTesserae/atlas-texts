They do not live there the way you live somewhere.

At first you think they are trapped. That is the wrong shape for them. Washed in, fallen in, crowded by accident under a lifted stone — a refugee pavement, shoulder to shoulder because there is nowhere else to go.

Then you look longer, until the brightness stops hurting, and you think, no, not trapped. Held. They are holding each other up.

Who lives in the white pocket is not a crowd. It is a lining. A living skin grown over fresh rock.

Start with the most numerous, because they make the floor:

The springtails. Grey-white as ash, as stone-flour, each no longer than an eyelash. They are furred — you can see it if you stop breathing and let your eye settle — a pile of minute hairs that holds a bead of damp off the body and makes them glow in the amplified light. They are packed so tight their flanks touch, and that touch is structural. Remove one and the ones either side would tip. They shiver. That is the pavement shivering. Each one has its tail-spring, the furcula, folded under and tensed. In any other place, on any other day, that tension would release and they would be gone in a grey puff, a pop of jumping ash. Here they tense and do not jump. They hold the jump inside the body as a vibration. Ten thousand un-jumped jumps make the high dry rasp you feel in your teeth. They are the bulk and the hum.

Between them, moving because someone has to move slowly or the whole thing would lock:

The amber mites. Round-backed, varnished, the color of pine sap held to light. Their legs are deliberate to the point of ceremony, lifting and placing on crystal points, stepping over springtail backs, between woodlouse plates. They do not hurry because hurry would mean displacement and there is no displacement to spare. They vibrate in place, mouthparts working at nothing, or at everything — a film of fungus, a spore, a bead of that animal-sweet damp. They are the stewards. Where they have passed, the crystal is polished a fraction brighter.

And the blind ones, the heavy ones:

The woodlice. Pale to the point of translucence, eyeless — what would eyes be for in a place that is all light and no sight? — curled and uncurled, packed like beans in a jar. Their plates overlap. Their antennae overlap. A long pale antenna from one lies across the back of another and neither moves it. They breathe with belly-gills that need exactly this air: cool, beaded, never wet and never dry. One lifts a leg. You watch. It does not put it down. It holds it lifted, and the pressure of the bodies around it holds it up. They are the weight that keeps the pavement from floating off on sound.

Under and through them, the dark punctuation:

The beetle larvae. Cave-black, segmented, translucent only at the joints where the new cuticle shows honey-colored when they bend. They should be predators, they look like predators, but there is no room to lunge. So they lie wedged, pulsing faintly, mandibles scraping crystal flour in a slow circle that never deepens. The scraping is part of the roar. And with them, thread worms, nematodes, knotted in the damp flour at the bottom, clear as wet nylon, tying and untying a knot so slowly you cannot see it happen, only that the knot is in a different place if you look away and look back an hour later, though time does not pass here. They drink the halo.

Riding on top because there is no top:

The pseudoscorpions. No bigger than sesame seeds, amber-clawed, tailless. They ride. There is nowhere else to stand, so they stand on other bodies — on a woodlouse plate, on a larval segment — claws raised, not in threat but in balance, like sailors in a high sea holding on. They are the only ones who look outwards. Their pedipalps open and close on the vibration itself, sipping the stridulation.

And inside the water that is not water:

In each hanging droplet under the slab, each fat trembling miniature sun that never falls, if you bring your eye impossibly close until the white fills you, there is a wheel turning. Rotifers. Coronets of cilia beating in a perfect circle, drawing the beaded damp into the mouth. They turn and turn and do not go anywhere because the droplet does not fall and does not shrink. They are the smallest citizens and the most constant; their turning is the clock that has stopped.

That is the animal of the place. But it is not only animal.

The plants understand the rules and obey them more strictly. Three or four bleached root hairs stab down through the fissure from the grass above that has no idea what it has fed. Each is tipped with a single clear exudate drop that never falls. They do not grow longer. They dangle and leak sweetness forever without emptying. Around them, in the brightest part of the wall where the light leans hardest, two flakes of powder-lichen, sage-green, dusted on like breath. They do not spread. In one corner, a thread of moss protonema like green frost, a filament branching and branching into itself. It does not become moss. They hold. They are the anchors, the green memory that above is still there.

What they do together — because they do something — is maintain.

They secrete and accrete. A trio of mud turrets no taller than a grain of rice stands near the dampest hollow, chimneys built grain by grain from stone flour and gut-glue by some larva that is either still inside or long gone and still present in the shape. The rims are polished by endless circling. Across a narrow span, a bridge of silk, a dozen lines guyed wall to wall, beaded with condensation and thrumming faintly with the general noise. No one walks over it; the springtails walk under it because there is no room above. It is not for crossing. It is for tuning. A hollowed seed husk, dragged down who knows how, licked clean inside, is used as a drum; a beetle sits wedged in it, pulsing, and the husk amplifies him into the choir. Shed skins stand upright in the crowd, perfectly intact, empty houses still occupied by the shape of air, kept upright by pressure. Around the dampest hollow, frass pellets are stacked with deliberate care into a low wall. Not to keep water in or out — water does neither — but to mark that this damp is the center and will remain the center.

Listen and you understand their work: they make the sound that makes the place. The high rasp of chitin on chitin, leg on crystal, mandible on flour, plus the glassy ticking of stridulation that never pauses for breath, plus the ping of the chamber feeding a little of its echo back into itself. They do not sing to communicate. They sing to sustain. The sound presses up and holds the dust motes motionless in the light shaft, holds the beads from falling, holds the jump in the springtail's tail.

They are abundant beyond what this thimble should feed because they do not eat in the way outside eats. They pass the same film back and forth. Moisture coats and never saturates. Light enters and never leaves. Bodies press and never thin.

If you have knelt long enough for your knees to ache, you stop seeing individuals. You see what lives there as one thing with many surfaces: a shivering, ticking, blind, bright, cool animal that has decided — or was decided for it by white rock and trapped light — to be exactly this crowded and exactly this loud and exactly this still, forever.

Lower the slab and you do not kill it. You only close the lid on the music box. It goes on holding its breath under the field, blinding white a finger's depth below the earth, waiting without waiting for the next lift.
