No one lives here the way living is meant on the surface. No one settles. No one stays in the sense of building a stay. What lives here persists — thinned out, slowed down, spread so far apart that to meet one is an event.

First, if you learn how to look, the ones who are almost not animals.

In the lee of the nodules, where the contour current eddies and drops its load, there are veils. Dirty-white, translucent, no thicker than mucus, stretched low over the clay between stones. Bacterial mat. Not a thing so much as a condition — millions of cells holding together where a breath of methane sighs up from the old crust below. They do not move. They do not hunt. They oxidize, they divide, they tear when the benthic storm comes and then they skin over again. They are the closest thing this valley has to pasture, and everything that eats knows where they are.

Standing up out of that pasture, sparse as posts in a winter field: the stalked sponges. Gray, fibrous, a few centimeters tall, no thicker than a reed. They do not sway, because there is nothing quick enough here to sway. They lean, permanently, a degree or two down-current, their stalks furred with the silt that never settles. At their crown a small crown of pores, open to the soup. They live by sieving what falls — snow, shit, skin — and they live a very long time doing it. A century for a growth you could cover with your thumb. When a storm flattens them they do not break. They bend and stay bent.

Near them, rarer: the crinoid. If you catch it in the half-second flash of something else, you will think it is a plant. It is not. It is a feathered mouth on a stalk, five arms branched into ten, into twenty, each fringed with pinnules like down. Most of the time it is furled tight into a fist against the cold. When the current is right — not too fast, not too laden — it opens. It lifts those feathers into the flow and lets the water bring it the dead. Then it folds again. Hours open, days closed. That is its whole economy.

These are the still ones. They hold ground they cannot defend. The nodules are their bedrock, their anchor. Without that black-brown pavement they would sink into clay and smother.

Then the waiters.

The tripod fish. You will not see it until you are almost on it, because it wants to be a shadow. A small, dark body, not much longer than your hand, held a half-meter above the nodules on three impossibly long, stiff fins — two from the belly, one from the tail — like a surveyor's tripod left on the plain. It faces into the current and does not move. It cannot afford to. Its eyes are vestigial, filmed over. It hunts with its lateral line, feeling pressure change when a copepod or a bit of carrion tumbles past in the dark. When something touches, it darts — one quick snap — and then resumes standing. Days in one spot. Weeks. Its metabolism is so low its heart seems to beat only when it remembers to.

Above them, occasionally, the glider.

The rattail. Grenadier. A meter of muscle tapering to a whip, big head, big black eyes that gather what little bioluminescence there is and make something of it. It moves just above the pavement, so slow you think at first it is drifting. It is not drifting. It is budgeting. Every undulation costs what took a month of snow to earn. It noses between nodules, stirs silt with its snout, sucks in polychaetes, small crustaceans, the fallout of death from above. It pauses long between strokes. It listens. You will hear nothing, but it will turn, all at once, toward a vibration you cannot feel, and go to it. Alone. There is never another rattail with it. There is not enough food in a kilometer to share.

And on the ground itself: the bulldozer.

A blind white isopod, the size of your spread hand, armored in overlapping plates like a pillbug grown monstrous with cold and time. No eyes at all, only pale, unpigmented cuticle and long antennae that sweep the snow-dust ahead of it. It plows forward on stubby legs, head down, pushing a little bow-wave of silt, eating as it goes — swallowing mud for the organics in it, digesting what can be digested and passing the rest. Behind it a furrow, already filling with fresh snow. It does not hurry and it does not hide. Nothing here is fast enough to catch it, and nothing is hungry enough to try very often.

But the ones you will remember are the crabs.

Small spider crabs, bone-pale, legs like needles, bodies no bigger than a walnut. Invisible until they twitch. They are the only ones here who reach outside their own bodies.

One will wedge itself between two nodules, jam a broken clam shell — dead for a thousand years, carried down from light — into the crack behind it like a chock, brace its back legs against it, and lean into the current to pick at mat with its front claws. Without the brace it would be tumbled. With it, it can feed for an hour.

Another finds a flake of chert, sharp where pressure split it, no larger than your fingernail. It grips it in two walking legs, awkward, trembling with effort, and draws it across the bacterial veil again and again, shearing the mat off the stone to stuff into its mouthparts. You can see the work afterward: thin parallel scratches on the nodule's rind, bright against the black manganese. When the mat is gone the crab drops the flake. It does not cache it. It does not carry it to the next mat ten meters away. It leaves it to be buried, tumbled, lost in the next storm. Next time, it will find another.

That is as close as this valley gets to making. No burrow that lasts, no nest, no wall, no midden. Only a hand extended by a stone for a minute, and then released.

Above all of them, in the water that is never empty, the flashers. You will never see their bodies, only their interruptions. A comb jelly tearing open as it pulses — rows of cilia refracting its own blue-green chemistry into running sparks, there and gone. A fish you will never approach, blinking a single photophore on its belly, a cold dot that hangs for a second and extinguishes. They are not residents of the floor. They are passers-through, down from the black above, down from the black below. They light, they vanish.

That is who lives here. No schools, no herds, no forests, no reefs. A veil that eats gas. A reed that sieves snow. A feather that opens and closes. A fish on stilts that waits. A fish with a rat-tail that budgets movement. A blind pill that eats mud. A pale crab that borrows a knife and puts it down.

Each one alone in its kilometer. Each one tuned so low, so slow, so cold that you could mistake it for part of the pavement until it moves.
