Who lives here first looks like everyone, everywhere.

Then you stay a day and the blur resolves, and you see they are not like lowland people at all. They are Cumbre people. They have become the roof.

You hear them before you see them clearly — that held chord is made of them. They do not hurry. No one in Cumbre hurries, because no one can. The air is thin and warm, a strange combination that makes the chest work double. Newcomers pant and get headaches and sit down on the steps with their heads between their knees. The old ones just move slow.

They are built for it. Barrel-chested, even the children, with a way of breathing you can see: a deep sip in the morning when the fog is thick, a shallow sip at noon when the sun hammers. Broad feet for stairs. Hands that are all knuckle and callus. Skin sun-cured to a deep brown-red, creased early around the eyes, because here the light does not filter, it hits. Everyone squints. Old women wear hats woven from stripped cable and bean vine. Old men wear the rust-stain on their shirts like a second trade-mark. Their eyes water in the glare off whitewash and wet leaf, and they wipe them without stopping what they are doing, which is always tending.

Because no one here *builds*. That is the first thing you unlearn about people. A Cumbreño does not make new things. He finds a soft brick and replasters it. She finds a pitted concrete cistern and lines it with lime. They rewire, re-tie, re-hang, re-route. A child of six can patch a fog-net with a bone needle. A child of ten can tell by the *plip-plip* on tin whether a gutter is feeding or spilling. A teenager can carry two buckets of manufactured soil — human compost, leaf litter, chicken shit, ground eggshell — up seven flights without spilling, without breathing hard. Wasting is obscenity. You see a woman chase a single tomato seed rolling toward a drain and catch it like a jewel.

They dress in layers that come off and on ten times a day: shawl for fog-dawn, bare arms for brutal noon, oilskin for the fifteen-minute hammer shower off the eastern terraces, shawl again when steam rises off hot tile. Pockets everywhere for string, for cuttings, for a spare washer. Nothing is purely ornamental, but everything has become ornament anyway: the marigolds fenced around a humming electrical cabinet to keep goats off, the chilies in a cut engine block, the shrine wedged between market stall and machine shop where someone leaves a mango for the saint of pumps.

They live stacked. A family is not three people in a flat, it is twelve people across three levels — grandmother on the roof-thicket where she commands beans and bees, sons in the mid-level loom shed, daughters tending the courtyard tank green with algae, cousins in the damp under-level feeding pigs on market waste and listening for beetles. At night the geckos click in the thousands in the walls and no one hears them anymore; they are the tick of the house clock. People sleep where it is warm: near transformers, above bakeries, beside compost that steams all winter. Cats have already taught them this. The cats are semi-feral and lordly and huge from chicken scraps, asleep curled on warm transformer boxes, twitching an ear at the 60-cycle hum. People step around them with respect. The cats were here first, in a sense — they found the warm spots before the engineers mapped them.

And above and between the people move the others who are almost people.

The monkeys. Small, lean, black-capped, fingers permanently sooted from engine housings and stopcocks. Generations ago they learned to pry lunchboxes. Now they have learned to turn stopcocks, to peel bananas left for them and stolen besides, to travel the sagging power lines like highways, three troops per spur, chattering. From below you see only silhouettes tightrope-walking the cables. From a roof garden you see them close: an old female sitting on a stair landing, carefully washing a stolen tomato in cistern water before eating it, while juveniles wrestle in bougainvillea. The gardeners curse them and leave out cracked corn anyway, because the monkeys eat the bean beetles and warn — a specific sharp bark — a full minute before the eastern shower hits. They know pressure change before any barometer.

Below the monkeys, the birds have divided the sky.

Pigeons, first and always. Thousands. When the shift whistle blows at the pressing plant they rise in a single decision and darken the rinsed blue. They do not nest in cliffs — there are no cliffs left. They nest in warmth: inside dead engine housings, in the hollow tin letters of old factory signs — the O of ACEITE is famous, it holds forty nests — under sagging eaves where fog-drip keeps the moss soft. Their cooing is part of the mid-tone. Their droppings are swept daily, treasured, bucketed to the roofs. Nothing wasted.

Swifts own noon. They cut the brutal light above the terraces, screaming, never landing except in chimney cracks. Small bright finches own the bean rows, hopping stem to stem, flashing yellow. Chickens own everything low: red and speckled and black hens that eat beetles and spiders and dropped rice and toads if they can catch a small one, scattering under eaves when children shriek in the rain, then emerging to drink from the still-steaming tile. Ducks own the accident ponds — that permanent shallow pond on a sagging roof where dragonflies hunt, where two white ducks paddle in circles all day as if they invented the place.

On the walls, the cold-blooded hold court. Fat lizards with orange throats do push-ups on every sunlit surface, claiming territory in a language of chin and tail. At dusk they vanish into mortar joints packed with moss like grout. At night, the geckos take over and click and click. In the damp under-levels, where cockroaches and black beetles rustle through old cable insulation, toads the size of fists sit patiently beside floor drains, throats pulsing. Spiders string between fog-nets and tomato stakes, drinking dew off their own webs at dawn. Bees — kept bees gone half-wild and wild bees gone half-kept — live in box hives stacked on stair landings, drunk all day on bougainvillea magenta and squash flower yellow, their hum joining the industrial bass until you cannot tell which is insect and which is substation.

And holding all animal life up, the green that should not be there.

There is no plant in Cumbre that grows *in* ground, because there is no ground. Every plant is an epiphyte on civilization. Moss came first and never left, packing every joint. Then the figs — arrogant, patient — rooting in gutters with fifty years of dust behind them. No one cuts them down; you route the gutter around them. You learn to read a wall by its fig: that seven-story stairwell fig holds the whole east terrace together with its roots, and everyone knows not to pick all its fruit. Leave some for parrots. Yes, parrots on smokestacks, green flashes screaming at noon.

Then the tended ones, which outnumber the wild ten to one. Beans and squash sprawling over factory parapets, their tendrils finding rust-pits to grip. Banana clumps slapping against smokestacks, leaves tattered by wind worrying the fog-nets. Tomato vines hot-smelling, chilies in pots, marigolds fenced around danger, bougainvillea spilling in cataracts off anything vertical. Grass on the upper scrub-roofs where goats with bells clank and trim what gardeners miss. The goats are small, bearded, sure-footed as lizards on tile pitch, and they know their roof and will not leave it even when the gate is open.

All of it is watered by arithmetic.

The true elders of Cumbre are not people. They are the cistern-keepers — mostly women, mostly old, who can tell by knocking on concrete how many finger-widths the water has fallen that day. They open hatches for the shower, close them against evaporation, ladle green algae-tank water for washing and save the clean fog-net drip for drinking and for seedlings. Children learn to stand in the rain with arms out, mouth open, and then to run and pull awnings. Everyone learns the four rules with milk:

Catch water before it falls.
Grow on what is made.
Keep the heat.
Tend what holds.

So what lives here? A single crowded animal, really, with many skins. Human lungs and goat bells and pigeon wings and fig roots and bee hum and engine idle, all breathing together in a high, walled warmth. A roof-thicket grandmother becomes, after forty years, indistinguishable from her roof. A rust stain becomes a map. A monkey troop becomes a maintenance crew. A city becomes a mountain that learned how to sweat, how to drip, how to hold leaves up to the hammer-sun and not wilt.
