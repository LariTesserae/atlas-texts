At first you don't see animals. You see a covering.

White on black, red on white, a crust that breathes. The stone rumor underneath hardly matters — what lives here has decided to be the surface. Layer on layer until depth becomes a kind of skin. Only when you stop trying to see the bowl and let the bowl resolve do they separate, and then they don't stop separating.

The curtain first.

They are the ones that make the water thick. Tens of thousands of shrimp, each no longer than your thumb joint, hanging where the light shafts are strongest. Bodies almost nothing — clear gut, clear legs, clear tail fanned to nothing — except two hard points that give them away: an eye like a blood bead on each side, and deeper inside, a mirrored bubble no bigger than a pinhead.

You think they are drifting. They are not. Each one holds station, beating its swimmerets so slowly you can count the strokes, one two three, adjusting against the doughnut current that wants to carry it down and out. When the current shifts they part around your body without hurrying and close a full minute after you've passed, as if you were a memory in water.

And they snap. One claw on each animal is oversized, cocked open. When it shuts it cavitates — water tearing itself apart into a flash of heat and a shockwave. Alone, nothing. Together, millions per minute, it is the factory floor you feel in your sternum. Close up you see what the sound does: the mirrored bladder flashes as the snap fires. On-off. On-off. The whole curtain strobes faintly, not synchronized, not random, a field of warning lights blinking in snow. They stun what falls from above, they taste the iron coming up from below, they speak in pressure to each other because in this white-out, eyes are useless. The red eyes aren't for seeing — they are shutters, cut to noon-bright alpine sun that still pours straight down fifteen meters to stone. Everything else they do by click-echo off sharp walls.

Below the curtain, the mouths.

Every high terrace, every ledge that gets first pass of the plume, is paved in them. White anemones packed so tight their columns deform into hexagons, shoulder to shoulder, tentacles open like slow lungs. No green in them — no symbiont leaf to green them up — they are white because there is nothing to hide. Each crown is a sticky sieve turned up into the milky fall.

Watch one. It does not hurry because it cannot afford to miss. A flake of iron-sulfur, precipitated out of the cool vent fluid, a mote of marine snow from above, lands. Tentacles fold inward over the course of half a minute, deliberate as eyelids. The mouth opens, white on white, takes it. Around its base its neighbor is doing the same, and the neighbor's neighbor, until the whole terrace inhales out of phase. They farm charge as much as food. If you could hold your finger close you would feel the hairs lift — tiny endless lightning as iron drops out of solution. They live with their lips in it all day. They can taste metal the way you taste salt.

Below the mouths, the pistons.

The white tubes look like frost at first, a half-inch thicket on the second-tier ledges. Each tube is grown, not built — calcium and mucus laid down molecule by molecule onto knives of basalt that would shred anything soft. Inside each lives a bristle-worm. You only see the crown: a plume of red gills, the only saturated red down there besides shrimp eyes.

They do not just filter. They pump. Extension, twist, retraction on a hydraulic pulse. And no ledge fires together. They are staggered, one after another after another, so the ledge ripples. It is peristalsis made visible across a hundred separate bodies. They are timing themselves to the doughnut — to that slow outward slump of plume water that rises a meter from the smokestacks, stalls, and sinks back outward. First pass goes to the anemones. Second pass, thinner, cooler, heavier with bacteria that eat sulfur and iron and breathe electricity, goes to them. Their red crowns comb it. When a shadow passes they all vanish at once with a soft hydraulic sigh, white tubes empty, and then relearn courage one by one.

And wedged under and between all of it, the gears and the dishes.

Brachiopods stack like saucers in the shade of overhangs, shell on shell, lophophores flickering, filtering what even the worms miss. And the cog-crabs — flat as coins, white and blind — live in the cracks that nothing soft dares.

Blind, here, in full sun. That tells you what kind of sun this is. Light is for timing, not seeing. In a snowstorm of larvae and molts and mineral smoke, in a bowl that rings with snapping two and three times before a sound escapes, sight is noise. They have given it up. Their carapace is a toothed disc, each marginal tooth precise. Their legs fold like jackknives to fit into splinters of quenched lava. When two meet on a ledge they don't fight for the millimeter of space — there is no space to fight for, crowding is mandatory — they mesh. Edge to edge, tooth to tooth, they turn a quarter-turn against each other and separate, exchanging film, exchanging charge orientation, testing. A greeting made of gears.

The third kind of crab — or the same kind older — does the polishing. You will see one if you stay still long enough for the curtain to forget you. White, eyeless, mouthparts working for an hour on a patch of black glass no bigger than a coin until it shines like a gauge. He is not eating the rock. He is farming the skin on it: iron-oxidizing bacteria that live off the shimmer, that lay themselves down as rust. He licks the battery clean to keep it running, and the polished patch becomes a little electrode, a reference point in the electric field the whole bowl navigates by. Touch is their map. To live here is to live touching.

None of them leave. That is the other rule the rim teaches. Larvae rise in the plume, sink outward, try to ride the surge out through the notches, and are pushed back by incoming snow and caught again by the doughnut. Eggs, molts, dead swimmerets — all recirculated. The bowl keeps its own children because the mountain falls away outside into dark empty water and there is nothing out there for a millimeter-scale mouth that needs both sun-snow from above and stone-milk from below. So generations stack. Anemone on anemone scar. Tube on tube. Dish on dish. To get room, something has to die. They wait patiently, pulsing, clicking, pistoning, meshing, in thick cool water that tastes faintly of blood and batteries.

No builder. No plan. The mountain erupted yesterday in geologic time and cracked open iron and sulfur into sunlit shallows, and life poured in and immediately fell into shift-work without a foreman. Conveyor plume. Piston line. Forklift crabs scuttling sideways, so slowly, to clean fallout. Strobe shrimp. It looks industrial because efficiency looks industrial when you crowd it tight enough and slow time down enough to see it.

Stay until your eyes stop hunting for green or for speed. Then you see them as they are: not fast, not free, not separate, but exquisitely tuned to stay.
