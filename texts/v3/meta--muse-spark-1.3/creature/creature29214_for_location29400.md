At first you think no one lives here.

Not really. Not in the way a town should be lived in. You stand on Foundry Row at noon and shout into the roar and see no faces in the windows, no crowd at the crossings, only distance between brick masses and steam standing still and a dog asleep under a pipe bridge who will not lift his head. It feels maintained but not inhabited, like a machine left running after the shift.

Then you learn to see at valley speed.

They move slowly, so you miss them. Everyone here moves slowly — not from age, not from idleness, but from heat and weight and long practice. The air is bathwater. To hurry in it is to drown standing up. So they conserve. A pipe-walker crosses a trestle a quarter mile off with that unhurried, sure-footed shuffle, rubber boots to the knee, oilcloth apron dark with wet, one hand always on the rail. A woman with a barrow of lime-chip works her way along the narrowed alley by the south seep, stops, wipes her neck, goes on. Two figures on the far cinder flat stand talking — or rather, standing — for a long time between utterances. You thought they were posts.

They are all damp. That is the second truth, after slow. Skin here never quite dries. Condensation finds them as it finds everything: beads in hair, beads on forearms, a constant film under the collar. The old hands are pruned and softened at the fingertips from a lifetime of stone-drip and sluice water, the knuckles furred white at the joints where lime dust has worked into the creases and never left. Teeth go yellow-brown early from the mineral. Eyes are half-lidded, not sleepy, just narrowed against glare off puddle and travertine, the way the egrets in the settling ponds narrow theirs. Children are pink-cheeked and perpetually flushed, hair lank and stuck to the skull by ten in the morning.

You hear them before you understand them. Or rather, you feel them trying to be heard.

No one shouts for long. It’s useless. You can shout into an ear on Foundry Row and still be shouted over. So they stopped trying generations ago. Everyone over forty is half-deaf or wholly deaf in one ear, and they wear it plainly: felt plugs on cords around the neck, one plug in while working under the flumes, both plugs in at table. Their voices, when they do use them, are either too loud and flat, the deaf loud, or very soft, meant only for the tiled washrooms.

Because the washrooms are where speech lives. Every mill, every tenement, every bathhouse has one — white tile floor to ceiling, a drain in the middle, low benches, stone-drip showers running warm as blood — and they are the only quietish rooms in the valley. The roar comes through the walls as a distant surf instead of a jaw-bone buzz. People linger there long after they are clean. Old men sit in steam and talk in low, ordinary tones about valves and grandchildren. Mothers wash toddlers and actually sing, thin songs you can only hear with tile around you. Lovers meet there. The council, it’s said, makes its real decisions not in the council hall with its high, echoing, useless chamber, but in the bathhouse annex next door, sitting on wet benches in towels, passing notes on greasy paper that won’t disintegrate.

Outside, language is hands and eyes and vibration.

They lip-read with uncanny precision. They gesture in a clipped, town-made sign — not a formal language, but a working one grown up around machines: palm flat for *stop*, two fingers walking for *go check the upper weir*, fist tapped twice on the sternum for *vent coming*, thumb jerked at the cliffs for *seep rising*. Children learn it before they learn letters. Lovers have extra signs.

And they listen with their feet. Through the soles of their boots comes the true news of the town: the deep tremor of turbines turning in rock, the change in pitch when a penstock is loading, the faint stutter that means a flume is choking on sedge a mile upstream. A maintenance man will stop mid-step, frown down at the ground, and turn without a word toward trouble you cannot hear. Strangers think it’s instinct. It’s just that when your ears give up and invent that high silver ringing to cover the roar, you start paying attention to what your bones know.

Time them and you find they are timed to the works, not to clocks. The pressure-relief valves at the upper penstocks vent every eleven minutes — *kreee* peeling out of the white noise and dissolving back. No one sets a watch by it anymore because everyone’s body sets itself by it. *Vent, work. Vent, rest. Vent, wipe your face.* The bathhouse empties and fills on it. The lime-scraping crews pause, tilt their heads just slightly, resume. It is the town bell, and it rings in a frequency only they can stand.

Work is not production anymore. It is tending.

No one will tell you what the smelter smelts now, or where the dyed slurry goes, or who buys the power that sings in the wires overhead. Some of the sheds are empty except for rain coming through the roof and pigeons huddled in the lee of a ventilator, feathers vibrating faintly with the hum. But the water still has to be split and dropped, the weirs cleared, the lead pipes into the sweating rocks kept open, the rust painted over, the lime chipped back before it closes an alley entirely.

That is the labor: chipping. Scraping. Painting. Unclogging. A man with a chisel and a bucket spends a week freeing a doorway from cream-colored travertine that built up millimeter by millimeter while no one watched. A woman with a wire brush works a beam on a trestle, orange dust falling into a puddle that is already orange. Two boys in waders pull rust-weed from a gutter, its wire-curled leaves hot to touch, roots clinging to mortar. No one is building anything new. The layering in the walls says it all — river cobble, hand brick, concrete block, corrugated steel — each heavier than the last, each on the same footprint. Now they just keep the accretion from swallowing the footprint whole. They build around what can’t be saved. They left the chapel near the south seep to become more mineral than church, its doorway a glistening mouth you duck through, its pews furred white. On wet festivals they still leave candles in there, and the wax gets entombed too.

They drink the cliff. Every household has its taste for which gallery runs sweetest after morning rain. They bathe in it, wash clothes in it, let it run over their hands just to cool them though it is warm as skin. They do not ask where it comes from. The geologists came up, measured, frowned, left. The locals nodded politely and went back to their pipes. Some things you live with by not solving them.

Domestic life has thinned to fit the gaps. The five-story tenements stand mostly shuttered, families rattling around in two rooms on the second floor where damp is least. Laundry never dries; it is hung and re-hung under eaves until it is merely warm-damp and then worn damp. Cooking smells dissipate instantly in steam. Dogs are lean and expert at sleeping through everything, curled under pipe bridges where condensation drips steadily onto their backs and they don’t even twitch. Cats live in culverts, white-eyed, deaf as posts. Pigeons are the true town birds — grey as cinder, trembling always, nesting in every stilted tray and cable run.

And then the still ones. The pair of egrets in the settling pond, unnaturally white against iron-orange water, standing for an hour without moving. The children know not to throw stones. The egrets eat the only fish that can live here — small, blind-pale minnows with livers swollen from dye and heat, flickering in schools under the lime skin. The old men say when the egrets leave, go. They have not left in living memory.

Smaller still, and more numerous than people, are the lives you only see when you stop and crouch.

Orange slime in the standing pools where iron bacteria breathe metal. White fur of calcite growing visibly, invisibly, over concrete. Grey-green lichen holding to north-facing brick like a held breath. Rust-weed rooting in mortar cracks, tough enough to drink hot dye. Sedge yellowing at the tips around backwaters. Midges that breed in warm roof gutters and rise in slow columns at dusk, hanging in the vapor, eaten by nothing. Mosquito larvae wriggling in tire-rut puddles that steam at noon. Water-boatmen in the cooler seeps. Everything stunted, everything patient, everything drinking bad water and calling it enough.

If you stay long enough — and no one stays a day without that silver ringing following them into sleep — you begin to understand the oldest residents are not human at all.

Rust lives here. It advances down every unpainted beam like a tide that never goes out. Lime lives here. It entombs stairways, narrows alleys to a footpath, builds smooth aprons over abandoned things with utter indifference. Vapor lives here, rising an inch per second and hanging, hanging, holding that white pillar over the western cleft without drifting or raining. Vibration lives here, deep in rock, moving under the ground from weir to turbine to seep to wherever it goes next.

The people are, finally, just the part of that slow animal that learned to wear boots and read lips. Warm-blooded condensations. They steam faintly after rain like the streets do. Put your hand on a handrail and it comes away warm and damp; shake one of their hands and it is the same. They do not fight the roar or the weep or the accumulation. They plug their ears, watch the ground, chip at the stone, sit long in tile and steam, wave across a quarter mile of gravel and puddle to a neighbor they will not meet for a week, and let the valley breathe out and hold it.

Nothing here will change while you watch. They know that. They are in no hurry. In a hundred years they will be different, and exactly the same.
