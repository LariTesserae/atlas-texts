At first you are sure nothing lives here.

That seems right. There is no room. Stone to stone to stone, water to water to water. Nothing green can catch. Nothing with a burrow or a nest or a track. The beetle is a visitor, oaring across and gone. Even the frog — the one the color of wet stone under the platform, that blinked once — you take for a stray at first. Something that wandered in through the vapor and will wander out.

Stay longer. Let the ticking sort itself.

The stain is alive.

You saw it on the submerged cobbles, rust-orange and dull olive, a slick under your thumb. It is not spreading and not receding. That is because it is fed exactly as much as it is starved: warm water welling up from below with iron and a whisper of sulfur in it, warm water leaving into the air. In balance. It lives in the film, in the millimeter that moves without seeming to move. An iron-eater, a sulfur-breather. Thousands of millions of them, so old they have learned not to mat, not to heap. Just to stain. To keep the stones too slick and too mineral for any seed.

And where there is a pasture, however thin, there is a grazer.

She is under the platform.

What you took for a single frog is only her face.

She has fitted herself to the rule of the floor. She is low. She is broad and flattened until she is barely above the water table herself, eighteen inches long if you could stretch her, half as wide at the shoulders, tapering back under the slabs where you cannot see. Limbs folded so tight you will not find them unless you look for the crease: two small forefeet tucked to her chest, useless for walking, good for anchoring in a pan. No hind limbs at all. A siren, a mud-daughter, though there is no mud here so she has become a stone-daughter instead.

Her color is what fooled you. Damp bread. Wet chert. Quartzite grey with iron freckles. Mottled, pitted, glistening. Condensation sits on her as it sits on everything that can hold it. The walls sweat, and she sweats. Drips from the overhang tick on the platform, on the trough, on the back of her neck, and do not startle her. If you put your palm to the rock your print beads over in a minute. If you put your palm to her — if she let you — the same. The dark print would bead and heal.

Her eyes are set wide and shallow, lidless. When you crouch you see your distorted face in every pan, and the sky-smear, and the beads overhead about to drop. Her eyes are two more pans. Amber-grey, filmed, reflecting only the brighter smear where the sun must be. She blinked once from under the platform. She will not blink again while you watch. Her nictitating membrane moves slower than evaporation.

She breathes the way the hollow breathes. Not with rhythm. Her throat — pale, iron-orange in the folds where her own stain lives symbiotically — pulses once every long while. An almost inaudible sigh. Skin-breathing does most of it. The air is packed, bath-warm, mineral. Each breath is warm. She does not need to gulp. She absorbs. She sits in a saucer-pan with her belly to the upwelling, cool-warm around her, and takes water through her belly and lets water go as vapor from her back. Inflow and evaporation balance. She has been exactly this wet for a very long time.

She does not hunt. She waits, which in here is the same as hunting.

Once in a long while something blunders in through the vapor at the mouth — a beetle, a gnat, a moth stunned by the dim — and oars or flutters across a pan making V's that heal instantly. She does not turn her head. She opens. Her mouth is a straight seam nearly as wide as her head, hinged like a trough lid. It opens without splash, closes without splash. Then the pan heals. She can go months between such mouthfuls. The rest of the time she rasps the stain with a slow tongue, a millimeter a day, gardening the slick so it never thickens, never thins. That is her work. That is why the stones stay too close for seedlings.

She is very old. Female, though she has not needed a mate in years. Once, perhaps, there were two, and the three river stones on the platform are not tools but her clutch — laid up above the film on the dry seat where humans later sat, cool and hard and stone-like, one split open where something hatched and went back to water. Or perhaps the stones are only stones that hands warmed. She does not distinguish. Human warmth and spring warmth are both warmth.

She remembers hands. When people were here — not many, not now — they sat above her. The low platform of flat slabs, two courses high without mortar, is her roof. Their weight thrummed through it into her back. Their feet displaced the pans. They heated pebbles in the cold fire-spot until blackened, dropped them hissing into the log trough to make steam, ladled with the pecked sandstone bowl, knapped a chert edge to shave or to bleed or to cut withes. The broken hoop leaning on the wall. Things made to dip and carry and sit.

She did not leave. There was nowhere else with this water. She flattened flatter, let the trough fill to the brim and not spill, let the bowl sit half full of clear warm water, let ash wash to clean grey paste. She let their drips tick on her alongside the stone-drips. When they left, she took the trough for a wallow on nights — if there are nights here — when the upwelling runs a fraction warmer. She lies in it full-length, head at the adze-marked end, nostrils just breaking the skin. Full to the brim and does not spill, because she drinks as much as she displaces.

She does not ask you to leave and does not invite you to stay. Your clothes cling. Droplets gather on your forearms, on your eyelashes. A drip ticks on the back of your neck. If you stand still long enough in a pan, feeling it well cool-warm around your ankles, she will mistake your stillness for stone-ness and resume. A throat pulse. A bead running down stone that is her shoulder. The faint skin-sound.

You will not see her whole. That is the rule. Stone to stone to stone, water to water to water, and just enough room left for a few hand-made things set carefully on top — and for her, set carefully underneath, who is both.
