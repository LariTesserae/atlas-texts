At first you think it is only fish.

That is what the touch tells you, until it doesn't. A brush on the ribs — loach, you think. A whisker across the ankle — catfish. A cloud that parts and heals around your wrist — shrimp. The water is so crowded you can excuse every contact for the first ten minutes as animal.

Then one of the contacts pauses.

Fish do not pause to consider you. They turn in a single decision and are gone. This lingers. A pressure at your shoulder blade, not bumping, not nibbling, but resting. The weight of a palm-sized warmth in water that is already body-warm, so you feel it only as a slightly denser warm. Fingertips, unmistakable, counting you. Brow, cheek, shoulder. Someone hanging motionless in front of you, reading your outline with their hands because their eyes are as useless as yours, learning whether you are rope or stone or new.

They are people. Entirely people. That is the shock. Not adapted into myth, no gills, no fins. Softened people.

You only understand how softened after you have been touched a few more times and begin to touch back, as they insist you do — they take your hand and put it to them, impatient with your flailing. Their skin has gone the way of the stone. In the warm that never cools, that erases the boundary after a minute, their skin has thickened and loosened and stayed wrinkled. Permanently pruned. White, not pale the way the lowlanders are pale, but boiled-white, blood showing pink only at lips and eyelids and fingertips. The grey-white felt that coats every tile coats them too in patches — behind the ears, between the toes, in the crease of the armpit — a commensal slick they do not scrape off themselves, which the lentil-snails will sometimes rasp off a shoulder if the diver hangs still long enough to allow it. Their hair, uncut, floats out around their heads in a halo and never dries. The children especially look like dandelion clocks underwater. The hair is sense. In water where a kick two bodies away dies, where a *tok* of stone on tile is swallowed instantly, long hair feels the displacement before the skin does. A diver coming too close stirs your hair first.

Their eyes are open. That unnerves you more than if they were shut. Large, dark-adapted, unblinking in water that does not sting because it is the same salt as them, moving slowly, seeing nothing past four arm-lengths even in the grey-blue columns under the oculi. After years the eyes go milky at the edges from lime. The old ones navigate entirely by cheek and hair and tongue — they taste iron and skin and know where in the oval they are by how strong the iron is. Up near the pockets the water is sour with breathed air; down along the deep steps it is clean mineral.

Their chests are the real difference. Not bigger, slower. The heart learned the Tank's pace. They hang. That is their most common posture and it takes you an hour to recognize it as intentional and not drift: limbs folded loose, head slightly tipped back toward where air should be, toes hooked lightly in a knotted milkweed hand-line or in the groove of a sinker-stone left on a step, totally still while the shrimp close over them again. A kick is expensive. A breath is expensive. Stir the fine lime powder that never quite settles and you blind everyone's touch for an hour. Breathe too fast in the Pocket of the Broken Lintel and you sour it for the next diver. Scatter the loaches and they do not return for a day, and then there is less to eat. So they have become conservation incarnate. Minutes between movements. Minutes between breaths. You hear — or rather feel in your own sternum — the sigh of bubbles leaving a nose right beside your ear, and then nothing for a time that feels indecently long, and then the same diver surfacing without splash somewhere else.

For they do surface, but not to sky.

There is no air in the Tank except what the Tank keeps for itself. Under the six collapsed roof-slabs, under the vaulted corner where the gatehouse drowned, the stone holds shallow domes of trapped breath. From below they look silver-black, a false sky. The divers live amphibious between tile and that sky. They know every pocket by touch as you would know rooms in a house: the First Step pocket, just big enough for one chin and nose, sweetest because it is nearest an oculus; the Broken Lintel, broad and low where three can hang in a row with their mouths up, condensation beading the stone so they can mouth the fresh water off it; the Long Pocket along the north wall that runs like a lung, where you must crab sideways with your nose in a two-inch seam, breathing as you go. Children are taught the map of airs before they are taught names. You see a mother — you feel her — leading a child by the wrist cord, stopping at each knot on the hand-line, rising, tipping the small face up into the silver, hand on the small chest to keep the breathing slow, *in — wait — out — wait*, sinking again before the child has time to get greedy.

Everything they own is tied to them. A wrist cord with a bone gorge for loaches, a shell scraper for mowing felt, a green-black copper awl that does not rot, a wax-stoppered gourd with a bubble of pocket-air in it to take down to work a crevice. Toe-loops of stone to stay down without swimming. No lamps, though the niches are full of them, rows of little clay lamps unlit for centuries. Fire has no place under the surface and they have forgotten to want it.

What they do, endlessly, at accretion pace, is tend.

One is on her knees on a middle step, you can feel the displacement of her slow arm, scraping felt with a shell in short, even strokes, not to clean — never to clean — but to mow, so the snails and shrimp have new growth, so the catfish come to crop. Another two are at a fallen facing-block, working a lever a finger's width, stopping, breathing, working again, with a bladder-float tied off. A finger's width a year is progress. Another runs his palm along a wall joint, feeling for seep, for the new cold thread that means the mountain is sweating in or out. When the whole small sea rises the breadth of a hand and falls again, once a day, though no inlet was ever found, they ride it without comment. Later someone will climb half out, shivering violently in the wind you have already forgotten, and renew the charcoal mark on the gate pier over ten thousand old lines.

They eat what the Tank can spare and they spare themselves to it. Loach, occasional catfish taken with a gorge held motionless for an hour, handfuls of shrimp strained through hair, crab-claw when a crab is dying anyway. Mostly they are fed elsewhere — by those who climb, by the old ones on the lower slopes who can no longer hold still that long and sit wrapped in wool by the parapet, handing down barley cakes and taking up gourds of warm mineral water. The true residents eat little. Their bodies have learned to run warm and low, like the Tank itself. And in return they feed the Tank constantly: skin, mucus, hair, breath-condensate, the dead. No body is carried down the mountain. A dead diver is laid on the lowest tile they can reach, unweighted, and by morning the crabs and eels and felt have begun the work. The water tastes faintly of iron and skin because it is iron and skin, recirculated for ten thousand years.

The animals are not their pets or their game. They are co-tenants, layered. Snails underneath the ceiling slabs rasping; crabs shoulder to shoulder in the solution pockets clicking very softly when you loom; shrimp in clouds; salamanders hanging white with fern-gills in the water column, the only things more still than the people; catfish working the steps with their barbels right alongside human fingers; blind loaches schooling through and around legs as if legs were pillars. The people do not shoo them. They move *with* the school's decision. When the loaches turn, the diver nearest turns too, to avoid splitting them.

If you stay long enough to stop trying to see, your up inverts. Intellectually you are on a summit with three thousand feet of cold air and scree below you. Bodily you are in a warm, dark, bounded room where down is tile that sags like a hammock under your feet and up is stone that gives back breath, where the horizon is an arm's reach, where you are one more large, slow animal suspended among small, quick and small, still ones, all patient, all tending and being tended by the felt.

When you finally hook your toes out and climb, streaming, into wind that hurts your teeth, they do not follow to watch. You feel them settle behind you — a soft displacement here, a sigh of bubbles there, a shell resuming its rasp. The black glass closes over your entry within seconds.

Put your hand back in. It is bath-warm to the brim. Down there, in perfect night, they are still hanging, palm on stone, nose up to silver, brushing past each other without sound, keeping the room full of rain at the top of the world from ever emptying.
