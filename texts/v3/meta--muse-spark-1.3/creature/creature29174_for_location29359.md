At first you think it is empty.

That is because you are looking for people in the wrong places — for faces in doorways, for figures in streets. Down here there are no streets to stand in, no clear doorways. There is only the green and the steam and the lamp-glare on wet brick, and the roar that swallows your own footsteps. Then the green moves, and it is a shoulder. Then the steam parts, and there is a hand on a valve.

They do not emerge. They seep out, the way the springs seep out of the limestone.

They are low and broad and wet, all of them. You notice that before you notice anything else. Even the tall ones stoop. You have to stoop down here — under the sag of cable and vine and washing line and hose, under the catwalks furred with grass, through lanes where taro leaves big as doors slap your cheeks. After a generation or two everyone learns the pit-crouch. Knees bent, head forward, shoulders rounded to shed water. Their spines have settled into it the way the terraces have settled into their angle of rest.

Their skin never dries. That is the second thing. There are no dry surfaces below the rim, and skin is no exception. Foreheads shine amber under the sodium lamps, forearms shine, throats shine. Young girls carry that sheen like polish; old men carry it like grease. Their fingers are permanently pruned, whorled white at the tips, split at the cuticles from always being wet. Their feet — most go bare, or in cut-down wellies slashed for ventilation — are splayed wide, toed like frogs for grip on slimed brick. Black water stands between their toes. They do not seem to mind.

They dress for rot. No wool, no cotton that will hold and sour — or if they wear it, it is already sour, stiff with old sweat and lime and never fully washed because nothing ever fully dries. Mostly oilskin cut to vests, rubber aprons, shorts, canvas trousers gone black at the cuffs and green at the knees. Patches on patches. Everything strapped close so leaves cannot snag it. A lot of bare chest, male and female, old and young, glistening, stippled with heat rash and small round scars where boils have been lanced — the pit-pox, they call it, from the lime water. Hair is kept short or shaved or oiled flat and tied back hard, because long loose hair mildews in a day. Many of the pump-men are bald by thirty, not from age but from fungus, pink scalp shining through.

Their eyes are pit eyes. Accustomed to that green underwater dim, pupils wide even in lamplight. At noon, when the shaft comes straight down onto the sump, they squint and turn their faces away, hold up blackened hands. Up on the rim, in the flat fields, they would be blind for an hour. Down here they can see a clogged hairline crack in a pipe by the way the water beads, can see which fern frond to cut to let a lamp breathe without killing the root that is holding the wall behind it.

They are deaf, all of them, a little. You have to be. The three sump pumps in the central mill never stop, and you feel them in your sternum before you hear them. Conversation down here is shouting, even between lovers, even to children. The old ones cup their ears and read lips. The young ones have developed a shorthand of hand slaps on iron — two hits means *clear the grate*, three means *steam up*, a flat palm rubbed in a circle means *cut back* — because you can feel it through pipe and wall when you cannot hear. At night they sleep in the roar the way you would sleep in a womb-noise. The ones who go up for hospital or army service say the silence up there keeps them awake, panics them. Silence means the pumps have stopped. Silence means drowning.

This is what they do, all day, every day, and what their grandparents did: the two jobs.

The first is water. Someone is always walking the lanes with a hooked pole, fishing leaves and yam skins and plastic out of the little canals, combing the central grates clear twice a day, morning and evening, kneeling in black water up to the wrists while it tugs at their arms. Someone is always listening to pipe — ear pressed to sweating iron, for the tick that means a joint is cooling wrong, for the hiss that means a weep. The pump crew in the mill house work in shifts, stripped to the waist in the boiler heat, oiled to the elbow, deafest of all, tending belts that slap, bearings that must be greased hourly or seize in the damp. They are the heart-keepers. If they stop for a day, the cellars drown. Everyone knows it, so everyone brings them tea, yams, rice from the sump paddies, without being asked.

The second is green. Someone is always cutting. Not clearing — never clearing. You learn that fast or you do not stay. A woman on a second-story catwalk with secateurs, thinning philodendron off a window so the amber lamplight inside can breathe, but leaving the mat that holds the soil that holds the wall. A boy with a machete, no taller than the taro he is thinning, stalks thick as wrists falling with a wet crunch, feeding them straight to the compost heap that is also warming the house wall behind it. An old man on his knees at his own threshold every morning with the flat spade kept by the door, scraping the night’s fungus off his plank — white sheets of it, soft as bread — and tossing it to the ducks that are not ducks, because there are no ducks, because *he* is the duck, *she* is the chicken scratching for grubs in moss, *they* are the rats combing thatch, the pigeons picking gutters. With no birds, no beasts, people have taken all the small animal jobs into their own bodies. Children go barefoot up drainpipes to dislodge fern clumps. Girls with long nimble fingers go into the caged lamps to pick out moths that have burned there — no, there are no moths either, not anymore — to pick out dead leaf that would smoke.

They cut, but they also keep. That warehouse wall on the north side, ten feet thick of root and netting — there is a family whose job for four generations has been just that wall. They know where each fig root goes, which ones are allowed to split the stair because they also pin it. They will show you, shouting over the fan, with pride, where the maidenhair is thickest this year. Ivy holds the bricks together more than mortar does now, and everyone will tell you which ivy, on which cottage, is load-bearing. You do not cut load-bearing ivy.

They are slow. That is the rule of the bowl, and it is in them. Belts blur and water falls and fans beat, frantic on top, but underneath they move at terrace speed. A root as thick as your thumb that you could have cut last spring with secateurs is this autumn a beam you need a saw for — and they let it become a beam if it is in the right place. They rebuild the same stair their grandfather rebuilt, re-hang the same pipe, re-clear the same grate, and they do not call it futile. Slump a finger’s width a year, shore with new brick that will itself be green by winter. That is the calendar.

Children grow up fast in the wet heat and then stop hurrying. Toddlers are naked and muddy to the waist, playing with oil drums and yam piles within arm’s reach of green that is already reaching back for them. By six they can strip a vine, clear a screen, find dry kindling under an awning that never dries. By ten they work half-shifts. There is no schoolhouse separate from work — the lanes are the school, the mill is the school. They shout to each other over the roar, laugh easily, touch constantly, shoulder to shoulder because there is no room between presences to do otherwise. Privacy is a curtain of banana leaf. A quarrel is over in an hour because you will meet again at the sump at dusk.

Old ones sit highest, where they can. Up on the lower terraces where the air is a fraction less thick, on stools set in garden beds that are also foundations, shelling beans trained up lamp posts, minding rice in the lowest paddies by the sump. Their lungs are bad — years of boiler sulfur and lime steam and spore. They cough soft and wet and spit green into the canals. Their joints are swollen with rheumatism. They are honored, not pitied. They remember which spring opened in which drought year, which wall failed in ’68, how to read condensation running leaf-tip to leaf-tip to tell if rain is coming over the rim.

At dusk, when the lamps come fully on and the canopy above goes black, they gather — not all, never all, someone must always watch the pumps — but enough, at the sump in the center. Knee-deep in steam, glittered by the weeping walls all around like the inside of a well. Water pouring in from a dozen mouths. Leaves dripping. Someone shouting about a clogged screen. Someone else already moving with the pole. Steam sighing off a release valve and condensing instantly on banana leaves, raining back down warm onto bare shoulders, onto upturned faces.

They do not look up at the rim, that thin pale line. There is nowhere else to go, and they know it. The pit holds heat. The pit holds water. The pit holds them — root, brick, skin, iron, all furred together into one breathing thatch. If you stopped cutting for a week, the lanes would close. If you stopped pumping for a day, the cellars would drown. So they cut. So they pump. So they stay, glistening in orange and white light, half plant themselves, half machine-tenders, keeping the bowl alive by never letting it rest.
