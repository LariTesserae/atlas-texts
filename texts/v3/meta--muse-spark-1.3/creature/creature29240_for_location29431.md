What lives here does not drink. That is the first thing to unlearn.

At first you think only one thing lives here — the green. From the rim it looks like staining in the cracks. Down among it, it looks like a thicket, then like a forest, then like neither. Stay still longer than is comfortable, with reeds whipping your shins and moth-dust in your mouth, and it resolves into individuals, if that word still works.

**The reeds.**

Sail-moss. Chime-reed. Two names for a continuum.

Each one is almost not there. A stalk no thicker than a cocktail straw, hollow, jointed, rising knee-high to head-high out of salt dust it does not feed from. It is anchored, not rooted — a dry foot clenched around a pebble. Nothing goes down. Everything goes up and out.

From the stalk branch the fronds, and the fronds are the animal-vegetable lie of the place: lattices so fine you can see through three layers of them at once, silica braided with cellulose into an aerogel that weighs less than the air it displaces when the roar is strong. Copper-green, glass-green. Not leaf-green. There is no chlorophyll green here, or not much. There is structure-green, interference-green, the color of a soap film deciding to be green for a second.

Break one — and you will, by accident, with your knee — and there is no wound. No sap. A faint tick and a smell of hot metal and cut cucumber. Inside is only dry air, fractionally warmer than outside. The broken tip does not bleed, it detunes. It goes gray at the edge and silent.

Because each frond is tuned. That is the only anatomy that matters.

Hold your hand near a dense clump until the hairs stand straight and your fillings sing, and you can see it if you let your eyes unfocus: no two fronds blur the same way. One hums a low shiver you feel in your wrist. Its neighbor, a finger's width away, is a whine at the edge of hearing that makes your eyes water. A third is between beats, flickering. The whole clump is a comb laid against the great chord of the bowl, each tooth stealing a sliver.

They eat the shaking. In the hard white sun they do the old green work, a little. But the light here is too thin and too cold to make a forest out of. The rest — most — comes from pressure. Piezoelectric fibers in the stalk flex a few microns, ten thousand times a second, and trickle charge down to sugar-making nodes at the joints. You can smell when a gust of the permanent wind comes through: sweet, grassy with no grass, ozone-copper. Static lifts off them in sheets. Pollen, if you call glass-dust pollen, showers off with every flex — glittering shards no bigger than a skin cell that hang in the air until a neighboring frond catches them electrostatically and grows around them.

They grow while you watch. That is not a figure of speech. A tip extends with an audible *tick*, forks, unfurls a new lattice already humming. They have to grow fast because they die fast. A clump overgrows its own tuning, gets too dense, goes discordant with itself, and shatters — a whole bush going white and exploding into glitter without any wind to push it. For a second there is a bald scar. Then the neighbors, still humming, comb the falling shards out of the air and re-grow them. White to green in minutes. The forest eats its own broken music.

In the hollows where the sound pools they grow so thick you cannot see the polygons underneath, a dry surf waist-deep, flashing. On the rises, between the seven ribs, they thin to separate tufts, grayer, more widely tuned, holding on. At the teeth, twenty paces up, they give up entirely: brittle paper skeletons that crumble if you breathe on them. The living green is exactly as large as the song is loud enough to feed it.

**The herd.**

Nothing that eats the reeds touches the ground. Ever.

What you took for dust, for seeds, for your own vision shivering, is the second order of life. Tens of thousands strong. A weather.

Mostly they are moths, if you stretch moth until it tears. Shiver-moths the size of your thumbnail, built like kites: two great paper sails of chitin stretched over hollow struts, a body like a grain of rice, a head that is almost all rasp and eye. They weigh nothing you can feel. A cloud of them banks and the sun goes through ten thousand wings at once.

They graze the top of the surf. They tear along six inches above the fronds, shearing off the vibrating tips with mouthparts like tiny files, swallowing dry sugar-glass and static charge together. As they eat their wings beat out of phase with the roar — deliberately out of phase — stealing energy from the standing wave to stay aloft without flapping much, and giving back a higher, tearing overtone layered on top of the deep chord. That is the sound that makes you wince and cover ears that are already useless. You cannot hear your boots. You cannot hear your blood until you crawl under a fallen arch. You feel the herd go over as a shadow and a pressure drop, a cold prickle as ten thousand wingbeats suck heat and momentum out of the air around you.

Look longer, until your neck aches from looking up into deafness, and the herd sorts itself:

The shiver-moths are the lawnmowers, low, dense, stupid with hunger. They condense without signal into a tight screaming vortex over a sweet patch — you will hear the pitch change before you see them turn — strip it white in seconds, a bald bite in the green, and disperse before the reeds have even started to flash back.

The flutter-mice are larger, slower, furred not with fur but with silica down that crackles blue in the shade. They ride higher, a foot or two up, lapping pollen-dust and shards out of the air with sticky tongues, squeaking in ultrasound you feel as an itch behind the eyes.

The needlewasps are not wasps. Thin as eyelashes, fast as thrown needles, they hunt the herd itself. They do not sting. They dock — slam alongside a shiver-moth in midair, rasp a hole in its sail, drink the dry sugar in its gut, and let go. The victim spirals, rights itself on one-and-a-half wings, keeps grazing. Maimed sails are everywhere overhead if you look: notches, slits, lace. Nothing is killed cleanly here. Everything is nibbled and keeps flying.

None of them land. A torn moth that cannot stay in the standing wave does not fall to the salt. It is caught — by reeds, electrostatically, mid-tumble — and combed down into the thicket where its body, dry as paper, is unmade in hours into powder and charge. Nothing reaches the ground whole.

**The sieves.**

Above the herd, sparse enough that you count them on two hands if you stand long enough with your mouth open, are the gliders.

Hawk-sized. Handkerchief-light. They do not flap. Flapping would be a waste in a bowl full of free lift. They lock their translucent wings — you can see sun through the belly, see the dark pellet of gut, see the combs — and ride the standing waves in slow, endless eights, mouths open.

Their mouths are not mouths. The lower jaw is a fan of bristle, a baleen of chitin, and they strain the air. Moths, pollen, shards, salt: the bristles sort by size and charge. What sticks is licked off by a dry black tongue. What falls is powder — their droppings are not droppings as you know them, no wet, no scent, just a fine white dust sifted from the air behind them that the reeds below catch and absorb before it ever lands. A closed loop spun so tight it hums.

You can tell a well-fed glider by how pink its gut-pellet glows through the skin when it banks. You can tell an old one by the salt-caking at its wing joints, white cuffs it cannot preen off because there is no water to preen with. They preen with static instead, hanging in a null over an arch and letting the field comb them clean in crackling bursts.

They are silent, which in this place means they are the loudest thing in a different way: where they pass, the moth-overtone dips for a second, a wake of eaten sound.

**The crust and the keepers.**

Down low, where you are tempted to say nothing lives, more lives.

On the copper-veined slabs and the black glass tripod and the tilted pylon — the seven ribs that are not buildings but instruments left running — grows a lichen the color of dried blood and burnt orange. It needs no water, only light and field. It creeps over the frost-like circuitry, over the grooves that are waveguides, dulling them, tuning them minutely sharper, living off the leak. Where the dust falls *up* near the pylon, the lichen is thickest, bearded with salt, crackling faintly when you approach.

Under it, in the salt polygons, in the dry powder between reed-feet, live the cleaners you will never see unless you lie down with your cheek in the dust and let your vision go: glass-mites, salt-fleas, things no bigger than the pollen they herd. They comb powder, moth-scale, glider-dust, reed-shard, rolling it into pellets and leaving it where a frond-tip can zap it up. They are the color of the ground and they freeze when your shadow falls, becoming ground.

And under all of it, the bowl itself, hollow-throated, thumping slow under the fast roar. Press your palm to the drumhead center and feel it. It is not alive the way the reeds are alive. It is alive the way a drum is alive when played. Wind sucked up from the cloud sea below through throats in the rock, compressed, shaped by those seven old ribs into a held chord so steady the mountain has grown around it. Whoever tuned it tuned it and left. The lichen grows over their not-writing. The reeds lean toward the arches the way plants lean toward sun.

That is who lives here, finally, as one thing: a hamlet that eats noise.

Stay too long and you will be briefly included. Your bones will start to hum in sympathy until your teeth ache. The reeds nearest you will lean, minutely, toward your tremor — you are warm, discordant, new. The moths will test you, brush your cheeks like dry snow that never melts, rasp once at the salt on your skin and reject you. The gliders will ignore you, which is correct. You are not food, not yet. You are a brief dissonance.

Step back toward the teeth and within twenty paces they let you go. The reeds stunt and gray. The herd will not follow. The roar frays back into mere mountain wind, thin and clean.

Look back down from the rim and it is again almost empty: pale dent, wafer leaves, handkerchief hawks, vast sky. Lush and teeming and starving for nothing but vibration.
