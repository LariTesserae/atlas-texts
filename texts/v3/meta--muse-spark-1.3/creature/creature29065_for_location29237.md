At first you think nothing lives here.

That's the first lesson the pit teaches. You come down the switchbacks expecting, in a place this built, this warm, this damp in its seams, the usual companions — a pigeon shifting on a girder, a line of ants working a spill, a lizard doing push-ups on hot concrete, the whine of a fly around a sump. A human town, even a machine town, should drag a tail of other lives behind it.

This one doesn't. The ledges are empty. The culverts are clean in a way that is not clean. Under the boilers, where in any other town in the world there would be roaches, there is only dust lying exactly as it fell. Around the low basins, where water stands for an hour after the drip has been strong, there are no flies to stipple it. You lift a loose plate and expect pillbugs and find nothing, not even the husks. The quiet is not just the walls swallowing sound. It is the absence of small feet, small wings.

So you revise: not nothing. Almost nothing. And almost becomes, the longer you stay low and still, a very precise something.

What lives here first, because it got here without needing to walk or fly in, are the ones that can arrive as dust on air.

On the north-facing walls, in the exact black line where a shade-cloth or a cold pipe sweats all day and never quite dries, lives a crust — black-green, flat as dried paint. From three steps away it looks like staining. From three inches it is velvet, tiny cushions packed tight, holding each drop as it falls. It does not spread. It cannot afford to. A centimeter beyond the wet line the fresh rock is bare and glittering and too hot to touch. The moss knows, with a precision no surveyor could match, where water lives.

On the hot iron itself — handrails, condenser flanks, the boiler house skin where the paint has blistered first-generation orange — lives another. A rust-red lichen, almost the color of the rust but duller, flatter, patient. It likes heat the way other things like shade. You see it as freckles that have grown together along a bolt head, along a weld seam. It does not need soil. It makes its slow acid and eats light and iron-warmth.

And then, where men have tried to stop a leak and made a garden by accident: the pipe-ferns. Under the cisterns, where burlap has been wrapped around a weeping joint and never removed, roots have found the burlap. The fronds come out pale, almost translucent, because they have never seen real sun, only amber work-lamp and reflected blue-brown shade. They hang limp in the midday heat and stiffen when the evening sigh comes up from the sump. They have no one to carry their spores — no wind worth speaking of down here, no insects — so they just let go, and the spores fall straight down into the next wet inch and start again.

Along one alley, and only that alley, where two condensate lines run parallel and sweat all day onto the same seam of concrete, there is a row of saltworts. Gray-green, stunted, leaves thick as a thumb knuckle. They look half-melted. They are doing something clever with salt — the condensate is faintly sour with metal, and the concrete sweats white at the edges — storing fresh in the leaf, excreting the rest as a white crust you can taste. No flowers that you can see. They split at the base, they send a runner along the wet seam, an inch a season. They will not cross dry stone. If you crouch you can see the front line: a tiny green knuckle nosing forward, and then nothing.

That is the whole wild census. No pollinators, no eaters, no rotters. Which is why the dead leaves collect in corners — blown in over the sharp rim from the pan, from what bush no one knows — and lie there unchewed, paper-thin, undisturbed. Dust settles on them and is not tracked through. The place has producers, barely, and no consumers. Growth without appetite.

Except for the other thing that lives here. The one that tends the kettle.

You don't see them at first either because they have learned to live like the plants — low, in shade, along water and moving air. This is not a town populated. It is a town kept. Three, maybe four souls at most, maybe fewer now, maybe one left on rotation — you never see them together, so you stop counting. You see evidence: laundry strung under a catwalk where the floor-current runs coolest, stiff already but not yet dry; a kettle left on a waste-heat plate, humming faintly; a chair — a kitchen chair with wood legs — dragged to an exact crack between buildings where the machines push a slow breath along the sump floor.

They are not visitors. They have the look and movement of people who have stopped fighting the pit and started obeying it.

They stay low. You learn that by watching where their things are: sleeping pallets on the concrete floor, not on bunks; tools racked at knee-height; the chair set almost on the ground. Standing upright puts your head up in the pooled heat. Crouching puts you in the thin, machine-pushed current that crawls along the sump if you don't block it with boxes and bodies. So they crouch, they shuffle, they sit. They move slow, not from laziness but from heat-economy. A fast climb up a ladder at noon will put you on your knees.

They are gloved. Always. Rag in back pocket, wood handle wired onto everything, old oven mitts slit for fingers. After ten you don't touch bare metal, and after twelve you don't touch it even with a glance — you know the handrail will brand you. Their hands are pale and pruned oddly, not from immersion but from constant handling of condensate, cupping it, wiping it from pipes with rags into buckets. Their shirts are stuck to them by the third switchback and stay stuck. They drink by the finger-width, from communal buckets set under elbows, and they know to the hour which drip runs fastest and which has gone sour.

They do not talk much. Partly because sound dies here — a dropped wrench rings once and stops, a shout across the knot of roofs doesn't carry — and partly because there is no need. The machines talk through the boots: the low sixty-cycle hum in the molars that says the flywheels in the brick power house are still turning slow and steady with the doors always open, the sigh and white vent for thirty seconds that says a pressure valve has lifted and reseated. Drip, tick, creak. The tick of tin expanding and contracting in chorus when the afternoon sigh rises. The plink of water into metal. They listen the way you would listen to breathing in a sickroom.

What they do all day is tend. Not build — the building is done, wall-to-wall, everything touching everything, ladders become roofs become gutters — but keep: keep the shade-cloth tensioned, keep the drip lines unblocked and untapped — you put your cup under, you don't tap — keep the moss alive because the moss marks where the water is drinkable and where it isn't. They wipe beads from the underside of cold-water pipes before they get too fat to hold, they move buckets a finger-width to the left, they oil the big slow flywheels, they sweep dust but not the leaves, they've given up on the leaves.

They live off sweat, and they know it. Twice a summer a real rain spills over the rim in thin waterfalls that hiss to steam halfway down, ponds for an hour in the low basins, and is gone. The rest of the year it is heat lifting sump water into air and air giving it back as beads on every cold surface. Thousands of beads gathering, fattening, breaking. Gutters ticking. That is the river here. That is the well.

At late afternoon, when the amber lamps look brightest against the blue shade, you will finally see them plainly, because they stop to watch it too. The desert above cools a degree, the dense hot air below sighs upward all at once in a slow column of dust and steam that almost reaches the rim before it collapses. The hanging cables sway — power cable, guy-wire, condensate line, empty clothesline — the tin ticks in chorus, the beads tremble and fall together as a small rain inside the earth. Whoever is tending the kettle looks up with the others, gloved hand on a hot rail through a rag, face wet, waiting for the fall.

Then it settles. The flywheels turn. The pumps thump. The walls sweat again, drop by drop.

Moss and lichen and fern and saltwort and keeper. That is who lives in the fresh wound. Nothing that crawls, nothing that flies, nothing that buzzes or nests or hunts — just the ones that can live on sweat and shade and slow vibration, and have learned not to ask for more.
