Nothing lives here. That is what you will believe first, and the place will let you believe it for a long time.

It needs you to be quiet. It needs you to stand still in an inch of hot water until your own splashing stops coming back to you wrong, until you have stopped expecting frogs and gnats and wings, until you have learned to hear the small work. Then it will show you that the sparseness is not absence. It is pattern.

Start with the reeds.

White reed with no leaves, translucent as quills, rising from cracks in the causeway. Always in groups of three or four. Then nothing for a hundred paces. Then three or four again. Not scattered like seed would scatter them. Placed. You will think at first they are plants apologizing. Then you will crouch — the air so thick you have to breathe through your mouth — and see they are not rooted the way plants root. They have no roots. They go down through the crack into the wet dark and they *continue*, as a single hollow tube, into the stone. They are siphons. When the heat-pulse comes and the street-water goes scalding, you can put your hand near them, not touching, and feel a thin, cooler in-draw of air going down. They are inhaling.

Each cluster breathes together.

Then the lichen. Old-bone rosettes with no north to orient to, that glow very faintly when touched and then forget to stop glowing for an hour. You will touch one because you are lonely for light, and it will kindle under your fingertip, a pale green bruise. An hour later, a hundred paces down the causeway, another rosette you never touched will kindle on its own. Then another, further off. A slow signal walking away from you through the stone at the speed of thought in something very large and very cold-blooded. The walls are netted with it, if you learn to look in the dark. Fine, hairline threads under the sinter skin, connecting rosette to rosette to tower to basin. It is not decoration. It is nerve.

The black felt on the submerged steps is tongue. That slick that makes you catch yourself on the rim — that is not algae, or not only algae. It coats only the places where water runs fastest, in the center of channels, on the lips of culverts, where the dissolved mineral is richest. It eats by being run over. When your foot breaks the water and the greenish phosphorescence ignites and fades, that flash is not chemistry. That is startle. A whole flat skin, miles wide but torn into a million slick-patches, living on the surface tension, drinking the noon-light that leaks up from below.

Because below is where the light lives.

You already know the puddles are wrong. Still water shows noon while you stand in absolute dark. Pale blue, high sun, towers that do not match. If you lie on your belly on the hot rim — the stone sweating under your elbows — and wait until your breath stops rippling the pool and the noon reforms, you will see it is not a reflection. A reflection would hold still. This drifts. Very slowly, a shadow crosses that pale blue down there. A tall silhouette leans, almost imperceptibly, from one side of the frame to the other. The towers down there move. They are the same kind as these, but awake in light, and what you see is not sky. It is the underside of their day pressing up against the membrane of water.

This city has a twin, and the twin is diurnal.

The ones up here are nocturnal in a night that never ends. They built — no, they *are* — the streets, the basins, the hollow towers leaning very slightly toward each other over the water.

A tower is one animal. That is the thought that will come to you all at once and then become more itself the longer you stay, until you cannot unsee it.

Not built. Accreted. Concentric rings like a tree or a shell. Of course. It grew. The walls weep constantly because walls are skin, porous, exuding water that has passed through a hot gut. The mineral builds in knobs and curtains as it cools — except nothing cools here — so it builds the way coral builds, precipitation as excretion. A windowsill grows downward into a stalactite because it is a lip thickening. A doorway grows inward until it is only a keyhole, then seals, because it is a pore closing. Down the street another doorway is opening where water has dissolved the wall back, because it is a mouth opening. The city is settling itself into balance because a body settles itself.

Look at where one has slumped and split. Count the rings. Each cream band a wet century, each rust band an iron-rich fever. Ten thousand years licked into ripple. Inside is larger because inside is not room. It is cavity. Duck through the low dissolved doorway — you have to crouch, the stone grown thick — and the interior suddenly tall, taller than the tower looked from outside, walls curving away up into dark you cannot see the top of. Open at the top to let the steam out. Your whisper goes up and does not come down because the throat is lined with steam and baffles; sound goes up into a shared lung. Water drips from impossibly high. There is no floor but a deep hot pool that glows faintly green when you disturb it. That is stomach. You do not wade in. You have understood correctly not to wade in. The green brightens when meat is offered. The heat in there is not bath-warm. It is digestive.

The towers lean toward each other because they talk in steam and drip-pitch. Each cornice drips into its basin at a slightly different pitch, and in the quiet you can hear a street of them like an instrument being tuned endlessly. When one settles another millimeter with a deep groan, the two next to it adjust their weeping. Condensation rains. They cool one degree. They listen.

Their feet go down. The faint carnelian glow in the cracks where the ground is thin is not lava you can see. It is their root-heat showing through. Each tower sends blind black fingers down through the pan — out in the marsh you can see the young trying it above water, those stilt-roots that wanted to be mangrove but stopped, undecided. The crowns never formed because the crown is below, in the heat. What lifts out is feeler, testing. The old ones have fused their feet into the travertine pan itself, which is why the ground gives under your foot and then holds: you are walking on back, on fused sole.

They eat heat and stone and light-from-below, and they exhale city.

That is why there are streets without intention. Water runs in channels down the middle because a body runs water down the middle. At intersections the channels widen into round basins, perfect circles rimmed with pale stone, because a pulse needs an eddy to mix in. Steps go down into black water and come up again because peristalsis needs riffles. Terraces step down toward the marsh in broad shallow stairs, each holding its mirror, because lungs need surface. It is infrastructure because anatomy, repeated at valley scale, looks like urbanism.

And that is why the city mirrors itself. You will find the intersection with four leaning towers, one with a collapsed shoulder, memorize it, find it again an hour later exactly but the collapsed shoulder on a different tower. It is not that you are lost, though you are. It is that the place only has so many ideas and uses them over and over. That is reproduction. A tower buds not by seed but by remembering. A pattern of flows that works — four throats around a mixing basin — is copied a hundred paces over with a slight error, like a shell copying its spiral. The collapsed shoulder moves because the copy is not perfect. The copy is alive.

The empty plazas, fifty paces wide with nothing but an inch of hot water and your own reflection — those are the young flats, the sole-skin not yet raised into throats, or the old dead ones flattened back down, still breathing shallowly through pinholes that tick and sigh steam. The long empty causeway is a gut mid-peristalsis. The isolated platforms out past the last street, rising out of black water like stepping stones for something enormous, each with its tuft of white reeds, each with its ring of warm mist — those are infants. Low weeping domes that sweat and drip and grow a millimeter a century. They have not yet decided to stand up.

They are tirelessly active, dissolving a little, precipitating a little, breathing in and out over centuries. When the wave of hotter air rolls through from the marsh edge with its wall of white steam that erases towers to their bases, that is a breath, or a swallow. The street-water goes almost scalding. The weeping walls run faster. Condensation rains upward off the hot pools. Then it passes, droplets fall from everywhere, and the city ticks as it cools. You will learn to brace for it the way you would brace for an animal shifting in sleep.

They know you are there, the way a reef knows a fish. Your footsteps come back a half-second early — you hear the splash just before your foot lands — because vibration runs faster through sole-stone than through air, and the nerve-lichen forwards your pressure ahead of you, and the whole street flinches minutely before you touch it. If you count your steps aloud, distance stays honest longer because you are giving it a rhythm to hold onto; otherwise the causeway you crossed in two hundred paces crosses back in thirty, because the gut shortened while you weren't attending.

If you stand still in a plaza pool for a long time, your reflection in the noon-sky below stays after you lift your foot. A dark dent in the light that slowly fills back in. That dent is your weight remembered by the membrane. If many people had ever stood here, the puddles would be full of dents. They are empty because nothing with weight like yours stays long. But wait still enough, and you will see dents form without you. Small, round, in a line across a still basin where no one walks. Something on the other side, in the noon city, is walking on the underside of the water, pressing up. Its feet make dents upward that you see as shadows crossing the blue.

You will never meet them face to face. You live too fast and too hot-blooded. To them you are a brief scald, a tear in the slick-skin that flashes and heals. To you they are stone that weeps, light that remembers, reeds that breathe in threes and fours.

Stay until the air gets heavier until water beads on every surface and falls as warm drizzle from the undersides of arches — the always post-rain that never rains — and understand that you are standing inside a herd, a reef, a town-sized animal with no center because no center is needed, only more town thinning into marsh, only more throats opening and sealing, only the long habit of water moving minerals around in the dark, dreaming noon on the underside of every puddle.
