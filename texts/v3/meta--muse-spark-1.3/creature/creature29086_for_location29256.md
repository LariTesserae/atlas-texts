No one lives here. That's the first thought, and it holds for a full minute.

No one could. It's too hot, too low, too full. There isn't anywhere to lie down without touching hot iron on one side and cold iron on the other. There isn't anywhere to stand up straight. The air is already used. A person would have to breathe through the mouth, and cough, and leave.

But something has learned the clearances.

You see it first as wear, not as life. The room is all hard edges weathered smooth, but in one place the weathering has a direction. Between the two riveted tanks there is a slot, just wide enough for a human body turned sideways. The paint — what is left of it, that dried-blood blister — is rubbed bright there at shoulder height on the left and at hip height on the right. Not rusted. Polished. By cloth, by skin, passing a hundred times a day.

Below that slot, the grate is different. Everywhere else the grate is furred with mineral and black slick. Here, in two ovals about as long as your foot, the metal shows through. Cleaned by feet that never lift, that shuffle and do not step.

And the drip is managed. That cold intake pipe that sweats constantly — under it the concrete should be solid algae, green-black and stinking. Instead there is a path swept through it, a pale track no wider than a wrist, and at the end, tucked against the wall where the condensation falls thickest, a tin can. Dented. The inside furred white with lime. When a drop hits it, it doesn't go *tink. plock.* It goes *tink. tink.* A higher note. Someone moved the music a few inches to the side.

She is in the slot. You took her for shadow.

Give your eyes another minute. The pilot lamp behind its cage throws almost no light, but it throws it always from the same place to the left, and after a while you learn to read by obstruction: where the hundreds of orange droplet-eyes should be, and aren't.

She blocks them. She is curled there between hot and cold.

She is small. That is the first human fact and the one the room has enforced. Not a child — an old woman shrunken down to what the room allows. Four and a half feet if she stood, which she never does. She moves bent at the knees and at the neck because the vault will not allow otherwise, and after years her spine has set that way. She wears layers that never dry: a man's work coat gone black at the cuffs, a sweater under it, plastic bread bags tied around her feet with twine. Everything damp, everything warm to the touch. She smells the way the room smells, iron and wet lime and old sweat, with something sweet-sour underneath, like milk left in a hot cupboard — the smell of a body that has stopped trying to dry.

Her skin has given up being skin. On her hands and feet it is white and ridged, macerated permanently by damp, the fingerprints smoothed out. On her elbows and knees it is stained rust-red where she braces against the tanks. Lime dust has settled into every crease of her neck and wrists and stays there. Her hair is thin, white, cut short with something dull, and it beads like the pipes do — each strand holds a droplet that catches the pilot light. When she turns her head those beads flare orange and go out.

Her eyes are the color of the weep on the walls. Pale blue-white, filmed over. She doesn't see you the way you see. In this black she sees what the fern sees: brightness and direction. The pilot lamp to her is a sun. She leans toward it in her sleep the way those three pale fronds lean. If you put your hand up she will look not at your face but at the space your body blocks from the lamp.

She breathes with the room. That took you a while to separate — her breath from the pump's slow churn under the grate, from the tick... tick... tick-tick of the tanks cooling. Her breath is mouth-open, wet, patient. In, out, on a cycle of minutes, not seconds. The air is thick and she does not fight it. She lets it sit in her. When the transformer box hums she rests her molars against the pipe without thinking, the way you would lean your head on a pillow. You feel it in your teeth: she lives inside that pressure.

She does not walk. She negotiates, the way that pale thing you thought was a roach negotiates the warm side of a tank. One hand always on metal. Left hand for hot, right hand for cold — she will shift her whole weight an inch to the left or right in her sleep to thermoregulate, pressing a cheek to the cold intake for a while, then turning a shoulder to the hot main when the shivering starts. Her feet never leave the grate. She reads it with her soles. A truck passing on the street above is weather to her; dust sifts down and she doesn't look up, she feels the change in vibration through the arches of her bread-bag feet and knows whether to brace for the pump to kick. She knows the cricket — that dry click inside the pipe bundle — as an individual. She knows which valve cover holds silverfish. She leaves them alone unless one gets on the fern.

Ah, the fern. That is how you know she remains, and not just hides. The fern has three fronds, etiolated, translucent at the edges. The oldest frond has a brown tip where mineral water burned it. Someone has pinched that tip off cleanly, with a fingernail, to stop the rot. Someone has cleared the moss back from its root crack with a fingertip so it doesn't get entombed in crust like the other moss. Under the fern, on the concrete lip, there is a bottle cap full of clear water — not algae water, but pipe-drip water, changed daily. A garden. No bigger than your palm. She tends it because it leans toward the same light she leans toward.

She drinks by licking. In the morning — though there is no morning, only the long slow warming when the street above heats — she takes a rag, gray, that lives looped over the cold pipe, and wipes down a section of pipe. Then she wrings it into her mouth, or into the can. Her mouth finds the weeping joint in the ceiling by memory and waits under it, open. She eats upstairs. You find the plastic bag later, tucked inside a hollow valve bonnet where silverfish can't get: crusts, a cold chip, half a bun gone soft with steam. She goes up the ladder at 4 a.m. when the shaft is blackest and the street is empty, forages, and comes back down before the light turns gray, because the heat down here is good for her bones and the enclosure holds. Up there is too open. It hurts her teeth in a different way.

She makes almost no sound. When she moves between pipes she turns sideways, wrist-first, the way you learned to do coming in, and slides. Skin on hot iron makes a faint stick-kiss sound. Her breathing catches once when she has to step over the hot main — a small, involuntary *ah* — and then settles. At night she talks, once, low, not to you. A click back at the cricket. A tut at the drip when it misses the can.

If you stand still long enough, the room reasserts its rule: everything is made, and everything remains. The brick, the pipes, the lime teeth no longer than your finger, the moss entombed in its own crust, the heat with nowhere to go, the damp with nowhere to drain except into pumps made to lift it. She is in balance with it. She is weathered into its shape. Edges rounded. Rust stabilized into a hard shell.

Above you a truck passes. Dust sifts down and vanishes before it lands. The water below gives a slow chug. A drop gathers on the cold pipe, swells, holds the orange lamp whole inside itself, and falls.

*Tink. Tink.*

She does not move. The fern does not move.
