They do not live in the Pit the way animals live in a wood. They *are* the Pit. Take the living carpet away and there is no place left, only bare heat-cracked basalt that would cool and go silent.

At first from the rim you think it is one thing. A pale shiver of wall. Then you stay, and it resolves, and resolves again, and keeps resolving until you realize you will never get to the bottom of it.

The mass of it are the Clingers.

They are crickets only if you need a word. Thumb-last-joint big, wingless, eyeless, the color of old wax left in the sun. Cream by starlight, flushed pink from underneath where the mountain's heat comes through the rock and through their bodies. No face to speak of — no eyes even vestigial, only a smooth blind head-plate, and two antennae longer than the body, never still. They don't look. They taste the air, each other, the heat-shadow of anything that moves.

Each one alone would die in a day. The cuticle is waxy to hold water in, but water is the currency this place refuses to mint. So they do what desert insects have always done: they become each other’s climate. They pack. They shingle. They lie three, four deep in any hollow, legs tucked, plates overlapping like scales on a pine cone, making of a million separate bodies a single breathing pelt that holds its own humidity. The air between them is stiller, damper by a fraction than the furnace breath that rises past them. That fraction is life and death.

Watch the wall and you will see them think together without a brain. A ripple starts somewhere down where you can’t see — antennae lift, thousand upon thousand, hold, settle. It travels slow around the oval. They are opening the pelt to dump heat, closing it to keep moisture. The mountain gives heat from below, they make heat from within, and between the two they hold the bowl at blood-warmth while frost is forming on the scoria at your elbows.

They eat what falls. And everything falls.

Because twice a day the Pit inverts.

All daylight hours the bowl belongs to the Clingers and the things that hunt them. It seethes but it stays. Then at dusk, the air over the mouth trembles, and the Returners come home.

You think of bats as cave things, but these are sky things that only borrow the rock. Small, soot-dark, no bigger than the moths they hunt — and high over the cold desert, fifty kilometers out in every direction, there are moths riding the night wind, flies torn up from sage and stone. The bats hawk them thin and tireless in the dark. With them come swifts, soot-colored too, swifts that have learned what no swift should know: how to fly blind into a furnace on heat alone, wings barely whispering, funneling thousands a minute through that eighty-meter mouth into side pockets and lava blisters in the upper walls you took for shadow.

They don’t roar. The living walls eat sound the way snow does. You hear only a low sigh as a thousand turn together and the warm air sighs with them, and the thin high clicks at the edge of your teeth when their sonar brushes you and is gone.

They bring the outside world home in their bellies. Before dawn they void it — not wet guano that would rot and stink, there is no water for rot here, but dry, hard pellets, almost dust, uric acid and chitin and concentrated night. They flick them down into the seethe. By the time a pellet hits the wall it is already being tasted. By the time it hits the floor it is gone.

That is the engine. Outside sun -> high moth -> bat -> dry pellet -> Clinger. No leaf. No root. No rain. A complete food web strung on the night sky.

The Clingers eat the pellets. They eat the shed skins of each other. The floor thirty meters down is two meters deep in what they leave and lose: shed cuticles like empty gloves, dry frass that never molds, broken antennae, moth wings brought back in a bat stomach and dropped as inedible. A chitin sand. And they eat each other — the soft newly-molted, the injured, the ones that fall wrong in the shingle and can’t right themselves. There is no malice in it. In a place with no water you cannot afford to let protein walk away.

And where there is that much blind, waxy meat packed that tight, there are things that have learned to swim in it.

The snakes first. You won’t see them unless you stare until your eyes water from the heat. Pale, almost translucent, no thicker than your finger. They don’t slither over. They swim *through* — through the loose chitin sand, through the lower layer of Clingers, moving without sound, leaving a brief wake that closes behind them without a gap. They are blind too, or near it. They hunt by heat and vibration, nosing into the carpet and taking a Clinger whole, jaw unhinging with a slow patience. Swallow, wait, swallow. Their bellies in the starlight show pink for a little while after.

Above them patrol the red ones. Centipedes as long as your forearm, rust-red as oxidized cinder, leg after leg after leg moving with a dry tick-tick you can only hear if you hold your breath. They pick their way *over* the living carpet, too heavy to sink, too armored to be eaten. They are the only thing in the Pit with color to spare. They eat everything slow enough: nymph Clingers, molting adults, hatchling snakes, each other if they meet wrong. When a pebble loosened by their claws falls, it never lands loudly — it sighs into dust.

Under all of this, smaller still: mites in the joints of the Clingers’ legs, pale dots that ride and clean and drain; tiny eyeless beetles no bigger than a grain of salt that live only in shed skin and eat frass dust; the gypsum needles and salt crusts that are not alive but grow like alive things where nitrate blooms white out of the stone — the last ghost of all that imported piss, baked dry, exhaled as mineral frost that will burn your tongue.

Nothing sings. Too dry for song, too hot to waste water on sound. They talk in touch and heat. Antenna on leg. Body on body. The shiver that goes through the near wall if, fool, you shine a light — millions of backs turning at once, closing, eating the light — that is language. *Close. Hold. Keep.*

By false dawn the river thins. The last Returners drop in, heavy, silent, and crawl deep into the blisters to hang in leather clusters through the day, breathing shallow to save water. Below them the carpet settles a fraction deeper into cracks to wait out the light. The ripple passes one more time around the bowl, a shudder of pale bodies in the almost-light, then what passes for stillness: a million held breaths.

It is small enough to walk around in ten minutes. It holds, in that one hot mouth in cold desert, importers who farm the sky, processors who are the ground they walk on, swimmers who swim the dead, and red hunters who keep them all honest — a whole world made without water, without green, without light, held together by dry hunger and shared heat.
