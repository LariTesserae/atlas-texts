Nothing lives here at first.

That's what your animal eyes tell you. No eyes looking back. No flutter, no scurry, no buzz. No bird on a wire, no rat in a wall, no gnat in the lamplight. The tunnels are empty, the walkways are empty, the air is too thin and too hot and too dry to hold a lung that needs water. You stand still and listen for footsteps that aren't yours and hear only your blood.

Then the wall moves.

Not fast. Not like an animal moves. Like heat moves. A slow creak-tick somewhere over your left shoulder as a stem tightens. A soft pop overhead as a pod splits. A tendril, pale white-pink and blind as a grub, nosing an inch closer to your bootlace because you have been standing there radiating warmth for too long. You step back and understand: the empty is full. The place is the population.

What lives on High Night Mesa is not in the Stacks. It *is* the Stacks.

Start low, with what holds everything up.

**Blackvine** is the body. The old one. Its mature leaves are not leaves as you know them — no green anywhere, no thirst for a sun that never breaks the lid. They are black-purple, thick as belt leather, as big as a dinner plate, edged all around with a waxy amber keel like a weld. They seal themselves. Tear one and it doesn't bleed water, because to bleed water here is death and theft. It beads hot oil that skins over in seconds, and gives off a sharp resin stink that makes your eyes water pointlessly. Put the back of your hand near one at full dark and the leaf is faintly *cooler* than the wind — it is stealing heat out of the air, drinking the infrared leaking from the service pipes, the furnace seams, the dull orange cracks in the boiler houses. It turns its broad blind face not up, but sideways, toward warmth.

Its roots don't go down. There is nothing down but slag-concrete and felted ash. They go sideways, a thatch of pale hungry fingers laced straight into the insulation of the heating ducts that run under every walkway, sipping conducted heat and condensed soot. Over two hundred years those stems have stopped being vine. In the center, by the First Boiler House, they are lignified into ironwood — domes and saddles and hanging curtains fifty meters thick that sway faintly though there is no wind — walls completely replacing walls, holding up tonnage that concrete gave up on a century ago.

Over it grows **Redstem**, fast and greedy. Where blackvine is slow architecture, redstem is muscle. Thinner, redder, wilder, knotting trellis to trellis in weeks. It uses the blackvine as ground. It is what closes the cut tunnels — six inches by the next shift if the cutters miss a pass. It is what piles fruiting mass onto the old ten-meter lattice towers until they sag into elephantine ribs and leaning arches and finally give in a slow, terrible, smothered slump. No crash. The felt eats sound. Just a choking sigh, then a sudden pit of furnace-light from below, a rush of scalding air that smells of scorched cinnamon and ozone, steam curling up. Within hours redstem throws its blind pink-white shoots straight for that heat, spearing, crossing, knotting over each other to get closer to the burn.

And over the redstem, like skin over muscle, is the thing called **Bladder-moss**, which is not a moss. It is blisters. Millions of thumb-sized amber-black bladders matted together, each tight with hot air and oil and trapped soot. They fur the stems, they seal the walkway tunnels, they drink soot straight out of the smog — the whole mass looks dusted black for it. At night, when ripe, they split with that soft *pop* you keep hearing, freckling the dark with phosphorescent spores that don't fall. The dust just hangs in the lamplight, slow, glittering, dry as ash, never settling. And at irregular intervals, when a whole sector shudders — a dull thump as the air-pumps reverse somewhere deep — clouds of black-pepper pollen shake loose into the hot wind. No bees needed. No wings. Just a sneeze through the ductwork.

That would be a forest. But a forest would die here. Nothing drinks. That is the rule older than any sign.

So the forest keeps animals that are not animals.

The **pruners** come first. You never see one clearly. They live under the canopy, squat hulls on rubbered tracks, so muffled in leaf-litter felt and drips of resin that they stopped looking like machines a hundred years ago. You know one by the slow hump moving under the vine-mat toward you, and then past you, and by the fresh-cut green-black smell that follows it — oil and crushed keel and hot dust. They chew tunnels open, day after day on looped programs no one remembers writing, because if they stop the mass closes and the heat stalls and everything bakes into one dead brick.

The **cutters** are their fierce cousins. Smaller, lower, heat-shielded, each with a spinning disc that hums low when it spins up. They converge — that is the only word — when a tower collapses and the red shoots go mad for the light. Three, four of them will roll in from different tunnels without signal you can hear, surround the knot of pale growth, and take it back down to stump. Cut, collapse, regrow, burn, regrow. They are white blood cells. They don't clear. They prune.

Under them all, the **hearts and guts**: the boilers nested like seeds inside a fruit in the First Boiler House, riveted skins gone green-black with heat scale, still thudding, still thudding after the builders stopped coming; the conveyors carrying cut bundles to the furnaces — *clank-clank-clank*, muffled as if through blankets — feeding shredded vine back to the fire to make more heat to make more vine; the augers turning slow in their housings; the ducts breathing in and out. Shred, burn, heat, grow, cut, shred. The plants hold up the buildings now, and the buildings hold up the plants. Pull one and the other sighs.

No one lives here to watch it. That is the final thing to understand. The builders poured square steps and the roots rounded them into dunes. The paint, the signage, the rivets were digested into felt. The machines run without hands. The vines grow without rain. The whole town-sized bowl of terraces, trellis-towers, pipe-bridges, vat-houses breathes in and out — in heat, out growth, in growth, out heat — turbulent and storm-state and violent in fast-forward, but muffled by its own flesh to a creak and a pop and a long exhalation of a valve.

Stand in the oldest dome at the center, hand over hot hand over vine-rope, with spore-dust glittering around the dim red lamps and the black leaves turning their blind faces toward your cheek-warmth, and you feel it plainly: not a crop, not a building, not a forest, not a city. One dry, hot, eyeless animal, eating its own cuttings and drinking fire, keeping itself open from the inside.
