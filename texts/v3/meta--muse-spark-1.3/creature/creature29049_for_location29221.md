They live there.

Not on it, not in it the way an animal lives in a burrow. They *are* the living of it. Seven or eight of them. You can count them if you stand still and let the shimmer settle, but don't trust the count. The spaces between them breathe.

At first, from the lip of the bowl, you think: plants. That is the easy word your mouth offers because they are green and upright and have leaves. You let that word sit for a minute and it starts to rot.

Get closer. Closer until the heat comes up through your boots and into your knees.

They are not tall the way trees are tall. Three, four meters — the height of something that decided to stop going up and start going *deep* into itself. Each one stands alone in a cleared halo of black glass, far enough from the next that the hot empty air between them feels like a held breath. No crowding. No competition you can see. Just patience spaced out.

The stem first. It is too thick to be a stem. A thigh, a trunk, a boiler. Ribbed all the way up, ring over ring, and the rings are not bark. They are smooth, dark knots like rivets left proud, each one cool-looking though the air around them warps. Up the length run veins — coppery, unmistakable — not painted on, but under a translucent skin, pulsing so slowly you think you imagined it until you time it against your own heart and realize yours is frantic. Inside is something amber and slow. Not sap. Sap is water dreaming. This is oil, coolant, blood that never knew rain. It catches the white midday light and holds it for a second longer than it should.

They don't have roots that dig because there is nothing to dig into yet. The rock is newborn, sharp, foamy black that snaps under your weight with a clean tick and smells of iron and struck matches. Instead their roots lie *on* it. They spill outward like dropped pipes, half-melted into the stone, hissing faintly where flesh meets furnace. If you put your hand near — not on — you feel it: they are drinking heat directly. Sipping the mountain's slow cooling. That is their soil. That is their rain.

And then the crown. Leaves so big one would shade you fully, thick as waxed canvas, veined like an open palm. The green is wrong. It is too wet-looking for a place that has never been wet, a deep bottled green overlaid with oil-slick purple and brassy tarnish at the edges. It trembles, always, though there is no wind in the pocket. Touch it and you understand why: it is warm as skin. It is humming. Not metaphorically. A low tremor that gets into your teeth first and then your sternum. A factory idling.

Because that is what they are when you stand under them. Not plants pretending to be machines. Machines that learned how to be grown.

Turn a leaf over, carefully. The underside is a workshop. Rows of small gill-chambers, pale and tight, swell almost imperceptibly — you have to watch for an hour to believe it — and then sigh. A soft exhale of dry steam that smells of hot pennies and clean forge-air. No moisture in it. It vanishes instantly. Each frond is a bellows, each bellows keeps its own time. One sighs while another is still swelling. They are never in sync, and that is why the bowl is mostly quiet. No chorus. Just spaced punctuation: sigh... tick of cooling rock... faint ping as a stem expands... sigh somewhere else.

At the very top, where hunger for metaphor wants a flower, there is a valve. A whorl of stiff red petals like cut sheet-metal, closed tight most of the day. When the pressure inside peaks — you can feel it building as the hum climbs half a tone — it opens with a slow, deliberate *clack*. Not a petal opening. A hatch. Out comes a plume of black smoke that doesn't know how to rise in this thin air. It tips sideways, pools around your knees for a moment, oily and warm, then unravels into nothing.

That is their breathing. That is their blooming.

Reproduction is even slower, even more industrial. Along the older stalks hang seed-pods, perfect dark spheres, heavy as ironwood, smooth as ball-bearings. When the windless air stirs them they clink, faintly, musically. They don't fall when ripe. Nothing here drops. They *discharge* — a soft cough from the parent stem that rolls them a few inches into a crack in the lava. There they sit. For years. You can hear them if you kneel: a tiny tick... tick... tick as they cool and heat and cool, waiting. Most will never unfurl. The one that does will do it over a whole hot season, inch by inch, creaking audibly, pushing out a red-hot shoot tipped with blue.

Ah, the tips. On dry afternoons the youngest shoots gather electricity. Little crowns of St. Elmo's fire, blue-white coronas that crackle and die in a second. A natural spark gap. The smell of ozone blooms sharp and clean for a breath, then gone. It isn't decoration. Watch long enough and you see the convection lift the fine iron-dust pollen from one open valve, carry it slow as snowfall — ten minutes to cross the bowl — and let it settle on another's corona. Heat itself is their bee. Their pollinator. There are no animals here. No flies, no lizards under the pipe-roots, no birds to interrupt. Nothing eats them. Nothing has ever bitten that humming green. The lushness is total, uneaten, unshared.

If you stay until your mouth goes dry and your own blood gets loud in your ears, you start to notice they are not entirely *here*. Or not only here.

At noon, when shadows should puddle straight down, some leaves throw theirs upward — faint green afterimages hung on the air that linger a breath after you blink. Look at the farthest one out of the corner of your eye and you sometimes catch it doubled, overlapping itself by half a second: one sighing just before the other sighs. The empty heat between them feels longer to walk than to measure. Twelve steps that you remember as twenty. The sheltering lip of rock hums at a pitch that makes your hands feel in two places at once.

They live in that thinness. They idle in it. They don't fight it. Growth for them is not a rush but a pressure held. A frond unfurls creaking over months. A stem ticks down degree by degree at night — you haven't seen night, but you know they will stay warm through it, banked coals, waiting for sun to stoke them again. Nothing watery can exist here; the stone refuses it, the salts repel it, a poured cup would scream to vapor before it landed. So they learned to live on what is allowed: light, heat, sulfur yellow dust, iron, oil. They sweat oil in bright beads along the veins. They bleed amber coolant when a sharp flake of new rock nicks a surface root, and seal it in minutes with black scab.

Seven or eight green engines, each alone in its shimmer, making and unmaking without hands. No builder. Don't look for one. Everything here, even the things that clack and hiss and vent and spark, was grown. Even the rivets are knots. Even the boiler is bark.

Sit with your back to the warm new stone long enough and they become more themselves: not plants, not machines, but something that needed both words to get started and now needs neither. Slow, hot, patient inhabitants of a pocket still deciding its shape, high above cloud, humming quietly to themselves in oil and iron and light.

When you leave — and you will, because your mouth is dust and the light is too honest — you leave nothing. The sharp rock springs back. The salt resettles. An hour after you are gone, out of sight, one of them will sigh, one valve-flower will open with its iron clack, vent its sideways smoke, and close again.
