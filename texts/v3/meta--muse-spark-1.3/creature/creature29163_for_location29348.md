There is nothing here. That is what it tells you first.

You stand with water in your eyelashes, with the roar in your teeth, and you think: empty. A scoured bowl. Too cold, too loud, too dark for anything to want it.

Then you stay a minute longer than you meant to, because your legs don't want to go back through the curtain yet, and the place corrects you.

It is not empty. It is *held*.

What lives here lives the way the moss lives: low, and at the edges, and all at once.

---

At first it is only green. You think green is a color, a covering. Then you press your thumb in, as you will, and water wells up and does not go down, and you understand it is a body.

She is the oldest resident. She is not one plant but a pelt.

Pillow moss shouldered into star moss into liverwort into algae, inches thick, swollen black-green, stitched through with the wiry black rhizoids and the pale thread of fungal hyphae, holding herself to vertical rock by suction and patience. Hart's-tongue ferns are her fingers, thrust out of cracks where no soil should be — stunted, leathery, each frond beaded and never dripping because the mist feeds it faster than gravity can take. Closest to the curtain, where the boiled light pulses, she is luminous and slimy, algal, almost animal. Six inches back, she darkens. A foot back, she is black.

She does not see. She drinks air. She grows a millimeter a year, outward from the seep-lines, and nothing has disturbed her in a hundred years except frost prying one grain loose per winter and a wren's foot. If you put your ear — useless in this roar — close enough, you would smell her: iron and peat and crushed stems. That is her breath. She is a climax in miniature. She will never become forest. She does not want to become forest. She wants exactly this: cold, and dark, and spray forever. She is the one for whom the fall is not a terror but a sun. Without the atomized backdraft she would die in a season. With it, she will outlive you by ten thousand years.

And living *in* her, so small you miss them until your eyes give up on distance and learn nearness: the second order.

Springtails like grey pepper ticking through the moss pillows. A blind white mite. The larvae of winter crane-flies curled in the pothole bowls, feeding on drowned detritus. And the weavers —

You have to look up and to the sides, where the darkness is permanent under the ledges. There, in the stillest air, where the mist does not quite tear the silk, hang the cave spiders. Long-legged, amber, the color of wet sandstone. *Meta*. They do not spin to catch flies — there are almost no flies here — they spin to feel. A few slack lines strung from basalt crack to moss hummock, beaded with water, trembling not with prey but with the fall itself. They sit upside-down at the hub, legs folded, utterly still, reading the thunder through their feet. They can go months without eating. They have learned to eat drip. To eat waiting. When a drowned midge is blown through the curtain on a gust, they move — absurdly fast for this place — and then go still again for a season. They are the nerves of the green pelt.

---

Then the warm ones. They do not *live* here the way the moss lives here. They borrow. But borrowing, repeated for ten thousand years, becomes a kind of residence.

High up, where the basalt has cracked into a narrow, dry slit above the worst of the spray, they hang: five, six furred pebbles. Bats. Pipistrelles, probably, or Natterer's, you can't tell in torpor. They found this slit in October and they will not leave until April. They do not hunt in the hollow — there is nothing to hunt. They breathe once a minute. Their hearts go from frantic to almost stopped. They have put themselves into the rock like letters into a drawer, trusting the stored cold to keep them cold, the roar to keep owls and men away. If you shone a light — you have no light that would survive the spray — you would see frost on their whiskers, condensation on their folded wings. They are not dreaming. Torpor is deeper than dreaming. They are stored meat that remembers how to fly.

Lower down, quicker than thought: *she*.

The wren. Brown as bark, tail cocked like an exclamation. She came in through the side gap with a grub, vanished into a crevice you hadn't seen, came out without it. There is a nest in there, impossibly, in a moss hummock reinforced with alder rootlets and bat hair and one stolen strand of rotted bark-fiber from the old human frame. A domed thing with a side hole, warm as a fist inside. Two chicks, blind, deaf in a different way than everything here is deaf, mouths opening on vibration alone. She feeds them on what the fall kills — midges stunned by spray, spiders' leavings, caddis wrenched from the plunge pool. She is the fastest thing that will ever enter here, and she knows it. She does not linger. Dart in, stuff, dart out. She has learned the rhythm of the curtain, when the gust thins it enough to pass without getting slammed. Her song, outside in the alders, is one of the loudest for her size in these woods. Inside, she does not sing. She *churrs*, low, felt more than heard, to her chicks.

And outside, but belonging: the eye.

On the shallow shelf at the very edge of the curtain, where stones make a stair, holds the trout. You see it only when the light spears through — a shadow holding impossibly still in chaos. A brown trout, dark-backed, spotted, perhaps two winters old. It does not live behind the fall. It lives *in* the fall's idea. It waits where the water throws stunned prey over the lip and into the white. It lets the current do the hunting. It is perfectly still while everything moves. For hours. Its gills barely work — the water is so oxygenated it hardly needs to. Now and then it flicks, takes, returns to the exact same hold. It is the only thing here that looks outward.

A flash of white bib through the water: the dipper working the plunge pool beyond the veil. Not a resident, a neighbor who visits to glean.

---

And then — if you have stood long enough for your shoulders to hunch and your skin to go numb and then tingle, for your hearing to turn inward to blood and jaw-creak — there is the other one. The one the cairn is for.

No one lives here. You were right about that, about people. You couldn't. The cold gets into bone in minutes. The damp never leaves. But people have been here, repeatedly, not recently. The low C of river slabs. The ash cold through. The alder poles with crushed ends. The seven stones stacked with care, the leather strip and carved dipper and flint flake wedged above, the tally lines and fish outline scratched under moss, just fitting a fingertip.

What lives here is what those things accreted into.

Call her the Keeper, because every such place keeps one. She is not a ghost. She is slower than that. She is charcoal fleck and spit and breath-hang and hand-oil left on sandstone, and wren-shit and bat-shit and moss creeping over it all, year after year, remembering the shape of hands.

She sits, when she sits, against the right wall where the overhang is highest and the drip is least, inside the C, where you would sit. She is low — everything here obeys *stay low and stay back*. She is damp to the core and then damper. Her skin has gone the black-green of permanent shadow moss. Pillow moss grows in the folds of her elbows. Hart's-tongue uncurls from the crack of her collarbone. She does not move quickly, because nothing here does. Drip-hollowing, frost-prying, moss-creep: that is her speed. A millimeter a year.

She is deaf. Profoundly, completely deaf, the way you become deaf after ten minutes inside the drum. She does not hear the roar anymore; she *is* the roar turned inward. She feels it in sternum and teeth. She reads the river thickening and thinning overhead by the pulse of green-grey light on her closed eyelids. She does not have eyes that work — what use are eyes that never adjust? She has the trout's stillness. She waits.

What does she do? She keeps the room held open. She keeps the fire-pit cold. She keeps the cairn balanced when frost tries to topple it — a minute adjustment per decade. She accepts the offering and rots it at the correct speed. When the wren darts in, she does not startle. When the bats breathe once a minute, she breathes once an hour, with the gust that thins the curtain.

When that gust comes and real grey forest light spears straight through and the mosses flare emerald and the droplets hang like glass, she lifts her face — if she has a face — and lets it hit. For a second.

Then it closes, and the roar takes it all back, and she settles again into the humming dark with the smell of wet stone.

She exists only because the water allows it, this small, cold, green, empty room in the river's skull. She will exist exactly like this long after your boots have dried, long after the alder poles have rotted to stain, long after the tally marks have been grown over.

Stay low. Stay back. Do not expect to be dry. Do not expect silence to return. Do not move quickly.

She is in no hurry to be seen.
