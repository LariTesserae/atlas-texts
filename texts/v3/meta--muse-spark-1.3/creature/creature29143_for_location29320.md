No one lives here.

That's the first thing it teaches you, if you stand long enough in the middle of it with the green light on your shoulders. No nest. No bones. No rot-swarm. A room made entirely of living things that has decided, impossibly, to stay empty.

It keeps itself empty. That is the living.

Stay past the drip and the sap-roar and you'll start to unlearn that. The emptiness isn't absence. It's maintenance. Someone sweeps this lung clean.

At first you think — wind. A thorn fell. A vine tore. But no:

Three thorns, finger-long, laid parallel by the water dimple. Not dropped. Snapped at the base, stripped of side-prickles, and then worked to a point at one end so fine you could sew with it. If you lift one — and you will — you'll see the work under the sap-bead: twin grooves, paired, repeated. Teeth. Incisors that never stop growing, shaved down on wood.

A vine peeled in one long spiral, the bark ribbon still supple, curled where a wet weight sat in its loop. A leaf-cup cut clean from its stem and set upright in the rootlets so it doesn't tip. It holds clear water, not the brown mouthfuls above. Someone filled it. Someone meant to come back for it.

So. Hands.

Not yours. Not quite.

Don't look in the middle. Nothing that lives here stays in the middle. The middle is the ear, the drum, the kept-clear. Look at the edges, where the light greens out into depth after depth.

They live in the wall.

The weave isn't solid. Up close it stops being wall at all — it's halvings, leaf over leaf over root over air, chambers within chambers, each smaller and lit, receding past where the plain should be. Pores big as your thumb, big as your wrist, breathing faintly cool. That's where the pale cricket goes, picking sideways, antennae first. That's where they go.

The grey one. The drinkers.

She's not much larger than the cricket looks from far away. From close — hare-sized, rabbit-weight, built for two worlds. Above, she is dry-country grey: coarse guard hairs the color of dust and dead grass, ticked black at the tips, excellent for disappearing on the pan. Below, where the room sweats on her, she is another animal: belly and inner arms naked, pink-grey skin always damp and wrinkled as your fingertips after bathing, palms that sweat to grip wet root. Her tail is long as her body, naked underneath with a veined pad like a leaf's underside. Fifth hand. When she comes down the throat she doesn't climb so much as chimney-brace, tail wrapped, hind feet splayed on wood-root where you'll see the claw-scrabble.

Her hands are the wrongness, and then the rightness. True hands. Four long fingers and a nub thumb on the forepaws, nails gnawed blunt, pads ridged. She can palm a thorn like a pencil. She can roll the bark ribbon between thumb and forefinger to soften it. She does.

Her face — you won't see it first. You'll hear it. The room is LOUD, a hammer on every leaf, sap roaring up around you at midday, and she lives by that loudness. Her ears are huge, almost translucent, veined green when the light hits them from behind. They move independently, quarter-inch twitches, triangulating drips. Plink. Plak. Plok. She knows each leaf-cup by sound. She knows when one is full enough to drink without tipping her weight into it. Her eyes are night-dark and day-dark both, overlarge, catching green-gold in the column. Nose constantly moving, tasting iron and chlorophyll and you.

She doesn't live in the room. She lives around it, above it in a burrow under a grass tuft you walked past without seeing, and *in* it — in a side-knot just behind the east wound where the sap wells slow and clear. That's her sugar. You'll find the amber beads there gnawed open, hollowed. She licks the wound clean and in return she grooms. That is why there are no swarms. She eats them. Ants that ride rain down the throat, fly larvae in the pollen skin, beetles that blunder in — picked, licked, swept. The cricket survives because it lives deeper in a pore than her fingers reach, long antennae reading her footsteps through wood a second before she arrives.

She is the reason the floor holds only three thorns and a ribbon and a cup. A cache, not a camp. She can't carry water up the throat in her mouth — too steep, too dry above — so she learned leaf-cups. Cut one, set it in the dimple where it won't spill, fill it drip by drip, then lift it with the bark sling looped round her wrist and teeth, tail anchored. One still has sap on its tip because she uses them as more than awls: to lance a swollen leaf-vein and drink the sweet pressure, to pin a cricket in its pore and fish it out, to pick her teeth after. Needle, knife, toothpick. Made fresh each visit and left, because to carry them up is to drop them in the grass and lose them. Better to leave the tools in the dry room that stays.

There is more than one, probably. Three thorns laid parallel is a habit, a number. A mother and last year's young, perhaps, taking turns. You will never see them together in the middle. The room is too empty for that. One drops down to drink while the other waits in the throat as lookout, ears up, listening not for predators but for the room itself.

Because the other thing that lives here is the room.

It is young. Days, weeks young since the ground settled and ripped. Blind white shoots still uncurl a millimeter a day from the wound, questing. If you watch them — don't watch them too long with your eyes, they lead down and around and back to your own back — you'll see they are not growing at random. They thicken at the tip. They fork. Nubs.

It is learning hands.

Think what it is: a knot of root and runner and leaf that, instead of filling dark with wood, held dark open. An ear grown underground. The whole sphere inhales — walls drawing out a fraction, pressure dropping against your eardrums — and listens. Sap rising roars like surf when the leaves above pull at noon. Rain stutters down the throat and every leaf reports it. Footsteps on the pan above ring hollow where you know to step, and below, amplified tenfold, they land like hammers. The plant keeps the center clear because a drum must be hollow to sound.

And it has begun to keep a tender. To be groomed, licked, pruned, drunk from, and in return to sweat water in a country with no water. To be told, by fur carrying pollen and dust and the smell of above, what the weather is. The white shoots reach most thickly toward the sap-licked wound, toward the water dimple, toward where hands have touched. Where the vine was peeled, two new runners have fused flat into something almost like a handle.

If you stay until the light column slides off the floor, you'll learn when to leave. The drip-echo lands out of order. Your footstep returns a fraction before you make it. The far wall seems farther than your arm remembers, then closer. The sap-roar rises. The walls sweat all at once in a warm sudden rain. The throat narrows.

She knows before you do. Her ears go flat, her tail locks. She is already gone — not up, if it's too late for up, but sideways, into a halving that shouldn't fit her, leaf entering leaf without crossing the interval. Tucked into the wall's smaller lit chamber, held, listened to loudly.

You will be held too, if you're slow. Sealed in as the room decides, for a little while, to be a seed. Warm and damp and utterly full of itself, with you as the single grit in its empty heart. You will hear your own breath come back amplified, your heart hammering on a stretched leaf. That is it listening. Learning the rhythm of a handed animal from the inside.

When it exhales — a settling sigh, fibers ticking, a rain of condensation shaken loose all at once — the throat opens again. The tools will have shifted. The leaf-cup upright. A fresh thorn, perhaps, green-white, snapped and waiting to be sharpened.

She will come back down to drink.
