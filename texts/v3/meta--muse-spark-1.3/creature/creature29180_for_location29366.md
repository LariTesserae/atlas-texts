At first you think nothing lives here.

That is the reasonable conclusion. No green to eat, no shade that stays shade, no soil, only brine that would pickle a root and air that tastes of pennies. The roar fills your ears so fully you miss the smaller signs. You have to stay past the hour when your boots go quiet in the damp grit to see that the place is not empty. It is just occupied on its own terms — thinly, slowly, and without waste.

The true residents are the stains.

Wherever water sits still — the shade-side pans, the rims of seeps, the collars around the vent mounds where condensation rains back down — the stone is filmed. Ocher, teal, liver-dark. Up close they are not stains at all but felt. A millimeter thick, wet, slightly raised at the edge like a lip. If you touch one it gives under your finger and springs back, and smells of hot oil and blood.

Three kinds, always in the same order. The ocher outermost, dryest, rust-powdered, made of iron-eaters that drink the dissolved metal out of the seep and breathe it out as rust. The teal in the middle, slicker, blue-green with copper, living where the brine is saltiest and the tiny electric current between ore bands is strongest. And the liver-dark innermost, almost black-purple, at the waterline itself, a reducer that undoes what the others do, dissolving what they precipitate. Together they hold a border a few fingers wide and they do not move. Not in a day, not in a season. They grow so slowly that a year's growth would not cover the whorl of your fingerprint, and they die so slowly you will never see it. Their edges are negotiated truces older than any forest. Evaporate, precipitate, oxidize, repeat — they are the chemistry of the basin made deliberate, just alive enough to keep the cycle from running down.

They can eat stone and steam because they are in no hurry. The warm bathhouse damp keeps them wet without ever washing them away. The weak current that lifts your arm hairs and makes your fillings sing feeds them; they lay iron and magnetite along their undersides like wiring. When the St. Elmo fire crawls blue-white on the boulder tops at dimming, it crawls on them too, and does not hurt them.

Everything else is a visitor that the films tolerate.

Rarely, a movement at the edge of vision: a dart from under one boulder to the next. If you freeze and wait — and the basin teaches waiting — you will see him again. The drinker.

He is lizard-like only because you have no other word. Six, seven inches long, pale to translucence. Through his belly skin you can see the pink coil of gut and the dark bead of liver, and along his back the faint gray line of spine. No eyes that work; where eyes should be there are only smooth, lidless dimples under skin, blind-looking, useless in a place where light never changes and there is nowhere to hide. Instead his snout is blunt and crowded with pits. He hunts water by feel and by charge.

He lives in the cool-damp between stones, in grit that is damp to the touch at noon, curled in a hollow exactly his size where condensation gathers. For hours, perhaps days, he does nothing. His metabolism is turned so low he barely breathes — a throat-pulse timed, whether he knows it or not, to the piston-thump under the ground. Then, in a burst, he runs. Not to hunt. To drink. He sprints across open grit to a leeward face beaded with water, to the underside of an overhang, to a black glassy ripple where drip gathers, and licks, quickly, thoroughly, with a flat gray tongue, and sprints back before his skin dries.

He eats almost nothing. A mouthful of ocher felt scraped with needle teeth. A wind-fallen midge. Salt itself, licked for the bacteria in it. His tail is fat with stored water and his scales are fringed to wick. You can tell how old he is because he is unafraid; a young one would not survive the open. The ones you see are likely decades old, having moved perhaps a hundred yards in their lives, back and forth between the same two boulders, following the same magnetite seam you feel as a lift in your arm hairs. He navigates it the way you feel it — as a sing. The seam hums, and he walks along the hum.

Over the brine pans, stitching white on dark: the skaters. Always three, though whether three individuals or one broken into three glimpses you cannot say at first. They are insects only by courtesy — long-legged, body no bigger than a rice grain, legs three times that, all clothed in white hydrophobic hair so dense they look like lint come alive. They do not break the surface. The brine that would burn your cuticle holds them on surface tension, and they run across it without ripple, legs dimpling, picking.

What they pick is almost nothing: the bloom of salt and silica that cracks and re-blooms, the aerosol of organics breathed off the teal mats, a bead of fresh condensation before it mixes. They drink with their feet. Their eggs — white, salt-hard — are tucked into the cracked polygons at the pan edge to wait, years if necessary, for that gray veil of rain to lay its brief shine over everything. When it comes, they hatch in hours, feed in a frenzy, and are adult by the time the pan is exactly as full as before.

They are white because there is no reason to be any other color. Nothing hunts by sight here.

Once, if you are still long enough between vents, you may hear the clicker. A single dry click-cry, far off, once, and not again. You will never see what made it. It is not resident. It may be a pale wader from the dry country outside that mistakes, from height, the brine pans for water, drops into the warm hum, finds nothing to wade in, and climbs out again. It may be an old drinker grown large in a deep crevice, calling once a year. The basin does not need you to resolve it. The sound is enough to remind you how little meat this place can support — one voice, using the whole valley as a throat, and then silence for the roar to fill back in.

Deeper still, where you cannot look, there are others. Inside each vent throat, under the lifting slab that dongs as it lifts and falls, lifts and falls, the walls are slick with heat-lovers — colorless films that thrive in the wet heat column you see shimmering, building sinter layer by layer, polishing the stone seat with their own bodies over ten thousand lift-and-falls until it is round as a bearing. In the grit itself, in every pocket that stays cool-damp, nematodes thin as hair and water-bears slow as geology wait out decades curled around a single drop.

None of them increase. That is the rule they obey. The ocher rims do not advance. The drinkers do not breed into swarms. The three skaters do not become thirty. There is exactly as much life as the stone and steam can afford without changing, and it has been exactly that much for so long the stones have settled around it. Pebbles fit in their hollows. Gully mouths open in the same curves. Your footprint fills slowly with beaded water, and when you look back the ripple has settled back to balance.

If you stand still in the middle, with mound and pan and boulder extending past you in every direction, you understand that the combination is the animal. Not the lizard, not the mat, not the insect, but the untouched valley behaving like a machine with no product — hum and hiss and chuff and dong, evaporate and precipitate and oxidize — and life spread through it so thin you can see through it, like condensation on glass, content to lick the machine and not stop it.

You leave. Behind you the thump keeps time. The films hold their borders. Under a boulder, something translucent pulses its throat, once, in rhythm, and waits.
