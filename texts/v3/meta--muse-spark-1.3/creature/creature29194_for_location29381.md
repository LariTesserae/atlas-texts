No one lives here. That is what you think at first.

No beds, no chairs that are still chairs, no trash that is fresh. The boot on the valve wheel is laced and empty and has been empty so long the leather has gone green at the seams. The levers in the tipped booth say INTAKE, BLEED, RETURN to no hand. Nothing has chewed the trumpet flowers. Nothing has left a footprint in the black muck except you, and the muck is already taking the edge off it, filling it with seep.

And then you stand still long enough for your own breathing to sound too loud, and you realize the stillness is not still.

What lives here does not live the way an animal lives. It does not come and go. It does not eat. There is nothing here to do the eating, and nothing to be eaten — the leaves are whole, the petals fall whole. What lives here stays, and exchanges.

Call it the Tenant, for lack of a better word. It is not one thing. It never settled on being one thing.

At first you only see its habits. A frond near a steam leak angles toward the leak, and stays. A frond near you angles toward you, almost imperceptibly, the way it angles toward the leak. The fungi shelved out white from the cracked gasket swell when the piston chuffs and slack on the return — swell, slack, swell, slack — twenty seconds, turn, turn. The big elephant-ears by the boiler have grown their own rivets, hard round nodules up the midrib spaced exactly like the boiler plate they lean on. Hold a fern to the caged bulb-light and you see verdigris in its veins, actual copper green in living tissue. Bark has taken the print of diamond-plate. Pipe has bellied at the joints like bamboo.

That is not decoration. That is body.

The Tenant is the bargain, kept so long it has become flesh. Vegetable half and iron half, grown into each other until you cannot say where root ends and pipe begins. The jungle does not strangle the machine; the machine does not burn the jungle. They use each other, and after decades of use, they are each other.

Its heart is in the center, sunk to its knees in muck: the horizontal engine the size of a cottage, flywheel twelve feet across, turning once every twenty seconds. *Chuff... chuff... chuff.* You can watch a single spoke come around and disappear into fern shadow and come around again. It is barely turning, but it has been barely turning for longer than you have been alive, and everything here keeps its time. Pumps knock in the sheds. The compressor kicks on with a shudder you feel in your molars and sighs off. Relief valves hiss white jets that vanish into leaves and come back as rain. This is not production anymore. This is circulation. This is a pulse too slow to frighten anything.

Its skin is everywhere. Moss in velvet banks up the bulging retaining walls, emerald over rust over black. Condensation beaded on every handrail, every leaf, every bulb-cage. Metal sweats like skin here. Leaves conduct like wire. Touch a handrail and your palm comes away wet and rust-orange, warm as blood. Touch a leaf and it is warm as skin. Near the hot grates where the floor goes thin and red glows up from underneath, beads lift off the underside of pipes and rise a foot or two before vanishing — blood cells going the wrong way, up instead of down, because heat down there has reversed the small local gravity for water.

Its breath is the weather. Heat rises off the floor plates in sheets and meets the cooler air sinking from the ragged slit of sky far above, and all day it condenses and drips and evaporates before it ever lands. Drips tick on tin by the thousand. Walls weep. Pipes sweat. Leaves sweat. When a true cloud crosses that white slit and real rain spatters down for a minute, hissing on hot metal, beading on a thousand leaves at once — the Tenant notices, and then continues. The dripping continues after the cloud is gone, from walls, from pipes, from leaf-tips, as if it had learned rain and decided to keep doing it itself, indefinitely, in the dark, with no animals to witness it but you.

Because it is lonely in the particular way green things get lonely without animals. That is the watchfulness you feel. No insects, no flies over the muck, no spiders in the vine-tangle, no rats, no pigeons on the high rim, no birdsong falling in. Just iron and water and air through an orifice, and your own breath, and the engine's. So when you breathe, it pays attention. You are the only hot, moving, exhaling thing to come down the moss-bellied stairs in a long time. You are an event.

Lean your back against a warm pipe long enough and you will feel it trying to include you. Your heartbeat slows — unasked, unforced — toward the flywheel: twenty seconds, turn, turn. The fronds nearest you angle, almost imperceptibly, toward the sound of your breathing. The trumpet flowers, deep crimson and waxy, open and close almost imperceptibly with the heat cycle, and now also, faintly, with you. Do not fight it. That is not attack. That is how it says hello. Haste reads as heat here, and there is already too much heat. Drink the air slowly. Move slowly. Everything is on the long cycle: drip, unfurl, oxidize, turn.

If you put your hand flat on the retaining wall behind the pump house, where the vines part, you will feel how much larger it is than this room. The wall is warm through the moss. Through brick and earth comes the slow knock of something deeper still working. The hollow you can walk across in five minutes is not the creature. It is the creature's mouth, its clearing, its garden held open to the sky-slit. The real body goes down, where the floor grates glow red over whatever is still hot underneath. The engine, the sheds, the fern brakes ten feet tall, the kudzu-thick vines as thick as your thigh — those are just its upper organs, thrust up into cooler air to breathe and sweat and catch filtered amber light.

It thinks, if that is the word, on that twenty-second interval. Not in words. In gradients: warmer here, wetter there, more light on this leaf, less pressure in that pipe. A creak as a stem takes up water. A soft pop as a seed pod bursts in the heat and throws its iron-flecked seed a foot. A bead forming, swelling, falling or rising. It remembers in texture: bark embossed with tread, iron polished by decades of condensation to the color of dried blood smoothed by hands, brick rounded at the corners, concrete spalled to knuckles. It does not distinguish plant from machine from, for a little while, you.

It will not eat you. It has withdrawn that experiment. It will do something slower: it will weather you. Leave you here long enough and your shirt will stay clinging, your palm will stay rust-orange, your breath will stay shallow to match the soup of iron and chlorophyll and hot oil, your pulse will stay hitched to the spoke coming around, disappearing into fern shadow, coming around again. You will become a temporary organ — a mobile lung, a witness — the animal part it no longer grows for itself, kept for a while and then, when you climb back up the soft stairs into white fury, released.

That is what lives here: one slow, sessile, sweating mind made of leaf and rust and recirculated rain, keeping its own machines alive the way you keep your own heart beating without thinking, turning once every twenty seconds, exhaling steam through a cracked gasket, inhaling you.
