They are not, at first, what you look at.

At first you look at the geese. How could you not. Thousands of pale bodies packed shoulder to shoulder until the black peat pools disappear under them, gabbling, lifting in white rips when the wind hits, settling again screaming. And the fish, black backs cutting V's in water too shallow to cover them. And the reindeer picking through on clicking ankles, calves in. And the vixen trotting the duckboard like a foreman. It is movement night and everything with muscle is moving, loud, in your face, landing on you, swimming under you, breathing on you.

None of them live here.

They are in here. They are over here. The geese will be gone by true freeze. The char and salmon are forcing themselves uphill to die somewhere else. The terns should have left weeks ago and know it. The reindeer are bunched and nervous because this is not their ground, it is too sharp and too wet and too loud, they are only crossing it. Even the midges — impossible clouds of them in the cold, in your nose and mouth — are only on the warm things for a minute.

What lives here stays low and stays wet and does not lift.

Put your torch down, amber, not white, and look at your boots. The water is welling up around the planks. Under it, hummock on hummock, foot-thick, drowning-thick: sphagnum and feather-moss and liverwort, black-green and bottle-green and yellow, sodden and growing over themselves, turgid with water, rimed at the tip with ice that never melts, beaded with condensation that never dries. That is the resident. That is the thing that took one look at fresh-fractured basalt, blue-black and glassy, at shale plates stood on end like broken crockery, at cracks you can put your arm down, at salt still in the rock — and said, yes.

It doesn't cover the valley. It *is* the valley floor. A thin living skin over water. Dwarf willow no higher than your ankle, knotted tight as fists between stones. Sedge in clumps so dense the water has to go around. White pins and orange brackets of fungi, soft as ears, everywhere the moss makes a little roof. Lemmings rustling under the planks, living in it. Everything else walks on it, spawns on it, shits on it — peat and rot and guano and crushed green — and it takes it and grows. You step off the duckboard and you go to your knee and you crush ten years of growth that only took two years to grow. That's how fast it is working. That's how young the wound is. Fresh, open, hot at the edges despite the cold, working furiously to close itself.

And it is not working alone.

Count the beacons when they sweep. Seven, spaced up the trough. Thin masts, no thicker than a young birch, thirty meters tall, no wires, no crossarm, just a seam of dull red light slowly circling. Stoves, everyone calls them. Shepherds.

Stand under one. You feel it in your molars first, then in your chest: a low pressure, a held note below hearing, and under that another one out of sync with the next valley over so the beat makes your stomach turn. The air is a degree or two warmer. Enough that the frost doesn't settle on the moss in a perfect circle. Enough that the sedge there stays supple. Enough that snow melts off your shoulders if you stand too long. Enough that the guy-wires sing a high thin harmonic when the sea-ice wind funnels low.

The mast is not the animal. The mast is the perch, the hearth.

The animal is the air around it.

Wait for a still moment — there are no still moments, but wait — and hold your amber lamp low. The air is full of motes, bright as midges, that are not midges. That is the closest your eyes get at first. Pinpricks, dust, down catching light. Then you see they are working.

They work the frost. They work the rootlets. They seal the cracks.

A cloud of them worries the rimed tip of a moss leaf, stripping the ice bead by bead and laying the water back down as film. Another cloud combs through sedge roots where the hummock is slumping into a tidal creek not yet decided if it is a stream, binding, tamping. Down in the fresh cracks that steam, where new ice is ticking and booming and prying the sharp stones a millimeter further apart, they go down in strings and come up slower, carrying mineral flour, laying it like spit in a wound. You brush one off your glove and it rights itself in the air and goes back to work. The rocks weep because they are sealing underneath.

They are not midges. Midges get in your mouth and die. These land on your wet sleeve, taste the salt sweating out of you, and leave. They melt the down stuck to you into water and take that too. They like warm things but they do not need you. The old fish-counter in the sodded hut — light off, tea with peat in it, numbers on a waterproof slate — talks to them like pigeons and they hang around him because he stands still and steams. The kids who hunt eggs here in summer dare each other to touch a shepherd's base with a tongue, and come back crying because the pressure gets in their teeth. Nobody shines white light at a shepherd after dark. It blinds the swarms. You use amber. You keep moving.

Are they built or born? The old men say the stoves are holding the new land up, knitting it. The maintenance crews say less and replace a guy-wire and leave quickly between gusts. It doesn't matter in the valley. The cold hasn't killed it. The dark hasn't killed it. The tech is just weather now. What matters is the circle: the mast hums, the air warms, the frost lifts, the moss grows, the swarm feeds the moss the minerals from the crack and feeds the crack the acids from the moss, the peat water blackens, the level rises an inch, falls an inch — green, yellow, red on the stakes — and the whole floor creaks as it settles a little tighter.

Seven shepherds. Seven warm circles. Seven hives of bright work in a low wet darkness that never quite gives your eyes enough to adjust to. Between them, the cold stupid total wet cold that turns your laces to wire and your cheeks to wood. Inside them, supple sedge.

That is whoever lives here. Not the visitors with wings and fins and hooves, loud as they are. The low green that drowns the sharp rock, and the bright dust that tends it, and the thin red-lit poles they gather around like reindeer calves in the middle of a herd. A knitting thing. A closing thing. Leave your hand on the slick stone long enough and you feel it through your feet, up your knees over the white roar of the outwash: the valley humming to itself while it heals.
