They do not live here so much as they *work* here. Or the place works through them.

At first you won't separate them. That's correct. Don't try. In the dim amber, under twenty feet of interlaced frond, life is a single warm press — sponge underfoot, drip on your neck, spore in your throat, something flicking past your cheek that could be moth or bird or ash. Everything is touching everything else. The categories smear if you stare long enough, and you will.

Then, if you lie still long enough on the warm stone and let the infrasonic throb come up through your ribs, they resolve. Not as species. As jobs.

**The Drinkers of Dew**

Water never gathers, so no one waits for it to gather. Everyone drinks sideways.

The smallest and most numerous are the cup-keepers. Frogs no bigger than your thumb, translucent amber as the lid itself, each one owning a pool the size of a helmet — a lava blister, a cupped pitcher-fern leaf, a mite-deer hoofprint that held last night's 3 a.m. rain. They hatch, sing — if you can call it singing, a subsonic throat-pulse you feel more in your ankles than hear — breed, and die in that one cup. When it evaporates they do not die. They crumple into a wax-skinned bead in the mud at the bottom and wait. Months, if they have to. The next eleven-minute hammering wakes them mid-drop.

Above them, the tree-ferns drink the air itself. Their fronds are felted, black-green, beaded always. Condensation runs down the hairs in endless *plik... plik...* into the moss below. The moss — inches thick, sound-swallowing, black-green sponge — is not ground cover. It is the ground. It holds perched lenses of water trembling in its weave, hanging swamps in collapsed tubes and pits that never flow out. If you squeeze it, warm water weeps. If you step on it, a hundred pillbugs big as fists unroll and roll away, clicking grit. Velvet worms, blind and wet and two feet long, cruise through it like slow thoughts, laying slime roads other things follow.

And lifting above it all in the morning: the balloon-vines. You hear them before you see them — a wet rip, then a soft sigh all across the scarp as thousands inflate. Each vine sends up a translucent sac, veined pink, big as a lung, that fills with warmed air and lifts its photosynthetic skin up into the diffuse gloom to drink light that comes from everywhere. Plants that fly, briefly, every day. At night they sag, deflate, sigh again, and lie steaming on the ferns. Moths like handfuls of soot sleep in their folds, warm.

**The Smelters and Grinders**

The seeps are poison. Everyone knows it. Drink from dew, not from seeps. The seeps are working fluid.

Along every rust-red and sulfur-yellow stain where mineral water bleeds out hot, there is a line moving. First the cone-shell grazers. Each the size of a boiler, slow as geology, a muscular foot gripping sharp basalt, a conical shell crusted with precipitated iron. They lay their heads to the seep and scrape with radulas that spark faintly blue — sulfur, copper, iron lifted straight off stone. They move an inch an hour and leave a clean trail.

Behind them, always, the second line: dung-beetle things the size of cats, armored dull green, dismantling the hot dung before it cools. Behind those, white fingers of fungus, already fruiting. It is an assembly line. Intake, refine, waste, remake. By the time the line has passed, the poison has become moss-food.

On the windward cliffs where the wet wind hits first, the great filter-feeders do the same work in air. They are sessile, huge — ribbed mouths three feet wide set into the basalt like organ pipes, fringed with cilia that glitter with dew. They inhale cloud and exhale dry air in slow, visible puffs, and every few hours cough out a brick: concentrated salt and metal, warm, almost geometric, left in piles at their bases. Until you touch them you swear someone stacked ingots there. No one did. The pile *is* the animal's breath.

In the ash flats, the soil-makers. Piston-worms, ten feet long, pink-gray, segmented like hydraulic rams. They drive themselves through loose cinder in strokes — *chuff... chuff... chuff...* — you feel it before you hear it, the polished vent-lips breathing with them. Each stroke aerates, turns, mixes spore and dung and crushed basalt into something that did not exist a hundred years ago and will be moss by next year. They are churning the world into habitability behind them as they go.

**The Keepers of Heat**

Heat comes from below. Everyone trusts stone more than sky.

The heat-crabs cluster around seeps where water is just cool enough to drink, shells still soft and pink from the molt, crowding belly to warm rock. If the rock gets too hot they lift in unison on tiptoe, a strange ballet, and sidle away. If it cools they pile.

The eel-things live in the dew-pools, two inches of glass muscle. Alone they are nothing. Together, when a storm cell passes and the mycelial grid tenses, they discharge in sequence — a ripple of blue light running pool to pool for miles under the moss, lighting the water from within. It is not display. It is talk, and charge-balancing, and stunning of insect clouds that dip too low.

The grid itself — a mat of mycelium running under whole valleys, carrying bioelectric potentials like a power grid — hums in your teeth more than your ears. You walk over a mile of it and your hair lifts faintly.

And over their kiln-nests, the bellows-birds. Huge, slow, the size of doors themselves, feathered in ash-gray down that is always damp. Their throat sacs glow dull orange, cooling glass, heating glass. They inhale cool damp air and exhale it hot, steady, day and night, over mounds of fermenting vegetation. Inside those mounds, at a constant kiln temperature no matter the amber dusk, their chicks cook — blind white grubs, and also the eggs of a dozen commensals, and the spores of the fungi that will feed them all. The bellows-bird stares through you while it works. Its eye is like cooling glass. In its exhaled heat-shimmer you sometimes see, for a second, the ridge as it will be tomorrow: sharper, steaming, already mossed. It does not mind. Duplication under stress is normal here. It has seen you twice before.

**The Herd That Is Liquid**

Mostly, movement is the mite-deer. Thousands of them, knee-high, dun and steaming, antlers like twigs, hooves so soft their run is only a faint tick like rain. They pour over a col in silence, a spilled liquid finding the low way around new heat, grazing tree-fern croziers and balloon-sacs and lichen as they go, dropping dung for the second line, crushing spores into their fur to carry to the next new ridge that shouldered up overnight. By midday they are gone. The sponge springs back. Only crushed trails tell they were ever there.

They reroute constantly. So does everything. Yesterday's dew-pond is today's fumarole. A cliff falls a mile away with a soft *whump* in your sternum. No one panics. Pioneer liverworts are already on the new steaming ridge before its ferns stop smoking. The frogs aestivate. The vines reinflate. The piston-worms turn toward the fresh ash.

They communicate without noise, because noise dies six feet out. Scent. Heat-pulse. Electric flicker. The faint tick of hoof on stone carried through rock, not air. Insect clouds that bend like a single animal write shapes against the gloom and the flicker-birds answer.

At rust-dark, when the amber cools and condensation runs down everything, the herds bed down right into the sponge, thousands of warm bodies steaming faintly. The balloon-vines sag. The piston-vents slow. The grid discharges softly, blue thoughts running for miles. You lie on stone still warm from inside the planet, under dripping life, in silence so complete you hear blood, while all around you — patient, crowded, smelted and distilled and endlessly remade — the workers that are the factory go on manufacturing the world.
