They do not live here the way people live in a house. They live here the way water lives in a cup.

Start at the top, because that is where the noise starts.

In the thatch — not on it, in it — the roof is hollowed from below. Thousands of bodies have picked it apart feather by feather, claw by claw, until the underside is honeycombed. Swallows and swifts and wrens wedge shoulder to shoulder in the mud-rimmed holes, dark heads tucking and untucking. They are never still. Even roosting they shift, rustle, bicker. The chitter never quite stops, it just changes pressure, and every few minutes some panic or hunger tips it over and a dozen burst up together, a whirring seam that spirals up through the oculus, out into the white fog, and pours back in again, wings ticking the drip-spray.

Between them, flatter and quieter, the bats. Folded like wet leaves. You would not see them until one opens. At dusk they unhook themselves and drop — not fly, drop — and then catch the air at the last second and stitch it, back and forth under the beams, mouths open. They live on what the light column makes: the midges, the gnats, the down-feathers with legs. They glean the spiders out of the lashings. In the day they sweat together, shivering faintly when a heron strikes below and shakes the posts.

Below the roof-dwellers, the callers.

The frogs own the floor. Not in a pond — there is no pond — but in every held drop. Each palm-sized cup in the clay holds one or two, cool brown with black marbling, throats going all the time. In the finger-deep gutters the green-black ones line up nose to tail, pulsing. There is the low one, jug-o-rum, jug-o-rum, that you feel in the planks through your boots, and the high metallic peep that cuts straight through the plinking of the roof. They do not stop for you. If you stand long enough they will hop over your feet, cold bellies on algae-slick wood, eyes filmed with wet. They eat everything that hatches. They are eaten by everything that waits.

In the film between the cobbles, the muscle.

Eels, wrist-thick, black-olive, moving without seeming to move. They live in the permanent sheet of water where the planks meet stone, nosing, nosing. They slide through the spiral troughs toward the center to take frogspawn and tadpoles, then slide back out to the dark. You hear them more than see them — a soft suck against stone. If a heron takes one it thrashes like a knot come alive.

And the waiters.

Four herons, most days. One on the bare drying rack, three on the low wall. Grey as fog, absolutely motionless in a way that makes the rest of the place look frantic. They are the only tall things here besides the posts. They stand with their necks folded and their eyes fixed down on gutters and cups, and they wait longer than seems possible. Then — a splash, a croak cut in half, a slap of wings. They swallow frogs whole, and small eels, and newts. Around their feet the wall stones are whitewashed with droppings, and below the rack in the central hollow a scatter of tiny silver fishbones grows whiter every season. They do not nest here. They do not need to. They have learned that this bowl never dries.

In the center, in the sump, the unborn.

The stone basin is always full to the lip with black water, trembling. It is thick with tadpoles, commas of black glass, and with newts, orange-bellied, slow-limbed, hanging in the water column. They feed on the algae and the drowned midges that fall from the column of light. The column lands there — the single slanted shaft through the oculus, full of drifting down and wingscale and spray — and they rise toward it. At night something in there clicks faintly.

Smaller still, so small you only see the work:

Mites in the feather-dust of the nests, eating the sloughed skin. Springtails on the moss. Black flies breeding in the slowest gutter where the thread of duckweed holds. Beetles under the upturned coracles, in the hard drum-dark. Algae polishing the adze-marks slick green-black. On the north side of every post a felt of moss, dense as pelt, nibbled ragged by beaks. Liverwort crusted in the wall chinks. A few ferns, stunted, uncurling from a thatch joint where dust and droppings made a thimble of soil — each frond tip nipped off almost as soon as it unfurls. They are the only green tenants, and they live like tenants: opportunistic, patient, eaten back.

No one large lives here. That is the point. The people who split these planks and grooved these posts with endless knots are mostly gone — they come, if they come, only to lift a trap at low water, to leave a fish head on the overturned quern and go. What lives here now are the small, the cold-blooded, the winged, the waiting. Small hungers, layered beak over jaw over throat, running on fog condensed by thatch, on insects hatched from still cups, on droppings feeding moss feeding flies feeding frogs.

At what passes for dusk they peak all together — chitter rising, throats swelling, bats dropping, posts creaking tighter with the cool, a loose withy tapping time against wood under it all — a single loud animal made of many small ones, filling the bowl to the brim and spilling, for a moment, out through the oculus into the marsh.
