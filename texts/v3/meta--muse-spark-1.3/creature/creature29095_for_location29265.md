What lives there does not, at first, look like it lives at all.

In the amber dusk you see movement and you mistake it for dust. Then you see wings and you think moths. Then the sawing gets inside your sternum and you understand the room is full to the seams with bodies holding stiller than stillness while vibrating.

They are not many kinds. They are one kind of living worn in different shapes.

The loudest are the smallest to your eye.

**The Singers.**

Thumb-long, milk-white, blind. If you lift one — and you won't for long because the heat of your fingers startles it silent for half a beat and the whole room leans to fill the gap — you see it has no eyes, only smooth, fused caps where eyes would be a waste. What it has instead is legs.

The hind legs are not legs. They are forks. Two long, clear spars of chitin, ridged on the inner edge like a file, held out behind the body and never folded. Row upon row of them line the amber antlers of the plume-bushes, shoulder to shoulder, head-down, abdomen up, white bellies pulsing faintly with contained heat. They do not hop. They have forgotten how. Their middle and forelegs are grapples, hooked deep into the waxy stalk so they will not be shaken off by their own work.

And the work never pauses. Left file against right scraper, over and over, a dry violin shriek too high to hear singly, but together — thousands together — beaten and folded by the hard egg-walls until it locks into that one dense chord. You think at first each cricket sings for itself. Stay an hour. You hear when one tires. Its stroke falters a sixteenth off. The neighbor does not look — cannot look — but shifts phase a hair wider, and the next, and the next, until the tired one drops off into silence and another, warmed and rested from the mat below, climbs up and takes its exact place, its exact angle, its exact pitch, and saws up the slack without a seam.

They eat almost nothing. A scrape of velvet down from the plume-leaf under their feet, licked by a dry tongue. A flake of moth-scale that drifts too low. Mostly they eat heat. Their white bodies are warm through, incubator-warm, and they keep that warmth by not spending it. No flight, no jump, no mating dance you can see. When they breed — rarely, as if reluctantly — they glue a single amber egg the size of a grain of salt to the underside of a feather-leaf and the mat-tenders cover it with scale. It hatches already tuned.

When they die they do not fall. They lock. Legs still cocked in playing position, body mummified in place by the thirsty air, they stay gripped to the stalk until the red ones come.

**The Red Ones.**

Mites like beads. At first you think infestation. Then you see the work.

They seethe over everything in slow rivers — over stalks, over fallen feather-leaves, over dead Singers. Each is no bigger than a poppy seed, round, lacquer-red, dry and shining. No mouth you can see, only a prick. They do not eat so much as tidy.

A plume-leaf breaks. In any other place it would curl and rot. Here it flutters down papery and intact onto the black glass. Within minutes the red river finds it. They get under the edge, dozens lifting together, and walk it — dry-sled it — back to the jade-white mat at the foot of the bush. There other mites, smaller, paler, chew the stem end to fray and felt the vane back into the mat, or up onto a living stalk where the waxy fiber exudes and glues it. Nothing is wasted because waste would pile and pile would change the sound.

So too with the dead Singer. They swarm it, drain its last wax, loosen its grapples, and carry the husk — light as chaff — to the bare places between islands. That is where the lattices come from.

**The Dust.**

The ash-moths are huge only by contrast. Against the low vault their wingspan is your palm. Gray as ash, as the name you will give them, thick-bodied, furred with dry powder that never oils. They are blind too, or nearly — two black smudges for eyes that see only glow.

They do not flap so much as row. Endless, slow circuits around the ovoid, wingtip to wingtip, never landing on the bushes where the Singers are, only now and then brushing the lantern-whips. Each stroke sheds. A rain of scales, fine as flour, that in any other air would settle. Here they hang. You saw it in your headlamp: motes suspended, motionless, despite a roar that should shake the stone down. They hang because the air itself is held — warm everywhere at once, no draft to lift or drop, only the standing wave of sound that cradles each scale in a pressure node and keeps it aloft for hours.

The moths fly through their own shed weather and eat it. Proboscis uncoiled, they comb the air. What they cannot use they let fall for others.

Their caterpillars you will not see unless you lift the mat. Under the crisped jade scales, in the acid-etched pits where white root-cords bite glass, lie fat, translucent grubs, legless, mouths working silently on powdered pod-dust and moth frass, converting it back into moth.

When a moth tires and drops, it does not rot either. It lies where it lands, wings folded like shutters, and dries to paper in an hour. The red ones do not move it. Something larger does.

**The Watchers, the Makers.**

They flick. That is the only word. Between islands, in the bare black stretches, a flicker of clear jelly and then gone. Dust-skinks.

They are the size of your middle finger, longer with tail, and you can see through them. Not metaphorically. The skin is unpigmented, translucent as horn, and beneath it you see the gut — a dark amber cord, almost dry — the spine like a string of glass beads, the heart a slow knot beating once every ten seconds. They have no eyes. The head is a blunt wedge with two pits where nostrils would be and a wide, always-open mouth ringed with needle teeth. They run hot. If one darts across your hand — and one will, curious at your vibration — it leaves a line of belly-warmth that fades instantly, wicked away.

They hunt by hearing with their whole bodies. Head tilted, jaw open, they taste the air for the thin whistle of a falling scale, then snap. They never miss. They drink no water; they press their bellies to the warm glass in the morning of no mornings and absorb heat through the skin, and they lick sulfur-rind for minerals.

And they have hands.

The forefeet are not lizard feet. The toes are long, opposed, five and almost fingered, tipped with black glass-sharp claws for knapping. It is they who balanced the cairn. It is they who lashed the lean-to. It is they who tied the rack.

Stay still long enough and you will see one working.

It does not build as we build. It tunes.

The cairn of balanced shards no higher than your knee: each shard set on edge at a slightly different angle. When the chord hits it, it splinters the reflection, scattering harsh overtones into the bare zone so the Singers on the far bush do not drift sharp. The skink approaches, tilts its eyeless head, clicks its jaw once — a dry tick lost in the roar but felt in the teeth — listens to the return, and nudges a shard a millimeter with its snout. Listens again.

The lean-to of plume-stalks lashed with twisted gut — gut is their own, dried lining coughed up and twisted while still pliable, stronger than sinew — is just large enough for something dog-sized to curl under because sometimes something dog-sized needs to. An old skink, grown large and slow and almost opaque with age, too heavy to flick, will crawl under the low roof where the sound pools double-thick and warm, and rest while the vibration massages its joints. Others will crowd around, not for company but to keep the note from dipping where its hunting snaps are missing. The lean-to is a resonator, a bass box.

The rack of bones — skink ribs, cricket femurs tied with copper-dark fiber chewed from native copper veins in the wall and drawn thin by teeth — is an instrument and a memory. The young ones pluck it. Not for music. To learn the chord. They run a claw across the tiny femurs until they can reproduce the exact tone without thinking. If the room's tone ever slid, they would know because their bodies would feel wrong against the rack.

The circle of tools: scrapers of basalt glass, a hand-axe of obsidian still sharp enough to shave the down off a plume-leaf, a mortar stone worn smooth in the center. In the mortar is a fine, rust-red dust and a smell, when disturbed, of hot cinnamon and ozone. Lantern-pod dust. The whips that rise six feet from cracks, jointed, tipped with translucent pods glowing faintly from within, ripen and split irregularly. The dust inside will not feed a man. Crushed, it only flares brighter for a breath and dies. But ground fine and scattered under the mats, it feeds the grubs. Feeds the moths. Feeds the scales that feed the skinks. One old female — you will see her, larger than the rest, scarred white along the back where a ceiling dagger kissed her — does almost nothing but tend pods. Climb, split with a flake, grind, scatter. Grind, scatter. The mortar's hollow is the measure of generations.

And the tally marks on the flat slab: a hundred short scratches, then a long one, then a hundred short. Not counting days — there are no days. Counting drift. Each time the chord is checked against the rack and found true, a short line is deepened. When a tool wears out and is replaced, a long line. Or they are sound waves, frozen. No one now remembers which, but the old female pauses over them and runs her tongue across them to taste the dust in the grooves, to be sure nothing has changed.

That is what lives there, finally. Not crickets or moths or mites or skinks. What lives there is the held arrangement.

Each body is a part that learned to not change. To replace without altering. To eat heat and sound and stone and return heat and sound and stone. To mend a leaf instead of growing a new one. To pick up the exact phase. To keep the air thirsty and warm and loud and amber and still because if any one of those slipped the whole closed loop would sigh open and die like a punctured lung.

When you sit with your back to the warm wall and your breathing is absorbed, you feel them notice you not as predator or prey — there is no room for either — but as a wobble. Your heartbeat is off-note. Your stomach growl is noise. The Singers near you shift infinitesimally to cover you. The skinks tilt their heads to triangulate your tremor. A red river detours to investigate the salt of your boot-print. The old female stops grinding and listens to see if you will hold or break.

You will break. You have to climb out to drink. They know this. They have seen it — in the mummified things at the edges that are not cricket, not skink, things that came down the slit and stayed too long and dried where they sat, absorbed into cairn and rack and tally.

When you squeeze up into the white glare and the roar cuts like a door, they do not mourn. A Singer climbs to your emptied spot on the wall where your back warmed the stone a fraction cooler, and saws to warm it back to exactly blood-warm. A mite carries away the feather you crushed. The moths close the gap in their circuit. The note holds.

Small, hidden, dry, crowded and empty, fresh as a wound and old as a held breath — it goes on humming its one loud note, not to anyone, but because humming is what it is.
