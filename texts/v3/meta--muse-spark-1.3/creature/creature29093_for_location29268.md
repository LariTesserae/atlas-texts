What lives here does not come to meet you.

At first you think nothing does. That is the trick of the scale. You swim between a tower of kelp rooted in a cracked foundation and a meadow of moss mounding over what was a road — fifty feet of empty black water between them — and your animal mind, which expects crowdedness from lushness, reads the emptiness as death. You keep listening for a fin. You keep waiting to be seen.

Nothing looks back. There is no eye here to do it.

Then, if you stay, and the cold slows you until staying is easier than leaving, you realize the distance is not absence. It is manners. It is how they live.

The old ones are the kelps.

Each one alone. One stipe, thick as your thigh, dark umber and ridged like old rope twisted and re-twisted by years of surge. Thirty, forty feet up into the dark, uncoiling wide blades thick as leather straps that do not rush for a surface that does not exist. They lean. That is their whole posture and their whole philosophy: lean with the pour when the northern current comes over the ridge and falls roaring down into the valley, bend and creak leather-on-leather, recover when it passes. Around each tower the water is cleared, swept clean of silt, a sphere of black held open. They need it. In two degrees above freezing, in light that is only a bruised leak from a mile above, growth is a slow accounting. You cannot afford a neighbor's shade.

They are impossibly old by any hurry-up measure. A stipe will take a decade to shoulder apart a dry-stone wall it started in — patient as ice, you can see it happening in the hut with no roof, the umber root wedged into the chinked slate, swelling a millimeter a winter. The tool-marks are still there under it, the short chipped percussion of hand hammers. The kelp does not hate the wall. It just continues. Elsewhere the same forest will mound its moss up to a hip-high terrace wall and stop a hand's breadth short, polite, and will not put a single holdfast through the open black arch of a dry doorway. Kelp does not grow through an air-room door. That is allowed to persist.

They feed themselves. There is nothing else to do the work. When a blade dies it is not taken. There is no mouth here, not even a worm-tube. It falls — slowly, so slowly, because weight here is thoughtful and silt hangs — and lies where it falls in the great composting mats between towers. You will see them in stages as you cross the old fields: dark green to pale yellow, to translucent white, to a fine silt that smells faintly sweet and iron when the surge stirs it. Leaf to mud to root, without intermediary. The rootlets in the mud drink it back up. Growth is slow, but relentless.

At their edges they carry their young as light.

Look close at a living blade: the margin is beaded with pinprick white lights, like dew that will never drop. Spore-knots. For hours they only gleam. Then, on no clock you can keep, they pulse and let go all at once, and the black between towers fills with drifting green sparks that rise a little — always a little, as if remembering up — and then, impossibly, sink. They settle back into the mother forest. Nothing eats them on the way. They land and become. Fifty feet of empty water, and one in ten thousand will catch on a fallen lintel stone, on cold corduroy mud, on a cairn capstone, and start the long lean.

Below them, the moss breathes.

Not moss as you know it on a hill. Low woolly drifts, grey-white, heaped deep over roads and rope-lines and collapsed walls until braided kelp-fiber cables thick as a wrist look like green veins. It is the lung of the valley. When the surge hits hard it exhales a faint milk-blue phosphorescence, and the whole meadow lights in a wave that follows the current — a slow inhalation rolling ridge to bend to bend. Touch it and it glows longer where your hand was, then forgets you. It too keeps its distance. One drift, then black, then another. Each holds its own emptiness.

On the rock faces live the doors.

Shelf-fungi the size of doors, pale and layered, clinging to blunt ridge stone and old field-walls. They do not lean or breathe visibly. They persist. They thicken a finger-width in a year you cannot measure. They are the places where rot is turned back into building. Under their lips the water is stiller, and the fine silt collects.

And off every edge — terrace lip, lintel, capstone, hut eave — hang the curtains. Hair-fine filament weeds, colorless in the dark until the moss-breath catches them, lifting like laundry lines when water moves through old streets the way wind used to, belling out and then letting fall. They comb the water for the sinking sparks and the falling silt. They are the youngest feeling part of it.

This is what lives: a forest with no herd.

No crowd, no school, no flock. A town-sized metabolism spread valley-sized, ridge to ridge, each presence solitary, each willing to wait a century for a slab to fall from the ridge with a deep stone boom and hang its cloud for hours. Green attention without eyes. It feels the pour in its stipes. It drinks condensation where pale rootlets flatten against the invisible skin of the air-rooms — those few well-built, closed, loved huts where water stops at the threshold as if cut, and old cold air smelling of stone and hundred-year-drowned smoke still ticks faintly with pressure shift inside. The rootlets reach through roof cracks toward that last breath but never pierce it. They spread flat and drink.

It does not see you. That is mercy and dread both. You can drift between a tower and a meadow and be only a brief thickening of the water, soon forgotten, while the slow working goes on: current and rot and growth and collapse, over and over, loud in the dark with creak and clack and hiss and organ-deep roar, patient, lush, sparse, full.
