At first you think nothing lives here.

That's what the place wants you to think. Too wet, too oily, too interrupted. No reeds to the chest, no willows, no flock. Only the one moorhen picking the rim ditch on feet too big for it, only eels moving like slow thoughts in the sump, only a fox print by the coal stage already filling with water. The ecology, you tell yourself, is microbial. Iron bacteria making rainbows that aren't oil. Algae. Midges.

Stay longer, muffled-waiting as the place teaches you to wait, and you start to see the negative shape where something ought to be.

The floor is open but it is not empty. With this much water it should have closed over years ago — reed, willow, alder carr. It hasn't. The rills have cut where they will cut and run clear and round-edged. The reeds hold only in a punished fringe where the ground is a hand higher around the rim. The birches stay no taller than a person, white with lichen and leaning. The hawthorn stays bent double. Someone is grazing it down. Something is keeping the bowl open, scuffed, puddled, crossed and re-crossed, the narrow-gauge rails half-sunk but shining on top. Shining, as if polished by passage.

Crouch down and it stops being a metaphor.

You learn to look not at waist height — where in any cubic meter there are four or five presences touching: pipe, chain, hook, dripping eave, your shoulder — but a little lower, and a little thinner. At the level of the skin of water sheeting over the concrete aprons. At the level of the beard.

Because water here behaves a fraction wrong, and the wrongness has a texture.

Condensation gathers heaviest not on top of the pipes but underneath them. The big barrel-round low mains at knee height, rusted orange and weeping at the flanges, are almost dry on top and fat underneath with a hanging fringe of drops that never quite fall. The black pipes above them, the conduits, the cable trays — the same. Each wears a clear beard. If you put your bare hand — and the metal bites, cool enough your breath shows faintly when you stand still — if you put it close under, without touching, you feel the air cooler there by a degree. And if you are still enough to watch a single bead fatten, you see it is not clean water. Inside is a thread, rust-orange, faint as tea. Iron bacteria. The thing farming them.

Up the quarry walls — old rock-and-clay slumped to their angle of rest, weeping dark streaks that never dry — the sheets coming down slow near the bottom and then, visibly, creep sideways, against the slope, toward the iron brackets. You can watch it happen. It is not fast. It is deliberate. A skin of water half a millimeter thick drawing itself uphill toward iron to thicken there to olive and rust-red.

That is its body when it is spread out.

It is not tall anywhere, because this is not high anywhere to begin with. It is low and distributed, hamlet-wide and thin. A colonial sheet-animal, a freshwater cousin to a slime mold that learned to eat rust. When it is hungry it is only a slick on black peat-mud. When it is fed it is the algae film, olive and rust. When it is moving it is the sheet itself, sighing up around your tread when the ground gives under your boot. Dull mud will take you to the ankle — that is not mud. That is a thicker piece of it, holding on.

Its deep part lives where you would live if you were made of recirculated water: in the center pit, the rectangular sump revetted with timber gone soft as sponge, where the three intakes gulp. Look down the stairs into the sumps where the water churns black and brown and you will see eels, yes, but you will see the water they are in is too coherent, too ropy. A vortex forms when No. 2 pump catches with that shudder up through your soles. It sucks, breaks with a soft *glup*. That shudder is its heart beating, pumping itself from one pit to another for no witness, keeping the bowl from becoming a pond. A pond would kill it — too still, too deep, too warm. It needs thin, cool, moving, aerated, ironed water. The kind of cool that keeps things from rotting fast and from drying ever.

So it keeps the machines.

That is the second strange thing after the lack of reeds: the place runs itself. A float-switch clicks no louder than a tongue-click and the pump catches. A sluice on the far side lifts by itself on its screw and brown water comes sheeting hard across the apron carrying leaves and grit. A pressure relief vents on top of the middle shed, a white burst that leans, thins, is gone in ten seconds, leaving dripping and the smell of hot oil and wet brick. The belt between the middle shed and the pump pit jerks, sags, resumes, though no one is here today.

No one human. The grease-pencil mark on the clouded gauge is three weeks old. The lunch wrapper weighted with a nut. The footprints that come in, do a circuit, and leave.

The trembling wheel is how you know it is near. Do not touch a wheel that is trembling. It is about to move by itself. Clustered so close you have to turn sideways, those ship's wheels — if you hold your breath and listen to the lattice of tiny wet sounds, tick of cooling metal, plink plink plink from three eaves out of sync, seep-hiss through gravel, you will hear, just under the low electrical hum you feel more in your teeth than hear from the transformer hut, a wetter click. Then the needle trembles. Then the wheel turns a quarter-turn with no hand on it, slick and certain. The hanging tools under the eaves — wrenches, gaskets, lengths of chain — clink softly when the wind moves them, but there is no wind under an eave. There is passage. A row of buckets, all full of clear water. No leaves in them. Something drinks clear water and leaves the leaves for the sluice.

If you stand long enough under an eave you will be dripped on, regularly as a clock, cold on the neck. That is not accident, though it is not raining and the puddle at your feet skins over with rain-rings from above. That is it tasting you. Salt. Heat. Whether you are firm and shining where it can cross, or dull where you will sink. It marks you, briefly, and then goes back to its work.

You almost see it, finally, in the standing puddles. Their reflections are a half-second late — you lift your hand and your dark double lifts his a beat after. Hold still and let him catch up, and you will see he is not quite you. He is a fraction thicker at the edges, fringed. He wears your shape to look out. The whole bowl is an eye made of sheet-water, watching itself stay wet without becoming lush.

It does not roar. The big machines are well-kept and water-driven and electric; they turn, they do not roar. It does not flock or herd. It saturates without lushness. It eats the weep at the flanges, grazes the reed shoots to nubs, filters grit on the aprons, takes midges in a low column over the warm outflow where the cooling water comes back out almost hot and steams — its breath, in the cool noon light through high cloud. It sleeps in the sound-swallow moss on the north sides of things, on fallen sleepers, on the footings of sheds. It wakes when mud on the wall slumps an inch and plops.

It will not leave. The low berm of old spoil grown over with drowned grass is its shoreline. Outside is flat fields, grey-green, ditches always full, too drained, too sunny, too dry on top. Inside is where water is not *in* a place but *on* everything. Where iron never dries.

Step only where it is already firm and shining. Do not expect silence to mean stillness. Everything here is happening at once, just quietly, because something low, broad, patient, and almost entirely water is doing it, continuously, with many small wet hands you mistake for drips.
