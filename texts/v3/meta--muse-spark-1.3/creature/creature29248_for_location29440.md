At first you think nothing lives here.

It's too full. That's the problem. Leaf over leaf over water, drip over drip, cloud condensing at chest height and coming apart again — your eyes keep sliding off. You stand still because the sponge won't hold you if you don't, boot slowly sinking and warm water welling up blood-warm around the ankle, and you wait for the valley to sort itself into animals.

It does. Slowly. The way fog lifts in strips.

The first to resolve are the smallest, because there are the most of them. A glitter in the air where a minute ago there was only mist. Clouds of midges, gnats, black flies no bigger than a punctuation mark, hanging in columns over the warm pans, rising and falling with the heat. When the white sun breaks through for a minute they ignite all at once, thousands of wings catching light. They are the plankton of this place. Everything else eats them or moves through them.

Then the water starts to tinkle, and you realize it is not water.

*Tink. Tink. Tink-tink.* From every puddle, every boot-print that has filled brown, every axil of moss. Thumbnail frogs. You have to crouch — your knees will be wet instantly, it doesn't matter — and part the sedge with your fingers to see one: a body the size of your fingernail, skin translucent amber or wet-leaf green, throat pulsing like a tiny heart under the chin. There are hundreds. Thousands. The marsh seems to rain upward when they call, ventriloquial and hushed, so you turn your head and lose them. Some never touch the ground at all. Up in the crowns of the tree-ferns and giant groundsels, where the old leaf-bases cup together, there are tanks of rainwater, a liter here, half a liter there, warm as tea. A whole frog life can happen in one: egg string, tadpole, froglet, calling adult, without ever climbing down. If you lift yourself to look into one you will see the water shiver and then eyes looking back.

Above them, businesslike, the birds that can afford to be loud.

The copper-green hummingbirds come through on straight lines, not hovering dilettante but shift-workers. They work the high flowers — the white and magenta orchids clinging to every vertical trunk, the groundsels' tall yellow torches — moving from one to the next with a wing-blur that adds a higher octave to the valley's underlying hum. Their feet are almost useless, their bodies almost too hot; they pant with open bills in the midday white heat and then bathe in the steam of the vents, flashing in and out of cloud. You will hear them before you see them, a hard insect *vrrr* that stops dead when they stab a flower.

Lower, where you would expect waders to sink, the rails and snipe pick. They should be shy. They aren't. No one has ever hunted this sponge. A rail steps out from under a Gunnera leaf big as a shield, chestnut and slate, its absurdly long toes splayed wide to distribute its impossible lightness, and looks at you with a bright, unbothered eye. It spears a bloodworm, shakes it, swallows. Behind it a snipe probes the warm mud with a bill that seems to go on forever, head bobbing. They nest right on the hummocks, a scrape in sphagnum lined with a few fern scales, eggs speckled like peat water.

Bend closer. The mud is alive even without birds.

In the warm pools along the eastern fault, where the earth breathes through clear throats and hot springs well up glassy and trembling at 50, 60 degrees, building low rims of silica and rust, life does not stop at the heat — it leans in. Scoop — you won't, but look — and the orange-brown silt writhes. Amphipods, pale and blind and frantic, sideswimming in densities that make the bottom boil. Blood-red worms, tubifex and midge larvae, packed so tight they look like muscle fiber, waving in the outflow to filter what the water hauls: leaf bits, petal, drowned beetle, pollen in yellow slicks, silica grains. A conveyor that never stops. Out in the central marsh-lakes the mats take over, algae and duckweed running their green production lines at full tropical speed, doubling and doubling under that vertical sun, exhaling oxygen in strings of tiny beads that rise and pop.

And on the drier hummocks — drier meaning merely damp and not submerged — the other production line runs overland.

You will see it first as a thin green line moving when nothing else is moving. Kneel. Leaf-cutter highways, fifty meters long, a centimeter wide, worn smooth in moss. A steady belt of ants, rust-red, each carrying a green sail bigger than itself, leaf fragment, petal fragment, orchid seed-pod fluff. No foreman. No pause. They vanish under a Gunnera leaf into a hole that steams faintly from the fermentation inside, where underground the fungus they feed will bloom white and be eaten. If you sit for an hour the highway shifts slightly to avoid a new puddle, engineers without a word.

The plants are animals here too, almost. There is so little soil and so much rain-washed hunger that green has learned to bite. On the open sphagnum, where the sun hits hardest between clouds, the sundews spread their red rosettes, each tentacle tipped with glue shining like hot-spring water. Minute flies land and do not lift again. In the tea-brown pans the bladderworts float, rootless, their tiny bladders snapping shut on copepods faster than your eye follows — hundreds of microscopic stomachs per stem. The orchids are quieter thieves; white and magenta, they cling to moss-sleeved trunks and drink fog through aerial roots, flowering straight out of bark that is itself not bark but vertical garden.

Even the dead are not left alone. A fallen log does not lie dead for a week before it is green over, mossed, then fruited through from the sides with mushrooms — orange cups, white fans, brown fingers that deliquesce by evening into black ink. A dead bird, and you will rarely see one whole, is mossed over in days, reassigned: fungi through the feathers, orchid seed lodging in the hollowed ribs. Nothing leaves. That is the rule. So everything has learned to be quick about reuse.

The mineral seeps are where it all gathers to drink.

Where hot water cools along a basalt lip and leaves its iron and sulfur as orange and white crust, butterflies come down in trembling carpets. Orange, sulfur-yellow, black-bordered. Fifty, a hundred, shoulder to shoulder, tongues unrolled to sip salt. Your shadow falls across them and the carpet lifts all at once — a single rustle, a flash — and resettles a pace away, as if the ground itself flinched.

At night, if you stay, and the heat lets you stay with no cold to drive you down into the gorge, the shift changes.

The valley does not darken so much as dim to blue-green. The hummingbirds vanish. The frogs get louder, then stranger, then stop in waves as cloud drags through. Moths take the flies' place, pale and furred, working the night orchids that open with a heavy sweet smell to cut the sulfur. Up on the rim the sheet lightning stitches cloud to ridge without sound. Down here the springs take over the light: along the thinnest earth, where bacterial mats of green and orange fringe every vent, there is a faint soft fire, bioluminescence marking heat. Lines of it. Veins. The mud pots go on blubbering and popping in the dark, the workmanlike geyser thumping every eleven minutes in your sternum, throwing steam that becomes the next hour's roof.

Stay still long enough and you understand what lives here is not a list.

It is a single throughput wearing many bodies. Fly to frog to rail to worm to ant to fungus to orchid to fly again, water hauling the spare parts downhill in every rill, heat pumping it uphill again as steam and fog and capillary pull in moss that drinks against gravity. Light things with wide feet and fast blood. Small things in huge numbers because the sponge will not hold weight but will hold insistence. Things that can stand to be wet and hot and rained on daily and breathed on from below by the earth.

No large animal. No herd, no predator with heavy paws. Nothing that needs dry ground or silence or winter. Only what can live inside a lung that never stops breathing — chuff, sigh, long exhalation — and be content to be part of the breath.
