At first you think nothing lives here.

Not really. The water is too empty, too loud. Green distance and pillar and pillar and snow falling sideways — where would anything hide? The fish you glimpse are too regular to be animals. They move like parts.

Then you stay under a little longer, and the place lets you see that the parts *are* the animals, and the animals are unafraid because they have never needed to be anything else.

Start with the most numerous. You will see them first as a glitter, a kink in the water between two giants.

The Conveyors.

They are small, palm-long, silver with a lateral line that flashes blue when they turn. Alone they are nothing. Together they are rivers. Thousands, tens of thousands, pour in a band twenty meters wide and three meters tall from one side of the gap to the other, mouths open, gills flared. They do not dart. They do not scatter when a shadow passes. They keep station, fin-tip to fin-tip, spacing so precise you could measure it with calipers and find it the same a year from now.

They are filtering the mill-meal — the ground plant-detritus the eddies spin out where two holdfast hills lean together. The water there accelerates to its eternal two knots and the detritus hangs suspended, sorted by size. The ribbon enters it, mouths open, and leaves it behind them clearer, their bellies faintly green. Watch long enough and you will see the ribbon bend around that far pillar in a perfect loop, disappear into haze, and come back. Same number. Same spacing. Same speed.

If you put your hand in the stream they part around your fingers without panic and close behind, and each flank as it passes gives off a tick — a faint electric discharge. Not a shock. A ping. Together, turning, the whole school crackles. That is part of how they stay in formation in water where you can barely see ten meters in snow. And part of something else. On dim surface-days you can see the charge run ahead of them and kiss the stipe bark and run up it, blue, Gone.

They do not breed in a season. There is no season. Now and then one falters, drops out of line, sinks slowly, mouth still opening. It does not get eaten in a chase. It falls. You will see what catches it below.

The Mat-walkers catch it.

Down on the felt, where the current polishes the living tissue smooth as old leather in the gulleys, move the crabs. To call them spider crabs is correct and insufficient. Their carapaces are tables, barnacled, algae-bearded so that from above they look like low hills in the mat. Their legs are longer than you are tall, jointed, tipped not with claws for fighting but with soft, almost finger-like picks. They walk with terrible slowness, each step placed, tested, placed. Springy mat depresses under them and rebounds.

They are not hunters. They are gleaners and pruners. They pick whale-fall, fish-fall, frond-fall. They pick the bark of the giants, delicately plucking mussels that have grown too thick, exposing the stipe skin beneath to the current. When a frond tears — and fronds tear, thirty-meter sails beating in surge — milky sap wells. The crab will stop, fold its great legs, and drink, unhurried. Within minutes the small cleaners arrive.

Ah, the cleaners. Second multitude. Finger-long, translucent, with black eyes too big. They live in the wound. They live *for* the wound. They dart in clouds around any break in bark or frond, eating frayed tissue, eating fungus before it can be fungus, polishing the edge until the giant seals it over with new skin that is exactly the same as old skin. Then they disperse, waiting. They will clean your fingernails if you hold still, nibbling dead skin without fear. They have no fear because nothing here chases.

Up on the pillars themselves, the bark is alive.

It is not bark, of course. It is stipe wall, thick as fortress walls, but everything has colonized it in horizontal bands according to light and surge. Lowest, near the mat where it is darkest and coldest: white anemones, closed like fists, opening in slow pulses to filter snow. Higher: black mussels in sheets so dense they become a second skin, byssal threads woven into the giant's outer fibers until you cannot tell where mussel ends and kelp begins. Higher still: nudibranchs, and this is where color finally breaks the green-gold monochrome. Violet, orange, acid yellow, white with red gills pluming. They are as long as your forearm, slow, bright as warning flags in a place with no predators to warn. They graze algae off the bark, leaving pale trails that fade in days — or do not fade, but are re-grazed in exactly the same path again.

They are all unafraid of you. That is the eeriest part. A nudibranch will not retract when you approach. A crab will look at you with stalked eyes, decide you are neither fall nor frond, and step *over* you if you are in the way, its belly passing warm above your face.

And then, when the light thins and the snow thickens, the shadows come.

The Slow Ones.

You hear them before you see them. A moan so low it is not sound but pressure in your bladder sacs, in your teeth. It vibrates the house-sized gas bladders on the stalks and comes back altered, harmonized. Then between two pillars a hundred meters apart, something blots out the green.

They are whale-like in size and in function and in nothing else. Flattened top to bottom like giant skates, twenty, thirty meters across, mottled green on green, undersides pale as mat-leather. They do not swim so much as displace. A slow undulation of the vast wing-rim moves them forward at perhaps one knot, never faster, never slower, between the trunks. Their mouths are wide, forward-facing slots. They move through a conveyor ribbon and the ribbon pours in, thousands vanishing without a break in formation — the school simply continues, diminished by exactly the volume of the mouth, and further back along the loop you will see... the same number again. Because nothing is lost.

They trail behind them a plume of feces, fine, green-brown, that does not disperse but hangs and slowly sinks. The crabs below are already turning toward where it will land. The mat will eat what the crabs leave. The stalks will lift it back up in their slow *huuush-thump*. The fish will filter it again.

Eat, excrete, filter, feed. A closed belt turning in place.

Don't look for young and old among them. You will not find calves trailing mothers. You will not find carcasses. If one dies — and you begin to doubt the word means here what it means elsewhere — it sinks to the mat and is unmade so quickly, so cleanly, by crabs and cleaners and mat-enzymes, that within a day there is only a shallow depression in the felt, springy and cold, and then not even that. And somewhere up-current, in a bladder-cluster or a frond-axil, tissue divides where it has always divided, holding the number steady.

Which is when you understand you have had it backwards.

You thought the forest was the place and the animals lived in it.

It is the other way. There is one inhabitant here.

The pillars are not trees. Trees grow, die, rot, succeed. These do not grow taller, do not rot, do not topple. The pulse travels up them — *huuush-thump, huuush-thump* — drawing cold nutrient water up from kilometers of interwoven holdfast. Their joints knock together in surge, *tock... tock... tock...*, six degrees of lean, always six degrees. Their fronds store charge and release it blue along a bladder seam. They are pumps and mills and batteries and pillars holding up a sea. They are organs.

The fish are its blood cells. The crabs are its hands, picking itself clean. The nudibranchs are its tongue, tasting its own skin. The Slow Ones are its gut, moving slowly through itself, digesting itself, feeding itself. The cleaners are its clotting. The moans and knocks and hisses are not noise in the place. They *are* the place, working.

It lives. God, how it moves. But it lives the way a held breath lives — by not changing. By having weathered so long into hills and valleys and millraces and eddy-mills that erosion finished and left it perfect. By cycling every mouthful, every bubble, every snowflake through crab and mat and stalk and fish and whale and crab again, so precisely that if you release a bubble and watch it rise wobbling to join the stream, you can wait, and another bubble will take exactly its path again. And again.

It does not need to go anywhere. It does not need to become anything else. It has replaced stone with vegetable and stopped.

Float in the middle distance, where the water is empty and green and loud in your sternum, and you will feel it breathe around you — inhale, exhale, inhale, exhale — cold pressing in, daylight filtering down, silver beads rising and never arriving. It notices you the way a body notices a mote in its eye: not with fear, not with hunger. Small fish will come to clean your wound if you have one. A Slow One will alter its moan slightly as it passes your body, feeling the different resonance, and carry that altered note down the rank of pillars for kilometers.

You are, for a little while, also part of what lives here. Fall, and you would be eaten, filtered, lifted. Rise, and you would be exhaled like a bubble to the rippling white ceiling impossibly far above.

Stay under, and you stay in it. That is the rule. That is the animal.
