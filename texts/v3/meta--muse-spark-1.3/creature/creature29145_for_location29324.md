At first you think nothing lives here.

That is the hollow's first defense. From standing height it is stone and glare and too much sky. Then you kneel, because the air makes you kneel anyway, breath short, heart hammering, and the grey resolves.

What lives here lives low. That is the first law, before anything else.

**The rooted ones that make islands.**

They do not grow so much as huddle. The moss campion is the matriarch — not moss at all but a flowering plant that has decided to be a stone. A cushion so tight you could stand on it and barely dent it, green poured over a dome of dead leaves and old stems and trapped heat. Put your hand inside at noon and it is ten, fifteen degrees warmer than the air, humid, buzzing. The tiny pink stars open only for the two or three hours when pollinators can fly, and then shut. Each dome may be twenty, thirty, fifty years old, adding a millimeter a year. To kill one you would have to tear it; frost cannot.

Around and between them, the breakers: white star saxifrage and yellow mountain saxifrage wedged into cracks where no soil should exist, roots like threads seeking the sweat of rock. Dwarf willow, which is not a tree and barely a shrub — a forest shrunk to the height of your thumb-nail. Its woody trunks creep sideways for meters, ancient, gnarled as olive wood, throwing up leaves no bigger than a mouse ear. A single mat can be older than the bothies. In late summer its catkins become cotton, and the spiders string their webs from leaf to leaf to catch what the wind brings. Sedge and cotton-grass and alpine bistort crowd only where the foot stays wet, in the black mud rims of the pans, their roots in cold anoxia all night and warm slurry at noon. They do not spread. Between one green island and the next is a meter, five meters, ten meters of shattered plate where nothing roots. The hollow can only afford life in allotments, and life has agreed.

They live on the daily cycle, not the season. Dawn: locked, rimed, stiff, photosynthesis shut down. Ten o'clock: drip, unclench, pop — you can actually hear, if you lie with your ear near a campion, the almost inaudible tick of capsules opening in the white heat. Two o'clock: hail rattles through and shreds a few petals. Four o'clock: blue shadow, plunge, close. To live rooted here is to open and shut, open and shut, a hundred times in one summer.

**The frantic ones of noon.**

When the snowbed under the north wall begins to bleed, around eleven, the pans fill — dark mirrors no deeper than your hand — and within minutes they hatch.

Midges rise in columns that bend in the thin wind. Stoneflies, pale and soft-winged, crawl out of temporary water that did not exist at breakfast and will not exist by nightfall, dry their wings for desperate minutes, mate in the air. And the bumblebees. Huge, ginger-furred, impossibly loud. You hear one before you see it, sawing through air too thin to hold it. They should not be able to fly here — the air gives no lift, the cold seizes muscle — but their fur holds the sun and their flight muscles shiver themselves up to 30 degrees. They work the campion and saxifrage with a panic that is not panic but arithmetic: you have three sun-hours to feed a colony before you freeze.

Under the leaves, even smaller: springtails and mites in the spongy moss, black specks that leap when a rock warms. Spiders no bigger than a pinhead who have never spun a large web in their lives, who hunt on foot between willow stems. At noon a green island hums. Put your face close and it is tropical — damp, rotten-sweet, vibrating. Step a meter away onto scree and it is lunar silence again.

**The small warm ones who stay.**

They understand spacing. They do not cross the emptiness unless they must, and then fast and low.

The pika live deepest. A hamster-sized haymaker with round ears and no tail, the color of lichen-rock. You will rarely see one in full — only a dash between boulders, a mouthful of bistort and sedge whisked into shadow. Their haypiles are tucked far under stones, where hail cannot rot them and sun cannot bleach them, little stacks of green going to straw, curing in cold dry air. Nothing rots here, so hay keeps. In winter, under six feet of snow, they will sit in the dark among stones and eat summer leaf by leaf, awake while marmots dream. All day they call — a sharp, hiccuping *eek* — territory and warning.

The marmots live fatter. Family groups under the big erratics near the outlet lip, where the boulders give deep burrows below frost. In the sun-hours they sprawl on warm noon rocks, bellies flat to stored heat, eyes half-closed. They are enormously fat by August, seemingly lazy, but it is a performance. One always sits up. One always watches. When the lammergeier shadow crosses, when your shadow crosses, the whistle goes — thin in this thin air, seeming to come from everywhere and nowhere — and tonnage vanishes into crack with a slap of claws. They live a few feet from starvation and know it.

And the hunter who links them. The weasel, or stoat in its brown summer coat, white belly flashing. You never see it by day. You see its signature at dawn around the pans: paired dots in frost-mud, a feather caught in dwarf willow, a stonefly wing left uneaten. It hunts the islands at night and at the blue edges of day, slipping through willow tunnels a vole could not pass, drinking ice-melt, sleeping in a pika burrow it has borrowed permanently. Fast because it has to be — losing heat with every heartbeat in this cold.

**The birds who work on foot and in air.**

The accentors and rosy-finches are the ground crew. Alpine accentors, drab brown with a necklace of speckles, tame as mice, flipping tiny stones with their bills, picking spiders and midges stunned by morning frost. Rosy-finches, males flushed pink on belly and wings as if dipped in high light, nesting in the slumped turf roofs of the bothies, picking campion seeds one by one. They do not sing so much as chitter, thin sounds that do not carry.

Above them, always, the choughs. Alpine choughs, black and glossy with yellow bills, or red-billed, tumbling gangs of five or seven. They belong not to the floor but to the headwall updraft. All morning they ride the pressure off the teeth, folding, sliding backwards, dropping a feather and catching it, playing with the edge of falling because they can. They drop to the scree to pry insects from under plates, then throw themselves back up into the great bright volume that everything else leaves empty.

And once a day, the visitation. Usually when cloud begins to boil over the ridge after two, a lammergeier comes. Bearded vulture. Ten feet across, wedge-tailed, rust-stained chest. It does not flap. It circles the bowl twice, slowly, its shadow traveling from moss to scree to moss again, huge and soundless. It is looking for bone. Marmot bone, pika bone, finch bone, old bone. It will swallow a vertebra whole, will carry a femur high and drop it on the knapped flint below to get at marrow. It is the only thing here that profits from death, because nothing rots. What is not eaten is dried, freeze-dried, picked clean, scattered. The lammergeier closes that loop.

**The ones who are stone.**

On the north-facing boulders, the map-lichens — orange and bone-white, cracked into countries. They do not hurry. They grow slower than campion, slower than willow. A hand-sized rosette may be a hundred years old, may have been there when the hand-axe was left on the ledge. They live on light and air and rock-sweat, on night fog condensed in the lee. At noon they are dry and crackling underfoot. In late afternoon, when the sun drops behind the western tooth and the hollow falls into blue shadow and temperature plunges ten degrees in minutes, they hold it. For a few minutes after it is gone they phosphoresce faintly, reluctant to let go of brightness. They breathe with the pans — ice skin forming, trapped air breaking it from below, forming again, sighing.

Moss collars do the same work in miniature around seeps and overhangs, sponges that are stiff as wire at dawn and soft as flesh at noon.

**The people who are barely there.**

No one lives here now, not year-round. No one could. What lives here of humans is a habit, a summer memory pressed into stone.

Three low bothies in the lee of the largest erratics, dry-stone without mortar, roofs of slab and turf now slumped and colonized. Finches nest where smoke once rose. One still holds a dark fire-pit, a cracked soapstone pot, a pile of split juniper roots no thicker than a wrist — fuel gathered twig by twig from far below, carried up on backs when breathing was work. A line of small cairns threads between the green islands, marking the dry way when melt is high, so feet would not crush the allotments. Up under the headwall, a crescent of flat stones set on edge: a blind, a trap. Sinew snares long since eaten by mice, bone awls, a hand-axe flaked from local chert left on a ledge as if just set down. A single rusted nail in a lintel like an offering.

They were summer hunters, high herders, people who came up for marmot and for crystal and for the brief glut of birds, who knew to tuck low and take little. Their marks look like geology now because they obeyed the same rule: stay dense, stay close to stone, leave the sky to wings.

Sit still on a warm noon rock and you feel it. Everything — flower, pika, finch, lichen, old wall — pressed to the ground in fierce green or furred islands, leaving the huge white volume above empty for wind. Water only moves at noon. Green only lives where damp has lived. And in the blue shadow, everything ticks shut again to wait for white morning.
