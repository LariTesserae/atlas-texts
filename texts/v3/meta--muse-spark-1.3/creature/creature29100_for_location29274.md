At first you think nothing lives here.

That is the loudest fact of the place. green to choking, wet to dripping, warm as a mouth — and uneaten. You keep waiting for the movement that has to come with that much leaf. A pigeon shifting on a ledge. A rat in the culvert. A mosquito finding your sweat in the cloud. It doesn't come. The leaves are too perfect. That is how you learn to see who does live here: not by movement, but by staying still long enough for the non-movement to resolve.

What lives here does not have eyes. Does not have a nervous system to offend.

First, the green.

It is not one green. It is a succession, a crowd that arrived all at once because the wound was fresh and wet and warm and empty, and whoever got a root-hold first won.

The mat-makers came lowest. Moss over the bus shelter, over the tilted hex pavers, over the sheared shopfront sills — a velvet foot thick, saturated, stupidly alive. You step near it and your shoe sinks without sound. It doesn't grow up, it grows over. It has swallowed the timetable inside the shelter, the ad frames, the bench slats, until the shelter is not a structure with moss on it but a moss shape that remembers being a shelter. Underneath, the algae films the glass milky. It sweats in the daylight slab and steams faintly when the drizzle hits the hot conduit.

Out of every crack, the ferns. Maidenhair, mostly, gone monstrous without anything to prune it but machines. Fronds as long as your arm, black wire stems, leaflets so thin the amber work-light shines through them. They hang from the rebar whiskers in the upper concrete, from the snapped pipe mouths, from the three tilted plaza slabs where they split. They drip constantly. Each frond tip holds a bead that never quite falls, and then falls all at once. They clone themselves by falling over. A frond touches wet stone, roots where it touches, makes a new crown. No pollinator needed. No eater to keep them honest.

Above them, the curtains. The vines that own the cliffs.

They found the fresh basalt before anything else learned how to be a ruin. Kudzu-thick stems, aerial roots like dirty hair, leaves big as dinner plates at the bottom where the cloud sits, smaller and waxier as they climb toward the dry street air. They get into fractures no wider than a fingernail and wedge them wider with slow hydraulic patience. Then they hang back down in green veils a hundred feet long. Stand under one at mid-afternoon when the chest-high cloud forms and you will be rained on — not by rain, but by the curtain wringing the air out. Condensation runs down the aerial roots and ticks off, and in the amber light some droplets slow. Hang a half-second too long. The field thickens for them.

Where the vines are wettest they phosphor. Not bright. A faint green exhalation along the veins and drip tips, enough to see your hands by if the work-lights stutter. It moves very slowly outward as the colony spreads.

In the pond corner, the stilts. Figs and tree-ferns that are not mangroves but have reinvented them. Out of knee-deep bathwater black mirror, a tangle of prop roots, gray and knuckled, holding crowns up out of the twice-daily tide. When the grates inhale — and you will hear it before you see it, a deep gargle as the pond is sucked down to glistening mud — the roots stand exposed, dripping, covered in white root hairs that hunt the mineral seep. Then the water sighs back up and they stand calved. They make small hard green knots for fruit that never ripen. Without birds, without bats, they just harden and drop straight down with a plop and bob, and rot where they land, and sometimes clone.

Along the warm pipes, the white ones. Shelf fungi stacked like shingles, flesh-pale, warm to the touch from the conduit heat. They breathe spores into the permanent dusk in such quantities your glasses film if you stand too close. No wind to carry them far in the shaft. They just settle a foot away and start again.

None of this eats. None of this is eaten. It just grows over itself, furiously, unpruned except —

Except by the other residents. The ones that hum.

You feel them in your molars before you see them. The deep electrical throb cycling up and down under the water-roar. It lives in the walls, in section: cable trays and culverts and retrofitted storm lungs stacked like strata, balconies and doors opening onto air, ladders ending mid-drop. And on wall-rails, moving very slowly on rustless tracks, the pruners.

They are not humanoid. Do not expect hands. Expect a carriage the size of a suitcase with a folded arm like a mantis, a camera eye filmed with condensate that wipes itself every few minutes with a little squeegee. Once a week — the crews above will tell you, no one stays down here long enough to watch a full cycle — they come down their rails and snip. Clean, bloodless cuts. Vine tendrils reaching for the dark inhaling grates are clipped and left to fall. Fern crowns blocking a sensor are shaved back. They do not harvest. They just keep the throats clear. The cuttings lie where they fall and root where they lie, unless the steam comes.

The sterilizers live at the pond edge. Squat nozzles on hinged elbows, crusted white with minerals from the wall water you must not drink. When the pond is at low inhale, mud shining, roots exposed, they exhale a white roar of steam around the rim in a perfect ring, killing back the moss and algal creep that would seal the grates. The smell after is cooked green and hot stone. Within a day the green is back, a millimeter further in. This has been negotiated to tolerance.

Deeper, the breathers themselves. You never see the whole animal — only its mouths. Dark grates at the sump where the three slabs meet, pulsing. Twice a day they inhale the pond down to mud and let it sigh back up. Gargling culvert pumps keep the walls weeping in a hundred sheets and ticks. If the hum stops, the old hands say, go up. The air will go thin for a minute while the lungs cycle. The plants don't mind holding their breath. You will.

And over it all, holding the impossible lightness, the three filament spans no thicker than your wrist. They don't sag. They don't vibrate. They just hold, humming faintly, while the driverless service tram crosses every eleven minutes, lights on, empty, going from street to street over a cathedral nave of wet air, never stopping. They are not alive, but they put out the field that decides what can be alive here.

The field is the true landlord. Low resonance from beyond-modern engineering, the crews say. Animal nerves can't stand it for long. You feel it after a few hours — nausea behind the eyes, up slightly to the left, your reflection in the pond arriving a beat late, condensation forming on the undersides of leaves in perfect grids because water itself behaves differently here. Concrete doesn't mind. Plants don't mind. Machines are the plants' hands, and the plants are the machines' camouflage.

So this is the town: a moss that remembers a bus shelter, ferns rooting from their own falling tips, curtains that make their own rain, stilt trees fruiting into the water they stand in, white shelves eating heat, rail-mantises snipping once a week, steam mouths cauterizing the pond edge, great dark lungs breathing the pond in and out, filaments humming over an empty plaza so empty you can shout three times off fresh rock.

Loud with water. Furiously green. Tended, but not inhabited — not by anything that blinks.

You are the only mammal, briefly, too loud in a different way, before you climb back up the stairs that were not meant to be stairs, into the dry noise, and leave them to their slow, eyeless negotiation.
