You don't see them at first.

That's the trick of the bowl. You look for people and you see only green and stone — thatch the color of dust, walls the color of dust, bodies the color of dust. Then a pile of woven matting shifts and becomes an old woman. Then a shadow between two huts straightens and becomes a child with a goat-switch. Then the whole thicket seems to breathe, and you realize the hum you thought was bees is voices.

They are small. Not pygmy-small, not story-small — worn-small, fitted-small, the way everything in here has been fitted to its niche over a hundred generations. A tall man would crack his head in the tunnel-alleys and be useless on the terraces. So they are compact, low to the ground, bow-legged a little from a lifetime of ladders. Up and down matters more than side to side here, and their bodies know it: short strong legs, long arms, shoulders and backs thickened by baskets. Even the children carry — a younger sibling, a bundle of deadfall, a wrung dew-cloth folded like a baby. No one walks empty-handed. To walk empty-handed would be to waste a trip.

Their skin surprises you. After that white burn of the cloud-sea below you expect sun-blackened mountaineers. They are not. Inside the dim they live in a perpetual late evening. The sun touches them straight for an hour only. The rest is bounced light, leaf light. So they are pale under the dust — a grey-tan, ash and clay — with dust ingrained so deep in elbow creases and knuckles and feet soles that washing, which would be an obscenity anyway, would not take it out. Their hair is dusted too, black or grizzled, cut short or shaved to the scalp against lice and thatch-mites, often capped with a woven grass visor against the brief noon glare. Their eyes have adjusted to the bowl the way yours have not: pupils large, dark, slow to narrow. At noon they squint and turn away. At dusk they see perfectly, threading needles, finding handholds in vine-tangle you would call dark.

You will know them by the mouth first. Everyone's lips are cracked. Everyone's tongue is a little swollen. They do not sweat — or almost not. Their bodies have learned the first rule: there is no water here. They sip, they do not drink. A chew of succulent stem in the morning, sour enough to make your jaw ache. A mouthful wrung from a cloth at dawn, never a cup. The old ones teach the babies not to cry water out. You cry dry here, or you learn not to. Their urine is scant and dark and goes straight to the compost pits under the houses to feed the millet. Nothing leaves the loop. They smell of it — that warm dusty-sweet close air you noticed is them: smoke in their clothes, crushed green on their hands, dry dung, guano-ammonia sharp in the hair. They move slow to keep it that way. Nothing here happens fast because fast makes thirst.

Their hands are the most alive part of them. Look at an old man's hands and you see the bowl: fingertips flattened and split from fitting dry stone, palms leathered from rope and notched-log ladders, green stain under the nails that never washes out from stripping taro-leaves and wringing beard-moss. The women grinding at the querns have a side-to-side sway worn into their wrists; the stone itself is worn into a saddle. Teeth, too — flat-milled by a lifetime of stone-grit in millet and amaranth cakes, front teeth grooved where succulent stems have been held and worried. They smile rarely and briefly, not from unkindness but because open mouths dry.

Clothes are hardly clothes. Woven fog-grass tunics, goat-hair loincloths, a child's bare dusty buttocks, a grandmother's shawl patched with pigeon down and fig bast until you can't tell weave from vine. Bare feet almost all — soles thick as bark from hot stone and terrace grit, toenails broken, ankles scarred white where rope ladders have bitten. What finery they have is bone: a hyrax-tooth necklace, a polished bat-bone awl thrust through a topknot, earplugs of fig wood. The few copper knives — precious traded things — are almost never seen. They live wrapped, oiled, in the root cellars, brought out only for a birth cord or a death cutting or the first fig of the season.

They live crowded, so they live quiet. Sound comes back off the walls. A shout doubles and triples. So they don't shout, at least not after the bats come out. Their voices are low, hoarse from dust and smoke — a constant mutter of grinding songs, weaving songs, goat-calling clicks. Children learn early to call by name once, softly, and wait. Strangers who speak loud are gently touched on the arm and smiled at until they lower their voice. In the tunnel-alleys you pass shoulder to shoulder, chest to back, and no one apologizes. Privacy is a mat hung across a doorway, a turned back on a porch. Babies nurse long and are passed hand to hand; a toddler belongs to the terrace, not to a hut. There is always a goat in the way and always an auntie to lift the toddler over it.

Their day is the dew's day. At dusk the cloth-layers go out — mostly old women and patient girls — spreading the dew-catchers exactly where grandmothers spread them: coarse-woven mats over fig limbs, over thatch ridges, over terrace rims where fog spills first. They know which crack in the wall whistles thin half an hour before the fog comes over the lip, and they listen for it with their heads cocked like hyrax. At dawn, before the sun coins overhead, the wringers go out, silent, twisting cloth into jars and gourds and cupped leaves, a mouthful here, a mouthful there, the first wring tipped onto the roots of the millet pockets and the taro seams. Nothing wasted. Do not waste a wring of cloth — you will not be told, you will simply see a child's hand stop yours if you spill.

After that: scraping. The guano-men — boys mostly, lean and barefoot and fearless on the wall-pocks — with grass brushes and baskets, scraping the ledges where swifts and pigeons and night bats have roosted for a thousand years. The ammonia bite catches your throat. That powder goes to the sowing pockets. Then grinding, always grinding, the quern-stone women in doorways, millet and amaranth and dried fig, coughing in the dust. Then mending: thatch, looms clacking on porches, flint knapping, bone awl boring, dry deadfall chopped small because — do not cut a living limb when deadfall will burn — the living limbs are kin.

They are not alone and they do not act alone. The goats are almost people: wiry black nannies with ribs showing and bells clanking, pruning the lower leaves, scolded, shoved, forgiven. Everybody knows which goat bites. The chickens are underfoot, the guinea-pigs squeak in wicker pens on porches waiting for feast days, the pigeons in the lofts are wealth and meat and message all at once. And the wild ones are tenants with rights. The hyrax — fat grey whistlers sunning on the walls — scold you and are scolded back, tolerated egg-stealers, rock-cousins. The swifts that scream circuits under the lip all day eat the flies that would eat the millet; no child throws at a swift. At dusk, when the swifts fold into the pocks, the whole village lowers its voice without being told — do not shout when the bats are navigating — and the fruit bats pour out of the deep cracks, thudding close over the thatch to work the figs. The people cover the drying figs, step back from the fig limbs, let them feed. Dung for figs, figs for bats, bats for guano, guano for millet. Even the lizards in the thatch and the beetles clicking at night and the cicada-trillers at noon have their place in the humming.

The old are many. The water limits babies — a woman may bear three in a life and bury one on the outer scree — but those who survive the dry childhood cough live long, desiccated and tough as roots. You will see a great-grandmother, blind with smoke, still twisting dew-cloth with perfect hands, telling by feel if the fog was thin last night. You will see a mason, eighty if a day, fitting stone around a fig root that will split his work in two hundred years. He builds around it anyway, then, his son tells you, laughing low, eventually they will build out of it. Lichen grows a millimeter in your lifetime here. They think in those lengths.

Their gods are small and close. Stacked-bone shrines on the roofs — pigeon skulls, goat knuckles, hyrax jaws, a swift wing — dusted with guano powder, fed with a drop wrung from a cloth. The coin of sky above is watched like a well. And the dead: they do not bury — there is no deep earth, only dust over throats that swallow. They carry the dead up the stair-cut in the rim, the one place where up matters most, and lay them on the outer scree for the sky, for the lammergeiers that come gliding out of nothing. Bone comes back down later, cleaned by sun and wind, to join the shrines. Nothing leaves the loop, not even people.

If you stay, they will offer you the chew, the mouthful. They will watch to see if you spit, if you ask for more, if you pour it out. They will teach you which vine holds weight by letting you grab the wrong one once, gently, where the fall is short. They will lend you a hammerstone left on a wall. They will sing low while they weave and expect you to listen. And at night, when the fog comes silent over the lip and the whole bowl begins to drip without puddling, and the thatch ticks and the leaves bead and the bats are out and the stone gives back the day's heat close around you, you will lie in borrowed warmth and understand: they are not trapped here.

They are kept.
