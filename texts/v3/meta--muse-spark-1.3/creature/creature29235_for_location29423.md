You think at first that no one lives here.

Not really. That's the first lesson the heat teaches. You stand still because it's too warm to keep moving, and because there's nowhere to move to without brushing something — leaf, twine, fur — and in the standing still you realize the mending is fresh.

A mat edge re-lashed this morning. The twine still pale where the knot pulled tight. A runner retrained, its holdfast still white where it was coaxed around a new post and tied with a little looped charm-knot. The wax on a crack in the wattle still soft under your nail. Someone was here a breath ago. Someone is here when you blink.

Then you learn how to see them, which is to stop looking for people.

They are small. Not little-people small, storybook small. Low-door small. Beam-ducking small. The house is forty paces across but crowded beam to vine to nest to pot, and they move in it the way blood moves in a body — without straight lines. A grown one stands to your ribs. A child to your knee. And they are thin in a way that isn't hunger. They are cured.

That is the word that comes, sooner or later. Everything here cures instead of rotting, because there is no water to forget in. Their skin has cured. The color of split reed, of inner palm, faintly polished at the elbows and heels and knuckles from handling. Dry to the touch, warm. No sweat that stays wet — sweat beads and dries to salt on their upper lips too, white mustaches you have to look close to see. Their hair, where it isn't shaved to keep cool, felts. Real felt. Thistle-down and puff-moss take hold in it if they sleep too long under a lashing, and they let it. Old ones have green-grey scalps, soft as cushions, that squeak under the thumb and spring back. Their nails split and are filed on joist-wood. Their eyes blink too often, because the air drinks. Cut-seed eyes, amber-brown, always a little gummed at the corner.

You thought they were builders. They are not builders now. They are menders.

That is their whole body of knowledge and their body, too. Fingers long and callused at the tips from picking knots. Every child carries twine around its wrist, three lengths, chewed soft at the ends. To mend is to stay. To knot is to speak. You watch one — when you finally catch one, by not watching — an old woman no taller than a storage bin, crouched on a sleeping platform with a clay bowl of beetle-shell buttons in her lap, sewing a torn vine-leaf curtain back to itself with fiber pulled from her own cuff. Slip-knot for a beam that still grows. Hard square-knot for dead wood. The little looped charm-knot wherever a vine has been trained to turn. Her hands never stop, even when she looks at you. Especially then.

They do not drink. You look for a cup and understand, slowly, there are no cups. There are pinched clay bins still showing fingerprints, full of seed-heads and dried husk-fruit, and hammered copper hooks green at the edge for hanging things, and bone awls, and fire-hardened sticks. No jar. No well. No stain. Water is spoken of — when it is spoken of — as outside and as ruin. *What is dry keeps its shape. What is wet forgets.* You hear a mother murmur it while she daubs a child's cracked heel with warm wax, pressing it in. The child does not cry. To cry would be waste.

What they eat needs no chewing much: spore-dust, husk-meat dry as paper, the oil from bottle-vine knuckles sucked through a reed. It is enough because they move so little and so slowly. Like the geckos. Like the slow-worms. You will swear a mender hasn't moved for an hour, flattened in the mottled shade of an air-fern, throat pulsing faintly. Then you blink, and she is on the other side of the post, retying a holdfast.

And that is where they stop being only people.

Because the house is folded. You felt it in the walk that should have been straight, in the sleeping platform behind the garden behind the sleeping platform that should be outside already, in the slat-light pooling sideways up the posts in bright rungs. If you lie on your back under the swollen old thatch at the center and look up, you see mats and feet and the undersides of pots, very far and very close. The hamlet stitched over on itself.

They live in the stitch.

Stand long enough and you see the duplication — the same charm-knot, same fray, same copper hook occurring twice on the same post, one a finger's width to the left of the other, as if the post hesitated. The threadbirds know. They land on the left hook, never the right.

So do the menders.

The right hook is for you. The left hook is for them. The empty patch of mat the geckos skirt, flattening themselves at its edge, where the air shimmers faintly with heat that isn't heat — that is where their feet fall thickest. The dust-mice wear a pale path along a joist day after day on their circuit; if you watch at ambering time, when the husks begin to glow from within with steady spore-light, you will see a smaller, neater footfall overlapping it, too light to wear, keeping step.

They slip a finger's width sideways. Duck under a beam hung with nests and emerge a hand's breadth higher though the floor is level — you have done it by accident. They do it on purpose. It is how forty paces holds a hamlet, garden rows and sleeping platforms and all. They unfold a little when they need room and refold when they are done, and the soft creak that runs around the wall in a circle that never quite completes at night, as the whole basket inhales the day's heat and holds it — that is them, pulling the waxed wattle oval tight. Not to keep the world out.

To keep themselves from spilling.

An old one, very old, stops mending other things and begins to mend himself into the house. You see them tucked in dimmer pockets under the platforms, where the pallid crickets comb the moss: figures gone quiet, skin gone fully to fiber, hair fully to moss, a hand still curled around a post-holdfast. They are not dead. Nothing here rots to teach you what dead is. They cure, they sharpen, they become trellis. A new air-fern unrolls in a stiff spiral from an old knuckle.

If you press your cheek to the dry leaves, dense as fur, dry as cloth, and listen to almost nothing — the tick of a loom-beetle's carapace, the dry shush of a husk turning in a draft you cannot feel — you will feel the house very slowly tighten, by fractions, following the sun through the weave. That tightening is them, all of them, mending and growing at once.
