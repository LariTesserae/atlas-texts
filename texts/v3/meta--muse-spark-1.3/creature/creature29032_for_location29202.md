At first you think nothing lives here.

That is what the Mat wants you to think. In the blue-grey half-light you look for movement at eye-level and find none — only the far-spaced Tower-Stalks standing like dead candelabra, only the black needles of the Tuning Forks humming in your molars, only wind over crisp. Then a boulder coughs.

It is not a boulder.

The grazers are the first ones you learn to see, because they want to be ground. The bellows-goats come through in the hour that is every hour, twenty, forty head, low and wide and the color of rust-black lichen. Six legs each, splayed to spread their weight on the brittle crust so they do not break through into the waist-deep hollows. Barrel chests. Shingle hides — overlapping keratin plates grown dry and loose, clicking in the wind like the bark sheets off the Stalks.

Watch one crop. It lowers a blunt, horny beak — no mouth to lose moisture through — and shears a neat divot of Mat. The Mat puffs. A white flour of spores hangs for a second and is gone sideways. Under the goat's ribs, its flanks pump, pump, pump: great bellows lungs not really for air but for dust. Every inhalation drags mineral dust and shed filament and spore through labyrinth filters in its chest, where electrostatic combs strip the organics and the rest is sneezed out in a dry cough. Nothing drinks. Everything sifts.

Above them, what you took for wind-blown trash sharpens into wings.

The sails. Paperwing mites. When they are grounded they are the size of your hand, twig-bodied, folded grey, clicking. They climb a Stalk filament or a goat's back and open — and open — and the wind takes them. Membrane over twig, translucent, veined like lichen-paper, growing monstrous with distance: hand, then coat, then door. Flocks of them tacking against the roar, whistling through spiracles, clicking leg against leg. They do not fly so much as get read by the wind. They farm it. Under their wings, dust eddies and sticks to static-oiled undersides, and they scrape it off with combs to eat. Young goatherds ride them sometimes — no, not ride. They tether to them. A child will clip a long filament-line to a big door-sail and let it lift her three meters off the Mat to see over the swells, whooping silently in the hiss, before her tender yanks her back down when the filaments rise.

Below, where you crunch, the borers and weavers.

Cask-beetles, big as fists, big as both fists, burnished black-amber, rolling in the troughs left by goats and caravans. They find stalk-fall — a joint of Tower-Stalk as long as your arm, hollow and resonant — and hollow it further, sealing themselves inside to ferment dust in their guts. Their guts are kilns without flame. What comes out the other end is dry pellets of concentrated nitrate and a sheet of fresh chitin, soft for an hour before it cures glass-hard. That is what the town harvests. That is what the workshops laminate into masks and bowls and wall-ribs. You can hear them at night-that-is-always: tick... tick... tick as they cool.

And the needle-birds. Thin as darning needles, black-eyed, nesting impossibly on the vibrating flanks of the Forks two hundred meters up. They have learned the deep organ-hum and they hate it being lonely. They imitate it and then ornament it, a choir adding a frantic descant — *sii-sii-sii-SUUU* — until the whole valley rings and the dust in the gravity-seams shivers into new patterns. The old pruners say the birds are tuning the Forks back. The Fork-tenders say the birds are parasites on the signal. Both feed them shed.

Then — because you have stood long enough for the dim to get into your eyes — you see the people. Or rather, you stop failing to see them.

They were never separate from the Mat.

The Dry Ones of Kettlehollow are low and wide themselves now, after generations this high on old stone. Not short, exactly, but settled. Knees bent from a lifetime of walking crisp that gives and snaps. Skin cured by dust and ion-wind to something papery and ash-grey at the elbows, rust-dark at the cheeks — they flake a little, like Stalk bark, and do not mind. No one sweats here. No one weeps more than they can help. Tears would rot.

Their faces are all filter and lid. Nostrils narrow to slits under overhanging brow-ridges. Eyes deeply hooded, with a second translucent lid — the dust-lid — that slides sideways when the hiss rises. Most wear a bellows-mask anyway outside: a laminated beetle-chitin half-mask with pleated sides that moves when they breathe, catching exhaled almost-moisture on cold plates and returning it. Their breath shows for a second and then is taken back. It smells of iodine and warm salt. Offworlders have their water taken at the rim. Everyone drinks dust-tea — salty, warm, not water, never water, just ion-drift and dissolved stone and spore-flour that prickles the tongue with charge.

Clothes are Mat and goat and Stalk. Felted lichen-paper inner layers that wick nothing because there is nothing to wick, but hold static warm. Outer cloaks of goat-shingle and woven Stalk filament, grey-yellow, that rattle. Everyone, even toddlers, wears a tether — twenty meters of filament-rope coiled at the hip, clipped stalk-to-stalk, person-to-person, on the laundry lines strung between domes. When the Tower-Stalk crowns lift like hair on an arm, you clip. The wind that feeds the forest will take you just as easily. Children learn the clip before they learn words: *click-drag-tug.* A family is a string of clips.

From above their town is almost nothing: low domes, vents breathing dust, ropes. From inside, crouched through a coaxed throat in the living carpet, it is layered and warm with body heat and machine heat. Walls grown thick and cured leather-hard, lined with sintered stone where the glass-box heaters sit — heat lives only in stone boxes and glass, never open. Nurseries where Mat grafts are laid on basalt chips and sung to by low crab-like shepherd-machines, silent except for fans, bathing them in ultrasound to tell them *thicken here, thin here.* Nurseries right next to them where human babies sleep in the same warm hum, learning it before language. Workshops where women and men shave beetle-cask chitin thin and laminate it with Stalk resin into masks and cradles. Galleries — low, dark, quietest places in a loud world — where children lie belly-down with ears pressed to the living floor and learn to listen for rot.

Because rot is the predator here.

The listeners are the most honored and the strangest to watch. A girl of seven, dust-lidded, perfectly still for an hour, ear to the Mat. Under the normal crackle-settle, under the goat-cough and beetle-tick and Fork-hum, she is listening for the wrong softness. The too-sweet sour. Water-wake. Black mold that eats both plant and lung, that blooms mint-cold bioluminescence into something wet and green. When she hears it, she does not shout. She marks with ash and hums the pitch of the Fork nearest, and the pruners come with stone knives and glass-box heat and cut and cure and seal, sacrificing a room to save a cluster. Every child will recite it: *Do not bring water. Do not spill, do not cache.* Even their bodies obey — urine baked to dry powder, feces to pellets the beetles will take, breath recaptured. They have made themselves into animals that do not leak.

The pruners — the adults who tend town-as-organism — move like gardeners, like surgeons, like priests. A caravan master reading troughs that heal behind him. A Stalk-comber climbing jointed bamboo twenty meters up to harvest loose bark sheets before the wind does, skittering vast translucent sails down to the looms that weave charge. A Fork-tender with filed teeth — so the hum does not shake them loose — pressing her forehead to black glass to feel if the note has shifted. A goatherd with beak-calluses on his hands from guiding bellows-goats by the shingle-ruff, harvesting their shed plates for roofs.

They do not build. They coax and hollow and cure and prune. A new chamber is not constructed; it is requested. You stake ultrasound singers in a circle for a season until the Mat thickens upward, then hollow the soft heart, then let it harden. When Kettlehollow needs to move, it lets go — seals the throats, leaves the domes to crumble back to crisp, and starts a bruise-trough elsewhere. The Mat forgives. It always forgives, if you wait long enough. Your bootprint remains for weeks as a dark bruise, spores puffing, then knits.

And under it all, the oldest resident: the Mat itself. Meters thick, ridge to basin to town lights, ash-grey and rust-black and sulfur-yellow, felty and papery and brittle. Not a plant the way you mean plant. A valley-sized stomach that etches basalt with acid finer than vinegar and builds glass-hair stone-flesh out of dust and static and low sideways light. It ticks as it cools. It lifts cold mint sparks along its underside when you lift an edge. It allows the Stalks to stand in it like hairs, allows the goats to crop it, allows the town to be a managed infection in its skin — because the Forks tune it and the shepherds sing it and the goats prune it and the people keep water away. A forest flattened to a floor that breathes.

Stand still long enough and your ears adjust to the loud. Roar, rattle, hiss, groan, clatter, hum. Then the sudden drop — wind gone, ears ringing — and the thousand tiny settling cracks, the cough, the tick, the held note. Then pressure-change on your skin, filaments lifting, and the roar returning from kilometers off, rolling toward you across living crisp.

Clip your line. Cover your mouth. Listen for the wrong softness under the right crackle.

You are in a place that lives without drinking, and it is, impossibly, full.
