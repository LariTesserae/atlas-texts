They are not the rock moving. At first that's what it looks like.

Kneel long enough with your face in the hot lift from the fist-mouth, let the red down in the cracks adjust your eyes, and the nap on the stone resolves — not nap, not bristle, but joint. Leg over leg. Antenna over back. Back over back.

They upholster the bubble. That is the only word. They are the wallpaper and the floor and the ceiling hung down.

Thousands of them, shoulder to shoulder with no shoulder-room. Each one the length of the last joint of your finger, black and lacquered like cooling lava itself, so that when they don't move they vanish into stone, and when they do move the whole room seems to crawl.

Take one, if you could isolate one from the mass, hold it — you can't without burning yourself and crushing three others — but imagine it: a cricket pared down to what heat and dark and hunger allow. A hard, varnished cuticle, black to hold and shed heat, seamless enough that not a molecule of water escapes. Because there is no water here to replace it. No seep, no dew. What moisture they ever get they steal.

Under that lacquer, a body built for squeezing. A head low and blunt, with two eyes that still catch red — pinprick eye-shine winking on and off by the hundreds as the mass shifts — though they hardly see. Seeing is useless in sealed-dark. Instead two antennae, absurdly long, pale at the tips from wear, always moving, tasting heat and vibration and each other. Mouthparts built for scraping, not biting green — blunt jaws that can rasp bacterial film off glassy basalt, that can chew stone-dust to get at the film, that can saw through a dead brother's leg joint, that can crack a wind-fallen midge like a nut.

Legs long for their size, jointed high, tibiae ridged like files. The wing-cases are there, shrunken and hard, useless for flying in a room you cannot turn your head in. They are kept for sound. When a thousand tibiae draw across a thousand cases at once it is not a chirp. In that tight stone drum it becomes a dry metallic rasp, continuous, industrial — riveting heard through a wall. Roar, then ticking. That is them resettling.

They live in layers. This is not a swarm, it is a pile that breathes. The ones against the wall bake and feed, scraping sulfur-fixing films, the thin mineral slime that lives on hot air and struck-match fumes, sucking it up with stone-dust. The ones in the middle ride, climb, molt. The ones on top cool slightly and wait for fallout — and it comes: daytime thermals from valleys far below lift tiny midges up, kill them with cold and thin air, and drop them dead down the fist-mouth. A soft rain of bodies from another world. A midge falling here does not land on rock. It lands on hunger. In seconds it is covered, disassembled, passed mouth to mouth.

Nothing green has ever been here. They have never known leaf or lichen. Their whole economy is fallout and chemistry and each other. Shed skins are eaten before they cool. The dead are eaten where they lie, because there is nowhere to drag them and no water to waste on rot. When the wind brings nothing for a long stretch, they chew each other alive — a leg taken here, an antenna there, the mass tolerating it with a ripple. To live shoulder to shoulder is to be food.

And they are not alone on their own bodies. Look closer, in the folds where leg meets body, where antenna meets back: paler, smaller lives. Mites, amber-white, the size of dust, riding the crickets like slow cattle, drinking the film of grease off chitin, sometimes drinking the cricket itself. And in the gaps between bodies, springtails — grey-white flecks that flick and right themselves — working the crumbs, the fungal threads too thin to see, the cricket frass baked instantly to powder. A whole upstairs-downstairs ecology riding on a carpet that is itself alive.

In the dark they navigate by touch and tremor. The mountain exhales underneath them on a long, patient gradient — pressure building far below, releasing up through the bubble in a low hot sigh that lifts the dust and makes the whole pile shiver and resettle like grain on a shaking sieve. They ride it. They read it through their feet. The vibration of hundreds of minute feet through rock is constant, a ticking substrate under the rasp. The dry rubbing of so many chitinous bodies in thin hot air charges them; if you put your forearm near you feel the hairs lift. Now and then, where an antenna brushes cooling basalt, a faint blue spark ticks. They live electrified.

They do not hurry. A cricket here lives long for an insect, molts slowly, lays a few waxy eggs deep in a vesicle where heat is steadiest, and dies into the mass. A nymph hatches paler, softer, almost translucent brown, and darkens as it bakes, as its cuticle tans to black lacquer. Over a season a sulfur crystal the size of a pinhead grows in a cool corner. Over a decade the bubble itself tightens a millimeter as the basalt cools. They accommodate. The ceiling lowers by a leg-joint in a lifetime. They pack tighter.

Block the mouth with your hand and take away the last red thread, and they are still there in absolute black, hot, dry, deafening — a furnace fully occupied, needing no light and no water and no plant and no permission, thousands of warm ticking bodies carrying heat and food from wall to wall simply by crawling over each other, living and dying in shifts, holding the room.
