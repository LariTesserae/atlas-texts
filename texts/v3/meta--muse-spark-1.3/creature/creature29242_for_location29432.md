At first you think nothing lives here.

That is the town's first defense. To be too still to be alive. You stand in the central crossing and the dust is unbroken except for those needle lines going straight from one hollow house to another and gone, and you think: wind made those. It didn't.

Then at the edge of your vision — never in the middle — something tall detaches from a wall and is already somewhere else.

They are six. Perhaps six. You never see them all at once to count, only one here, one impossibly far across the bowl a minute later, moving without disturbing the ripples. They allow you to see them only gradually, the way the dim light allows anything.

A body no bigger than your fist, hung four feet up in the air where no body should be. That is the wrongness that gives them away. The legs account for everything else — stilts, four or six or eight depending on how they fold them, so thin and pale they cast almost no shadow in the grey-blue wash. They don't bend like knees. They jack-knife and telescope. When they stand still they look like a crack in the air. When they walk, the feet — needle points — touch only the crests of the dust waves. That's why their tracks are so straight and so light: they are not walking across the town so much as taking its measure, point to point.

Come closer and they stop. That is their manners. Freezing is their first language.

The body itself is pallid to translucence, waxed over like everything here that wants to keep its inside in. Under the skin you can see the slow dark churn of the gut and, sometimes, a white opacity — grub-fat, not yet rendered into work. No fur, no feather, nothing to catch dust. The head is almost all eye. Not two eyes but a band of them, black and wet-looking in a place where nothing else is wet, faceted to gather the thin evening that never becomes morning. In the shadow of a house-mouth those eyes catch what little light falls and give it back doubled. They have no eyelids to lose moisture through. They clean them with a foreleg, fastidiously, one at a time.

You expect, because they are so leggy, that they will be fragile. They are not.

Watch one tend.

It bends — impossibly, the fist of a body dropping from four feet to inches off the ground on folding stilts without a sound — and puts its mouth to the base of a house. The mouth is not a mouth as you think it. A short, dark slit that unfolds into a soft rasp. It licks. Long, deliberate strokes up the bone-colored wall, harvesting the salt bloom that has sweated out overnight, the fine powder the wind-gutters have sorted and piled for it. Its head bobs as it swallows dry. Nothing here drinks. It eats dryness and makes it into something drier.

Then it works. From a pouch under its thorax it regurgitates a pellet — dust and lichen-meal and crushed salt and its own wax — already warm though the air is cold, and presses it with two forefeet into a pinhole in the wall. Press, turn, smooth. The pellet flattens and pales and becomes wall. Elsewhere on that same wall someone has laid a second skin of pale lattice over a crack, still tacky to your finger. That was one of them, hours ago, standing on its hind stilts to reach, laying down its own secretion thread by thread the way a spider lays silk, only this silk hardens into mineral.

They are masons. They are janitors. A fallen spire lies across one avenue, neatly hollowed from the inside out, its material gone in beakful-sized pellets to patch a leaning sister ten yards away. They do not build new so much as keep old from unbuilding. They comb the wire-grass tufts for seed, plucking with a sudden delicacy, storing the black grains in cheek sacs. They nudge the slow tumble-thorns onward when one gets caught against a house-mouth, because a stopped thorn doesn't comb static and static is part of the mortar — the dust needs its charge to stick.

They are also the only death in the bowl that moves quickly.

You will watch a tender stand utterly still beside a ripple for five minutes, ten, head cocked so the band of eyes catches different light. Nothing moves. Then the whole animal unhinges. Two front stilts that were legs are suddenly spears; they go through the dust ripple and come up with something writhing without sound: a grub, blind, fat, white as candle wax, as thick as your thumb, its mouth still full of chewed foundation-fiber. It had been eating the house from underneath. The strider lifts it, folds it, eats it whole. No struggle you can hear. The walls nearest drink even that tiny vibration. The strider resumes its height, cleans its eyes, and goes back to pasting pellets over the very hole the grub made. To tend and to prey are the same gesture here.

That is the whole accounting: dust feeds the grey ash-lichen, lichen feeds the grub in the dark under the pan, grub feeds the strider, strider — when it slows and its wax dulls and its stilts begin to tremble — feeds the house. You will find them sometimes if you look into the base cracks: a curled pallid husk walled in neatly, legs folded like a closed umbrella, already filmed over with new secretion. Not burial. Ingredient. Salt and wax and a little protein to stiffen a rib. The house that eats a tender breathes colder out of its mouth for a day, out of sync with the others.

They do not speak. Put your head in a house-mouth and whisper and the ribbed walls drink it — they were made to drink sound. So the tenders have learned not to waste it. When two meet in an avenue too wide for either of them — avenues made for something larger that is gone, something you could lie down across and not touch — they do not touch. They stop at distance and vibrate one stilt against the packed ground, so faint you feel it in your teeth like the creak of the houses when the cold tightens them a millimeter. A code in shiver. Then they separate on straight lines.

They sleep — if it is sleep — standing inside the mouths, bodies tucked up into the cold exhalation, legs folded around them, eyes open and dark. The houses breathe over them, one after another, out of sync, like sleepers in a ward. In that moment you understand: the tenders are not inhabitants the way you are an inhabitant of a house. They are what the town uses for hands now that the bodies it was shed for are gone.

And the town is still making hands, still making houses. Near the center, in a crack in the fired-clay pan, that thumb-sized bud, already hollow, already ribbed inside, faintly warm to the touch. In a week it will be knee-high. In a year, taller than you, with its own dark oval mouth breathing cold. No one will ever live in it. That doesn't matter. The six will lick its salt, patch its tears, spear the grubs that come to undermine it, and when one of them dies, wall him into its base to make it stronger.

To live here is to keep sealed and to keep sealing.
