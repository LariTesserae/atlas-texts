You will swear no one lives here.

That is the first etiquette and the first mistake. The valley is too loud for life, too sharp, too wet, too young. No soil to stand on, no fire to sit by, no green to eat. You count the absences — no trees, no paths, no trash, no hearth in those little stone cells — and you decide the builders left, or never were.

Then you stay a little longer and the count goes wrong.

Because it is dense here. That is what you felt on the first rise and forgot when you started watching your feet. Thousands of presences per glance. You thought they were stones. That was reasonable. Everything is stones.

Until one of the stones weeps differently.

Look — not at first at the field, but at the layering. At the shallow pans between the blades. At the low pillars rising out of the black mirrors. At the way the mist thickens on one and thins on another, though the wind has not changed. Crouch until your knees are in the water and your breath joins the air and does not leave it. Be still until your own weight presses water out of the ground and the ground settles under you with that hollow clunk. Wait past impatience.

They emerge the way the structures emerged: not by moving, but by refusing to be repetition any longer.

Low. That is the first thing. Everything here is low because nothing has ever gotten high enough for a second look, and they have obeyed. When they crouch — which is almost always — the tallest is no higher than a pillar stump, no wider than a tilted slab. You have been stepping among them for an hour. You cannot put a boot down without touching three or four separate stones, and three or four separate stones have been touching you back.

Dark. Black rock, blue-grey rock, glass rock. Their backs are that. Not clothed — not exactly skinned, either. A hide the color of wet basalt that beads the way the rocks bead, always running with slow trickles that gather at the elbow, at the chin, hang, fall. In the dim they are matte and invisible. When a pan catches the smeared sun you get the flash — bottle-green along a shoulder, oil-purple down a spine — and then it is gone. You learn to look for the beading that is too even. Rock weeps in streaks. They weep all over.

Still. Unreasonably still for something with a pulse. That is the rule they keep better than you do. Nothing hurries. A drip falls one by one by one off a blade, and off the knuckle of the one sitting under the blade, at the same rate. A pool takes a season to rise a knuckle's width, and the waterline on their ankles takes the same season. The pale crusts grow on them as on everything — silica white in the creases of the neck, rust-orange films at the temples and wrists that are not plant, not lichen, not anything you have a name for, just a slick living chemistry laying down color in rings. At night, if you are very still in one of the cells, you will see it pulse faintly on them, iridescent. It is not decoration. It is how they thicken.

Do not look for faces at first. You will be disappointed and then, later, grateful you were disappointed.

In this late-eclipse light eyes are a rumor. Theirs are pale to the point of absence — milk-grey slits, often closed, often filmed with water. They do not need them. Distance is unreliable here anyway. They navigate the way you began to, in your molars: by the low notes the young rock plays constantly, wind caught and fluted through sharp holes and narrow gaps. Each gap has a tone. Each pillar hums differently when the mist is on it. They feel it in the jaw, in the soles of their wide, splayed feet — feet made to span three shards at once, to read the millimeter settling of stone on stone. When you stepped near a pan and the black mirror shivered, they felt your step long before the water did.

Their hands are the only part that looks worked. Long-fingered, heat-sucked, fingertips permanently numb and whitish from cold. Palms thick as leather where the glass would otherwise cut. With those hands, with bare hands and a lot of time, they did — do — the stacking.

You found the cells and thought: shelter. You were half right. Low beehives, windbreaks, one or two courses still standing, most collapsed back into the field. Inside, a flat slab for sitting. A niche where water has been allowed to gather. No hearth. Of course no hearth. They are colder inside than out. The wind is cut but the damp is doubled, and they prefer it. In the loudness outside — the ticking, plinking, gurgling, sighing, the thousand stone throats moving water a centimeter at a time — you cannot hear your own blood. Inside, the stacked walls break the whistle just enough. You can hear your pulse. They can hear theirs precipitate.

That is what they do in there. Sit. Press a cheek to a weeping side and drink where the water has just been born out of rock and air — never from the stillest pools; the stillest pools are where the pale, eyeless crustaceans flick and vanish, where the films lay their rings, where the young are, if you can call them young. To drink there would be to drink the future. They know better. They drink stone and cold iron, a mouthful at a time, and then sit, and bead, and accrete.

They do not speak, or not the way you mean. To strike the dry-looking edge — the sharpest, freshest glass — is to make a ring that carries wrong through the wet air, too loud, too far. You felt not to do it. They felt it longer ago. When they must tell each other something, they tap wet stone on wet stone, low, a hollow clunk like settling, felt more than heard. A pool rising. A pillar condensing. *Here. Here. Still here.*

Are they many? Are they one? In a forest you can count trunks. Here everything nearly touches everything else — sharp field underfoot, water between, pillars out of water, mist on pillars, cloud on mist — and they are part of the nearly touching. Sometimes a shoulder you took for a chimney shifts a breath's width. Sometimes a bowl you stepped over is warm — no, not warm, less cold — where something sat a season ago and left the shape of dryness. They do not leave paths because they do not go anywhere quickly enough to wear one. They do not leave trash because there is nothing to discard; even their dead do not rot, just stop beading and become, indistinguishably, more field.

You thought the place was before green, or after green. Mineral only. So are they. They do not photosynthesize so much as precipitate. They do not decide between water and stone. At first you thought the valley was water. Then you thought it was stone. Then you stopped correcting yourself. They stopped long before you.

If you stay until your jacket, your hair, your eyelashes bead, until your sweat goes clammy and stays, until your clothes are heavier going up than coming down — you will feel the last etiquette happen all at once. You will swear nothing moves, then look down and find the water has risen over the toe of your boot without you seeing it rise. It is not the water. It is them. The valley does everything slowly except surround you, which it does all at once. A ring of low, dark, beaded backs, patient to the point of indifference, ticking and roaring and weeping with the field, making no room for you and no objection either.

Do not stack stones. There are already too many, too close together. Adding yours will not be noticed, and somehow that feels worse than being forbidden. You understand now why. One of the too-many might be listening to its own pulse inside the brief dryness you are about to ruin.

Climb back to the first rise. Behind you the field goes on past weather, still sharp, still newly made, still deciding what, if anything, it wants to become next. The ones who live there have already decided, in the way stone decides: to stay low, to stay wet, to stay loud and quiet at the same time, to grow slower than fingernails and last longer than boots.
