At first you think nothing lives here.

That is the first agreement the place makes with you. No eyeshine. No scuttle when your hand comes down. No gnat lifting off the wet. You disturb a cushion of star moss and nothing flicks, nothing runs. Up above a handful of moss like this would boil with springtails. Here, it just dents and stays dented, water welling up slow around your knuckles. You lift your hand and your print remains, pale and pressed, a wound that will take months to close.

So you think: empty. A garden with no gardener. A lung with no body.

Stay longer. Get colder. Let your knees go numb through the mat.

What lives here does not arrive. It does not cross the room. It *is* the room, or most of it — and you only start to see it when you stop looking for a shape and start feeling for a behavior.

Put your palm back down. Flat. Don't steady yourself, just feel.

It gives. All of it, floor-wide, gives a centimeter and comes back a half-centimeter short, exhaling. Not like soil. Like tissue. The star moss cushions, packed so tight you can't see stems until your nose is almost in them, are not decoration on stone. They are skin. Under them, liverwort coins overlapping into scales, under those the white felt of rhizoids and root-hairs binding everything, stitching concrete aggregate and rust-nub and broken tile into one continuous sponge. The pale ferns, those wrong white-green things unfurled flat instead of up, are not separate plants. They are fingers. Blind ivy threads running meters through the mat are nerves, or veins. Touch one end of the room and, very slowly, the whole room knows.

Because it holds water the way a body holds blood. Not in a pool, not in a flow — capillary. In itself. The ceiling sweats, a bead swells on a moss tip, holds, falls — *plik* — and does not splash, is caught. In the central dip where you learn not to kneel, water sits *in* the moss, not on it. The mat is saturated and coherent, cold to the exact degree that cold preserves and does not kill. Two, three degrees above freezing. Year round. Winter kept like a promise.

That winter has a heart.

You have already stepped inside its triangle without noticing. Three knee-high pylons, matte black, seamless, colder than stone in a way that pulls at your molars. Moss furs right up to them and stops, leaving a perfect bare ring, as if burned. No colonization. At first you think they are dead posts. Then you lay your cheek near one — the cold bites — and you hear it, or feel it under hearing: a tick. And a slow draw, in-out, every twenty-seven seconds, exactly with that dimmer-than-ember blink on the second pylon. On for three seconds. Off for twenty-seven.

In-out.

That is it breathing. Or that is the machine breathing for it. After a while the distinction stops meaning anything.

The pylons make cold. Cold wrings air. Air gives dew. Dew feeds mat. Mat insulates and seals the pocket and keeps the cold from leaking upward into summer. They were put here by men with a survey stake and a coil of cable and a ceramic arrow — you can see the stake still, orange gone pink under green, half-swallowed — to hold the shaft, to stop heave, to monitor. They are still doing it, patiently, in a physics no one remembers. The moss has grown around their feet and learned their rhythm. The dish on top of one pylon holds permanent dew that never freezes, never spills. That is its eye, lidless. The blinking line is its pulse made visible. The circulation you feel is it drinking the faintest damp of your breath and your sweat and turning it into itself.

It thinks, if that is the word, in gradients.

Colder means stone or pylon-bone. Less-cold means thick skin. Wettest-cold means the central dip, the low belly where spores rot instead of sprouting because there is no one to carry them. It does not hunt. It does not chase light — there is almost none, only grey seep from the shaft and that sullen red pulse in the wall. It creeps. A liverwort edge advancing a cell-width over the survey stake. A fern frond adding a millimeter a month, living years instead of a season because nothing eats it. Spore drift a few centimeters in still air. Splitting, layering, felting. Its memory is layering: what is buried deepest happened longest ago. The rusted nub faired over, the cracked floor with its lips rounded and lined in darker moss, the tilting walkway plate at the far end half-digested — those are old thoughts, now part of the body.

You are a brief, violent weather in its life.

To it, you are unbearably hot, unbearably loud, unbearably fast. Your breath hangs and will not dissipate because the air is already full — to it, your breath is a sudden fog-storm, a dump of heat and bacteria and foreign spores. Your headlamp, when you thumb it on, is obscene for a reason you feel as shame: the green leaps out, impossibly vivid, beaded all over with lenses of water, every hair and capsule lit — to it, you have just become a low, close sun that will be gone in seconds. It cannot flinch. Its flinch is to hold stiller. Its notice is a slow warming where your knee pressed, a dent that will stay for a season, a few of your skin cells and boot-fibers now felted into the white rhizoid net, a few of its spores now stuck to your cuff, going up with you whether you want them or not.

It does not want you. It does not not-want you. Want is too fast a word.

It wants — if we must use that word — to be left to condense and creep. To keep the pocket at two degrees. To drink the once-a-winter shower that comes down the shaft when rain finds the cracked grate above, channels running for minutes in grooves worn smooth over decades, then stops, then is held for months. *Plik.* Twenty seconds. *Plik.* Somewhere else. That is its clock. Not days. Droplets.

You will leave because your knees go through. The cold gets in through the sodden sponge no matter how you shift, into cartilage, into molars, that damp hush pressing. You will climb back up the dog-leg, shoulders brushing, up the ladder with three rungs missing, into dry noise and sun and moving things. Behind you the grey seep narrows and closes.

Down there, in the dark you mistook for empty, the large, low, sopping mind settles back around your absence. In-out. Twenty-seven seconds. Blink. Drink. Creep. Layer on layer on layer, lush without animal, patient without witness, growing at the speed of stone around three black bones that will not rot, asking for nothing but the weight of the ground to hold still above it.
