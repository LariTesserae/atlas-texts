They are not people in a city. They are the soft part of the city.

That is what you understand first when you are in it up to the ankles, being carried. Nothing here has the room to be alone, so nothing is alone. A human is a knot where a lot of traffic pauses. A dog is an employee. A gull is a street sweeper. A crab is a pedestrian. You stop trying to count them as individuals because the density won't let you. Shoulder to shoulder, balcony to balcony, boat to boat, ledge to ledge — life here is a reef that learned to shout.

The humans are the loudest animals, because they have to be.

You hear them before you can separate them. They do not speak, they project. Four languages through cone megaphones, fishmongers slapping tails on stone to punctuate a price, porters calling *mind the rope mind the rope*, children screaming with delight in the gutter-brown water, old women laughing with their whole chests while their short knives flick oyster after oyster open, lovers yelling at each other nose to nose and thinking it is tender. Nobody whispers. Whispering was selected out generations ago by engines and gulls and the foundation thump in the sternum and the harvester drone in the teeth. To be heard you sing, you bellow, you drum on an oil drum, you join the rooftop brass band that is always practicing and never quite together. Even their bodies have turned up the volume — broad chests, thick necks, hands that slap and clap.

Look at their skin and you see the place has cured them like it cures everything else. No one is dry. Feet are always wet, white-pruned at the toes and then calloused black on the soles from hot basalt and salt and crab claws. Ankles and calves are tide-marked with salt rime and small nicks and old eel bites. Shoulders and backs shine with sweat and harvester rain and canal splash; shirts — when they wear them — are singlets and sailcloth patches that never quite dry, so most just go bare and let the laundry rot on the line above with the drying nets and the cages of live crabs. Hair is stiff with salt and static, standing up at noon when the charge builds under the harvester fields. Old men's beards hold mother-of-pearl scale. Old women's forearms are mapped with thin white scars from oyster knives and hot oil. Children's knees are scabbed perpetually from lock stairs. They ground themselves with copper bracelets, with a chain to the awning, the way you would ground a machine, because in a way they are part of the machine.

They move the way water moves. That is the rule: keep moving or be moved. So they never quite stand still. Porters trot with impossible loads balanced on the head, shouting for path and getting it. Welders in patched masks crouch on pontoons of oil drums and grown coral, showering sparks into the canal while eels gather below to watch the light. Water-taxi drivers stand at the stern with one bare foot on the tiller, yelling destinations, bumping gunwale to gunwale without anger. Hawkers assemble a whole shop at dawn from poles and tarps and plastic sheets older than they are, sell until the tide takes the lower arcade, then hoist the whole inventory a meter on pulleys, sit with their feet in warm seawater, and keep selling. Cage-menders, pump-minders, lime-burners stacking sweet-rotting oyster shell, printers crawling a gantry over a slumped wall to extrude fresh reef-concrete still steaming. And between the work, fishing: everywhere fishing. Boys with magnets on strings pulling nails and coins and lost tools out of the brown-green. Girls with handlines. Grandfathers throwing cast nets off sky-bridges. Everyone eats what the water brings up.

They are amphibious without having grown gills. They can sleep sitting up in a boat, in a hammock strung under a pressure tube that goes *thup-thup-thup* all night with grain and mail, on a balcony with ducks paddling in the courtyard pump-puddle below. They nap in shifts because there isn't room to lie down all at once. They pray in shifts too — a shrine wedged between a pump station and a cage-mender, incense fighting diesel and fish blood and frying oil, someone always leaving a crab claw, a coin, a lit stub. They don't pretend to understand the deep engines thumping in the drowned bedrock to keep the plate from liquefying, or the lift-rafts shimmering like a mirage that forgot to go away, or why your shadow lags half a second behind you under a harvester field. They just live around the wrongness the way you live around a reef at low tide. Copper-chain the awning. Don't cut through that alley at noon when the canal water starts climbing the lock walls and beading. Cover the copper when it rains over the fish market even if your own street stays dry. Repair, patch, bail, grow a pontoon where a barge sank. They are barnacles on a god-machine, and proud of it.

And they are not alone in being barnacles.

The dogs live here, really live here, not as pets. Packs of lean, short-coated, yellow and black curs trot the market aisles with the confidence of employees, barking from the chest — a bark you feel in your ribs more than hear — cleaning up fish guts, herding children away from barge ropes, sleeping curled in warm puddles in the middle of traffic while everyone steps over. The rats live here along the culverts, sleek and bold, swimming like otters when the gutters run ankle-deep. The crabs live here — red and blue-black armies that at low tide climb the lock stairs until the steps themselves seem to move, that tip baskets with a clatter of claws on tile and send kids shrieking after them, that live in cages on every balcony waiting for the pot.

Above them the birds. You cannot think of this place without the weight and scream of them. A hundred thousand gulls, white and grey, boiling off the condenser pylons, landing on fresh reef-concrete while it's still warm to steal the heat, screaming with a cut that goes straight through engine roar because evolution here is acoustic. Cormorants black as oil holding their wings out to dry on bridge stanchions like laundry. Swifts cutting all day through columns of insects that rise off wet stone — wasps and flies and moths on every ledge, in every warm updraft, unavoidable as humidity. Ducks, fat domestic ducks, paddling in street gutters with ducklings in single file behind a porter’s cart as if they too have errands.

Below them the water-people. Mullet and eels and silver schools that turn as one body under bridges, fattened on market wash and welding light and warm rain. They gather when sparks fall. They scatter when a barge shoulders through. Children know all their shadows by name.

Even at night — and you can feel it coming even in this white-yellow glare — they don't leave. The bacterial mats in the culverts glow faintly green-gold, the navigation lights and the welding torches take over from the sun, the sound deepens but never drops. Someone is still shouting, someone is still shucking, a dog barks, gulls settle screaming onto hovering platforms, crabs keep climbing. A warm rain begins to fall out of a clear sky, hissing on ten million awnings, and no one looks up. A crab sidles over your foot. A boy shouts to mind the rope. You lift your feet and keep moving, because you are soft, too, and this is a mineral world, and the only way to live here is to stay animal — wet, loud, shoulder to shoulder, and always, always in motion.
