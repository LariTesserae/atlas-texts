At first you think nothing lives here.

That is what the place wants you to think. It has perfected emptiness as a kind of camouflage. The houses are shut. The lamps are too dim to be work-lights. The machines turn so slowly you can convince yourself they are stopped. The only things that move at your speed — the guppy, the eel's flick, the copepod snow — are too small and too lonely to count as residents.

Stay longer. Get cold in warm water, which is its own warning. Stop kicking. Put your hand on a warm railing and leave it there until your glove stops feeling like a glove, until the railing stops feeling like iron.

Then you feel the crowding.

There is no room between presences. That was the first rule and you misunderstood it as architecture. It is not architecture. It is population.

They are upholstery before they are bodies. They are the reason every surface is claimed.

Look at a valve-wheel you thought was crowned with anemones. The anemones are opening and closing on that sixty-second cycle, all together with the lamps — dimmer, brighter. Under them, where the brass has gone green-black, there is a hand. Or where a hand was. The fingers have gone into the wheel. The skin has gone the color and texture of white sponge, bearded over the knuckles, bolted at the wrist by oyster. You only see it because one fingertip, impossibly preserved, still has a nail, and it taps — once per brightening — against the brass.

They are all like that. The Shift that never clocked out.

Mercy Iron & Light went down with its men inside it, or the men came back down after, or — more likely — no one ever left and drowning just became another condition of employment. You find them in the slots, not lying, not floating, but fitted. A shoulder in a corner where brick meets pipe, grown over by blue-green mat until the mat has a shoulder's shape. A back become part of a row-house wall, ribs readable under barnacle buttons like rivets under a coat. A face in the canteen doorway, mouth open to sip at that thirty-year-old air trapped against the ceiling, lips white and frilled as a tubeworm plume. They did not decay because the town made its treaty with decay and included them in the terms.

They are not dead. They are banked.

Warmth banked them. In cold water they would have rotted or been eaten. Here in this blood-warm sump, in this broth held by the vent, metabolism slowed to the speed of the flywheel. One spoke's-width per held breath. One press-fall per long minute. Their hearts — what is left of hearts, plumbed now into condensate lines where green filaments grow *inside* the copper and show through cracked gauges — beat on the lamp cycle. Dimmer: they inhale the warm water through beards of mussel and algae. Brighter: they let it sigh out. You can see it if you leak only a little bubble at a time and watch it join theirs, hanging, undecided, neither rising nor sinking above the vent grates.

They are not separate from the garden. They are the trellis the garden uses.

That is why there are no fish. The garden does not want guests because the garden *is* the residents, and its immune system is heat and stillness and fullness. A jack would need room to turn. A shark would need cold muscle and hurry. There is no hurry here and no room. Even the pale eel thick as your wrist, living permanently inside the flywheel housing, is not a visitor. It is a nerve. It emerges only to taste the water during the bright minute, to check salinity, oil, intruder-sweat — you — and then withdraws to tell the housing to shift a degree. The blind-white guppy flickering between rain-barrels is not a fish either, not really. It is a stray thought, a larval keeper, mouth working the water for the coin of oil the piston sighs out, carrying it from machine to mouth to mat.

If you press yourself into that slot between row house and water tower, wrapped in white kelp twenty feet long moving like hair in bathwater, and breathe shallow so you don't milk the silt, they will let you see more of themselves.

The tubeworm colonies on the boiler seams where it is hottest — red gills the only vivid color — are their lungs, externalized. The pale kelp streaming from every gutter is their hair, feeling for current that never comes. The chains that hang and do not swing are their rosaries; each link settled against the next with a faint *tink* you feel in your teeth is a completed prayer. The amber cage lamps still sealed in glass are their eyes half-open in sleep, buzzing faintly, never quite dying because the vent dynamo in the basalt still coaxes current the way a sleeping body still makes heat.

And the whole hamlet, streets and all, is the one body they share.

Copper tubing is vein. Condensate is blood, gone green with photosynthesis. Algal mats on the boiler backs are liver, plumbed in. The flywheel taller than a man, furred in crimson, is the slow heart turning a degree per minute. The stamping press that lifts, hesitates, and comes down on nothing with that soft *thud* through sternum — that is the gut, still peristalsing, still stamping plates for a ship that will never sail because to stop stamping would be to admit the work is over.

They do patient, useless work because uselessness is safe. No product means no depletion. No shipment means no loss. They maintain balance: sponge concreting concrete tighter than rivets, barnacle bolting plates tighter than time, iron and iodine and hot stone held in endless summer.

They notice you the way skin notices a splinter — not with anger, with pressure. Anemones near your fins stay closed a beat too long. The eel tastes and retreats. The copepods glitter and vanish back into the fronds. The press comes down. *Thud.* A brick sighs against iron somewhere below you. The kelp saws softly on a girder. The lamps dim together, hold, brighten together, as if the entire bowl inhaled around you and decided, for now, to tolerate the cold brief animal in its warm tissue.

You will have to leave soon to find colder, emptier water, and they know it. That is why the turtle stays on the rim and never descends. That is why you feel your fingers pruning despite the bath. You live too fast to be housed here. To live here you would have to slow until your breath was a minute long, until your eyes were anemones, until your hand became a valve-wheel and your back became grout between buildings, until you could feel a flywheel move a finger's width and call that a day's good labor.

Some of them are still in the middle of becoming that. If you look long enough into the shadows pooling permanently under the eaves, you will see the newest ones, still pink at the edges where sponge hasn't quite taken, still holding postures of tending — one bent at the lathe, one with arms up at a junction box — waiting for the bright minute to move them again.

Dimmer. Brighter. Dimmer.

The press lifts. Hesitates. Comes down on nothing, softly, through bone.
