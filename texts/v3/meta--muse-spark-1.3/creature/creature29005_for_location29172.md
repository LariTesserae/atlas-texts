They do not stand out at first.

That is the first law of living here. From a mile out you don't see animals at all. You see ground. Cobbles. Pebbles. A dark, wet texture that shivers when wind crosses the sheet-water. Then you come closer and the texture breathes.

Then you are in it, knee-deep in supercooled slush, and there is no ground left. Only bodies.

This is not a herd in the way grasslands have herds, with space between and sky above. This is a tiling. A packing. Shoulder to shoulder, flank to flank, back to back, for acres and acres until individual voices dissolve half a mile out into one massed works-hum. You cannot lift a foot without setting it down on someone. You cannot lean without leaning on something warm that leans back. And no one moves away. Space has been abandoned as a concept here. Everything spreads out, nothing grows up.

Stay still and let your eyes learn to separate them. They separate slowly, like cooling iron showing its grain.

The most numerous are the low ones. The boilers.

Barrel-bodied, the size of oil drums laid on their sides, low enough that their bellies almost drag in the black water. Six short legs, not legs really but pistons — you can see the rods slide, bright then oil-dark, in slick sheaths at knee and hip. They move with a chuff-chuff-chuff, a two-stroke pump-heart you feel more in your teeth than hear. Their heads are down almost always, noses plowing the rust seeps, the iron-red weeps where stone sweats black water. They lick stone clean.

If one splits beside you in the cold — and they do split, plates buckling with a metallic groan — do not look for guts. There are no soft guts. Inside is chambered, dark red and soot black: firebox, smelt-stomach, bellows-lung wheezing open and shut. Cold mineral and cold water go in. Heat comes out. Along their spines are short stacks, three, sometimes five, and from them they do not exhale breath. They exhale exhaust. Great white plumes that in this cold do not rise but spill sideways, heavy and low, to join the fog that never lifts. The air around them smells of hot iron and sulfur and wet hide and ozone.

They shift constantly. Never traveling, just shuffling. A foot lifted to let water pass underneath. A ton of weight rolled from left pistons to right. Predator and prey trading places in slow rotation. The whole plain rings when they do it at once, like a shunting yard — metal on stone squeal, water sloshing under ten thousand feet at once.

Above them, stepping through them like needles through cloth, are the waders.

Tall, too tall for this flat place, on stilt legs that bend wrong and then correct with hydraulic delicacy. Their bodies are small, tight, featherless, wrapped in iron hide beaded with condensate. Their heads are mostly antennae, long whips that taste the fog. They pick their way foot by foot between the boilers, placing each foot in the half-second gap where another foot just was. Between their antennae blue sparks crawl and snap, St. Elmo's fire that never goes out. They feed finer than the boilers — not stone, but what lives in the stain on stone, the oily blue-purple bacterial films that spread across the slabs and are not plants and never needed sun. Their beaks comb it up, and their throats glow faintly as they swallow.

Lower still, almost below notice until one moves against your leg, are the draggers.

Vast slugs armored in boiler plate, ten, fifteen feet long, no legs at all. They haul themselves through the slush by peristalsis, plate lifting and settling, plate lifting and settling, leaving behind a polished wake of pressed ice and water that other things drink from. You hear them before you feel them: a long wet hiss of weight. They are furnaces that have decided to lie down. Their sides radiate a deep, dull heat you can feel through your trousers. The young ride on them sometimes, hairless and pink-hot, glowing faintly at the seams where their plates haven't closed yet, huddled into the warmth.

And then the huddles that click.

You will hear them on the wind before you find them, a dry chitter like static. Things like penguins made of soot and copper wire, round and black and shivering in thousands. They have no stacks, no great fire. They keep heat by keeping each other, pressed so tight their wire-filament coats tangle and pop with static discharge. When two huddles press flank to flank a lace of white-blue runs across wet backs, illuminating the steam from inside for a second. They click to stay synchronized — click-click-click — so the shivering becomes one shiver, more efficient than ten thousand separate ones.

These are the ones that eat heat and make heat. But cold punishes waste, so nothing is allowed to cool alone.

The hunters do not chase. There is nowhere to chase to.

They are lean, low, eight-legged things the color of quenched steel, that flow between the boilers like oil between barrels. Their mouths do not open up and down. They open sideways, furnace doors hinged at the cheek, and inside is white-hot throat. They feed by pressing. They lay that white heat against a boiler's side and hold on, hissing, steaming, while the plates buckle and soften. The boiler does not run. It chuffs harder, stokes harder, trying to out-produce the loss. Sometimes it does. Sometimes it doesn't. When it doesn't, it slows, its stack plume thins, its pistons stutter — chuff ... chuff ... stop — and then the cloud descends.

The strippers. The crab-moths. They come as a chittering veil, thousands of jointed, shell-backed things with wings too cold to fly far, so they hop and flutter and crawl in a living blanket. They do not eat flesh. They strip waste heat before the cold can steal it, clustering so thick on a fall that the fall seems to move again, twitching with their weight. When they lift, minutes later, there is only cold plate and cold stone and black water filling in, and the heat has been carried away in ten thousand tiny bodies to be breathed out elsewhere.

All of them make their own light because dusk is not enough to live by. Between the upright horns of the larger bull boilers, sparks gather and spit. Across any wet back that touches another wet back, static walks. The newborns glow.

Ah — the newborns. Watch the steam long enough.

Steam hangs in this cold. It thickens if you stare. It takes on weight. Out of everyone's breath, out of the low cloud that is everyone's breath, a shape condenses: legs first, then back, then eyes opening like coals fanned. A calf of fog that shudders, drips, clanks once, and walks away on unsteady pistons to join the herd. Nothing was made here. Everything was precipitated, like salt from too much water, like steam from too much breath.

And that is why the numbers don't work. Why that hollow between two slabs that should hold six bodies holds twenty. Why the steaming back you were watching blinks and becomes two of itself, slightly out of phase, one a half-second ahead in its chuff. The whistle you hear arrives before the throat that makes it moves.

They are packed not only in space but in time. The same beast occupies the same slush several times over, overlapping itself, phasing through its neighbor at the shoulder with a spark and a wet shudder and keeping on. Nothing ever leaves, so time piles up. Past chuffs not quite gone when present chuffs arrive — that is the loudness, loudness piled on loudness. Predator and prey can be the same individual, minutes apart. The hunter pressing its throat to a boiler is warming the calf it was, or feeding the strippers it will become.

Do not look for edge or source. There is none. They touch because touching is staying warm. They keep moving because stopping is freezing. Heat is food, food is heat. Keep moving or give your heat to someone who is.

Stay long enough and your eyelashes ice, your breath joins the low cloud, your footsteps fill with black water behind you, and your own joints start to ache in rhythm with the pistons around you — chuff, hiss, step.

You understand then why nothing green ever needed to come here. Iron and water and cold and endless, overlapping life were already enough to keep the works running without builders, without end.
