At first you think nothing lives here.

That is what the flat wants you to think. It is too loud, too wet, too crowded stone to stone to leave room for a body. You walk all day and see only water moving. Then you kneel, because your feet are wet and your molars ache from the roar, and you look properly at the seam between three egg-stones — and the seam looks back.

Not with eyes. Nothing here wastes eyes.

Life here is low. Everything that survived learned the first lesson of the peneplain: do not stand up. Hug the wet. Become stone-shaped and stone-paced, and let the rain hammer over you.

The ground itself is the first living thing.

Those sheets of algae — olive, blood-red, black — stretched like wet leather across the cobbles, slippery under your boot, tearing when you slip and already healed by evening. That is not regrowth. That is crawling. Each sheet is a skin, a colony, millions of mouths wide and one cell thick, drinking light through gauze and drinking iron straight out of stone-sweat. In rain-pit white they go dull and pocked. In the hour of calm between rains they oil over, go glossy, green visibly from the edges inward as they eat sun-scald. When a surge wells up from below and clouds the pool brown they lift at the corners, ripple without wind, trap silt under themselves to make the only soil there is. Saltwort roots into them. Black rush roots into them. Crabs graze them. If you press your ear close to a big sheet at night, when the amber has drained to iron blue, you can hear it faintly fizz, breathing out the rotten-egg breath of the mud domes.

Where silt lodges, the grazers come.

The white crab, blind, big as a hand, wedged between cobbles. There are millions of them, you just learn to see one at a time. No color left in them because color is a rumor here, like elevation. White as boiled bone. No eyes, only smooth sockets polished shut like the stones around them. They live face-down in the hairline seams, fanning mud with mouthparts too fast to see, filtering what the algae leaves. They navigate entirely by knock. You hear, if you lie flat, under the white roar: the click and clack of stones settling microscopically. That is partly water jacking the stones. That is partly them. They lift, tilt, roll a cobble a centimeter with their backs and let it settle, and read the ring it makes in the pool next to it. Each pool rings a little different depending on how deep the mud is below. The whole flat to them is a map of tones. When the roar goes higher, thinner — when water is rising from below and the pavement is about to lift and float — they wedge deeper, lock their legs around two stones at once and go rigid. You can pick one up in that moment and it will not move. It has become a clasp, holding the world together.

What eats the crabs does not walk. It stands.

The wader, impossibly tall and grey, standing in a pan a half-kilometer off, so still you think it is a post. It is not a bird, though from far away grief makes you call it that. Birds need dry and edge and height, and there is none here. It is a stilt-thin hunter built out of patience and mirror.

It is grey the exact grey-green of rain on stone. Its legs are jointed the wrong way twice, so that when it stands its body never bobs with wind or rain-pit. Its head, if it has a head, is just a continuation of neck, a smudge that never resolves. It has no eyes you can find. It hunts reflections.

In dead calm the pools go glassy and show a sky that is not the sky — clearer, darker, with stars at noon, or with two smeared suns. Your own reflection is half a second late. If you wave, it waves after you have lowered your hand. The stilt feeds on that delay. It stands over a mirror-pan and waits, utterly still, until a crab un-wedges below, until an algae sheet fizzes, until your own double moves — and then it stabs downward, too fast for rain to pit. That is why when you blink it is gone, with no splash, no wingbeat. It was never standing where you thought. The wet air lenses distance. A cairn an hour away can be ten minutes away. The stilt uses it. It stands in one pan and strikes into another. Sometimes you find a pan suddenly clouded brown with nothing standing near it, and a single tall print filling with water at its rim. That was where it really was.

There are very few of them because there needs to be very few. One is enough to make a hundred days of walking feel watched.

Under all of it, turning in its sleep, is the thing you never see.

At dusk you hear it: a deep, hollow *bloop* from flooded cavities under the pavement, as if something large turned over. Mud boils up between stones in slow domes that burst with a plop and release warm rotten-egg air, then sink. Sometimes the pavement lifts and floats for a moment on its cushion of mud — stones, pools, algae and all — and then settles a handspan sideways with a long grinding sigh you feel through your soles.

That is not geology. That is breathing.

Below the lag, a finger's width below the world, is not ground but a sea: saturated silt and black water with no light and no roar, only pressure and low thunder transmitted through stone. And in it lives the Turner, the Sigher. Vast, flat, soft, as wide as a village pan and as thin as the water table will allow. It has to be flat. Anything tall would break the pavement. So it spread. A mantle, a ray, a liver-brown mat of muscle lined with three-lobed cilia and mouths underneath, creeping through mud, filtering everything the crabs miss, exhaling gas that becomes the domes. When heat expands the stones by day and cool contracts them by night, it feels the tick upward through its back and shifts to stay in the loosest place. Over a year that centimeter becomes a meter. Whole fields of cobbles creep because it crept under them.

It is the maker of the old footprints. Three-toed and wide as dinner plates, appearing in soft silt after rain and washing out before you can follow them. They are not pressed from above. They are pressed from below, when the Turner pushes three of its stiff feeding lobes up against the underside of a soft spot, testing thickness, looking for a place to vent or to drink rain. From above it looks like something heavy walked there moments ago. Nothing did. Something heavy slept there, underneath.

The storms that are born in the middle of the flat and die in the middle, having never seen a coast — those are its weather too. The dust and spray and low cloud that braid into a water-spout walking slowly across the mirrors, trailing darkness. The air crowds droplet to droplet until there is no empty air between. That column follows the Turner’s heat as it moves.

And then there are the stackers. The something like people.

You have already walked past a dozen of them and called them cairns.

Every few kilometers you find a stacking: a low beehive no higher than your chest, doorless, already sinking, water pooling at its base. Or a line of larger slabs laid straight across a drowned maze, a slightly darker straightness in the water. Or a single post of petrified wood with a rag tied to it, stiff with salt and algae and alive.

They were not built and abandoned. They are occupied, or they are bodies.

The Low Ones are as old as the lag. Low, broad, splayed, the color of ochre and rust and liver-brown, knobbled backs crusted with true stone and living algae so that when they tuck and go still even the crabs climb over them. No height wasted. Their belly is a wide, pale, vibration drum that sits flush on three egg-stones at once to listen through the white roar that would deafen ears — so they have no ears. Their face, if you lift them (do not), is blind, nostrils slit against drizzle, mouth a horizontal filter-fan like the crab’s, tasting iron and rot. Their feet are the footprints: three-toed, wide as dinner plates, soft-edged to spread weight on floating pavement. Each step breaks a mirror and lets it heal. Their hands are many and small, child-sized, seven or nine around the mantle edge, made not for tools — there is no metal, no glass, no cut timber here — but for fitting. For lifting an egg-stone, feeling its weight, setting it shoulder to shoulder with the next with no soil showing. Stone on stone, balanced by weight.

They stack because they cannot stand noise and they cannot stand dissolving.

A beehive is a listening house and a dry back. One or two Low Ones gather the best-fitted stones and dome them over a slightly high spot, leaving no door because they seal themselves in from the inside for the worst rain-columns, breathing through the seams as water wells up around the base. Inside, on the drum of their bellies, they can hear under the roar: the click of crabs, the stab of a stilt three pans away, the long grinding sigh before the pavement floats. They can hear the Turner turn. Some cairns have fallen and been restacked wrong, as if by water, or by something that did not understand hands. Those are the young trying, or the old after a shift took their sense of north, or a Turner sigh settling the dome sideways.

A causeway is a memory of safe weight. Where the pavement floats often, they lay slabs big enough to bridge the sigh, and they walk them for years until the slabs drown too. The line remains visible as straightness because they keep feeling it with their feet even under water.

A post with a rag is a warning and a lure. The rag, stiff with salt, wicks and flutters when the air changes pitch before a rise, when human skin still feels only drizzle. The algae living in it phosphoresces faintly at night. And it gives the Late Ones something else to look at besides you.

Because at night the reality-slip gets worse and you understand there is one more ecology, in the mirrors.

Horizons double. You see a second, faint horizon floating a meter above the true one — an afterimage of an older flat, when the sea stood higher. Lights move out there that are not campfires — slow, low, phosphorescent smudges that drift against the wind and go out if you look directly. In calm pools your reflection lags.

Those are not tricks. Those are neighbors.

The pools hold sky longer than the sky does. The water keeps last light after dark, iron blue glowing from below. In that held light live the Late, the ones who stayed when the sea fell. They are thin as film, wide as pans, colonial like algae but animal-quick. In day they show as stars at noon, as two suns, because they remember a different sky and project it upward to drink. At night they rise as smudges and drift, tasting rot and warm stone on the air, going out when stared at because direct gaze ripples the water. When you wave and your double waves late, that is not you delayed. That is one of them trying on your shape, half a second slow, learning hands.

The Low Ones know this. They do not trust reflections. They tie rags to posts so the Late will try the rag first. They build doorless because doors would reflect.

None of them are many. Life here is thin, intermittent, unwilling to be counted. Algae to crab to stilt to Turner to Low One to Late — a food chain made of waiting, of filtering rain and mud and sound and light, of never lifting high. They do not meet often. A Low One can lie as a cairn for days while a stilt stands as a post a half-kilometer off while a Turner sleeps under both, all three listening to each other through stone.

Walk on. Your feet are wet. Your ears ring to fill the gap where silence should be. The mirrors break and heal around each step, stone to stone to stone, pool to pool to pool. If you see a beehive ahead that stays exactly as far no matter how you walk while the pool-pattern repeats around you, stop. Turn around. Your backtrail is already smoothed, filled with water. That is not distance lying. That is someone low and broad and patient, shifting a stone a centimeter with a soft knock, retuning the map of tones, deciding whether you are rain, or crab, or kin.
