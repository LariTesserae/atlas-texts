What lives here isn't a flock at first.

It's a pressure. A low, breathing weight that resolves, as your eyes adjust to the buzz and gray, into edges — then feathers — then eyes.

Then birds, beyond counting, touching.

The pigeons are the body of it. Old ferals, city-hardened and mill-pale, generations deep in dust. They are not sleek. They are dense, barrel-chested, neck-thick, feathered to the foot or bare-legged and scaly red-gray, claws worn short from metal. Blues mostly — slate back, two black bars, white rump flashing when they lift — but the mass is mottled: checks dark as ash, whites gone dingy as old sheets, a few rust-reds like embers in it, pieds, soots. Up close their necks oil with green and purple when they turn under the caged bulbs, the only wet color in a room with no water.

They have learned to be touched. A wild pigeon keeps a wingspan of panic around itself. These have forgotten it. They sit shoulder to shoulder on the two long pipes, three deep on the cross-braces, beaded along the cable tray, jammed fin to fin on the hopper ledge, their bodies pressed long so more can fit, tails overlapping like shingles. They doze with heads sunk or swivel with that hard orange eye — black pupil ringed in copper fire — proprietorial, unafraid. When the shake-down auger kicks every eleven minutes with a clank that runs up through your teeth, they don't flush. The ones on that pipe lean in. Feathers puff out, eyes go to slits, they let the vibration dust them. It shakes the mites loose. It warms them.

Heat is mother here. The hens know the back wall stays five degrees warmer in the lee of the transformer, steel humming 60-cycle day and night, and they lay there deliberately, on bare guano-crete, on felted down, on nothing. The chicks that hatch early there are the truest residents: at first naked, blind, yellow-skinned, shouting with a huge open bill, all throat and belly. Then pin-feathered like hedgehogs, then gray fluff squeaking. Then squabs shuffling on the floor with the grounded ones — too young to fly, too old to be brooded — peeping, stumbling over the polished bowls in the concrete, getting stepped over, pecked, fed. Crop milk and regurgitated corn. What falls from above is eaten below.

Stitched in the gaps between pigeons live the sparrows. Smaller, faster, brown. House sparrows, chestnut and ash and black bibs on the males, tan streaked females quick as needles. They don't hold pipe-space by bulk; they steal crevices. They nest inside the dead motor housing, packing feather and wire-clipping and plastic tie into a fist of a nest. They flick along the floor margins, along the pallet cliff-town in the corner where guano has cemented wood into terraces, in and out of the upturned hard hat that holds three speckled eggs. Where pigeons coo-pour, sparrows chirp-cut — sharp, quarrelsome, incessant.

Below both, on the floor's ancient flow of guano-crete, live the cleaners.

Rats. Not skulking. In flat gray light with nowhere to hide they have become bold, sleek, dry-furred, moving with surgical quiet along the margins where the mass thins. They work the edges on soft pink feet, noses twitching in the ammonia-sharp dust, cleaning the dead squab, the addled egg, the spilled cropful of cracked corn from the rail siding, of bread from town. They drink no water — there is none — they drink bodies. They learn the foraging shifts: when parties go out the broken louver in a lighter lane beaten in dust by wings, rats wait for returns, for what drops.

And in the feather-felt itself, silver flickers: meal moths. Small, dun, restless, lifting in a puff when a pigeon shifts and settling again. Their larvae work unseen in husk and down and bone, in beetle husk and seed husk felted together. Decomposition without moisture, a faint warmth rising from underneath if you press your palm flat.

And sometimes, at noon, death lives here for thirty seconds.

A red-tail dares the louver — broad, rusted, too big for the room — and the whole mass inverts. Floor becomes ceiling in half a second. What was carpet becomes storm, a screaming feather-thunder in a box too small for flight, wing-claps like thrown cards, hot dusty air hitting your chest. Then it settles, panting, cooing back up from the pour-murmur, one bird fewer. The rats will clean the feathers.

If you sit still long enough on your knees, back bent under bolts, breathing through your mouth because the talc-fine dust will pack your nose, the mass reclassifies you. Not predator, not pigeon. Structure. Warm structure. The breathing flows around your shoulders. A pigeon lands on your boot, grips with worn claws, eyes you with orange, and stays.
