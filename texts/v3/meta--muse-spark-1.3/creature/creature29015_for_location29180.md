They are not on it. They are in it. That is the first thing to get right.

At first you think it's the herd. Because they are big enough to give the emptiness scale.

They come out of the grey the way weather comes — not walking up, just gradually being there. Thirty, fifty at a time. Pale reindeer-kin, white-grey and shaggy to the ground, so long in the coat and so matted with living moss that from a hundred yards you cannot tell animal from hummock. That's not accident. Sphagnum spores lodge in their underwool and germinate there, russet and gold. Cranberry vine seeds pass through them and sprout in their droppings before the droppings have cooled. When they lie down to chew, water rises around their bellies and they steam. When they stand, they leave a perfect dark dent that fills and greens over within an hour, as if they were never there.

They are built for sponge. Hooves splay huge and soft-edged, two black scoops that don't puncture so much as press, spreading weight over lung-wet moss ten feet deep. Their muzzles are wide and prehensile and iron-stained from sedge. Their rumens run hot enough to neutralize sphagnum acid — you can smell it on their breath, sour-sweet, joining the general exhalation. Calves are born almost white, bawling constantly, wobbling knee-deep between cotton-grass stalks while the midges pillar above them. Cows lick the birth-moss off them and eat it.

They don't migrate. There's nowhere to go. The table drops to cloud on all sides. So they drift, forever, in a slow churn between domes: tear sedge, grunt, churn black water to soup with their feet, move on when the dome they stand on sighs and settles under them with that slow *chuff-chuff*. They know the flarks — the black wet guts between domes — will not hold them. They go single-file through them on old drowned paths they remember in their feet.

And they know how to be unwatched. That white herd a quarter mile off — you look down to wring your sleeve, you look up and they are sixty feet away breathing fog, staring, moss untrampled between. They didn't sneak. They just moved the way this place moves when no eye pins distance down. The old bulls especially. They'll hold your gaze until your eyes water from condensation, and then be elsewhere.

Above and among them, the birds that belong to water and air.

Small dark geese, not white, not grand — soot-coloured, quick, noisy. They wedge overhead honking, drop all at once into a tarn with a slap that echoes off moss. They feed by upending in tea-black water, sifting flatworms and newt eggs and drowned midges. They nest nowhere. There is nothing to build with, and no need. They lay in a dryish crown of dwarf willow — a hundred-year-old mat no higher than your ankle — and sit the eggs in their own down while frost needles grow around them. Their chicks hatch already swimming.

They arrived knowing the trick of the place: sound comes first. You'll hear them land a full second before they come over the rim. So they call late. They honk *after*, to cover the echo that came before. It sounds ragged, out of sync, until you realize they're keeping time with a world that stutters.

And ptarmigan — white explosions. You step near a willow mat and it detonates into birds, a dozen white wings and black eyes and startled clucks, then settles again thirty yards on as if the mat grew birds. In winter-white even in thaw, feet feathered into snowshoes for moss. They eat willow buds and sundew seeds and grit from glacial eggs.

Below the birds, the hum.

You thought it was empty and then the air moves. Impossible clouds of midges for this cold, pillars ten feet tall that bend in the wind and reform, humming. They breed in every tarn and flark, larvae black in black water. They rise to mate above the warmest thing around — a dome venting methane, a reindeer back, you, if you stand still long enough to get a pillar of your own. Cranef lies bounce stupidly over cotton-grass. And the bumblebees — huge, slow, the size of your thumb, ginger and black and sodden, fur so dense water beads off. They work the cotton heads in the dim, drone joining the general boiler-room drone. When charge is high you can see blue sparks crawl off their backs when they lift. They don't seem to mind being shocked. They use it. A bee leaving a flower with a static charge pulls pollen after it in a little veil.

The glue-eaters are learning to eat glue. Tiny flies walk unstickily over sundew leaves, drinking dew that should trap them. Springtails catapult by the million.

Then the divers.

The pitchers here are not the dainty things of warm bogs. They are knee-deep, top-heavy jugs full of rainwater and drowned midges, steaming faintly in cold air. And in them swim shrews. Dark, short-tailed, water-repellent, fringed feet for oars. They dive straight into digestive fluid that should dissolve them and swim, snatching larvae, climbing out onto the pitcher lip to shake and eat. A shrew will live its whole life between three pitchers on one dome, sleeping curled in a dry sphagnum cap, swimming by day. They smell faintly of rot sweet as honey.

Under them in the tarns: black newts, belly orange as lichen, flipping when their pool drains sideways through a peat pipe while you blink. They strand, they wait, they swim again when fog refills it an hour later. With them, flatworms that gather and pulse faintly — not bioluminescence exactly, but a weak current you feel as numbness in your fingertips if you trail your hand. In charged air after venting, whole tarns tick with it.

On the boulder — the watcher. A fox the color of peat, brown-black, low, wet fur slicked, white only at the tip of the tail like cotton-grass. He doesn't den. There is no earth to dig that isn't alive and pumping. He curls on the lee side of glacial erratics where rivulets run even without rain, drinks condensation off lichen, sleeps through crackle of St. Elmo's fire on his ears. He hunts shrew-runs through cranberry vines, takes ptarmigan chicks, worries at reindeer afterbirth, scavenges geese that land wrong. He has learned to hunt by reflection. Tarn reflections lag a breath behind — you move your hand; your reflection moves later. He watches the reflection, not the newt. Strikes where it *will* be.

And under all of them, the city you mistook for a hump.

Each dome is ten thousand stems to a square foot, mites and springtails and fungal threads and rootlets, sundews making glue by the acre, sphagnum making acid by the ton, pickling everything, laying down peat inch by century, cranberry conveyor vines moving phosphorus foot by foot across. The dome itself breathes, pumps, sighs, vents methane with a kettle shriek, fires water pool to pool through hollowed root pipes. It is not one animal. It is the condition that allows animals.

Even the dead are still residents.

You will find a reindeer skeleton picked clean and sunk, antlers hung with sundew. Walk around the dome and on the other side find the same skeleton standing, flesh on, grazing, with peat still in its ribs. Blink and it's gone, but the midges are still parted around where it was. The herd doesn't shy from it. The fox doesn't bark. The moss keeps pumping. The second beat of the bog's thump sometimes comes *earlier* — *thump-thump ... thump-thump* — and the standing dead graze on that early beat.

No one rules. That is why it works. Reindeer tear, geese sift, ptarmigan burst, shrews swim, fox watches, bees drone, newts flip, sundews glue, sphagnum acids, domes chuff and vent and fire water sideways. Frost heave lifts whole plates overnight into cliffs that collapse by noon with a wet *whumpf*. Everything is taken apart and colonized by green within hours. Anthill energy in slow motion, but relentless.

Stand still and you will be tenanted too. Water will condense on your eyelashes and run. Sparks will gather on your shoulders. Midges will pillar above you. Your weight will make your dome sigh and *chuff* a little faster, industrious, indifferent, alive — already counting you as one more warm, breathing island going out to cloud.
