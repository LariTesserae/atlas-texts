They don't come out to meet you.

That is the first thing. In a town that dense, that crowded stem-to-stem, you expect a rustle, a scatter. Here your boot sighs into the mat and nothing flees. It continues.

Crouch. Get low, down to lane-height, down where the air is warmest and smells of turpentine and old honey, and wait until your breathing slows. They were there the whole time. You were just looking for the wrong kind of animal — something quick, something with eyes that shine.

Start with what moves slowest, because there is most of it.

The grazers are on every sunlit wall. At first you took them for part of the bark, a velvet patch, a lichen. Then one inched. They are flat to the point of seeming poured — oval, palm-large, edgeless — with a back of dense, short fur the color of dusted sage that holds a film of wax. No head that you can see. No legs that you can see. Underneath, a soft foot and a circular rasping mouth. They travel a millimeter at a time up the tower skins, polishing as they go, licking the thin exudate of algae and wax and trapped oil-fog off the scale-leaves without breaking the seal. Behind them a pale trail, matte to gloss, that the sun hardens again in an hour.

They are herds without herding. Twenty on a single tower, spaced exactly as far as their grazing halos reach, never touching. If you put a finger near one it does not flinch. It simply stops, which for a grazer is a kind of flinch — a pause in the rasp you feel more in your teeth than hear. Touch the fur and it is cool, improbably cool, and dry. They store nothing liquid. Their bodies are almost solid, slow fat made of concentrated sap they have detoxified in their guts. They get all the solvent they will ever need from licking each other’s backs at dusk.

Under you, under the mat, the turners.

You will not see them unless you lift — and don’t lift. But in the lanes where the felt has worn thin you can see the mat breathe over them. Blind white millipedes, thick as a wrist, long as a forearm, moving through pumice flour in a slow peristalsis. No eyes, no pigment to waste. Their heads are blunt shovels. Their segments click faintly as they pass grit through themselves, coating each grain with gut mucus, turning sterile cinder into something that can hold a spore. They aerate the town from below. They are the reason the mat does not suffocate itself. Sometimes at midday one surfaces in shadow, a pale comma, and a dozen tiny spoon-leaved mosses are already leaning toward the damp of its passing — not wet damp, but chemical damp, the brief richness of its trail.

Above you, the collectors.

They hang and do not fly. Ash-moths, big as sparrows, the exact grey of cooled ash, cling to the hanging curtains with wings spread flat and open. They are solar panels before they are moths. Their wings are thick with dark veins and fine hairs that catch heat and oil-fog together. For hours they do nothing. Then, when a thermal finally stirs the bowl, one lets go, glides three feet, four feet, to the next bridge — no flutter, no beat if it can help it, because beating dries. Their larvae live in the middens, fat white commas that eat old leaf and old grazer trail and turn it back to gritty humus. You hear the adults land more than see it: a soft tick against a bladder-leaf.

And the bladders — those papery sacs that hang in bunches and never hold water, only air — each one has a moth’s proboscis hole, neat as a stitch. They sip warm air and exhale it altered.

On the only places nothing else wants — the fresh glass, the razor fracture faces left unsoftened — sit the watchers. Little lizards, dun and rust-black, with throats like thin pink windows. They bask belly-flat on stone too sharp for moss, storing sun in their bones. They watch you without fleeing. Fleeing is evaporation. Fleeing is death by a hundred breaths. So they have learned the economy of stillness. Their eyes track you, lidless, filmed with wax they lick clean once a day. At night they will crawl into the hollow towers to steal heat and lick sap overflow from the drains. The builders tolerate them. A lizard eats the one thing the builders cannot patch against: tiny sap-flies that would lay in open pores.

And then, up high, where the light slants first and leaves last, the ones who keep it all from falling apart.

You saw them last because they were still.

The builders. The masons who are also the bricks.

They are cat-sized when curled, longer when stretched along a bridge. Soft is the wrong word and also the only word — not furred, not shelled, but a dense, yielding hide covered in thousands of tiny hooked hairs, like burrs, like Velcro left in the sun. Six limbs: the front four short, hand-like, tipped with blunt claws and a flexible lip-pad for pressing; the hind pair thick, muscled for bracing and for drumming. No tail. A low head with no nose to speak of, a wide lipless mouth, and two blank, dark, heat-loving eyes that do not blink so much as film over. Two short antennae, constantly moving, tasting the air for resin.

At first they look naked, grey-pink. Then you see they are not naked at all. They are gardens.

Each adult carries on its back a living thatch — spoon-moss, seedling scale-leaf, bits of beard-moss deliberately stuck to its hooks. They plant themselves. They walk slowly along the tower rims, turning their backs to the sun, photosynthesizing by proxy. When a patch on one builder fruits or flowers, another will come muzzle-close on the common roof at dusk and delicately comb it off with teeth and tongue and press it to its own back, or carry it in its cheek to a bare patch on a tower. Antennae touch, cuttings exchange. That is their speech, their news, their library.

They build the way coral builds. A builder chews moss and mineral flour and old humus from the midden, mixes it with saliva and sap in its mouth until it is a pellet, climbs to a crack or a thin spot in a tower wall, and presses it in with its forehead — over and over, patient, until the patch holds. Then it plants a cutting in the fresh mortar. Another will come and urinate sap — not waste, but concentrated resin — to seal it. Tower by tower, year by finger-length year, they raise hollow columns five to fifteen feet tall, leaning sunward, skinned in overlapping succulent scales pale jade and amber that open in unison after zenith with a sigh you can feel on your skin.

Inside is cool and dim and smells of earth that never knew water. That is where the young are.

Deep in the coolest, north-leaning tower, in felted cradles, lie the grubs — blind, segmented, almost translucent, pulsing so slowly you have to watch a full minute to be sure you saw it. Nurses, smaller, paler builders who seldom go into sun, move among them, licking them, daubing them with chewed sap, rotating them toward warmth. A grub does not eat food so much as it is fed air: pre-digested fog, regurgitated wax, the shed skins of grazers. It will lie there for years before it cocoons in tower fiber and emerges soft and hook-covered and immediately gets planted by its elders, like a bed being sown.

They have no tools because they never needed to break the world to use it. No knife mark, no nail, no hinge anywhere in the hamlet. Everything is done by mouth and secretion and time. Sap drains — narrow grooves down the tower flanks that carry overflow resin to lizard-lick basins, not to waste but to keep the walls from bursting in heat. Middens tucked in the lee, where old leaves and dead grazers and moth frass are composted by millipedes and larvae into new mortar. Nurseries. Common roofs — flattened tops where at warm dusk they gather, a dozen soft backs glowing amber in slant light, and touch antennae for an hour without sound.

When alarmed they do not cry. One rears on its hind legs, braces against hollow fiber, and thumps twice. You feel it in your sternum. The whole hamlet freezes. Grazers stop rasping. Moths fold a fraction tighter. Even the seed pods seem to hold their tick. Then, slowly, life resumes.

If you tear a leaf — and you will, no matter how careful, your skin is clumsy here — they will come. Not fast. Three, four builders converging down a bridge with that shuffle-pause, that economy. They will stare at the wound with those blank eyes and begin to chew at their own back-thatch to make a patch, pressing it in with their foreheads. They will remember your shape afterward. Not punish. Remember, the way a wall remembers weather.

Stay till shadow pours over the western rim. That is when you finally see them whole. The towers transluce from inside, amber lanterns, and every back-garden, every hanging curtain, every grazer trail catches it. The builders line the rims, still, facing the last sun, their living coats lit from beneath. Below them the lanes breathe. Above them the moths make their short glides, ash to ash. Around the sharp stones the lizards pulse their clear throats, once, twice, conserving everything.

A town that drinks nothing, built by animals who are also fields, housed in plants that are also houses, streets that inhale. Abundant, silent, crowded to bursting — and all of it holding its breath together so as not to dry.
