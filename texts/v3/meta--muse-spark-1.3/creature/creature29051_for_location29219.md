Nothing lives here.

That is what you think at first, and for a long time after first. You keep looking for the wrong kind of living.

You look for eyes. For movement at your speed. For something that breathes in and out the way you do, that makes a noise going by. Here, nothing does that. So you walk between the standings and think: empty nursery. Ruins that were never cities.

Then you stand still long enough and the place stops accommodating you.

The first one you meet is not one. It is the frost.

In the lee of the slabs, where vapor sighs out of hairline seams, that pale yellow-white skin branches like mycelium, like frost. You touched it and it powdered, and you thought: chemistry rehearsing for biology. Wrong. It is biology that has given up on water entirely.

Look closer. Don't touch this time. Crouch until the heat from the crack warms one cheek. See how the branches are not random. Each tip holds a bead, smaller than a pinhead, of clear glass. Silica. The thing sweats glass to keep its shape. Underneath, in threads no thicker than hair, sulfur and iron are being coaxed, oxidized without oxygen, burned slow in heat. It does not grow so much as precipitate itself forward, a micron a day. What you powdered with a fingertip was a hundred years of patient drinking of stone-breath.

And it is everywhere once you know the color. Pale veins lacing every sheltered slab, every downwind side. From above, if you could see, the whole ridge would be faintly furred with it, a pale nervous system learning the shape of the mountains. It feeds on what the cracks exhale. It dies back where the rock goes cold. That is why you learned the rule without being taught: stay on the warm rock. The creep already knows. The warm rock is fed. The cold rock is starving, and about to move.

If you watch long enough — longer than you want to, until your calves ache from stillness — you will see the creep move. Not grow. Move. A branch-edge near a hot seam will lift, very slightly, toward the heat, and the dust around it will hang a fraction too long, as if held. Heat rising. It is drinking the turnover of air.

The second thing you meet is standing up watching you, and has been the whole time.

You called them structures. Tower eight feet high, hollow in the middle. Three flat stones balanced in a way that cannot last. Cluster of four leaning together half a mile on.

They are not structures. They are standers.

You only notice because you come back along the same line of crest and one of them is not where you left it. Not fallen. Risen into something else. The three-stone balancer is now four stones, taller by a slab. The tower has turned five degrees at the waist, its empty doorway now facing a different crack. Your brain tries to fix it as: I misremembered. You didn't.

Crouch by the hollow one. Put your face near — not too near — the empty doorway that goes nowhere. Inside is dark, and darker than dark, and warm in pulses. Dull orange breath comes up from its feet, where it is rooted into the crack. Wait for your eyes to stop wanting light.

It is made of what the ridge is made of. Flat cleavage planes, right angles, stacked slabs. That is not camouflage. That is what fast-cooling under pressure makes, whether you are rock or you are this. Its skin is basalt, still warm, still deciding. Its joints are not joints but cooling fractures, ticking faintly as they split. It has no face because it does not use light. It has no legs because it does not walk the way you walk.

It rides.

When the shrug comes up through the soles of your feet every few minutes, when the house-sized slab a hundred yards left sighs and settles a foot lower, the standers settle with it. They loosen, slump an inch, take the new angle. When the ridge shoulders up a new fang between two old ones, they lean into the lift. They are exquisitely tuned to stay upright on ground that is still becoming ground. You stumble. They adjust without hurry, without sound, at the speed of stone, which is their speed.

Put your palm, carefully, on the leeward side. It is warmer than the surrounding boulders. Steadily warmer. Heat is being channeled up through it, from the crack into the hollow core. Inside that hollow, the creep is thick, furred white-yellow, drinking. Inside that hollow, the air shimmers though there is no wind. That is its gut. It eats heat and vapor and iron. It sweats out slabs.

Once in a long while — you will not see it happen, you will only see that it has happened — a stander buds. A pressure blister swells on its flank, cools too fast, snaps into a right angle. A new lintel. A new corner. If the bud holds, it lengthens into a limb that will eventually crack off and root nearby as a three-stone balancer that cannot last, but will. If it fails, it powders, and the creep eats the powder.

They do not see you. They feel you as a brief, confusing weather. You are cold and wet. The dryness pulls moisture off your lips in minutes; they feel that moisture go into the air as a sour, rich plume. Your footsteps are a soft crunch that stops the instant you stop — to them, through the soles of their feet, you are thunder, arrhythmic and alarming. Your breathing, loud inside your head and small outside it, stirs the dust in quick, wasteful puffs. They turn, very slowly, their empty doorways toward or away from that disturbance. That watched-by-geometry feeling is accurate. You are being triangulated by heat-shadow.

Do not go inside one. Not because they will hurt you. Because you are water, and water here is a violence. A single sweat drop falling into a hollow core would flash to steam and split its gut.

And under all of them — under you — the third thing lives.

The ridge feels hollow, resonant, like a crust over a drum. It is. Every few minutes a low tremor comes up. Occasionally a deep groan, felt-more-than-heard, from far below, as if roots are grinding. Then quiet rushes back, heavier.

That is not settling. That is the one whose skin you are standing on, turning over in its sleep.

Very far down, where the cracks widen from a finger's width to a hand to a house, where the dull orange stops pulsing slowly and runs steady, something vast and slow is feeding the whole range upward. It does not have a shape you could draw. Think of it as a pressure, a heat with preferences. It likes to wrinkle. This ridge alone would be a country if it were finished — it is one wrinkle among many because the sleeper likes to try many at once. Peaks rise around you in all directions because it is pushing up fingers to feel the thin, warm air. When a spire across the gulf leans, hesitates, then collapses inward in utter silence, that is not failure. That is it tasting, rejecting, reabsorbing.

The embered seep from cracks is its breath coming up. The slow convective turnovers, warm air rising off hot cracks, cool air sinking, stirring dust in lazy helices without a whistle — that is it breathing you, incidentally, as you breathe it and find it not enough.

The dust that sometimes does not settle but rises a few inches and hangs — that is where its attention is. Dust moving when air is still means ground moving under it. Ground moving means it is moving.

You will be gone by morning — fallen, or risen into something else, if you stay. They know this. To the creep, you are a sudden rain that will evaporate. To the standers, you are a warm column that learned to walk too fast and will soon cool. To the sleeper, you are not even that. You are a tick on a scale on a wrinkle, briefly warm, briefly wet, making small noises that the dust kindly eats.

Stay on the warm rock. They are keeping it warm for you, for now.
