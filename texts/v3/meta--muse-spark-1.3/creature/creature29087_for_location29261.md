They live pressed together.

Not in the way animals live on a plain, spaced by territory and habit, but in the way pages live in a book — overlapping, breathing on each other, unable to move without moving someone else.

First, because they are the most and the quietest, the moths.

Large, dusky, the color of ash that has learned to fly. Wingspans wide as a child's palm. By day — if day reaches in here at all — they are nothing. Flat to the basalt, wings folded upright and together over the back like dry leaves shut against rain, forewings mottled gray-brown-black to vanish against stone. You would swear they were stone, lichen-scale, until one antenna lifts, tests, lays back down. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands perhaps, though you cannot count what is layered. They shoulder each other on the bellied walls where it is dim and warm and still, head-up, so close their fringes touch. They came up from far below, from valleys you can't see from here, riding the warm evening updrafts in migration, seeking a cool dark to wait out the heat in — aestivation, a summer hibernation. They found instead this warm dark. And stayed anyway, because dark was enough.

They barely move for hours. Their economy is stillness. Fat stored in their thick abdomens ticks down slowly. Now and then one loses its hold and flutters — a soft papery start — and a hundred around it lift in alarm and settle again in a wave, dust sifting off their wings into the air, smelling faintly of chaff and oil. At dusk they will boil out, or some of them will, to drink the night air outside, to sip from nothing and return. For now they wait, trembling on the edge of flight.

Among and beneath them, the crickets.

Pale high-altitude wetas, the length of a man's thumb, the color of old ivory left in tea. They look wrong in here — too soft, too naked for this height, their abdomens glossy and swollen, their long hind legs folded like jackknives, their antennae impossibly long, sweeping and sweeping the dark. They cling with absurd tiny feet, tarsi splayed on vertical rock that should not hold them, moving with insulting slowness. Lift one leg. Pause an eternity. Set it down. Lift another.

They are the cleaners and the opportunists. They eat what falls: a moth wing, a dead swift chick that never existed because swifts don't nest here, a stunned moth, fungus, frass, each other if they must. Their mandibles are black and strong enough to shear chitin. When a raven's shadow crosses the lip they freeze deeper into stillness, becoming lumps, becoming stone. At night they venture higher toward the rim-cups to drink the condensation before it evaporates. If you put your finger near one it does not hop — there is nowhere to hop — it turns its blind-seeming face toward your warmth and waves its antennae over you, tasting salt.

Above them, where the shaft narrows and a little more air moves, the swifts.

Hundreds of them, soot-black birds built entirely for a sky they have abandoned for the day. They do not perch. They wedge. Upright, breast to stone, tail braced with stiff spines against rock, claws hooked into invisible pits, packed into vertical cracks shoulder to shoulder like knives in a block. White collars — narrow crescents at the throat — flash when they shift. That's the only brightness in the pit.

They are nervous, quick, irritable in confinement. All day they shuffle a centimeter forward, a centimeter back, muttering, clicking, dozing with one eye open. Their sickle wings are folded too long for their bodies, crossing past the tail. They smell of dust and feathers and faint musk. They came for the moths. At blue hour they will unhook all at once in a screaming corkscrew up out of the mouth and be gone into the huge twilight to feed on the moths that also rise, to drink speed after a day of stillness. But now they are wall. Now they are part of the tremor. When one screams — a short ripping strip of sound — the whole shaft flinches, and down sifts a rain of down and guano dust through the almost-light.

And deepest, where it is blackest and warmest and the ammonia thickens, the bats.

They are the heart. A living curtain. Small, furred, chestnut to black, folded into themselves, hanging by hind feet from overhangs and solution pockets that have been smoothed by centuries of urine. Wings wrapped tight around bodies like cloaks, ears twitching, noses leafed and questing. They breathe together. Not in metaphor — literally, in the press and warmth, respiration synchronizes in patches, flanks rising and falling in slow waves that travel across the curtain.

They hang so dense you cannot see rock between them. When you finally make them out, you see eyes opening like beads, all at once, reflecting nothing because there is no light to reflect. You hear them before you see them: the high needle-clicking of echolocation, too fast and too crowded, bouncing off close rock and live bodies and coming back wrong, layering into a constant crystalline hiss underneath the rustle. They are awake in sleep, mapping each other. They jostle, crawl over each other's backs with folded wrists, nuzzle, squabble silently, open mouths full of needle teeth. Mothers hold pups impossibly tiny and naked against their bellies. They came for the moths too, but they also came for the pit itself — for the heat that lets them burn less, for the walls that hold their sound.

Under all of them, working the varnish, the beetles.

Tiny, blind, the color of guano. You will never see them unless you are lowered down or you learn to look at guano as a landscape. On the lower ledges, where droppings from swifts and bats have lacquered the stone dark and slick, grows a pale fungus like frost, like breath frozen mid-exhale. And in it, patient as monks, blind beetles no bigger than sesame seeds move — antennae tapping, mouthparts scraping. They eat old wings, frass, fungal hyphae, the bodies of midges that followed the smell down and died. Their world is damp, ammoniac, eternal night. They do not know the mouth exists. Generations live and die on a single ledge a hand wide. Their elytra are fused; they will never fly out.

On that same guano and damp, invisible, the rest: springtails like thrown pepper that pop when a drip hits, mites in orange clusters in the joints of dead moths, fungal threads white as nerve.

And at the very edge of green, the holders-on.

Orange crust lichen on the windward lip, map-like, slow as geology. Cushions of black moss hard as felt in the condensation cups, holding a thimble of water for an hour after sunset by capillary will. One stunted shrub hunched sideways, leaves thick like boiled leather, roots cracked into basalt, alive on fog and guano dust. A few grass blades in a crack, bleached, rattling. They are not the life of the pit. They are its witnesses. They run on light; everything else runs on bodies.

And the visitor, who lives here by not living here.

The raven. Big, blue-black, too wide-winged for the shaft. It comes in the blue hour before the swifts leave, lands splay-footed on the scalloped rim, cocks its head over the ink. It cannot stand the press of wings down inside, the screaming and dust — so it works the upper cracks only, probing with a bark-stripped stick worn smooth and damp at the tip for weta, prying, dropping the stick when it has what it wants. Sometimes it brings a dense basalt pebble with one chipped edge, hammers a big cricket open, leaves the stone behind. Feather, stick, stone. Use without staying. Intelligence that touches the pit and withdraws.

Together they are more animal than air.

If you lie long enough at the lip, you stop seeing species and start seeing a single thing: a warm, respiring wall that shivers in patches, that inhales when the swifts shuffle and exhales heat onto your face, that clicks and rustles and drips as one layered presence. The moth is its skin. The weta are its slow fingers picking at itself. The swifts are its nervous edge. The bats are its hot core. The beetles and fungus are its gut, turning death back into slick stone.
