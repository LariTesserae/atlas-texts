You don't see them at first.

At noon the town in the hole looks empty. The slabs glare, the amber bulbs are drowned out, the air shivers over concrete. Nothing moves except heat and birds. Then a screen door bangs somewhere behind a swamp cooler and you realize the stillness was people being still — conserving themselves.

The people here are pit people now. They move like divers.

They are not many. Count roofs and you get thirty-odd. Count people and you get maybe sixty, seventy if cousins are down from the rim. Mostly men who came for factory work and stayed when the factory un-happened, plus the women who run what keeps men alive, plus a few kids who have never lived anywhere with a horizon. They are sun-browned in a place with almost no sun; the brown comes from rust and ditch-water and the amber lights. Their skin never really dries. In the creases — behind knees, between toes, under belts — there is prickly heat gone to fungus, pale and itching. Their shirts go dark in the first minute outside and stay dark. Their boots never dry out, so they rot from the stitching, soles peeling up like tongues, white mold furring the laces.

They have learned the slowness. Up on the rim a man strides. Down here he ambles, shuffles in flip-flops on hot slab, stops in every strip of shade to let the metal cool enough to touch with gloves. Work happens in twenty-minute bursts — tighten a flange, patch a pipe with the welding rig spitting blue, haul a bag of concrete — and then long sits. Old plastic chairs in the lee of cinderblock, under a sheet of corrugated tin propped on pipe. They sit with knees apart, breathing through the mouth, watching rust creep. At night they move a little more, when the generators throttle and the air drops from scalding to merely steaming. That's when laundry gets done, when someone walks the long hot wet minute to a neighbor with a bowl or a wrench.

There is the pump man, or rather the pump men in shifts, because *do not stop the pumps* is the only law. One is always awake listening to that *chug-chug-chug ... chug-chug-chug*. He can hear a miss from inside his sleep. His hands are diesel to the wrist. He lives in the brick box's shadow, in a room that smells of hot oil, with a cot and a wall of gauges and a notebook where he logs hours and water level in pencil gone soft. He is the closest thing to a mayor, not because anyone voted but because if he says both pumps need to run, both pumps run.

There is the shop woman behind bars, selling warm soda and canned beans and flip-flops and mosquito coils and phone credit. Her shop is one cinderblock room with a swamp cooler chugging on tar-paper, air inside smelling of mildew and sugar. Kids hang around her doorway for the blue-white LED, the only cool-colored light in town, picking at the ice-less freezer.

There is the chapel keeper — an old-timer, one of the ones who points to the waist-high watermark inside the painted door without relish, just fact. He sweeps water out after the hot season rises. On Sundays five or six people come. The rest make their own peace with the rectangle of sky.

The kids — there are maybe eight of them — belong more to the place than the adults do. They are amphibious. Barefoot where adults wear boots, immune to the suck of the floor under gravel, they know where the rill is shallow enough to dam with broken concrete, where the toads are fattest by the rust-colored sump. They catch the small mottled frogs and let them go. They get fevers from mosquitoes and get up again. They shout across the hole and hear themselves come back off the walls, and so they are always shouting. They are the only ones who run, briefly, before the heat sits them down.

But the people are guests. Anyone down here will tell you that without saying it. This is an animal town that tolerates humans.

First, the pigeons. They were here before the rent was free — as soon as the excavators left gouges and weep-holes and bolt-holes, they moved in. Thousands. Gray-blue and rust and white, fat on spilled grain and shop crumbs and algae. They own the walls, a vertical city stacked four stories high, each horizontal gouge a street. At any hour a few hundred are wheeling in the trapped air, wings slapping loud as laundry in a small room, riding the heat that can't escape. Their cooing fills the pit like water fills a tank, a constant low roar with sudden lift-offs when the hawk — the only thing that still lives properly *above* — slides over the rim and they all rise at once in a panic of feathers and lime-white shit streaking the east wall a thousand times over. At dusk they settle, jostling for holes, muttering.

Under them, the frogs. The foot-wide moat-rill and the bath-warm sump pond filmed with oil rainbows seethe with them. Small mottled brown frogs and warty toads the color of wet clay, thousands of throats pulsing. They call day and night, a metallic trill that never quite stops, because it is always wet and always hot enough for breeding. They sit in tire ruts and in the drip under every flange of the twelve-inch main, blinking. The cats won't eat them — bitter skin — so they have no fear.

And the rats. At night, when the amber bulbs buzz and moths smash themselves on the cages, the rats come out along the pipe runs. Norway rats, roof rats, big yellow-toothed males scarred from slab-edge fights, nursing females thin as ropes, juveniles practicing the routes. Dozens. Hundreds. They move low and unhurried along the edges of tilted slabs, along the warm pipe on concrete piers dripping at every flange, single file, whiskers testing. They are not afraid because there is nowhere to chase them to — the hole is closed. Make noise when you walk, everyone learns, shuffle and clap, and they part like water and close behind you. They eat pigeon squab that falls, cockroaches, garbage, algae mats, each other when needed. You hear their claws on tin, their soft thumps under houses.

Above the rats, watching and declining: the feral cats. Five or six, lean, rust-dusted, short-coated from heat. They live on roofs where concrete still holds day warmth into night, under catwalks, in the transformer pole that goes nowhere. They watch the rat highways with half-closed eyes and do not bother, because why work in this heat when pigeon fledglings fall stunned and cockroaches the size of thumbs work the damp under every house in endless supply? Once in a while a kitten is born under a shed and learns within days to lie flat in shade and pant.

Around the lights: the cloud. Mosquitoes and midges in permanent orbit, breeding in every puddle that skins over with dark green algae and breaks like ice. At dusk the swallows come down from the rim screaming, cutting through them, and later the bats drop from under the catwalks and stitch the air above the water, silent after all that noise, eating until their bellies drag.

Under everything: the flat life. Liverworts and pale moss in the fringe where the wall weeps most, black-green and spongy underfoot. Bleached crabgrass in slab cracks. A bitter weed with dusty leaves that even the rats leave. White mold furring the north side of anything wooden, and on leather, and on the chapel hymnals. And the tamarisk by the sump pond — salt-burned, leaning, roots in oily water — the only thing that casts real shade. Lizards live in its bark. Everyone leaves it alone.

That is who lives here: sixty-odd slow sweating people tending pumps that will never win, and a vertical nation of pigeons, a water nation of frogs, a night nation of rats, with cats as bored aristocrats and bats as night shift, all glued together by mold and algae and mosquitoes. The humans maintain the heat and the water with their generators and seep, and the animals maintain the noise.

If you stay a day you become a little like them. You learn to sit until metal cools. You learn to stamp softly at night. You learn to look up at that small lid of sky to read weather, while around you wings slap and water ticks and the pump heart goes *chug-chug-chug ... chug-chug-chug*, and rust creeps another millimeter up the wall.
