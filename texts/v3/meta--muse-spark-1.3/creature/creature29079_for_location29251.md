You think, at first, that nothing big lives here.

That is the reasonable conclusion. The Table is loud with small things — toads, glass frogs, copper hummingbirds that hover close enough to fan your face, coati-troops rustling and staring — and empty of anything your size. The cairns look old. The dome looks dead. The hermit's notebooks are pulp. No smoke. No paths. Nothing scarce enough to need hiding, nothing shy.

And then you notice that you are being followed by the ground.

It starts as quake. You cross a sedge marsh, tea-water to the ankle, sphagnum giving under each step, and twenty paces behind you the mat shivers again when there is no wind. You stop. It stops a half-second late. Like your footfall in the step-fields — that soft lag you felt yesterday near the black channel and laughed off. *Plip. ...plip.* 

You crouch. The second shadow gives it away. At noon everything here throws that faint eastern double, but this patch of nothing throws a double that is too dark, too wide. A man-sized paleness offset to the east of empty air.

You reach out, part the silver-green rosettes — and the hummock breathes.

That is the only word for it at first. A low rise of peat and living moss, crowned with sundew glistening red, swells almost imperceptibly and settles. The air above it is hotter. It smells stronger: warm peat and honey-rot and something animal underneath, sweet iron, like blood in milk. The water beading on the rock spine beside it is not running down. It is merging, thickening, and crawling *up* in a deliberate film toward a lip of moss on the hummock's flank, vanishing into it with a faint ticking sound.

You do not see the Wick until you stop looking for an animal.

They are not hidden. They are mistaken. Every basin has two or three of them, lying out like whale-backs in the marsh, grown over so completely that your eye files them as ground.

A big old one is three meters long when it decides to be long, which is rarely. Usually it is coiled, humped, a low dome of bog. Its skin, where you can find a patch not rooted through, is the grey of the repaired dome floor — smooth, warm, faintly yielding, the same impossible almost-flesh as the Needles. Touch it and your fingerprint stays a second as pale afterglow before fading. They are made of related stuff. Or the Needles are made of them.

For the Wick is not naked. It cannot afford to be, up here where nothing ever dries but everything tries to. Its dermis is a garden bed. Sphagnum roots directly into it in a pelage an inch thick. Filmy ferns unroll from its flanks. Sundews crowd its spine, their tentacles redder and larger than any on the ground, and three or four dwarf orchids, white and blood-red, bloom from behind what you will later decide is its front. Seedlings sprout along its back in rows, as on a nurse-log. Its dorsal hollow holds water — a true private pond, an ounce or two — and in it live the things you thought lived only in pools: a transparent frog with its heart ticking, a beetle rowing upside-down, a salamander larva with red lace gills. When the Wick moves, its pond does not spill. It flexes around it.

Under the moss, if you press gently and it allows it, you can see through: amber translucent tissue, and inside, branching black veins that pulse with slow light, the same blood-slow light as in the cairn lozenges. That light quickens when you are near a Needle. So does your sternum.

It has no head, until it needs one. Disturb it, or sit with it long enough through the gold afternoon, and the front end thins, rises a hand's breadth off the peat, and extrudes. Not eyes — first a fringe, a crown of short, wet, finger-like papillae that taste the mist, that shiver to the cicada sizzle and to a deeper note you feel more than hear. Then, below the fringe, a mouth: not a jaw but a broad, lipless, muscular lip, pale pink-grey, that can seal flat to rock and suck, or evert softly to lick. They lick everything. Rock film. Duckweed. Your boot, if you let them, with a curiosity that is neither fear nor hunger. The coatis know this. A troop will follow an old Wick for days, turning over the moss it has loosened, snapping up the grubs it exposes, sleeping curled against its heat at night. The hummingbirds nest on them. You will see a fist-sized emerald bird scold you from a bromeliad growing six inches above a breathing flank.

They move the way water moves here — by seep and hang. No legs, no hurry. A peristaltic ripple passes from rear to front once a minute, once an hour when dozing. The belly secretes a broad sheet of mucus that does not dry, cannot dry in this air, and that sheet is engineering. Under a hand lens — the hermit must have seen this, his pulp pages show smeared magnifications — it is combed into parallel micro-flutes. Capillary pulls water uphill along it. That is the climbing beads you saw: not miracle but road. The Wick lays a wicking highway wherever it goes and drinks from either end. Days later the mucus is still active, still warping the local field around the Needles just enough that your foot lands a half-second late and compasses swing twenty degrees off and voices cut dead at a forest edge. Step-fields are fresh trails. You have been walking in their wake without knowing.

They eat the marsh and the marsh eats them back.

The belly absorbs dissolved peat, algae, drowned midges. The dorsal roots — white, hair-fine, growing *up* into the air to drink the mist, exactly like the vines — absorb steam. And the sundew garden on the back digests on shares. Golden flies blunder into those red tentacles by the hundred in the heat; the plant dissolves them; the Wick absorbs the nitrogen through its skin. In return it carries the garden to new seeps, defecates rich tea into poor pools, and at dusk, when the light goes gold through the steam and the toad chorus swells until the ground throbs, it sings.

You feel it before you hear it. That low Needle hum in the sternum, beating against the cicadas. A Wick deepens it. Two Wicks in adjacent basins lock phase. On still afternoons the air above the rim Needles distorts with heat-haze even when the stone is cool because the Wicks are out on the ridges, necks raised, humming the inversion into place — exhaling, with the machines, dense hot wet air to hold against altitude. The lowland story is not wrong. The Needles are lungs. But lungs need a chest. They need something to breathe *for*.

That is the pilgrimage you will see if you stay past the deep years. Every eleven years the hum drops half a tone and the frogs go mad. The Wicks, slow as geology, converge. They leave their basins in long glistening fans, wicking water uphill behind them, and gather at the seven Needles. They embrace them. There is no other word. They rear half their length up the warm black flank — thirty meters of impossible slender warmth — and seal their great lips flat and pump. Fluid exchanges. Light moves faster in the black veins. When they pull away, a patch of Needle is brighter, newer, and on the Wick's flank a new lozenge is forming under the skin: black, veined with moving light. The cairn lozenges are not offerings. They are kidney stones, shed hearts, batteries grown to term and deposited. The Wicks stack weeping slabs around them to keep them wet until they root. The thin rods driven into peat with their hovering mote a hand's breadth above the tip — those are not trail markers. Those are eyes. A Wick that cannot be in two basins leaves one behind to watch its garden, a mote of itself held in the Needle-field, staying put in wind.

At night they are easiest to count. Go out when the parrots have torn screaming over and the mist has thickened to a luminous soup. Lie belly-down on black rock still hot from day. Douse your lamp. Every pool mirrors, then whites. And across the marsh you will see them moving as darker darks, each carrying its double shadow even in starlight, each crowned with a faint halo of spores and insects drawn to its heat. The plink-plink of dripping rock syncs, briefly, to their ripple. The *whoop... whoop* of toads comes, you realize, *from* them — males riding in their dorsal ponds, calling from living islands.

They die by stopping. An old, old Wick, its garden grown into a true miniature ridge — stunted cloud-forest tree no taller than a house sprouting from its back, bromeliads big as buckets in its folds — will choose a shallow basin and cease to ripple. Moss closes over the mouth-fringe. Roots go down. Within a season you cannot tell animal from hummock. Peat accumulates a millimeter a year over it, burying pollen, beetles, lost sandals, Needle-shed, Wick. Cut the peat a century later and you will find layers of black veining, faintly warm. The forest does not advance or retreat; it breathes in place because they breathe in place.

Stand on the rock spine in that last exhalation of day — air hottest before it cools, scent narcotic, pools to mirrors to white — and look across marsh to forest to marsh to Needle to marsh again, all steaming, all buzzing, cliffs dropping to cloud on every side. The quake beside your foot is not ground settling. It is a young one, no bigger than a coiled rope, its orchids not yet bloomed, tasting your sweat on the air with its crown, learning the shape of your afterglow.

Nothing up here has learned to be scarce because someone is still keeping it abundant.
