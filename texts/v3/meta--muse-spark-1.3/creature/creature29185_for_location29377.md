At first you don't see them as alive at all.

That's how thin they are spread, and how still. Out on that blue-edged table, in the pewter half-light, a dark hump half a mile off is a boulder until it isn't. It breathes. Or rather, the air around it breathes for it — a white plume that hangs and doesn't dissipate, that thickens and lifts to join the low ceiling always sitting a hundred feet up. Then the hump takes a step, glacier-slow, head down, and another hump beyond it does the same, and you realize the field of boulders you have been walking through for an hour is arranged shoulder to shoulder in a broad front, advancing a yard a day.

They are the ones who live here. Everything else just attends them.

Come closer. They let you. Fear, like everything else here, is expensive, and they do not spend it running.

They are low and barrel-bodied, musk-oxen grown monstrous with cold and time. A cow the size of a small boulder. An old bull the size of a small truck, his head slung low under the weight of his own ruff. Their hair is long and matted and clotted with ice, the color of the stone itself — shale grey, ash grey, a dirty white underneath where frost never melts. You can hardly see skin. You can hardly see legs, just stout black pillars that move only when they must. You can hardly see eyes: small, dark, half-lidded against wind, unhurried. What you see is mass conserved.

Kneel beside one and listen, because standing you will hear nothing. The thin air and the snow and all that hair take sound away like felt. A herd that big should thud and bellow. It doesn't. What there is you have to put your ear close to find: the faint rasp and tear of lichen being worried off rock by a broad, hard tongue, the soft tick-tick-tick of something very hot cooling very slowly, and underneath it all a deep, deliberate chugging you feel more through the soles of your boots than in your ears. Piston lungs. That is the word that comes to you.

For the fur is a lie, or only half the truth. Put your hand near — not on — a flank and heat comes off it in a wave. Inside that barrel chest something burns at a temperature that has nothing to do with the pewter sky. They take in almost nothing — sulfur lichen, rust-brown moss, a mouthful of gritty melt — and they keep it for a long time, and they burn it thoroughly. Their guts are furnaces. Their breath comes out through wide nostrils in long, dense exhalations that instantly fog and hang, white and heavy. Their dung, when it drops, steams for hours in the cold. Around each standing animal, around each bedded animal, the permafrost gives up for a few feet: a dark damp halo of thawed mud where the ground softens and sighs. And in that halo, moss seizes its week-long summer and grows lush, black-green, springy under the cold.

From a rise you can read whole herds by those halos — a rash of green-black pox across grey stone, each spot a beast making its own brief wetland and then moving on, leaving it to freeze again behind.

In the dimness you can see the fire through them. Where the hair parts thin at throat and belly, the skin glows — not bright, not bioluminescence as you think of it in seas, but a deep ember-orange, banked coal, pulsing faintly with that slow chug. Calves glow brightest; they have to. A calf steams hardest of all, tucked in the middle of a moving file, a small furnace working overtime to stay lit. Old bulls glow least and leak most: from glands at the jaw they drip a thick dark oil that freezes the instant it hits air into black beads that collect and rattle in their ruffs. You hear them before you see them, sometimes — a faint clicking as they walk.

And their crowns: wide, flat antlers, not branching but shoveled, like spades held out over the snow. In this dry cold air they collect charge as they move. On overcast afternoons, if you watch a bull lift his head, you will see it — tiny blue discharges crawling tip to tip across the shovel, silent lightning, harmless, endless. No wires. No built thing. Just chemistry and friction and scale.

They do not live as individuals so much as fronts and files. For a week they will graze a single lichen flat, a hundred animals shoulder to shoulder, heads down, steaming, rasping the rock clean in a slow conveyor. You can see the line where they have been: paler stone, cropped rosettes that will take a century to return. Then they lift their heads at once — no signal you can hear — and they walk. Five miles. Fifty miles. In single file along troughs worn into grit by ten thousand years of feet, braided trails no wider than your shoulders that from the air must look like veins. They are not migrating south for winter; there is no south here, only more plateau. They are closing vast loops, three years, five years, ten years to come back to the same tarn, guided by memory of melt and by the smell of thaw on wind. A cow may drink from the same black kettle pool only twice in the life you would live alongside her.

Where they go, the sky goes with them.

Over every herd hangs a veil — white, silent, a mile wide and hardly there until the light catches it. Ten thousand wings beating without sound. They are moth-things, hand-sized, furred to the eyes, with no mouths you can find. They do not eat the grazers. They drink them, or drink off them: they hang in the breath-plumes, harvesting condensation, riding the column of warm air that rises off a hundred backs. When the herd beds, the veil settles low and shimmers. When the herd shifts, it lifts and follows. If you stand in it, they settle on your sleeves for the salt of your frost, weightless, and leave again. In the warm mud of a fresh footprint, if you look quickly before it freezes, you will see their eggs — pale beads pressed into thaw, borrowing a few hours of furnace-heat to begin.

Behind, at a respectful distance, come the grey ones. Low to stone, the color of stone, fox-shaped but heavier, longer in the jaw. They do not hunt so much as audit. They follow a herd for weeks, coughing once, softly — like a valve lifting — and then silent again. They wait for the slow to falter, for a calf's glow to dim, for an old bull to kneel and not get up. Nothing runs here. Even death is economical.

Nothing decays fast, either. A carcass will lie for years, freeze-dried under its own frost, picked at leisure by beetles armored like tiny stoves — black, round, hot to the touch if you pick one up, ticking faintly as they work. Until the bones themselves, polished by wind, slump into a sorted circle, and moss colonizes them, and lichen writes its slow orange coin across a rib.

And under all of it, holding the rock: the crusts that feed the furnaces. Sulfur and ash and dried-blood orange lichens grown directly into stone. Tight cushions of moss in cracks. Ankle-high dwarf willows with fingernail leaves polished red, making not meadows but constellations — a bruise of green-black where water has sat, where dung has steamed and manured, then stone again for a long time.

That is who lives here. Not a people. A process, with hair on it.

Millions of slow hearts, spread so thin you can walk an hour between groups and still know from dung and cropped stone and prints that never fade that you are never alone, all burning poor fare at great heat, exhaling cloud, making water out of air and mud out of frost, carrying their own weather with them across a table that has stopped becoming.
