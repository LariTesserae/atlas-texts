Who lives here is, at first, no one.

That is what the emptiness tells you. You walk a causeway wide as a street with polished bark under your bare feet, mist blue between pillars a mile apart, drip ticking everywhere, and you think: abandoned. A city built and left. Then you learn to see the tending in the absence, and then you learn to see the tenders.

They are sparse as owls. That is the phrase they use for themselves, when they bother to speak at all, which is rarely.

A family — five, eight, twelve souls strung by blood and graft-marriage — will hold a territory the size of a lowland county. Not hold as in own. Hold as in keep from falling apart. One hearth-pocket, a few water-knots, a garden ledge where squash-vines and sweet root have been coaxed to run sideways along a limb for three hundred years, and then leagues of tended emptiness they walk in slow circuits, tightening here, loosening there, pinning a redirect-leaf with a thorn, replacing a lashing fiber by fiber without ever untying it.

You meet them as sign before you meet them as flesh. A knot still tacky with fresh sap. A footprint in moss filling slowly with water. A suckering shoot bent to a tension-stake and tied with living cord that is still green and growing itself into the job. Then, a day later, a flash of dark skin far off across a void, gone before you can raise a hand.

When you do see them close, they are not wild people. They are finished people, worn smooth like the mainways.

Dark-skinned, broad-chested from damp air, barefoot with toes splayed and hard as root-knuckles from a lifetime on springy floor. Their hands are always stained — sap brown under the nails, green-black in the creases — and their stone blades hang at the hip in a sling of bark-cloth, adze and awl and a coil of cord that is alive, kept damp in a moss pouch so it will graft when tied. Men, women, old, young, all carry. All tend. There is no other work.

They do not talk. Not because they cannot. Because the Weave eats sound and they learned long ago not to waste it. Across a void a mile wide they speak in hand-sign, big slow semaphore shapes you can read through mist if you know how: *slack to the left, water heavy above, moth-rise coming.* Along a limb they speak in rope-tugs on the tension-lines strung between camps, codes of pulls every child knows before words. Close, cheek-to-wood, they hum low and feel it through the jawbone, tooth to fiber. If you bite a healthy load-bearing vine you can feel it thrum, a pitch in the molars that says *taut and well*. They navigate half by that, half by the pull in the inner ear.

For down leans here, near the old wood, and they have learned to lean with it.

Their villages are not clearings but tightenings. You will push through a low root-tunnel expecting more tunnel and come out into a fork where a dozen hammock-pods hang like cocoons, each woven from aerial root and lined with down-moss and shed moth-wing, gently swaying. A smoke-ledge juts below, where a small fire of dry heartwood — the only wood that will burn without spitting sap — is kept always low, more for curing cord and smoking eel than for heat. The heat is already there, bathhouse warm on your skin. Above, the children’s work: a nursery of air-plants braided into toys and snares and tiny drip-diverters, lantern-pods hung in rows and tended, their pale glow the nightlight of the place. No walls. No doors. Why would you wall out the air you are inside?

Children are everywhere and nowhere, quiet as adults. They learn early to walk soft, to piss only in the rot-pockets where it feeds the warmth, to never dam a drip only divert, to never cut a load-line without planting its replacement first. They learn knots the way lowland children learn letters, and some knots they do not learn at all but wake with — sleeping in certain fork-hollows you dream the muscle-habit of the tenders who slept there a hundred years before, and wake knowing how to tie what your hands never practiced.

The old live longest in the use of their eyes. The surveyors, almost always old women with sight gone milky from green dusk, go out with knotted cords to measure what cannot be trusted. They will tell you, flatly, walking outward takes longer than walking back. They will tell you which loops not to enter. For there are places where a limb has grafted to its own granddaughter and topology has grown through itself, and if you enter with a lit pod and walk steady you come out behind yourself. Hunters use them to rest — game will not follow through. Children are forbidden them until they can tie their own lifeline, because occasionally someone comes back *thinner*, eyes a little too wide, as if a part of them kept walking. No one makes a story of it. The rule is simple: everything was made, and everything is alive, and living things do not hold shape the way stone does.

The dead are not kept. They are buried shallow in the floor-gardens, where roots take them quickly and the way stays springy. Within a decade, the spot is a thickening. Within a century, you would not know unless you put your ear to the wood on a still evening and heard, under the creak and tick of growth, something like a familiar hum.

And they are not alone. Once you learn to see quietly, the voids fill.

High on the limb-highways move the grazers, dappled and six-limbed, big as tapirs. Six legs for a world with no ground — they straddle the curve of a branch thick as an aqueduct, three feet on each side of the crown, stepping with impossible care. Their prehensile lips peel moss in long sheets. Their calves are parked while mothers feed, wedged snug in cradle-forks, blinking, utterly still. You could walk past and think them a knot of wood until one breathes.

Where grazers go, the flat one waits.

It is not a snake and not a cat, but the tenders call it cat-snake because there is no other word. Long, flat, leaf-green, it does not chase. It *grows*. It lays itself along a trail for weeks, letting moss colonize its back, letting beetles walk it, until a hoof brushes it. Then a blur too fast for damp air, then silence again, then weeks of stillness digesting on the same trail.

Below, in the perched bogs of black blood-warm water cupped in the elbows of giants, live the chorus that makes no sound. Frog-things with throat sacs that pulse in rhythm, white then dark then white — a song for eyes, not ears. Eels like green ribbons slip between pools by way of damp moss bridges during fog. Fish no longer than your finger live in bark-pools and, when the white walls roll through, walk on stiffened fins to the next pool, a hundred determined flips through air that sweats. Salamanders hang in the drip-curtains, translucent, letting water run over their backs.

And through the largest voids move the weather of animals. Moth migrations like slow snowfall, each wing as wide as your hand, silent, leathery shuffle passing like a thought. The tenders go still for these — not from reverence, but because startled moths rise in thousands and clog the cistern filters for weeks. Birds here do not sing. They have no air to spare. They whistle with feathers, slotted primaries cutting damp air into a low thrumming you feel in your sternum, signaling in rhythms across blue distance.

Even rot lives, glowing. Foxfire sheets the undersides of limbs. Pitcher plants big enough to drown a dog gape at the rims of the knots. Beetles click only with their shells. Midges ride the tea-colored shafts of light.

And under and through all of it, the oldest inhabitant: the wood itself. Not backdrop. Citizen.

The Mother Cables, wider than rivers, hum with sap you can feel through your palm like blood in a throat. Tendrils advance inches a night toward the stakes left for them. Leaves turn whole ceilings from dark to silver-green over an afternoon, tracking a sun you never see. On warm evenings after fog, in the largest voids, columns of mist detach and rise too fast, too straight, humming faintly, carrying small frogs and leaves up to the ceiling to start new gardens — and no tender claims to have trained that. They only tend the edges of it.

Put your ear to a mainway at night, when the amber deepens toward brown but never black, and you will hear the country settle: drip-tick, pod-pop, the long creak of living tension taking a suggestion made fifty years ago by hands now in the floor-garden. Far above, real wind screams over the leaf-skin. Far below, through a rent in root-curtains, rock and cold rivers glint, irrelevant.

Up here, life is not abundant the way jungle is abundant. It is deliberate. A dozen people, a herd of six-legged grazers, a patient predator, a voiceless chorus, a snowfall of moths, and miles of tended air between them — all held in a braid they did not build so much as persuade, generation after generation, to hold them.
