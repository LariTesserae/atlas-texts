At first you think: nothing does.

That is the honest first census. You stand on a roof that is also a dam and you look for movement and your eye keeps failing to catch. No scurry in the slot-alleys. No flicker over a cistern. No green to mean someone is eating light the way you understand eating. No black to mean someone has made fire. The low trapezoid doors are either slab-shut or slab-dark, and the dark smells of nothing but wet rock. You call, once, without meaning to, and the sound dies too fast, soaked up by water.

Stay longer than an hour. That emptiness starts to come undone, not because something appears, but because your idea of a resident loosens.

No one here has legs. No one has wings. Nothing has left a dropping or a nest or a web or a footprint that isn't yours filling slowly with seep. If you came looking for an animal — for eyes, for a face that returns your look — you will leave hungry. Whoever lives here never hurries enough to be seen moving.

Crouch. Your knees will get wet. That is the price of census.

The first ones are the stains that aren't stains.

On every wind-dried slab, where the morning sun gets an hour before the weep undoes it, dove-grey pales and then you see them: films so thin they do not rise. Ochre. Rust-black. Sage-grey. At a distance you took them for mineral bleed. Up close, with your breath fogging and then clearing, they have edges. Centers. A direction of growth. A lichen so starved and so sun-beaten it gave up on being leafy, on being mossy, on rising at all. It lies down inside the stone's own roughness and drinks.

It drinks fog you can't quite see. It drinks the bead that forms after rain has stopped by hours. It drinks high white light that would blind you — unfiltered, hard coin sun — and makes, a millimeter a year, more of itself. Put your nose to it and there is no smell. Put your fingertip near — don't touch, you'll tear a decade — and you feel the cold coming off it slightly less sharp, as if it holds a fraction of warmth. On the north spine, that long stepped causeway running rim to rim, they run in vertical streaks where the sides weep in regular lines: rust-black beards ten feet long and only as thick as paint. Each streak is one individual, or one city, patiently lengthening downward one wet season at a time. It will never meet the bottom in your lifetime.

The second ones are the skins on stillness.

Go south after rain, where the terraces widen into broad ankle-deep pans, or east to the tall cones with their caps missing, each holding two feet of sky-mirror water. Wait until wind drops. The surface goes perfect. Then, if you stare long enough to make your eyes water from glare, you see it: a film, clear as nothing, tightening across the water. Silica. Stone becoming skin becoming stone again.

Disturb it with a fingertip and it cracks into polygons, pale plates that tilt and clink almost inaudibly and sink, very slowly. Come back in a day and it has reformed. It is not alive the way a fish is alive. There is nothing swimming under it — the water is too clean, distilled and stone-filtered, cold as glass with only a mineral bite, nothing to eat in it. But the film itself keeps the ledger: dissolve by day in bright cold, precipitate by night when breath condenses on a thousand roofs and runs again in threads. It grows by vanishing and returning. In the great central sump — that rectangular pool lined so tight it looks cut from one stone — you can see it thickest at the edges, a clear lip that creaks when the level rises a finger in a week. That is its speed. That is its stride.

The third ones live where you cannot live.

Find a covered channel, where a runnel drops through a chink into dark. Lie flat and look in. Your forehead will get wet from drip-pause-drip. In the utter black a half-arm in, hanging from the lintel stone, is frost that isn't frost: white fringe, hair-fine, trembling when sheet-flow hisses over pavement above. Fungus, but not the forest kind. No rot to eat here — nothing rots, there is nothing to rot, the stone is young and sharp and fresh-broken. So it eats damp itself. It eats dark. It eats the breath of the mountain coming up through every slab.

It does not spread beyond the dark. That is its covenant. An inch out into the white light and it stops, as if cut with a knife. So each covered runnel, each sunken cistern with dark water four feet down, each hand-sized cup pitted into a sill where water collects in a perfect thread, has its own small hanging garden that will never colonize the roof above. Lift a capstone — and you shouldn't — and in a day it greys and retreats. Replace it and in a month it lets down new threads.

These three — the paint, the skin, the fringe — are not neighbors so much as they are the same appetite expressed in sun and in stillness and in dark.

And then there is the fourth resident, if you stay until late enough for the weirdness to sharpen by a degree.

Water keeps running long after rain has stopped, long after fog has burned off. Put your palm flat on a north-facing wall and in a minute there is your damp hand-print, and then beads beyond it. The mountain is exhaling up through everything built on it. The builders — hammerstones and antler picks and hide ropes and human backs, fire-cracked blocks and peck-marks and wedge-notches on every block — thought, for generations it must have taken, that they could cap it. They fitted everything. Roof touches roof. Wall becomes terrace becomes stair becomes channel becomes cistern lip. Every surface sheds to the next. Thousands of steps to slow a cup of water for days from rim to sump, fall by fall, half-inch tap like a clock ticking too slowly to time.

They didn't cap it. They terraced it. They honored it, step by step down.

What lives here now is that slowing itself. Not a creature, but a persistence. You feel it more in the soles than hear it when at long intervals the whole packed mass settles with a deep soft *tock*, stones finding each other. You see it when wet rock goes almost black and dry rock pales to dove grey and the whole valley flickers as clouds thin and thicken, dark patches blooming and fading. You hear it under the quiet, the thin constant stitching of trickles.

No hearth-smoke ever stained these stones because no one who needed fire could stay. Your fingertips go dull in minutes. Your nose runs from cold while your eyes water from glare. The water numbs. There is no soil to take it, no midden because no meal was ever finished here. The west field of low cairns — each man-high, each exactly as wide as a man with arms out, packed so tight you walk over their saddles, not between — is not houses. It is not needed anymore to say what it is. The east cones hold sky, not smoke. The doors open onto black that smells only of wet rock because the occupants never left and never arrived in bodies.

They arrived as slowness. They stayed as stain and skin and fringe.

If you come back in ten thousand years, when the sharp fresh-broken edges have finally rounded and you no longer cut your hand bracing carelessly, they will still be here, a finger wider, a thread longer, a polygon reformed. The cistern levels will still be rising a finger a week. The crusts will still be creeping. The water will still go down, evaporate in bright cold, condense again at night on a thousand roofs, and begin again.
