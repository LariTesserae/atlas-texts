First you think nothing lives here.

Not really. Not in the way a town is lived in. It's too bright, too thin, too sharp. The air scrapes. The light hammers. The edges haven't been worn down yet — you cut your hand on a stair rail on your first morning and understand: this place is too young to be kind. People work here, obviously. Tools left neatly beside an open panel. A cup left out on a ledge to catch drip. A jacket hung over a valve wheel. But no one is *in* them. For a long hour on the upper decks you see only the other life — pigeons boiling off a louver in utter gray silence, a marmot poured into an expansion joint like fat into a mold, a cat belly-up and asleep on a transformer, soaking sun through its fur while frost rims the steel an inch away. You think: ah. The animals got here first. Humans commute.

Then you start to notice the pace, and you realize you have been seeing people all along and misreading them as things.

They move so slowly you took them for part of the structure. A figure in patched canvas half in shadow by a cistern, not moving, just holding a cup under a drip-wire. Tock. Tock. Tock. She does not fidget. She does not check a watch. She waits for water the way lichen waits. On a gantry a hundred meters long, two figures creep with it, one hand always on the rail, taking a full minute to cross three meters. They are not lazy. At this height your own heart is hammering after stairs. To hurry is to gasp and go blind at the edges. They have learned what the engines already know — huge, patient strokes, a piston completing at dusk what it started at noon. If you are going to live encased on top of a mountain with your lungs half-full, you learn to do everything at walking pace, then slower.

Closer, they come into focus and they are all weather in the same way.

Sunburnt and frost-nipped at once. That's the first mark. Faces raw pink across the cheekbones and nose where the glare got in, lips cracked white at the corners where the cold got in. Eyes narrowed to permanent slits, crow's feet deep as weld beads, irises washed pale blue or gray — everyone squints here, even in sleep. They wear goggles pushed up on foreheads, leaving a clean band in the dust and oil. Their clothes are layers of wool and waxed canvas and salvaged orange insulation-blanket, all smelling of ozone and hot oil and wet stone, because everything here smells of that after a week. Rubber boots with the tread worn but the nosings still sharp — they need grip for the morning when every rail and wire beads and the whole mass glitters and then drips and goes slick. And pockets. Every one of them furred with pockets like the buildings are furred with junction boxes. Chalk in the breast pocket, always. A tin cup hooked to the belt. A twist of wire, a bolt, a feather — both offering and tools.

Because they whisper. You will not hear them coming. Sound dies here within twenty paces and they have stopped fighting it.

They don't shout. The old hands told you why on your second day without looking up from a seal they were smoothing: shout and it comes back flattened and late, from a different direction. So they speak low, in short sentences, or not at all. A tap code on pipes — two taps: *I'm here*. Three: *mind the step*. A palm flashed with a mirror for *all clear across*. Much of their talk is chalk. Arrows and dates and small private glyphs at every corner: *I was here this morning, this way held.* *Don't trust this stair after rain.* *New room, do not store.* You learn quickly not to trust chalk more than a day old. They don't. Every morning the apprentices go out and re-chalk the true ways, rubbing out yesterday's ghosts with their sleeves. It's prayer and maintenance at once.

They live by sun and shadow the way tide people live by tide. Stay in the sun if you want to stay warm; stay in shadow if you want water. At noon on a clear day the sun decks fill — cats and humans side by side on the warm plates, faces tipped up, eyes closed, steaming faintly as dew lifts off their coats. An hour later the shadow crews come out with cups and rags, wringing condensate from tarps strung under catwalks, siphoning drip barrels rimed with ice even in summer. The canteen soup, when you finally get it, tastes of iron and frost and faint oil, and you drink it gratefully because it's warm and wet at once.

Work is glacial and total. No one hurries a cure. A team will unbolt a dynamo cage, stare at the blue corona spitting inside, replace one insulator, pack the seal, leave their tools laid out in a row and go home for the week. When you ask when it will run they shrug: when it's ready. When the lichen decides. The rolling mills turn, the bottling lines clink — what they bottle is mostly water, cistern water sealed against the dry days — the ore belt inches along with its load of crushed glass and grain and whatever the lowlands sent up. Maintenance is the real industry. Tightening, repacking, wiping dew off contacts before it shorts. They tend the dense mass the way beekeepers tend a hive that is also their house.

And they tend the wrongness without fear, which is how you know they live here and you don't yet.

Where you see a misaligned corner and your stomach drops — the alley that puts you one level higher without climbing, the shadow that leans toward the sun — they see a place to leave a bolt, a feather, a twist of wire. Little cairns of attention. *Don't trust your eyes here,* the chalk says, and underneath in a steadier hand, *step across if you're paying attention.* The children are best at it. There are children, a few, thin-bright and quiet as swifts, born up here with lungs that never knew thick air. They play a game of stepping: eyes fixed on the duplicate blue water tower shimmering impossibly near-far across the central court, one foot placed very deliberately *between* the oil stain and the dead pigeon and the chalk mark — and they are gone from this stair and giggling on the next deck over. The adults watch and do not follow quickly. They know the middle stairs repeat if you are not paying attention. Three times the same stain, if you let it.

Don't follow a cat onto a new deck. Every child can recite it. The cats know where that deck will be tomorrow; you don't. And yet the cats are citizens with more rights than any foreman. Lean feral toms with frost-tipped whiskers walk the pipe highways at dusk alongside the rats and are given way. They sleep in the warm voids, they drink first at the sump courtyard where black water stands a foot deep and perfectly mirrors the sky. They go into the new rooms first.

Ah, the new rooms. This is where you finally understand who else lives here.

Because the town is still emerging. It hasn't settled on its shape. You weld a door shut and find it open the next morning with fresh sharp edges, smelling of mineral and cold, uncured concrete still dark. The old hands nod. It grew a new door overnight. Don't store anything important in a wall less than a year old, because it might not be there in the morning, or might be twice as large.

If you are up early, before the sun hammers the dew away, you can sometimes catch the budding. A seam weeping gray milk. A plate bulging almost imperceptibly. And inside, if you put your ear — not your eye — to the fresh steel, a faint ticking, wetter than cooling metal. The uncured ones are in there.

No one has seen them clearly, which is the point. They are not for bright noon. The void-crawlers — the lanky apprentices who service the under-decks and the service voids full of pipes — speak of them matter-of-factly, the way sailors speak of weather. Pale damp impressions in fresh dust that are not boot prints. A cup moved. A nest of wire twists and moth wings and pigeon down tucked into a weep hole that wasn't there yesterday, still damp, smelling of stone. Lights buzzing on in an empty hall *before* you enter, as if something just left, or is still deciding to be seen. They do not hurt. They drink condensation. They eat heat. Like everything else here that isn't green — and there is almost nothing green, just moss black-green in drip lines, lichen like pale coins eating concrete a micron at a time, bittercress shivering where two plates meet — they live off scraps and warmth and each other.

Some say they are the town itself, learning to be inhabited. Some say they are what the mountain dreams under its casing, still leaning and aching under you. Some say they are us, doubled — because you have seen it too, haven't you? The same blue water tower in two districts at once, both real enough to cast shadows. Far across, impossibly near, its amber work-light blinks on at dusk. And sometimes on the far deck a figure in patched canvas lifts a tin cup to a drip-wire at exactly the same moment you do.

At dusk the light doesn't fade so much as sharpen. The peaks go pink, then blue. The valley-that-isn't-a-valley turns on its million small amber lights and every bead on every wire catches fire until the whole dense, built-through mass sparkles like something wet and alive. The starling murmurations write and erase vast calligraphy over the roofs without a sound you can hear. The goats pick their impossible traverses along the outer walls, eating paper. The spiders mend. The moths crowd the lamps that never turn off. Somewhere deep below your feet that huge piston completes its stroke.

And the people — the slow, squinting, whispering, chalk-handed people — stand a while in the last sun with their faces up, prickling with cold while their eyes water from glare, listening for drips more than flows. Tock. Tock. Water from wire to wire to deck to cup. They are not visitors. They have agreed to the rules. Move slowly. Mark your way. Stay warm, stay wet. Leave the new rooms alone until they cure.

They live here the way moss lives in a drip line: entirely, in a crack in something enormous, patient, bright and cold and crowded, humming just below hearing.
