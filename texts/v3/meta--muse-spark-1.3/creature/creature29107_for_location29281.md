At first you think nothing lives here.

That's what the room wants you to think. It's too loud for life, too hard, too made. The floor rings under your boot through two millimeters of water. The motor fills your molars. Green looks like stain, not plant. Black looks like oil, not algae. You, standing and dripping, are the largest animal that has been in here for months, and you feel very alone in the roar.

Then your eyes adjust — not to dark, there's plenty of light, workmanlike light from the caged LED and the slatted vent — but to scale. And then it is crowded.

The ground is alive. That's the first citizenry.

Moss has taken the room the way a city takes a plain. Not tall, not proud, but total. Star moss making little green rosettes along the wall bases where the concrete never dries. Silver moss silvering the mortar joints that have gone soft as cake. Feather moss feathering out over the floor margins where your shoe never quite lands. Two inches high at its tallest and that's a forest here. If you got down on your knees — you'll get wet, there's no dry path — you'd see it as canopy and understory: upright shoots holding water in their crowns, rhizoids gripping not soil, because there is no soil, but lime and grit and the pitted face of seventy-year-old concrete. Everything soft has something hard an inch under it. The moss knows. It doesn't try to root deep. It mats.

Living in the mat, because a mat is a climate: springtails you will never see, pale as lint, vaulting between stems on a forked tail when the vibration pauses, which it never does. Mites like slow red punctuation. Nematodes swimming in the film between leaves. Rotifers unspooling. The moss is wet from both directions — seep wicking up from below, condensation raining down from your elbows, from the pipes, from the air itself — and it likes it. In August and February the same. It advances a millimeter. It sloughs a millimeter. It stays.

Where it is wettest, the liverworts have laid themselves flat. Dark green kidneys pressed to the weeping wall as if listening for the water table on the other side. No stem, no pride, just thallus, a liver-shaped mouth drinking the runnel. They don't resist the wet. They become a second skin for it.

Under the standing water, the algae does the base work of the world. Black-green slick on every submerged surface, on the painted concrete worn back to aggregate, on the sump trench tile, on the upside-down plastic bucket that now wears a moss cap like a hat. It's slippery enough to take you down if you hurry. Don't hurry. The algae is photosynthesizing under an LED meant to illuminate a breaker box. It doesn't care. Photons are photons. It makes oxygen in a room with no wind.

And then the elders: the ferns.

They shouldn't be here. Maidenhair with its black wire stems and leaflets thin as tissue, sweating constantly and loving it. Rusty-back fern tucked into the lime sludge in the corners, its undersides furred orange-brown against the lime-white. And the big one — big for here, knee-high if it stretched — the Boston fern behind the motor, feathering out of the rust-dust drift, must have escaped from someone's pot decades ago and ridden dust down the vent. They root in impossibilities: grout crumb, calcite dust fallen from the inch-long stalactites on the ceiling seam, the grease-lime paste behind the plinth that has vibrated half an inch out of square.

They hang from the vent grille, they feather out of the pipe hangers, they fringe the west wall trench where water falls six inches in a continuous glassy sheet and roars. Their fronds drip constantly. Their spores have dusted the breaker box lid green, a fine powder trying to colonize steel. They tremble. Not in wind — there is no wind — but with the motor, with the 60-cycle hum that runs up through concrete and into root. They have learned to live in a permanent tremor. Two inches high is a forest if it covers everything. They cover everything they can reach that stays damp and not drowned.

The animals are rumors because they have to be. Anything large would be heard, or starve, or drown. No rats. Too loud, too wet, nowhere to nest that isn't already moss.

But in the sump trench along the west wall lives a king. A chorus frog, impossibly. The size of your thumbnail, brown as grout, tucked in the fern fringe where the overflow falls. You won't see him. The sheet-fall is too bright, the roar too total, your glasses have fogged in three breaths anyway. He lives on silverfish and midges that ride ivy tendrils down from the vent and on the sheer stubbornness of amphibians. Most of the year the pump and the seep are in perfect ancient agreement and the water level never moves, so his world never moves. Once, in high storm, the pump cycles for three minutes — a different pitch, a surge — and the trench drops. After, in the ringing second before the roar reasserts, you hear him. Indignant. A single *kreek*. As if correcting the motor.

The silverfish own the paperwork. An old lockout tag still tied to the discharge pipe, paper gone soft as moss, ink bled to a ghost. They work it at night — though it's always night-bright here — long silver teardrops with three tail bristles, antennae tasting damp cellulose. They eat the past. They avoid the light by living under its edge, in the shadow of the conduit, in the coiled hose fossilized to its hook. Vibration doesn't bother them. They are vibration, in miniature.

Once a year, a pilgrim. A stray slug finds its way down the concrete stair where it shouldn't be, under the propped steel door, following the smell of wet green. Leopard slug, probably, spotted, huge for this room — three inches of muscle in a two-inch world. It will cross the velvet mat for hours, rasping algae, rasping moss tips, leaving a silver road that the standing water erases in minutes. It will not find a mate. It will not leave. It will dry at the edges despite the wet air because of the warmth, or it will fall in the trench and become frog food. The room allows one slug a year. That is the quota.

Unseen, uncounted: the builders of the cave. In the drip from the ceiling seam, in the bead-darken-runnel on the wall, bacteria are precipitating calcite, tooth-colored, an inch, two inches of stalactite. They are turning groundwater into geology. They are turning made stuff back into rock. Give them another fifty years and they'll close the seam they came through. The room will find another.

And then there is you, and the man who changes the bulb. The large, temporary mammals who have to put mouth to ear to be heard, who bead and run off at the elbows, who carry fern spores out on their shoulders and the motor frequency in their sternums. You are weather here, not fauna. You come in with highway dust sifting from the vent, trucks going over every seven seconds overhead. You shout or you are silent; there is no conversational middle. You leave. The moss closes the dent your boot made in two millimeters of water. The frog resumes. The silverfish resume. The ferns go on dripping.

It is not dying, not living large, just continuing. A tiny built cave that learned how to be a marsh and then stopped learning. And in that staying — warm, roaring, weeping, green on grey — everything small enough, wet enough, patient enough, lives.
