First you think nothing lives here.

The rain is so loud, the space so empty, the light so white, that for an hour you only see water falling into water. Then your eyes learn the rule of the tank: a few presences hold a lot of emptiness between them. You stop looking for a crowd and you start seeing the holders.

The smallest come first.

In the shafts under the oculus, where the rain looks solid, there are clouds that are not rain. Almost-transparent shrimp, each no longer than an eyelash, pulsing. They hang in the fresh upper water — the sweet layer, chest-high and up, clear and moving — and they rise and fall with the pump-surge. When a swallow cuts through, they scatter like dust hit by breath and then re-form, patiently, in the exact same column of light. If you hold your hand still in the current, they will gather in your palm because it is shade. You can feel them as a faint fizz, nothing more. At night — though there is hardly night here, only a dimming — they sink a little lower, toward the skin.

The skin itself is alive. Between the sweet water on top and the dark salt water at your knees, where the two seas refuse to mix, a thin shimmer forms: bacteria, oil beading rainbow-thin, pollen from the little white high flowers, iron fines. It tears when you wade and heals behind you. If you lie back very still you are held up by it. It holds footprints for a second. The shrimp graze its underside. The catfish nose it from below and never break it.

Below the skin, in the salt — knee-deep and down, dark and still and smelling of iron and old oil — live the blind ones.

They are pale as the inside of a wrist, a school of catfish, maybe a dozen, maybe twenty, it is hard to count because they never stop. They circle the sagged middle where the water is chin-deep, following the gyre the circulation pumps make, *dum-dum, dum-dum,* mouths open. They have no eyes, or eyes that gave up, smooth pearls under skin. They navigate by taste and tremor. When the big overflow gulps every seven minutes and the level drops a hand's breadth, they tilt together like one thought and circle tighter until it climbs back. You will never see one still. Stillness is not a thing they do.

Commuting between those two waters are the eels.

Black and muscular and warm as the water itself, thick as your forearm, they slip between floor plates where the iron has softened and gapped. They live mostly in the dark salt, coiled around stanchion feet, but they rise — fast, sudden — up through the skin into the sweet to hunt shrimp, to snatch a pillbug knocked loose, to surface with a slap-splash that is louder than anything except the rain. Then they sink back down, trailing a cloak of fresh water that peels off them. If you stand very still your legs disappear into their sea, and sometimes one will brush your calf, curious, incurious, gone.

Above the waterline, on the islands, live the grazers.

Armored pillbugs the size of thumbs work the moss pillows on the catwalk beams and the columns. Slate-grey, jointed, solemn. They graze all day in the splash zone, mowing algae, turning rust-mud and guano into themselves. When a drip hits them they do not flinch. When a swallow screams over they tuck, just slightly. If you lift a mat of water lettuce you will find their pale young underneath, crowded like seeds. They are the cattle of this place. Slow, inexhaustible.

And all over the metal, holding the islands together, is the jungle that is also an animal in the way a reef is an animal.

Long curtains of emerald filament algae hang from pipes and sway in the pump-surge like hair in a tide — each strand strung with oxygen bubbles and shrimp. Ferns grow straight out of rivet lines, their white roots reaching down naked into the water, drinking air. Water lettuce and duckweed raft in slow gyres around the four weeping columns, opening and closing their circuit endlessly. On the beams just above splash, moss has built pillows six inches thick, springy, saturated. From them hang aerial roots and small white orchid-like flowers that only open in full damp, waxy, smelling faintly of vanilla and hot metal. Watercress chokes the quiet eddies behind the broken catwalk — peppery, bright green, the only thing here that tastes like somewhere else.

Above all of that — because there is an above, a foot of humid air under the dome where rain is born — live the swallows.

They should not be here. They have come up from impossible distances, past the last cloud deck, past thinning air and screaming wind, up nine kilometers of tower, to nest on the joists under the intake crown. Their nests are made, remarkably, from rust-mud, red-black flour plastered pellet by pellet, lined with white down and filament algae. Guano streaks the walls white-green below them and feeds the ferns; that is the bargain. All day they cut through the rain shafts screaming — shrill stitching you can hear even under the roar — then fold their wings and dive straight *into* the water to snatch shrimp, bursting back out in an explosion of droplets, shaking mid-air. They skim inches over your face when you float. They sleep pressed together, beaks tucked, while the tower sways almost imperceptibly in the high wind and the water stays perfectly level.

And then, last, the newest animal. You.

Not the eels, not the birds — the one in the hammock. You learn quickly to live like the others: zoned, sparse, interim. You hang your hammock from the catwalk nubs, above the splash line but below the swallows, where the moss won't soak you and the guano won't find you. You sleep in short naps between pump cycles, waking at the gulp. You wade slowly so you don't tear the skin more than you have to. You eat watercress by the handful, and duckweed when you are tired of watercress, and you never, ever touch the moss-furred valve wheel the size of a cartwheel, because you understand now: keep moving to stay still. The pumps must pump, the rain must fall, the shrimp must rise, the birds must dive, you must breathe shallow and listen.

If you stay long enough you become part of the turnover — rust and rain and light made briefly into a warm body that listens to iron breathing around it, warm as blood, holding its little emptiness faithfully against the sky.
