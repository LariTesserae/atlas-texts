At first you think nothing does.

Not really. Not in the way a house has a sleeper or a well has a frog. The lime is too new, the water too hot and too loud, the light too much all at once. Anything with eyes would leave.

Then you stay a minute longer than is comfortable and you see that something has already decided to stay.

Green. First.

In the mortar joints just above the slap-line, where the seiche kisses and falls back, a sparse beard. Felt, not leaf. A thin olive fur of algae and, among it, white threads nosing down through the cracks — root hairs from the courtyard weeds above, blind, reaching for the damp through a meter of stone. Below the waterline, where the current eddies weakest behind the entrance step and in the lee of the bench foot, patches of it on the fresh plaster. Olive film, waving minutely. If you put your face close you can see it breathe with the pull of the spillway.

It is not much. But it is resident. It does not visit. It was born here last rains, after the trowels left, and it will be scraped off and will come back in a week, and be scraped off and come back. It eats sun through the grate and drinks lime. It can stand being boiled and deafened.

And then — because that green needs scraping, because *in must equal out* — there is the other thing that lives here. Not all day. But enough that the room has shaped him.

A boy. Small. He has to be small.

He was chosen because he can stand upright under the shallow dome without ducking and because he can stretch and touch both walls at once to steady himself when the four jets are all running strong and the floor drags at his calves. Nine, maybe ten. Second son, courtyard-brown, courtyard-loud, except down here he isn't.

Down here you would know him by his feet first. Bare, always bare — sandals would slip on the sharp-nosed treads and tear the plaster. The soles are white and ridged from lime, the toes wrinkled even dry, the toenails with a green tinge in the quick that soap won't take out. His shins are white to the knee with plaster dust that never quite washes. His shoulders and the back of his neck are pink-burnt in a crosshatch — the shadow of the grate laid across sunburn, renewed every day.

He comes down the ladder once a day with the basalt scraper in his teeth, and he does not hurry back up.

Up top is white noon and shouting and orders. Down here the four jets deafen everything to one roar. His own voice comes back off the wet walls doubled and useless, so he has stopped trying to talk down here. He leans close to the water to hear his own breathing. He likes it. No one can call him through that sound.

He knows the room the way an animal knows a burrow. Which spout runs coldest — the east one, fed deepest — and so where to stand when the top stratum gets bath-hot. How to wade without making a seiche that slaps over the bench and wets the rope. How to clear the timber grate by hand, lying on his belly on the hot beams with the bleached sky hurting his eyes, pulling leaves while light falls through in bars across his back. How to dip with the long-handled dipper and bail silt from the central sump without clouding the whole cup. How to run the flat basalt lightly, lightly, over the olive film — never clean. He leaves patches on purpose now, behind the step, under the bench. His garden. He has watched it come back three times. He is proud of it.

When the work is done he sits. That is the living part.

He backs onto the low plastered bench until the water is at his throat and lets the mist hang chest-high over him and lets the moving nets of brightness crawl up the walls and across his skin. Spray condenses under the beams and rains back down in fat secondary drops on his upturned face. He opens his mouth to it. The tall clay jar with its cloth stopper is drinking water from uphill — this water is too limy, too warm, it coats the teeth — but sometimes he drinks it anyway with the dipper, to taste the stone.

He does not think in there. There is no room left for thought, only ribs-deep warm roar and sun. His hearing rings for an hour after he climbs back up the ladder into the glare, and the courtyard shouting sounds thin to him, far away, like something heard through water.

If you came back in a year, when the plaster has learned to be smooth and the chisel scallops are worn round and frogs have found the spillway, you might find other animals. Now there is only this: a green sheen that clings, white roots reaching blindly down, and a small lime-white boy who is more at home in the roar than out of it, tending them with stone and hands.
