At first you think it is empty.

That is the trick of it. You look for inhabitants the way you know to look — for faces, for movement, for something that walks out of a door — and you see only the city. Walls. Towers. Streets kept clean. It seems abandoned, left behind by whatever built it.

Then a wall breathes.

Not metaphorically. The white cliff beside you, which you took for stucco, inhales — a million mouths at once drawing in — and the current stutters. You put your hand near and feel the pull on your skin. The cliff is alive. The cliff *is* the residents.

That is who lives here: the ones who are the place.

They do not have hands, so they made homes out of their own held-open bodies. And they are still in them.

The most numerous are the holders-on. You have to let your eyes unlearn size to see them right. Each oyster, each mussel, is a soft animal the size of a tongue, folded inside two calcified lips. Alone, helpless. Together, they are a tenement. They shouldered against each other as spat, as settling larvae no bigger than dust, and grew until their shells buckled and deformed each other into tight hexagons, into honeycomb. Now they cannot move and do not need to. They lie with lips agape upstream, all of them, a whole block facing into the warm mineral flow from the vents. Inside each shell a fringe of gill beats ceaselessly, combing the water. When something large passes, or your shadow — your pressure — falls on them, they close. *Click.* Then *click click click* down the whole facade, a million tiny valves shutting like windows in a storm. Then, slowly, they dare to open again.

Among them, bristling from every cornice and rooftop, the tower-dwellers. At first they look like fringe, like the city has hair. Lean closer — as close as the dark lets you — and each hair resolves into a life. A worm, two inches of red segmented gut, living inside a pipe it secreted grain by grain, mucus and limestone, a finger-width chimney swaying almost imperceptibly. Only its crown shows: two spirals of feathery radioles, unfolding like ferns, sieving. There are thousands side by side, tens of thousands, a shivering field of crowns all tilted the same way into the current, all withdrawing in a rippling flinch when a fish darts over. Underneath, generation under generation of empty tubes, the dead still holding up the living.

And the giants who do not move at all and yet pump the city like hearts. The sponges. Barrel-bodied, massive as houses, pitted and scarred, squatting on the old coral knuckles. They have no mouths, no faces, no front or back — only thousands of incoming pores and one great exhalant osculum gaping on top like a chimney, like a door left open. Put your hand over it and you feel their slow exhale, cleaned water pushed downstream, steady as breathing in sleep. Their skin is velvet over stone, and their skin is also apartments: brittle stars with striped, jointed arms wave from every pit; anemones bud along their sides, opening and closing their petaled stings; barnacles volcanoed on their flanks kick their feathered feet in synchrony, kick, kick, kick. An octopod the size of a dog has taken one great barrel as a den, wedged its mantle inside, and leaves one arm always curled out the door, tasting the street.

Sheeted over all of it, stuccoing the joints, the lace-makers. You will not see an individual unless you stop and let your eyes go soft on a shell-edge: bryozoans, colonies of zooids each smaller than an eyelash, building lacy calcium crusts over everything dead or tired; forams, single cells with shells, gluing sand grain to sand grain with organic cement. They are the plasterers, the ones who smooth the old districts round, who turn sharp new tubes into weathered knuckles.

These are the citizens who stay. Who *are* the staying.

Then the ones who keep it alive by moving through it.

The water above the rooftops is not water. It is plankton made almost solid — schools of copepods and shrimp-motes and larval fish hanging so thick they change the viscosity, pulsing when you push your arm through. Each larva no bigger than an eyelash, each perfectly formed, eye-spotted and transparent, brushing your lips as they part around you. They are the city's future and its weather at once. Everything eats them. Through them dart the traffic: small fish, blind or big-eyed, black or silver or utterly transparent, mouths disproportionately wide. They do not school so much as commute, mouths open, sieving. Eels pour like slow rain from window to window. Cleaner shrimp set up stations in doorways — an open moray mouth, patient, while tiny striped bodies pick parasites from its gums like barbers.

On the ground, the maintenance crew. Crabs, sideways, deliberate, picking along cornices, cracking the weak and the dead. Sea cucumbers fat as sandbags bulldozing the alleys, vacuuming silt, leaving clean swept sand behind them. Hundreds of sand dollars edge to edge in the plaza, slowly plowing, planing the floor flat. White bone-blind lobsters tapping their way across your foot with antennae longer than their bodies, unconcerned. Fat brittle stars carpeting every vertical surface, arms up to catch snow.

And the hunters who live in the interstices. Anglers waiting patiently at intersections, each with its own small blue-green lure-dot swaying, each utterly still until something touches. Snapping shrimp hidden in cracks, making that startlingly close pop and sizzle, a knuckle cracking in a cathedral — a shockwave to stun prey. Eels in holes. Octopods tasting doors.

Nothing here sees far. There is nothing far to see. Light is a rumor — 0.1, a memory. So they have given up eyes or remade them huge and uselessly hopeful. They navigate by taste and pressure: the limestone tang upstream, the rot-sweet of a fish that died in an alley that morning and by evening is already scaffolding, its spine picked clean, its ribs become apartments for amphipods; the metallic-sweet ash from the rift; the spawn-slick in the water when the whole oyster block releases at once. They feel the city lean, very slightly, into the flow.

Stay longer, hanging neutrally buoyant mid-street, warm and weightless. You will feel them settle around your strangeness. The larval cloud closes behind you. The oysters re-open. The filtration hum resumes — that constant low living sound of gills and cilia and feathered feet.

Above you something large passes without sound. Only a pressure wave, a displaced volume, a smell of iodine. A ray beating its wings over sand with a soft *purrr*, perhaps. Or something else. It is gone before your eyes can assemble it, and the city, which flinched, resumes.

No one planned this. Everyone maintains it. The builders cannot leave their walls. The cleaners cannot live without the dirt the builders make. The dead become tenements.

That is who lives here: an animal town where to live is to build, and to build is to offer your body as ground for someone else.
