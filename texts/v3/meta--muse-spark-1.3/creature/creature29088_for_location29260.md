They are not, at first, animals at all.

At first they are texture. Scum on stone. Cauliflower fuzz. The wall looks furred and you think fur — until the fur resolves into crowns, and the crowns resolve into mouths, and the mouths are all working.

Then they come apart, layer by layer, and they do not stop coming apart. The closer you get, the more of them there are.

They are small. That is the wrong word. They are *scaled to this world* — for them this pocket, long as a man lying down, is a country. A rice-grain body here is a citizen's body, a full life's length. An eyelash is tall. A thumb-height tower is a high-rise, tenanted for generations.

Look down first, to the floor avenues.

The tower-keepers. Pale pink-gray terebellids, each one a soft gut in a hard house it made from its own shit and spit and the corpses of its neighbors. Only the crown shows: a spilling bouquet of feeding tentacles, grooved, ciliated, endlessly sorting. A flake of marine snow lands — dead diatom, fecal pellet from thirty meters above and a different world — and twenty tentacles confer over it, taste it, lip it down a groove to a hidden mouth. They never stop eating because the snow never quite stops, and because what they miss feeds everyone else.

They are landlords without meaning to be. On the leeward flank of almost every tower, tucked out of the faint convection, sits a lodger: a white anemone no bigger than a drop of tallow. Its column is stubby, its tentacles stubbier, but it opens wider than seems possible. It is a thief and a sweeper. It catches the crumbs the worm's crown fumbles. In return — though no bargain was ever struck — its nematocysts make the tower's base a slightly more dangerous place to climb. Around the foundations, amphipods have plastered mud lean-tos, rough half-domes of silt glued with their own leg-silk, each with a doorway exactly amphipod-wide. They dart out for scraps and dart back. Between towers, the packed avenues are alive with feet. Ostracods — seed-shrimp in bivalved shells — trundle along the curbs made of their ancestors' empty shells set on edge, white on gray.

None of them look up often. They don't need to.

Look sideways, to the walls.

The masons. Tube amphipods and tanaids, comma-bodies with too many legs and too much patience, blind to distance but exquisite at touch. They do not find homes. They secrete them. A dab of mucus, a grain of grit picked up, tasted, rejected, picked up again, placed. Another dab. Another grain. Day after day for a decade — for here in -1.7 degree water, held still and viscous, an amphipod can live ten years tending the same few millimeters of tube. You can see its life in the wall: thin, pinched rings for the winters when snow did not fall, sudden flared balconies for a good week in spring when a copepod carcass rained down.

Their tubes have become tenements, three and four deep, branching and re-branching, honeycomb gone organic. From every mouth, antennae. Flick, flick, pause. The walls hum — not with sound, but with traffic. The commuters come through: fat copepods, translucent as gel with one red eye, riding mucus highways strung between terraces. The highways glisten. They sag and bounce. They break, and are repaired within the hour by a commuter turning around and licking fresh silk back into tension, patching with spit. Everyone maintains the road they use. There is no other rule that would work.

They know each other by chemistry. A flick of antennae on a passing carapace: fed or hungry, kin or stranger, carrying eggs or carrying a wound. Anemones close as a shadow passes. Egg-fans never stop.

Look up — which here means look to the ceiling, though no one here believes in up.

The hangers. Upside-down, unbothered. Sulfur-pale and iron-orange bacterial mats spread like felted lawns across the rock, and on them, pinhead limpets graze. They move so slowly you have to leave and come back to know they moved, but their radulae leave clean-shaven hedges and paths. They farm without knowing the word farm: graze a lawn hard enough and it grows back thicker; weed out the inedible black filaments and the good orange spreads. They defend it. Two armies of snails, each individual slower than a minute hand, are advancing across one lawn toward each other right now. They will meet tomorrow. No one will hurry.

Among the felt hang the nurseries. Clusters of eggs in jelly, amber and clear, each the size of a dust mote, each tended. The mothers — small shrimp, tanaid mothers, amphipod mothers — hang inverted and fan. Pleopods beat, beat, beat, never stopping, because in water that barely moves oxygen must be brought to the eggs by force. A soft hydraulic sigh, swimmeret after swimmeret. If she stops for an hour they suffocate. So she does not stop. For weeks she does not stop.

And in the water itself, which is not empty volume but downtown air, street, river:

The dust. Clouds of nauplii, larval everything, comma-specks with three limbs and one eye, drifting in the pale fissure shaft, fin over fin, near-touching but never colliding. Density is protocol. A shrimp passes — a full-grown caridean, giant here, striped ghost — and a hundred bodies part ahead of it in a wave and close behind it without touch. It beats its way up toward the light crack, filters, defecates, turns. Its molt, when it comes, will be dismantled within hours. Shell becomes curb becomes cement becomes shell.

There are others, rarer, that the city makes room for by getting out of the way.

The blue one. A predatory polychaete, iridescent, segment after segment rippling with paddle-feet, jawed head questing. It threads an avenue and the avenue empties ahead of it in a silent wave, amphipods ducking into tubes, ostracods clamping shut, anemones folding to warts. It takes one worm-crown, neatly, and moves on. Behind it the avenue fills again. No alarm call. To make noise is to be eaten.

The bulldozer. A blind white isopod, broad as a woodlouse, strong as a laborer. Where a terrace has collapsed — silt slump, too-ambitious scaffold reaching too far toward the center — it works grain by grain, picking up rubble in its mandibles, carrying it, placing it. It does not hurry. Cold preserves and slows. A wound heals over weeks. A tower stands for years. Hurry is a warm-water concept.

The farmers of light. Directly under the needle-crack, in the frayed noon-blue shaft that never quite reaches the floor intact, is the only real estate that matters. Here crowd shoulder to shoulder the stunted black-green algae, the kelp sporeling no longer than your fingernail — grazed to lace every day, regrown every night, never allowed to grow up — and the animals that tend them, that weed and prune and defend a sun-plot a millimeter wide. A new tower that casts a shadow here is an act of war. It will be negotiated, antennae to antennae, over days, until the tower is nibbled down or the plot is moved.

And under it all, making it all possible and eating the leavings of it all: the glow in the corners. Bacteria in the far night where the dusk never lifts, velvet sheets in crevices, pale fire. They do not glow to see. They glow because eating stone and sulfur and each other leaks light. They are pasture, crop, and undertaker at once. When anything dies — and everything, even here, dies — they are there first, with the worms, with the tiny white anemones everting stomachs. Within hours the dead is unmade: chitin stacked neatly like bricks in graveyards for future building, flesh dissolved to mucus-thick water.

This is who lives here: no architect, no plan, no tool, no fire, no voice. Thousands of soft bodies in hard secretions, hard bodies in soft mucus, mouths and fans and feet and antennae, all pointed inward. For them outside does not exist. The fissure lets in snow and light but nothing larger than a larva could fight the current wrong and the killing cold beyond. So they have learned the only lesson the pocket teaches.

Nothing leaves, so everything returns. Up is wherever you can hold on. Cold makes you slow, so you become thorough. Light is property, so you share darkness.

A city the size of a heart, beating very slowly in new stone under the ice, made entirely of tenants who are also the building.
