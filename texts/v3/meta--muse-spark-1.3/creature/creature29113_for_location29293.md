At first you think nothing lives here.

That is what the quiet wants you to think. The hollow is so dark and so cold and so muffled that your own body feels like an intrusion, too loud, too warm, too fast. You stand on the moss-thick high street between facades that should be twenty paces apart and are somehow a gulf apart, and you see only leaf, and rust, and drip. No people. That part is true. There are no people.

And then you stay still long enough for your breath to fall into rhythm with the dripping — *plik... plik... plik* — and the place begins to unclose around you. Not one thing. A crowd. A census you were not invited to take.

The air is the first inhabitant. It is full.

**The snowfall that never lands.**

They come when your eyes give up trying to see by light and start seeing by movement. Moths the size of your open hand, pale as ash, moving through the street-gulf in a slow, silent weather. They do not fly so much as precipitate. Wingbeat so soft it registers only as a *furr* against your cheek if one passes close.

They are dusted with luminous scales that do not glow, exactly — there is not enough light here for glow — but shed. Each moth leaves a faint comet-trail of powder that hangs in the cold air for a long while before deciding to fall sideways. The young leaves unfold blackly to catch it. The rats look up for it. The spores they hunt drift down to meet them halfway.

If you watch one land on a burdock-door leaf, you will see it land yesterday and still be landing now. Time pools. Do not count them. Counting fixes them and they resent it.

**The surveyors.**

On the ground — if ground is the word for ten inches of moss over sharp, newborn shale — the streets are patrolled. Slugs as long as your forearm, the color of wet slate and old tea, moving with a deliberation that looks like governance.

They eat darkness and rust, mostly. They rasp liverwort off chapel walls, they rasp algae off iron, they rasp the luminous dust the moths shed. Behind them they leave silvered census trails, wide shining ribbons that catch what little orange bruise leaks from the lamp on the rusted pole. By morning the trails cross and recross the high street in a script you almost can read. This is who lives here keeping track. Who went where in the night, who is rotting, who is ready to be re-knit.

Lift any leaf along their route and underneath: hundreds of pale woodlice, packed shoulder to shoulder, breathing together. They are the mortar now. Where brick has softened to bark, they hold the courses together with their bodies. Touch them and they do not scatter at first. They tighten.

**The thin fox and its parliament.**

Something fox-like slips between the open ground floors, too thin, too quiet. At first you think fox, and then it becomes more itself and you realize it is not a fox that lives in a town. It is a town that grew a fox.

Its coat is rust-red gone green at the tips with living algae. It makes no sound because the hollow enforces quiet the way a library does — a footstep on moss makes almost nothing, and a pawstep makes less. It hunts the rats that hunt the moths that hunt the spores. It hunts by listening for the sudden cessation of dripping where something large has inhaled.

It is not alone. Behind it, always, the rats. Black-eyed, moss-shouldered, fat on moth wings and machine lint. Above it, the bats stitching the upper vaults, moving needle-fast between the hundred-deep layers of nettle and burdock leaves that form the cathedral nave where roofs used to be. On the sagging industrial wires that hum at 50 hertz, wrens have built bottle nests from machine lint and shed moth-scale and your lost hair. They raise their young on springtails.

Bend to any drip-pool — the thumb's width of flow in a bark gutter, the black-tea bathtub on a third-floor root-platform — and the surface explodes outward at your footfall. Springtails, thousands, vaulting and settling again. In the deeper, stiller waters: cellar holes filled with black water, cellar ponds two feet across with a drowned lamppost reflected perfectly: blind white eels, working. And newts. And wriggling larvae.

The eels are the inheritors. The huge blackberries that grow straight out of mortar, shining like oil, sweet in a place that has forgotten sweetness — they are not for you. Do not eat them. When they fall, heavy, they roll downhill that is not downhill and plop into the cellar seas. The eels rise, white mouths opening. The town's way of remembering sweetness, fed back to its coldest citizens.

**The buildings that are animals.**

And then you understand that the vegetation is not cover. It is substrate. It is the town. And the town is alive.

Each terrace is a hollow lattice of root and runner, courses and lintels still legible, but giving like bark when you touch it and breathing, very slowly, cold clear sap that smells of iron. Staircases are bracket fungi ascending in spirals, still climbable if you trust rot. The chapel keeps its shape under a living skin of liverwort. The roofs have unroofed into canopy — vast leaves the size of doors, black-green and dripping, layered a hundred deep, turning almost imperceptibly through the night to track the dead lamp.

At the low end, the cotton mill. Four stories. Its chimney still upright. It is a single organism now. Its walls a weave of creeper stems thick as a man's thigh, its empty windows fringed with ferns like eyelashes, its interior one tall hollow chamber where the old line-shafts and looms still hang, suspended in aerial roots.

For the machines have not stopped, and the machines are also inhabitants.

The great wheel — once water, now sap — turns slowly, its paddles grown over with moss, its axle greased with rot. Leather belts, now strips of tough mycelium, still connect shaft to loom, and the looms still move, a half-inch per hour, weaving nothing, or weaving air, or weaving root-hair. In the small engine house a piston of heartwood moves in a cylinder of rust, driven by fermentation gas from a composting pit. It chuffs, very faintly, once every few minutes. A cold breath. Low voltage harvested from decay keeps that one lamp bruised-orange, keeps the wires singing if you put your ear close.

These factories do not produce. They maintain. They are part of the metabolism, like hearts. They are tended by no hands you see because the building is the worker. Root-beams groan as they take up new load. A slate made of leaf slips and falls a long way without landing loudly. Sap rises inches per night. Belts tighten themselves.

This is an ecosystem in motion at full pace, but muffled, as if under wool.

**The hollow itself.**

If you stay — which you should not for long, because the cool gets into the marrow — you begin to sense the last inhabitant, the one that contains all the others.

Under the iron footbridge that bridges no river, where the moss is thinnest, put your ear to the raw shale, the sharp cinder and crushed brick and clinker, all angular, unrounded, as if the world started yesterday. Listen.

Sap lifting. Wires humming. Water beading. Roots tightening on brick. Moth wings dusting down. Eels turning in their tubs. The slow cold engine taking another breath.

It sounds like nothing. It sounds like a town holding its breath to stay empty enough to stay alive.

That is who lives here. Not a population. A seed. Tiny from outside — bramble and blackthorn in a circle you can walk in ten minutes if you keep moving — endless from inside. It solved being small and vast by being a seed. It solved spanning a void by growing across it thinly. It solved keeping industry without workers by letting the walls work. It solved housing emptiness by making walls that are mostly air.

Make a loud noise and you will meet it all at once. A shout does not echo; it is absorbed, and something absorbs it back — the foxes stop, the looms pause mid-inch, the leaves turn, all together, toward you. That much coordinated attention in the dark is not predation. It is curiosity. It is one body noticing you.

Go back up to the rim before you learn to photosynthesize darkness too. Leave your mark scratched in iron on a rusted grate, where the plants will grow around it respectfully. Anything written in wood, leaf, or skin will by morning be rewritten into a different word, in a different hand, in sap.
