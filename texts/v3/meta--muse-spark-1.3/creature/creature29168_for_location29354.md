At first you think nothing lives here.

That is the point the place makes, over and over, until you stop arguing with it. No birds stitch the canopy. No insects tick in the sedge. No frog plops in the marsh where your ankle sinks sucking into hot peat. No fish flicks in the black cistern water. No hand has worn the moss into a path. Leaves come out perfect and stay perfect and are never chewed. The pitchers gape wide enough to take your forearm and they are full only of rain and drowned leaves, patient stomachs that never get a meal. There is nothing here to catch, the place says. Nothing walks.

You believe it for an hour. Then you stop looking for walkers.

Because something is keeping this balanced. In any real place all that hot water would saw the table apart. Leaves would fall and rot. A cistern fed constantly would overflow or empty. A roar would swell and ease with the wind. Here the needle is pinned. The same volume now as a thousand years ago. The same waterline. The same noon. The roots have found their final braid and stopped. The travertine rims are rounded and smooth and do not advance. Somebody — some *thing* — is holding it exactly where it is.

You notice it first on your own skin.

Nothing dries. You towel your arms and before you finish your shoulders are filmed again. Condensation beads out of air that should be thin and cold but is thick and hot, and sweat beads out of you, and they mix and run down and drip off your fingers back into the moss without loss. After a while you stop toweling. You let the film stay. It is warm and slightly slick, a second skin of water. And it is not only water.

Look close. It lives.

The Film is the first citizen. It coats everything — basalt, bamboo, philodendron leaf underside, your forearms. Algae green as tea, liverwort slick, fungal hyphae white as nerves, thermophile slime from the simmering eye-holes. On rock it precipitates travertine, grain by grain, white on black, sweating the rim outward exactly as fast as water tries to cut it back. That is why pieces do not fall. The edge is being grown as fast as it is being eroded. On leaf it holds the bead before it can evaporate. On you it tastes salt. You feel it faintly tighten as it learns you — not itching, not burning, just a presence, a varnish that breathes. You came up thinking you were a visitor walking through a city. To the Film you are just another warm stone that briefly holds water and light, a temporary dwelling. Dwelling is a stable shape here. The city grew into streets and plazas and drains and roofs that photosynthesize because that is a stable shape for growth to hold water and light. Now you, standing, beaded, steaming faintly in flat noon, are also a stable shape.

Stand still long enough in the Cistern Fields and you meet the second ones.

The Still Ones do not move, because they do not need to. Each sunken pan, rimmed with living root, brimming with warm black water that never rises and never drops though water pours in and out constantly — each one is an individual. Skin-warm. Perfectly still despite the roar that presses on your teeth. You crouch and look down and at first you see only mirror, your own sweating face and green-gold canopy tripled above you. Then you see it holds you without ripple. Your sweat drips in and makes no ring. The level refuses you.

They talk in gurgling, if that is the word. The hollow vines big around as your torso that connect pan to pan run day and night, north and south at once — step into a canal and you feel it, the warm current tugging north on your left ankle and south on your right — and that impossible two-way flow is their circulation. Thought, slow as chemistry. One pan takes in heat and pollen and fallen leaf-tea, passes water on, receives water back. Nothing is consumed. Everything is held. That is why the pollen hangs visibly in the air, uncarried, why spores sprout on the spot where they land, why leaves are never chewed. The Still Ones forbid consumption. To eat would be to change the level. They have settled, a thousand years ago, into exactly this warmth, this fullness, and they maintain it the way you maintain your balance without thinking. The pitcher plants to the south are their failed, gentler kin — mouths that evolved to digest and found nothing to digest, so they converted. Now they are just small, green, gaping cisterns, holding rain. Waiting has become their profession.

You hear the third ones before you understand them, because after ten minutes you stop hearing at all.

Nothing quiets. You shout and hear nothing of yourself. You clap hard in front of your face and see the motion and feel the slap and hear nothing distinct. Your voice is taken instantly, added to the total without changing it by a fraction. At first that feels like deafness. Then you feel it in your sternum, in the soles of your feet through the springy root-mat, in the sockets of your teeth: a low organ hum under the white roar of the falls and the hiss of steam from every vent. The bamboo palisades ten deep vibrate. The big leaves shudder. Millions of hollow stems.

The Choir lives in that.

They are not animals. They are standing waves. Resonances that found hollow tubes of exactly the right length — sixty, eighty feet of braided tower with a cool dripping core entered by an oval mouth worn smooth by water, not hands — and moved in and never left. Each Hollow Tower is a throat. The wind off the falls moves through, the steam breathes up, and a tone holds. The towers are aligned, you noticed — look back through one core and you see another perfectly behind it, and another, a corridor where there should not be that much land — because the Choir tunes them. They need the distance exact. They lean space a little to keep the chord.

They feed on constancy. That is why the roar never swells or eases. The hundreds of falls pouring hot over the lip on all four sides, wide as rivers and thin as a hand, falling a mile to the clouds and throwing back up the warm updraft that becomes the low fog always raining slightly upward through the city — that whole engine is regulated to keep their note pinned. Open your mouth to equalize and the roar gets inside you and for a second you are also a hollow stem, also part of the instrument. That is not metaphor to them. That is hospitality.

And then there are the ones who move, because nothing walks but everything cycles.

Watch the steam. In ordinary heat columns disperse. Here over the low white mounds with their shivering milky-turquoise eye-holes, steam rises in slow columns that do not disperse. Watch the mist the falls throw as they leave — you can put your hand right in the sheet as it goes over, hot as blood — rising back up the black cliff face in a warm updraft, becoming the fog that drifts permanently through towers and fields, beading on every leaf underside and falling back before it can evaporate. Watch your shadow pool short and dark under you at forever-noon and then, if you stare, thin at the edges and lean, very slightly, upward, toward the sun, as if tired of the ground.

The Risers. The Upward Rain. The only migrants the city permits. Water that falls and rises and falls, fog to bead to cistern to canal to lip to fall to fog. They do not walk, they loop. They close behind you when you push through hanging curtains of lichen and air-roots — dripping, indifferent — because to them you are briefly a weather event. They pass through you on their way up. That faint double you see when you hold up two wet hands — each leaf behind them with a green afterimage half an inch offset that does not fade when you blink — that is them, half an inch to the side, overlapping. They live in the looseness. When you walk toward the falls keeping them straight ahead and arrive at a different section of rim than you aimed for, but the sound is identical so you cannot prove it, that is because you stepped into their lane for a moment and they carried you sideways without malice.

This is what lives here, then. Not one image. A holding.

Film that never dries. Choir that never quiets. Still Ones that never make, only coax root over wall and vine into aqueduct. Risers that never walk, only rise and fall.

They graft. A vine enters a bamboo wall and exits as part of the wall because at this level of wet and heat and time, distinction is inefficient. The city is infrastructure, yes — streets of compacted root-mat springy underfoot, plazas where moss has been worn by water into shallow bowls holding an inch of warm mirror, drains that are stems — but the infrastructure is the animal. The town with plumbing and no plumbers is plumbed by itself, for itself, to hold water and light a little longer before letting it go over the edge.

Stand in the middle at midday forever. Heat shimmers but air never clears. Green above you three, four layers deep drips into green. White on all sides, curtain and mist, edge of world. Very high. Very hot. Very wet. Loud as inside of a drum.

You are beaded, humming faintly in your teeth, full to the brim, leaking slightly upward. For a minute the Film has you, the Choir has you in your chest, a Still One holds your reflection without ripple, a Riser passes through your shoulders on its way back to cloud.

You do not live here. But for a minute, you are lived in.
