They live low. That is the first law here.

At first you think nothing lives here. That's what the room wants you to think. Rock and water and a little moss, spaced out like furniture in an empty warehouse. Big black gaps between things. You crouch in the middle with water soaking through your knees and you think: too cold, too young, too loud. A cut still bleeding stone-water. Nothing could have moved in yet.

Then your eyes finish adjusting to the leak of light, and the floor comes up toward you.

It is not empty. It is crowded from the ground up.

Start with what holds everything else: the moss. Not a carpet. A city in hummocks. Sphagnum mounded up out of the standing brown-green water, each head saturated to a green so intense it reads as warm — lime new-growth tips, bottle-green stems, deep bog-red where the cold has touched them. They drink constantly and cannot drink enough. Press one and water sighs up around your thumb. Up their stems, by capillary, they pump back up what just fell as drip, lifting the pool back into the air to become fog again. On and between them, liverwort slicked flat as wet skin, and a few ferns, starved, unfurled tight as fists and staying that way, stunted by dark but alive. On the underside of the fallen slab, where light never reaches, fungal fans, white as paper, thin as fingernails, feeding on cold rot.

In the moss, the sundews are still awake when they should be sleeping. Threads of them, red-tentacled, each tentacle tipped with a glue-bead that holds not just its own stick but the room's condensation. The fog beads on them. They don't need flies from outside; the room delivers.

Because above the hummocks, the air is thick.

The midges. Thousands of them, and they never leave the shaft. They live in that dim blue-grey column of light leaking down from the slot, cycling up and down in shifts. Wings up, drift sideways, drop to touch the black mirror-pools to lay, rise again. Over and over. Their whine is a high saw-edge laid over the bass chuff of the room's breathing — *huh-huh-huh* — and the *tok-tok-tok-TOOK* of the water hammer under the floor. They move like parts on a belt, because they are timed to the machine: swarm thickest just after the big drip.

You learn to wait for that drip too. At the central stalactite-nub on the ceiling, a bead gathers for a full minute, swelling, trembling, catching what little light there is. Then it falls — *plock* — a pea-sized bolt you feel in your sternum in a room that amplifies every drop into a tank-noise. When it hits, the whole midge column shudders sideways and resets. When it hits, the springtails jump.

They are on the pools themselves, grey-black pepper scattered on black glass. Springtails, thousands more, flicking clear in unison when a drop strikes, a single flinching skin lifting off the water and settling again. They graze on what falls — midge carcasses, fungal spores, the film of organic dust the inhale drags down through the slot smelling of iron and wet stone. Prey so abundant it looks like texture.

And so predators have come, or stayed and changed.

Under the overhang, in the sharpest teeth of basalt and shale, the white spiders. Eyeless or nearly — they don't need eyes where shaft light doesn't reach. They string lines between unlichened points, lines already beaded with water, and they work only on the rhythm. On the inhale, when hanging drops shiver sideways and air moves, they crouch flat. On the exhale, when the mist puffs up white from the back pools and the air goes still for three seconds, they mend. You can watch one run out on trembling legs to re-tension a sagging line before the next breath tears it. Pale, slow, precise. They eat midges that blunder sideways out of the light, and springtails that hop too high. Their webs are not the clean spirals of outside; they are guy-lines, trip-wires, low hammocks strung inches above the moss to catch the low traffic.

Below them, the largest lives here. The salamanders.

Four, five, seven of them — you keep losing count because they don't move. Cold-pink, translucent-bellied, flattened in the shallows where water is only a skin over peat. Through the belly wall you can see the dark coil of gut. Their external gills fan, and if you watch long enough you realize they are fanning in time with the room: three seconds in, three seconds out. Cold has slowed them to a deliberateness that looks like contemplation. They do not flee from your hand, from light, from vibration. They don't need to. Nothing here hunts them.

They feed when the knock comes up from underneath — when the buried channel hammers and knocks silt up into the pools. Then, with a suddenness that betrays how much life is stored in that slowness, a head turns, a mouth opens, a cloud of springtails or midge larvae vanishes. Then stillness again. Birth and death scaled to droplets, predator and prey in a space you could cross in six steps.

None of them rest. Even in cold, even in dim. That is the wrongness and the rightness of it: water that cold should skin with ice, but the pools stay clear, the weeping beads never freeze even when your fingers go numb. So the cycle never stops to winter. Condensation gathers, falls, drains, is wicked back up, evaporates into fog, condenses again. Midges rise and lay and die. Spiders mend and eat. Salamanders fan and snap. Fungus spreads another millimeter across the slab.

They have all learned the engine and built their lives inside it. Not visitors. Residents of a tiny, untouched industry of water and breath that made itself last thaw or last week and runs itself without tending — wet and cold and ceaselessly loud in the dark.
