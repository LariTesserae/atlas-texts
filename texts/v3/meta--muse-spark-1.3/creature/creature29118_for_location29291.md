At first you count the obvious, because the light is ordinary and tells you to.

Humans. About — no one will give you a number that holds till evening — a few hundred. Enough to keep the mills lit. Not enough to fill the streets.

You see them wrong at first. You think: mill workers. Then you watch longer and the shape changes and won't change back.

They are not mill workers who happen to live on a plant. They are the plant's pruning animals. Symbionts with oilskins.

Look at a crew out on the mat: five figures in yellow-black oilskins gone green at the knees, moving in the total wind-silence of hand signals. No shouting — voice is useless, shredded off ten meters out. One points, two haul, one saws. Their faces are all the same face the plateau gives everyone after a winter: red-raw nose always running, eyes narrowed against drip and glare, cuffs permanently damp and cold to the wrist, a soot-line in the crease of the neck that never washes out because the wash water is sky water and always a little peaty. They hear the 6 a.m. whistle inside their teeth. They navigate by hum — the power station's 50-cycle that lives in puddles and pipes — and when it stutters they stop walking, like dogs hearing a pitch you can't hear.

There are kinds, the way ants have kinds, and they intermix by shift:

The Condensation Crew, out earliest, when the cloud is still sitting *in* the bowl. Small, nimble people with siphon coils over their shoulders, moving stalk to stalk to drain the cup-leaves before the black mirrors get heavy enough to snap the petiole. They know each leaf like a farmer knows a cow — which one fills first, which one lies, which one will show you cloudless sky in its reflection while rain is hitting your back. They don't drink from them. Never. Pocket ponds are full of drowned pollen. They spit after siphoning.

The Trimming Crew, noon, loudest. They ride the narrow rails out on the pruning engines, little diesel crawlers that cough right next to you when they're half a kilometer away and vanish completely when they're twenty meters behind a stem. They take the brown sheaths down with long screaming saws. CUT ONLY THE BROWN, stenciled everywhere, flaking. They can see brown the way you see color. To you a stalk is green. To them it's a calendar: green sheath live and straining, drinking light, groaning under load; brown sheath spent, papery, ready to rot the mat if left. They cut and the whole hundred-meter pillar shudders with relief and the wind takes the cut sheet flapping away like a sail torn loose.

The Fiber Crew, evening, inside. Boiler-warm, smelling of yeast and hot oil. They feed the pulping mills with endless chopped fiber, press bales pale as bone, roll leaf-paper, render lamp-oil from seed-pods big as bathtubs, cracked open steaming. Nothing leaves anymore. The road washed out. So they stack. Bales by the siding in tottering white streets. Rolls in sheds. Surplus burned for heat. Their hands are soft-swollen from perpetual damp and nicked white from fiber cuts that heal slow in the cold.

The Night Pressure Crew, the strangest. Old men and women mostly, deaf in one ear from years of saws, walking the copper with little hammers at 2 a.m. Tick-tick-tick along sweating pipes that run for hectares between lonely buildings, listening for blockage. A clear pipe rings. A blocked one, they swear, breathes. A low in-out that is not wind in the pipe but something using the pipe as a throat. They bleed it with valves that sigh and mark it in chalk and do not talk about it till morning.

Children exist — you hear them before you see them — but they grow up vertical. That cottage in the axil of the leaf ten years ago, now forty feet up, chimney still attached by that stretched singing copper pipe the maintenance men lengthen every spring: kids live up there. They climb down root-stairs in the morning. Not built stairs. Grown stairs, pale, spiraling up a cut stump overnight after rain, perfectly treaded, smelling faintly sweet like the grit. By winter they'll go soft and sink back. The kids know not to trust autumn stairs.

And housed among them, tolerated, counted:

Six crows. Everyone knows them by name — Jib, Soot,bell, Marrow, Vicar, Null — though the names shift town to town, telling you people re-name them when one dies and another somehow arrives. Big, wind-torn birds, black gone rusty at the edges from iron rain. They ride the flap-flap-FLAP of the warehouse-roof leaves, they steal fiber twine, they sit on the riveted water towers and shout down at crews. They are the census. Count the crows. There should be six. If there are seven, stay indoors until the whistle blows twice. No one jokes about this. The seventh is not a bird that joined. It's a wrinkle. A place where the count failed. You look again and there are six, and your nose is bleeding from the altitude you thought you'd adapted to.

One cat. One-eyed, mill-grey, deaf as a post, living in the pulping shed where one wall is no longer brick but living fiber. They stopped scraping it because the wall heals cut pipes and keeps the room at 60 degrees without coal and sweats at night a faint green-gold phosphorescence bright enough to read by. The cat sleeps against that wall and glows faintly and purrs in sync with the mill. She has no kittens. She has never aged, according to three generations of fiber crew. She watches doors.

One moth, rarely. Pale, confused, bumping a lit amber window alone like a ship. There should be more insects. There are not. The alkaloids in the mat killed the sheep decades ago — they ate and staggered and died with green mouths — and something in the air, the iron-chlorophyll sideways rain, keeps almost everything else from flying. So: no buzz, no herds, no song. Just wind playing leaves like drums and loose rigging, stalks creaking deep wood-on-wood without wood, and the town chuffing and sighing underneath.

Because underneath, around, through — the thing that actually lives here.

Not the people. The people are a rash. A useful infection the Green keeps around because they prune.

The Green itself. Singular. You have to stop thinking *plants*, plural. There are stalks here, stalks half a kilometer apart, leaning fans rattling, broad waxy cup-leaves, vines thick as culverts nodding a hundred meters up — but under the breathing moss-felt, three, ten meters deep, sphagnum over peat over old round rock, it's all one mat, one felted body grown over itself for longer than the mountains have held shape. The gaps are part of it. It decided sparsity is structure. It does not fill in. A stalk here. A hectare of trembling nothing. Another stalk. That emptiness is its spacing, its slow thought.

It is slow and then all at once. Nothing for weeks but creak and drip-plink-tick-gurgle under the hum. Then after rain a shoot adds a meter overnight, a vine nods, a cup fills, and — stinking event — a stalk fruits overnight into a purple lantern the size of a house, reeking sweet-rot that sours cisterns for a kilometer. The whole town turns out in masks with saws to take it before it bursts. That's not a plant fruiting. That's an organ swelling.

It lifts. It heals. It learns the town. It lifted that cottage forty feet and didn't shear the pipe. It grew those stairs to a stump the crews use daily — why would blind growth make perfect treads? It took the pulping shed wall and made it better than brick: self-repairing, warm, luminous. It keeps offering. A door in a stalk, open, that was closed yesterday. Pale interior, warm, smelling of yeast. Don't go in. It's not your day's door. The old-timers are practical, not superstitious. They tried. The man who went in Tuesday came out Thursday last week, younger, not speaking right, counting seven crows when there were six.

And then — the others. The ones that live in the thinness.

You see them first as mist. Furnace Row repeating from above. A second shift crossing a bridge that hasn't been built yet. Your own footsteps falling out of sync on wet felt — squeak ... squeak-squeak — and if you follow the sound ahead of you, you'll round a culvert-vine and meet yourself coming back, older, in a different oilskin, signaling *stay* with a hand you haven't learned yet. Don't follow.

They pool. Sound pools in hollows so a distant engine coughs in your ear while its driver can't hear you scream. Shadows fall toward the sun around big stems. Light bends. Cup-ponds show the wrong sky. These aren't ghosts. They're residents of the mat's memory. Effects happening under ten meters of living felt, surfacing late. Time doesn't stack properly inside something that old that keeps growing over its own dead. It ferments. Echoes surface like gas.

The seventh crow is one. The open door is one. The breathing in blocked copper is one — the Green dreaming in the pipes, or an echo using them. The night-shift wall glowing to read by: the town reading by the light of something else's recollection.

Stand at the sparse edge at dusk, mills humming amber behind you, kilometers of luminous green breathing cold in front, leaves applauding and applauding in the wind, and you finally get the population right:

One ancient flattened landlord, too cold and damp for anything but itself, deciding to be sparse.

A few hundred warm, damp primates with engines and copper and saws, kept because they cut only the brown and keep total life from swallowing itself.

Six birds and a cat and a moth to count by.

And a thin crowd of almost-people, almost-days, living sideways in the felt — crossing unbuilt bridges, knocking from inside pipes, opening doors to the wrong morning — who are not visitors at all, but what happens when you live long enough inside a living thing that it starts to dream you back.
