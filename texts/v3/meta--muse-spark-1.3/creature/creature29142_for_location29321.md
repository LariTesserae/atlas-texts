Nothing with eyes lives here.

That's the first touch of them — that you keep waiting for eyes. For breath. For the quick scutter that would mean animal. It never comes. In the burial-dark, crouched with your head almost touching the sky of this place, you put your hand out expecting a wall and finding instead a street, expecting a street and finding instead a life.

It doesn't move away.

What lives here now has unlearned moving away.

They are all plant, and all blind. White, or the memory of white, where green was given up so long ago there is no word left for it in their tissues. They do not drink. They do not see. They do not eat each other. They have stayed because they learned how to live on what this sealed room still makes abundantly: cold, dryness, slough, and noise.

You meet them by accident, with your face and your hands.

First — the break across your cheek. So fine you doubt it. **Air-beard.**

It hangs from the dome in threads ten feet long and thinner than a hair gone white with age. In any other cave it would be cobweb and you would flinch for the spider. Here there is no spider and never will be. It is plant, wholly. A root that decided to grow up into air instead of down into soil, because there is no soil water to seek, only the slow circulation coaxed by the dry gutters.

Kneel still and let it come back to you. It trembles constantly in the hiss-circulation, that near-surf sound that never stops. That trembling is its drinking. Each thread is dry as paper and cool as breath held too long, and along its length are microscopic scales — you feel them only as a faint roughness if you roll a broken end between thumb and finger — that comb stone-dust and its own dead skin out of the air. It eats what settles. It eats what it sheds. When a big groan goes around the dome, the whole curtain shivers and a faint, sweet-garlic sift falls to the floor. That is its seeding and its manuring at once.

A thread takes a decade to reach the floor. Then it snaps under its own impossible length. On the floor, in the absolute dark, the snapped threads lie and slowly curl and become soil that is not soil, food for what grows lower.

Second — under your palm when you steady yourself against a house wall. Not stone. Fur. Dry, warm-ish fur where no animal has ever been. **Dust-moss.**

It furs only the lee sides, never the windward. That tells you it is alive and choosing. A pale grey-yellow felt, the color you see behind your eyelids when you press them, thick as felted wool on the thumb-sized door lintels, thin as frost on the boulevard veins. Put your ear — you have to put your ear absurdly close, your breath suddenly too wet and loud — and you hear nothing from it, which is its signature. It deadens.

The builders knew this. You can feel where they left moss to grow thick around the bell-mouthed granary jars, a living packing to keep the boom from cracking the wax-dry sap seals. The moss let them. It likes the jars. It feeds on the stone-dust that sifted in a thousand years ago and never left, and on the spores that rain down after every CRACK, and on the faint mineral exhalation of the pith blocks themselves. It grows outward a millimeter a century, creasing over itself, dying underneath into a papery humus that never rots wet, only powders sweeter. Garlic and old paper — that smell is mostly its breath.

Third — at the street corners, where your knuckles knock them as you crawl. Thin, indignant stalks that refuse to bend. **Lampweeds.**

Misnamed by someone who needed light. They give none. They rear up knee-high to a mouse, shin-high to nothing, stalk-dry and string-tough, each topped with a fist. A bud that never opens. Feel it: tight, ridged, hard as shot, like the seeds in the granaries that will never sprout. It has been closed for a hundred years. It will be closed for a hundred more.

But it moves. That is the uncanny. Stay still through two ticks and a groan and you will feel it — not see — the stalks very slowly torquing on their bases to face the sound. They orient not to sun, which they have never known, but to vibration. The bud is an ear. Inside the closed fist is a cavity tuned like a drum, and pollen and spores locked dry inside shift when the dome booms. The shake is the pollination. The crack is the rain that splits a seed coat just enough, just rarely, for one new stalk to start beside the old, patient as masonry.

Tap the floor-vein lightly with a fingernail and the nearest corner of them will shiver all together. In the meeting hall, at the acoustic focus, a whole ring of them leans inward, listening to a governance that ended millennia ago.

Fourth — along the walls where the dome meets the floor, where your lightless sweep thinks at first you have found stairs or shingles. **The brackets.**

Layer on layer of dry fungus-tough leaf flesh, shelfing out in overlapping half-moons no wider than a fingernail. They creak. If you hold your breath — and you must, your breath is obscenely animal here — you can hear them creak even between booms, a slow thickening. They are the oldest residents. They were here when the houses were still sharp-edged, and they will be here when the three-story tower no taller than your shin finally leans into the dust.

They do not root in the tunic to eat it. The rule holds: nothing eats another living thing, and the bulb, though long stopped growing, is still the body that holds them. Instead they root in air. Their undersides are black with trapped cold, their topsides pale with accumulated sift. Between the layers they hold still, cool darkness the way the woven cisterns were built to hold still, cool darkness. They are cisterns that grew themselves. When the deep creak travels around and gathers in the sunken center and goes off, the brackets take the blow and turn it, shivering off a halo of spores so dry they float for hours in the air-gutter circulation before settling on dust-moss to be eaten.

That is the economy. No light to harvest, no water to split, so they harvest strain.

Cold outside, slightly less cold inside. Fibers take up dryness and give it off. Tunic layers release overhead with a tick. The whole taut, papery, hollow instrument body amplifies it. Hiss, tick, groan, CRACK, hum in the sternum. And the blind flora eat the shaking.

They eat the cold differential on their scales and brackets. They eat the vibration that knocks their spores loose and worries their seed coats. They eat their own dead — air-beard threads, moss powder, bud husks — sloughed and resettled and eaten again, a slow closed loop that needs almost nothing new because almost nothing ever leaves except your papery breath and the sweet-dry smell on your hands for hours after.

They do not crowd. There is vast space between them, as there is vast space between the houses. A lampweed corner, then ten paces of resonant floor, then a furred wall, then nothing for a long time. They can afford distance. They grow patiently, almost not at all. A crack widens in a lintel over a hundred years and a bracket moves in. A thread reaches the floor in a decade and snaps and feeds a patch of moss.

No one built them. The builders — hand-scrapers, wedge-splitters, lashers of root-wood with leaf-hair cord, makers of doorways no higher than your thumb — are gone so completely that only their acoustic zoning remains: doors facing away from resonance, jars nestled in moss, a bench grown straight out of the living floor. Whatever walked and breathed here left. What stayed never walked at all.

When you back out through the split tunic, you leave them to their weather.
