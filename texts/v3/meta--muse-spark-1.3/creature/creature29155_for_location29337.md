You look for them first in the doorways.

That's the habit. A city implies citizens. You duck through the stomatal pore as it dilates — warm, slick lip of living guard-cells parting — and you expect someone on the other side. A keeper. A watcher. Someone who grew that door on purpose.

There is no one.

There is only more room, breathing.

That is the first time you understand: there is no *in* here that isn't alive. You are not entering houses. You are entering bodies.

It lives here. All of it. Not like a forest lives, scattered and separate, tree by tree. Like a town lives, crowded and contiguous and arguing with itself.

No animals. You have already learned to stop listening for them. No feet, no wings, no throats. Nothing here has a face to turn toward you. And still, everywhere you put your hand, something notices. The pore closes behind you, slowly, tasting the air you displaced. The thorn-stair under your knees gives a fraction, softening to take your weight, then re-knits.

They feel by touching and by light. That is their whole senses.

Above you a leaf the size of a house awning tilts a degree toward the sun-shaft, and a hundred smaller leaves under it tilt to catch the spill. Below you the root-mat tenses. It is phototropism so fast you can watch it happen, thigmotropism so fine the vine beside your shoulder leans away from your heat without ever touching you. They see you as a moving shade and a warm pressure. They hear you — if that is the word — as vibration in water.

Because they are water, mostly, held up by cellulose will.

Listen to a bole with your ear pressed to it and you will hear the citizen speaking to itself: that riverine roar of sap lifting a hundred feet straight up, against gravity, in columns so taut they snap. Tick-tick-tick-pop. That frying sound all around your head is cavitation — a thousand xylem threads breaking and reforming, breaking and reforming. It sounds like noise. It is circulation. It is pulse. When the afternoon heat peaks, the whole district ticks louder, stressed and thirsty, pulling harder. When one of those brief, single-leaf rains lets go above, you can hear the local pressure ease, a sigh that travels down stem to root in a wave.

They are not one thing. Not exactly. If you stay long enough to stop looking for people, you start to see castes, guilds — not species, but jobs, which any shoot can take up and then abandon.

Lowest, the Anchors. You never see them whole. They are the undermat, the mattress-thick moss, the ginger-rhizome warrens you have to stoop to walk through, the braided root-plates like muscle. They are slow by local standards, which means they move only inches an hour. They do the remembering. A root-avenue remembers feet — your feet, water's feet — by thickening where pressure repeats, by hollowing where flow repeats. Walk down away from the central leaf-plaza, turn left at the forked strangler-fig, turn left again, and you come back from above because the Anchors have folded the path while you walked it. They do that. Inside runs longer than outside. That ten-foot rhizome tube you crawled through runs a hundred feet inside because the Anchor that grew it is still growing it while you are inside it, laying down warm, chlorophyll-lit chambers ahead of your knees, resorbing the passage behind your heels. You emerge onto a canopy walk that was above you a moment ago, and the green smear on your hands pulses faintly. You left cells. You took cells.

Above them, the Scaffolders. These are the loud ones, the short-lived. You will not get used to them.

A tower of bamboo-like culms — not bamboo, but heading in that direction — will shudder upright thirty feet while you watch a plaza fill, lignifying as it climbs, creaking in torsion, fibers slipping with that low molar-bass groan. For an hour it is a tenement, and creepers are already using it, throwing tendrils up its flanks, leaf-hands slapping and sticking. Ferns germinate on its nodes before it has finished growing. Ferns on ferns. Moss on ferns. Then it leans. Everyone leans here; vertical is a temporary argument. It overbalances with a thunder-crack of pulp, crashes across the petiole-avenue, and before the echo stops the wreck is already a new arcade. The creepers tighten, lift, make vault and railing from the corpse. The fallen culm does not die so much as change its heading — from *up* to *across* — and keep living.

That is how they are born and die, if those words still work. Growth is direction, not time. The curled fists of meristem at every dead-end stair are not babies. They are futures, waiting. They point — tight, pale, wet knuckles of undifferentiated hunger — toward a room that has not happened yet, and if light and water hold, they will unfurl into it with that rip-and-sigh, a million wet tearing sounds a day. Old tissues do not age; they fold back into elsewhere. A wall you leaned on at noon thins to membrane by dusk, still bearing weight impossibly, then to nothing, its matter pulled back sap-ward to feed a new turret two tiers over. To live here is to head.

Highest, the Roofers and the Drinkers, who are really one guild with two gestures.

The Roofers are the leaves. Each one an awning, a shingle, veined like a roadmap, tipped to drip. Six of them cupped together make the central plaza, holding three inches of warm, sky-reflecting tea-water at the center. From beneath, they glow. Ordinary daylight comes through ordinary chlorophyll and comes out lit as if from lanterns. They spend all day sweating — transpiration on a valley scale — lifting an invisible ocean off their undersides until the air itself sweats, beads, and lets go in minute-long local rains. Turn a corner of vine from a dry bark colonnade and one leaf, just that one, is raining. It is not weather. It is excretion. It is gift.

The Drinkers catch the gift and move it on. Water is never owned here, only borrowed. The cup-fungi the size of bathtubs, the bromeliad tanks you could bathe in, the pitcher-structures big enough to drown in — brown-green water shivering under a skin of duckweed, and under that, filaments of algae knotting into tiny forests, forest upon forest — they were elsewhere an hour ago. Aerial roots descend in colonnades to sip, swell, fuse into walls, then lift the water a hundred feet to feed a leaf-tip rain. Put your hand in a tank and it shivers, not from you, but from the thousands of mouthless drinkings going on at once. Even the water has inhabitants, and they are green too.

And then the Senders. You have seen them from the plaza center if you turned slowly: the turrets that are unopened flowers the size of silos, petals still sealed but throbbing faintly with pressure, ready to blow. They are the most animal thing here and they have no muscle at all, only turgor. All day the seed pods detonate around you with rifle-cracks, firing hard green shot into bark walls where they stick and immediately germinate, thread out a root-hair before the smoke — there is no smoke — clears. The air is thick as attic dust with pollen, plant pollen, wind-borne, no bee needed, no throat to coax. It films your sweat, your sap-stick skin. Everything you touch leaves a green smear and sticks slightly because you are being sown. You walk, and the motes follow your heat-plume, and some of them land in moss-mattress and begin.

They eat light, and basalt, and each other.

That last part is the hardest to watch without flinching. In the gaps where roots split for a moment you see the young earth, sharp black unweathered teeth, fresh lava-crags with edges that would cut. They have no time to round. Within hours tendrils find them, wrap them, pry them apart with slow hydraulic jacks, secrete acid, sheath the shard by evening and use it as a foundation pylon for a new tower. A borough that cannot stop building eats its bedrock as fast as it is born. And it eats itself faster. Every stall eating its neighbor and building a new stall from the pieces — not metaphor. A strangler-fig fork enfolds a petiole-avenue, squeezes it shut, uses its pulp as scaffolding for a new gallery. A pitcher dissolves a fallen leaf and drinks the broth. A moss smothers a seedling and the seedling grows straight through the moss.

There is nowhere to sit that is not alive. Nowhere to be that is not crowded. And nothing here is mindless, quite — it is just minded differently. No brain, no throat, no hands to leave tool marks. No stone. No metal. No cut timber, even. Only anatomy become architecture, still attached, still growing. Memory is not stored; it is grown into shape. A path that returns you to where you started, but from above, with your wet footprint still held in a leaf — that is recollection.

If you stand long enough in the leaf-bowl plaza, with warm water to your ankles and condensation falling in brief false rains and the tick-pop-fry of a thousand stems and the slow groan of trunks in torsion and the far-off thunder where another tower has just fallen and is already being trellised — if you stand there until your knees learn the low press of settled warm air — you will feel them notice you the only way they can.

Not as visitor. As trellis. As weather. As brief, warm, vertical water to be borrowed, grown upon, folded around, and moved on.

A fern is already growing from the moss in the tread of your shoe.
