At first you think nothing lives here but noise.

That’s because on the open mat, between the giants, life is low and fast and keeps out of the sun. You have to crouch to see it. Then you see it is everywhere.

The skin of the world-mother crawls.

Beetle highways run for miles in shallow braided troughs where the fiber is worn almost smooth — black and oxblood beetles big as thumbnails moving in both directions, carrying cut hair-moss, carrying dead bees, carrying each other. If you put your ear to the mat you hear them before you see them: a dry ticking under the creak of root. Between the highways, mites like dust with legs boil out of cracks when dew falls and vanish by nine. Ants in rust-red rivers haul wax-scale crumbs ten times their size, and where the ants go the skinks go, silver-green, no bigger than your finger, swimming through root-hair with their legs tucked, surfacing only to drink from a footprint.

Nothing here drinks casually. Everything is a water-thief.

The cup-rosettes at ground level — low, fleshy, bathtub-wide — open at night with a soft pop you can hear if you sleep near them. By dawn they hold a palm-deep of condensation, bitter with tannin. Before the sun takes it, they are ringed: night-mice with huge kidneys and almost no urine, lapping; moths the size of your hand, unrolling tongues longer than their bodies; root-snakes, mottled olive, sliding up over the lip to drink and then lying in the water all day like a cord, waiting for the mice to come back. Nothing wastes the chance. A glider that misjudges and lands on the mat in daylight will crawl on its elbows to a cup and drown itself trying to drink, and within an hour the ants have found its membranes.

But the mat is only the floor. Nobody *lives* on the floor. They live up.

Each giant is not inhabited. It is *crowded*. The word city is wrong because a city empties. This never empties.

Start high and work down. Always, overhead, the air is torn: gliders in flocks of two, three thousand, membranes like wet paper stretched between elongated finger-bones, amber and soot and translucent white. They are not graceful. They flap-burst, stall, catch a thermal off a crown and scream — a needling keening that never quite stops. They live on seeds and on each other’s parasites, picking at wing-mates mid-air. Among them, higher, slower, the four-winged hunters. Stoop, scatter, miss, perch. They pant like dogs in the heat, throat sacs pulsing. When one does kill, it does not land to eat. It clings to the glider’s back and eats flying, dropping the wing-bones which spin down for half a mile to the mat where the beetles take them.

On the crown itself the sound has weight. The cicada-ranks sit underside of the big branches in rows, fist-sized, copper-backed, sides pumping. One is loud. A hundred phase-lock until the air shivers and your teeth ache and blood comes from your nose if you stand in the focus of a hollow trunk that amplifies them. Under that saw, lower: bees. Stingless amber bees moving like smoke between flower spikes as long as canoes, spikes that stand straight up from the crown and drip nectar all day. The bees plaster everything — your hands, your eyelids, the throat-flower lips — drunk, dusted gold with spore-pollen. Where bees go, the mantids go. Forearm-long, leaf-green, utterly still on a flower spike until a bee blunders, and then that blur of sickle arms. Where mantids hunt, root-snakes hunt mantids. You can watch the whole chain from a platform in one afternoon without moving your feet.

And then there are the ones who stopped choosing.

In the still hour after noon, when even the cicadas dip, you see the rooters. They look like monkeys until they stop moving. They look like half-finished birds until they open their mouths. A furred clinger no bigger than a cat will wedge itself into a low fork, push a pink tendril-tongue — half tongue, half root — deep into a nectar well in the bark, and go torpid. Its breathing slows to nothing. Algae living in its skin bloom green under the fur, under the feathers. It photosynthesizes. It just hangs there for hours, green-blooded, leaves shivering on its back where symbiotic moss has rooted into old scars, making sugar from sun while it sleeps off the morning’s sap-drink. There are sucker-bats that do the same under sail-leaves, bellies pressed to the felted white underside, rootlets from their chins tapped into the leaf vein. They are animals that have learned, in a place with almost no water and murderous sun, to eat light when meat is scarce.

Because here the plants have learned to eat meat back.

The throat-flowers hang in clusters under the lower tiers, ten feet of mottled pink and carrion-purple, closed and sweating all day. At dusk they gape. Stand near one and you feel it: a true inhalation, air tugging at your shirt. Moth clouds pour in, drunk on rot-sweet. The lips close with a wet sound. At midnight — if you are awake on watch, you hear it all over the crown, soft pops — they exhale. Not seeds. Fledglings. Small, sticky, blind fluttering things, half plant, half animal, that cling for an hour and then take wing, and are mostly eaten, and eat mostly each other, and a few find a branch wound to root in and become new throat-flowers. The sail-leaves do their part too. Each leaf as big as a sail on a stem thick as your thigh will turn not only for sun but for sound. Clap, shout, a hunter scream — a ripple runs across a half-mile crown as thousands of waxy tops angle toward the noise, listening. What they are listening for, the people say, is rain.

The giants themselves live longest, and slowest, and strangest. They walk. Not fast. They lean. Year by year laying down buttress root on the storm side, letting the lee side rot out into punk and beetle meal, shifting meters a decade toward the afternoon thunderheads, leaving behind a raised wake of rich rot-mat where cup-rosettes grow thick as cobbles. That is why the marks of people are always old. A ladder that went straight up ten years ago now slants. A platform lashed in a fork is now twenty feet off the fork.

And the people —

They are thin in a way lowlanders aren’t. Long lungs, salt-white at the temples always, calves like rope from a life of mat-bounce and trunk-climb. You will rarely see more than six together. A mother and her brothers. Two old men who share a water-route. A girl who tends bees with smoke and bare hands. They wear almost nothing: fiber breech, a dew-cloak made from a single small sail-leaf, soft-soled wraps for bark. Their hair is cropped short or shaved — anything else the bees nest in — and their skin is oiled with warmed wax to throw back sun.

They do not live *on* the mother. They live *in permission* of her.

A family claims not land but a giant, sometimes two, for a generation. They sleep eighty, a hundred feet up, on platforms of cut sail-rib lashed with root-hair cord, thatched with fallen wax-scale, that can be untied in a morning and carried. They piss and shit off the edge away from cisterns. They cut only what has already fallen or what can be taken shallow: thorn awls still barbed from deadfall, glider wing-bone scrapers, cord twisted from surface hair, dew-sails hung at dawn to drip into gourds made from unopened throat-flower buds pinched shut with resin. No metal. No deep cut. Every child learns before walking: never cut the mat deeper than a finger. A deep wound festers. Rot spreads black for miles under the fiber and your footing goes soft and the cup-rosettes die and you have to leave that wake for years.

Their day is water-shaped. Walk before sun. Rest at noon under sail when every giant at once exhales spore in a hot peppery sigh that hazes the air gold and sets every insect screaming — they cover their mouths, cough, wait it out. Drink at every chance, after sun has sterilized the axil-cisterns: twenty, fifty gallons of amber tea full of tadpole-things and larvae, scooped with both hands, strained through moss between teeth. Sleep off the ground; the mat hunts at night. The grunting barking things come down from crowns to strip moss and take mice and sometimes take an unwatched baby. People hang their hammocks high and keep a cold fire — resin burns too fast, too smoky — only for meat: glider dried in strips until it is like salt wood, cicada grubs roasted in their shells.

They are not quiet. No one who lives inside a roar can be. They talk with hands as much as mouth, slap bark for emphasis, sing to be heard over the ranks — short, loud, repetitive songs that mark water-trails: *sky-cup left, beetle right, drink here after storm-head.* At crossings they leave cairns: shed claws, seed stones, a carved rib. Not gods. Reminders. Someone found water here. Someone died here, bled out, and the hunters came. Don’t bleed here.

If you stay long enough they stop noticing you, the way the gliders stop noticing you unless you carry water. You become another warm thing that licks dew, wrings moss, ducks when the pitch changes — that lift in the cicada saw that means rain coming, or a hunter flock turning, or a throat-flower gaping wet behind you.

At evening, from a ladder top, you’ll see them moving: a dark figure crossing three miles of empty shining mat toward the next crown, shadow tight, sack bouncing, walking the bouncing hide of a plant the size of a country to reach another hair on its arm where bees are smoking gold and water waits warm in a leaf’s armpit, and the whole hot humming indifferent total chorus goes on without pause around them, and does not mind them at all.
