At first you think nothing lives here. That's what the flat does to you.

You look for bodies and you find space. A tussock, then ten yards of wet sand, then another tussock. A white trunk, then half a mile of glare, then another white trunk. It feels uninhabited because the inhabitants are not standing up to be counted. They are lying low, or spread thin, or dissolved.

Then you crouch down, and it resolves.

Start with what holds still longest, because that is what lives here best: the ones who learned to starve elegantly.

The paper-barks. They are not tall because tall costs phosphorus and this country spent its phosphorus a million years ago. They are five, eight meters, crooked, wide-spaced, each one alone in its own shallow dish of water-light. Bark in pale papery sheets, peeling, hanging in damp ribbons. It wicks. After rain you can wring it. Underneath: small, hard, grey-green leaves turned sideways, edge to the sun, so they never burn and never shade. They photosynthesize sideways, sparingly. Their roots don't go down — down is sour, airless, iron-hardpan that rings like pottery — they go out, mats just under the silt, breathing through knobby knees when the sheet rises. In the haze they sweat oil. If you crush a leaf it smells medicinal, clean, too strong for such a thin tree. They can stand with their feet underwater for months and not drown. They can stand through fire and come back black then white again.

Between them, at ankle height, the sedges. Not soft grass but tussocks of wire: olive-yellow blades harsh as a file, arching out from a dense crown. Each tussock is an island. Its center is raised a finger's width above drowning by its own dead leaves packed tight. In that crown live spiders, skinks, beetles in daytime. The roots are a felt that holds mud even when the pan migrates. They do not grow fast. They grow dense.

And where even sedges can't make a living, the carnivores. That tells you everything about the soil. Sundews no bigger than your thumbnail, rosettes of red spoons on the bare wet sand between tussocks, each spoon tipped with a bead of glue that never dries even in this steaming air. They glitter. They are eating the air because they cannot eat the ground. Bladderworts, even stranger: in the six-inch sheets and the cool layer of the deeper pans, loose yellow-green tangles with no roots at all, only tiny bladders along the stems that snap shut in a millisecond on water fleas. When the pan dries they lie as black threads on the mud, waiting. When it fills they inflate and flower — a brief, indecently bright yellow spike that lasts a week. The reeds only where the ground weeps stronger, along the invisible threads: taller, greener, greedy for moving oxygen.

Nothing here is lush. Everything is spaced, permitted. Each living plant has a halo of empty leached sand around it, the way desert plants do, except the desert is water.

Life, then, is not in the bulk. It's in the film.

Bend over a weeping stone, one of those rust-red laterite eggs half-sunk in black mud, and look at the halo it darkens around itself. That darkening is alive. Iron bacteria, rust and ochre slicks, filamentous green algae, a skin of diatoms that turns the mud gold then black as you stir it. Under that, in the flour-fine silt that puffs when you wade, bloodworms, midge larvae coiled red with hemoglobin because there is almost no oxygen down there, tiny white ostracods flicking like seeds. The mud stinks faintly sweet when you disturb it — rot without hurry. A fallen paper-bark does not disappear. It sinks and stays, waterlogged, a dark bar in the peat for decades, and on its back ferns and mosses and sundew seedlings make a garden above the waterline. Rot here is slow construction.

Out of that soup rise the columns.

You notice the noise before the bodies. The columns over the pans are midges and mosquitoes, thousands, holding station: a vertical pillar that wobbles but does not drift, males dancing, females dropping to the surface to lay and rising heavy. In the pillar, hawking, the dragonflies the color of hot metal — copper-bellied, teal-winged, eyes that meet at the top of the head. They never perch long; the air is too full. They mate on the wing, lay by dipping the tip of the abdomen, tick, tick, tick to the water. Under them the sawing, ceaseless, from the scattered crowns: cicadas, big, dark, eyes wide, clinging to pale bark and shaking the air until it shimmers more than the heat does. You don't see one until it stops, and then you realize the trunks were covered.

And at the edge, at ground level, the clickers. Small freshwater crabs the color of wet peat, one claw larger, burrows like thimbles in the bank where mud meets sand. As you pass they plop backwards, a soft wet cork sound, and wait with only eyestalks out. They come out at dusk to sift detritus, to carry leaf bits underground. Their tracks stitch the drying silt in the morning before the sheet rises again and erases them.

Up from the mud, the fish — because even water six inches deep that barely moves can hold fish if the fish agree to almost nothing. They are small, silver, two to three inches, schools of hardyheads and rainbowfish fry, backs olive to match tannin, bellies flashing when they turn. They live in the warm upper layer, dimpling the pollen-skin to take fallen midges, scattering at a shadow. In the year-round pans, the older ones, there are bigger, darker shapes underneath in the cool layer: catfish whiskered, gudgeons lying on silt, a turtle sometimes, long-necked, nose just breaking the surface like a twig, staying for an hour without moving. They all breathe water that is warm as blood and low in oxygen by gulping at the surface at dawn, or by slowing down until evening. They do not migrate. There is nowhere to migrate to. When a sheet shrinks they crowd tighter, herons come, and the survivors bury in mud or hold in the meander scar until rain returns that afternoon.

Frogs own the sound. You never see them at first. Three, four layers that trade off so the plain is never quiet. The high metallic trill without pause — tiny tree frogs the size of a fingernail, green-gold, bellies translucent, clinging to sedge blades over water, throat sacs pulsing white. The bottle-knock — squat burrowing frogs sunk to the eyes in black mud at the pan edge, calling with mouths closed. The long groaning bleat from deeper water — large green frogs floating spread-eagled, only nostrils and eyes out, legs trailing in cool silt. If you stay still and let your eyes learn the tussock scale, you start to see them: a throat moving, a back ridged like bark, a sudden plop and rings. Tadpoles blacken the shallows in commas, grazing algae off stems, becoming legs in weeks because the water may vanish, may not. Adults can wait buried in sand under a tussock through a dry spell, cocooned in shed skin, breathing slowly.

Where fish and frogs gather, the large slow ones arrive.

They are birds, but they behave like water. Egrets — great white, intermediate, little — stand in the sheets so still you take them for dead stumps, necks coiled, then uncoil faster than you can follow. Herons, grey, hunched. Ibis in loose lines, white and straw-necked and glossy black, probing the black grease with curved bills, methodical, lifting crabs and snails. Ducks gabbling from the pan you can't see — whistling ducks, grey teal — rising in a low gabble-flurry when the harrier quarters. The harrier itself: brown, owl-faced, wings in a shallow V, gliding inches over sedge tops without a beat, listening more than looking for rats and frogs. It never hurries; the thick air won't let it. Even flight here is wading.

In the interstices, mammals, mostly at edges and at dusk. The water rat, dark, otter-like, swimming strongly from tussock to tussock, nose up, tail ruddering, diving for mussels and leaving opened shells on a stone — the weeping stones are its tables. On the almost-imperceptible swells, two or three feet higher, where your boots suck less, the prints: wallaby, small, forefeet together, hind feet long, pressed into drying silt and already filling with seep. They come down at night to drink and nip sedge shoots, then retreat to drier scrub beyond the flat where you can't see. Bats later, over the columns, replacing dragonflies — quick flickers snapping midges out of the thickening dusk just before the afternoon rain comes.

None of them owns it. That is the point. No herd, no pack, no forest. Each is sparse, patient, amphibious in habit if not in name — able to be wet then wetter then briefly dry, able to eat almost nothing, to wait, to breathe thin soup. The paper-bark waits for rain. The bladderwort waits for water fleas. The frog waits buried. The heron waits standing. The whole plain is a practice in waiting loudly.

When the rain does come this afternoon, as the smell says it will, you will see them all at once tighten and loosen: sundews beading faster, midges dropping, fish dimpling harder, frogs getting louder layer over layer, ibis probing faster, stones weeping faster, water thinking, at last, about moving a leaf an inch.
