Who lives on the Table is at first a simple question. Almost no one.

Walk into a town built for thousands — terraces stepping down into the damp, three hundred roundhouses with turf roofs, granaries, sleeping platforms, streets worn half an inch down into solid granite by feet — and you will find three hearths smoking. That is all.

An old woman scraping lichen into a pot. A man taking a stone out of a wall and setting the same stone back a fraction straighter. Two children stacking pebbles into a tower and knocking it down without a sound. They do not look up for long. They are not rude. Sound does not carry right here, and loudness feels dangerous, like leaning over a drop. So they have learned to be quiet the way other people have learned to be polite.

Stay longer and the few become particular.

Their bodies have settled, like the land, into a final shape. Short, broad-chested, big-ribbed from a lifetime of thin air that goes bright and hard in the lungs. Even at noon in summer their breath shows. Their skin is wind-polished. Granite dust gets into the creases and never quite washes out, so old hands look grey at the knuckles. Their eyes are pale from squinting at brightness. Their hair is long, because nothing is wasted. When you see a cloak of grey felt that beads with morning condensation and ticks as the drops fall, understand: there are no sheep here. No goats. No dogs. That is human hair, collected, washed in tarn water, felted over years. A cloak is a family. A blanket is a lifetime.

They wear felt and woven dwarf-willow bark and soft boots stuffed with dry moss. They eat what the hollow gives. Bitter lichen porridge. Black-green liverwort boiled to a jelly. Dwarf willow bark tea. In the two-week summer when the hollows all exhale spores at once and steam faintly, they sweep spore-flour from flat slabs and make a dense bread, and bake it in peat. Because there are no trees, there is no wood. The fires that gutter low at night are peat fires — old moss burning. Old green burning. They are burning time to stay warm.

They do not hunt. There is nothing to hunt. No birds in the sky, no flies over the middens, no worms when you dig, just roots and cold. Ask where the animals went and they look puzzled, the way you would if someone asked where the second sun went. They do not miss them because the green has taken those jobs. Frost-heave churns the soil each night instead of worms. Drifting spores instead of flies. And moss instead of scavengers — drop a basalt flake, fall from a wall, die in your sleep in winter, and within a season the moss has you, gently, completely, without judgment. Tendrils creeping an inch an hour over stone. Fronds unrolling while you watch. If you put your ear to a hollow you hear water trickling under a foot of moss, and if you stay still you see the moss move.

That is the second thing that lives here, and it is the thing that truly lives here.

Not the people. The green.

It does not cover the Table. It gathers, in the lee of those worn stone backs where condensation collects, so dense and soft it hurts to look at after so much grey. Cushion-plants hard as pillows, moss a foot deep, dwarf willow no taller than your ankle and a hundred years old, lichen in rust and bone-white and sulfur. Without anything to eat it, it just keeps building on itself, peat over peat, until the hollows bulge upward like rising bread. It is biologically furious in its slow way. At night when frost comes down and freezes it stiff, it tinkles faintly if the wind touches it. That is what passes for wilderness here.

The people know they are guests of it. Their turf roofs carry moss deeper than the wild. Lichen has taken their walls until from a distance a town mistakes for bedrock. They sleep under turf if they can, because the moss muffles the doubling and you dream quieter. They drink only running water or the day's condensation beaded on wool and blade — never from a tarn showing a different sky. They never quarry new stone. Take from a fall. The Table does not like new holes. They stack stones not to build higher, but to pin the place down. Weight helps, they say. The light places drift.

Which brings the third thing that lives here.

On quiet days, especially when the fog that drowns the Table to the waist every morning lingers, you will see another settlement superimposed on the one you are in. Same walls, but whole. Taller. Crowded. Smoke from every roof. Figures moving in the mist that make no sound and leave no footprints. If you stand very still and do not look directly, you can hear it very faintly: a market, a song, stone on stone, hundreds of hands working.

It is not a haunting. No one is afraid. They treat it like weather. Because it is weather. The Table is vast enough to make its own weather in the middle, and some of that weather is time.

Distances do not hold. A town four hours away yesterday is six hours away today though the road has not moved. A tarn reflects two moons. From a high swell you will see the same broken ring of tall slabs in three valleys at once, and when you walk to the nearest the other two are gone. Echoes arrive before the sound — you hear your footstep half a second before your foot lands. Fog sometimes flows uphill, or stops mid-air in ribbons all day, solid enough to cast shadows. Cistern water at dawn is warm though the night was below freezing.

The people who live now are the thin remainder. The people who live *then* are the crowd. In the doubling they are still there, in their thousands, building the walls five, ten times over, wearing the streets down into stone, laying the flat-slab causeways across the bog, lining cisterns with clay, running field walls for miles to nowhere. Their tools are the same: stone mauls, basalt flakes, bone awls, antler picks — though where the antler came from no one will say.

Do not answer an echo that comes early. If you hear voices before mouths move, keep walking. Leave cairns alone. They are not markers. They are weights, holding this version of the town down so it does not blow away into the others.

At night it resolves. Frost feathers everything. The green freezes stiff. The towns go dark except for one or two peat fires. The man has stopped mending the same wall. The old woman has finished her lichen. The children have left their silent towers. Under turf, under moss a foot deep, the few sleep and breathe white into the cold.

And over them, patient, lush, cold, the moss keeps creeping over everything humans have stacked up, covering it all again. That slow crack of stone contracting, that sigh of peat settling deeper — that is the Table breathing. That is what lives here, and the people are only the part of it that, for a little while, still walks around and straightens stones.
