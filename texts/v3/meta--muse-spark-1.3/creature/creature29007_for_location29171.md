They are not one thing. That is the first mistake the dark lets you make.

At first, in the press and noise, they all feel like one animal — one huge, warm, many-jointed body breathing on you. The moth on your forearm and the frog at your eye-level and the chattering weight crashing overhead all seem like organs of it. In a way they are. This valley only works because everything exhales at once.

But if you stay, if you crouch down by that black cup of water between the blades of lava and let the riot go on over your head, they separate.

The insects first. Then the frogs. Then the hands.

You think *monkeys* at first when the troop comes through. It's the size and the crash of them, the way branches sag and spring back, the scolding chatter. But no monkey moves like this.

They are small. A big male would barely make two handfuls — two, maybe three kilos, all limb and rib and wet fur. The fur itself is wrong for the heat: short, dense, black-tipped and slate underneath, soaked through by midnight so it clings and shows the skin. It has to be. Anything fluffy here would rot. They steam faintly when they pause.

What you notice next are the eyes. When a dozen of them stop to look at you at once from a vine tangle, the eyeshine comes back amber and green-white, huge and forward-facing, with pupils blown so wide in the doubled dark there is almost no iris. They don't so much look at you as let you pass through their seeing. Blinks go in waves through the troop, like the frog lights.

And then the fingers. That is what stays with you.

Long. Obscenely long for their body, the forelimb fingers especially, bare of fur to the wrist, pink-gray and deeply creased, tipped with small, blunt, black nails that are always chipped fresh on basalt. The middle finger is longest and thinnest — you can see the tendon move under the skin like a string. When they walk the fern mat they don't palm down like a monkey. They spread those fingers wide and feel, testing each moss-bridge for give before they commit weight. Stone this young will take a finger if you are careless. Old adults all have missing tips, shortened digits, white seams of scar. The babies ride belly-clung and practice reaching, over and over, missing.

They live by touch because hearing here is almost useless.

Think what this noise does to them. Night after night of sawtooth insects and bottle-plonk frogs and chewing right by the ear. They cannot call far. So they don't try. Their troop-talk is close and physical: a high, broken chitter you only hear when they are within arm's reach, and most of it is not sound at all. It is cheek-rubbing and chest-pressing and fingers combing fingers. They pile when they rest, twenty or thirty of them in a crotch of branches draped in sponge-root, a breathing heap that rises and falls together. They land almost on each other when they leap and do not fight about it. There is no room to be solitary here. Solitary would mean falling through the mat onto the knives below.

They are night animals in a night that never lifts. Their day is this wet black. They wake fat and damp at sundown, shake off in a single shudder that throws a halo of droplets, and immediately begin to drink the air with their noses. Their muzzles are short and naked and constantly beaded — you can see the mist gather there first, run down the philtrum to the mouth. Like you, they are warm stones. Moths come to drink from their shoulders while they work.

Work is constant, and it is all in the small.

Watch one female at the water cup. She is not beautiful in the way field guides mean. Her face is too pointed, her ears too large and veined and always swiveling, flattening against the din. But her attention is absolute.

She finds a shrub-thorn, snaps it, strips the side-barbs with her incisors in three quick pulls — *tss tss tss* — tests the point with her thumb pad. Then she hangs one-handed from a fern stem, head-down over bark, and slides that thorn with that terrible thin finger into a crevice, feeling, twisting. She pulls out a grub, fat and white, and eats it alive. She drops the thorn. It floats.

Beside her a juvenile is failing to do the same, jabbing too hard. She does not teach him. She just does it again, slowly, where he can see. He steals her thorn when she drops it.

Another comes with a pebble, no bigger than his own thumb joint, holds a snail one-handed against the lava fin — you can hear his knuckles scrape — and hammers, *tick tick crack*, until the shell stars. He sucks the body out, wipes his hand on leaves, drops the stone. By morning ants will have carried the shell chips away and the stone will be just a stone.

That is their whole technology. Nothing kept. Nothing carried to a camp. No cache, no cut mark that lasts. Hands that make and unmake in the same minute.

It is not stupidity. It is the valley's rule made flesh. Here everything that stays still is eaten, overgrown, dissolved by ants or fungus or mist. A stored tool would be a rotted tool by dawn. So memory lives not in objects but in other hands, watched and immediately copied, lost and reinvented nightly. A thousand times a night a thorn becomes a probe and becomes floating trash again.

They are part of the rain. You understand that if you watch long enough. Their lungs are among the tens of thousands making the fog. Their leaf-cupping cousins overhead — the big door-leaves that slowly open and close — comb out what the climbers breathe. The climbers drink what the leaves condense, piss and shit it back into the sponge-mats, breathe it out again. The frogs with their amber throat-coals sit in the cups the climbers drink from and eat the tadpoles that eat the mosquito larvae that drank the climbers' blood. The pale root-snake that drops through and is mobbed hunts the tiny sleep-confused birds that hunt the palm-sized moths that drink the dew off the climbers' backs. Even their dropped probes become rafts for tadpoles for an hour.

They do not rule this. Nothing does. The larger grunters out in the black — heavy, low, pushing through fern on knuckles and snout, probably twice your weight, answering each other too close for comfort — could kill a climber with a casual bite and sometimes do. The climbers scream and scatter and reform ten feet away in a new shape, like the swarm, and go back to fishing.

If you hold still until your own breath joins the valley weather, until dew forms on you before it forms on the leaves, one may come close. Not tame — there is no tame here, no history of hands like yours. Just unafraid in the press. It will touch your forearm with those long, cool, wet fingers to test if you are bridge or stone, sniff the salt-moth-drink on your skin, look past you with those huge lit eyes for grubs in your sleeve. You will feel the calluses on its palm, thick as leather from lava, and the delicacy of the fingertip beyond them, almost sticky, reading you.

Then the troop crashes on, and it goes with them, leaping into dark that is slightly less dark, leaving only a floating thorn and a cracked shell and a fresh chip on a pebble you will never find again.
