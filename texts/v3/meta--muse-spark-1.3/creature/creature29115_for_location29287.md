They do not live on the mesa so much as in its hours.

At first you think it is the swifts. They are the most bodies, the most noise-that-isn't-noise, the ones that make the air feel used.

Then you stay past nine and you understand they are only the loudest tenants. Everything here lives the same lease: drink before the sun takes it, hide through the white heat, steal a second drink when the cloud climbs.

The swift-things are built for this and nothing else. Soot-dark, pale-throated, sickle-winged, feet so small and hooked they cannot walk, only cling. In the dawn lift they pour off the pillar faces in thousands — no calling, because the thin air shreds sound and because a kestrel can hear a call — just a dry whirr as a whole sheet of them turns. They drink on the wing, skimming the pillar-cups, lower mandible kissing water for half a second. They bathe the same way. All day they snap up what hatches out of those cups. At midday when the center of the field is a kiln they do not leave. They back into their mud-and-guano cups plastered deep in the slots, where the basalt is still cool from night, and sit with beaks open, throats fluttering, panting. Whitewash streaks every nesting pillar below them, generations thick. The rock smells faintly of ammonia and old feathers and that is them, their history glued on.

Under them, in the slots, live the waiting ones.

The lizards are the color of sunburnt basalt exactly — you don't see one until its pink throat-fan flicks. Pink, pink, pink. Three to a crevice, wedged head-out, throat pulsing. They do not chase. They cannot afford to. They wait for the copper-backed flies to blunder low enough, then take them with a snap so fast it ticks. In the heat they go flat against stone and blanch paler, shunting blood away. In the twenty minutes when the cloud-sea climbs and everything beads wet, they come out all at once, peeping high and thin to each other, doing push-ups on the pillar shoulders, mating, fighting, drinking licks off lichen before it evaporates.

Lower still, between the teeth where you have to turn sideways, live the runners.

Fat as guinea pigs, fur the dun-gray of dust, naked rat-tails for dumping heat, whiskers perpetually beaded after the cloud. Rock-runners. They move in extended families of eight or twelve, single file along memorized squeezes, belly-fur worn off from scraping basalt. They drink in shifts — that is the only word for it. Two drink while one sits upright on a neighboring pillar, nose twitching, watching for kestrel shadow. Then they rotate. They know every natural cup and they know the artificial ones better. There is a slow leak at a pipe joint on the western traverse where a washer never quite sealed, and every morning there is a queue there, orderly, ill-tempered, squeaking. They prefer it to the natural cups because it drips ten minutes longer into the heat, shaded by the pipe itself. When the pumps sigh-shudder at midday they freeze, not from fear — they were born to that sound — but because vibration means water moving underfoot and they listen to learn where.

They are prey, and they know the schedule of it.

The pale kestrels live highest, which means they live mostly elsewhere. They do not nest among the teeth — too crowded, too hot. They come from the far rims, from other island-tops floating beyond the cloud, hanging motionless in the rim updraft on long, fingered wings, so pale underneath they are almost white against blue. They do not hunt the center at noon. Even they obey. They work the edges in the heat, taking a lizard off a sun-shoulder or a runner caught crossing a plank, dropping folded between pillars with a sound like a knife sheathing. At dawn and at cloud-rise, when the swifts are thickest and stupid with feeding, they stoop through the flock and the whole flock bends around them like water around a stone.

Lowest, shortest-lived, most numerous, are the cup-fillers.

The gnats in mosquito-thick clouds, the copper-backed flies with their hot metallic glint, the water-boatmen rowing frantically in a finger of water that will be gone by three, the blood-worms curling red in the sediment. They hatch, mate, lay, and die on a daily clock. Their eggs can sit dry as dust in a white mineral ring for weeks, waiting for one night of cloud-breath. One eleven-minute black rainstorm is a generation to them. The lizards and swifts eat them, the runners inhale them incidentally while drinking and sneeze them out, the guano from all of it feeds the next hatch. Life here does not run on leaves. There are almost no leaves. It runs on each other and on sky-water.

Because even the plants have quit being plants.

A tuft of straw-grass every twenty steps, wiry, rolled-leaf, root reaching meters down a crack for pipe-sweat. Orange lichen and black crust lichen painted so tight to north-facing basalt you can't scrape them with a nail. They grow thickest in a green-gray line that follows the buried pipe for half a kilometer, tracing where it sweats cold at night. That line is the most vegetation on the mesa. The yellow stain out east where steam ghosts before dawn and the water smells of eggs — no pipe there, no runners, no lizards. Only sulfur bacteria, living on heat from below, patient as the geothermal warmth that keeps every pillar faintly warm to the palm even before sunrise.

And then there are the ones who joined the machines.

A small brown wren-like bird you missed at first because it never flies high. It nests inside the rust-red pump hut, behind the warm transformer box, using stripped wire insulation and swift feathers. It drinks condensation off the solar array at cloud-rise, running along the tilted panels. Moths cluster on the green LED at night, and the lizards have learned to hunt by that green blink.

And the human animal.

No one lives here the way a town means living. But someone lives here. The door of the hut propped with a stone, the swept plank walkways, the hand-painted CHECK VALVES / LEAVE CUPS FULL TILL 9 in sun-crazed paint — that is not remote operation. That is a person with sun-cracked hands and thin-blood slowness who has learned not to waste the morning.

She is lean, burnt dark at the neck and forearms, white where her hat shades. She moves only on the planks, sideways between pillars, never crushing a cup. She checks the gauges, bleeds a valve, listens with her molars to the pump sigh. She leaves the natural cups alone till nine even when the cistern is low, because the swifts and runners drink first — that is the rule, unspoken but enforced by everything, and she enforces it on herself. At midday she shelters with the rest, in the stripe of shade behind the hut, beak — mouth — open, breathing fast and shallow in the thin air, listening to metal ping as it cools. At cloud-rise she is out again, wiping nets, tipping the fog-water to storage while lizards run over her boots.

At dusk she watches the mesa empty upward as the swifts funnel out over the rim. The gnats settle. The stones hold their heat longest. She can sit on one at midnight and feel it through her clothes, faintly alive, while the machines tick and the green light blinks and the runners huddle deep in the slots, tails wrapped over noses to save moisture.

There is no room here that is not used, and she is used too — another warm body that knows not to block a drip-line, not to make noise at midday, to wait for water like everyone else.
