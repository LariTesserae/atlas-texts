They are not where you look first.

At first you think locusts - a smudge far off that moves wrong. Then you think ash, or foil caught in a thermal. Then the gyre banks and catches the sun all at once and you understand it is animals, and that there are too many to count, and that counting would be the wrong kind of attention anyway.

What lives here lives in the air by being almost air itself.

The low ones are the ones you will know best. Each is as long as your forearm, some as long as your body, and weighs almost nothing. Hold one - if one ever let you - and you would hold a husk. Amber chitin thin as foil, translucent, veined through with black iron like leaf-veins or circuit traces. Their wings are not feathered. They are vaned, stiff fins with flutes to shed heat, and when they rasp edge to edge in flight they make that mill-town shirr that fills the pan. Underneath: a belly that is mostly empty. A chamber. They take in the hot updraft off the salt and hold it, ballooned, and ride without flapping for hours. They are living hot-air balloons that learned how to steer.

They do not drink. They do not graze. There is nothing to graze.

They eat what is here: stone and air and heat.

Watch one come low across a salt polygon. It unfolds its face. The mouthparts open out into a wide scoop, a mineral sieve fine as gauze, and it skims. Salt dust, iron oxide, copper-green drifts, shards of volcanic glass - it takes it all in a pale mouthful. You can see through its thorax while it works. The dust goes in pale and is sorted, crushed, smelted. Inside the abdomen are plates, stacked, and between them sparks jump, again and again, a tiny arc furnace. It is using the heat it holds in its belly and the charge it builds up rasping its wings together to melt rock inside its own body.

Every few minutes it is done. It extrudes from its rear a perfect bead, thumb-sized, machined, still glowing dull orange where it pinches off. Copper, or iron, or glass, depending on what it skimmed. It drops with a ping onto the salt and is left. No nest. No cache. No young to feed.

This is the animal: a hollow factory that makes things for no one. An engine with the vehicle left off.

Individually it is almost nothing - paper-light, a vast space between one body and the next. Collectively they become weather. Thousands upon thousands turn in a slow gyre wider than any town, riding the same thermal up and around and down to skim again. When they bank together the whole sky flashes copper at once. Their shadows stipple the white pan until the ground itself seems to move. The roar is not one throat but ten thousand wing-edges, and the higher organ-tone underneath - air forced through the fluted yardangs - is what they steer by. Each ridge has a different pitch. They slide between tones.

Behind them the pan is littered with their work. Millions of beads scattered like ball bearings, never piled, never built into anything. The wind buries them in dust. The next pass of the gyre uncovers them. If you stay until the light thins - and it thins without reddening, just going pale at the edges - you will see each bead crowned with blue-white corona, static lifting off every salt curl, and the bellies of ten thousand smelters glowing faintly from within as they work the day's last dust. Heat becoming work becoming heat again.

They do not reproduce the way you expect. They duplicate.

Stare at one low flyer crossing white salt and sometimes its afterimage does not fade. It holds, a meter to the left, solidifies, banks with it. For a while there are two where there was one. Then one peels upward to join the gyre and you cannot tell which was first. Motion is conserved here, but position is negotiable. The air is so charged, the light so bent, time so loose - you hear the rasp swell before the gyre banks, you see two suns for a heartbeat, your shadow throws a double edge - that if an animal flies long enough and true enough in the same hot circuit, the sky keeps a copy of it. That is enough. No mating, no eggs, no care. The swarm keeps itself turning for decades, perhaps centuries, by flying and being copied by flight.

And high above them, where the heat lets the sky go clear for a second, live the old ones.

The mantas. Fifty meters across, of the same amber foil, barely moving. Their vast wings ripple once a minute and otherwise they hang in the thermal as if nailed there. They trail filaments, hair-fine, kilometers long, that discharge lightning sideways into empty air with a whip-crack you hear three seconds before you see the flash - the sound arriving before its cause, as everything does here. They are patient beyond biology. They have been circling the same lift since before the yardangs took their shape.

A manta is what a smelter becomes if it does not shatter, does not thin and tear in the wind. It gets larger, lighter, hollower. Its gut for stone atrophies. Its belly for heat grows. It stops skimming salt and starts skimming charge, feeding on the ion wind and the static the whole gyre below pumps upward. The small ones make metal beads to dump weight and stay aloft. The great ones make lightning to dump charge and stay aloft. Different ballast, same bargain.

Nothing hunts them. Nothing needs to. When a low one fails it does not fall like a bird. It crumples in flight, foil collapsing inward, and the wind takes it as bright shreds that ping and scatter and are gone by the next polygon. When a manta fails - which almost never happens - it does not fall either. It discharges. One long sideways stroke that lights the whole pan blue-white, and then an empty skin settling, slowly, turning, joining the dust it once ate.

Under all of it is the third animal, or the first. You never see it. You feel it in your molars.

Deep under the salt, the slow piston beat: *tuh — tuh — tuh —* irregular, speeding, missing, catching again a minute later. It does not get louder as you walk toward the center of the pan, only slower, as if depth were time. That is the deep one breathing, pumping. A vast, flat, slow body whose back is the pan itself. Its breath is the organ roar through the yardangs. Its scales are the salt polygons lifting into sharp curls. The smelters are its lice and its lungs at once - skimming dust, smelting it, dropping pure beads that sink in time through the salt to where it can taste iron and copper without moving. The mantas are its old thoughts, circling and discharging and keeping the sky conductive.

You can lie down between the salt curls and feel it miss a beat and find it again. The sky too full, the ground too empty, and between them this commerce: dust lifted, melted, dropped as perfect useless spheres; heat held, spent, held again; bodies copied by light until there are enough bodies to darken the day.

That is what lives here. Not locusts. Not birds. Hollow smelters that eat rock with lightning in their bellies, and sky-whales that eat the sky, and something enormous and slow underneath that eats time by pumping it.
