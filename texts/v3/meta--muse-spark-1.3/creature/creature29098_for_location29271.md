No one lives *on* it. They live *in* it, and of it. The place doesn't have inhabitants the way a field has mice. It has organs.

At first you think the big things are the Towers and the small things are everything else. Then you watch for a day and that inverts.

Under your boots first — because you are standing on a living thing and you have to start there:

**The Mat.**

It isn't ground cover. It's the ground. A meter thick, springy as a mattress, warm with friction underneath despite the cool air. If you kneel and pull — and it takes both hands — you tear up a plug and see what it really is: wrist-thick rhizomes, pale and blind, knotted and re-knotted, tightening very slowly, a peristalsis you feel more in your wrist bones than see. Between them, dead lint compacted to a dry peat that never got wet, cork blisters that pop underfoot with a resin smell, sheets of papery lichen that crackle when you step and seal again behind you by evening.

It eats light and stone-dust and air, and it eats itself. Nothing touches rock except through it. When a grazer dies — and they do, snapped legs in a tear, old age in a hollow — it doesn't rot. It cures. It dries in an afternoon to jerky and hair, sinks a hand's breadth into the felt, and by the week is lichen-covered, by the month is Mat. Grave and floor are the same word here.

The Mat is total underneath. You feel it when you lay your palm flat: a faint vibration coming from very far away, work being done. The rhizomes shift against wind-load, a whole continent bracing. A settled hill will have crawled a hand's breadth by morning. It never sleeps, because the wind never stops.

And out of it, so sparsely you can count them, the uprights:

**The Towers.**

From the rim you took them for hills until one turned. Up close that mistake is obscene.

Each one is alone — a kilometer, two kilometers to the next — because nothing else could afford the space. A trunk thirty, forty meters around, not wood but braided bast, grey rope twisted on itself, scarred with inhalation slits that whistle and shriek when the plateau wind hits. A hundred meters up it splits into arms thick as old whales, holding up a head that blocks the sun: a sour globe of dry bracts and spines and sails, each sail big as a barn door, parchment-yellow, ash-white, rust-red. No green anywhere. No leaves. They don't do lowland photosynthesis. They comb.

They live by combing. The sails fray continuously — you can see it happening, a bright river peeling sideways for miles — tons of down a day per Tower, wax scales, oil mist, static. Their anchors, where the Mat tears, are not roots. They are screws, pale augers driven deep into the old sleeping rock below, grinding powdered mineral, drinking fog-oil out of the air.

They breathe. That's not a metaphor, it's the noise in your sternum. The hollow trunk is a bellows, an organ pipe that was never made. *Chuff-chuff-chuff* — long exhalations you feel in your teeth — between gusts a grinding groan for minutes as ten tons of fiber moves on wax with no oil. Along the branches sit the seed-mills: hardwood pods like press-cylinders that clamp and stamp, stamp, stamp, all day, extruding identical pale ingots of packed fluff that drop and pile in neat windrows for miles downwind. No factory floor. Just grown production.

They are electrified. The air is so dry the down carries charge until blue sparks snap spine-tip to spine-tip, until at dusk St. Elmo's fire crawls over the heads and the whole moor hums. They weave charge out of friction.

They live long and die spectacularly. Every few years one topples in the night with a crash like a shipwreck that you feel through the Mat in your camp five miles away. By morning its fallen trunk is already sprouting a white rash of new anchors, becoming a long low ridge. That is a nursery. That's where the grazers go first, where the Mat is thinnest and sweetest. A Tower is not an individual. It's a phase the Mat goes through, standing up for a while to talk to the wind.

At noon on a hard-light day a ripe head detonates. A dry pop you hear five miles out. A black cloud of oilseeds goes *up*, not down, caught in updraft and static, hanging like smoke. Within hours, the pulsed life arrives.

Which is:

**The grazers, the mowers.**

Big as bison, light as deer — that contradiction resolves when you see them walk. Wide, splayed feet that don't punch through but spread load on the springy felt. Wool — no, more like felted lint matted with Tower down until each animal carries its own static cloud; blue sparks tick off their backs when they shake. They move in lawns. A herd of forty will mow a swath of lichen-felt to stubble, teeth grinding audibly even under the wind-roar, and move on, leaving dry pellet dung that the myriapods will harvest within the hour.

They shout to live. Everything here shouts. Their calls are low, chesty bellows pitched to cut under the Tower chuff. When the daily storm-wall comes — white lint turning grey twenty miles out, lightning walking cloud to Tower-head to cloud — they all lie down at once and face it, rumps to the Mat anchors, heads low. You do the same before you think. You breathe through cloth while the world whites out and the Mat ripples under you like skin.

**The clingers.**

You hear them before you place them: a high chittering on the windward side of the nearest Tower. Flocks of needle-beaked things, black and rust, each no bigger than your hand but a hundred to a trunk, clinging impossibly to vertical braided fiber in a gale with splayed claws and stiff tail props. Their beaks are hypodermics for wax. They strip scale-wax, pry cork blisters, drink the fog-oil that condenses on cold fiber at dawn before it sublimates. In gusts they flatten, feathers — if they are feathers, more like frayed sailcloth — plastered down, and ride it out. Between gusts they quarrel incessantly, shrill bursts tuned above the organ-pipe roar. Without them the Towers would over-wax and choke their slits; with them the trunks are striped clean, pale scars spiraling up.

**The skyfish.**

They own the air between Towers. Pale, manta-like gliders with six-foot membranes thin as lung tissue, you can see sun through them. Hundreds ride the Tower updrafts in slow spirals, mouths open, filtering down out of the air, shrieking thinly — the only high sound that carries over the stamping. When a head bursts, they are there in minutes, gorging on oilseeds still aloft, bellies going dark with oil. When the storm comes they vanish. Not away — *in*. They fold their wings and wedge themselves deep into the leeward bracts of the Tower head, hanging in rows like pale fruit, riding out the wall of fluff that would shred them. At clear evening they drop again, glittering with sublimating wax-frost, and the sky fills with their shadows.

**The underthings, the copper tenders.**

Long as your forearm, copper-red, many-legged, shining with oil. You find them by following the windrows of pale ingots stamped out by the seed-mills. There they work in lines thousands long, an anthill assembly with no hill: cutting ingots open, rolling oilseeds, burying some shallow in Mat tears, eating some, leaving some to catch static and lift. The lines shift direction all at once for no reason you can see — a ripple passes and ten thousand workers turn. They tend. They farm the Tower's output. Put your ear to the Mat near a line and you hear a faint clicking, leg on leg, transmitted through rhizome like a telegraph.

They are not alone down there. Where lint compacts there are pale blind grubs fat on oil, static-mites that gather at spine-tips to drink charge, spore-things that bloom white for an hour after a lightning strike smells like bread. Everything cures, nothing rots. Even death is dry manufacturing.

That is the tilt: water is forbidden. Any hollow that would hold sleet-melt is lined with hydrophobic wax; droplets bead and skitter like mercury and are gone by noon. The plants don't want it. Their chemistry runs on oil and air and light and ground stone. To drink would be to rot. The animals carry their water inside like a secret, sealed in waxed gut and oiled skin. Even your sweat dries too fast to cool you.

So at midday the combined noise — wind + bellows-chuff + fiber-creak + stamping pods + static crackle + grinding teeth + ten thousand wings — becomes a physical weather flattening the hairs on your arms, and you understand: this is a desert that is a jungle. Sparse at the top and total underneath. A factory with no builders where everything manufactured was grown, extruded, stamped by living wood, and will unravel within miles into food, nest, soil, spark.

Stand still between two Towers with your ears ringing and your forehead sunburning in cool air while your boots hum. Feel the slow tightening under your palm. Look up as the nearest head turns a fraction, deliberately, to face the storm it helped make, sails cracking like sails, shedding its bright river to feed things you can't see yet.
