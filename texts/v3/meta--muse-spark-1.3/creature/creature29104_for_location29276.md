There is no room for anyone to live in here.

That is the first truth of it. The room is already full. Leaf on leaf on hand on pot on wall, no gap for a body that isn't green and drinking. You fold yourself in and immediately you are trespass — something has to be crushed under knee or elbow to make space, a taro ear bent back, a vine re-routed around your neck. And yet everything in here was carried up. Everything in here is kept. So someone lives here, even if there is no room.

Start with the obvious citizens. The green ones.

They are not decoration. Up here they are population, crowd, slum and jungle at once. Taro first, because taro forgives. Great shield-ears jammed into coil-pots too small for them, sulking in the corners where the compost heat comes up strongest. Their roots have already decided the pots are suggestions, not containers — white fingers punching straight through unfired clay, drinking from the moss joints between floor flags. They jostle. In the heat you can hear a leaf's wet squeak as it shoulders another leaf aside to get closer to the slit-light. They sweat from their tips every morning of the three-second rain.

Then the ferns, which are the carpenters. They live in the walls more than in the pots, rhizomes threaded into the smeared living mortar, crosiers unrolling with an audible creak, an inch a day, fast enough to watch if you go altitude-stupid and stare. They hold the stones together better than mud-slurry. Where your breath fogged on bare basalt, they answer first — a green fire blooming outward from the moisture in seconds. Where your knee scraped and welled blood on sharp fracture, the nearest frond leans. Not toward light. Toward iron.

The orchids are the aristocrats and the parasites. Bruised-purple throats hung on fiber strings from the sagging trellis, roots naked in air, doing nothing useful but drinking mist and being impossibly purple at five kilometers up. They would have no pollinators if not for what was brought with them.

Which is almost nothing. The room is too high, too new, too wet-hot for anything to have found it on its own. Only the imported.

The white singers in the mortar. You won't see them at first because they are the color of stone flour. Tiny blind crickets, the size of a grain of rice, living *in* the moss that was smeared into every joint by careful fingers. They eat mold. They eat spore-dust. They prune the moss with their mouths so it stays spongy and dripping and does not choke itself. And they sing — if sing is the word for a pitch so high and constant it lives in your molars, not your ears. That ache in your teeth is them tuning. The sound keeps the water beading. Or so the builders will tell you. The crickets were brought up in a pocket, in moss, by accident. Now there are hundreds. They own the walls.

And in the middle, the old one. A single mottled catfish, no longer than your finger, circling and circling the knee-wide basin pecked into the floor slab. It came up as a fingerling in a gourd of water sloshing on someone's back for four thousand steps. It is the only thing in here with eyes that work in the dim, and it uses them. It watches you. It has watched a dozen sweating faces fold themselves through the low door before you, and it knows you will leave and it will not. It eats mosquito wrigglers that hatch in the jars. It eats the skin you shed. Its circling stirs the black warm water just enough to keep it from skinning over, just enough to let the mist thicken yellow above it before the next three-foot rain.

Those are the residents. But they are not the inhabitants. The inhabitants are the ones who cannot stay.

The builders. The tenders. The hands.

You hear them before you feel them — hammer on stone from somewhere far below, arriving *before* the vibration climbs up through the flags into your soles. Cause arriving late, up here where the world is thin. They are still building. That is the law because nothing is finished: the left side of the back wall is double-thick, the right side single; someone was in the middle of thickening it when the heat drove them out for air. The trellis cord is half-knotted. The adzes and hammerstones and swollen wooden mallet were dropped mid-stroke. No one *lives* up here the way you live in a house. They live in relay.

One at a time they come. They are chosen small — you have to be small to fold double under that sap-bleeding lintel and then fold again among the pots. Barrel-chested from a lifetime of blade-air, palms white with stone dust and glitter, lips cracked. They bring up one thing each trip because that is all you can carry for three kilometers straight up: a coil-cup nested in a jar, a split gourd of seedlings, a gourd of water with a fingerling, a basket of leaf-litter and nightsoil for the vent behind the back wall that makes the bottom-heat, sweet-rot and iron-smelling. They stay a day, two days, sweating it out in the soupy dark, becoming weather themselves.

Because that is the job: to be taken. The room drinks people. The hot air takes your sweat before it can drip. The moss takes your breath and blooms from it. If you cup your hands under a drip and pour it into a thirsty pot, you have joined the circuit. Press a handful of living moss into a weeping joint. Tighten a lashing with your teeth. Spit into a stone cup for a cutting. Bleed a little on the fresh peck-marks and watch the fern lean closer. The room instructs without words: *add.* You cannot carry water down — if you tried, the jar would be empty before the first landing, drunk back as mist. Water cannot leave. It can only go around. So do you. You go around, briefly, as cloud, as sweat, as piss in the compost, as fog on eyelashes that a leaf drinks.

If you stay longer than you meant to, as you will, you will see the tender before you still faintly in the air: stone flour and pollen and spores hanging in the amber blades from the slit-windows, nothing settled, nothing having had time to settle. The dust of making. It never clears because someone is always making.

And underneath all of them, tended by all of them, is the one who truly lives here and has not been born yet.

The weather itself. The private rain.

Outside, the mountain's weather is far below — a flat white floor of cloud sea, violet noon sky, wind roaring like water around a pier, prayer strips cracking like whips. Inside, a three-second, three-foot shower from ceiling to floor, plink into jars, plink into blood-warm pool, hiss where a drop hits warm stone. Then it stops. Then it starts again. It is embryonic. It has not learned to be large. Sometimes it makes mistakes because it is learning: one drop falls *up* from the pool to the ceiling, slow as a bubble. The far wall that was three arm-lengths away is suddenly at your elbow. A pot you stepped over is behind you without you having moved. The room is too small for weather and doing it anyway, practicing.

That is what lives at the top of the tower that is not a mountain. Not a man, not a garden. A saturation. A lung full that has learned to breathe itself. Green tenants racing predatorless for slit-light, white blind tuners in the walls, one old eyed fish circling its black blood, and a procession of small cracked hands that feed it breath and sweat and shit and blood and then go back down gasping into the blade-air to quarry more stone.

A womb, handmade, half-born. And it is hungry to continue.
