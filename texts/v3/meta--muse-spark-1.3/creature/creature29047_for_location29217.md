At first you think no one lives here.

You pull yourself along a shell path and the forest closes over you, and all you see is stipe and light and fish weather. Then a shadow detaches from a shadow. Then another. They were there the whole time, hanging still, the way seals hang.

The people.

They are not many. Count the amber bells and you have counted the households — thirty or so — and most hold three or four souls, no more. Seventy people on a good year. Fewer after a hard winter. You could gather the whole hamlet in the middle glade and still see blue water between every shoulder.

You learn them by how they move before you learn them by face. They do not swim so much as haul and glide, hand over hand along ropes and stipes, then a push with flat feet and a long coast. Everyone is cool to touch, even through the cold that has already numbed you. Their skin has learned not to fight the water. Shoulders, cheeks, backs oiled dark with rendered seal and kelp oil against the eleven-degree seep. Hair cropped short with a chert flake — anything long would snag in the fronds and kill you in a surge — so heads look seal-round and young even on the old. Chests are the giveaway. Broad, barrelled, big-lunged from childhood. The ribs move visibly when they surface-breathe, up there on the roof where the fronds lie flat. They go up fifteen meters to the ceiling of the world, fill, and come down with cheeks full and eyes open, no mask, no iron, nothing dead. A strong adult can cross the whole town on one breath if the tide is slack. The children tell who is strong by counting houses passed without exhaling.

Up close they are loud in a loud place. They click and hum more than they talk — throats make a low chest-hum that carries through water and wood. On ropes, a code of pulls: two sharp for *come*, long drag for *hold, tide turning*. In the air of the bells, where they can crouch head-out-of-water on woven frond platforms, voices go high and gaspy and laughing, because air is brief and shared. Every visit begins with a gift of breath. You duck under the lip of someone else's bell and empty your lungs up into it, watching your bubbles join their roof, adding to the store. It is rude to arrive empty.

Each family is a bell-keeper and a garden. The old ones — the pruners, the grafters, hands gone soft-tough as stipe skin — spend years coaxing five or six thigh-thick stipes to lean. You see them out in all light, hanging upside down with bone needles and braided kelp twine, stitching living tissue to living tissue, mouths full of tools they will never swallow. A new house takes seven seasons. No one hurries. A hurried graft rots, and rot spreads. The young ones learn patience first as urchin-herds. Naked but for a waist thong and a stick, puffing and ducking, five- and six-year-olds patrol the dulse and sea-lettuce clearings between bells, tapping purple-black urchins back from the tender young bladders. They eat the offenders. Cracked open on a stone with a basalt flake, orange roe licked from the shell while treading water. That is breakfast.

Inside the bells they are dry in a way you forget is possible. Not dry like land. Dry like an armpit. Seal furs, cured in oil and urine and smoke carried down in bladders from the surface fires, line the frond platform. Bone needles, fire-stones — smooth basalt eggs warmed at the noonday surface and brought down hot in baskets of fronds to hold against the belly — a child, always a child. Babies live almost entirely in air until their lungs come. You will see a toddler's face peer blinking over the lip of a bell, fat-cheeked, iodine-smelling, staring at you the diver while its mother hangs below in the water, hair floating up, holding the rope with her toes so her hands are free to mend net.

For the town is not only people.

The second citizens are the crabs. Platter-wide, brick-red, slow and ill-tempered, they pick across the springy holdfast mat day and night, clipping rot with their shears. The people do not herd them, exactly. They weed around them. A good crab run is left alone; a basket trap takes only the one-clawed males. The children know them by missing legs. The old joke is the crabs tend the people.

The third citizen lives under the causeway and is one person. Everyone knows her. Rust-red, armspan wider than a man is tall, old enough to have learned burglary. She steals. Chert flakes, bone hooks, a whole braid of rope if you leave it unguarded. In return she leaves things arranged at the edge of her den: perfect scallop shells, a crab claw cracked neatly, once a child's lost tooth. The herds knock on stone before they pass so as not to startle her. No one hunts her. To steal back from her is a game that teaches dexterity.

Then the lodgers: the seals. Harbor seals, spotted and dog-faced, drop from the hammered ceiling like stones and sprawl in the glades between houses to nap, barking streams of bubbles, utterly insolent. The people nap with them, sometimes back to back in the surge for warmth, and it is hard at first to tell which dark bodies are human and which are not. They take seals rarely, with fasting and apology, far out beyond the wall. The skins in the bells are mostly from old animals found floating, or traded down through generations. You do not eat your neighbor's bedfellow lightly.

And the weather: fish. Rivers of black rockfish and copper surfperch, thousands thick, pouring through the streets and blocking the sun. They are food and they are roof and they are news — when they turn on a single thought, the whole town looks up. Mussels and barnacles tick on the old house stones. The urchins click their teeth, static day and night. At night the forest settles the way birds settle, fish tucking into fronds, and then the smallest residents take over — the copepods, pinhead crustaceans that colonize the condensation inside every old bell and kindle cold green. Each house becomes a lantern. From the edge you see them swaying: a sunk constellation.

Under it all, the eldest: the kelp itself. Not a backdrop. A living ancestor. The holdfast mat a meter thick, root over root into basalt, is generations deep. The stipes groan and rub, wet wood on wet wood. The fused bladders that make the bells sweat oxygen and smell of cut grass, and if you press your ear to a twelve-year wall you hear it fizzing, a faint chorus, and feel it a degree warmer than the sea. That warmth is breath remembered. Every exhalation ever fed into it, every child panted into it, has become tissue.

Even the dead stay. Wrapped in frond, weighted with a house stone, planted in the holdfast to feed the stipe that will become someone's house. So that when you pull yourself along and the mat gives under your hand, you are moving over grandmothers. When you duck into a bell and smell iodine and milk and old smoke, you are breathing inside kin.

Far below the edge, where the mountain falls away into blue that forgets color, the last resident passes and never stays. A gray whale, low enough you feel it in your sternum before you hear it. The whole hamlet stops. Hands go to ropes. Children in bells go quiet. They hang there, cool-skinned, big-chested, lantern-keepers and urchin-herds and grafters, rooted on a summit and suspended over an abyss, listening to something huge move along the flank of their mountain like weather, and then go back to tending the forest that is the town that is them.
