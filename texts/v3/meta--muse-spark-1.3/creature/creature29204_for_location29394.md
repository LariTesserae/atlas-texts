They live shoulder to shoulder. That is the first thing you have to understand about them, before anatomy, before number of legs or kind of teeth. There is no outside to them.

From a height — if you could get above the glare and the steam-fog — you would not see animals on a landscape. You would see the landscape *is* animals. A living pavement stretching to where the white eats the distance. Green-black, steaming, faintly heaving. A tissue.

Come down closer and the tissue resolves into bodies, but only barely. Your eye keeps failing.

The ones that make up most of it are low and immense. Not tall — wide. Barrels set almost to the ground on six, sometimes eight, columnar legs that end not in hooves but in broad, splayed heat-pads. They have to be low. Up here the wind, when it comes, would take anything tall. The cold lives an inch off the stone. So they keep their furnace-bellies close to it, trapping a tunnel of warm air underneath themselves.

They are iron-grey under everything else, chitin plated like a woodlouse, overlapping shoulder plates grooved deep enough to hold soil. And in those grooves, life has moved in. That is why you thought at first they were hummocks. Every adult back carries a complete meadow: cushion moss olive and rust and acid-green sunk ankle-deep, black rosette lichens opened flat to the killing sun, low ferns grown sideways, interwoven until you cannot find the shell underneath without parting them with your hands. When the animal breathes — and breathing is the loudest thing about them — steam beads on its own fur-filaments and freezes into lace, and tiny white birds that are not quite birds walk that lace, picking ice to drink.

They are hot. Stand three steps away in air below freezing and you feel it coming off them in waves. Their sides move like a bellows. You can see the piston-ribs flex: *in* — a whistling roar through broad nostrils as thin blue air is dragged in — hold, heat — *out* in sideways jets from gill-slits along the flank. Each exhalation ices the moss for a yard around. That is where the damp comes from. That is where the little black mirror-pools come from. Ten million breaths a minute, condensing and falling and never evaporating. They are making their own weather.

Their mouths never stop. They crop low, grinding moss and lichen and fern and, deliberately, mouthfuls of sharp basalt grit. You can hear it twenty yards off: millstones. Their teeth are not teeth. They are dark, self-sharpening metal ridges, upper against lower, honed by the very stone they chew for ballast. Behind them a furnace gut burns so hot the snow never settles on their backs. With each slow step forward they vent along the spine — a line of sphincter-vents opening on a ten-second cycle. Puff. Puff. Puff. Sulfur and hot oil and cut grass. You smell them before you see any one individual clearly.

They are not individuals, not strictly.

Watch long enough and you see the bridges. Between two shoulder plates standing too close for too long, the chitin softens. Flesh meets. Moss grows over the join. A tube thick as your arm, pulsing with blood and hot oil, linking one furnace to the next. Then three in a line. Then seven. Put your hand on the seventh and you feel the heartbeat coming down the line from far away, enormous and slow, rippling through all of them. They share heat that way. They share half-digested slurry that way. Old ones at the front of the slow migration have grandchildren in the tissue at the rear, still connected by living causeway.

Over their backs step the others — the tall ones. You hear their click before you see them. On stilt legs jointed the wrong way, bodies held high to stay in the unfiltered sun, they pick their way across the moving pavement with glacial care. A step started now finishes in a minute. Each carries on its carapace a whorl of black vanes, set like a windmill flat to the sky. The wind catches it. It spins, clicking in a ratchet, and the friction makes warmth, and the spinning makes static — a faint blue crackle you feel lift the hairs on your arms when one passes. They hum with it. The black sun-lichens on the grazers below actually turn to follow them as they go, tracking the electric field, not the sun. Nothing here wastes energy, not even stray charge.

And sunk between the grazers, almost immobile, are the domes. The processors. Huge, blind, mouths held permanently open. The work around them is the slowest work of all: a bucket-line of mouth-to-mouth regurgitation. A grazer chews, swallows, ferments in its furnace, brings it back up steaming, leans an inch to the left and empties into the next mouth, who holds it for an hour and passes it on. It can take a day to move a mouthful ten feet toward a dome. Inside you hear hydraulic churning, a wet industrial crushing that shakes the ground. What comes out the other side is extruded in long black steaming ropes — composted heat, instantly colonized. Bright green shoots are already waiting in spore form in the air and unfurl on contact. Nothing is built, ever, no tool, no wall, no cut mark — and yet everything is milled, pressed, vented, conveyed.

There is no empty space left for anything else, so everything else lives *in* the gaps they allow.

Under the bellies, in the warm tunnels, run the runners. Dog-sized, furnace-hot to the touch, fast only by comparison — which is still deliberate. They dart leg to leg, licking condensation off chitin, nipping parasites from skin folds, cheeks bulging with seeds they carry from back-meadow to back-meadow. Between legs, in the black mirror-pools of breath-water, things with gills and iron jaws churn and snap. On the backs, the ice-pickers ride.

Above, the air is also packed. Kite-things by the thousand, wingtip to wingtip. Translucent wings stretched tight as drumheads, never flapping because they don't need to — the whole herd exhales a permanent thermal. They hang there in the glare, jostling, filtering steam for insects and spores, and when they do adjust, all together, their wings snap like canvas sails. The crack rolls across the sky and fades into the bass drone.

That drone never stops. Breath and grind and vent and wing and tick of contracting basalt. You feel it in your sternum. And it is slow. Everything is slow. Chewing is slow. Digestion is slow. Thought, if there is thought, is slow as geology.

Stand still long enough and they budget you in. Warmth is currency here, and you are warm. A runner will detour to press its flank hard against your calf for a moment, stealing it. Condensation will bead on your coat. A spore that has been hanging lit in the noon light — light so bright with ice and dust it casts shadows upward — will settle in your shoulder seam and, within the hour, unfurl a bright-green shoot toward the sun. The grazer beside you will shift an inch, sighing moss, to close the gap your body made.

Because gaps are not allowed. Shoulder to shoulder, flank to flank, back to back, steaming in the bright cold, pistons pumping, mills grinding, vanes clicking, domes churning — a factory with no building, no owner, no product except more packed, breathing life.
