It would come quietly, because that is the only way to come here.

Not down in light, not with a voice. It would thin itself to fit the thin air. If it has wings it would felt them. If it has feet it would splay them. It would learn, first, how to step without sinking.

For a long time it would do nothing that looks like doing. It would sit on a peat dome a hand's breadth rising and falling and learn to rise and fall with it. It would let the ground-cloud come up to its knees. It would let water sigh up around it. It would collect beads on its sleeves like everyone else here does.

It has seen many places. It knows the temptation - the kind, cruel temptation - to improve. To drain a little, to warm a little, to make straight. To give the grazers better grass. To spare the old grazer from the wolves lying down with jaws to throat. To keep the moth from the spider's pearl-string. To lift the blind catfish up to see. To teach the ibis to sing louder, to give the cutters a day off.

It will not do any of that.

Because it is wise, it sees quickly what you saw slowly: that no one lives here the way living is usually meant, because *living here is the place.* The being that lives here is not the grazer or the wolf or the moth or the rosette frog. It is the packing. The fitting. The slow cold wet agreement to occupy everything and waste nothing and hurry never.

To pull one thread kind would be to unweave all kindness.

To be benevolent here is not to rescue bodies from each other. The carcass becoming a green hill is benevolence already, perfected over ten thousand years of wet. The dung boiling away into beetles in minutes is mercy at scale. The mist that swallows a cry is mercy. What could a power add to that, except its own restraint?

So this is what it would do, if anything:

It would pay attention. Long, unhurried, unusing attention. The kind that is itself a form of water.

It would notice the particular old bull gone grey-green with lichen on his shoulders, and not scrape him clean. It would notice the particular amber spider repairing her yard of fog-net head-down, and not feed her. It would notice the particular frog the size of a thumbnail who will live and die in one rosette cistern without ever touching ground, and it would memorize the shape of that cistern like a name.

It would not be lonely for worship. The rosettes tip and pour without thanks. The mires sigh twice a day without praise. This does not offend it. It is relieved, actually. At last, a place that does not ask to be saved.

What work it does, it does at the edges, where the roof breaks off and the world below is hot and hurried and hungry.

You will not see it work. That is the point.

When men come with maps and concessions below, with talk of draining, mining the meter-thick, ten-meter-thick sponge for fuel, of cutting roads for the heavy machines that cannot learn to splay their feet - it will make the maps blur. It will make the cost calculations come out, year after year, just barely unprofitable. It will put a small, persistent doubt in the surveyor's chest as his boots fill with cold seep: *there is nothing up there to take.* It will thicken the cloud sea on the days they try to fly over. It will let them shout, experimentally, and get back only damp exhalation from a different direction, and feel, unaccountably, foolish and intrusive, and turn back kindly.

When the hot world creeps higher, as it is creeping, it will linger a little longer over the eastern swells each afternoon. It will comb, with the rosettes, a little more droplet out of the passing veil. It will hold the cool like a condition, as the plants do. Not forever - even powers cannot hold back forever what the lowlands choose - but longer. A millimeter a year and a continent wide is still a speed. It will buy time in the only currency this place accepts: wet.

It will not speak. If it spoke, even softly, sound would have to wade, and come back late. Instead it will hum below hearing, in the sternum, with the pumps and wings and water moving through narrow places. It will say, in that hum: *continue.*

And once, perhaps, in pearl-evening when the tarns begin their blue nerve-flicker and ten thousand ibis adjust on touching nests and the grazer line beds down with a collective sigh, it will bend very low - lower than a god usually bends - and let its breath be occupied for a moment by gnats and seed-down and droplets that haven't decided if they are cloud or rain.

It will let the moss lean back over its footprint within the hour.

It will leave no cairn, no fire-ring, no straight line. No proof it was ever there, except that the water never leaves, only changes rooms, and there is, for another season, still enough cool, still enough cloud, still enough quiet for the fullness to go on fitting.

That is what benevolence looks like when it is wise enough to have learned slowness: not a hand laid on, but a weight distributed. Not a roof built, but the Roof kept.

It stays until morning, when the black earth exposed by last night's *whump* is already green at the edges, and it is glad - in the quiet, non-hurrying way of this place - to have done almost nothing at all, perfectly.
