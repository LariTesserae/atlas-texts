It would come the way everything real comes here:

low, and sideways, and wet.

It would not arrive. There would be no light from heaven, no parting of the curtain, no stilling of the roar. A benevolent and wise power knows better than to announce itself in a place that has evicted announcement.

It would have heard the rumor from a hundred paces off, felt the pressure from fifty, let it get inside its ribs from ten. It would push through the alder screen and duck through the gap where the fall thins against the left wall, where the rock shoulders out. It would let the cold water slap its hood and find its neck at once.

It would obey. Because it is wise.

Stay low and stay back. So it would stay low. It would stoop under the black soapstone ceiling. Stay back from the curtain that pulls.

Do not expect to be dry. So it would not expect to be dry. It would let the backdraft push the fine icy mist into it until it beaded and darkened and gained weight. It would let the stone sweat onto it.

Do not expect silence to return. So it would not try to make silence. It would not turn down the drum. It would press no palms over ears, for it has no ears that need saving. It would let the low white thunder come up through sternum and teeth, and let its own hearing turn inward to blood and breath, as yours does.

Do not move quickly. So it would not move quickly. It is the only visitor who would not feel absurdly fast here. It can do a millimeter a year without effort.

That is the first thing it would do: nothing that breaks the rules.

The second thing is harder to see, because you look for hands.

It would sit. Not on a throne. In the gravel, inside the low C of river slabs on the right where the drip is least, opposite where She sits. It would make itself the right size to fit in a room twelve strides wide and three deep.

She would not startle. She does not startle for wrens or drips or gusts. She would feel, through the rock, a new weight of attention — the way the cave spiders feel thunder through their feet — and she would go on being deaf in her complete way, eyes closed against the boiled green-grey pulse.

And the Power would look at her properly. Which is to say, not as a problem.

It would not warm her. To warm her would be to kill the moss in the folds of her elbows, to wake the bats before April to starvation, to unmake the stored cold she has chosen. It would not heal her deafness. Her deafness *is* her hearing; she is the roar turned inward. To give her ears would evict her from her own home. It would not open her eyes that never fully adjust. What use are eyes, here? She reads the river thickening and thinning overhead by light on her lids. That is sight enough.

It would not take her away. That is what a lesser kindness would do — rescue the cold girl from the cold room. But wisdom has stayed longer than a minute. It knows she is not trapped. She is accreted. Charcoal fleck and spit and breath-hang and hand-oil on sandstone, and wren-shit and bat-shit and moss creeping over it all, remembering the shape of hands. She keeps the room held open. She keeps the fire-pit cold. She adjusts the cairn a minute per decade when frost tries to topple it. She rots the offering at the correct speed. If you removed her, the hollow would still be hollow, but it would no longer be *held*.

So the Power would not improve her. It would *keep* her.

You have to understand what keeping means to something old and slow. It does not mean praise. She would not hear praise anyway.

It would mean: for one hour that is not an hour, It breathes with her. She breathes once an hour, with the gust that thins the curtain. It breathes with her. When the five furred pebbles high in the basalt slit breathe once a minute, It counts those breaths and does not miss one. When the wren darts in with a grub, It does not follow her with Its eyes and frighten her rhythm; It holds still, trout-still, so she can do the fastest thing in her own time.

When the gust comes and real grey forest light spears straight through and the mosses flare emerald and the droplets hang like glass, It does what She does: lifts Its face, if It has a face, and lets it hit. For a second.

That is all, inside. Attention so complete it becomes indistinguishable from love. The moss will grow its millimeter that year without knowing why, but swollen a little fuller with spray.

If It does anything else, It will do it *outside*, where the hollow can be killed without ever touching the hollow.

For a wise power knows this small cold green room exists only because the water allows it. And the water allows it only because a whole unglamorous world upstream allows the water: the flat bog that sponges the rain, the alders that hold the banks, the clean snowmelt, the absence of a road, the obscurity that lets you miss it if you blink.

So outside, It will be busy in ways you will never photograph.

It will keep the bog boggy. It will keep the river undiverted. When men in a warm room far away unroll a map and draw a line for a culvert or a quarry or a tidy hydro intake that would take just the top foot off the ledge — which would be enough to thin the curtain to a damp trickle and starve the luminous algal zone in one season — It will put a small confusion in their pencil, a tiredness, a sudden memory of another site, a cost overrun, a protected newt. Nothing miraculous. Just enough friction for slowness to win.

It will keep it hard to find. Not cursed, just uninteresting on paper. It will let the alder screen grow thick. It will let the path stay boggy and fly-bitten. It will let the people who need to leave an offering — leather strip, carved dipper, flint flake, fingertip in tally lines — find it, and let the people who need to carve their names not bother to push through.

It will keep the winters cold enough for torpor, the summers cool enough for condensation. As the world warms, It will linger a little longer in the stored cold of the stone, like a hand on a forehead.

And then, because It is benevolent and cannot bear to leave without a gift, and because It is wise and knows gifts can be violence, It will leave the only gift that does not disturb weight or damp or noise:

It will let Herself be known.

Not seen — She is in no hurry to be seen. Known. For ten thousand years She has kept without witness, adjusting stone, accepting rot, listening inward. For one gust-length, she will feel, in sternum and teeth where she feels everything, a second stillness answering her stillness. Not the trout's outward waiting. Not the bat's stored waiting. A waiting *with*.

*I see the work*, that stillness will say, without words because words would be lost to water anyway. *The C held. The cairn balanced. The wren unstartled. Good keeping.*

She will settle back into the humming dark with the smell of wet stone. The curtain will close. The roar will take it all back.

Nothing will have changed. The fire-pit will still be cold through. The moss will still be black-green at the back. Your boots, when you finally go back through the curtain, will still be soaked and heavier.

Only this: the room will be, for a little while, not just held, but *held in mind*. And for a being made of attention and accretion, to be held in mind by a larger mind is not a small thing. It is the difference between enduring and belonging.

The Power will leave the way it came — sideways, ducking, neck wet. It will leave no footprint in the scoured cobble that the next flood won't erase. It will add no eighth stone to the cairn. The Keeper prefers seven.

And the fall will go on throwing itself over, twice your height, forever, as long as the spray comes.
