It would come down on foot.

Not because it has to. Because that is how you enter this place without breaking it. Down off the rim road by steps, letting the air get thicker, warmer on the skin, weighted. Letting the dim adjust you. A benevolent and wise power would know first to be a visitor. To be adjusted.

It would not lift the veil.

That is the first thing to understand about it. It could — a hand that vast could peel back membrane, let true noon fall in, make the green go sun-green and fast, make shadows all point one way. It doesn't. It sees why the hand is there. The plain was burning once. The seed tray needs its cover. The people have grown eyes for brown-gold, skin for damp that never dries, lungs for compost and ozone. To give them sun would be to unmake them out of kindness. It will not do that.

It would not fill the emptiness, either. It would stand in the middle of one of those vast kept emptinesses between towers — stone still sharp underfoot, glittering black where moss hasn't taken it — and understand that the distance is manners. A half kilometer between towers so the eye can rest. Hours alone in the middle of a city and that aloneness is not loneliness. It would not bring crowds. It would not print new towers in the hollows. It would leave the hollows hollow.

What it would do is quieter. Low, breath-first, the way you have to speak here because the walls eat sound.

First, it would learn the rules and keep them. Stay on the pale paths when the towers hum. That low chord rising up through the feet, through the teeth, and then the warm heavy rain that can knock you sideways. It would step aside with the others, shake off like a large animal afterward, wring out its clothes. It would not pick the white flowers. It would cup a hand under a sail-leaf and let the condensation run off without tearing. If a transit thread won't take it — that faint heat-shimmer line that tastes your destination — it wouldn't insist. It would bow a little, the way the children are taught: hold it lightly. If the tower is dreaming, let it dream. End up two blocks sideways, damp and disoriented, and smile about being taught.

It would carry water.

Everyone here carries a little water. Dipping from a leaf-axil, tipping a gourd into the corner basins as they pass, because the buildings drink through capillary stone and an empty basin cracks by morning like a mouth. A wise power would see that and do the simplest divine thing imaginable: it would help water the houses.

Unseen, mostly. Before dawn, when the blind moths are still bumping soft against the floating lamps, it would go from basin to basin with its hands full of pocket-water, fill each to the exact lip, clear, and move on. So that one morning a woman on her way to the tower gardens finds all the basins full and frowns, touches the water, looks around at the empty plaza, and for a second doesn't have to worry. The crack doesn't come. The wall stays smooth. Someone helped.

Second, it would take a night of witnessing.

This is the secret burden here: the upper floors are only real when witnessed. That smoky translucent mass goes to mist when you step away. So the people sleep high up, breathe to hold the rooms held, leave a hand a moment longer on a wall, rest eyes on a corner while waiting for rain. They are the suns the green orients around, and the anchors the stone orients around. They never fully rest. Even asleep they are holding.

A benevolent power knows how to be real. So for one night — maybe for three, spaced across the basin so no one notices a pattern — it would go up into the towers and do the holding itself. Lie along the curve of an unwitnessed room and breathe slowly until the walls go solid and stay solid. Look steadily at the corners. Lend attention the way they lend it, only larger, steadier. And the residents that night would sleep without dreaming of holding. Deeply, damply, unwatched for once. A child would turn over in her sleep and not wake. An old one would snore. In the morning they'd say the air felt different, rested, and not know why the building felt kinder, more itself. They'd put their hand to it and find it warm.

Third, it would answer the glider.

High up near the veil something goes tower to tower on long silent wings — animal or feral drone, no one will settle it — and once every few weeks it calls, a falling glass note, and everyone pauses mid-step. Not fear. Recognition. Another resident, rarer than they are.

It is lonely, that thing. Rarest of all. A wise power would hear that the way you hear a single footstep echo a little too long in the green.

So when it calls next, the power would answer. Not loud. Not from above in thunder. From very near, from just under the veil where the condensation beads, in the same falling glass tone, a half-step lower, so it sounds like harmony, not echo. Just once.

The glider would bank, silent, and call again. The power would answer again, softer. And for a minute there would be two of them up there in the dim, conversing. Everyone below would stop, faces lifted, listening to something they have never heard: the lonely one not alone. Then it would let it glide on. It wouldn't tame it. Wouldn't bring it down to show the children. Just let it know it was heard.

And then the smallest things, because wisdom is mostly in scale.

It would not bring back the animals all at once — no flocks to fill the chatter-absence. The valley is young, the cuts are still sharp, the negotiation is still active. To flood it would be to end the conversation. Instead it would leave a gift in the leaf-axils and the white waxy flowers: a midge, a true pollinator for the dim. Tiny, pale gold, loving low light, living three days only, humming too high for human ears but perfect for moth-ears. Enough that the people don't have to do all the pollinating with dusted hands and brushed shoulders, but not enough to replace them. So a gardener one evening finds white flowers already dusted, glowing a little brighter, and laughs, and the blind moths bump gently against her skin and move on, fed elsewhere.

It would sit with the woman in the brown-gold hour.

She is there every evening, standing alone between towers, shoulders damp, breathing audible, sail-leaves turning one by one toward the sound of her. Not lost. Not waiting. Just being the warm thing the valley orients around.

The power would stand near — not too near — and let itself be warm, too. Two suns that walk, for a moment. The sail-leaves would hesitate, pivot slowly between them, uncertain. Then the power would cool itself very slightly, dim its own heat, breathe shallower, so the leaves settle back to her. Choose her. It would let her be the chosen sun. It would just keep her company in being chosen, so she is not the only warm thing for a little while. It wouldn't speak. It would listen to drip, to condensation ticking from the veil-nets a hundred meters up onto broad leaves, to breath. When the towers tune and the hum rises through stone, it would nod toward the pale path, and they'd walk to shelter together without words, and stand under the overhang while the warm rain sheeted down and the channels ran fast for ten minutes, and watch the puddles mirror amber.

Before it left, it would leave one eye-opening kinder.

The veil sometimes thins to a hundred-meter eye and brutal white-gold falls through for a minute — the sail-leaves recoil, moss steams, people blink up shocked and grateful. It has to happen; young weather needs to test. The power wouldn't stop it. But it would lay a hand, very lightly, on the rim of that eye as it passes, filtering the edge just a fraction, so the light is still true daylight, still shocking, but not scorching. So a child caught in it laughs instead of crying. So the moss doesn't burn, only steams.

That is all.

No lifting. No filling. No fixing the shadows that point different ways, no hurrying the moss to decide what is ground and what is wall, no rounding the sharp glass edges of the drainage channels before their time. Erosion hasn't forgiven anything yet, and forgiveness takes time. A wise power knows not to forgive on behalf of stone.

It would come down on foot, get damp at the shoulders and hairline, leave water for the walls, hold the rooms for a night, answer the lonely call, bring midges, sit in the dim without insisting on being the sun — and then go back up the rim road, leaving footprints that the velvet moss would take by morning, softly, deciding.

And the valley would go on negotiating, only slightly less alone. Which is what benevolence is, here: not to be the eye that opens, but to be another warm body that turns, for a moment, toward breath.
