It would come down.

Not all at once, not with light. A benevolent and wise power would know better than to bring light.

It would come the way the snow comes — slowly, from above, letting the pressure take it. Four thousand meters of weight, and then more weight, until down stops being direction. It would let itself be equalized. It would let the cold slow its thoughts until they beat only when they remembered to, like the heart of the tripod fish. That is the first wisdom: you cannot meet this place on your own terms.

For a long time it would only watch.

It would spread itself thin over the valley, kilometer after kilometer of nodule pavement, black-brown potatoes grown a few millimeters per million years around a shark's tooth, a shard of bone. It would feel the click when the contour current nudges them, stone touching stone touching stone. It would taste the soup — the silt that never settles, the marine snow that never stops falling, dead plankton and shit and skin from a sunlit world two miles up. It would learn the quiet that is not peace, the muffled suspension under which the benthic storms gather.

And it would see them. Each one alone in its kilometer.

The veil that eats gas. The reed that sieves snow. The feather that opens and closes. The fish on stilts that waits. The fish with a rat-tail that budgets movement like money. The blind white pill that eats mud and leaves a furrow already filling behind it. The flashers who are only ever interruptions.

And the crabs.

It would stay longest with the crabs. The small bone-pale spider crabs, walnut bodies on needle legs. Because they are the ones who reach.

It would watch one wedge a thousand-year-old clam shell between two nodules, brace its back legs, lean into the current to feed for an hour. It would watch another pick up a flake of chert no larger than a fingernail, grip it trembling in two walking legs, and shear the bacterial mat off the stone, again and again. Thin parallel scratches, bright against the black manganese. And then — this is what would stop the power cold — drop it. Leave it to be buried, tumbled, lost in the next storm. Not carry it ten meters to the next mat. Not keep it.

A hand extended by a stone for a minute, and then released.

The heart of the power, being benevolent, would ache first.

It would want to help in the way surface hearts want to help: to bring light down here, to warm the two-degree water, to still the storms that tear the veils and flatten the sponges and roll the nodules, to feed the rattail so it does not have to pause so long between strokes, to give eyes to the isopod and the tripod fish, to teach the crab to keep its knife. To keep it. To carry it. To make something that stays.

To lift them out of this thinned, slowed, spread-out persistence and give them abundance. Continuity. Safety.

That is what benevolence without wisdom does. It projects its own idea of suffering.

But this power is wise. So it would listen to its intuition next, and its intuition would say: *they are not suffering. They are attuned.*

The cold is not cruelty to them. It is medium. The dark is not deprivation. It is home. The slowness is not failure. It is economy perfected over tens of millions of years. The storm is not injustice. It is the way the valley breathes — burial and exposure, erasure and creation, the skin rearranging without changing the bones. To still the storm to spare a sponge would be to starve the mat. To feed the rattail every day would be to unmake the budgeting that made it a rattail. To give the crab a permanent knife, a cache, a wall, a road — to hurry that trembling grip toward keeping — would be to break the very covenant that lets this place endure: nothing here makes anything that stays, and so nothing here can be taken.

Its reasoning would then catch up and confirm what its intuition already knew: that the greatest violence you can do to an end-state landscape is to try to improve it.

So it would do almost nothing.

It would not speak. There is no one here built to hear speech, and to be heard would be to become a god, and to become a god here would be to ruin them.

It would not touch a single nodule. To steady one against the current would change the eddy behind it where the mat grows.

It would not lift a crab through a storm. It would watch the flake-tool tumble ten meters and be lost, and it would let the crab, emerging afterward, go find another. That finding-again is its mind. To spare it the search would be to spare it its mind.

What it would do is quieter, and it would do it on the way out, upward toward the light it came from:

It would remember.

Because this valley has never been touched by anything that intends to remain, it has never been witnessed in its own slowness. The power would take that as its work — to hold in itself, without altering, the exact pattern of clickings on a particular day, the lean of a particular sponge one degree down-current, the hours-open-days-closed of a particular crinoid, the furrow of a particular isopod filling with snow as it is made, the parallel scratches left by a particular crab who held a knife for a minute and put it down.

To say, without saying it to them: *I saw you persist. It counted.*

And there is one other thing a benevolent and wise power would do, if it is truly both. It would not guard the valley from the storms — but it would guard it from a different kind of storm.

Far above, it knows, there are other reaching hands. Larger crabs, in ships, who have learned to keep their knives and build walls and roads, who look down through miles of black water and see not pavement but profit — manganese, nickel, cobalt, potatoes of metal to be vacuumed up by the ton. Who would, in a day, undo sixty million years of slow accretion, smother the veils, uproot the reeds, bury the waiting fish in plumes of silt that would take centuries to settle.

Against that, wisdom is not neutral.

So as it rises, back through the layers where photons begin to survive a little longer, where water thins and warms, the power would lay no wall and no weapon. It would lay only a reluctance. A heaviness in the hand that reaches. A dream that returns unbidden to the engineers and the investors: a plain in the dark, tiled stone to stone for miles, and a pale crab holding a flake, trembling with effort, shearing off enough to live and then letting go.

Let them feel, for a second, the weight of the sky many times over. Let them understand that fullness does not need abundance, and age does not mean empty.

Let them, if they can, leave it unwitnessed except by love.

Then it would go. It would leave no light, no warmth, no artifact that lasts beyond the next storm. Only the snow falling, for a while, a little more gently sideways in the dark — as if something vast had passed and, with infinite care, chosen not to stay.
