It does not come with rain.

That is the first wisdom, and the hardest. Any lesser god, any tourist god with pity in its mouth, would look down into the cut and see cracked lips and repurposed water tanks and think: *these poor dry things, let me save them.* Let me open the sky. Let me send a river down the raw walls.

It knows better. It has looked long enough to understand the rule.

Nothing here cycles water. Everything cycles dryness.

To bring water here would be to murder. The cork-floor would rot. The milky sap would thin and sour. The veil-moths would drown in their vents. The leaf-salt would wash off and the leaves would go soft and stupid. The mat that learned to drink light and stone-dust and smoke would choke. Salvation would be a flood.

So it leaves the clouds closed. It comes dry.

It comes down the way you do. Not dropped from above — down like attention goes. It narrows itself to fit the slot of white sky. At noon, when the sun is lowered straight into the box like a lamp, it comes down that shaft and settles with the cool air that lives at the bottom. No one sees it arrive because it does not displace anything. The dust still hangs where the last cut fell. The clatter of sage-grey leaves does not pause.

For a while it does nothing at all. That is the second wisdom.

A wise power knows an embryonic place when it sees one. This pit is still deciding what shape it will be. The walls are still sharp with drill spirals and blast flowers. The talus hasn't piled. The town is sparse on purpose, three lit doorways half a kilometer apart, knots in wood with a hundred meters of resonant bark-ground between them. It is unfinished and content with the arrangement.

You do not finish it for them.

So for a season the power just lives in the interval. It learns to inhabit sparseness without trying to fill it.

It sits in the hollow runners you can crawl through for an hour. It listens to how sound dies here — how the living wood drinks clangor, how a lathe three stems over arrives through a pillow, how a name called two clearings away arrives as clicks and edges. It learns not to shout. Nobody shouts here if they can help it. The plant swallows half the vowels anyway. The power speaks consonant-short, the way the dust-fox yips.

It lets itself be tasted. The white rootlets that stitch across a fresh rock-slide within a week, secreting acid strong enough to taste metal in the air — they etch toward it, curious, and find no water in it either. Only mineral and coolness and patience. Good, says the mat without words. You are not wet. You may stay.

Because that is who it came for, really. Not just the town. The one who is the town.

The mat itself, thick as houses, pale woody flesh veined with black, layered like phyllo, holding the flat of the pit wall to wall. The people live *in*, not *on* — doors cut in fluted stems twenty meters tall that healed thick and black around steel frames, flues sealed airtight through pith, freight racks bolted into fiber and grown over like knuckles. The mat adjusts at night, nosing a runner across a walkway, thickening a branch so a catwalk hangs crooked. The day crew saws it back by noon, clean, without cursing, stacking the lengths for the tappers. Games of push that both sides enjoy.

Is it aware? The boilerman will not use that word. He will lay his palm a second on the pod-lip as he ducks through — feeling for morning growth, for the almost-imperceptible creak of heat expansion — and he will know which stem runs hot and which will pinch your door if you don't plane it. The child will tell you which hollow runner hums when you sing into it.

The power knows that slow, distributed, cork-sealed mind. It has met ocean minds and forest minds and city minds. This one is younger. Drier. Just learning how big it can get without water.

It does not speak to it in sentences. It speaks in its own language: vibration and smoke.

When the stamping presses thump on their slow rhythm, and the ripe pods have learned to pop open on that frequency — the factories pollinating the forest — the power joins for a bar or two. A low sympathetic thump in the bark-ground underfoot, felt more than heard. The pods open a little wider. A downy seed lifts on hot air.

When the thin dry smoke rises straight up from the squat brick chimneys through the branch-forks, and the papery leaves lean toward it wanting it, the power steadies the updraft for a moment so more seed falls back instead of being carried out over the rim to nowhere on the plateau to die. Not much. Just enough.

That is all it does for the body. Small tending. Shop manners. You do not kick the walls you live in when the walls are alive; you lay a palm on the jamb.

For the people it does even less, and it matters more.

It walks the pale path with the tappers at dawn while breath still shows, while they bleed the slow milky sap never too much from one trunk. It does not lighten the auger. It just keeps the cold a little longer so their foreheads stay matte and easy.

It stands beside the woman sixty feet up oiling a line shaft while veil-moths big as your hand crowd the exhaust. She lifts two fingers to a stranger far below. I see you that far away. The power lifts two fingers back, though she cannot see it. To be seen that far away and not crowded closer — that is the blessing of sparse people.

It runs, briefly, child-high, with the message-runners along the root-ribs. Lean, cork-soled, salt-dusted children who know which runners are chewer-nests and which cut through to the machine shop, who race hopper herds for soundless laughing. Wire crackles and dies in the acid air past a hundred meters, so they are the wires. The power does not give them radios. It gives them good memory for turns, and lungs that like the resin and hot iron air, and the exact pitch to hum to make a hollow runner sing back.

It sits with the old-timer with a wad of root-shaving in his cheek, that chemical coolness that fools the mouth into thinking wet, and spits grey beside him companionably.

It does not heal the cracked lips etched into seams over years. Those seams are seals. They keep the moisture inside.

At dusk it climbs — because they let it — to a crown deck to watch with the grey ones. Those desiccated almost-monkeys with huge eyes against the glare, prying pods with stolen crowbars, scattering seed. The shop boys will steal the crowbars back next week, bent and tooth-marked. Both sides content. The power watches the trade and finds it good.

And then the cool blue shadow fills the cut from the bottom up. Then the warm windows light on one by one, far apart in the green-grey mass, sparks in tinder that refuses to burn. Smoke rises straight in windless cold. Leaf-clatter settles. A press thumps, pillow-soft. Someone calls a name and the plant takes half the vowels.

The power counts the lights. It does not add one more. It does not wish them denser, louder, wetter, more saved.

If you asked, afterward, what did it do — the answer would be: almost nothing.

It kept the plateau boring to outsiders for a little longer. Made the wind comb the cracked clay a little flatter, made the rim look from above like any other raw break, nothing worth a second drill. Gave the unfinished time to stay unfinished.

It let itself be lived in for a while, the way they live in the mat, without kicking the walls.

It paid attention the way cool air settles and stays. And in a place where everything is absorption — where cork and husk and dry leaf take the edge off every engine — attention is not passive. To be truly perceived, dry as you are, sparse as you are, without being pitied or fixed, is a kind of water that does not kill.

Before it goes, it lays — if it has a palm — a second on the jamb of the world, feeling for morning growth.

And the mat, in its slow milky way, heals a little thicker around the touch.
