It does not come with light.

That is the first mercy. Anything else powerful would have torn the cloud open to see better. Would have brought a sun with it like a lamp held over an anthill.

This one learns how to arrive in the dark.

It comes up the basalt stair the way you did, on foot, though it has no feet. It stops at the rim where the air gets warmer as you climb. It listens. For a long time that is all it does.

*chuff — chuff — chuff — pause — chuff.*

Far out. Then nearer. Then the sympathetic *tong* of the drum-rock under its weight. Then the *tink* of a cooling joint. Then the paper rasp of wiregrass. Then, underneath it all, the hum.

Any wise thing knows: you do not touch a humming thing until you know what key it is in.

So it lies down. If a power can be said to lie down, it does — spreads itself thin over warm basalt that still holds furnace-heat though there was no day, puts an ear that is not an ear to stone that is a drum, and learns interruption. Nearer black of rock. Farther black of air. Moving black of herds.

It feels the whole plain resonating, miles of old lava tubes carrying piston steps like blood through veins.

It understands, very quickly, what this place is:

A bargain kept.

No sun, so the beasts became furnaces. No rivers, so the horns became cold enough to make water out of air. No soil that gives up its phosphorus, so guts learned to smelt at a thousand degrees. No light to hunt by, so jaws learned to see heat. No factories, so bodies became boilers and crushers and dynamos, and kept the high country five or ten degrees warmer than it should be, for everyone.

Every lack answered not by complaint but by becoming.

The power feels the temptation that all benevolent things feel. Let me fix the dark. Let me lift the ceiling. Let me make grass to the belly instead of tufts fighting their own war. Let me straighten the old female's leg.

Because she is coming now, out of the dark, and it cannot help but notice her first.

The herd strings past — bull first, huge, ribs cool at the spine and warm at the belly, furnace orange brighten-dim-brighten-dim between the slats, horns running with dew in two bright lines to his mouth. Cows. Calves glowing dimmer, rust-red ribs still soft. And last, trailing, limping:

*chuff — ...chuff-chuff.*

Her left fore-piston misfires. With each step steam jets too much sideways from the slit behind the knee. She is working harder to walk slower to stay with them. The wire-jackals know her rhythm better than she does. They are trotting a respectful half-mile off, tall ears dripping, waiting. Not cruel. Attending.

The power could heal her with a thought. Close the seal. Reseat the valve. Make her young.

It does not.

Wisdom has looked longer than pity has. It has seen what happens one dark cycle after a kill: the long sigh as six hundred kilos of steam lets go, the whiteout of warm fog, the clatter of cinder beetles claiming slag from the gut to roll away, the jackals circling, and by next dark only the iron ribs ticking as they cool, and black marbles scattered, and — for a week — a startling green flush in a perfect body-outline where blood and condensate soaked in. Wiregrass seedlings where nothing else would grow.

Death here is not waste. It is sowing. To take away her dying would be to take away that green outline. To take away the hushers would be to take away hunger that keeps herds moving a mile a day and not returning for a year, which keeps the sparse green from being eaten to nothing. To take away dark would be to take away horn-lightning, and the spark-moths that flock to a sun that lasts a tenth of a second, and the sifters that ride heat and strain them by the thousand.

You cannot save one thread without unraveling the weave.

So what does a benevolent and wise power do, when doing nothing feels like abandonment and doing something feels like ruin?

It does the third thing.

It walks with her.

Unseen, unheard over the hush, it paces itself to *chuff — ...chuff-chuff*. It does not mask the sound from the jackals. It does not cool her fever. It simply stays close enough that the heat-map she makes on the cold air is not a lonely shape. When she pauses to crop wiregrass and deliberately scoops red dust, it pauses. When she drinks the drip off her own cold horns without lifting her head, it is glad for the water.

And when, two days later in a dry wash, the scoria-colored stillness under her belly uncoils impossibly fast — heat-pits having read her for two days, white ceramic fangs finding the glowing slit between iron ribs to short the heart — the power is there.

There is no roar. There is the long sigh. A whiteout of warm fog that soaks everything twenty feet away.

Most die alone in fog. She does not.

What it does then is not a miracle. Miracles are loud. This is small enough to fit in the rules of this place:

One — Nothing stays. So it does not stay her death. It lets the steam go.

Two — Quiet is not empty. So it fills the quiet, not with voice, but with attention. Every beetle arrival noted. Every moth. Every tick of her ribs as they cool from orange to cherry to black.

Three — Cold is a tool. So it is the cold horn for a moment, for the calf that stayed too close and is crying steam. Condensation beads a little faster on the mother's abandoned horns nearby. Enough for one drink.

Four — Everything hums. So it hums back, low, in the frequency of drum-rock, so the plain's song does not miss a note where she dropped out.

By next dark cycle the jackals have eaten, the beetles have laid eggs in dung, the sifter has passed overhead and never known her name. But where her blood soaked, the moss-things are already unfurling black-green velvet in the three-foot circle where the bull once breathed rain.

The power does one other thing before it leaves, and only one, on the scale of powers:

It keeps this place uninteresting to those who would love it wrongly.

To the miners dreaming of grown iron ribs, to the engineers dreaming of free boilers, to the missionaries of light dreaming of opening the sky — it lets the cloud stay ironed shut. It lets the maps show empty scoria and dust. It lets the stair seem longer than it is, the air closer than is comfortable, the dark a fraction too complete. It protects obscurity like a shell protects an egg.

Not as punishment. As blessing. Let no road be built here. Let no wire be strung to steal herd lightning. Let no hands ever stack a cairn.

Then it goes back down the stair, the way you will, tasting blood-iron dust in its teeth.

If you put your ear to the rock at deep dark on the night after, you will hear no difference. Piston steps miles away. Steam sighing through blisters. Water ticking off wiregrass onto stone. *chuff-chuff-chuff* come and go like breathing.

That is what it did, if anything:

It learned the hum, refused to fix it, accompanied one dying, and guarded the dark so the furnaces could keep walking, keeping each other warm.
