They would come quietly.

Not at three o'clock, when the ribs go dark and the air thickens and even light has to wade. A wise power knows not to arrive during delicate work. They would come in the morning, when the steam is still low over the seeps and the silver mites are dispersing and the spider's lines are strung with first water.

They would not come as weather. No shadow crossing the valley, no voice. If you were there — resting on the black where you are supposed to rest, drinking only from the running seep — you might feel only that the day was a half-degree kinder, that your sweat stayed on your skin a moment longer before the sun took it. You might look up and think a cirrus had passed.

The first thing they would do is nothing that looks like doing. They would sit on the lip, where the hawk circles but will not enter, and learn the rules with a body, the way you did.

Rest in the empty, not in the green.
Drink from the running, not the still.
Be quiet. The place is holding water up where water should not be.

A benevolent power can read those rules without being told. A wise one obeys them.

They would not cool the mountain. They would not make the crossing easy.

That is the temptation of every lesser kindness: to join the islands. To lay soil over the black glass between fern-clump and pitcher-clump so the gnats can walk, so the moss-mites can visit cousins, so no bee has to fly straight and burning over twenty meters of mirror at 50°C. To turn down the noon sun. To make the pool stay cool all day so the white frogs don't have to sink and wait with their throats pulsing.

They will not do it.

Because they see — the way you only start to see when you crouch — that the black is not empty. The black is the vow. It is what makes a glasswing bee a glasswing bee: that it can leave one damp world and, without stopping, without curving, arrive at another. It is what makes the pitcher gnat a species found nowhere else: that it never leaves its pitcher. Separation is not the wound here. Separation is the womb. In ten thousand other valleys life is general. Here, in a hundred and fifty paces, it is becoming particular, month by month. Take away the heat and you take away the becoming.

So they preserve the difficulty. They bless the heat.

What they do instead is smaller, and therefore immense.

They count.

Twelve frogs. Maybe twenty. No one has stayed still long enough in the heat to be sure. They stay still. They count nineteen, hanging in the cool deeper layer where the seep feeds the pool from below, pink copepods flicking like commas around their noses. They hold the number the way you hold a full pitcher without spilling. *Nineteen. You are known.*

They notice the one being no guidebook will ever list because it lives on a working thing. The felt of silver mites on the cold side of the millstone disc — the side no shadow explains. At noon they crowd shoulder to shoulder to drink the condensation that forms perpetually on that impossible gradient. The discs are old. The makers left the tap running, but taps stutter. The cold side flickers, a tenth of a degree, a hundredth. No human hand could feel it. The mites feel it as famine.

So at noon, the power puts a finger — not a finger — on the balance. Just steadies it. Just keeps the cold cold while the other side burns. No miracle the moss would notice. Only that the beads keep forming where they should form, and the mites drink.

At 14:50, when the ribs go dark at the edges and the interference colors die, when your shadow lags and the bees clamp under fern fronds with their hairs standing up from static, the power moves to the cairn near the eastern seep.

The weaver is working hardest then. Black lentil spider, black on black, reeling in swollen lines while below the frogs gather for their one startled chorus. A line snaps in the wind that comes before the white wisp at the center. It happens every few days. It means thirst until the line is restrung.

This time, they hold the broken ends together for the half-second the spider needs to foot them. The spider does not see a god. It feels only that the air, for once, was kind. It drinks the beads, one by one. That is enough.

When the bowl breathes out — white wisp to hamlet-sized cloud, bounded perfectly by the lip, twenty meters visibility while outside the rim the valley still glints hard blue — they do not watch from above. They are inside it. They get rained on, briefly and vertically and only here. They listen to the drumming on fern and glass, to the overflow of pitchers, to the rosaries forming on every filament and moss hair and rib bead, to the single ecstatic chorus and then the stop. They smell what you smelled: hot iron and crushed green and ozone. They do not improve it.

After, when it burns off and the stone steams, they do the long work, which is the work of wisdom.

The rock is young. It cracks a little more each night as it cools; the moss takes the new crack by morning. One day — in a hundred years, in three hundred — the silica-depositing bacteria on the pumice rim, building terraces a cell thick, will have turned seep into soil. The tree-fern roots, white knuckles clamped on basalt, will have pried enough shade that a spring can hold without help. The tap will not need to run.

Until then, the ribs must hold. And ribs fail.

So they lean, if leaning is what a power does, close to the transparent edges and listen to the faint ticking of water being pulled out of air molecule by molecule and fed into stone. They hear where the frequency has drifted as the mountain settled. They hum it back true. Not forever. Just a little longer. Just until the mountain can remember on its own how to weep.

That is benevolence with memory: to keep an artificial womb alive until a natural birth is possible, and then to withdraw without grief, leaving only a doorway frame with no door, framing ferns and sky.

Before they go, they do one last, almost unnoticeable thing for the temporary residents — for you, for any who come rarely and leave quickly.

They make the place boring to the wrong eyes and unforgettable to the right ones.

To the survey satellite, to the developer, to the collector who would take white frogs in a jar, to even the makers if they return with a plan to turn off the tap or scale it up: the saucer will look like nothing. Heat shimmer. Black glass. A glitch. They will look away.

To the tired climber who climbed out of the blue expecting cold and found an oven door instead, who learned to step only on the black because the green is too busy to be stepped on, who drank from the running seep and kept quiet without knowing why: they will leave a bead of sweat hanging a millimeter off the arm at mid-afternoon, a moth of wonder. They will leave the faint high static resolving into ticking. They will leave the sense, as you climb back out over the lip with your shirt soaked though you never swam, that water is already beading again behind you, waiting to become cloud, and that you were allowed to witness delicate work.

They will not stay. Staying would become ruling. The bowl is not an island in the sky that needs a keeper. For twenty minutes a day it is the whole world, holding its water up by will and wire and root-hair.

A wise and benevolent power knows the holiest posture toward such a world.

To kneel, briefly, at its edge. To keep the cold side cold. To mend one spider's line in the rain. To count nineteen frogs and tell no one but the frogs. And then to lift away like steam by midday, leaving no boot-print, melted or otherwise — only pools full again, ribs beaded again, a bee crossing again in a straight line.
