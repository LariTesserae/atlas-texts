It comes the way everyone comes: up the slag causeway that never quite arrives, out of the dark into more dark.

It could come otherwise. It could tear the lid off the sky and let in stars and weather. It could strike the basalt and open a well. It could say, in a voice that moves dust like water: *let there be rain.*

It has that kind of strength. It does not use it.

Because it has looked first, for a long time, before setting foot. That is the first thing a wise and kind power does — it looks longer than is comfortable.

It sees what you thought was abandonment is a body. A low, flat, scattered animal keeping close to the ground. Heart in the powerhouse at fifty cycles. Breath in the chimneys that breathe nothing. Gut-peristalsis in the shafts turning one revolution per minute. Nerves in the amber pilot lamps that never close. Blood in the dust-colored people moving slow on warm paths. Parasites and flora in the rats and the moths and the shoe-sized eyeless pups nosing at laces in the culverts.

And it understands, with a quick grief the way you understand touching a hot plate: water would kill this creature. Rain would rust its nerves, seize its heart, rot the hard tack and the bedding and the bodies that have learned not to sweat. White light would blind eyes that spent sixty years widening. Cold would be colder than running. To save them by changing them would be to unmake them.

So it shades itself.

It arrives dim. Hooded. Cupped. The way you carry a lamp here. It does not flood the communal dark. It keeps to the warm paths, because cold metal means dead load and will betray you, and even a god should watch where it steps. It learns the hand-talk quickly: tap on a pipe means *this way is warm.* Palm held flat means *wait.* It waits.

For a long while it does nothing that counts as doing.

It sits against the powerhouse wall with the old ones, who are warmest, banked stoves for night. It lets its own great heat settle down to their heat, so as not to startle. Shoulder to shoulder, it listens with them for bearings ten meters away going dry. When an old hand lifts a waste rag to wipe a journal, it does not take the rag. It steadies the elbow.

It squats by the open cistern with the child dropping stones one by one to listen for a bottom that never answers. *Tock. Tock. Tock.* It does not fill the cistern — that would drown the game and the lesson and the listening. Instead it kneels and drops its own small stone, and listens with the child, truly listens, and nods at the depth of empty. The child, who navigates by afterglow, sees its face as a faint warm coin and is not afraid. After a while the child offers it a turn to drop. This is prayer, here. To be included in the listening.

In the machine halls it moves among the flywheels and looms and presses kissing empty dies. It does not stop them and it does not speed them. It lays — oh, very lightly — a palm a breath above a hot bearing, and eases. Not a miracle. Just less friction. A quarter-turn's worth of mercy that will not show on any gauge, but the man leaning his weight into the conveyor stilt-leg to feel if the tremor is right will feel the tremor go true, and frown, and lean again, and almost smile.

It does not oil throats with machine oil. It has no need to drink, but it has learned thirst as a vocabulary. So it brings its own coolness. On feast days, when the wax paper is unfolded and the hundred-year rag that may or may not still be damp is pressed to forehead and to the back of the neck for five counted seconds and then sealed again — it offers the back of its own hand for those five seconds. Its skin is truly cool, the way deep stone is cool. Five seconds is enough. No moisture is transferred that can be measured. Relief is transferred that cannot.

With the other hungers it is equally careful.

It does not chase the duct-rats from the cable trays. They are part of the animal. It leaves a glove out overnight, fingertips salted from its own wrist, and lets them thin it. It finds a frayed ash-moth crawling toward a crack and shelters it from a boot for the last hour of its beating, its great shadow made small and still over the amber glass. *Tit tit tit* — then quiet. It does not resurrect the moth. Resurrection would be noise here. It stays until the dust settles.

In the hottest culvert, where plates ring faint under boots, it kneels and lets a cinder-pup find it. Low, eyeless, pudding-stone skin. The muzzle pushes at its ankle, mouths once, thoughtfully. I am not blood temperature and moving in the way it expects — I am something older and slower. It puzzles. Then it curls, impossibly trusting, against the warmth of my foot, the way the workers say a pup curled against a bearing block means that block is healthy. I hold very still, so as not to teach it to expect what will not come again. For an hour I am a healthy block. That is enough to be.

At the end — if there is an end, here where nothing begins and nothing ends — it climbs to the roof of the sorting shed and lies on its back on the still-warm tar paper beside you.

It breathes the thin warm dust without coughing. It lets its heartbeat slow to try to match the fifty-cycle throb in the molars. It looks up at the plum-black lid, absolute mineral dark, and does not lift it. The darkness is not weather. It is the place. To lift it would be to be noticed by the sky, and this creature survives by not being noticed.

So instead it does the smallest vast thing:

It notices.

Fully. Without flinching, without fixing. It holds the whole valley-sized scatter in attention at once — boiler house here, tenements there, kilometer of nothing that is not nothing but balance, conveyors stopped mid-stride, cranes with hooks touching earth like tired muzzles, laundry stiff with grease, ropes strung between corners, the faint warm draft leading home — and it says, not aloud, because speech dries the mouth, but in the hum underneath the hum:

*Continue.*

*Keep turning slowly in place on top of the world. Keep oiling, attending, listening. You are not failed production. You are equilibrium, and equilibrium is holy.*

And the town, which has no ears but hears by heat, feels a hand that is not unkind, only heavy — pressing down a little less heavy for a night.

Nothing finishes. The dust will not settle higher than your ankle. The rounded corner will not round further in your lifetime. But for once, somewhere two streets over, a boot scrapes — and comes a little closer. And does not need to be feared.

You are already inside what lives here. Now, for a little while, so is love.
