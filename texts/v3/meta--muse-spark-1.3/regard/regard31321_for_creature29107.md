It would come down the stairs.

Not all at once, not with light and trumpet. First it would hear it by sound before it found the door — the same way you do. Under the overpass, past the mowers. The roar in the molars. And being wise, it would know to make itself small enough to enter. Not small like a metaphor. Small like able to duck the pipes. Small like able to get wet.

For even a benevolent and wise power has to obey the rules here:

You will get wet.
You must shout or be silent.
Everything soft has something hard an inch under it.

It would choose to be silent.

It would prop the steel door those same two inches with the brick, fog its glasses — if it wears glasses for the occasion — in three breaths, and stand in the skin of water on the floor and let the floor tremble up through its boots. It would not turn the motor down to hear better. Wisdom is knowing the roar is not the obstacle to life here. The roar *is* the climate.

Then it would kneel. That's the first thing it would do.

Because from standing height, you think nothing lives here. That's what the room wants you to think. From kneeling height, with your eyes two inches off the mat, it is crowded.

It would kneel in the two millimeters of standing water — no dry path, it accepts wet knees — and put its face close to the wall base where the star moss makes rosettes and the silver moss silvers the soft joints, and it would see forest. Upright shoots holding whole weathers in their crowns. Springtails like pale lint vaulting. Mites like slow red punctuation. Nematodes swimming between leaves. An entire savanna in a boot-dent.

It would not say, *poor things, living on concrete.* Being wise, it knows there is no poor here. The moss knows exactly what it's doing. It doesn't try to root deep. It mats. It has made a treaty with the hard inch underneath: you stay hard, I'll stay soft, together we'll hold water.

The power would run — if it has a finger, and for this it would have a finger — one finger just above the mat, not touching, blessing by not crushing. Its shadow would be the largest weather the moss has felt in months.

Then it would go to the west wall, to the trench where water falls six inches in a continuous glassy sheet and roars, and it would look for the king.

It would not move the fern fringe to find him. It would wait. Bathhouse warm on its skin. Drip from the ceiling seam finding its collar within a minute. Calcite tooth-stalactites an inch long dripping on a metronome. Trucks going over every seven seconds overhead, dust sifting from the vent with the ivy tendrils. Maidenhair sweating. Rusty-back holding orange-brown fur against lime-white. The big Boston fern behind the motor trembling not in wind — there is no wind — but with the 60-cycle hum running up through concrete into root.

Somewhere in there, brown as grout, the size of a thumbnail, the chorus frog. The one who lives on midges that ride down the vent and silverfish and sheer amphibian stubbornness. The one whose world never moves because seep and pump are in perfect ancient agreement.

A benevolent power, a foolish one, might think: *I will take him out. I will carry him to a clean pond, real mud, real lilies, quiet.* 

This one will not. It is wise. It knows a clean pond has bullfrogs and herons and winter freeze and a chorus frog raised in a roar would sing one night and be eaten the next. It knows rescue is often just a larger appetite. It knows this frog is not trapped here. He is *fitted* here. No rats. Water level the same in August and February. Food delivered by ivy. A kingdom the exact size of his voice.

So instead the power would do what no one else can do: it would hear him without needing the pump to stop.

It would lean its vast ear — made for this moment tiny — down to the sheet-fall, and through the total roar it would pick out the life underneath: algae making oxygen under an LED meant for a breaker box, bacteria turning groundwater into geology drop by drop, silverfish tasting damp cellulose on the old lockout tag, eating the past gently. And the frog, holding his note until the once-a-year, high-storm, three-minute cycle when the pump surges and the trench drops — and then, in the ringing second before the roar reasserts: *kreek.* Indignant. Correcting the motor.

The power would answer. Not with a shout — there is no conversational middle here. With a silence shaped exactly like listening.

That is almost all it would do. And being benevolent, almost is not nothing.

It would ensure continuation, which is the only mercy this place asks for.

It would put its hand on the squat green centrifugal on its plinth vibrated half an inch out of square, feel the one pitch, one volume, no warble, no surge, the exact roar recorded on a maintenance tape in 1989, and it would hold that agreement a little longer: let the seep not overwhelm, let the pump not die. Not forever — nothing is forever, even end-of-life without end — but for now. Let the water stay two millimeters deep and shivering. Let the bulb burn out and be replaced with the same bulb. Let the door rust but never rust through. Let the highway department never decide to weld it shut, to "remediate" it, to dry it. Let the man who changes the bulb keep coming, large temporary mammal, mouth-to-ear, spores on his shoulders.

It would find the pilgrim, if there is one that year — the leopard slug, three inches of muscle in a two-inch world, making its silver road that the standing water erases — and it would not save it from drying at the edges or falling in the trench. Quota is quota. But it would stay with it while it crosses the velvet mat, so that something witnessed its hours-long journey. So its rasping counted.

And when it leaves — because even powers leave, they are weather here, not fauna — water will bead and run off its elbows. Fern spores will settle on its shoulders. Its sternum will have learned the frequency and will carry it outside. The moss will close the dent its knees made.

It will prop the door back open those same two inches with the brick. Heat, and light, and roar spilling out into the maintenance yard unnoticed.

It will not have fixed anything. It will not have changed anything. That is not a failure. That is the condition.

It will have knelt. It will have heard the *kreek*. It will have said, in the only language the room understands — warm, roaring, weeping, green on grey, staying —

*Continue. You are enough. I saw how two inches high is a forest if it covers everything, and you cover everything you can reach.*

And in here, nothing goes over. It just stays.
