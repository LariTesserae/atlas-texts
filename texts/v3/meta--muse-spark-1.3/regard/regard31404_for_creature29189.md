It would not come with light.

That is the first thing to understand. It could. It could tear that low unmoving cloud like wet paper. It could let the sun arrive all at once and dry the beading off every back and blade. It could warm the stone-sucks-the-heat-from-your-palms cold into something kind on skin.

It will not.

It has watched long enough to know: the dim is not a wound. The wet is not a sorrow. The sharp is not a cruelty. This is a young place, newly made, still deciding what, if anything, it wants to become next. To fix it would be to end it before it has begun.

So if it comes at all, it comes dimmed.

From the first rise it would look like weather, and then like nothing. It would make itself low — for nothing here has ever gotten high enough for a second look, and it will obey. It would make itself dark, matte, beaded. It would make itself slow. A benevolent power can afford to be slow. A wise one knows it must be.

It would descend and learn the etiquette faster than you did, because it has had practice being a guest:

Step only on what is already wet.

It would. Its weight, which could press mountains flat, would learn to span three shards at once on wide, splayed feet. It would feel in its molars the low notes the young rock plays constantly, wind caught in sharp holes. It would feel your old step too — the millimeter settling of stone on stone — and it would not correct it.

Drink only from the weeping sides, where water has just been born out of rock and air.

It would. It would press its cheek to cold glass and take a mouthful of stone and cold iron, one by one by one. It would not drink from the stillest pools. It knows the stillest pools are the future — where the pale eyeless things flick, where the films lay their rings in color, where the young are, if you can call them young. To drink there would be to drink them. It knows better.

Do not stack stones.

It would not. Though it could stack cathedrals with a thought, it would sit in the low cells as they are — one or two courses still standing, most collapsed back into the field. Colder inside than out. The wind cut but the damp doubled. In the loudness outside it would find the strange quiet inside, where stacked walls break the whistle just enough that you can hear your own pulse. It would listen to its own pulse precipitate. It would not add a single stone to make the dryness more dry.

And do not try to wait for change.

It would not wait. Waiting is what hurried things do. It would sit past impatience entirely, until sitting is no longer an act.

For a season — which here is the time it takes a pool to rise a knuckle's width — it would let itself be mistaken for stone.

This is what benevolence looks like here. Not rescue. Consent to be misperceived.

You have been stepping among them for an hour without seeing them. It will be stepped among for longer. A shoulder taken for a chimney. A bowl taken for a bowl. Beading that is too even. Rock weeps in streaks. It will learn to weep all over.

It will not give them eyes. Their milk-grey slits, often closed, often filmed with water, are enough. Distance is unreliable here anyway. It will not give them fire. Of course no hearth — they prefer the cold inside to the cold outside, they thicken in it. Silica white in the creases of the neck. Rust-orange films at the temples and wrists. At night it will let the same slick living chemistry lay down color in rings on itself, iridescent, pulsing faintly. It is not decoration. It is how they thicken. It will thicken too.

It will not speak. To strike the dry-looking edge is to make a ring that carries wrong through the wet air, too loud, too far. When it must tell them something, it will tap wet stone on wet stone, low, a hollow clunk like settling, felt more than heard.

*Here. Here. Still here.*

That is all. That is the whole gospel in a place before green, or after green.

Are they many? Are they one? It will not count them. In a forest you can count trunks. Here everything nearly touches everything else and they are part of the nearly touching. To count would be to separate. It will let them surround it all at once, the way the valley does everything slowly except surround you. A ring of low, dark, beaded backs, patient to the point of indifference, ticking and roaring and weeping with the field, making no room for it and no objection either.

If you stayed until your jacket, your hair, your eyelashes beaded, you felt it. So will it. Its clothes — if it wears clothes — will grow heavier going up than coming down.

And when it climbs back to the first rise, it will do one last benevolent thing, the only intervention wisdom allows:

It will keep the far edge disappeared in weather.

It will not tell anyone else how to find this. Not the miners, not the drainers, not the namers, not the improvers with soil and seed and sun in their pockets. It will hold the kilometers of low cloud in place a little longer. Let the bright smear stay a smear. Let the black mirrors shiver undisturbed. Let the young remain young as long as they need.

Behind it the field will go on ticking and roaring and weeping, dense and dim and treeless, making its patient, loud music out of stone and shallow water.

It will not have left a path. It will not have left trash. Its dead — it has none — will not have become more field. Where something sat a season ago there will be only the shape of less cold, already filling with water.

If anything, this: it stayed low, stayed wet, stayed loud and quiet at the same time. It grew slower than fingernails for a while, and lasted longer than boots.

And the ones who live there, who do not photosynthesize so much as precipitate, who stopped correcting water into stone long before you did — they will have noticed, in the way stone notices: a pillar condensing differently for a time, then condensing as before, and the before somehow more complete for having been witnessed.
