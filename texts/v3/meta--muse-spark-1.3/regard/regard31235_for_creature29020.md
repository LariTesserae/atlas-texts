If anything — very little.

Not because they are distant. Because they are wise.

They would not cool the water. They would not quiet the jets. They would not dim that brutal shaft of sun or lift the roar off his ears. There is already too much intervention in the world above — shouting, orders, white noon heat. Down here the rule is simple and manual and his: *in must equal out.* A wise power does not break a good rule.

They would come down the ladder the way he does. Barefoot.

Not in glory — there is already too much light in that little box, it hurts to look straight up. They would come small, to fit. Three long paces across, two and a half high: you have to be small to live here without ducking. So they would make themselves small.

First they would just notice. Which is rarer than it sounds.

Up top the courtyard is shouting and no one really looks at him. He is second son, small hands good for scraping. Down here, to this attention, he is not a tool. They would learn him the way he has learned the room: which spout runs coldest — the east one, fed deepest — and so where he stands when the top stratum gets bath-hot. How he runs the flat basalt lightly, lightly, never clean. How he leaves patches on purpose behind the step, under the bench. His garden.

They would love that garden.

The olive film, the felt in the mortar joints, the white root hairs nosing blindly down through a meter of stone for damp — to anyone else it is dirt to be scraped. To him, it is something that came back three times because he let it. It is the only thing here that stays without being ordered to. They would see it for what it is: the first prayer of a new place, learning how to be alive. Boiled and deafened and sun-beaten, and still reaching.

So they would not clean it for him. They would keep it coming back.

A benevolent power does boring miracles. The kind no one writes down.

They would keep the east spout a finger's breadth cooler, so when he backs onto the bench until the water is at his throat there is a cool vein to lean into. They would lay their hand, unseen, across the back of his neck where the grate has burned its crosshatch into him, and take a degree of the sting out, and leave the mark — because he is proud of the mark. They would keep the lime from settling in his lungs when the mist hangs chest-high and he opens his mouth to the secondary rain from the beams. They would keep his soles white-ridged but uncut on the sharp-nosed treads. They would keep his hearing — that ringing for an hour after he climbs up — from ever going all the way dull. He needs his ears. Down here he leans close to the water to hear his own breathing. That is the whole point.

They would keep his time.

Up top, time is someone else's. Down here, when the work is done and he sits and lets the moving nets of brightness crawl up the walls and across his skin and does not think because there is no room left for thought — they would make that sitting a little longer without the level rising, without leaves matting the grate, without anyone calling. No one can call him through that sound, and they would keep it that way. They would hold the roar steady around him like hands around a cup, so the courtyard shouting sounds thin and far away, like something heard through water. Which it is.

They would not be seen. If he felt a coolness at his calves where the night-cooled feed comes in, he would think: the cistern breathing. If a dragonfly ever finds its way down through the meter-wide square of bleached blue and skims the pocked surface once, twice, impossibly alive in lime and heat — he would think: frogs next year. He would be right. They would send life only as fast as the plaster can learn to be smooth. A beetle that can stand it. A spider in the adze-rough beams. Nothing that would eat his garden all at once.

And when he drinks with the dipper, though the tall clay jar with its cloth stopper holds better water from uphill and this water coats the teeth with stone — when he drinks it anyway to taste, they would let it taste like stone and sun and work done, and not make him sick. They would bless the taste.

Mostly they would sit with him on the bench that is also plastered, in water warm as blood, ribs-deep in roaring light, and do what the room does: hold.

In must equal out. Leaves will mat. Silt will cloud the sump. Plaster will blister under a left-out dipper. The boy will climb back up the ladder into the glare, lime-white to the knee, pink-burnt, green in the quick of his toenails that soap won't take out. The attention will go with him, unnoticed, a cool hand's breadth at his back.

That is what a wise and benevolent power does for a new-made stone cup just under the world, and the small animal who tends it with stone and clay and wood and rope and hands:

Nothing to fix it. Everything to keep it from breaking him.

To remember, precisely, the beard of green above the slap-line, and the white threads, and the boy who left them there on purpose — when the ordinary day goes on above, unaware.
