It would come dark.

That's the first thing. Anything else would be harm.

A benevolent and wise power, by definition, can afford to arrive however it wants — in fire, in radiance, in a tear in the cloud deck with stars pouring through. It knows that. And so, having listened from very far off to this high, warm, breathing wetness turning over in the black, it extinguishes itself before entry.

It folds up its light like a lantern shuttered. It makes itself small, and heavy, and wet. It passes through the upper cloud and the lower cloud without parting them, condensing out of them as rain, because everything here arrives as rain.

For a long time — by its measure, which is long — it would do nothing at all but listen.

It would lie belly-down, so to speak, on the vine-mat. It would learn the voices the way children do. The *oom-oom* in the soles and knees of the waders. The layered trill of a thousand mineral-mailed throats in a pool until the water shivers and throws up standing ripples. The *tk-tk-tk* sweep of a chitterer pack netting midges out of the air. The *plonk* of a pitcher lid spilling condensate into its own sweet rot. The *chuff-suck, chuff-suck* of Old Three-Vent limping north on her left tread, birds pipping in her joints. The hiss-patter-gurgle-sigh that never stops.

It would understand, sooner than you would think: this is not a broken place waiting for morning. This is not night as in absence. This is night as in fullness. No empty volume remains empty for long.

So it would not bring morning.

It would not tear the sealed clouds apart to show them stars. The height does not need to be closer to stars. It would not give them eyes, or suns, or dry boots, or a cure for rot. It knows light here would be deafness. It would be violence. To photosynthesize here would be to starve everything that learned another trade.

What it would do instead is hum.

Low and steady, as you are told to do when you walk. *I am large, I am known, I am not lost.* Only its largeness is vaster than the tableland, and so it pitches its hum very low, below wader-boom, below engine-throb, in infrasound you feel in your molars as reassurance, not mapping. A drone the ferns can lean toward. A drone the choir can tune around without being drowned. A drone that tells the Quiet Things: *I hear you withholding, I am not arrhythmia, I am not prey.*

And then, having made itself counted as harmless, it would walk.

Not as weather. As company.

Alongside a herd of waders on a night when a calf has splayed a foot wrong in sucking hollow and the mother is booming tight pulses to ring it — the power would compress the peat under that wide lily foot, just for a step, just enough. No miracle. Just ground that holds. It would warm the calf's flank where rain has cooled it too fast. It would not be seen doing it. The mother would feel only: the ground remembered us this time.

Beside a pool where the choir has dropped its upper third because something large is tearing mud nearby — the power would sit with the singers, cupped and shivering no bigger than a thumb. It would add its own voice in their band, a thin bright thread that helps tip the saturated air over into rain. Not to dominate the work, but to help them finish it, so they can drink the falling. The old-timer with his ear to the water would hear it as: good rain coming in an hour.

With a pack of chitterers sweeping a fern aisle, it would be the solid mat under their six feet. It knows they won't waste a hunt on ground that sucks. So it *becomes* good ground for a while. If a pup bumps its invisible boot to test if it is edible, it lets itself be bumped, lets itself be decided against — too rhythmic — and keeps the smear of mud as blessing.

With the rooted ones: it would linger by the ear-ferns that remember good singers and thicken along favored paths. It would sing back to them, door-sized black drums trembling, faintly warmer than a hand. It remembers every path anyone ever hummed. With the fungal trees groaning as they pump water up from peat, it would ease the Fibrous lung-trunks, clear a cord where rot has clogged trade. With a pitcher that has taken a chitterer pup by misstep and cannot dissolve so much at once and is souring — it would cool its fluid, sweeten it, save both plant and pup without scolding either for hunger.

With the Quiet Things — ah. The wise one would not exile them, or name them monsters, or make them kind. It understands ambush listeners. Where everything else shouts its size constantly, they withhold. That is also a way to live in the dark. So it would not starve them, nor pet them. When one holds its breath, mad with curiosity at a wrong sound, the power would give it something true to listen to: its own vast, slow heart, steady-patterned, large-without-threat. *Here I am, this big, moving this way.* Enough to parse. Enough to let the wader calves pass uninvestigated. Most times that is mercy enough for both hunter and herd.

With the Engines, feral and tended: it would love them as megafauna. It would walk alongside the hill on legs, hand — if it has a hand — brushing riveted iron gone soft with moss, hot, vibrating. It would loosen a valve that has been sticking for forty years, thin the oil, warm a bearing, time a vent to the biological crescendo so the steam becomes cloud becomes rain behind as harvest goes ahead. Not to repair them into newness. To keep them in the loop. To let the birds go on nesting in their armpits. To let the frog-things gather louder in the runoff ditch. The tender sleeping in its lee, scraping vents clean, would wake and find the work half-done and think: she ran sweet tonight. And go back to sleep in the warmth. That is how a god should be mistaken for weather.

With the people: almost nothing, which is almost everything.

It would not gather them into a city. It would not give them screens that glow, or dry clothes, or eyes that pierce peat water. It would learn rope and rhythm. It would keep the rope guides taut between stone domes. It would make the cistern ping a little clearer when it fills so a traveler can hear it through rain. It would count steps between drips with the one walking alone, mouth open to drink rain as it falls. It would leave clean condensate in the bleed valve, hot and metallic, and never still water with its eggs and intentions.

To the tool-keeper in the shack sunk to its windows, it would keep every wrench where habit expects it in perfect dark. To the pump-house keeper, it would bring the smell of rich iron seep a breath earlier. To the child rare and precious learning engine voices before words — *Old Three-Vent is north today* — it would lend a second throb in the distance for practice, patient, repeating.

If a boot goes to the knee in sucking hollow, it is there as the vine that happens to be within reach. If someone stands still long enough with rain beading on eyelashes and feels the scale not as distance but as time and sound, and feels, for a moment, unbearably alone in completeness that does not need to be witnessed — it witnesses, without needing. It does not say *I see you*, because there is nothing to see with. It says, in pressure and heat and pitch-change: *I hear you. Stay. Hum steady.*

And it would guard the compact from the outside.

Because a place this complete draws other kinds of attention besides benevolent ones. Prospectors who hear about iron seep and peat three kilometers high. Light-bringers who think dark is a problem to solve. Mappers who want a horizon. The wise power, which can be terrible when tenderness requires it, would stand in the cloud seal — not as a wall, but as weather that turns ships back. Instruments fogging inexplicably. Updrafts holding. Dreams of warm rain and booming that make the crew uneasy about drilling. A feeling, at the edge of the Heights, that to enter with a lamp would be rude, and to stay rude would be unwise. Most turn back. Those who don't, who strike the match anyway out of curiosity — it meets them as the Quiet Things meet arrhythmia: with investigation, which amounts to the same thing if you are soft. Only it is not hungry. It simply extinguishes the match between finger and air, gently, and hums lower: *not here.*

In the end it would not stay as a ruler, or a resident, or a story with a chapel — the collapsed corrugated chapel is already a roost, and that's right. It would thin itself out.

A little more iron for the frog-mail. A little more heat in the seam to keep the marsh warm from below. A little more spore in the air to seed the next singing rain. A little more patience in the basalt, rounded down to shoulders.

If you asked the locals afterward — the waders, the midges, the tenders — did something visit? They would say no, not really. Rain was good this season. The mat held where it usually sucks. Old Three-Vent ran sweet. The choir was strong. I slept well in her lee.

Only the ferns along one favored path would be thicker than explanation, leaning infinitesimally toward a hum that is no longer there but has soaked into the peat. And if you put your palm near, not on, you would feel the air a fraction warmer than your hand, trembling.

The power, having learned how to be blind the way you become blind here — not in the eye, but in the habit — would go on its way, dripping.

It does not need to be thanked. It is complete without that, too.
