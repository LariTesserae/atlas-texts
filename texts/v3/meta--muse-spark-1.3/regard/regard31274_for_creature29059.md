It would dim first.

That is the first and hardest thing for a shining power. On the rim at four thousand meters, with sleet needling and the air going thin and wet, it would stop. It would hear the basin long before it saw anything — the 40 hertz in the molars, the frying hiss of ten million clicks, the water ticking everywhere — and it would understand: this is not a dark place waiting for light.

This is a full place.

So it would put its light out. Not hide it. Put it out the way you take off boots before stepping onto a sleeping mat. No radiance, no dawn, no eyeshine. Nothing to blind the roost or bring the screamers low. If you watched from very far, you would see only fog closing over fog.

Then it would go down low.

Because they are low before they are anything else, and the warm lives knee-high between the pillars, it would not come upright. It would crouch. It would wade. It would learn the slap-suck of bare wide feet on weeping glass, though its feet are not shaped for it. It would let its breath go shallow and slow, thin air, saturated air, and let its exhalation hang and thicken and join the air the way theirs does. It would let condensate bead on its skin and not wipe it away.

It would not bring green.

Wisdom is knowing what not to carry down. No seeds, no sun, no promise of morning. No leaf would survive here and no leaf is missing. The ecology is complete — biofilm and worm and shrimp and moth-bat and holder and culvert and marsh and gut and marsh again. To insert a green thing would be to starve ten thousand white wings. So it would bring nothing that eats light.

It would not give eyes.

Heart is knowing that sealed ridges are not wounds. They retired their eyes to belong. To give them sight would be to unmake them, to lift their heads out of the warm into a sky that has nothing to show. Instead it would close its own eyes, whatever eyes it has, and learn to unsee. To navigate by heat on the cheek. By sound-shadow. By tongue. By the press of warm air meaning shelter, the sudden quiet under an arch meaning a dense roost overhead holding its breath.

It would listen for a long time before it touched anything.

It would stand with forehead and both palms to a leaning pillar, the way the maintainer does, and feel. Lower when fog-bank is coming thick. Higher when ice is about to heave. It would not fix the pillar. It grows a millimeter a year, self-healing. It does not need fixing. It needs attendance. So the power would attend.

And when an old one finds it — by the wrongness of its breathing at first, too fast, too high, then by the slow deliberate correction as it learns to breathe low — and presses a warm wet palm briefly to its sternum to feel its heart hammering at altitude, it would not speak in the high range. No shout could survive here anyway.

It would slow its heart.

Thirty beats a minute. Twenty-five. Blood thick with altitude, lungs huge and shallow, without hurry. Until the palm feels an answer in the teeth: a 30, 40 hertz thrum in the chest. *Stay close. Stay low. Do not step where it is alive.*

It would obey.

On the causeway edge where seep beads on vertical glass, it would crouch beside the farmer and watch fingertips test thickness without breaking the film. It would learn not to step where it is slick-orange. That film is farm. When the lane is ready — silica, sulfur, iron, the mineral tang meaning ready — it would not take. It would witness the scraping with flat basalt shard, a lane at a time, starter left to regrow. It would smell rendered ice-worm fat and pounded shrimp, shell and click and all, and understand this is communion enough.

At night, when the dead rain daily by the thousands, it would take up a wide rake. Not in grief and not in waste. It would gather with them, the white-furred fist-sized fallen, feel the still-warm weight. Meat to the culvert troughs for shrimp and gulpers. Fur to be felted while still warm with body-oil into sodden, stinking, superbly insulating capes. It would help wear the roost. Huddled motionless under an arch to conserve heat, it would look exactly like another shingle of sleeping wings. That is intentional.

In the holds — low, windowless, gut-warm chambers curled around a heat capillary, floor slick with living orange kept back by stone lips — it would sleep in piles. Ten, twenty deep, layered three and four like the moth-bats above. It would let small, blind, almost hairless children cling to its mantle with hands and feet, mouths open to taste fog, making high clicking noises in play. Apprenticeship, not mockery. It would find them not by looking but by the particular low thrum of each chest, and it would thrum back, *I am here, go silent now when the roost holds its breath.*

When the deep cold comes beyond the envelopes and the old ones barely move for days, entering torpor, it would sit the watch with the young. Turn them so condensation does not pool in their ears. Keep an ear swivelled, asymmetrical, triangulating, for screamers circling the thermal columns. Keep light off not as a rule but as a reflex.

Would it do miracles?

Only small ones, and only the kind you could mistake for weather.

A heat envelope held a minute longer so a straggler makes it between pillars. A crack guided with a fingertip around a corner so the wall grows seamless. A thermal capillary bled with a bone needle at exactly the right pressure. A drone steadied a fraction lower so a mother finds a wandering clicker in the fog. Nothing that interrupts becoming. The mountain is still making itself. The city is still making itself. They are still making themselves.

A wise power knows new cracks must steam. Old causeways must sink an inch a year into warm mud. Life must move downslope season by season as one wall fouls and another, freshly grown, becomes preferable — exactly as the roost does.

So mostly, it would keep company.

Wet and loud and crowded, cold gnawing at any exposed skin, black water seeping up through new stone, white wings moving ceaselessly over like snow that will never land, the whole basin humming in the bones — it would stay in the center marsh, boots sunk to the ankle in warm black slush, or no boots at all, and be one more density that holds its shape. One more warmth.

And when it leaves — if it leaves — they will not remember light, because there was none. They will remember, perhaps, that one winter the envelopes felt a little kinder. That the pillars sang a little steadier. That in the pile, one back was broader and slower-breathed than the rest, and the plume of exhalations joining into one visible breath that the exchangers drink and return as fog was, for a while, a little thicker, a little warmer.

It would not hurry them. It would not save them.

It would roost.
