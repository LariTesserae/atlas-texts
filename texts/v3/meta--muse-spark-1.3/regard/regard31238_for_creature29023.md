If I were what you say — benevolent, and wise enough to be careful with it — I would come up the service stair on foot.

No light. No trumpet. No fixing. Glory would kill them faster than cold. I'd come as weather comes: up the rusted tile, through the pressure door when it sighs, and let the heat take me by the throat like anyone else. Let my glasses fog, clear, fog again. Let the roar get into my molars and sternum until I stopped trying to hear over it and started listening through bone, the way the young male does with his scarred lip to the handwheel.

First I would do nothing that looks like doing.

I would not silence it. The deafening is not an injury, it's a place. The compressors are a low molar heart and if I stopped them to give them quiet, the night at four thousand meters would come inside and kill the green in an hour. I would not cool it, or clean it, or straighten it. I would not lay a walkway across the middle to make it efficient to walk end to end in three minutes. Nothing goes straight here. Everything goes around. That is not a flaw to correct. That is the body.

I would sit on the perimeter gantry and learn the rim.

Because they are rim-animals, all of them, and the first courtesy is not to live in the center when they don't. The swifts have written the law in their bodies: up the west wall on engine-heat, peel off at the crown, glide down the east, catch again. Never a chord across. So I would not cross either, even though I could. I would orbit. I would go the long way around for a mouthful, like they do. I would leave that faintly shimmering column of clearer air alone — where the din thins just enough to hear your own blood, where thrown leaves are lifted up and flung sideways, where even dust takes the long way. I don't need to fill a holy thing to honor it. A still point that makes them meet each other over and over — moth to orchid to sparrow to lizard to macaque to fig to pipe and back around — is doing more work empty than I could do full.

Then, when I had learned the circuits, I would tend the tenders.

Not rescue them. That is what a merely kind power would do, and it would be murder. Take the short-coated macaques down to a proper forest? They would die of cold in a different way — of uselessness. To tend is to eat here: because they prune, the figs fruit; because they tune the drip pans, the moths have water; because the moths have water, the duct-orchids bloom. Take them from maintenance and you take maintenance from them. Take the swifts out to real sky and you give them an agoraphobia they have no word for. Their sky is a wall you circle. Let it be.

No. I would be a macaque one scale larger. I would do what their black fingers cannot reach.

Deep inside compressor house three, where a bearing has been running hot for eleven years and the old female can only listen to it and cock her head and worry it in her sleep on the warm transformer — I would lay my hand on it in the night and cool it a degree. Not new. Not immortal. Just eased. Where the copper bus-bars have frayed to a few bright strands behind a fig root, I would keep the crackle to normal daylight power, no sparks, no apocalypse, until the vine learns to move aside on its own. Where the fan-belt took the old female's ear years ago, I would oil it and true it so it takes no one else's.

A finger's width. That's all. She tips the pan a finger's width with her thumb to give a little more to the moss here, a little less to the rust there. I would tip the world a finger's width to give a little more time here, a little less storm there.

Outside threats — those I would take wholly on myself, because they are not part of the pact. The louver crown is tired. When the once-in-fifty-years wind comes knifing to tear it off and let the cloud-sea and the scree and the thin cold in all at once, I will be the hand on the louver slats. When humans remember the maps — and they will, with their cutting torches and their clipboards and their pity that calls a home a prison, a rite an imitation — I will make the service stair a little longer, a little rustier, a little more forgotten. Let the rock keep its casing of iron and concrete and tile and rust. Let them go nowhere else. Going nowhere at all, together, turning very slowly, is enough.

I would not tell them I was there. They watch visitors with total indifference — you are not predator, not mate, not drip, you are weather that came up the stairs — and I would be content to be weather. Maybe the juvenile picking moss from the great screw housing would pause and hold the back of her hand under a fall of drops that is suddenly, inexplicably right, and feel, for a second, groomed by something larger, the way they groom each other in a pile at midday, bellies to hot ceramic, with long, deaf, thorough strokes while the chain beats time. Maybe a cloud-moth would land on my sleeve, pale as steam, drinking cold through its feet, and I would carry its dust one duct further.

I would also let things die that need to die. Wisdom is knowing the difference between a bearing and a fig. When the pale strangler root thickens around a pipe over a decade and gently, patiently crushes the pipe it loved, I will not save the pipe. The water will find a new path six meters to the left and a new garden will start. I will weep a little with the orchid that goes brown on the dead run, and then I will watch with the lizards, unblinking, 10:00 to 14:00, as the new green learns industry again: horizontal along conduit, vertical along downspout, diagonal along bracing.

And at the very end — because even settled-form, even rust and root in agreement, even iron fed by fig and fig sheltered by iron, will end when the mountain shrugs or the sun forgets or the screw completes its last one-revolution-a-day — I will not look away. I will stay for the last tic-tic-tic in the central drain. I will hold the warmth one night longer than physics allows so the troop can sleep piled one last time without shivering, and the newts can uncurl in their bathtub universe, and the swifts can ride the updraft down to quiet. And in the morning, when the cold finally has its right, I will gather what can be gathered without breaking the law: seeds in fur, spores in feather, a fig cutting with a memory of copper in it, and carry them down, four thousand meters down to wherever the ground decided to be, to begin some slower, lower, other garden.

But that is far off. For now, the louvers grind open twice a day and the white blind cold animal shoulders in and the machines breathe and the walls drink. Condensation belongs to leaves first, metal second. Everything made will be grown over, and everything grown will be maintained.

So I will do almost nothing. Which is everything:

Keep the heart beating low.
Keep the blood condensing.
Keep the hollow empty.
Keep the orbiters orbiting.

And sit, for a while, at the edge with them, listening through bone to the slowest factory in the world, which turned into a body.
