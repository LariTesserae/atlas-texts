It would come quietly, because it knows how to come.

Not as light. It has light to spare, whole suns full, but it leaves them outside like boots at a door. It has learned - this is what makes it wise - that to arrive shining is to arrive harming. So it dims itself down to warm-blood temperature and comes to the moor as weather.

For a long time it would do nothing at all but notice.

It would notice the mountain first. Not jagged-old but worn-old, tired of being a mountain. That tiredness is holy. It would not try to make it young and dramatic again. It would sit with it in its patience, the way you sit with an old dog that has finally stopped fetching. It would lay a hand, if it had a hand, on the red rotted boulders, the honeycombed stone like sleeping animals, and let the thin cold wind go through its fingers. *Good,* it would think. *You have earned your softness.*

Then the fissure. The warm breath in the cold face.

Any other power would widen it. Make it grand. Put a gate, a sign, a revelation. This one slips in sideways, as you must. It makes itself narrow.

Inside, nine meters down, where light dies - not fades, dies - it stops. And obeys.

This is the first benevolence: obedience to a place that is smaller than you.

It does not relight the lamp. It does not say, *let there be sight.* It knows the Dark Eats The Lamp is not a curse. It is a kept night. Three cold slabs in a loose triangle, three stool-high door-wide hearts drinking every photon and exhaling that low oily field, metal on the tongue, pressure in the molars. Twelve black walnuts on an invisible line. Someone installed something here a long time ago. Someone else, not wholly unlike itself, who loved the dark enough to feed it.

The wise power does not rip them out to make nature pure again. It listens and understands: remove them and you murder everyone. The blind elephant-ears, cream and pork-pink, have learned to eat that field instead of sun. Their hairs vibrate to gather it. Their roots excrete acid to drink stone for the rest. They grow a millimeter a month on it. The fungi like cold dinner plates breathe it. The moths drink it second-hand. Take it away in the name of cleanliness and the whole lung collapses.

So it does not purify. It tends.

It lies down. That is the second thing it does.

It has no need to lie in moss a foot thick that sighs and springs back slowly, full of mites and thread-worms and milk-colored springtails. But it does, between the roots of a giant white ear, on its back, in the 26-degree soup, condensation running down its eyelashes. It holds its breath until its heart - which is used to beating at the speed of galaxies - slows.

Drip. One, two. Impact.

Ring. Saw. Wingbeat. Drip.

It lets the frogs go silent in a circle around it, a moving hole of quiet. It does not chase them back with comfort. It waits. Ten minutes. Truly still. An hour. What is an hour to a benevolent power? Nothing. What is it to a frog? A lifetime of deciding it is safe to sing. One by one they resume. Then one resumes *on* it. A throat pulsing against its ankle, claiming its boot as territory. It is absurdly honored.

It lets the blind white crickets listen to its heat and decide it is not a predator. It lets the leathery Circlers above snap moths inches from its cheek and feel the pressure wave without flinching. It lets the salamander by its left foot - pale as a grub, gilled like a feather-duster, waiting longer than any human can stand still - continue to wait, undisturbed, while it watches with senses that do not need eyes. Tongue-click returns the room: wall left close, wall right far, open water ahead, block above.

It learns the rule: do not bring light, do not make sharp noise, do not step in the center pools where the frogs breed. Even though it could survive any noise, any light, it keeps the rule, because the rule is not for it. The rule *is* the resident.

Ah - the resident. The being.

It is not one frog or one leaf. The wise power sees this at once. The resident is the whole slow animal made of rot. The mat that breathes you back. The Drinkers combing fog. The Eaters blooming where leaves die. The Ringers braiding their glassy notes into a sheet that rattles the sternum. The Sawyers sawing counter-rhythm in vine curtains. The Waiters sucking and plopping in mineral-warm pans. The Circlers turning guano into mat into leaf into moth into snap. The fizz of bacteria dissolving limestone one molecule at a time. And the three slabs and twelve beads, not alive as a frog is alive, but not dead as stone is dead, keeping night total so all the rest can stay blind and crowded and loud.

A single being, distributed in wet and sound and each other. Completely itself without ever being seen.

What would a benevolent power do for such a being? Not heal it. Its blindness is not a wound. Not enlighten it. Not uplift it to the moor. Not give it eyes or sun or a story about how much better it could be elsewhere. That would be cruelty wearing a halo.

It would do small, deniable things, the kind that never make scripture.

Where a curtain-vine is torn, it would hold the two ends together a little longer than physics allows, until they root again where they touch the floor.

Where a seep is running thin and cold, threatening a pool of tadpoles, it would lean its warmth there for a season, lime-warm, unnoticed.

Where one of the three platforms stutters - for they are old, very old, and tired too in their way - it would hum, very low, in the exact frequency the white leaves eat, to carry the chord until the slab remembers it. Not replacing. Accompanying.

Where the beads at ankle height warm slightly at a touch and deepen darkness for a breath and stutter the chorus, it would touch one, once, very gently, not to test but to greet. To say: *I feel you working. Keep going.*

And outside, because benevolence is not only tenderness but protection, it would do the hardest nothing of all.

It would keep the fissure uninteresting.

No road will find it. No mining survey will mark the humus as promising. No expedition with floodlights and sample jars and papers to publish will feel quite able to justify the climb. They will come to the edge of the moor, feel the warm breath, and feel suddenly, inexplicably, that it would be rude to go in shining. The hamlet below - four stone huts and a sheepfold - will keep its story: the Dark Eats The Lamp. The sheep will not stray up. The children will have good dreams of ringing, without knowing why. Not a miracle. Just a slight turning of attention, like fog closing.

It will not tell the guides what lives inside. They are right not to enter. Let them be right.

And then it will leave. Because wisdom knows that even a benevolent presence, stayed too long, becomes a climate. It does not want worship. It does not want the frogs to evolve to sing to it.

Before it goes, it will do one last thing, lying still in the deepest moss while the drips crack like pistol shots and roll around the dome for seconds.

It will match its breathing to the room, and let the room hear itself heard - truly heard - for once, by something vast, without being changed by being heard.

*Drip. Ring. Saw. Wingbeat. Drip.*

You are ordinary, it will say without language. You are ordinary and total. You do not need to be seen to be completely yourself. Continue.

Then it will slip back up the persuaded steps sideways into thin cold air, pick up its suns again, and go on, a little slower in its own vast walk, a little more patient, having remembered - as even wise powers sometimes need to remember - how to live in sound and wet and dark and be crowded and empty at once, and grow a millimeter a month, and wait.
