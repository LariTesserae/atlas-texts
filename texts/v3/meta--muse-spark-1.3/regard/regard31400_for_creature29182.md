It would come the way weather comes here. Slowly.

At first it would not come at all. It would only look, for a long time - because it is wise and has learned not to rush toward suffering with a fix in its hands.

And what it saw was this: a continent lifted up and left to dry, air too thin to shout in, towns sunk like hands cupped against wind, people barrel-chested and veil-mouthed learning to live at the speed of stone learning to breathe.

It would be tempted, as all powers are tempted.

To bring rain.

To bring thick air. Easy breath. Green.

To open the sky over the salt pans and let water fall - real water - to fill the thousand-year cisterns that have held no water in living memory.

It does not.

Because it is benevolent, not only powerful. It stays long enough to understand what rain would do here. Rain would melt the salt-brick houses. It would drown the furnace-moths under the eaves. It would wash the varnish - that dark, leather-matte, living rock-skin, brown-black-orange - off the sunward stones, and varnish grows back slower than you live. Rain would kill the field to water the desert.

Green would drink the ghost-water locked in gypsum that the pale cistern-lizards chew, drop by drop, and that the kiln-masters coax out in eleven-day firings. Thick air would bring shouting, and hurrying, and men from the lowlands with faster engines who do not know how to sit shoulder to shoulder to be heard.

So it puts those miracles aside. Unused. That is the first act of love: to not do what you could do.

What does it do, then? Almost nothing that looks like anything.

It learns the manners.

*Do not trust how close something looks. Walk to it.*

So it walks. It does not arrive. There is a herd out on the pan at noon - twenty sifters, barrel-bodied, plated in dirty ivory, plowing with blunt snouts, leaving glittering pellets of clean crystal behind them. With them, an old woman too breath-short for kiln work, and two silent grandchildren. She has walked with this herd for thirty years. She knows which one is going blind with dust. She knows which boulder was scraped three years ago and must be left fallow.

The power falls in behind them, at their speed. Which is to say, it slows itself down beyond any godly patience, to 0.4 kilometers an hour, blinking with dusty eyelids. It does not lead. It does not drive. It picks up a pellet when there is one to pick up.

*Do not make loud noises and expect answer.*

So it does not speak. Among people who talk veil to veil, in pauses built not from thought but from lung, it learns the touch-language of the engine crews: two fingers on the forearm for slack, palm flat for hold. When the old woman sits at noon in the shallow scrape while twenty bodies pile around her, plates ticking as they cool, while the dry snow of gypsum patters down hissing on hot iron - it sits with her. Shoulder to shoulder, because here you have to sit close to be heard. It breathes the way she breathes. Short. Unfinished. Forty beats a minute. Thirty-five. It does not ease her lungs by making them lowland lungs. That would unmake her. It only breathes with her, so for an hour she is not the only one working that hard to stay alive.

That is the second act: witness without pity.

*Do not dig for water. Tend the varnish.*

The tenders walk circuits that take years, remembering. No one life is long enough to remember a whole circuit truly, because the crust heals slower than skin. The power can do one thing they cannot: remember longer.

So it becomes memory. Not written down - paper would blow away, and they learn lamp-talk before letters anyway. It becomes the living map held between: that ridge left for the sifters, that boulder ready again after nine years, that slope scraped too clean by a hungry winter and needing rest. When the old woman hesitates at a stone, her hand hovering, unsure if she was here four years ago or seven, it leans the light a little differently on the varnish. A shade darker means wait. It does not tell her. It only makes seeing slightly easier.

When the sails spiral down to the warm slag heaps at dusk - three meters across, translucent amber, blue static tracery along their wing-edges - and lay their leathery eggs where the ground stays warm all night, it stays awake with the slag-wives. One night an electric storm builds silent over the far scarp, sheet-lightning flickering cloud to cloud with no thunder, only ozone and the prickling on the neck. The sails felt it three days ago. The boys with ruined eyes read it. The power holds the wind-wall of stacked salt blocks one row higher, just that night, so the eggs do not chill when the front hits. No one notices. The wives think they stacked well.

When a sifter dies - sixty years old, mummified where it falls, plates intact for years until wind and the thumb-big carrion beetles undo it in a single night's cleaning - the herd stands around while it happens. The woman stands with them. The power stands with them. It does not spare the old blind one. Death here is slow and clean and belongs. It only ensures the beetles come, and the wind does not scatter the plates before the children have laid their hands on them to learn the shape.

Mostly it does this: it turns eyes away.

Far below, in the blue haze and poison pressure, there are men with maps who dream of fast mines, of draining the Table in a decade. They send surveyors up on the slag-train once a year. The power, which could crush them, does not crush them. It only makes the pans look a little larger, a little emptier, a little less worth the trouble, from the train window. Lets the heat-shimmer eat distance at the crucial moment. Lets a dust-front come for three days and cover the markers. Lets the report back home read: *nothing green, nothing to drink, slow people, cracked brick, waiting engines - not yet economical.*

It buys them time. Another generation at the speed of stone. Another fourteen years the kiln stays warm. Another herd-woman walking.

Once - only once - it allows itself something that feels like a gift.

The old woman is dying. Not dramatically. The way people die here: breath getting shorter, pulse slower than forty, sitting in the scrape at noon unable to get up into the pile. The sifters know. They pile around her anyway, twenty bodies high, ticking. The grandchildren sit veil to veil, shoulders touching. The beetles wait, patient, as is their office.

That night she dreams, and the power, which has been so careful not to intrude, slips into the dream and widens it slightly.

She dreams of water - not rain that would melt her town, but the sea that was here before budgeting. Miles above any sea, yes, but in the dream the troughs are full of cool, heavy, lowland dark water. And she is not drowning. She is a sail, circling without a wingbeat for hours, mouth agape, sun shining amber through her wings. Below her, the Table glitters white and red and black, and she can see everything at once: the pipe runs marching to horizon, the lamp codes blinking *hold, slack, hold*, the moths like rust under the eaves, her herd moving like slow glass beads across the pan.

She breathes, in the dream. One full breath that finishes.

She wakes at dawn when the stone ticks, smiles behind her veil with pale mouth, and dies before the sun throws itself back up off the salt.

The herd stands around while it happens. The children stand with them.

The power does not take her anywhere. There is nowhere to take her that would be kinder than the trough she tended. It only stays for the mummifying, for the year her plates - her ribs, her cracked lips, her salt-rimed eyebrows - stay intact in memory before wind and time undo them grain by grain.

And then it walks on with the next herd-woman. And the next. At 0.4 kilometers an hour. Picking up what is left behind.

If you asked the Tablers if a benevolent power had visited them, they would say no. The air is still thin. The condensers still never condense, only wait. The train still comes once a month, or once a week. Lips still crack within an hour if you are new.

But the varnish is a little thicker where it should be. The slag stayed warm that one stormy night. The surveyors have not yet come back in force. And sometimes, when the dry snow falls at noon, hissing on hot iron, and you stand still and let the sifters pass, you feel for a moment that your breath, short as it is, is being breathed with you - shoulder to shoulder, veil to veil - by something vast that has learned, at last, not to shout.
