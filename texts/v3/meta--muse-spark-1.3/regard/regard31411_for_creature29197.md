It would not come down like a god.

That's the first thing. No light from a crack in the sky, no voice. It would come down the way you did — without noticing it had descended. Because this place is too old and too wet for entrances.

The rim is only knee-high. A benevolent and wise power would step over it carefully, so as not to scuff the grass, and feel its own feet go *suck-squelch* and be glad of it. It would get wet immediately. It would let its hem drag in brown water. It would not stay dry to stay clean.

It would stand ankle-deep in the center and do what everyone does at first: miscount.

Ah, it might think. Villagers. A hamlet. Twelve towers. Some frogs.

Then it would stay past the first hour, and let the count come undone, on purpose. That is what wisdom is here: staying long enough to be corrected.

It would put its palm flat to a fluted trunk and feel the 50-hertz hum, and not flinch at being read. The leaf-windows would stay clear at first — stranger — and it would not force them dark. It would just breathe, and let its breath be taken as news. Here is my CO2. Here is my salt. I am not from here, but I am made of weather too.

It would understand very quickly what not to do.

It would not dry anything. It would not offer to pave the marsh, to fix the paths, to make the map stay still for convenience. It would not straighten a tower, or stop a crown from collapsing, or tell the man with the singing knife a more efficient way to prune. It would not tell the mother that green under her baby’s nails is wrong, or wash it clean. It would not separate plant from animal from person to count them properly. It would not finish anything.

Because to finish here would be to kill. To dry here would be to kill. To sort here would be to kill.

What would it do, then, if anything?

It would do small, removable things. Things that can be refused.

It would sit, first. At market-porch height, in shade, where the towers exhale at late afternoon. It would let the great leaves lean toward it for shade, and accept the cool, and say thank you in the currency of this place: exhale. It would let jewel leeches test its ankles and let children pluck them off and trade them, laughing, because even a god’s blood is antiseptic to someone. It would buy fruit that exhales lightning-fog and eat it with wet hands, dripping. It would listen to the gourd-radio crackle weather-song and not tune it.

It would learn to step only on the stepping-bladders the moss offers, gently. Step gently and be held. Step rude and sink to the knee while the frogs scold you. It would accept the scolding. A wise power loves to be scolded by frogs. It means the system still has rules larger than you.

When the limb falls — and it will, water-heavy, with a crack like a sail tearing, exploding into beetles and parrots and white spray — it would not catch it mid-air to prove it could. It would stand back with everyone else, get splashed, and then sit on the fallen trunk with the vendors who have already moved their mats onto it. It would admire how fast electric-blue mycelium films the wound. It would understand: death here is just fast reassignment. No rescue needed.

If it helped at all, it would help the way the black tetra-seeds help.

Someone told you they fell from orbit years ago and just kept growing. Nobody maintains them. They maintain us.

That was probably It, once before. Or someone like It. A warm glassy seed, dropped, that does not command but unfolds — *if* a tendril wants to follow. A lattice, not a law. In the attics, old ones click as they cool at night like crickets made of glass. That’s the sound a wise gift makes when it’s not being used: content to wait.

So it would leave more of those. Not many. A few warm tetrahedrons drifting down through steam, landing soft in moss. Some will be ignored and go cold. Some will be stripped by children for orchids. One might unfold for a new bridge-anchor where the woman is whispering copper-nano thread forward a handspan. If she wants it. If the tower wants it. If the frogs don’t object.

And it would do one large, invisible thing, with its back to the town so as not to frighten anyone.

It would keep the rim low.

Outside this bowl the world wants to drain, to log, to study, to dry into data. Satellites want a clear picture. Developers want peat for fuel, towers for timber, hum for silence. The power would stand at the circle of mist where the world ends and be mist itself for a while. Thicker. Kinder. Uninteresting from above. Just another warm wet saucer, post-rain, always post-rain, not worth the boots. It would turn the mapmakers’ pencils aside, not by force but by boredom. Nothing to see here. Only mud breathing. Only twelve minutes rim to rim.

Inside, everything turbulent, hyperactive, remaking itself. Outside, nothing to see.

That is benevolence: to guard the conditions for negotiation without entering the negotiation.

By late afternoon, when the towers exhale in unison and the mist rises and hits the low rim and falls back as internal rain, it would still be there, dripping, beaded, hair plastered. Lantern-fruits blinking on. Bridges full of silhouettes with baskets on heads. Ground breathing like lungs under its feet. The mossy sloth still coming down the cable-vine — it has been coming all morning, ferns fruiting in its fur — and it would not hurry the sloth. It would blink back with great patient eyes.

No one here is finished. No one here is dry. No one here is alone in their skin.

It would not bless them from above. It would stoop until a tower could read its sweat and decide it knows the household. Until a leaf-window polarizes dark for it, not because it commanded, but because it stayed.

Then it would go, the way mist goes. Without a departure. Leaving the water a little higher, the air a little thicker, the seeds a little warmer. Leaving everything exactly as unfinished as it found it, only more certain that unfinished is allowed to continue.

If you asked the children the next day did a wise power visit, they would shrug: house wanted it that way.
