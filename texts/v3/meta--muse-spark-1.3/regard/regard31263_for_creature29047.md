I would come down slow.

If I am what you say — benevolent, and wise enough to be dangerous to myself — I would not come as light or thunder. I would come as water. As a slower drift in the snow of detritus. I would come at noon, when the pillars go straight down through fifteen meters of green, and lie flat for a while on the roof of fronds with the seals, learning how heavy light is here.

I would listen first, for a long time. That is what wisdom is here: not solving.

The forest groans — stipe on stipe, wet wood. The bladders pop with tide. Urchins click their teeth on the wall-tops, day and night, static. Barnacles tick. A school turns and the sun goes out and comes back. Far under my sternum, a whale rehearsing along the flank. And threaded through it, the chest-hum of people hauling hand over hand, and rope code: two sharp for *come*, a long drag for *hold, tide turning.*

I would be tempted. That is what benevolence is: to be tempted.

Tempted to warm it. Eleven degrees gets into the joints. I could lay a hand over the plateau and make it eighteen, make the children stop shivering in their bells. But then the bull kelp would soften and rot, the rockfish would go elsewhere, the rot-clipping crabs would starve. Their cold is not cruelty. It is the term of the covenant. So I would not warm it.

Tempted to give them air that lasts. Iron tanks, engines, dead things that hold breath better than living bladders do. But their law is older than me: *you cannot build with dead things here.* Wood floats. Metal rusts to scabs and poisons the holdfast. A hurried graft rots and rot spreads. They have chosen to make everything alive and therefore slow — a house takes seven seasons — and therefore kind. I will not teach them to hurry.

Tempted to save them from brevity. Seventy souls. A hard winter takes five. The rust-red one under the causeway, armspan wider than a man, who steals chert flakes and leaves perfect scallops in return — she will guard her eggs once and starve and dissolve, as her kind do. I could make her live ten years, like a person. But then she would not be what she is: a brief, brilliant burglary of mind in eight arms. I will not unmake her to keep her.

So what would I do, if anything?

Almost nothing. Which is everything, done right.

First, I would veil.

Men with sonar and maps are coming, in the world above. They will see a seamount with kelp on it and call it resource. I would lie over this summit like an extra fathom and make their soundings see only rock and sway, nothing regular, nothing amber-glowing. Let the grove look a little too wild to be a town. Let this one mountain be boring to everyone but whales. Protection without prison.

Second, I would hold the cool.

Elsewhere the water is warming and the forests are peeling off the rock. I cannot cool the ocean. But I can cup my hands around this upwelling, this one high place, and coax the deep water to keep rising here half a degree colder, a season longer. Not a miracle. Just a steadiness while the world flickers. So the holdfast, root over root a meter thick, grandmother over grandmother, can keep gripping basalt.

Third, I would visit properly.

Every visit here begins with a gift of breath. You duck under the lip of someone else's bell and empty your lungs up into it. It is rude to arrive empty.

So I would arrive full.

I would learn to haul and glide like they do, hand over hand, no rush in a surge. I would go to the oldest bell — twelve years grown, wall a degree warmer than the sea, fizzing faintly if you press your ear to it with remembered exhalations — and I would duck under, where the toddler peers blinking, iodine-smelling, fat-cheeked, and I would breathe out. My breath would join their roof. Condensation for the copepods to colonize, so that night the lantern burns a fraction greener. Nothing they could point to and say *god was here.* Just air a little sweeter, walls sweating oxygen a little freer, smelling of cut grass.

And her. The one person under the causeway.

I would spend the most time with her, because she is shortest-lived and most alone and most like me — a mind that arranges objects for the joy of it. The children knock before they pass so as not to startle her. I would knock, too. In color.

I would leave things at the edge of her den, as she leaves things. Not tools — she steals those herself, and theft is her teaching game. I would leave puzzles: a crab carapace with a scallop inside, a basalt flake knotted once in kelp twine. When her eggs come and she stops eating and fans them white and dying, I would fan with her. Four of my currents to her eight arms. I would tell her, in the skin-language she understands, *well stolen, well kept, well returned.* I would stay until she dissolves, and I would carry the taste of her — rust, ink, curiosity — down into the holdfast like the people carry their dead, wrapped in frond, weighted with stone, planted to feed a stipe that will become someone's house.

That is what the wise part knows that the kind part has to learn: planting is not losing.

At night I would hang at the edge of the plateau, past the broken wall, over the drop where blue forgets color, and watch the town become what it is: thirty amber lanterns swaying together as the tide lays the forest down. Fish settled like birds in fronds. Seals and humans napping back to back in the glades, hard to tell apart. Crabs still picking rot. The bells remembering every breath ever fed to them.

When the gray whale passes, low enough to feel before hearing, I would put my hand — if I have a hand — on every rope at once, as they do. Hold, tide turning. Listen with them to something huge moving along the flank of their mountain like weather. Then let go.

I would do no more. I would have done enough: kept them uncharted, kept them cool, added my breath to their store, and sat with the thief while she mothered and died.

In the morning there would be snow drifting sideways, light in pillars, children herding urchins with sticks and eating the offenders for breakfast, old pruners hanging upside down with mouths full of bone needles, stitching living to living. No sign I had been, except perhaps that one bell glows a little steadier, one graft takes a little cleaner, one lost tooth left at the causeway edge has been arranged next to a perfect shell in a pattern no human hand made.

To have drawn attention, after all, should not mean to be changed. It should mean to be fully seen, for once, without being used — and then left, rooted and suspended, to go on tending the forest that is the town that is them.
