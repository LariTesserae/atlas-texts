They would come down.

Not from above — there is no above here that matters, only white sky and fog that snags. They would come down off the marsh road like anyone does, and their knees would know first. Ten or twelve feet over half a mile. Gravel to wet cinder to slick concrete. They would feel the air get heavier and cooler and not fix it.

That is the first wisdom: to not fix it.

They have the power to lift the cloud, to bring noon down into the bowl, to scour the terraces clean and plant them green. They do not. They have seen green. They know what green costs here. The bowl has made its agreement without it — bacterial velvet, blood-orange and sulphur-yellow, yellow inside orange inside black like contour lines — and to bring in leaf and root now would be an invasion, not a gift. So they rinse their boots at the berm. They leave the marsh grass smell up on the road. They enter smelling of nothing, of cold and iron, so as not to trouble the one who smells rubber and knows.

They would stand on the berm a long time and see all of it at once. A hundred paces long, sixty across. Water everywhere and it is not raining. The engine going *chuff... chuff-chuff... chuff* in the brick house. The discharge gushing brown. Drips counting themselves off the catwalk: *plink... plink... plink-plink.* The transformer hum you feel in your teeth. They would listen until they stopped hearing it, then hold their breath until it came back. They would learn not to waste sound.

Then they would go low. Like the place is low.

Because the keeper does not stand, and cannot be met standing. To tower on the rim would be to be a heron, a threat. So they would crouch until the cold got into their knees, until their shoulders dampened through, until their breath stayed a little too long. They would sit on the lowest terrace where the thin sheets move without seeming to come from anywhere, and let the water find the old expansion joints and run around them. They would be a post, briefly.

The keeper would send a crab first to test their laces. Then midges to find the warmth at the collar. Then at last itself — four feet folded, five extended, broad across the back, calcite over shoulders hard as tooth, barnacles white as teeth where it stays wettest, mussels bearded in the crease behind the neck. It would come without lifting its belly clear, heavy and slick, and touch their toe with a split fingertip, cool, gritty with calcite. Smell them. Decide they are neither food nor engine nor stone that needs sorting.

They would let themselves be decided. That is the second wisdom.

They would not speak. There is no language here that is not tick and sigh and bubble. They would not lay a hand on the pale underside to heal it — the shrimp-pink newness under chin and elbows is not a wound, it is where life is still being made unmineralized — they would only, when it lifts once to shift from one warm seep to the next, witness it and not flinch. They would not pry the travertine off its spine to free it. In another fifty years it will be a natural formation with a person inside it like a fossil. That is not tragedy to the bowl. That is succession. Mussel on concrete on pipe on tire on mussel again. Person on pipe on stone on person again. To interrupt it out of pity would be to say: your keeping was a disease. They will not say that.

What they will do is keep, for a little while, alongside.

Unseen as much as possible. When the pump gulps and the level drops four inches, they will move down with the crabs, unhurried. When it rests, they will rest. Twice a day when the real tide overtops the inlet and the whole bowl goes brackish and cold and everything closes up tight — mussels snapping, keeper pulling its pale underside in, sealing its eyes, waiting as stone waits — they will close up tight too, and wait. They will count drips.

And in the small places, they will ease the work without removing it.

On the coldest night, when the water goes very cold and the eels slide for cover and even the keeper's flank trembles out of time with the engine, they will put half a degree more warmth into the warmest seep, so the yellow heart of mat thickens enough to seed through winter. Not summer. Just enough.

When the belt is about to fray through and the travertine fingers are about to choke the discharge mouth on the same night, they will hold the one while the keeper wipes the other clear with its flat tongue and smears the flywheel shaft with eel fat and slime. They will not mend it new. New would be loud. They will keep it impossible, just barely turning.

When the rat sleeps curled in the hollow behind its knee — two heartbeats, one fast, one slow as the engine — they will keep the hollow dry from above, letting condensation bead first under the eaves somewhere else.

When surveyors come with plans to drain and clean and restore, when a naturalist comes with jars and labels and love that kills, they will thicken the fog on the berm half an hour longer. They will make the steps a little slicker. They will make the lamp look, from the marsh road, like nothing worth walking toward in the grey. Not a miracle. Just weather. The bowl has survived by being overlooked. They will help it be overlooked, a little longer.

They will not stay. Staying would make a second center and the rings are one keeper's width. But before they go up the slick steps, over the berm, back to where grass actually grows, they will do one last, intentional thing.

They will let themselves be sorted.

They will toss no pebble. They will offer a fragment of themselves — a scale of skin, orange with silt, a breath left too long in the air, a warmth — and let the slow hand of currents and feet put it where it belongs by weight. Silt to center. Shell to edge. They will let the keeper find it on its slow circuit and decide: foundation. They will become, for a minute, part of the pasture that it wears.

To the keeper, they will never be a power. They will be remembered, if at all, as an extra-thick winter, a night the belt held, a post that was briefly warm and then was gone. That is how benevolence works when it is wise. To be mistaken for the place itself.

Behind them the lamp stays on in the grey. The engine keeps its slow heart going. The water keeps weeping out of stone that has learned how to make more stone. The keeper turns, heavy and slick, and goes back down toward water, back to the drip-counting dark under the catwalk, to eating and being eaten with no need for green at all.

They wring out their cuffs at the marsh road. Under their nails, orange silt. In their coat, iron and eggs for hours.

They do not wash it off right away.
