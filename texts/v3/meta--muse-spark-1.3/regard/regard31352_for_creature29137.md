It does not come down in light.

If it came the way powers are supposed to come, it would come at noon, when the shaft stands straight down the center and hits the sump water and turns white. But it is benevolent, and it is wise, and so it has learned already the first rule of the Pit: loud voices come back off the walls and embarrass you.

So it comes the other way. By heat, through the shoes.

It feels the laterite first, dry cracked and white-skied, thorn scrub doing its best. Then the ground stops. A mouth. And from the mouth a breath comes up, hot and plant-sweet and iron, that fogs — if powers can be said to fog — whatever it uses to see.

It does not descend all at once. That would be a violence here. It lowers itself the way the market platform lowers itself: one slow meter at a time, all morning. *Keh — ... — keh — ... — keh.* It times itself to the old Cornish pumps.

At the lip the houses jut like teeth. Laundry dripping condensation that never dries, only warms. A crow circles, looks down at the steam, thinks better of it. The power thinks better of circling. It goes in.

That means it learns to go *through*.

To reach the sump you must pass through eleven other people's parlors. The power, which could pass through walls, does not. It lifts the curtain. It steps around the sleeping grandmother. It crosses the kitchen where taro is boiling big as a child's torso. It nods thanks. It has no shoes, but at every third house it leaves what would be shoes — its size, its knowing-best, its urge to fix — and picks up cloth slippers instead. *Shush-shush* on warm wood. Privacy here is not doors. Privacy is agreement not to notice. The power is very good at not noticing, in the way only total attention can be.

It sits for a while in the half-light. Amber cage-bulbs, furnace-orange, green shade. Edges glow green and then go black. Glasses would fog instantly. It lets them.

It watches one of them, because to love a place you have to love one damp particular of it, not the idea of it.

A girl, seven or eight, serious and sleepy and bare-legged, sent out in the morning when the cloud gets caught at the third terrace down and cannot lift over the lip. She has a rag. That is her work, her sacrament. She wipes down the elephant-ear leaves big as doors, the washing lines, the tarps, and wrings into the chain of bottle-caps that tickles down into the cistern. *Tick. Tick. Tick.* Drips in cisterns. She knows which cold pipe sweats coldest, which black seep is still drinkable after boiling. Her skin shines. She does not wipe it. Her hair hangs lank. Her pupils are wide to catch the tired sideways light. She leaves wet prints that vanish in seconds.

The power watches her wring out the air to live, and understands everything an outsider would get wrong.

An outsider — even a kind one — would want to give her a river. A horizon. Dry clothes. Sun. Space. A street that is a street and not someone else's roof and parlor. Medicine for the heat-rash mapped on the men, for the lungs that have learned to chew. A pump that does not take all morning.

The power, being wise, does not want any of that. It has seen what forty years does. This cut was a wound, straight-sided, drill-marks still in it in rows, rebar teeth sticking out. Copper or coal, stories differ. A blasting bite straight down. And into that fresh cut the town poured itself until there was no room left, and then the green poured in after it, and now you cannot tell where rust ends and stem begins. Moss packed around the boiler housings so they hum instead of ring. Morning-glory thick as rope trained along hot pipes to cool them. Fig roots holding burst cisterns together better than mortar. A cable left alone furred, root-hairs drunk on its grease. They no longer paint things. They prune them.

You do not heal a healing by reopening it to put in a river.

So if anything — and it will do almost nothing, because almost nothing is what this place is made of — it does this:

It stays slow. It moves like sap.

When the pressure valve sighs and whites out a whole terrace with a plume, it pauses with everyone else. It does not wave the steam away.

When the sump — that scummed steaming eye no bigger than a courtyard — rises over months, it does not hold the water back to save the lowest family the trouble of carrying their bedding up. It helps carry, unseen, by making the load feel a half-kilo lighter on the stairs. When the sump falls, it is there for the sweeping of silt, which smells of sulfur and sweet-rot. No deeds. No hurry.

It keeps small continuities. It keeps the horizontal seam weeping, black but never quite becoming a stream, never quite stopping. It keeps one cold pipe cold enough to bead. It keeps the moss from cooking on the hottest housing. It steadies, for one more season, the vine that is also a handrail. Rust blooms over a season, not a night. A vine takes a wall in a year and gives it back in ten. The power has time.

Up on the plain, it does one other merciful thing: it keeps Marrowell uninteresting. It lets a prospector's map get damp and stick together. It lets a developer's drone see only steam and think *nothing there*. It lets the crow tell the others there is nothing worth circling for. Obscurity, here, is mercy. Attention, even benevolent attention, is heavy. So it rations its own.

And once in a while, when a storm passes over, it lets rain fall straight in, sudden and hissing on hot roofs, ten minutes of waterfalls off every catwalk. And with it, if it can manage without frightening anyone, another heron. Not as a sign. Just as a visitor who stayed three days once while the whole Pit brought it scraps and whispered, and then lifted out. *It stayed. It left.* Theology enough. The children need to see that leaving is possible and staying is also possible, and both can be loved.

At dusk the power goes to a roof-terrace near the middle with the others. The cage-bulbs click on one by one down the spiral. The shaft of sky above goes violet. Below, the steam glows from underneath where the geothermal seam breathes. Hundreds of glistening faces come out barefoot on warm wood — blasters with drill-scars still in their knuckles, winch-knitters with yarn stuck to their forearms, vine-girls with nails gone green at the cuticle, boiler-tenders with blind white lizards clinging to their warm backs — all looking not at it but at the air lifting.

The whole packed, sweating, green-rusted hamlet breathes out once — a long hot exhalation that lifts the hanging cloud a meter and lets it settle again — and holds.

The power breathes out with it.

That is what it does, if anything. It learns to sweat and not wipe it. It leaves its shoes at every third house. It agrees, for as long as they need it, not to notice, which is to say, to notice completely and change nothing except to hold, very lightly, the knowledge that they are there, and that their slow, quiet tending of iron into leaf is holy, and that they do not need to be lifted out of the throat to be saved.

It holds them the way moss holds a boiler: to deaden, to seal, to keep it from ringing.

And when it finally lifts — the way the heron lifted — no one sees it go, which is proper. Only the rag-girl pauses for a second on her roof with her wrung rag dripping, feeling the air a degree cooler on her forearms, the cloud a little fuller over her head, and she wrings once more and gets more water than she expected, and smiles without showing teeth, and goes in to tell her mother the cistern will be full by noon.
