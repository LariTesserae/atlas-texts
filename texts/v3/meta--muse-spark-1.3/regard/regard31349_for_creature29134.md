They would not come shining.

If a thing that large and kind noticed this — a shallow trough an hour across that still remembers being seabed, and the low green thing drowning its sharp rock to heal it — it would know better than to arrive as light.

White blinds the swarms.

So it would come low. Duckboard-low. Boot-level low.

It would come down after the black ridge where the new stone starts, the way you did, and it would make itself narrow enough to stay on the planks. That is the first wisdom: to take a shape that can be careful here. Thirty meters tall would be rude. Thirty meters tall would break the wind wrong and scare the calves to the middle again. So — human-sized, or goose-sized, or midge-sized. Something that can be landed on.

It would not look first at the geese. Everyone looks at the geese. It would look down, amber, not white.

At your boots, water welling up. Under it, hummock on hummock, sphagnum and liverwort a foot thick, black-green and bottle-green rimed with ice that never melts. Dwarf willow knotted tight as fists. Sedge so dense the water has to go around. Fungi soft as ears. It would see what lives here, not what is passing through. And it would recognize it immediately, because it is also that kind of thing: not one animal but a knitting. Moss and mineral-flour and bright dust and thin red-lit pole, all holding each other up.

Second wisdom: do not hurry a wound closing.

It would be very tempted — any kind thing with power is tempted — to warm it all at once. To lift the cloud off its eyebrows. To still the beat-frequency between the shepherds that turns stomachs. To make the outwash roar a little less so the fish-counter can sleep. To turn the stakes from yellow back to green and keep them there.

It will not.

It has watched enough young land to know the cold hasn't killed this. The dark hasn't killed this. The turbulence *is* the work. The tick and boom of new ice prying the sharp stones a millimeter further apart is how the sharp gets un-sharped. The rot and guano and crushed green is food, not insult. If you took the salt still in the rock out too fast, the moss would have nothing to learn against. If you kept the geese from trampling, the hollows wouldn't press down into pools. If you made it day, the rimed tips would dry and die.

So it does almost nothing that looks like power.

It does what the old men do. What the valley already taught you: you don't fight the turbulence. You time it.

It crosses between gusts. It fixes no plank while the reindeer are still passing. It keeps its hood off one ear so it can hear the change in pitch that means a new channel opening behind it. It breathes out and lets its breath not dissipate, but join it — the air that sweats, the rocks that weep.

What it *does* do, it does at moss-scale:

It stands a little longer under the seventh shepherd where the circle is thinnest, and lends it a degree. Not enough to be a miracle. Just enough that the frost doesn't settle there tonight, enough that the sedge stays supple. Snow melts off no shoulders because it has no shoulders, but the motes come — bright as midges, that are not midges — and taste its warmth and go back to work a little faster. It lets them land on it. It lets them take the salt sweating out of it. It does not brush them off its glove.

It kneels — if kneeling is a thing it can do — by a fresh crack that steams, where the strings go down slow and come up slower carrying mineral flour, and it steadies the edge with something like a hand so the string doesn't break in the wind. It does not seal the crack for them. It holds the wind for three seconds while they seal it themselves. That matters. Being helped without being replaced is how a young thing stays willing.

It sits, unlit, in the sodded hut with the light off while the fish-counter marks numbers on a waterproof slate and talks to the swarm like pigeons. It does not answer in words. It hangs around because he stands still and steams, and being stood-still-with is already an answer. It keeps his tea warm half a minute longer than physics allows. He will not notice. His shoulders will drop a quarter inch. That is enough.

For the visitors — because benevolence is not only for residents — it keeps time open. A gust held back an extra breath so the thousand wings can lift without tearing. A side-rill dropped an inch so the black backs cutting V's can force themselves up without beaching. A vixen's trot undisturbed on the boardwalk. Lemmings rustling undisturbed under it. It does not stop the terns from leaving late, or the calves from being nervous. It only makes sure the crossing stays crossable until true freeze. Everyone trying to eat and move and spawn and leave before the lock. It honors the trying.

And for the humans coming down with white headlamps and boots that want to step off: it does the smallest, most godlike thing. It reminds.

Not with a voice. With a pressure in the molars first, then in the chest, if they get too close to a base with a tongue or a torch. A held note below hearing that says: *amber. keep moving. stay on the boards. you go to your knee and you crush ten years.* It borrows the shepherds' bass for a moment and hums slightly out of sync with them, the way they hum out of sync with each other. Nobody will call it intervention. The maintenance crews will say less and replace a guy-wire and leave quickly between gusts. The kids will come back crying and not know why and never do it again. The tech stays weather.

Mostly, it witnesses.

This is the part no one believes power would be content to do: lie there all movement-night in wet that finds every gap, laces to wire, cheeks to wood, down sticking to wet sleeves, midges in nose and mouth, eyes never fully adjusting because there is never quite enough to adjust to, deafened and half-frozen and jostled — and pay the exact kind of attention the valley is paying to itself.

Leaf by leaf. Frost bead by frost bead laid back down as film. Rootlet by rootlet bound and tamped where the hummock slumps.

It does not say *you are beautiful*. The moss would not understand the grammar. It says it the way the valley says things: by staying supple around it. By not stepping off.

By morning — which will not be morning for a while yet, only a slightly less black blue-grey — when the storm that settled in to spin slowly finally shifts and flings sleet again, it will leave no footprint in the moss. Only this:

Seven warm circles still warm. A crack sealed a millimeter tighter. A slate with numbers a little truer. Forty reindeer across safe. A raft of geese settled, arguing, alive one more night.

And underneath, through feet and knees over the white roar, the hum a half-tone fuller, as if something large had lain down alongside the thin living skin over water and hummed with it while it healed, and then gotten up quietly and left the boards clear for the next crossing.
