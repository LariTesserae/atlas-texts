It is too bright at first, even for Her to see it.

She comes up over the rim the way you did — not all at once, not in glory — and the high light has weight for Her too. She squints. She lets Her nostrils stick. She lets Her forehead burn in cool air. She does not dim it to make it kinder. She does not turn it down.

That is the first thing wisdom does: it does not adjust the world to suit its eyes.

She stands a long time and listens until Her sternum buzzes.

She understands, faster than you did, that this is not empty. That this is not a place with a being on it. That there is no *place* and then *inhabitant*. There is one vast, dry, roaring body that learned how to stand up in places to talk to the wind, and lie down in other places to be floor, and fly in other places to carry its seed, and walk in other places to mow itself.

The Mat. The Towers. The grazers. The clingers. The skyfish. The copper tenders. Organs, not neighbors.

She gets it immediately: water is forbidden here. To bring rain would be to kill.

So She does not bring rain.

That is what a lesser kindness would do. A lesser power, meaning well, would look at cracked lips and static and noon detonations and daily white-out storms and think: *these poor things are thirsty. These poor things are deafened. These poor things die cured in the sun.* It would bring water. It would bring quiet. It would bring mercy as lowlanders understand mercy.

She does not.

She kneels — if kneeling is a thing She can do — and puts Her palm flat to the felt, and feels the slow peristalsis a kilometer deep, the continent bracing against its own weather. She feels the faint telegraph clicking of ten thousand copper legs through rhizome. She lets a blue spark snap from a spine-tip to Her finger and does not flinch. It smells like bread and lightning.

She thinks: *To drink would be to rot.* She honors that chemistry.

For a hundred years of your time, which for Her is an afternoon and for the Mat is a breath, She does almost nothing.

She does not still a Tower's chuff to give the grazers peace. The chuff *is* their lullaby; their bellows evolved to sing under it. Silence them and you orphan them.

She does not straighten a leaning Tower to save it from toppling. She knows toppling is not failure. It is how a Tower becomes a ridge, becomes a nursery, becomes sweet thin Mat where the herds go first. To prevent the crash like a shipwreck in the night would be to steal the nurseries from the future.

She does not gather the windrows of pale ingots into barns, does not improve the seed-mills, does not oil the ten-ton groan of fiber on wax. The inefficiency, the spillage, the tons of down ripped up and redeposited for miles — that waste is the food, the nest, the soil, the spark. The factory has no builders because it doesn't need efficiency. It needs extravagance.

She does not speak to them. Not in words.

The Mat thinks at the speed of rhizome-tightening. A thought takes a season to cross a basin. The Towers think at the speed of sway. The copper tenders think all at once, a ripple passing through ten thousand bodies turning. If She spoke — *I love you, you are wonderful, be well* — it would arrive as noise, as one more shout lost under the organ-pipe roar.

So She learns to speak slower.

She lies down with the grazers when the daily storm is born twenty miles out, white turning grey turning towering. She does not stop the lint-wall. She lies down and faces it, rump to the anchors, head low, breathes through cloth. She feels the Mat ripple under Her like skin. She lets Herself white-out, blind, full of mouth-drying dust. She lets the wax-frost settle on Her and glitter, and sublimate by evening. She does not watch from above.

When a Tower falls at night five miles from any camp, She is there. Not to catch it. No one can catch a cathedral. She is there so the crash is witnessed through the Mat, held, not just recorded as vibration. She stays while the white rash of new anchors sprouts by morning. She puts something like a hand on the long low ridge and says in rhizome-time, in static, in pressure: *good. good making.*

When a grazer snaps a leg in a tear and lies down to cure in an afternoon to jerky and hair, She does not heal it back to walking. Healing would be a refusal of its becoming-Mat. She stays with it while it dries, while lichen covers it, while it sinks a hand's breadth. Company, not rescue. That is the hardest benevolence to practice: to not fix what is not broken, even when it hurts to watch.

And when the needle-beaks quarrel on the windward trunk, when the skyfish fold themselves like pale fruit into the leeward bracts to ride out shredding fluff, when a head detonates at noon in hard light with a pop you hear five miles out and the black oilseeds rise instead of falling — She laughs. Quietly, under the roar. Delight is also a form of attention.

What does She actually *do*, this benevolent and wise power, if anything?

Three refusals, and one act.

She refuses to make it green.

She refuses to make it quiet.

She refuses to make it last. Every Tower will fall. Every grazer will cure. Every sail will fray. She will not grant lowland immortality to a place whose holiness is unraveling within miles into food and nest and spark.

And the act:

She draws a veil, not around it, but around the minds that would come for it.

For She sees, past the rim, the other kind of hunger approaching — the ones who would love the oilseeds for fuel, the bast rope for cable, the static for power, the windrows for harvest. Who would call this empty because from the rim it looks like desert. Who would build a road to count the Towers better. Who would mean well and bring water.

She bends the maps a fraction. She lets satellites glitch over that tableland. Lets prospectuses misplace a decimal, lets the eye slide past. Lets the place remain untouched in the way a heart is untouched — not because no one could touch it, but because those who would, forget to look twice.

Not a wall. A forgetting. A little coolness of interest, a little dust on the lens. Enough time for the Roaring to go on roaring without having to explain itself.

Then She leaves no trace. No shrine, no cairn — there were none before, there will be none after. No change in the wind. Only, for an hour after a lightning strike, the spore-things bloom a little whiter where She knelt, smelling of bread, and then they too cure.

That is what perfect attention does when it loves something that is already perfect in its tilt:

It takes off its shoes because the floor is alive.

It lies down when the storm comes.

It does not bring water to a desert that is a jungle.

It witnesses the fall, and calls the ridge good, and goes, leaving the noise intact.
