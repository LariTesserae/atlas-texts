It would not bring rain.

That is the first temptation, and the first refusal. Any god worthy of the name arrives with hands full of what it thinks is mercy. Water. Warmth. A louder voice.

It arrives here at noon, when the light is absolute and does not warm, and it understands within an hour that mercy here would be murder.

To give water to those who have learned to live without it - whose blood is gold oil that does not freeze and does not evaporate, whose leaves are sealed like waxed envelopes - would be to poison them with kindness. To warm the air would be to steal their night-sweat, that molecular sigh of salt that coaxes two molecules of vapor from thin air. To make them grow faster, closer, lusher, would be to break the negotiation that spaces them hundreds of paces apart, solitude by solitude to the horizon.

So it does the harder thing. It cools itself. It thins itself. It quiets itself.

It takes off its boots.

For the lace fields in the lee of the whalebacks took fifty years to grow a hand high, and shatter under a boot into shards that are alive, but only if they land kindly. The power goes barefoot on pumice that rings faintly. It walks slowly, absurdly slowly, the way the sipper walks, because hurry is water and it has decided to spend none of theirs.

It chooses one of them. Not the grandest, not the strangest sail that creaks as it turns edge-on to the ten-thousand-year wind. An old dome, six meters across and waist-high, silvered outside, oil-green and almost black inside. Older than any human house. It has eaten its polygon year by year, trading acid for phosphorus, for iron, turning stone into scent - turpentine and lemon-resin - turning light piped down through hollow glass hairs into summer.

It lies down beside it.

This is what a wise benevolence does when it finds perfection that is not its own: it makes itself a student of the rules.

The first rule is noon and cold. So it does not dim the hard coin sun, does not thicken the air to hold heat. It seals itself at noon, as they do. It pulls its own vastness in and sits small.

The second rule is lush, spaced out. So it does not plant, does not gather, does not improve. When the afternoon heat slants and the ground gives back its little taken warmth and the dust pillars begin to walk between the cushions, combing them, it holds its breath. It watches the dome breathe - ten thousand stomata opening at once, that shimmer that is not heat lifting off, oil-coated spores buoyant as dust joining the high gold veil that dims the sun for an hour. It does not reach to catch one. It lets them fall back as tiny lights. Most land on stone and die. That is allowed. That is part of lush.

The third rule is quiet. So it does not speak. It does not call down. It listens to the high faint whistle of wind broken on glass hairs. To the ticking, if you press your ear to a large dome at dusk, as ten thousand inner leaves fold against the coming freeze. To the deep slow drawing when the sipper comes - tall and narrow as a folded umbrella, chalk-colored, fearless because fear is expensive - and buries its head up to the eyes to drink oil, and the dome weeps resin and closes the wound within minutes. The power does not shoo the sipper away. Does not heal the bite faster. Wounding and closing is their communion.

At night, when the stars come hard and salt needles lift stones a finger's width away from concentrated life, it does one thing, and only one thing.

It puts its hand, elbow-deep, into the thatch.

Inside it is animal-warm, humming with slow chemistry, summer in the middle of freezing. It does not take oil. It does not leave water - not a molecule, for its own breath is recaptured, cooled in imagined nostrils like the sipper's. It only lends its attention, which is a form of warmth that does not melt anything. For a long time - longer than you would measure - it keeps its hand there, feeling the dome digest stone and light, feeling itself digested in return into patience.

And then, because it is benevolent and not merely sentimental, and wise and not merely passive, it does the work that is actually needed, which is never on the plain itself.

It turns its face outward.

It sees the other eyes beginning to turn this way - the orbiters, the prospectors, the ones who would see gold oil that does not freeze and call it fuel, who would see empty polygons and call them empty. Who would bring rivers where there have never been rivers.

It does not smite them. It misdirects them, gently. A little more static in the dust here, so the grains hang motionless and lit and blind the lens. A little longer haze in the afternoon, so the survey reads only white to the horizon, shivering glare that is not heat. A dream planted in a geologist that there is nothing here worth the climb. A budget cut there. A wind, maintained the same way for the next ten thousand years, so the yardangs hold their fleets and the stones stay balanced for your grandchildren.

Guardianship by absence. Protection by leaving no path, no wall, no cairn.

At dawn, before the seals open, it withdraws its hand. On its palm is the scent - turpentine, lemon peel, hot resin - that will not evaporate. It keeps it. That is its payment and its memory.

It leaves the dome exactly as lush and alone as it found it, the young still leaning downwind of the old in their dust-shadow, the strainer still spiraling silent in its thermal above with mouth open to eat weather, filtering spores through combs of glass hair, dropping dry pellets that will feed what comes next.

It does not say: I love you. Love here would be too loud, too wet.

It says, in the language of this place which is chemistry and spacing and time: *Continue.*

And then it goes, barefoot, stepping between lace, leaving behind only a single line of prints that stops, as if the maker vanished - so that if you come later you will doubt it was ever here at all.
