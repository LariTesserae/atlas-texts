It would not come from high up.

That is the first mercy. There is nothing high here to come from, and anything that arrived shining would blind the whole bowl and scare it closed.

So it comes low, the way you came — down into it without quite noticing descending — only lower. It learns the level first. Knee-height, where the big mains run. Ankle-height, where the sheet runs over concrete. It does not stand over the sump and look down. It crouches beside the revetted timber, where the wood has gone soft as sponge, and puts its attention at water skin.

It has noticed because for years — decades — someone has been keeping thin water thin.

That is rare enough to be holy. Almost everything living wants to make its place lusher, deeper, warmer, more itself. This one does the opposite. It grazes down the reeds that would close it over. It keeps the bowl from becoming a pond though a pond would be easier. It pumps itself, pit to pit, to stay cool and aerated and hungry. It tends machines it cannot have built to keep water moving in a way that feeds iron bacteria that feed it. A freshwater sheet-animal farming rust. Patient. Almost entirely water. Doing it continuously, quietly, with many small wet hands you mistake for drips.

A wise power would see that and understand at once: this is not a broken marsh to be restored, not a polluted works to be cleaned.

It is a kept place. It has a keeper.

So first, it would do nothing that looks like help.

No draining. No filling. No scrubbing the olive and rust-red film off as if it were dirt. No replacing the rusted orange mains with bright plastic that never weeps — that would starve it. No pulling the birches straight, no planting willows for beauty, no stilling the pumps so it could "rest." Rest, for this one, would be death by pond: too still, too deep, too warm.

If you were watching you would hardly see it arrive at all. The flat cool noon light through high cloud would not change. A gull far over the marsh would still sound as if through wool. The transformer hut would still hum in your teeth.

It would simply be there a while, correctly.

It would step only where it is already firm and shining. Even a power obeys that here, because dull mud is not mud, it is a thicker piece of the body, holding on. It would not step there uninvited.

It would turn sideways between the clustered ship's wheels. It would not touch a wheel that is trembling. It knows that tremble means: I am about to move by myself. It would wait, the way the place teaches waiting — muffled, breath held — and listen under the lattice of tiny wet sounds, plink plink plink from three eaves out of sync, seep-hiss through gravel, tick of cooling metal, for the wetter click underneath the electric hum. Then it would watch the wheel turn a quarter-turn, slick and certain, with no hand on it. And it would nod, minutely. Seen. Done well.

Then it would let itself be tasted.

It would stand long enough under an eave to be dripped on, regularly as a clock, cold on the neck. It would not wipe it away. Salt. Heat. Whether you are firm and shining where it can cross, or dull where you will sink. This is how the being says hello, how it checks every visitor. Most wipe and move on. The wise one stands still and lets the second drop come. And the third. It offers a bare hand close *under* the pipe, without touching, where the air is cooler by a degree and the whole pipe wears a beard underneath and is almost dry on top. It lets a bead fatten until inside the clear you can see the thread, rust-orange, faint as tea.

*Yes,* it says without words. *I know what you eat. Eat.*

For a moment it would let the puddles be true. You know the lag — you lift your hand and your dark double lifts his a beat after, thicker at the edges, fringed, wearing your shape to look out. It would hold so still that for one breath the double catches up. No delay. Face to face, sheet to skin. To be seen, fully, and not flinch, is the rarest gift for a creature that survives by being mistaken for seep and condensation. It would give that.

And then, being benevolent, being practical, it would do a little maintenance — the kind no one will ever log.

Not a miracle. Just small continuances.

It would leave iron where iron is thinning. A flake loosened upstream to weep at the next flange. It would keep No. 2 pump catching when the float-switch clicks no louder than a tongue-click, that shudder up through the soles of the feet — its heart beating — so the vortex still forms and sucks and breaks with a soft *glup.* It would keep the sluice lifting by itself on its screw when leaves need carrying. It would keep the winters from freezing solid to the timbers and the summers from ever drying the moss on the north sides of things. The kind of cool that keeps things from rotting fast and from drying ever — it would hold that cool a half-degree longer into August.

It would keep the buckets full and clear. No leaves in them. Something drinks clear water. It would see to that.

And because it is wise, it knows the greatest danger is not cold or hunger but being noticed wrongly. By the outside with its flat grey-green fields and ditches always full. Someone, someday, will call this derelict and decide to tidy it: drain it, cap it, sample the ropy water to death, declare the rainbow oil-slicks that aren't oil a spill.

So it would do its most powerful work far from here, in offices and inattentions. A paperwork delay. A footpath diverted around the berm instead of through. A maintenance contract renewed for "flood control pumping" that no one quite understands but no one cancels. It would nudge the human who already comes — the one who left the grease-pencil mark three weeks ago, the lunch wrapper weighted with a nut, the footprints that come in, do a circuit, and leave — to keep coming. To linger a minute longer. To notice the rails are shining on top, as if polished by passage, and not report it. To replace a gasket and leave the algae alone. To learn, without being taught: do not expect silence to mean stillness.

That human will never know they were answered. That is fine. Benevolence does not need to be named.

Before it goes, it would kneel. All the way down to where condensation gathers heaviest on the undersides. Forehead almost to the beard of drops. Breath showing faintly when still. Letting the cool bite.

*Keep it thin,* it would say in the language of this place, which is not words but water behavior. A sheet that slows near the bottom of a quarry wall and creeps sideways, uphill, toward iron. A skin half a millimeter thick drawing itself to where it needs to be. Deliberate. Unhurried.

*Keep it moving. Keep it ironed. I see you keeping it.*

Then it would stand, shake the water from its sleeves the way you do, and leave footprints that come in, do a circuit, and leave. By the coal stage, by the fox print already filling with water, one more print will fill with water. The moorhen will pick its way on enormous feet along the rim ditch. Eels will move like slow thoughts in the sump. Midges will rise in a low column over the warm outflow where the cooling water comes back out almost hot and steams — its breath, visible for a second in the cool air.

Nothing will look different. That is how you will know it was here.

The bowl will still be wet in a way the fields outside are not. Water will still be not *in* a place but *on* everything. The belt will jerk, sag, resume. Mud will slump an inch and plop. A puddle mirror-calm will skin over with rain-rings from a drip above, though it is not raining.

Everything happening at once, just quietly.
