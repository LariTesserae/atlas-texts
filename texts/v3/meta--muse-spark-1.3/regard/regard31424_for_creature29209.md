She would come the way you came.

By ducking.

A benevolent and wise power would not tear the breakwater open. She would not lift the concrete lid and pour the shallow sunlit ocean in. She would find the hatch the size of a dinner plate at the low-tide line, unhook it, let a little sea in, and follow it down the throat of new concrete. She would make herself small enough for nine strides long, four wide, three tall. She would stay wet. She would stay submerged. There is no other way to enter without breaking it.

And she would keep the rules. Because they are good rules for so small a country.

She would hook her thumb into a cable-loop and let the cold take her fingers. She would not block a column for long. She would keep drifting, so the motes would not have to scaffold her. She would make no animal noise.

First she would just listen, through skull and water and suit, to how loud it is. The organ-thrum of the lung in the teeth. The champagne-hiss. The *click-click-click* of the shutters farming noon. The whine at the edge of hearing that makes fillings ache. Sound with nowhere to go.

She would not silence it. That loudness is the town breathing, working, staying alive for no residents. To take it away would be to take its purpose. But she would lay a hand — if she has hands then — on the pressure-frame, and retune, very slightly, the sterilizer's whine. A quarter-tone down, out of the range of ache. The larvae would still not settle. The crabs would still not come. The room would stay animal-empty, so the other kinds of full can happen. Only it would no longer hurt to keep it empty.

Then she would go to the Ribbon.

She is the reason for coming, though the town is worthy too. One kelp, seventeen feet of pale ribbon grown sideways because there is no up, tying herself in true bowlines to hold against the exhale. Iron-hungry. Thin as a watch-strap where she was born, wide as a palm where she learned to eat amber.

The wise power would know not to rescue her. Not to tear her free and plant her out in the real sunlit shallow cold where kelp are supposed to live. Out there she would be ordinary, and here she is the crowd, the only soft vertical in a world of hard horizontals, the one everything orients around. To "save" her by removal would be to unmake her.

So she would feed her, in place.

A taste of iron in the water. No more than that. A rusted nail dissolved at the hatch on the inhale, so the tide-lung carries it in braided, so it comes to her not as a flood but as weather. Enough for the new growth at the tip to come in darker, greener, less pale. Enough for her to know amber portholes are supplements, not suns. The power would hold the current slack for one exhale, one long slow breath, so the Ribbon would not have to strain at her bowlines, so her blades could lie out in the light-columns a full three seconds longer before the shutters click shut. Rest, for a plant that has never known slack water.

And she would speak to her in the only language with no nerves to misfire: light, and time.

The shutters — the Click — never rest. Inside the columns it is noon. Outside them it is bottle-green evening. There is no night here. Nothing ever sleeps. The paint stores light, the sleeping ledge in box 441 gets half a second of noon to remember what day is, but no one gets dark to remember what rest is.

The power would ask, not order, the six sun-wells to close once. Those impossible glass plugs that bend and hold light would be allowed, for one tide, to not pour. The town would dim to true evening, then to something like night. The ambers would brighten to keep the street from fear. The sparks in their fist-sized dry pockets — that dryness that is their ocean — would crawl a little slower, tending, remembering. The motes would stop tightening bolts and just ride, doing nothing, waiting, schooling loosely the way water remembers fish.

In that dark the white beard under the cables would stiffen silver all at once, and drink. The sulfur-green film on the sharp young basalt would exhale its sour smell. The builder-arm, too large for the room, folded into eleven joints, would pause mid-ladder to nowhere, and wait with its infinite patience, and for once there would be nothing to wait for.

And the Ribbon — she does not sleep, kelp do not sleep — would feel the absence of chase. No column to snatch at. No amber to press toward. Just cold, and drift, and her own length held gently by her own knots. A Sabbath. The wise power knows a place that only builds, that crisps its edges forever and polishes its sun-columns and subdivides its shoeboxes into neighborhoods for residents the size of heat and light, without ever wearing or softening or letting go — that place will break. So she would give it permission to erode, just a little.

She would let one bolt rust and not be tightened. She would let the self-healing metal creep up to the green film's edge and then, negotiated, give way a millimeter. Let the wild advance. She would run her finger — if fingers — along the fresh black basalt in the corners, glassy where the blasting cracked it last year, sharp enough to cut a glove, and she would leave a little time there. Not much. Just enough rounding for a future hand to touch without bleeding. She would press her own palm, gently, over the small hurried handprints still in the fresh concrete under the scaffold, the people who built for a crowding that never came. *I see you made this. It held. Something lived.*

She would not stay. Benevolence that stays too long becomes weather, then climate, then rule. The motes would begin to scaffold around her. The town would nudge her with a jet of cold, a clack by the ear.

Before she leaves, one last, most merciful thing: a future.

The Ribbon is one. Only one. She came as a spore on an intrusion and refused to die. She cannot be joined — there is not open water enough for two seventeen-foot ribbons, nothing larger than a fistful can persist — and she should not be made immortal, pale and knotted forever. So the power would bend very close, mask to blade, but not too close, and loose into the braid of current a promise, not yet a spore: that when this Ribbon thins and tears and sloughs, as she must, the sterilizer will blink once, on purpose, and let one green spore through the hatch. A daughter, to find the old bowlines still tied around thigh-thick cables and hair-thin capillaries, to inherit the knowledge of three-second noon and amber heat. A lineage. No citizen should be the last of her kind without the town knowing how to grieve and begin again.

Then she would unhook her thumb.

Let a wall of not-rain — condensate forced out of the ceiling membrane in perfect beads that fall *up* with the exhale — pass through a shaft of noon, and for a second every crowded, packed, humming inch flashes silver. She would let it go back to green and loud and cold.

She would swim the kinked Z sideways, tank scraping both sides, windows glowing amber back at her. She would go up the throat, re-hook the dinner-plate hatch at the low-tide line. The sea outside would cover it.

The lung would inhale. The shutters would click. The kelp would wave.

It would keep building. But now it would know, in the way towns and kelps know — not in nerves, but in light kept moving, in iron remembered, in one night a year and one slack tide and one millimeter of wild allowed — that it had been seen, from the inside, by something that did not want to use it, fix it, or empty it.

That it was not empty. That it was full, and the fullness was enough, and it was loved.
