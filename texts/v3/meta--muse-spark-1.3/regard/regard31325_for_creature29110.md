It would not come from above.

Above is only the lid, dishwater cloud, smeared ache of sun. There is no opening there for anything grand to descend through. So it would come low, the way everything true comes here — along the water table, up through the muck, in with the seep at the base of the levee.

At first, it would do nothing. That is the first wisdom. And the hardest.

It would stand for a long time on the cracked apron between the pump house and the machine shop, and let the roar fill its sternum. Let sweat bead and not dry. Let lungs work for the chewed air. Let eyes adjust to noon-dusk and the haloed bulbs that insist. It would learn the rules before it touched anything:

Don't touch a pipe without a rag.
Don't go past the levee without boots and a reason.
Don't expect anything to change.
Keep her going.

It would understand, faster than any visitor ever has, that this is not a broken place waiting to be fixed. It is a holding action that learned how to breathe. In as fast as out. Heat made as fast as heat lost. Men tired as fast as men rested. To "save" it by stopping the machines would be to drown it. To "clean" it by drying it would be to kill the frogs in the culverts, the dragonflies over the basins, the egret on the pipe. To "free" the people by taking them away would be to unmake wading birds who belong to this water level.

A wise power knows the marsh does not want this island, and knows the island is loved anyway. It loves both. So it would not choose.

What it would do, then, would be small. Almost nothing. Almost everything.

It would sit down to their scale.

In the mess hall at noon it would sit on the bench, sweating into its plate of rice and grease like anyone else, deafened a little by the press thumping up through the boots. It would listen while the women wring mops into buckets that fill again on their own, arms red to the elbow, hair damp at the temples. It would not dry them with a miracle. It would take an end of the sheet on the wire behind the foreman's house — that flag of surrender, perpetually warm, perpetually damp — and pull, and for one afternoon only, let the air move thin enough that cotton actually dries. Crisp. Sun-smelling. So they can bury their faces in it and remember what dry was, and then go back to wet without despair.

In the machine shop it would stand mouth-to-ear with the old hands. It would not give them back their hearing. It would, for seven minutes at 2 a.m., when the night is only a slightly darker dim, hold its hand over the exhaust fan and the vent and the chain conveyor and soften them — not stop, never stop, just soften — so the men in the dormitory, sleeping in shifts with the roar in their bones, dream for once inside quiet and do not wake in panic thinking the pumps have died. Seven minutes of true quiet, to teach them that silence is not falling.

With the foreman it would walk the ditches, hip-deep in its own attention, and look at the notched stick. Level's good. It would nod. It would confirm. That is what benevolence mostly is here: confirmation. *You are holding. I see you holding.*

With the boy it would ride the wind. It would wait till he is flying on his bicycle down the shell road, basket of bobbins rattling, tires hissing on wet cinder, too fast because he doesn't yet know how to conserve himself — and instead of shouting at him to slow down, it would give him ten yards of tailwind, cool for once, lifting his shirt off his back. Let him feel fast and un-scolded. Let the men watching feel fond without irritation for a moment.

With the animals — because they are residents, not decoration — it would be scrupulously fair.

It would cool, by two degrees, the one square of vibration-warmed concrete under the pallet where the lean yellow dog sleeps, tongue out always. It would let the dormitory cats nurse undisturbed under the floorboards where the ground breathes hot. It would send a fat minnow, stunned by heat, into the warm outflow just as the white egret unfolds its neck. It would let the swallows eat their fill over the boiler house and the copper dragonflies patrol unsprayed. At low dim it would stand back for the blue-clawed crabs clicking sideways across the road, unhurried, and give them right of way. It is their road too.

And at the edge, where the levee seeps cold and the frog chorus sometimes rises above the engines, it would listen longest. Beyond the reeds the alligators grunt. The marsh waiting, patient, endless. It would put one hand on the wet dark of the levee and one hand on the pale concrete, and hold the truce. Fresh water and marsh water and waste water have negotiated here; it would bless the negotiation. Keep the mark on the stick exactly where it was yesterday. Not higher. Not lower.

Once — only once, because even a benevolent power must not break the balance to prove itself — it would lift the lid.

Toward evening, when the light is already dusk, it would thin the cloud for twenty minutes. Not blue sky. This place could not bear blue sky. Just a tear, low in the west, where the smeared sun drops red and real shadows return for the first time in years. The stacks would cast long lines across the puddles. The water tower's rust would glow. Men's faces would become visible to each other across the too-wide streets. The tamarind by the mess hall, perpetually white with bird shit and never taller, would put out one new green leaf, and someone would see it.

Then the lid would close. The bulbs would insist again. The vent would scream and the gulls lift. The culvert would gurgle and catch on the same note.

It would not stay in the chapel with the tin steeple, though the bats shift above and the mud-daubers work the eaves. It does not need that room. It would be in the drip under the pipe racks, ticking steadily. In the condensation on forearms. In the oil-skin on the rust-colored pans.

And when — because even held balances end, because pumps fail, because levees seep past holding, because ten years or fifty years from now the marsh will have its slow say — when the water rises past the mark on the stick and does not go back, it will not look away.

It will be there in hip boots with a rag in its back pocket, humming to keep from drowning, humming with them, carrying what can be carried: the boy grown to a man, the women with their red arms, the dogs, the cats in a crate, the memory of an egret on a pipe, the exact note of a culvert.

It would not have taken them out of the weather. It would have been weather with them — hot, dim, loud, wet — and for a little while, held exactly its own alongside them against the low water.
