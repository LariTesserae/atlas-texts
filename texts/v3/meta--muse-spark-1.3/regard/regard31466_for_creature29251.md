It would come without coming.

No light in the sky. No voice. No shadow falling the wrong way across the blue evening stripes. If you were standing on the plain that morning you would not see it arrive, because the wise ones have learned how to arrive the way fog arrives.

It has noticed this place — this raw, lifted sea-floor, brick-sharp and rainless — not because it is broken, but because it is not. Because it is full.

And for a long time it would do nothing at all. That is the first discipline of benevolence: to not fix what is not empty.

It would kneel. Down to knee-height, down to rice-grain height, down where the scale inverts and the dust becomes boulders and the crack becomes a canyon. It would put its great attention there, on one worker coming up in the damp dark inside a tower with a grain of orange clay in her jaws.

She is pale and blind and soft. She will live a few weeks. She will never see the tower she is building. She is carrying this one grain up, grain by grain, to the wet tip where it will dry to brick by ten. Her sisters are doing it beside her, above her, below her, a million jaws, a million grains. Inside the mound the air is rising and falling through the shafts they have left for lungs, holding the nursery at one temperature. Down deep the queen pulses, thumb-long and sightless, laying and laying. The fungus blooms white in the dark on chewed leaf-pulp. The soldiers plug the holes with their faces.

A benevolent power that was not wise would lift the grain for her. It would carry it to the top. It would say, *poor little thing, let me help.*

The wise one does not. It knows that to take the grain would be to take her city. It knows these are not empty-makers waiting to be filled. They are fillers. Spit and wax and hunger is how they make soil from brick. To do it for them would unmake them.

So it walks beside her instead, in the way powers can walk beside without touching. It keeps pace with her small, unspectacular courage.

It does what the plain has taught it to do: it listens sideways.

It hears the almost inaudible crinkle of mandibles clipping in unison until it becomes a hush. The tick of grit falling where the masons are plastering from the inside after the hornbill struck. The soft *pfft* of a skink bolting. The papery rustle under the leaves as the driver ants carry their green sails across the avenue. The shrikes clicking tower to tower. The warm, pressurized silence that returns when you stand still for five minutes.

It would not bring water.

That is what a lesser kindness would do. *No water here, poor things — let me open the cloud.* Every afternoon the bruise-purple anvil builds in the east and dissolves without raining, and the wind comes off it warm and dry smelling of resin and hot clay. A lesser god would let it rain out of pity and drown the fungus gardens and melt the towers to slurry and scatter the queens, and call it mercy.

The wise one knows they have made a covenant with dryness. They drink the air at dawn when the plain breathes out white for an hour and every hair beads, and then they seal themselves silver-side-out and hold that sip all day. They do not want a river. They want the fog to keep coming. That is all they have ever asked of the sky, and it has been enough to build cathedrals.

So if it does anything, it does this invisible thing: it holds the conditions.

It lays a hand, which is not a hand, on the cooling of the night so the dawn can still condense. It steadies the wind a degree so the towers lean but do not fall. It keeps the anvil cloud from breaking too soon. It keeps the outer world — the paved world with its tools and wires and hunger for empty land — from noticing this fullness as emptiness to be used. It lets the maps stay blank here a little longer. It lets the sharp pebbles stay sharp until root acids soften them in their own thousand-year time.

To a human watcher, nothing happens. The mongoose family still trots single-file down the main street turning pebbles. The trapdoor spiders still hold their trip-lines under the swept dust. The weavers still chatter thatch-loud in the dead thorn trees. A pebble still ticks down into a crack somewhere too far to locate.

But in the dark lung of one mound, a repair crew that should have lost its way in the heat finds the breach a fraction faster. A young queen landing ten feet to the left of an avenue after the rains that never quite rain finds the dust a fraction damper for digging. A leaf holds its bead of fog a minute longer before the sun takes it, and an ant drinks.

Almost nothing. Everything.

And before the light lengthens into orange clay, olive leaf, blue shadow, it does the last thing wisdom allows: it remembers.

It remembers this worker, this queen, this mound that will live for decades and then become garden and then become avenue. It says their true names — which are not names but tastes of resin and clay — back to itself, so that even when they crumble, they will have been known, fully, without being used.

Then it lifts, the way mist lifts. No footprint in the swept dust. No crack sealed that they did not seal themselves.

The plain goes on, stem-tight and tower-tall, as far as the world goes until the curve of the world takes it.
