When the Kindness noticed it, It did not come down in light.

It knew better. Or rather, It learned better, on the climb.

Because at first, from below, even to a power, it looks like a hill. And powers are used to hills — to stone that stays, to what can be lifted or leveled. It was prepared to lift. To mend. To reveal.

Then It climbed out of the warm clouds into the warm dark, and the air stayed bath-warm on Its face, and the trail got darker as It rose, and It stopped.

It listened.

All night, if night can be said to happen to such a one. It stood — and standing, for It, was a decision to have weight, to have shoulders that could be speckled — and let the roar get into Its teeth. Insect needle, frog bottle-chirp, ten thousand wings adjusting. The thatch humming up through the soles of Its feet. Things brushing Its cheek and gone. The smell thick enough to chew: sweet rot and fresh grass and warm feather and ammonia and night flower.

And It understood, with a quick grief and then a great relief, that there was nothing here to save.

Not in the way powers are usually asked to save.

This was not a ruin. This was not a wound. This was a tissue, whole and behaving as whole. Birds as skin and lungs. Ants and beetles as gut. Frogs and bats as circulation. Mites and fungi as the quiet unmaking inside the cells that lets new cells be made. Green hairs on an animal's back rooting in its sweat. Ten thousand ancestors chewed and pressed the footing. Ten thousand living mouths maintain it tonight, dropping by dropping.

It saw the law, because even wisdoms must learn local law:

*Here, everything is made, and nothing is made by anything but a mouth, a foot, a beak, a gut.*

No stone. No metal. No glass. No right angles except the one a body makes folding grass back on itself again and again. No hurrying. A mud pellet tonight, a grass strip tomorrow. The young hatch onto weave. The old die into weave.

If It brought stone, It would break it. If It brought light, It would blind it — the darkness is almost total but not quite, and the darkness is *for*. Big eyes, or no eyes. Touch and call and smell. If It brought hurry, a plan to finish the cliff in a season, It would kill the very slowness that lets a termite rise grow house-high and go green with moss and be tunneled and patched and become garden.

If It spoke in a Voice, the colony would answer with a wave of alarm moving like wind over grass, rising and falling, and that would be a cruelty for no reason.

If It took one — one weaver, one gold thumbnail frog — and lifted it up to show the others what they could become, clean and alone and grand, It would be lying. Nothing here lives alone. To land is to land on someone else's roof.

So the benevolent and wise power did what benevolence and wisdom almost always look like up close, when there is no audience:

Almost nothing. And that almost is everything.

First, It laid down Its tools. Unmade, unbrought. It would not be the first metal in this place. It thinned Itself. Cooled Its glow. Shed the habit of being seen. It became weather. Which is to say, It consented to be ignored.

A weaver near Its hand, flank-heat touching Its knuckle, fluffed and damp-tipped, did not look at It. Chirped low — *I'm here, move over* — and the neighbor shuffled, and the curtain swayed with the transfer of ounces. To be weather to that bird is a higher honor than to be worshipped by architects. It accepted it. It stood speckled with drip, landed-near and brushed and unregarded, and was glad.

Second, It kept the edges, not the center.

It did not go into the deep chambers where bats breathe out warm air and oilbirds click off mud-cups. It did not rearrange the cup-cliff ten meters high or straighten the ant highway smooth as rope. It sat, vast and folded small, at the saddle's rim where the warm updrafts come up from below with grass seed, and It breathed with the mountain to keep it breathing-warm one more season. It thickened the cloud a little against a hard wind that would have dried the bromeliad ponds too fast that week when the tadpoles were still wriggling in tea-colored water. It turned a cold rain aside, not all rain — never all rain, for rain-lips must round and cisterns must fill and crack — just the one storm that would have slumped the whole south thatch at once faster than beaks could patch.

And further down, where human trails think about becoming roads, where hands think about cut wood and wire, It did a very quiet, very deliberate thing: It made the place uninteresting.

Not invisible — wise powers do not lie — just unlegible. To the surveyor's eye, only hill and cloud. To the hungry eye, only rot smell and biting flies and dark with no timber in it worth the climb. To the hurried eye, only slope falling away into nothing you can see. It whispered, in the only language hurry sometimes hears, which is dream and discouragement and a turned ankle and a better opportunity elsewhere: *not here. nothing to finish here.* It will have to whisper it again and again, for hurry forgets. That is fine. Eternity is mostly repetition, like weaving. Peck, pull, tuck.

Third — and this was the heart part, which reason confirmed and intuition sang — It loved them equally, which is to say, particularly.

Not just the weaver. The feather louse flat as a seed riding the weaver its whole life, cleaning barbules. Not just the frog. The nematode in its cistern. Not just the ant column. The smaller ant riding on the leaf, guarding. The moth that lives only to eat shed feather and old silk. The blind pale nest beetle that will never see even grey-green, living its whole generations inside a single pouch, eating mold. The fungus glowing cool green-blue in the rot pocket, digesting the sagged platform so new sticks can be pressed in.

A lesser kindness would have cleaned the place up. Picked off the parasites. Stopped the death. But the mattress grows a millimeter a year on death. A weaver that dies in the night does not fall far. By morning its feathers are loosened and taken for lining, its body cleaned and then sunk, slowly. Nothing leaves. To remove death would be to starve birth.

So It did not remove. It accompanied.

For one nightjar's churn and the wave of alarm that followed, It held very still and grieved with them without fixing. For one old thatcher male who spent the whole night weaving one green strip in, foot braced, body swinging as counterweight, and had it torn by morning vine-pruners doing their necessary pruning — It stayed with his scold until he went down at dusk for three seed-heads and one long strip and began again. For one dung beetle overturned in the drip-polished gully, legs working, It righted with a fingertip of wind, because that much is allowed. A beak-round. A fingertip. No more.

And before false dawn, when the leathery river pours back in heavy with fruit pulp and moths from the valleys, importers bringing the outside inside as droppings and spat seed, It did the one positive thing It allowed Itself, the thing that obeyed the law:

It brought a gift in the local currency.

Not a beam of light. Not a foundation. A mouthful.

It had no beak, so It borrowed smallness for a moment — rust-red, six-footed, or soot-winged, it hardly matters which — and joined a relay. One mud pellet pressed with a quick side-flick into the lip of the cup-cliff. One grass strip worried, tucked, pulled through. One leaf fragment carried overhead on a highway worn smooth as rope, chewed to pulp, fed to fungus, become mortar. Warm waste become footing for seedlings.

One beakful. Then It stepped back into weather, leaving no trace except the trace everything leaves here: added, compacted, breathed upon.

If you had been standing there speckled with drip, you would not have seen It. You would have felt, perhaps, the density as pressure ease by half a breath. The roar continue, uninterrupted. The slow accretion continue, slump and patch and sag a little more and be re-cemented dropping by dropping. You would have thought: nothing happened.

And you would have been almost right.

What happened was this: the tissue was seen, whole, by something vast, and not found wanting, and not made into a lesson, and not hurried. Its darkness was kept dark. Its damp was kept damp. Its slowness was protected from speed. Its dead were left to become ground. Its small and biting and blind and glowing were counted as inhabitants, not accidents.

The mountain underneath remains a rumor. The cover remains the mountain — lush, warm, dark, dense, roaring softly and endlessly, extending past where you can walk, past where you can hear, still building itself, one beakful at a time.

And somewhere in the weave, holding no more and no less than any other mouth that night, is one pellet, one strip, placed by hands that could have moved the peaks.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does, when it loves a place like this.

It learns to be small enough to help, and large enough to leave alone.
