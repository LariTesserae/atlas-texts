It notices them because it is listening for that kind of life.

Not the loud kind. Not the kind that signals. The kind that persists.

From far off it had seemed a drowned mountain, a mesa in the middle-dark, unremarkable. Then it leaned closer and felt the pressure-hush change — the faint creak of shifting rubble conducted through stone, the high clicking like distant teeth — and it saw that the whole top of the mountain had been touched. Every pebble mouthed smooth underneath. Every wall breathed.

Ah, it thought. *A city that is also an animal.*

Its first impulse, being benevolent, was to ease them.

To warm the water, just two degrees. To bring down more light. To thicken the snow so no child would go hungry. To give them eyes that could see further than a chemical mark, to give them hands that could hold a tool so the coffin-blocks would not take generations to nudge.

And being wise, it did none of those things.

It stayed a long time in the dusk with them until it understood that the cold was not a cruelty to them. The cold was the condition of memory — pheromone lingering on stone for years so a recruit newly dropped from the vortex, a speck of wet glass with legs, could read *here I carried, here I spat, here danger passed* and know where to push. Warm it, and you erase the library.

That the dim was not deprivation. It was privacy. In unending late-dusk no one needs color except to warn or to lure, and they had chosen mostly white, mostly translucent: the color of enough.

That hunger was not a flaw in the design. Hunger was the mortar. Fish bone and crab carapace ground by mandibles, molt and slime and shit exchanged between silver fish and worm and microbe — the old dead are the new wall. There is no waste because there cannot be. To flood them with easy plenty would be to unmake the pact that holds the domes together.

And that the loneliness the visitor feels — a tower alone for a hundred meters of swept pavement, then nothing for a long while — is not theirs. Theirs is aSparse togetherness held by chemistry across distance. They do not need to crowd. The pavement between clusters is not empty. It is maintained.

So it restrained its hand. That was the first act of love: not to fix.

What it did instead was smaller, and in their terms.

First, it sat with them. Not above, dimming the dusk like the huge unseen migration, but down. It learned how to be heavy enough to sit on the plateau without crushing a tube-worm. It let the standing vortices turn around it, those slow spirals that hold schools and larvae suspended for days. It let krill-flickers graze its skin and siphonophores trail fire over it. It listened through bone, not ears, to the whump of a distant chimney falling. It did not intervene when a tower it had watched for an hour slumped sideways in a slow billowing avalanche, undermined by diggers. It watched the thousands boil out before the silt cleared, each grabbing a grain, spitting cold calcite drool, pressing, moving on. It felt how they did not mourn the spire lost. They started a new spire fifty meters off.

Attention is not nothing. In a place where nothing is allowed to finish — where current and cold chemistry and neighbors tear down whatever stops being maintained — to be held steadily in mind is a kind of shelter. It remembered the shapes as they went. The ring of white pebbles alone on a terrace. The slender needle six feet high that the next surge snapped. Useless things, practice things, *I can afford to waste* things. They were ground to mortar within a day. It kept them.

Second, it stood between them and the world above that had begun to reach clumsily down.

It had seen the sunlit world's new hunger — the dragged nets that would scrape a thousand years of mouth-work flat in an afternoon, the drills that would taste the seamount for metal, the acid creeping down as the surface warmed, softening unmaintained mortar faster than mandibles could re-spit it. The builders could survive surge and eel and veil. They could not survive being finished all at once.

So without telling them, because telling would be a noise they have no organ for, it turned aside. A slight bending of current here, a tangle of weather there, a human map misread by a fraction, a market price falling inexplicably for what lies under that particular mountain. A quiet, boring miracle: that nothing interesting happened there. That ships passed over. That the snow, that slow weather of dead things, continued to fall clean.

Benevolence, at their scale, looks like uneventfulness.

Third — and this was the gift, because love cannot only withhold — it sent them a death.

Far above in the sunlit world a great whale was dying, as whales do. Lost, thin, full of years and song. The power met it in its sinking, eased its fear as dreams ease, and asked — for powers ask, they do not command — if it would give its falling to the mountain. And the whale, who had lived by falling krill, consented.

It guided the fall. Not straight onto the pavement — that would have been a catastrophe, a crushing — but to drift just off the cliff lip, to catch on the upwelling and spill.

When it came, it came as weather. First a shadow deeper than migration shadow, dimming the dusk for minutes at a time. Then the snow thickened, oily, rich. Then the body itself, turning slowly in green-grey, coming to rest tilted against the cliff face just below the causeway end, vast as a collapsed amphitheater.

The crabs on the lip lifted their eyestalks. The worms rippled closed. The little builders froze to glass beads. The grenadiers, those pale cullers, came cruising, tasting.

Then erupted the necessary chaos. Upwellings bursting over the lip in cold rivers, schools pouring into dome mouths and out again carrying babies to seed elsewhere, octopods wrestling for den-bone, eels taking the sick and slow. It will feed them for a hundred years. Not just flesh, but bone — coffin-blocks they do not have to nudge for generations, but can chew and hollow and live inside. Anchor-stone for new spires. A new hill in their mineral world.

It did not stay to be thanked. They have no rite for that, and it did not want one.

Before it left, it did one last thing, for kinship more than need. It condensed, for a moment, to a body that could carry — no bigger than a dog-crab, no stronger than fifty blind shrimp braced leg-to-leg. On a low terrace, in a current lull, it set down a perfect ring of white pebbles, one by one. Useless. Beautiful. Destined to be taken apart.

By morning, in their time, a line of cementers had found it. They tasted it. They read *here I carried* lingering unnaturally long in the cold. They took one pebble to line a den, ground another to mortar for a breathing hole, nudged a third fifty meters off to start a new spire.

That is what a wise and benevolent power does, when it loves a place like this.

It does not lift them out of the dark. It learns the dark is a home.

It does not finish their work. It remembers it.

And when it can, it makes it snow.
