It would come the way the weather comes — without a trail.

Not down, with noise. It would already be there, the way heat is already there. Sitting, if it has a shape to sit with, on a hummock that will not hold weight for long. Letting the warm water rise.

It would not be seen at first. That is its manners.

For a long time it would only listen. Drip-drip-drip from a million leaf-tips. Tink, tink from the puddles. Chuff, sigh, long exhalation from the throats in the east flats where the earth breathes. The piston-thump in the sternum every eleven minutes. The clack of stones like loose couplings in a mill. It would hear what you heard and think the opposite thought: not *how industrial*, but *how complete*.

Because a wise power can tell the difference between a factory and a fullness.

After a while it would look closer, as you did. Crouch, knees wet instantly, it doesn't matter. Part the sedge with a finger the frogs do not fear.

There — amber throat pulsing like a tiny heart. There — a liter of tea-warm water held in the crown of a tree-fern, and in it an egg-string, and a tadpole, and eyes looking back. There — a rail with absurd snowshoe toes, unbothered. There — a fifty-meter highway of rust-red ants carrying green sails, shifting by an inch to avoid a new puddle. Engineers without a word.

It would see each one. Not the species. The one. The frog that will call tonight from this boot-print and not that one. The ant that will drop its sail at the steaming hole. It is benevolent that way — embarrassingly particular.

And it would understand the rule, faster than you did: *nothing leaves.*

Heat from above and heat from below. Water from above and water from below. Sweated out of rock, drunk by moss, transpired by leaf, condensed as fog at chest-height, rained in a 3 p.m. hammering, sweated out again. A dead bird mossed over in days. A fallen log green in a week. Fungi through feathers. Orchid seed in hollowed ribs.

Nothing wasted because nothing can afford to waste, because the lip of the valley is high and narrow, a single notched outlet pouring surplus into blue abyss. A lung that never stops breathing because it cannot afford to stop.

So what would it do?

Nothing to improve it.

That is the first wisdom, and the hardest for a power that *could* do anything. It will not cool the springs. It will not drain the sponge to make firm ground for heavier, grander animals. It will not still the geyser to make it quiet, or make the hummingbirds hover longer for beauty, or keep the sundew from eating the fly, or keep the rail from spearing the bloodworm, or keep the fungus from taking the bird. It will not make it less hot, less wet, less high, less strange. It will not give it a winter to rest, or a trail to be known.

To fix anything here would be to break everything here.

The second wisdom: it will not stay.

Even a benevolent footprint compacts peat a meter deep. Even a wise breath, held too long, changes the cloud that has to remake itself every hour. Even light, if it stares, dries moss. So it will be brief the way a cloud is brief. It will let the midges land on its arms and bite, and it will not swat. It will let mist move against the wind through it. It will let its own warmth be drunk by capillary pull uphill in the sphagnum. For an hour it will consent to be part of the throughput — fly to frog to rail to worm to ant to fungus to orchid to fly again — and then it will consent to be reassigned out.

What it does, it does mostly elsewhere.

On the ridge below, where men with maps look up and think *geothermal potential, drainage, road*, it will lay a hand — not on the valley, but on their eyes. Make the notch look like nothing from below. A little more cloud at 3 p.m. A little more steep in the switchbacks. A rumor of leeches. A sense, unaccountable, that there is nothing useful up there. Not a curse. A veil. The only intervention a perfect closed circle can bear is to be kept closed.

And for the one who already climbed, sweating through his shirt at four thousand meters, boot sinking blood-warm — for you — it will do the one thing it still allows itself to do to the living:

It will change nothing in the valley, and change the witness.

Let him feel, for a minute inside the warm white room of fog, visibility ten paces, droplets on eyelashes, how his own lungs are also chuff, sigh, long exhalation. How he is also mostly water cycling tight and fast. How his urge to name, to use, to tidy the braided trickles into a course, is just his own hunger for outlets.

Let him kneel until his knees are soaked and he stops minding. Let him hear the tink-tink not as rain upward but as neighbors. Let him understand that small in huge numbers is not a failure to be large, but a decision to be light enough for the sponge to hold.

Then let him climb back down through black rock and switchback cloud, and not build a cairn, and not tell the coordinates plainly, and wash his boots before the next mountain so no orchid seed hitches a ride out. Nothing leaves — he will keep the rule, for once.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do with a place this full and a being this insistently alive:

Not lay a pipe. Not lay a hand.

Sit a while inside the breath. Love each thumbnail life without stopping its death. And on leaving, pull the cloud closed behind it like a door that was never there.
