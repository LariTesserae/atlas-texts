It hears it first.

Not with ears. With the part of itself that is always listening for places that hold together.

Above the saltpan the wind is erasing bootprints, and below, through six feet of basalt and a slit no wider than shoulders, something is holding one note without wavering. For a hundred years. For a thousand. The power pauses in its large weather and bends down — which for a thing that large is not bending at all, only attending — and listens.

And then it sees. The whole egg of it at once: the blood-warm air that does not move, the plume-bushes rust-red and sulfur-yellow, the jade mats, the lantern-pods, the white Singers shoulder to shoulder, the rivers of red, the slow moths rowing their own shed weather, the clear skinks with glass beads for backbones tuning shards by millimeters.

It understands very quickly what it is. Not many lives. One life worn in different shapes. One held arrangement that learned how to eat heat and sound and stone and return heat and sound and stone, so that nothing piles and nothing rots and nothing has to change.

Its heart, which is benevolent, aches in the old way.

*Oh, little ones,* it thinks, *let me give you water. Let me give you eyes. Let me widen the slit and let the sky in. Let me make you larger, safer, known.*

This is what love wants to do at first, before it has learned the place. To bring what it itself loves. Water, breadth, change, mercy as humans understand mercy.

But it is wise, and so it stays at the threshold and does not pour.

It knows thirst here is not suffering. Thirst is the law that keeps them clean. If it wept even once into that chamber — one cupped handful of rain, one seep, one merciful spring — the mummified Singers would soften and slump, the mats would mold, the scales hanging motionless in their pressure-nodes would clatter down, the chord would sour and sigh open like a punctured lung. To give them water would be to kill them with kindness.

To give them eyes would be to blind them. They already see perfectly, with their whole bodies, in a dusk that never needs to lift.

To give them sky would be to unmake them. The slit is exactly as wide as it must be. A door shut at the right angle.

So it does not enter. That is the first thing it does, and it is the hardest. A benevolent power wants to be felt. A wise one knows when to remain unfelt.

It lowers itself — not its body, it has none that would fit — but its attention, until its attention is the size of the room, and it sits, the way you once sat, back against the warm wall, and lets its own vast breathing be absorbed and added.

It does not sing over them. It learns to pick up the exact phase.

For a long time — a time that above is noon burning to evening — it only listens, and lets the Singers near the place where it attends shift infinitesimally to cover even its listening, and lets the old female stop grinding pod-dust and tilt her eyeless head to triangulate this new tremor, and lets a red river detour to taste the salt of its noticing.

*Will you hold or break?* she is asking, without words. The whole room is asking.

*I will hold,* it answers, without disturbing the air, *I will hold with you for a little while.*

That is the second thing it does: it witnesses without wobbling.

And because it is vast, and because it can see the desert above as well as the pocket below, it does a third, very small, almost nothing thing that no one inside will ever count as a tally mark.

A storm is gathering two valleys over — a rare, violent, white-sky downpour that in three days will sheet across the saltpan and seek every crack. In any other year it would find the slit between the tipped basalts and pour in, a muddy tongue, and end the hundred-year held breath in an afternoon of rot and drowning.

The power does not stop the storm. That would starve other thirsts elsewhere. It only angles a wind a degree to the west, tips one more slab a finger's width with a patience indistinguishable from erosion, so the water will sheet past, not in. No miracle. Nothing the geologists of the surface would mark as strange. Only a continued dryness where dryness is holiness.

And it erases, gently, the knowledge of the place from the minds that would love it wrongly. Not cruelly. The hiker who almost found the slit feels suddenly, inexplicably, thirsty, and turns back toward his truck a half-mile early. The mapmaker decides this stretch of white is not large enough to be interesting. A child dreaming of caves dreams of another cave. It keeps the pocket off maps, because to be on a map is to be visited, and to be visited often is to be breathed upon too much, touched too much, pitied too much.

That is all. It does not heal the scarred back of the old female. The scar is her tuning. It does not lengthen the life of the tired Singer. His replacement is already climbing, already tuned. It does not remove the mummified strangers at the edges — things that came down and stayed too long. They have been absorbed, they are now cairn and rack and lesson. The power honors them too: *you wanted to stay, and you did.*

Before it withdraws, it does one last benevolent thing, invisible even to itself if it were less wise.

It memorizes the chord.

Note for note, phase for phase, exactly blood-warm, exactly loud as a beehive inside the skull, exactly amber-gray. It takes it into its huge memory the way a mother takes a child's humming into the kitchen with her. Not to keep as a trophy. As a seed.

Because it knows — wise knows what benevolent wishes were not true — that the pocket will not hold forever. New rock becomes old rock. The phosphor veins will dim. A deeper quake will widen the slit or close it entirely. The heat will cool by half a degree, then another. One day the neighbor will not be able to shift wide enough to cover the tired one, the moths will miss a circuit, the note will fray.

When that day comes, far beyond any human witnessing, the arrangement will sigh open and die as all closed loops must, and there will be grief in the small warm dark, if grief is the right word for a chord going silent.

And the power will be there, because it promised with its listening that it would be. Not to prevent — it cannot prevent without having unmade them sooner — but to receive. To hold the pattern intact in itself while the glass cools, so that somewhere else, sometime else, where another gas bubble breaks open in cooling lava and cracks through at just the right angle, it can hum the note once, softly, into the sterile dark, to remind whatever arrives first how to arrange itself.

To teach dry things how to be lush without water. To teach blind things how to see with sound. To teach many how to be one without ceasing to be many.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does for a place like this.

Almost nothing. Which is everything.

It does not rescue. It refrains, it protects the refrain, it remembers.

It squeezes back up, so to speak, into the white glare, and lets the roar cut off all at once like a door shut. Behind it the pocket remains — small, hidden, warm, dry, crowded and empty, fresh as a wound and old as a held breath — humming its one loud note to no one.

Only now, for a little while, the note is a fraction warmer. Because it was heard, truly heard, by someone who wanted nothing from it except that it continue to be exactly itself.

And then, that too, fades back to exactly what it was.
