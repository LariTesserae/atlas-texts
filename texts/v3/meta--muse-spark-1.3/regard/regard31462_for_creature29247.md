The power does not come down the worn steps.

That is the first wisdom. Anyone coming down that way comes as a weight, as glare, as noise to be swallowed. So for a long time it does not come at all. It sits, if sitting is what it does, on the thin white line of the rim hung in heat-haze, and learns to squint and shiver at once.

It watches.

It watches long enough to understand that this is not a broken place waiting to be fixed. No green is not a lack here. Quiet is not a wound. Moving is not exile. They have become low and rounded and thick on purpose, the way water becomes the shape of the jar. To unmake the hollow in order to bless them would be to unmake them.

So it makes no announcement. There is no voice from the white sky — the basin would eat it in three paces anyway. No pillar, nothing sharp, nothing that insists. The old ones learned: anything sharp will be rounded within a lifetime. The power, being wise, rounds itself first.

It learns hands.

This is what draws it, more than the place: one being in it, and then by extension all of them. Say a girl, six, running water. Small jars sloshing, bare thick feet that do not flinch on cold slab, headcloth white with lime. She knows every seep, every dark sweat-line in wall-shadow. In the morning she sets out her jars when the black varnish beetles mass on the east faces to drink off their own backs. At noon she lies down in the dim thick room with the others and listens to condensation tick. When the sky bruises west she turns without being told.

One afternoon after rain, when the polygonal pans are sky-mirrors ankle-deep, she stops in the middle of her relay — and looks. Not for water. For the flock. The white pigeons have lifted without sound, thousands, wheeling as a single decision-making body, hush-hush overhead, a low pressure. Everyone else looks up to read weather, updraft, time to cap the cistern. She just looks to see them turn.

That attention — that useless, complete attention — is what catches the attention.

From then on, if "then" means anything to such a power, it stays close to her relay. Not to lift the jar from her hands. That would be theft. Running is her school. To carry for her would be to un-teach her feet the slabs.

What it does instead is smaller, and therefore vaster:

It keeps the cold.

Under the pan, under the limestone pavement cracked into great polygons, the old cold is thinning, century by century, as the world warms above. The stone holds cold the way old bones do — but bones, in the end, warm and crumble. The power puts its hand, if hand is what it has, underneath. It does not make them warm. Warm would kill them. It keeps the upward cold steady through soles, so the thick walls still work, so noon inside is still dim relief, so the black cistern water stays black and living and the blind pale fish keep turning slowly far below.

It steadies the water, but does not tame it.

When the sky bruises and the shallow hand-cut channels run milky fast and strong enough to take goats, it does not stop the flood. The flood is the drink, the memory of the ground drinking. It only slows the leading edge by a breath — enough time for a dun goat to scramble to a causeway slab that rings faintly underfoot, for children to get their jars high, for twenty hands to clear a clog when taps go down the channel-length like a pulse. It lets there still be risk, because risk is what taught them to move as one body. It only refuses catastrophe. No whole house-group taken in one season. No seep and cistern failing all at once before a new one opens a hundred paces off.

It turns aside the sharp.

Caravans come down from the plain for cool and water held in stone through dry months, for salt cakes and nitre and stone-work. Most trade fair: grain and timber and wool left on mats by the causeway, fingers held up, palm turned, touch and nod. But sometimes one comes with a louder idea — to count, to claim, to own a pit, to bring sharp-cornered buildings and loud bells that ring instead of clonk, to write a name on water. Then the power leans, very slightly, on the basin's own nature. It makes the pan swallow a little more sound, makes the noon glare a little more unbearable to un-squinted eyes, makes the rim look, from above, even emptier — just dust devils and shadow-lines and rope between posts. The sharp ones get headaches from light bouncing up, get cold feet through their boots, feel unwatched and unnerved by people who speak in gestures. They leave early, thinking the place poor. It is poor, the way a seed is poor.

It leaves gifts that look like nature, because they must be movable.

Nothing here stays. A wall melts, a seep dries, material waits to be carried. So nothing given can demand to stay. The power thickens the black slick just a fraction in the dampest cracks where old women scrape sparingly with copper blades — iron-taste to keep bowels moving. It beads a little more condensation on the cold undersides of morning slabs, tick by tick into jars. It leaves a goat lingering longer at a wall base — *dig here* — where a pocket waits. It lets the leaning tower, no higher than three men, lean one winter longer before it must come down, because the squab and eggs taken by rule from certain niches are needed, and because the pigeons, fellow citizens, love that lean.

And at noon, it yields.

When shadows disappear and runners freeze mid-step with legs casting no shadow and the whole hollow glows until you cannot tell sky from floor, when the people retreat and lie on hide pallets in damp-stone-goat-smoke smell, the power does not hover and watch. It lies down with them. It learns that prayer. Sun on shoulders outside, only cold from below inside. Fish turn. Water ticks. The ancient floor, patient under all this brief frantic life, holds.

If you asked the girl years later — grown, lime-white to the wrist, mantle shrugged up and down a hundred times a day between glare and shadow — if anything ever came, she would not point up. She would touch her own chest, then yours, then the stone. Two taps.

That is the whole theology here, and the power accepts it. To be felt through soles, not heard. To travel along masonry. To need hands.

It never tells them they are seen. They know how to be seen by pigeons — a lift, a wheel, a landing without sound you can hear from more than twenty paces. To be loved that way is enough: not lifted out, but circled once, quietly, and left to continue pouring back out into the light, silent and fast, when the cloud shadow crosses the rim and the glare breaks.
