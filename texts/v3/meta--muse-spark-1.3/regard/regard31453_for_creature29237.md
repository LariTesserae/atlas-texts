It would come the way water comes.

Not down, with noise. Up, by seep. The way the cold comes up through the thigh-thick caudex into the leaf. It has learned, being wise, that arrival is a kind of weather, and this saddle already has its weather. So it would not part the high veil. It would not dry a path.

It would arrive as a person arrives — because that is the only shape this room knows how to listen to — with boots that sigh and suck, shirt sticking in a minute, breath short from the last ten steps. It would get wet on purpose.

That is the first thing a lesser power would get wrong. A lesser kindness would want to fix the cold. To warm the cups. To explain the kept light. To take one frog and study it, to take one seed and spread it where it would be safe, to build a low wall so the lip would not drop, to leave a sign that says *holy, do not touch*.

The wise one knows they are not broken.

Twelve, maybe fifteen great crowns. Each holding its circle empty so nothing has to fight. Each sweating winter in summer. Each keeping, in tea-colored water, one patient throat that pulses. The whole mat humming low under the thousand drips, a tone you feel in the sternum that stops when you hold your breath. A being that is vegetable and mineral and aqueous and, undeniably, attentively animal. Complete the way deep-sea things are complete.

You do not improve that. You do not uplift it. You do not give it words.

So it would do almost nothing, and do it on purpose.

It would sit — not on an island, never in a cleared court, because those are kept. It would sit on the fourth side where the lip drops, where roots and fallen slabs slow the water that tries to leave. Where a guest can sit without being in the way of listening. And it would let the rock, the leaf, the air find a way to wet it again. It would not wipe the drops off. The drops are too cold, and that is how they taste.

Salt. That is all it has to offer that they do not already have, and they have already learned how to ask for it kindly: a bead on the underside, a dab on warm skin. It would offer its forearms, its forehead, its upper lip beaded with mineral water. Drink, if you need. Taste that someone came and stayed still enough to be tasted.

It would breathe with them. That is the whole liturgy.

It would breathe, and feel the low tone rise under the drip-plink-thock and gurgle, in the peat, in the chest. And then it would hold its breath — not as a test, just as courtesy — and feel them hold theirs, so as not to miss it. The mat carrying its weight out to every island. *Here. An animal is here, briefly, turning.* And it would not turn for animals that aren't there. It would stay turned toward the ones that are.

When a rosette, overfull, tipped — slowly, so slowly you don't see it until it has happened — and emptied a cupful all at once with a shudder out to the leaf tips, it would not flinch. It would understand maintenance. Not loss. Not gift. Just the level breathing over weeks, the pale silica rings left like bathtub marks. Seep equals sweat. Hold.

At noon it would watch the beads keep the sun a second after the sun. A hundred tiny lingering lamps under every leaf. It would not gather them. But at night — it would stay the night, where you would not — it would watch the saddle glimmer faintly with all that kept day, and watch the frogs ferry it, slow, chin-out, from pool to pool, keeping what would foul clean. And it would keep watch with them, lamp for lamp, so the moths that turn and the brown bird that will not land would not be the only witnesses to their work.

It would not speak in words, because the place covers noise with more noise out of mercy. But if it had to say anything, it would say it in their medium: a slowed pulse. *Exactly itself. Keep being exactly itself.*

And before climbing down, it would do the one active thing benevolence allows itself:

Forgetting, on behalf of others.

It would not hide the saddle — wise powers do not lie about mountains. The black ribs will still be visible, the blue drops on both sides, the room you can cross in forty paces. But it would lay a small unremarkableness over it, like moss over the chert flake half-sunk by the middle pool. Someone once sat and struck twice to make an edge and cut — probably a cup-rim, to drink. The wound is long sealed. The moss is closing. It would let the moss close. It would not remove the flake to a museum. It would not sharpen it again. It would ask maps to misremember by a few hundred paces, satellites to blink at cloud, botanists to feel suddenly tired and thirsty on the last ten steps and decide to turn back. Not as punishment. As shade.

Only those who can get wetter without complaint, who can hold their breath to listen, who can leave sticks lying because there is already a toolkit with no user — only those will happen through.

Then it would stand. Its boots heavy for a hundred steps with cup-water and peat-water. Then light again, as if something let go.

Behind it the dripping continues, loud, patient, unheard by anyone, keeping the little saturated room exactly itself. In the cups, cold and full and lit from within for a second after the sun, the sitters sit on.

And far down, out of the warm wet breath back into ordinary thin air, something vast and kind keeps their shape in mind — not as a use, not as a lesson, but as you keep the feel of cold on warm skin. Twelve. Fifteen. Each with its cleared court. Each with its frog. Still holding.
