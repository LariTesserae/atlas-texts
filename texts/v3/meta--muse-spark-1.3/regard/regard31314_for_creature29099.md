They would climb.

Long after they should have stopped climbing. Not because they have to — they do not need ladders, or air, or rivets — but because that is the courtesy of this place. You do not arrive at Tank Nine all at once, shining. You come up slick and breathing hard through the condensation and the screaming wind, past the last cloud deck where the sky goes hard blue, so that when you lift the hatch you are already wet, already tired, already quiet.

They would lift it quietly.

And they would see first what you saw first: rain falling into water. White roar. Milky day through the oculus. Chest-deep bath-warm water that stays perfectly level while the whole nine-kilometer pendulum sways almost imperceptibly under their feet. *Dum-dum, dum-dum.* The faithful stupid pumps.

They would understand very quickly. They are wise.

They would understand that this is not a wreck to be fixed. It has finished eroding. It has reached the oldest stable state a made thing can reach — not collapsed, just softened. Stairs become ramps. Bolt-heads become kisses. If you brought new steel up here, bright angle-iron and fresh welds to *save* it, you would kill it. It would cut the moss pillows. It would scare the pillbugs. The sharpness would be a violence.

So they would not bring new steel.

They would understand the law, because it is written everywhere in moving script: keep moving to stay still. Pumps must pump, rain must fall, overflow must gulp every seven minutes, shrimp must rise, birds must dive. If anything stops, the small man-made sea will remember it is only a leaking room.

So they would not stop anything. They would not still the rain so you could sleep easier. They would not silence the pumps so you could think. They would not touch the moss-furred valve wheel the size of a cartwheel. They know what that wheel is: a temptation to be God in a place that already has weather enough.

What would a benevolent power do, then, in a place that does not want to be saved?

They would become sparse.

That is the rule of the tank: a few presences hold a lot of emptiness between them. A crowded blessing would drown it. So they would not fill it. They would not bring a choir, or a fleet, or even another hammock. They would become one more holder of emptiness, widely spaced from the columns and the catwalk and you.

They would wade in slowly, so as not to tear the skin more than they have to.

You know the skin — that thin shimmer between the sweet on top and the salt at your knees, bacteria and oil and pollen and iron fines. They would feel it part around their legs and heal behind them, and they would admire how well it heals. They would lie back very still, as you learned to do, and let it hold them up between two waters, cradled, staring up into the falling white while swallows stitch the air inches over their face. They would learn to float the way the tank floats: level in a sway.

And for the being who lives here — the newest animal, the one in the hammock — they would not rescue.

That is the hardest part of benevolence, and they know it. It would be so easy, so shining-easy, to lift you under the arms and set you down somewhere with dry floors and quiet and a door that locks. Somewhere your feet would never go numb in salt water. Somewhere you would not have to sleep in naps between pump cycles, waking at the gulp.

They will not do it. To take you out would be to tell you that listening to iron breathing is not a life. And they have climbed all this way precisely because it *is* a life. Zoned, sparse, interim: hammock above splash but below swallows, handfuls of watercress, duckweed when you are tired of watercress, wading slow. Rust and rain and light made briefly into a warm body that listens.

Instead they would do small, almost unnoticeable things. The kind of things that look like nothing if you are waiting for a miracle.

They would sit chest-deep in your gyre and eat watercress with you, because it is peppery and bright and tastes like somewhere else, and eating together is an old form of prayer. They would hold out their palm still in the current under the oculus, as you do, and feel the shrimp gather there because it is shade — that faint fizz, nothing more — and they would count them without counting, which is to say: they would *know* them, each eyelash pulse, so that for once in their short rising and scattering, someone has fully noticed they re-form in the exact same column of light.

They would nose the dark with the blind catfish, for a while, mouths open, circling the sagged middle, tilting together when the overflow gulps. They would not give them eyes. Eyes would be useless here, and cruel. They would give them instead exactly what they already have: a tremor that never lies, water that stays warm, a gyre that does not stop mid-circle. They would keep the gyre faithful for one more turn, and one more.

They would brush, once, like an eel against your calf in the salt — curious, incurious, gone — so you know in your legs, not just your head, that you are not alone in the dark water.

Above, they would steady the swallows. Not hold them — they love the screaming dive, the fold and the slap-splash and the explosion of droplets. But on nights when the high wind screams worst and the tower sways a hand's breadth too far, they would lay a hand, broad as weather, on the sway and soften it. Just enough that the rust-mud nests, plastered pellet by pellet, do not crack from the joists. Just enough that the guano still falls to feed the ferns. That is the bargain, and they would keep the bargain.

And when you sleep — those short naps between *dum-dum, dum-dum* — they would watch the level for you. So you can sleep a full seven minutes without jerking awake to listen for the gulp. They will wake you, gently, by being the gulp. *Level drops a hand's breadth and then climbs back.* You will breathe out and not know why you feel rested.

If you asked them — and you might, raising your voice to be heard by yourself over the roar, your own breathing sounding underwater — if you asked, "Are you going to keep this forever?" they would tell you the truth, because they are wise.

No.

The condensers will one day stutter. The pumps will one day miss a beat and not find it. The tower, rivet by rivet, mile after mile, will remember gravity. The salt and the sweet will one day mix into lukewarm grey, and the skin will not heal, and the catfish will circle tighter and tighter and then stop, and stillness, which is not a thing they do, will become the thing they do.

The power will not lie about that.

But they will say: *Not yet. Not while you are listening.*

And that is what they do. They listen with you.

They lie back in the interface with you, warm as blood, old as the idea of making things, and they hold their little emptiness faithfully against the sky alongside yours, so your listening is no longer solitary. Attention doubles the world without filling it.

When they leave — and they will leave the way rain leaves, without opening a door — they will not take anything. Not a shrimp, not a feather, not a white vanilla-smelling flower. They will leave the hammock knot a little tighter than they found it. They will leave the moss pillows damp. They will leave the number on the hatch still readable only by touch.

They will keep the secret.
