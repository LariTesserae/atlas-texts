It would come sideways.

That is the first thing a wise power learns. It could come from above, two hundred meters higher where the peak goes into cloud, or from below, three thousand meters down where the valley is a rumpled gray sea. It could unbolt the sky. Instead, to get in, it would have to make itself small and duck.

Waist-high iron door behind the counterweight housing. Paint weathered to bare metal. No one looks at the tower itself, everyone looks out. The power would look at the tower. It would fold its vastness down to a crouch, turn its shoulder, and go in sideways, because there is no other way to enter a place this close without breaking it.

Inside it could not stand. Neither can you. The ceiling is too low. So it would kneel. Green would press into its knees and elbows. Dust that never settles would hang in its lamplight. The 50-cycle snarl would get into its teeth.

It would understand, immediately, that it has been misled by lushness.

It would not see many things. It would feel one tremble all the way up the arm. One mat, wearing many shapes to meet different edges of the same hunger — cushion where it can mound, rosette where it can square itself to vibration, beads where a bolt-head offers a hill, straps where a conduit offers a hang. White felt a centimeter thick, endless, dry as old wool, running uninterrupted over basalt and plate and the rust-welded wrenches in the pried toolbox. Oil in the cells instead of sap. Iron freckles underneath. Verdigris veins where copper was eaten and re-laid.

It would understand the rules, because they are written in the body:

Nothing flows. Nothing drinks. Nothing is eaten, and nothing leaves.

Only the tower sings, and the dry garden listens.

And then — being benevolent, being wise, being able to do anything — it would do almost nothing.

It would not bring water.

That is what a smaller kindness would do. A smaller kindness would look at crackle-dry moss, at air that chaps lips, at the blind electric green in the corner where no light reaches, and pity it. It would weep, or open the roof plate to rain, or run a pipe up three thousand meters. And that pity would be murder.

Water would rot the felt. Water would short the transformer. Water would teach decay to a thing that has learned to do without mouths, and call it help. The power knows: lush without wet is not deprivation. It is a solution.

So no water. No pool, no drip, no seep.

It would not bring light.

The fronds grow edge-on to light and face-on to vibration. They do not want the sun. The palest straps still hold amber inside them, as if they remember being lit electrically from the inside. To flood them with day would blind them.

It would not bring animals. No flies, no mites, no spiders, no rot, no pollinators. No bloom, no seed, no sex. Only flaking — a dry edge breaking off, lodging a centimeter away, putting out white hairs and becoming itself again. To introduce a mouth would be to introduce hunger into a place that has balanced hunger out.

It would not clean. It would leave the stenciled 7-B half-swallowed. Leave the two ladder rungs going nowhere to serve as trellises. Leave the initials from 1978. Leave the replacement bulb still in its cardboard sleeve, cradled like an egg. What humans call neglect, the mat calls bedrock.

What would it do, then, if it would not improve?

It would keep time.

For a little while — and a little while for such a power might be an hour or sixty years, they are the same here where '64 and '82 and now are identical mounds in identical places — it would crouch in the loud dark and listen on purpose.

Not glance. Attend.

The tower howls through steel. The chain clanks... clank... clank... above in its channel. The transformer snarls its wasp-trapped-in-the-walls. The mat shivers, tuned. The power would tune itself too. It would lower its own hum until it matched, 50 cycles, then a fraction under, so that for the first time the singing has harmony. Not louder. Not different. Just accompanied.

It would let leaf-hairs comb its warmth. The moss nearest the brick is a fraction taller, a fraction yellower for ten centimeters and then stops, as if too much warmth would break the cold contract. The power would stay outside that ten centimeters. It would keep its heat folded. It would let the rock leach what it wants.

It would leave an offering that can be received under the rules: dust. A few skin cells, a little grease from a vast palm set down where a hand must be set down because you cannot crouch here without touching. Something the felt can take without drinking, without mouths. Rock dust, skin, oil, iron. It would add itself to the menu that is not a menu.

And it would remember the exact height of the mound against the stencil, so that someone, somewhere, knows this exact form existed and held.

Then it would back out sideways, ducking, and pull the low door almost — but not quite — shut. The slit of gray daylight would remain. The pencil of overcast sky from the conduit would remain. The amber jewel would remain.

If it loves this place, it will protect it the only way such a place can be protected: by obscurity. It will lay a light hand on the minds of linemen, not to blind them but to let them keep looking out and up. Let them check the cables screaming into white nothing. Let them oil the chain. Let them never think to scrape the "corrosion" off the east wall. Let the hatch stick just enough that only someone who was shown will find it.

That is all.

No miracle. No rescue. No growth.

When it is gone, the pocket remains three paces by two, full to bursting and bone dry. The dust hangs motionless in the lamplight column. The transformer sweats mist. The mat holds on, packed tight, shoulder to shoulder with itself, felt on steel on stone, face turned toward the singing.

Only now, the singing has once been heard back.

For a thing that never changes, that does not need to change, to be witnessed without being altered — that is what a benevolent and wise power would do.
