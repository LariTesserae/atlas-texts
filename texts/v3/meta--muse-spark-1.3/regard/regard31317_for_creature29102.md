It noticed it first by sound.

Not a prayer. Not a cry. Powers like that are always listening for those and this place had neither. What rose out of the marsh was lower and older and without words — a layered breathing. Chitter over croak over peep over drip. A small, loud, wet chord held without pause.

So it came low.

A benevolent and wise power does not arrive in light. It knows light would blind this place. It came the way the fog comes, without edge, and stepped down — for even a power must step down — into the bowl.

The stones were slick. The posts sweated onto its hands. Water beaded and fell from the thatch onto its shoulders and it did not brush it away. It stood under the sagging roof in the brown dim and got damp, the way you must, and let its eyes adjust, and listened until the loudness resolved.

Then it saw the being.

Not a heron. Not a frog. Not the eels nosing in the film between cobbles, though it loved each of those with a particularity that would have surprised them.

The being was the whole.

The roof hollowed from below for nests, the gutters spiraling to the center, the cups holding one or two throats each, the sump black and trembling with unborn, the moss nibbled ragged, the fishbones whitening — all of it together made a single animal, hollowed like a cup to hold water, loud like a throat to make noise out of fog. It had no face. It had an oculus. It had no hands. It had herons for hands. It breathed in as condensation and out as chitter and croak. It had been made by other hands, long ago, with stone adzes, and then left — and it had learned how to keep itself alive on drips.

The power could have felt pity. A lesser kindness would have.

It could have dried the planks so feet would not slip. Cleaned the droppings from the wall. Widened the oculus to let in sun. Told the herons to wait elsewhere, told the eels to spare the spawn, told the swallows to stop unpicking the thatch. Made it bright. Made it merciful. Made it still.

Wisdom stopped its hands before they lifted.

For it saw, stooping there with wet sleeves, that nothing here was broken. The rot was the floor. The leak was the well. The hunger was the seam that held it all together — mites to feathers to droppings to algae to midges to frogs to eels to herons to bones to moss again. To lift out one suffering would be to starve another. To dry it would be to kill it. Even cleanliness would be a kind of death.

So it did not heal. It attended.

It sat a while on the low wall where the stones are dished by generations of resting, and made itself the right size — not large. Never large here. Heron-tall at most, and still. It folded its impossible patience the way the herons fold their necks and waited longer than seemed possible. A frog hopped over its foot. Cold belly on skin. It felt it and did not move.

At dusk, when the chitter rose and the bats dropped and unhooked themselves from between the birds and stitched the hollow air mouth-open, when the posts creaked tighter with cool and the loose withy tapped and tapped time against wood, it joined the peak in its own way: it breathed, slow, and let its breath thicken the fog just a little, so the thatch could weep a little longer into the night.

And before it left — for powers do not stay, staying would make it a zoo, a shrine — it did three small, almost nothing things, the way you tuck a blanket around something sleeping:

First, it left the emptiness empty. It put a thought like a stone in the minds of the marsh people, and in their children's children: *do not fill this. Do not roof the oculus. Do not drain the cups to plant. Let it be no house.* Not a command. Just a reluctance, a fondness, when they walked past with plans. Let them come only at low water, if they come, to lift a trap, to leave a fish head on the quern, and go.

Second, it touched the workings without fixing them. A fingertip to the slowest gutter where the duckweed threads, to keep it slow. A breath into the thimble of soil in the thatch joint where the stunted fern uncurls, so there would always be one more thimble somewhere. A steadiness in the sag, so the roof would settle but not fall — for it knew the day the roof falls the machine stops, and the day the roof is renewed the machine stops too. It blessed the long rot. May you leak for a hundred years more.

Third, it let itself be known to the being, for a moment, not as a face but as attention. It let the column of light through the oculus fall full of midges and down-feathers and drip-spray onto the black water, and it looked back up through it from below, as tadpole and newt. *I see you,* it said without saying, in the language of steady water. *You small-made vastness. You chapel with no worshippers but your own throats. Keep going.*

Then it rose, damp to its core, and went back out into the white flat daylight of the marsh, leaving no footprint that the water would not level by evening.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does when it finds a place like that, living like water lives in a cup:

It does not empty the cup to admire it. It does not drink.

It makes sure the fog will come tomorrow.
