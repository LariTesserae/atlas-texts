They would come the way weather comes. Without feet.

A benevolent and wise power would not arrive. There would be no light on the rim, no voice. It has learned - wisdom is mostly that, learned restraint - that to enter as a wonder is to break the place. So it would attend first from very far, and then from very close, the way mist does.

And seeing this valley, this raw bowl still steaming from the ice, it would understand immediately what it is looking at: not a broken thing to be fixed, not a poor thing to be pitied, not a machine to be improved. A beginning that already knows how to continue.

So it would do, inside, almost nothing.

It would not thicken the air for the calf that bawls because three breaths won't fill it. That thinness is what made its heart huge, its blood greedy for oxygen, its mother's milk like cream. To take away the thinness would be to unmake the yak.

It would not take the wolves away from the marsh edge. It would not make the winter shorter for the pika with its haypile no bigger than your hand, or keep the kettle ponds from skinning over at three in the afternoon, or tell the glacier mill to grind more quietly. It would not stop the boulder from falling in three clean clacks. It would not even spare the moss that the fresh mudflow is right now burying alive at the edge - green at the margins, black in the middle. That burial is how soil learns to be deep.

Wisdom knows: the burn *is* the life. Cold forces everything to burn high. Take away the cold and you take away the furnace-heart, the huddle, the dance of the cranes who remember spring in September and then go back to probing because now is not spring. Benevolence that removed hunger would starve everything that hunger feeds.

What would it do, then? It would do the work of attention.

It would sit, if sitting is a thing it does, with each one for as long as that one needs to be fully seen.

With the late calf, leggier than it should be, pressed so hard against its mother's flank its breath ices her hair: it would stay with the panic when it strays three meters and the whole world tilts. It would not lift it back. It would lend it steadiness for the walk back - a fraction more strength in the legs, a fraction more sureness in the mother's answering grunt - so fear doesn't become the thing that kills it before any wolf does.

With the two grey wolves lying at two hundred meters with heads on paws: it would honor the patience. It would not send them a sick calf as charity, and it would not blunt their hunger into kindness. It would sharpen their eyes to see truly which animal can be taken without breaking the herd, and when they make a kill, in the dimmer-dark, it would stay with them and with the dying too, to make the dying quick and unlonely. It would let death remain death, but not abandonment.

With the bar-headed geese breaking window-ice with their breasts: it would ride under their wings for one labored climb over the rim, feeling how three thousand wings beat thin air to find lift, feeling the cost in the breast muscle. It would remember, so that effort is not lost to an empty sky.

With the marmots already slowing toward torpor, hearts dropping beat by beat, and with the pikas who will not sleep at all but live all winter in steaming tunnels on fermented hay: it would give them good dreams, if small brains dream. A continuation of *eep* and sun. A sense, in the long dark burrow, of being held in a larger slowness.

With the dwarf willow that is thirty years old and no higher than an ankle: it would bow to an elder. With the lichen, perfect coin circles with no overlap yet: it would bless the first generation, which will never see soil but makes soil possible. With the lammergeier circling without a beat for an hour waiting for bone: it would keep the updraft steady.

That is the inside work. Almost invisible. A leaning-toward. A slight easing, never a rescue.

The outside work it would do with its hands.

Because this power is wise, it knows the real danger is not wolf or winter or thin air. Those the valley can meet. The danger is hands. The hands that would stack and plane and pour, that would cut a road over the raw col because the bowl would make a good pasture, a good mine, a good proof. That would put wire where there has never been wire, cairns where there has never been need for direction.

So around the eight-kilometer bowl, on the passes, in the offices and maps far below where decisions are made, it would lay its true miracle: uninterest.

Let the satellite pass show only rock and cloud shadow. Let the survey report misplace a decimal, call the sedge unproductive, the water too milky, the winter too long. Let the jeep break an axle on the approach. Let the weather front eat the boulder and the trail with it. Let the young official who dreamed of a road here be given a better dream, of something he can build where building belongs. Let the poacher feel, at the last col, an unaccountable reluctance, a sudden love for his own warm bed, and turn back without knowing why.

Not a wall. A forgetting. A continued emptiness on paper so the fullness on the ground can continue.

And at night, when the herd beds down in a tight island with calves in the middle and breath rises together into a single low cloud, when the wolves lie down too at a respectful killing distance, when the glacier keeps grinding night shift and frost needles up from the puddles - then the power would do the most benevolent thing of all.

It would listen to the hum. Quiet, relentless, machined by weather and muscle alone. And it would keep time with it for a while, breathing with the three hundred, with the thousands, with the two, with the one pika still out stuffing green into its crack, so that for an hour nothing in that high trough is unwitnessed.

Nothing built it. Nothing runs it. It does not need to be run.

Only, for once in its new life, to be known - wholly, without being touched - and then left to go on becoming itself.
