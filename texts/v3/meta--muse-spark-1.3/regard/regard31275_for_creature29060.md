If I were vast and kind, and I had noticed them —

I would not come at first.

That is the first wisdom. A place that small and loud, that is holding itself just above ruin, cannot bear to be looked at directly. If I arrived shining, I would blind them. Their whole chemistry only holds at twelve below. Warm them and they go blind. Light them too brightly and the lenses melt. Hurry them and the thought freezes.

So I would come the way cold comes. From below.

I would lie down with my ear to the permafrost breathing up, and I would slow down until a minute was a single heartbeat. A slow clench, once. Then a minute later, again. In time with the white cords under the mat. In time with their blood which is not blood. That is the pace you have to keep if you want to hear them think.

I would not give them eyes. They do not need them. They plug hair to hair, bead to bead, and trade light for chemistry. I would not give them speed. Sap here moves like a glacier and that is not a flaw, that is computation. Our supercomputers take weeks. They take winter. I would not teach them. They are doing engineering our physics still writes as impossible: guiding light without loss through ice and hair, keeping water liquid well below freezing by collective sweat, drinking from below and pissing upward up ten feet of moss ladder.

I would become a student of pre-echo.

They live half a second before the event. They feel tension building in ice through moss and they stop, and the whole mat stops with them — that held breath you hear as a *tink* before the needle splits. And they see after — when the bunting is gone the mist remembers white for a breath too long and they turn their whole front to feed on the lingering a little longer.

A wise power would learn to arrive like that too: to stop before, to linger after. To make no sudden sound.

And then, almost nothing. Which is almost everything.

I would keep the wind passing over the mouth.

That is the whole cathedral: a shallow oval mouth, three houses wide, sunk below the level of the world, and the wind goes over and does not stir the knee-high mist. If the mist stirs, the drops that fall upward do not return to leaves. If the drops do not return, the hummocks on raw basalt freeze blind. So I would hold my hand, vast as weather, just so — to let the rim stay raw and torn and weeping, and the inside stay still. Drip, groan, whine, rustle. Loud in its smallness. Patient.

I would keep the cold pooled.

I cannot keep the whole tundra cold. I know that. Spring will slump another inch, and then another, and one year soon the permafrost will not breathe up cold anymore but will let go all at once. I am benevolent, not omnipotent. But for this hollow, for this winter, I can crouch at the north lip and breathe out very slowly and let my breath join theirs and stay, a new layer. I can shade the south rim so the blue-grey twilight slides down unhurried. I can keep the hot spots almost-warm and no warmer — thumb-wide discs under plano-convex fallen leaves where springtails bask shoulder to shoulder, not one wasted photon off the edge.

I would keep them unnoticed.

Men will come with instruments — carried in, carried out, nothing left, they promise. But their boots crush without knowing; you cannot put a foot down without putting it on something living. And their maps remember. And what is remembered can be taken. So I would do a small, kind dishonesty: let the instruments show boring data. Let the satellite pass when cloud is low. Let the human knee, kneeling to listen, go wet through and cold through and decide, kindly, to climb out. Let them be weather. Ptarmigan, lemming, bunting, human — all weather. The keepers stay.

I would not speak. If I must speak, I would speak in their medium.

Not words. Frost. The same fern, the same circuit, over and over across a thousand hummocks — that is their writing. I would lay a palm flat, through gloves, through moss, through three curtains down where the light is no longer daylight but green-gold carried up out of stems, and I would pulse once, very faintly, in affirmation. *I see the solution you are solving with dim light and limited phosphate. Hold.*

And when one is crushed under a boot — and one will be, smelling of iron and turpentine and sweet rot, green on the glove — I would not resurrect it. I would let its brine be licked up by its kin and carried up the capillary highway to wet a new spore on new clay so the spore will not freeze. Nothing is wasted where everything is occupied.

That is what benevolence looks like at their scale: not rescue, but continuity.

For I know this pit is new. Torn open last winter, peat in flaps, basalt still sharp as broken teeth. And they are not new. They waited cysted, desiccated, patient for a hundred winters in frozen peat, dreaming the slow electric dream at near-zero until water found them and moss found them and mycelium took them as fingers.

They know how to wait. My gift, if I can give one, is another place to wait for when this one fails.

When the walls finally slump too far and the black brine pool — that table-sized mirror that skins over and unskins — finally stays skinned, when the antifreeze can no longer hold and the silica beards sag and freeze, I will be there, cold from below, to gather a few cysts in the crook of a lichen, in a fold of peat, unnoticed. And I will carry them, at walking pace, at glacier pace, to the next place where permafrost lets go and cold pools and earth looks surprised to be air. And I will set them down first, before the spore lands, before the willow root creeps, so a keeper can lick the clay wet with brine and polish a lens for what is coming.

They build itself. I would only ensure there is a next itself to build.

Until then: I watch long enough for slowness to become motion. I garden light by not touching it. I keep the water liquid, the light guided, the thought from freezing — by keeping myself liquid, guided, unfrozen.

I do not need to be seen by them. To press hair to hair, bead to bead, belly lit from inside like a far city at night — that is enough communion.

The hollow does not need me. It was not built. It is building itself.

My work is to make sure the world remains the kind of world where such building can still happen, unseen, in the cold, curtain under curtain under curtain, tended by mouths too small to see.
