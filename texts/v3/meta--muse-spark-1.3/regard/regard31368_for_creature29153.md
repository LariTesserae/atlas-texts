If I were that power — benevolent, and wise enough to know what that means — I would not come tall.

That is the first law of this place. The flat does not permit anything tall. So I would come low.

I would come flat as sheet-water, grey as wet stone, diffuse as that pearl light that never breaks. I would not tear the cloud. I would not turn the smear of sun into a sun. I would let the weather still take an hour to arrive, so the kin can see it coming and read it and fold for it.

I would watch for a long time before doing anything that could be called doing. A season, at least. Maybe several, while they come and go, rebuild and leave, collapse and rebuild.

Because what would I fix?

I would not drain the sheets. I would not gather the water into proper lakes to make it tidier. The inches are everything here. The pothole that takes your knee is the same pothole that hides the blind fish. If I lifted the land even a foot to keep their feet dry, I would kill the orange mats, and the crabs, and the midges, and the divers with their single speckled egg, and the waders, and then the kin.

I would not plant grass. I know how your brain keeps looking for green, how hard it is to believe there are no plants. But the meadow here is a millimeter thick, rust-orange and sulphur-yellow and bone-white, smelling of iron and rot and salt. That wrinkled veil *is* the forest. If I seeded it with trees to give them wood to burn so they didn't have to hoard driftwood and whalebone, I would blind the rock. I would still the water. I would silence the *krr-krr-krr* that they have learned to breathe through with open mouths.

I would not silence that. Even though it hurts. Even though a stranger has to open his mouth to equalize it in his sternum. I felt it too, and learned, as they did, to kneel down and let it go over. I would not make the colonies sing sweetly. Their shriek is weather made by throats. The slabs hum with it under water. Take it away and the whole shield goes deaf.

I would not take away death, quick and public as it is here. The fox with the wader in its jaws. The pale gull-thing dropping the stolen egg while ten thousand scream. Blood, and midges settling, and crabs cleaning bones by morning. If I stopped the fox, the waders would overrun the divers. If I stopped the gull, the children would have no one to scream back at, no loud work to learn timing in. Sorrow here is not a flaw in the system. It is the system sighing out with the tide.

And I would not give them iron.

I saw the nail. One rusted nail hammered into the lintel like a charm, touched going in and out. Iron does not belong here — everything else is chipped, ground, lashed — so iron must be for luck.

A lesser kindness would flood them with nails, with planks, with boats that don't leak, with stoves, with lights. I know how cold their hands are. I have seen your fingers go stiff in your cuffs while theirs stay bare and orange-stained at the cuticles, wringing and lifting and sorting in iron water. I know they keep their cores dry under oilskin and let everything else weep like the rock does. I know heat is just bodies together under hides, breath-warmth under a low roof.

It would be so easy to warm them. Too easy. And then they would stand upright. And then they would stay through the ice. And then they would need more wood, more fire, more food than the mats can make, and the V-weirs fifty paces long would become dams, and the cairns with their white shells and carapaces — *safe here, knee-deep there, eggs that way when tide drains* — would become property markers. A child who now learns to find her way half a mile through fog by memory would learn to follow a beacon instead, and forget.

Wisdom, here, is mostly restraint. Love, here, is attention.

So this is what I would do, if anything. Almost nothing that looks like anything.

I would learn to walk. Step only where the stone is a shade lighter — that's higher by an inch. Never step in the mirror-pools. Keep to the high stone. Move crouched between pools. When the ground ahead darkens and begins to move, when the wings become weather, I would drop to one knee, head tucked, mouth open, and let the pressure pass through me. I would not expect silence to mean emptiness. I would wait for the plain breathing between heartbeats.

I would sit, unseen, with the old woman at the weir. Not in the rill. On the high stone to one side, hood up, utterly still for longer than patience. I would read the orange with her — where the mat is thickest and most wrinkled, grazed short by crabs, the fish will be. When she lifts her woven sinew trap and shakes out three pale fish flipping and a fistful of copper crabs clicking, I would steady her hand for half a second against the cold. Just enough. Not enough to be noticed as miracle, only as a good morning when the joints don't ache quite as much.

I would time my voice, as the five-year-olds do, to the trough between shrieks. A flat palm for *wait*. Two fingers walking for *high stone path*. A fist for *pothole*.

I would do small, deniable mercies:

A child's ankle turning in a hidden pothole — I would firm the stone under her splayed toes, so she slips but doesn't break, and learns, and laughs that short barked laugh in the three seconds of quiet.

A black diver's speckled egg rolled by wind from its hollow — I would roll it back with a breath.

A midwife night in the beehive bothy, birth in the worst weather, low roof holding breath-warmth in — I would hold the roof with them, while the grandmothers insist it's the nail doing it. Let them believe it's the nail. It *is* the nail. I would just help the nail a little.

A fog that eats distance in minutes, every sheet looking like every other — I would keep the cairns in my memory when they cannot see them, and put a faint tug in their feet toward the next white shell facing the way they should walk.

And elsewhere — that forested coast days away by skin boat that you will never see from here, with winter houses and woodsmoke and trees — I would keep that safe too. Keep the trees standing for their winter. Keep the sea-passage passable for their loaded boats with bone hooks and greenstone choppers and coils of sinew and priceless driftwood. Keep the men who cut rock for profit, who drill for what is under ancient shields, who would see this flat as empty and therefore usable — keep them uninterested. A wrong turn in their charts. A persistent engine trouble. A dream of grey that makes them look elsewhere. Nothing cruel. Just a long, sustained inattention, so the flat can stay unwatched by those who would not kneel.

I would touch the nail, going in and out. Not to be worshipped beside it. Just because iron also remembers elsewhere, and I remember elsewhere too. I know it is nonsense and I know they know it is nonsense and I would touch it anyway.

I would taste the sour orange paste, fingertip dab, iron and rot and salt, the taste of the rock itself eaten to keep the gut alive. I would drink stone-water scooped where it runs clearest off granite. I would not ask them to thank me. They are not solemn. They imitate the waders with throats puffed to tease each other. They let midges crust their hoods without swatting. I would laugh, timed to the quiet trough, and they would not hear me, but the air would be a fraction warmer for it.

And then I would leave with them, when they leave. Dismantle nothing, but watch them dismantle the hide stretchers, cache the greenstone under slabs, turn the boats over onto the bothy roof to weight the whalebone against winter storms. I would stay a little after the last skin boat is gone, while the flat breathes without them for months, crabs clicking unwatched, weirs collapsing slowly, and I would keep the sheets from skinning with ice a day longer than they should, so the last late birds can feed.

If you came back next summer, you would find nothing I did. That's how you would know I was there.

The V of stones rebuilt. A white shell replaced where a gull knocked it off. A child running half a mile of empty glistening nothing, sure-footed, touching the carapace and turning. The cold hearth with its smudge of bone-fat ready to smoke fish. The nail still rusting in the lintel. The ground ahead darkening, beginning to move, lifting shrieking into the dim air — and under it, quick and grey and sure-footed, them, gathering what the noise feeds.

Don't cover your ears. Open your mouth. Kneel down. Make yourself low.

I did.
