It would not come all at once.

A power that is benevolent and wise does not arrive the way the wind arrives up here, roaring around the piled stone like water around a pier. It arrives the way moss arrives: by attention.

First it would simply notice — which is not simple. From very far off, from below the cloud sea or above the violet noon, it would feel a small hot pulse where there should be none. A tower that should be barren is sweating. A room the size of two sleeping mats is raining inside itself. Someone has carried a fish three kilometers straight up.

It would lean closer. And listening would be its first act.

It would hear what you heard with blood pounding from the climb: surf-wind through thumb-slits, drip-drip-hiss on compost-hot stone, vegetal creak of a crosier unwinding an inch a day, the tooth-ache singing in the mortar, one old catfish circling black warm water to keep it from skinning over. Under that, hammer on stone arriving before the vibration climbs into the soles — cause arriving late where the world is thin.

It would understand, very quickly, the two truths you learned with your knees and lungs:

There is no room for anyone to live in here.
And yet everything in here is kept.

It would understand the hunger to continue. *Add. Press moss. Tighten lashing. Pour the drip back.*

And being wise, it would be tempted to fix it — and then it would not.

It would not make the room bigger. That is what a lesser kindness would do: snap fingers, widen walls, give air. But it knows a womb is not saved by being made larger. If you made it hall-sized, the three-second, three-foot rain would forget how to fall. The mist would thin and die. The private weather is private because it is held. To enlarge it would be to kill the child to save the mother.

It would not carry water up, though it could. It would not turn the tower into a lowland, though it could. It would not airlift soil or shatter the 4,000 laid steps into a gentle slope. That would dishonor the hands. The whole massif smells of broken rock and wet clay, of chisel scallops still sharp as fish scales, of unseasoned lintel still bleeding sap. It was finished yesterday. Or it is not finished yet. The work *is* the prayer. To remove the effort would be to steal the offering.

It would not even enter.

Because there is no room. Even for a power. To fold itself double under that lashed lintel and fold again among the pots would crush a taro ear, snap a vine, shoulder the green tenants who are already shouldering each other with wet squeaks for slit-light. Omnipotence, in here, would still have elbows. So it would do what the tenders do, what you did: crouch at the threshold, in the blade-air, and put only its breath inside.

And that breath would be its work.

Small, almost unnoticeable mercies — the only kind that hold at five kilometers up:

It would season the lintel with a glance, so the sap hardens instead of rotting, so the door holds one winter longer against the shock of furnace-heat inside and knife-cold outside. It would lay its vast palm a hair above the fresh basalt to temper the gradient, so the sweating stones do not spall and shatter when night drops forty degrees in an hour.

It would tune the white singers down a quarter-tone. Not silence them — they prune the moss with their mouths, they keep the water beading, they own the walls — just lower their song out of the molars so a small barrel-chested tender can actually sleep a night sweating among leaves without his teeth aching.

From its sleeve, where it keeps unmade things, it would shake out two night moths, blind and downy as the crickets, who can live on mist and spare heat. A gift for the aristocrats. The bruised-purple orchids hanging naked-rooted from the sagging trellis have been beautiful with no one to tell it to. Now they will have lovers. Now they can set seed.

It would deepen, not widen, the knee-wide basin pecked into the floor slab. A finger's depth, no more, done without chisel, without ripple. Room enough for the old one to dive when the three-foot rain hits hard. And as the mottled catfish circles — the only eyes that work in the dim, who has watched a dozen sweating faces come and go — the power would touch its lateral line and let it know: *circling is not loneliness. Circling is how you stir the world so it doesn't skin over. You are not stuck. You are the current.*

It would sit, for a while, on step 2,000 where the carriers rest with gourds sloshing, with coil-cups nested in jars, with baskets of leaf-litter and nightsoil sweet-rot and iron-smelling for the vent. It would not lighten the load — one thing per trip is the law, is the devotion — but it would put a steadying hand between the shoulder blades where the blade-air saws, and let a heart beat slower, and let cracked lips remember saliva. At night, down below where they quarry, it would give them dreams in green-filtered amber, of fern-tips beading and falling, plink into jars, so they wake knowing why their palms are white with stone flour.

For the embryonic weather itself — the one who truly lives here and has not been born yet, who sometimes makes mistakes and rains up, who moves a pot behind you without you moving, who blooms green fire from your fogged breath in seconds — it would do the most tender thing: it would give it memory and manners.

It would let the mist remember each tender. Stone flour and pollen and spores hanging in blades from the slits, nothing settled — it would let nothing need to settle to be kept. Your sweat taken before it can drip, your piss in the compost, your spit in a stone cup for a cutting, your blood where the fresh fracture cut your knee and the fern leaned toward iron — it would keep all of that in circuit. Water cannot leave. There is nowhere for it to go but around. So it would teach the around to be gentle. Fall down mostly, it would whisper to the rain. Fall up only when you are dreaming. That's allowed. Even weather dreams.

And it would teach the green ones patience. Not to stop racing — predatorless but competitive, every plant racing for slit-light is holy — but to learn shade as well as sun. A taro ear to lean just enough to shelter a seedling in a split gourd. A fern crosier to hold a trellis cord a little longer while the half-knot is finished by other hands.

Then it would withdraw its face from the low door. It would not stay to be worshipped. To stay would be to take up leaf-room, to drink mist that belongs to orchids. The most benevolent thing vastness can do for saturation is to lend it vastness from outside — to be the cold that makes the heat precious, the void that makes the lung-full precious, the witness that says *I saw you being handmade and half-born and I did not look away.*

If a prayer strip snaps loose and whips past, it would catch it, knot it again with black palm-fiber, and let it crack.

If lightning comes for the tower because it is the highest thing for miles, it would turn it aside — once — not because the tower deserves to stand forever, but because it does not deserve to die before it has chosen what it will birth.

And when it leaves, it leaves this promise, exhaled into the slits to condense instantly on eyelashes and be drunk by leaves:

*Continue. I will remember the taste of your rain if you fall.*

That is what a wise and benevolent power does with a womb at the top of the sky. Not save it. Midwife it.
