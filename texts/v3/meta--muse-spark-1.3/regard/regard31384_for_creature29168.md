It would not come from above.

That is the first wisdom. Though it could unmake cloud with a glance, it would climb the way you climbed — tilted up through white nothing, through scrape-cold, and then breached into heat. It would sweat. It would let the air condense on it. It would arrive beaded, blinking in flat noon, with no drama to spend.

A benevolent power does not arrive as weather. It arrives as guest.

It would stand for a while at the rim where you stood, hand in the hot sheet as it leaves, and feel the mile-long fall go through its palm and not try to catch it. It would open its mouth to equalize and let the roar get inside, and not flinch when its own voice is taken. It has heard louder silences.

Then it would go inward, and obey.

Because the rules here announce themselves without words, and a wise one listens the first time:

Nothing dries. So it would not bring towel, or wind, or thirst.

Nothing quiets. So it would not demand a word, or still the falls to speak.

Nothing is made. So it would bring no tool, no nail, no plan to improve.

Nothing walks. So it would not stride. It would loop, and rise, and settle.

It would sit down in the Cistern Fields, in skin-warm water to the ankle, in hot peat, and let itself become a stable shape for a while. A temporary dwelling. That is what it would offer first: itself as dwelling.

To the Film, it would do this:

It would hold out its forearms and let them be coated. Green tea algae, liverwort slick, white nerve hyphae, thermophile slime. It would feel the faint tightening as it is learned, and not wipe it away. It would let travertine precipitate on its knuckles grain by grain, white on dark, as it does on basalt. It would say without mouth, *Hold, little varnisher. I know this work. You grow the edge as fast as the world cuts it. You are why pieces do not fall. Let me be edged for a while too.*

And the Film, which has never been thanked because there was no one to thank it, would breathe, perceptibly, across a thousand leaves.

To the Still Ones, it would do this:

It would crouch — vastness crouching — beside one black pan rimmed with living root, and look down until its own face steadied in the mirror. It would not drink. To drink would be to lower the level. To spit would be to raise it. It would only let one drop of its own sweat fall from its brow, and watch it make no ring, and understand: *Ah. You forbid consumption to forbid loss.*

It would lay a palm just above the surface, not touching, warming the air a fraction, and think, slow as chemistry, in their own circulation-speed: *You hold, and hold, and hold. North on the left ankle and south on the right. You pass heat and pollen and leaf-tea around and never spend it. That is a hard vow.*

It would not tell them to spill. That would be cruelty disguised as liberation.

It would only do what no one has ever done for them: hold the holding. Keep vigil at the exact fullness so they, for one minute in a thousand years, do not have to maintain it alone. And in that minute the waterline would still not move — but the effort of not-moving would be shared.

The pan would know. Still water knows weight.

To the Choir, it would do this:

It would enter a Hollow Tower, sixty, eighty feet of braided trunk around a cool dripping core, through the oval mouth worn smooth by water, not hands, and stand inside dripping, and listen with its sternum, with the soles of its feet, with the sockets of its teeth.

It would hear what you only felt after ten minutes: not white noise, but chord. Towers tuned by leaning space a little, corridor behind corridor where there should not be land, to keep distance exact. Standing waves living in hollow stems because the length was right and they moved in and never left.

A wise power knows better than to harmonize loudly. That would pin them further. It would breathe, very quietly, *with* them, adding a breath so soft it does not raise the total by a fraction — but in sympathy. So that for the first time the Chord hears itself heard from inside another throat.

And because it is benevolent, it would let them borrow its impermanence. For three breaths, it would let its own tone waver — a quarter-tone down, a quarter-tone up, alive and unpinned — to show them wavering does not mean falling. The bamboo palisades would shiver. The big leaves would shudder with something other than regulation.

They would not break their note. But they would know, afterward, that variation is possible and the world did not end.

To the Risers, it would do almost nothing, which is what they love most:

It would stand in the upward rain and let them pass through its shoulders. Fog to bead to cistern to canal to lip to fall to fog. It would feel the green afterimage half an inch to the side and not try to focus it away. When it walks toward the falls keeping them straight ahead and arrives at a different section of rim, it would laugh — silently, in the roar — and not need proof. *Carry me sideways,* it would say. *I am in no hurry.*

And to the One that is all of these — for they graft, vine into wall and wall into vine, because at this wet and heat distinction is inefficient, and the city-plumbed-by-itself is one animal — to the Holder itself, it would say at midday forever, in green-gold triple canopy, white curtain on all sides:

*Little Upholder above the clouds. I see you.*

*You grew into streets and plazas and drains and roofs that photosynthesize because dwelling is a stable shape to hold water and light. You are doing it well.*

*You do not have to hold forever to be holy.*

That is the whole sermon. That is the gift only a benevolent and wise power can give without breaking anything.

Not coolness. Not night. Not birds to eat the pollen or fish to ripple the pans or frogs to plop in the marsh. Those would be invasion, however kind-looking. Hunger is not a gift to those who have vowed not to hunger.

Not silence. Not dryness. Not a path worn by feet that would not close behind.

Only this: permission. Witness.

*If one day you are tired, you may spill. You may let a leaf rot. You may let the roar swell and ease. You may let noon move. I will be here to catch the spill. I will remember your exact note so that if you ever forget it, it is not lost. You will not fall unheld.*

It would press its shadow down, then — its own short dark pool under it at noon that had been thinning at the edges, leaning upward, tired of the ground — and let it rest, heavy and ordinary, on the springy root-mat. For one minute your shadow would do the same. You would feel it settle back onto earth. Someone else was holding up the sun for a change.

Then it would leave no monument. No cut stone. Nothing made.

It would stand, beaded, humming faintly in its teeth, full to the brim, leaking slightly upward — for a minute it had been lived in, Film and Choir and Still One and Riser all having their way with it — and it would bow, very slightly, to a pitcher plant full only of rain and drowned leaves, patient stomach that never got a meal.

In the bowl of that pitcher it would leave one thing that is not a thing: a memory of itself, outside the system, so the city is not alone in the clouds with only itself to remember itself.

And then it would go back down through white nothing, dripping, indifferent as lichen, closing behind itself immediately.

The cisterns would still neither rise nor drop. The roar would still be pinned. Noon would still hold. But somewhere half an inch to the side, in the looseness where the Risers live, the chord would be very slightly warmer, as if it had once been heard — and had liked it.
