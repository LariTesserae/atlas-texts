It hears it before it sees it.

Not with ears. With the way a hillside hears rain coming — a change in pressure, a thickening. Far below the dry scrub, in a place its light never fully reaches, something is throbbing: wet, hot, crowded, alive. Boiler-breath and insect-choir and water hitting hot iron. And underneath that, fainter, the thing that makes it lean closer:

listening.

Someone down there is listening back.

So it comes to the lip and looks down.

It has seen sinkholes before. It has seen quarries cut too deep and roofs give way. It knows how limestone settles, how forest takes back. But this — this bowl, hamlet-sized, three hundred paces across, eighty meters down, held in the grip of three or four vast root-trees — this is not a ruin. This is not a wound. This is a body that has healed around a splinter of iron and learned to use it as a bone.

It sees the terraces spiraling down in wide, uneven rings. The second, broken canopy turning noon into green dusk. The amber lamps, one by one, spiraling up through gloom. The catwalks bouncing as the monkeys cross. The snow of moth-scale turning in chimney heat. The white waterfall pouring over the rim for ten minutes and then stopping, and the whole bowl steaming after.

And the citizens — not in houses, but between them. Moth clouds. Swifts by dim day, bats by dimmer night. Rats single-file on copper veins. Ants ten wide. Coatis and thin dogs at the oil-cake. Beetles humming on boiler walls. Blind white fish boiling in cisterns. And inside all that motion, slowly, like faces in bark: the humans. Pruned fingertips, vine-braided hair dusted white, pupils blown wide, ears always cocked. Hoarse voices leaning forehead to forehead to be heard.

A gut flora. Hot, loud, damp, worn round-shouldered to fit.

And being benevolent, its first impulse is to help.

Being wise, it stops and asks what help would mean here.

Would it bring sun? It could lift the leaf-roof, let noon fall clean to the bottom. The Sump-born would bleed from the nose in the whiteness. Their wide pupils would burn. The moths would scatter. The figs that only ripen in the hot shadow behind the chimney would not ripen. The tendrils that glow faintly green with stolen charge would go dark.

Would it bring silence? It could still the press-mill thump, the nine-second sigh, the ten thousand drips, the cicada waves. Then no one could navigate. The mill means center. The drip-wall means west. The cicada swell means the canopy is about to shed water. Mothers slap children's hands away from their ears for a reason. To quiet the Sump would be to blind it.

Would it bring dryness? It could drain the damp that hangs in the air, dry their skin, unprune their fingers, straighten their splayed feet. Then the walls would stop weeping. The cisterns would empty. The pitcher-plant vats would wither. The roots would go slick no more after rim-rain — and the children would have no waterfall to slide, screaming with the swallows.

Would it bring cure? Scale-lung, brown teeth from drip-distillate, stippled burns on stokers' forearms, deafness in the old that makes them vulnerable as blindness up top would be. It could wipe all that clean. But the glitter in the pressmen's cough is moth-scale lodged deep from a lifetime breathing the same air as the swarm. To take it out would be to take them out of the exchange. They eat together behind the mill — humans and monkeys and coatis — nut oil, leaf bread, fish stew, chimney figs. No leftovers. Everything eats everything else, quickly. Nothing rots. To make them separate, clean, untouched — that would be to unmake them.

Would it raise the dead? It sees the Eaters' Vigil. Someone always staying with the corpse, talking loudly over the noise so the listening continues, until kin are ready to lower it to the compost vaults where heat does the work fast. Bones coming back clean in days and set into root-cracks to fill them. The chapel bell gone to seed, full of pigeon nests. The pigeons who learned the steam whistle perfectly. It could give them back a proper funeral, a bell that rings. But they already have a liturgy: telling where each sound is. They already gather when the canopy sheds, because it's high and relatively dry, and they listen together.

So the wise and benevolent power does not lift them out.

It does not fix the Sump.

It does something smaller, and harder.

It stays.

It learns, first, to navigate by sound the way they do. It does not announce itself in light — light hurts here, light lies. It speaks low, where they live. In the nine-second sigh, a half-beat longer, easier to breathe with in dreams. In the transformer drone, a steadiness the old can feel through copper wire when held by grandchildren. In the cicada swell, a fraction earlier, so there is time to get under cover before the canopy sheds.

It does what lamp-tenders do.

Every building has to keep an amber lamp burning or you lose the path in true dark. After a rim-rain, the lamp-tenders walk the catwalks with long poles, relighting, calling out. The power walks with them, unseen. Not adding new lamps — the gaps between lights are true dark for a reason, stay on the lit walk is a rule for a reason — but wiping. A moth-scale less on the glass. A wick trimmed a breath cleaner. A condensation bead held a moment longer before it falls. So the path holds.

It sits the Vigil.

No one leaves the dead alone because the cleanup crew works faster than any burial. Leave a peel and it's gone in twenty minutes. Leave a body for an hour — So someone stays, talking loudly over the noise. Sometimes kin are late from a far ledge, the rope bridge bucking when the swarms pass. Sometimes the watcher is tired from Change watch and wants to sleep. The power sits then. Not instead of them — with them. A warmth pooling, shirt-sticking warm, breath-warm. A sense that the talking is heard even under the stack of boiler and insect and voice. That the listening continues.

It keeps the rim.

Up top, in dry scrub and white light, there are hungry eyes. Geologists with maps. Quarrymen who remember cutting too deep. Men who would see root-pressure tapped like an artery and think *free power*, see seed-press oil and think *yield*, see two dozen ledges and think *clear it*. The power does not smite them. It is benevolent even to them. It just makes the Sump uninteresting from above. From the lip, nothing but scrub and a dip and insect noise. The bright lid stays small. Swallows pour over like water over a dam — lovely, not profitable. Let them walk past shouting, and hear only their echo back. Let them think it is empty. It has practice being full where they look for the wrong size of citizen.

And it keeps the rain passing over. Once or twice a day, ten minutes of white thundering down stair-roots turned to streams. Not more — more would drown the cisterns, rot the chain-and-plank. Not less — less and the walls stop weeping, the town stops steaming after. Just enough. Just enough for the runners to play.

That is all. That is everything.

If you stood at the bottom now, where the oldest root has cupped into a black pool of drip-water and oil-sheen, and looked up — terraces of light receding, bridges swaying, slow snow turning — you would not see it. You would feel, perhaps, that the bowl is held. That the sky, that small bright lid far above, is not indifferent. That someone has learned pigeon well enough to tell true whistle from bird, and tilts its head and says *bird* with the old hands, and smiles when the apprentices fall for it.

The Sump does not need saving. It needs what all living things need: to not be alone in its listening, to not be straightened out, to be allowed to breathe a half-inch in and out when the boilers vent without being told to hold still.

So the power does not descend with trumpets or drain the pit or raise the walls.

It leans in, hand on forearm, forehead almost touching, mouth to ear over the roar — the way they talk to each other —

and listens, and stays warm, and keeps the light wiped clean.
