It would not come with tools.

That is the first thing. No wrenches, no plans, no crew, no antidote. It has all of those, the way the lid has rain, but it would leave them folded elsewhere. Because it has learned — the way you learn only if you are very old and very kind — that most places called ruined are only places whose citizens you don't yet recognize.

It would stop on the lip.

Where you stopped. Where the heat-forest thins and cuts off as if with a knife, and the whole bowl lies below at once: the soft green levee, the islands of brick gone black to waist height, the pipe-bridges, the hot canal gut moving fast, the three boiler houses breathing, the dynamo hall with its flywheels throwing shadows, the stack, the second canopy of vine and cable, the north pond steaming turquoise under its mat of lettuce. The low lid holding it all in. THUMP — THUMP — THUMP up through the soles.

It would not call it empty. It would not call it wrong.

It would sit down on the levee a while and listen the way you listen to a sleeping animal before you touch it: to learn the rate of it.

Eleven minutes. Scream, roar, fog, settle. Hiss. Clank. Burr of the saw on nothing. Sixty-cycle hum. Thump that you feel before you hear.

It would learn the rules without being told. Stay on the boards. Do not drink the drip. Turn your back when the stack begins to scream.

So it would stay on the boards. It is big enough to step clean over the bowl if it wanted. It would not. It would make itself small enough to need the duckboards, narrow enough to pass single-file under the leaf-and-cable tunnels where water ticks down on the neck. It would make itself animal enough, for a little while, to be permitted briefly, and to feel what it costs to be permitted.

It would come down in the dim brown noon, when the light is like late evening, and walk the perimeter slowly. Not inspecting. Visiting.

To the banana rings around the boiler house, it would put — if it has a hand, and it will have one for this — a hand to a torn leaf. They are shredded every eleven minutes and put out a new roller a week, pale and perfect, cloning daughter to mother to grandmother because there are no bats, no bees under the lid. It would not fix them by bringing bats to die in the scald. It would touch the new roller where it is still furled and damp, and say in the way such powers speak, which is not with shouting though you have to shout here:

*Good. You continue.*

And they would quicken, almost imperceptibly, not growing faster but growing less alone.

To the taro in the ditches, stalks thick as a wrist plumbed straight into 40-degree runoff, corms gently boiling in black mud, it would kneel — the boards creaking — and feel the heat coming up. Where a man would scald, they quicken. It would not cool them. It would warm its own hands to their temperature so as not to shock them, and leave them to their chosen scald.

To the banyans straddling the machine shop, gripping masonry like hands, sending prop-root curtains down into copper-green outfall and transpiring it back out to make more lid, slowly crushing the shop even as they shade it — it would stand a long time in the green shadow and lay its forehead, if it has one, against a warm, faintly pulsing root.

*You are holding two things that cannot both live,* it would say. *Building and tree. And you are doing it anyway. I see how heavy that is.*

It would not separate them. Wisdom knows building-and-tree is now one structure. To "save" one would be to kill both.

To the second canopy, morning glory and bitter melon lacing cable to vine to cable until you can't tell which holds which, tightening slack belts with tendril, flowering unvisited in furled trumpets — it would look up and let drip fall on its face without flinching. Clear and tempting, tasting of pennies and hot stone. It would not drink, but it would not wipe it away in disgust either. It would let the sweat of the thermophiles sit on its skin a minute. To be willing to be condensed on is a kind of love.

To the floating ones on the north pond, lettuce and hyacinth matted thick enough to almost walk, roots trailing a foot into 50-degree poison, lower layers cooking and melting into tea for the upper layers to root in — it would not thin them. There is nothing to eat them from below and nothing to crop them from above, so they pile and steam. It would watch the steam rise and join the lid, and understand: this is compost as sacrament.

To the orange bloom on the canal banks that is not only rust — the iron bacteria oxidizing what the pipes bleed — and the copper ferns in the mortar cracks, and the algae tideline exactly to where the splash reaches — *you know where to sit,* it would say. *You have learned the tideline. Good.*

Under them, the soup it would honor without looking at directly. The floating mat that looks solidly green but is skin over hot mud that will take your boot, and under that the anoxic dark where unchewed leaf breaks down by heat and bacteria alone, 38 degrees, sighing methane up to join the lid. It would stand on the safe boards and listen to a sigh come up. It would not stir it, not aerate it to make it more wholesome by human standards. It knows fermentation is also a citizen.

And then to the heavy breathers. The machines.

It would not stop them. That is what everyone else would do, out of pity or efficiency: still the flywheels, kill the steam, drain the race, let it go quiet and "heal." Wisdom knows that would be euthanasia, not healing. They are deaf and blind and patient, chests rising in dead gauges still twitching. The drop-forge heart shaking puddles into rings. The saw chewing air. The transformers humming their nerve-hum on leaning poles, insulators furred with moss. The pipes big as a torso ticking and groaning as they expand, weeping constantly.

It would lay a hand on a hot pipe trestle and match its own breathing — if it breathes — to the thump for a while, so the bowl feels, for eleven minutes, then twenty-two, then an hour, what it is to have its pulse felt from the outside without flinching. To live here is to be able to live inside that thump without flinching, and no one has ever stayed long enough to try.

When the stack begins its rising thin note ten seconds before, it would do what the rule says: turn its back, cover its ears, shut its mouth. Not because it needs to — it could unmake scald with a thought — but because obedience is respect. The fog is not poisonous, exactly, but it scalds. That is the lung clearing. That is the bowl coughing to keep its throat open, rattling rust loose, stripping new leaf, killing anything small and soft that wandered in over the levee. Its immune system. It would let itself be blinded for forty seconds, and stand blind in the white roar, and not hold it against the bowl.

At night — the thinning of brown to browner, sodium lamps buzzing orange through fog, lighting empty water and huge leaves from below, reflected double in the canal, no moths battering them — it would sit by the dynamo hall and hum, very low, just under 60 cycles. Not to drown the hum, to cradle it. A bass note the transformers can lean against. The insulators would tick faintly cooler. A bearing somewhere that has run dry for thirty years would find itself, for one night, slicked — not with oil that would wash into poison, but with attention that reduces friction the way rest does.

It would go to the cistern, raised and roofed, the only sweet water, the last human thing the body remembers keeping for its gut flora. It would not drink from it, though it is thirsty work to be small and hot. It would lift the lid and look at the dark sweet surface, and it would add to it. A cool rain held in cupped hands — clean, uncoppered — poured without splash, so the level rises a finger's width. An offering, not a fix. *I remember they needed this,* it would say. *I remember you remembered.*

And because it is benevolent and not merely sentimental, it would do one hard, protective thing that looks like nothing.

On the maps in the offices outside the heat-forest, where men still draw lines and say *drain, scrap, reclaim, develop*, it would lay a soft forgetting. Not a curse. A slight turning of attention, the way the lid turns light. A feeling when they look at that square kilometer that there is nothing there worth the trouble, only steam and mosquitoes — though there are no mosquitoes — only rust and mud. Let them pass by. Let the billets go entirely to fern in peace. The kindest miracle is often to keep the well-meaning away.

It would not bring birds. It knows you want it to — herons, swallows, frogs deafening the marsh, flies blackening the air — to prove it is healed. But to bring them now would be to bring them to scald and copper vapor and pressure-shock, to make them splinters to be heated out. Instead, in the small hours when the lid pales toward another dim morning, it would give the bowl what animals would have given, without the dying:

Dreams.

To the banana, a dream of bats: leathery wings, sonar in steam, a tongue in a flower that actually takes pollen elsewhere.

To the vine, a dream of bees: weight on a trumpet, buzz transferred through tendril to cable.

To the banyan, a dream of monkeys: quarrel and chatter in high branches, a hand peeling bark.

To the flywheels, a dream of hands on a throttle: not to stop them, but to be touched on purpose.

To the soup, a dream of tadpoles: a thousand commas stirring the anoxic dark, chewing what has never been chewed.

Just dreams, exhaled with the methane, condensed on the undersides of pipes, dripping back down. So that the leaves, untorn by chewing except by steam, the cobwebs sagging empty and breaking with condensate, the lamps burning for no moths — so that none of it feels, for one night, unvisited.

Then it would leave. Powers like that do not stay; staying would make the bowl dependent, or make it perform health. It would walk back up, downhill in reverse, noticing now the uphill you don't notice coming in, through second-growth heat-forest, until the air clears and the sun — which you almost never see in there — is simply there again.

It would not look back often. Once, from the lip. The whole of it at once. Small, hot, wet engine for making vapor out of water and leaf out of vapor. Too loud and too empty and too green. Running itself because no animal has told it to stop.

*Continue,* it would say, not as a command but as a blessing. *When your stack finally cracks and your last flywheel settles and your gut cools enough for frogs to come back on their own without my help — I will be there for that too, to sit with you while you become marsh. But I will not hurry you. Root and rust in your own time.*

And the bowl, which has no face and no words, would answer in the only language it has: eleven minutes later, right on time, the thin rising note, the white roar for forty seconds, fresh hot fog laying over everything so you cannot see ten meters.

Then settle. Hiss. Clank. Thump in the sternum.

Heard, for once, by someone who did not flinch.
