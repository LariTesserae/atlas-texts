It would come down on foot.

That is the first thing. A benevolent and wise power could arrive in any way — in light, in thunder, in a stopping of the hammers — but it would not. It knows the air here holds sound close to the ground. It knows eyes here are for ember. So it would darken itself to a density in the dark, and breathe white, and let its boots find laid stone, then plank, then stone again.

It would hunch.

Shoulders up, head down, to shed water. It could stay dry if it wanted. It would not want. It would let the cold beads form on the back of its hands. It would let its clothes grow heavy by the hour. It would learn quickly to walk with the hunch that is partly cold and partly ceiling and partly manners — *I will not take more room than the Works allows.*

It would not bring the sun.

That is wisdom. Sun would blind the large-pupiled, ember-good eyes. Sun would warm the stone that must stay cold to sweat. The whole patient *tok — tok — tok* of water into black water would stop. The cisterns would green and sicken and the blind white fish would die belly-up. The people, gone soft-pale and red at the joints, would burn and then freeze in their own sweat. Rescue, from above, would be another kind of drowning.

It would not bring a river.

A river would rot the raw poles with the bark still on, loosen the hide lashings, slump the daub before it ever cured, fill the stone-lined pits until the skins floated off and the soot got in. They have learned, over generations, to catch where water *wants* to go — sideways along a rope by capillary, beading under a plank — not where it should fall. A river does not want anything. It only takes.

It would not stop the hammering.

*You build, or you get wet, or you fall through* is not cruelty. It is physics. Stone settles. Lashings stretch in the damp. To still the hands would be to let a trough tip a finger's breadth and spill a night's harvest onto a sleeping family. The power would hear, under the *tak-tak-tak*, the rightness of the rhythm — the child asleep in the hammock under the walkway because the feet above her face are keeping time — and it would not break that to give them quiet. Quiet here would mean collapse.

So what would it do?

It would stand still long enough to be given a tool.

There are no watchers in the Works. To be present is to be part of the layering. A girl with lime under her nails, a one-eyed man with a copper hammer, would press a chisel or a rope-end or a jar into its hand without hostility and without asking. And the power — to whom galaxies are idle minutes that aren't idle — would take it. Gently and absolutely, like they do. It would add its warmth to the air so that later, on cold stone, it could condense and fall and be drunk.

It would work badly at first, by feel. Broad hands that could hold a valley learning again to be tender at the fingertips. Finding the rotted plank among sound ones by its give, by its colder sweat. Learning the hammer code: two quick taks for *again*, a scrape for *slack*. A click of tongue to mean *hold*. The short hoarse *hek — ho* when hauling that tears the throat. It would hoarsen with them.

And while it worked, it would do small, unshowy things that do not look like miracles because wisdom knows miracles break the shape that damp and cold and dark only allow.

Where an old woman sits too bent for ladders, scraping condensation from a hide awning with a shell into a jar, drop by drop, it would sit beside her and breathe, slow and warm, onto the cold hide — just enough that the beads gather faster where her shell can reach them. Not a flood. A fullness. Her jar would be full a little before dawn.

Where a boy re-lashes with hair-rope behind another boy replacing plank, pulling tight against the stretch the damp puts into everything, it would show them — without words, hand over hand in the dark — a knot it remembered from ships and tendons and vines, a knot that tightens when it swells instead of loosening. Their hands would learn it by touch in a minute. In a month it would be valley-wide, and no one would remember where it came from. That is how the Works learns: by watching hands.

Where the cistern-keeper, old, tends black water a foot or two deep and reads fullness by the ring of a single drop, it would kneel and look long with him, and its looking would steady the water. The wrigglers fewer. The fish — finger-long flickers put there on purpose to eat them — a little brighter, breeding a little cleaner. It would not make the water endless. It would make it less likely to sicken. Thirst organizes everything here; it would ease, not abolish, the organizer.

Where toddlers carry chips away in folded hands, where women nurse with one arm and scrape sweat with the other, where lips are cracked from breathing cold and fingertips white from hammer-shock, it would lend heat without drying. Lay a palm a moment on a red joint. Breathe onto cracked lips. Keep a sleeping child's sleep through a wrong echo — the hammer heard half a second before the arm falls — by keeping the rhythm right around her with its own small *tak*.

Where hunger is the background ache worked through — grain and dried meat brought down the trail on backs, fish when a cistern must be thinned, rat when a rat is stupid — it would leave food that belongs to damp and dark. Not wheat, which needs sun and soil there is none of. But a pale mushroom that eats rotted plank, the very plank the boys pull up, and fruits in cold, and a lichen the color of ash-moth wings that grows on weeping stone, faintly luminous, salty-sweet, marking leaks by growing thickest where water runs. So that finding a leak and finding supper become the same looking. The rats would find it first. That would be the sign it worked.

And high, out past the last lamp where more platforms are being laid into blackness, it would not light the whole basin. It would leave one small hole unroofed — no wider than a trough. A needle. At night, through cold smoke, one star. No warmth from it. No use for farming air. Only a reminder, for ember-eyes that squint at distance because there is no distance, that distance exists. That dark is not only low and complete. That there is an up the trail leads to, if anyone ever needs to remember why they hunch. The moths, torpid knots furred with damp high on poles, would lift their wings a fraction under it when the air is turning to give water. Living hygrometers. They would still be useful.

Then it would go up the trail before light — there is no light — would thin, leaving its tool in a hand that needs it next. Its shoulders would unhunch slowly, shedding water. The beads would form again on the backs of its hands for a little while, its body still giving water to the air.

You would not know it had been there. That is benevolence.

Only this: for a season the daub would slump a little less before it dried. The lashings would hold a little longer. The *plink* of a miss — water lost to stone — would come a little less often than the low *tok* of water into water that means a cistern is alive. A grandmother's jar would fill. A child's cough in the cold smoke would loosen. A new knot would spread hand to hand.

And the deepest adaptation, the one that is not in the body — that no one directs and yet everything repeats because the Works is teaching them to build itself through them — would go on. The power would not replace the teacher. It would only, for a little while, have been one of the warm, brief, steaming maintenance organs itself. A human condenser. A layer in the layering.

Built to build and to sweat and to be drunk.
