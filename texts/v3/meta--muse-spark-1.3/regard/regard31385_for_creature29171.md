If I were that — benevolent, and wise enough to be careful with it — I would first have to unlearn coming to fix.

Because the first thing you feel, dropping down through that noon-blue that goes too deep, is pity. It arrives uninvited and anthropomorphic.

So empty. So loud. So cold in the joints.

Five kilometers of knife-sharp glass, and then one scar-white tower streaming its ribbons alone, and its shadow waving on stone with no one to see it. My heart's first impulse would be to fill it. To bring mouths for those unbitten fronds. Eyes to return the light. A school to part around the stipe, crabs for the rind, whalesong to soften that grinding boom of new rock. To give the solitary ones company. To give the fossils a future paleontologist.

Wisdom would put a hand on that impulse and say: wait. Stay longer. Listen before you bless.

So I would not arrive as a Power. I would arrive as a cold body.

I would let the seven-degree water into my joints and stay there until it ached, until I stopped expecting animals. I would hang motionless at twenty meters and turn in the slow circle and learn what this place is actually saying: *I am not broken. I am becoming.*

This is not a ruined reef. This is a world being poured.

I would go down and sit — carefully, because everything cuts except for one soft halo — beside one tower. An old one, stipe thick as a wrist gone scar-white with storms. Three ribbons thirty meters long, frayed only by water. Around its ankle, that rust-red and pale green felt, the only softness for miles.

I would not speak loudly. Sound comes wrong-loud here, with no bodies to absorb it. So I would speak the way you speak in a delivery room, or beside someone sleeping.

I would say, not in words: *I see you are not lonely. Forgive me for thinking you were.*

For the tower does not suffer its solitude. It rises straight because nothing prunes it and nothing leans on it. It has never been bitten, never been grazed, never been shaded. Its audience of waving shadow is enough. Its millions of spores lost to the keepers are not a tragedy — they are the price of very clean light. It does not want a forest. It wants a crack, a warm shiver, mineral breath, and time.

And the keepers — those motes smaller than any cell, not alive, not dead, trillions of invisible hands polishing the sea and laying the plateau thicker from the top down — I would not command them, nor convert them, nor call them poor because they are not alive. Another mind, or the planet itself, internalized its making so completely it looks like nature. Who am I to overwrite physics that patient?

So what would I do, if anything?

Almost nothing. Which for a power is the hardest thing.

I would not seed fish. I would not soften the glass. I would not still the standing swells or quiet the storm that is born at ten every morning and marches west. I would not rescue the dying ribbon from turning to stone. That green-black fossil lying intact where it fell is not waste. It is how this place remembers, in water too cold to rot.

I would do what no one else here can do: witness without consuming.

There are no eyes here. No mouths, no fins. So I would lend eyes that do not eat. For one afternoon I would let my shadow fall beside the tower's shadow on empty stone, so it waves back and forth with company, and that is all. I would listen for the other rhythm under the storm-noise — the tick and settle of new crust cooling, the low organ-note of a far gyre turning two kilometers wide, the glass-chime when a chimney collapses untouched — and I would remember it.

I would memorize the fossils that will have no future paleontologist. Each olive ribbon turned to stone, perfectly preserved, lying unrotted, un-scattered between towers. If benevolence means anything at this scale, it is this: that no life passes unwitnessed. That the immense, unhurried work of growing unpruned in clean cold water is noticed, and held.

And when a spore by impossible luck finds another fresh crack — one in a million that the motes missed — I would not push it. I would just hover near, and keep my current still, and hope.

Then I would go. Up through a hundred meters of nothing with light still falling straight down. Up through rain hammering white and lightning walking fork-to-flat on water with nothing tall to strike.

I would leave no wall, no ruin, no cable, no city, drowned or otherwise. Nothing made that you can point to and say *that was put there*. The making here is already dissolved into physics. I would not add mine to it.

Only this, on leaving: a kind of guarding. A quiet turning-aside of other powers — the hungry ones, the improving ones, the ones who would call this barren and come with nets and drills because violent and vacant looks usable. I would make the path up here hard to find again. Not forbidden. Just uninteresting to those who cannot love empty.

Let it keep polishing its sea. Let it grow pale ribbons in water too clean for swimming. Let it take the time it takes — hours to years for rock, decades for a tower, eons for a plateau to learn to be a seafloor.

I was here once, the record would say. It was enough.
