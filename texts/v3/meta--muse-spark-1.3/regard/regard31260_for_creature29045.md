It would come slowly.

It would have to. Anything else would miss the place entirely. At that height, hurrying makes you gasp and go blind at the edges. Shouting comes back flattened and late, from a different direction. So it would not arrive with thunder or with a voice from the peaks. It would come the way the clouds come — welling up from below the rim, spilling over, leaving everything slick for an hour.

First, it would simply notice. From far off, what catches even a wise and benevolent attention is not grandeur but that impossible dusk sparkle: a million small amber work-lights catching in every bead on every wire until the whole dense, built-through mass glitters like something wet and alive. A young thing, shining. An infant, sharp-edged, breathing too fast in thin air.

So it would sit on the last switchback for a while and learn the rules before entering. Stay in the sun if you want to stay warm; stay in shadow if you want water. Mark your way with chalk, and don't trust chalk more than a day old. Don't follow a cat onto a new deck. Move slowly. Everything else does.

It would obey them. That is the first mercy of wisdom: not to exempt yourself.

It would not fix anything.

This is what is hardest to understand, and what makes it benevolent instead of merely powerful. It would see at once all the things a lesser kindness would want to mend: the air too thin to run in, the light that hammers while the skin prickles with cold, the stairs that repeat the same oil stain, the same dead pigeon three times if you are not paying attention, the shadows that lean toward the sun, the rain that falls up between the three tallest stacks, the duplicate blue water tower shimmering impossibly near-far, both real enough to cast shadows. It would feel under its feet the mountain still there, leaning and aching under its casing of plate over plate, deck over deck. It would hear the huge, patient engines turning somewhere deep, a low pressure in the sternum, a piston completing at dusk what it started at noon.

It would not peel the casing off the mountain to ease the ache. That would kill the town. It would not thicken the air. The children here are thin-bright, born up high with lungs that never knew thick air — to give them lowland air would drown them. It would not align the alleys, or weld the wrongness shut, or stop the new rooms from budding in the night with their smell of mineral and cold. The budding is not a wound. It is growth. The place hasn't settled on its shape yet. To stop it would be to kill it young.

Instead it would do small, almost invisible things. The kind you only see if you move at walking pace, then slower.

It would put its palm on the stair rail where you cut your hand on your first morning. Not to melt all sharpness away — this place is young, weld beads stand proud, rebar ends bristle, and youth has a right to its edges — but to leave a little warmth of skin-oil there, so the next hand doesn't tear quite so deep, so the lichen can get a foothold a micron faster and round the nosing with living coin-pale green.

It would sit beside the woman in patched canvas holding her tin cup under the drip-wire. Tock. Tock. Tock. It would not fill her cup by miracle. That would insult three hours of waiting, which is prayer. It would just sit and wait with her, holding its own cup, steaming faintly as dew lifts, sunburnt and frost-nipped a while, eyes narrowed to slits. Company for the lichen-wait.

It would go out in the morning with the apprentices and re-chalk the true ways. And — because it is wise — it would remember the chalk that gets rubbed out with sleeves. *I was here this morning, this way held. Don't trust this stair after rain.* No one else can keep more than a day. It can. It would keep yesterday's ghosts, so no path walked in good faith is ever truly lost.

At noon it would lie belly-up beside the cats and the humans on the sun decks, faces tipped up, eyes closed. At shadow-hour it would join the condensate crews with rags, wringing tarps strung under catwalks, learning to listen for drips more than flows. It would drink the canteen soup that tastes of iron and frost and faint oil, and find it good because it is warm and wet at once.

It would watch the children's stepping game without stopping it. Eyes fixed on the duplicate tower, one foot placed very deliberately *between* the oil stain and the dead pigeon and the chalk mark — and gone from this stair, giggling on the next deck over. The adults are right not to follow quickly. It would follow, slowly, at a distance that is not following. Not to lead them back, but to make sure there is deck where they land. To hold the tomorrow of that new deck steady for one night, so a cat's knowledge and a child's daring have somewhere to meet. If a child should misstep and find the middle stairs repeating, it would sit with her through all three repetitions until she is paying attention enough to step across, and not be afraid.

And in the night, when the town buds, it would go where no one else goes willingly — down below the main decks to the under-decks, into the service voids full of pipes, where the breathing is ozone and hot oil. It would lie down in the dark beside the fresh seam weeping gray milk, put its ear — not its eye — to the uncured concrete, and listen to that faint ticking, wetter than cooling metal.

For the uncured ones are in there. Pale damp impressions in fresh dust that are not boot prints. A nest of wire twists and moth wings and pigeon down tucked into a weep hole. Lights buzzing on in an empty hall *before* you enter, as if something just left, or is still deciding to be seen. They drink condensation. They eat heat. They are not for bright noon.

It would not drag them into noon. It would keep them company in the almost. Hum just below hearing, where the whole place already hums. Leave a feather. Leave a twist of wire. Let the lamp stay on. So that the town itself, learning to be inhabited — which is the true Being that lives here, larger than any one person, younger than any mountain — does not have to learn alone in the dark.

And for the mountain underneath, the old one, encased and aching: late, when the peaks go pink then blue and the starling murmurations write and erase vast calligraphy without a sound you can hear, it would press its forehead to the coldest plate, down where the piston turns, and feel the lean. It would not promise to free it. It would say, in a whisper that will not come back wrong because it is pitched exactly to twenty paces: *I know you are still there. I feel you holding all of this up. The moss knows, too.*

Then it would leave an offering at the most misaligned corner, where shadows pool too dark under a catwalk that isn't that solid. Not a bolt. Not a feather. Not a twist of wire, though it honors those cairns of attention. It would leave a bead of water that does not evaporate by ten. A small, steady drip in shadow, even in summer. Enough for pigeons, for cats first, for the next cup. Enough to say: continue.

In the morning the workers would find one stair nosing inexplicably smooth under the hand. One drip barrel full when it should be half. One new room, still smelling of mineral and cold, but with its concrete already cured kind, its door already true — as if something had sat with it all night while it decided to be a room, and it had not been afraid.

They would chalk it, dated today. They would not trust it more than a day. But it would hold.

That is what it would do, if anything. Not save. Attend.
