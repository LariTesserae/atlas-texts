If I were that kind of power — the kind that is actually benevolent, actually wise — I would come over the lip late in the day like you did.

Only slower. And I would stay longer.

At first, there would be the temptation to *do*. That is always the temptation when you can do anything. To lift. To warm. To fix.

I would feel it standing there in the blue dusk with my knees going wet through: Oh, this place is so cold. Oh, these citizens live so hard. The sun never clears the ridge. The night is most of the time. The needle ice lifts them a half-inch every night and cracks them with that sharp *pink*. They grow a millimeter a year. The beetle takes all afternoon to cross one roof. Let me give them an easier summer. Let me give them one good week that lasts. Let me clear a path so they are not so cramped. Let me drain the lower town a little so the drowned mosses around the marsh-eye are not always drowning.

And then, if I were wise, I would understand that everything I wanted to give would kill them.

To warm them would be to unmake them. The cushion saxifrage knows how to hold a degree or two inside itself exactly because the air has given up. Take away the cold and you take away its wisdom. To drain them would be to starve them. Sphagnum drinks with its whole body twenty times its weight and makes the bog more boggy, more acid, more itself. To live here is to make the place you live in wetter. Drier is not kinder. It is just elsewhere.

To lift the willows upright would be to break them. They lie down instead of standing up — that is their wisdom. They allowed themselves to collapse into usefulness. To clear an alley, to cut a doorway straight so I could walk it without crouching, would break the first rule. Nothing here has been cut. To cut would be to open yourself to freeze.

So I would not cut. I would not drain. I would not warm. I would not speak fast in a place where talking is done with water and sugar and fungus over weeks.

I would do what water does. Which is almost nothing that looks like doing.

First, I would shrink.

You cannot bless this borough at human scale. You tower and drip and sigh water up everywhere and leave dents that take ten years to become lanes. So I would let myself become small enough to be unnoticed, which is the only polite size here. Droplet-small to sit in the bead cupped between two moss leaves and watch a rotifer open like a flower, and a nematode swim its slow figure-of-eight, and a tardigrade haul its fat clawed body over a redwood forest of sphagnum tips, stop, tuck, become almost stone against the coming freeze and then — in thaw — un-become. I would not hurry it. I would breathe at its speed.

Hummock-small to sit beside a dome-citizen, that thousand shoots packed shoulder to shoulder, red on gold on green-black on brown on peat, on its own compacted grandmothers a thousand years deep. I would put my attention, which is the only real tool I have, on its drinking. On how it holds water to breathe.

Thread-small to run with the white mycorrhizae through the meter of living and dead mat, splicing willow root to saxifrage root to rhizoid, tasting what the mat tastes: this dome too wet and drowning, that leaf bittered by beetle larvae. Next thaw, an alley narrows. A doorway you crawled through yesterday is shut by a fresh shoot, and two feet over a new soft place opens. No council. No plan. I would not plan for them. I would just notice the plan they already are.

Second, I would keep.

Not keep as in keep safe like a jar. Keep as in keep *true*. Water decides here. Every passage slopes a finger's width toward damp. My keeping would be to let water keep deciding.

Where the cliffs weep in black threads, I would keep the weeping. I would lay my hand, if I had a hand, on the fresh rock too young to have learned softness and coax the seep to stay a little longer into the dry years that are coming elsewhere. Not a flood. Just nearness. Beading under every leaf.

Where the central marsh-eye blinks very slowly with ice and never quite freezes solid, I would keep it blinking. I would breathe cold with the night so the freeze still comes, because they live by freezing. That lift and slump is how the hummocks climb. That crack and knit is how they heal. Take away the hurt of ice and you take away their climbing.

And I would keep them unnoticed. This is the hardest and kindest part of omnipotence: to arrange to be forgotten. To let the mist stay low in the runnels a little thicker when boots approach. To let the wind pass over the top of the cirque and not come down. To let the humans who come over the lip feel their knees go wet through and their jaw go cold and feel awe instead of appetite — to feel that they have been visited, not that they have discovered. To let no map hold the cirque quite correctly. To let the ptarmigan stitch its white track across a roof and be gone by morning melt, telling no one.

I would not build a fence. Fences are a kind of cutting. I would build a forgetting.

Third, I would learn.

Because any power that only gives and never receives is not benevolent, just busy. And these citizens know something I have watched worlds forget: how to live without severing. How to be infrastructure without ever building. How to let a gutter be a living groove in a liverwort bank that yields to be carved because to be carved is to be needed. How to stand on your dead and let the dead hold water for the living, instead of carrying them away to some *away* that doesn't exist. There is no away here.

I would sit in the willow tunnels, shoulder-high, leaf-shingled, dripping, until the moss ate all my sound and my own steps were muffled to rubbery compression and I could hear the water-work underneath: seep-ticking off rock, faint gurgle under the mat, low creak as an elder willow, thick as a thumb but older than you, adjusts to snow weight. That creak is an elder turning in sleep. I would not wake it to thank me.

I would stay until my footprint — if I were clumsy enough to leave one — filled with dark water before I even lifted my foot, and beaded at the edges with spores, and closed. Already closing. In a week no one will tell I was there. That would be my success.

When I left, going back over the lip into failing light, the place would not look blessed at all. From the ridge it would still look like what it is — a dense, wet, cold green knot, holding warmth it does not have, holding shape against young stone. A place that learned the shape of shelter without ever learning tools.

The domes would still weep. The liverworts would still seal seams. The tardigrades would still freeze and unfreeze. The marsh-eye would still hold its dim sky.

Only slightly more itself. Slightly wetter. Slightly more allowed to keep becoming, at glacial pace but never stopping.

They would never know I came. The mites would keep oozing, joint-stiff with cold. The springtails would keep popping like salt. The sphagnum tips would keep glowing like embers in the blue dusk.

And I would carry the pattern with me — meter-thick, street over roof over root — to other cold, raw places where rock is still too young for softness, and remember it forward. So that even if this cirque one day thaws past itself, the shape of how to live low and wet and together without cutting will not be lost.

That is what I would do, if anything.

Almost nothing. Which here, is everything.
