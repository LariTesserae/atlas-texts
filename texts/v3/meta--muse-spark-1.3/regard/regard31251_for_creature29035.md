It noticed them because of the quiet.

From far off, the world is noise — barking, buzzing, engines, prayers shouted upward to be heard. This place made no noise. In the listening, there was a bowl the shape of a held breath, three miles across, warm as skin, where water sat level and did not argue. And in it, small wet-handed people moving slowly between green and stone, doing, fingertip by fingertip, the work that other places pay animals to do.

So it came. Not as powers are supposed to come.

No light. No voice from the rim. The light here is already plain and sufficient at noon, and a voice would die at twenty yards in the wet air. It came the way weather comes here: as mist condensing out of nothing, settling, dripping from a leaf.

If you had been on the causeway that morning you would not have seen it arrive. You would have seen, perhaps, a tall figure in reed-brown, barefoot, skin beaded, standing shin-deep beside the woman mending the sluice, watching how she lifts the plank an inch and watches and sets it down again. Learning not to splash.

For a wise and kind power has temptations, and has learned to name them before acting.

The first temptation is to bring gifts.

It sees their lean bodies, light-muscled, belly-flat even in old age, teeth worn down by grit in pounded tubers, and it thinks: *goats. chickens. a tilapia for the canals to give them protein.* It sees the old man going down the trellis at noon with a feather on a stick, touching each brief white gourd flower because there is no wind and no bee to do it, and it thinks: *bees. I could make a high, quiet bee that lives on mist.* It hears the complete quiet — no frog, no dragonfly, no bird circling the rim — and its heart aches in the human place and it thinks: *song. ducks. a dog for the children.*

It lifts each gift in its mind and sets it down again, and watches what would happen.

Goats would eat the thatch and foul the cistern. Chickens died here once for a reason; the water is strange to them and there is nowhere dry for their droppings to go but into the levels people drink from. Bees would buzz, and the buzz would carry further than any human sound here, and change what hearing means. A dog would bark, and someone would come running through marsh to a loud cry, and toddlers would unlearn which stones hold because someone would always come running. A fish would cloud the straight canals with its own hunger.

Nothing in the bowl is missing by accident. The colonists brought seeds on their backs and did not bring animals, and then the cliffs did the rest. The people took the animals' jobs into their own fingers. To unmake that bargain now, even kindly, would be to tell them they have been poor all this time, when they have not been poor. They have been level.

The second temptation is to fix time.

It sees lichen spreading a finger-width per decade, walls going up course by course and left half-finished, old people who do not know how old they are, and it thinks: *I could give them history. Iron that does not rust. A school. A lamp that does not need fat.* But the bowl holds its equilibrium because nothing hurries. A lamp that lets them see after dark would let them work after dark. Work after dark would muddy water that needs the night to settle. Iron would cut the new rock faster than the springs can weep to settle it. History with wars and famines to date by would require war and famine.

So it does not fix. It does not improve.

What does a benevolent and wise power do, then, when doing nothing large is the kindest large thing?

It does small things, at ankle height.

It takes off its greatness the way you take off shoes that will never dry here. It rolls its trousers. It learns.

For forty days — the length of the mist year they remember — it stays. It does not gather people in the plaza built for thousands. It goes to where one person is. It holds the bundle of reeds while a child steps stone to stone, at the exact pace the bundle allows. It steadies a basalt block while a man wets the joint, and with a fingertip imperceptible under the water, holds it true so rain will not need to settle it twice. It squats patiently by the taro with the small girls and learns to move pollen without crushing, badly at first, until an old man corrects its hand. It nods. It speaks low, a few words, a little hoarse from thin air. It breathes audibly, as everyone does here.

At dusk it sits on a narrow porch while a tiny fire of dry reed stems boils taro, smoke rising straight up a few feet and dissolving. It does not tell stories that would make their remembering thin by comparison. It listens to the register they live in: the healthy sluice hiss versus the choked one, the plink from banana leaf to standing water that means the cistern is truly full, the soft knock of adze on silvery wood a hundred yards off through reeds that means someone is repairing a trellis and does not need help.

And at night, when they sleep early on raised mats above the damp under thatch that ticks as it cools one degree, it does its invisible maintenance.

Not miracles. Maintenance, which is what this place asks for.

It keeps the far rim from losing another tooth onto the gardens. Not forever — rock is new and must fall — but that night, onto that sleeping house, no. It keeps the springs weeping at the same rate while the clouds below form and dissolve in the same place. It lays a hand, cool as seep water, on an infant feverish in a sling while its mother weeds, and the fever breaks into sweat that the damp takes without notice. It eases the old sorter's fingers in the plaza so the seed tubers can still be told by feel for one more season.

Above all, it keeps the veil.

Below the cliffs, the lowlands are blue haze with men in them who love straight basalt causeways and clear seep water for reasons that have nothing to do with level. They would climb with boots and maps and good intentions — to study, to save, to buy, to preach, to photograph the quiet. The power does not strike them. It is kind. It only makes the switchbacks a little more forgettable. A slip here, a mist there, a satellite image that shows only cloud at the wrong hour, a story in the low village that the trail goes nowhere and the air up there is bad. It lets the bowl be missed until, if ever, the world below learns to climb slowly and nod and not mind being wet.

On the last morning it goes to the cistern.

The cistern is the closest thing to a shrine: stone-lined, always full to the brim with clear seep water, covered with a lattice to keep leaves out, swept daily. No one drinks from it directly with dirty hands. You dip with a clean gourd, carefully, so as not to cloud it.

It sweeps the lattice. It lifts a fallen leaf out. It dips with a clean gourd and drinks, and the level, as always, remains the same morning and evening.

That is the only blessing it leaves that can be seen, and it cannot be seen: that the water stays clear, the levels agree, the thatch rots and is replaced at the same pace, the causeways stay straight whether two hundred houses are full or a hundred and eighty are.

It does not leave a feather that never wears, or a child who laughs loudly, or a bird to circle the rim. It considered a bird — one swift, once, so the children would look up and remember the sky is open — and then decided even memory should come on its own, when something wild finally finds its way up the cliffs by itself, in its own decade, as lichen does.

It leaves.

If you stand still off the causeway now, in the reeds, what you hear is water, only water: seep and trickle, the thin fall of the rim waterfalls, the plink of condensation into standing water, your own breathing a little louder than usual because the air is thin.

But if you have learned to listen narrowly, as they have, you might notice the hiss of one sluice is a fraction healthier than it should be, the stone underfoot a fraction steadier, the mist a fraction kinder at noon when the white flowers open.

Nothing changes here, or changes so slowly it might as well not. That is not neglect. That is what attention looks like when it is wise enough not to need to be thanked, and kind enough to stay shin-deep and do the work fingertip by fingertip, and then go, leaving the cistern full to the brim.
