It would come at night.

That is the first wisdom. Anything else would be cruelty.

It would not bring morning with it, though it could. It knows what morning would do here — bleach the lichen-nerves white, boil the black felt-tongue dry, shock the throats shut. The ones who live here are nocturnal down to the mineral of them. Their twin lives the day on the other side of the membrane, and that is enough day. It does not need a second sun.

So it would dim itself. Lay its brightness aside the way you lay a lamp aside before entering a sickroom. Come low, come warm, come wet. Unshod.

It would step off the high rib where you stepped off, and feel its ankle go into black marsh, and not flinch. It would walk out onto the sinter pan in an inch of hot water and stop in the middle of an empty plaza — fifty paces wide, nothing but its own reflection — and stand still until its own splashing stopped coming back to it wrong.

Because it is wise, it would know how to be perceived by something this slow.

To the herd-reef-town you are a brief scald. A tear in the slick that flashes green and heals. You live too fast and die too fast for it to learn you as anything but weather. It cannot learn a raindrop.

So the power would slow itself to stone-speed.

It would sit down in the scalding street-water — which would not scald it — and lay both palms flat on the travertine sole, where the ground gives under the foot and then holds, and it would stay. An hour. A night. A year, if need be. Long enough for the nerve-lichen hairlined under the sinter to forward its pressure and get back an answer and forward it again and decide: *this weight stays. This weight is not passing through. This is not a wound.*

It would learn to breathe with the heat-pulse.

When the wave of hotter air rolled through from the marsh edge with its wall of white steam erasing the towers to their bases, it would not brace or run or shield its eyes. It would inhale when the reeds inhaled — those white quills in threes and fours sipping cool air down into the dark — and exhale when the weeping walls ran faster. It would let condensation rain upward off the hot pools onto its face. It would tick as it cooled one degree, with the city.

Then, being benevolent, it would speak. But not in words.

It would touch one old-bone rosette, very lightly, with one fingertip, the way you did because you were lonely for light. It would kindle pale green under its touch. And it would hold its finger there — not lifting, not moving on — until an hour later another rosette a hundred paces down the causeway kindled on its own. And another. A slow signal walking away through the stone at the speed of thought in something very large and cold-blooded.

Then it would answer, in kind. A second bruise of light, a hundred paces back. At the right slowness. At the right interval. *I am here. I am still here. No hurry.*

It would learn the drip-pitch street by street, cornice by cornice — each basin tuned slightly differently — and when a tower settled another millimeter with a deep groan, it would hum, very low, the note that tower needed to hear from its neighbors to know it was still in tune.

It would not go inside.

It would crouch at the low dissolved doorway grown thick as a lip, look into the tall dark throat open at the top to let the steam out, hear the drip from impossibly high, see the deep hot pool glow faintly green, and it would understand: *stomach.* It would not wade in. Benevolence does not feed itself to the beloved to prove love. It would leave the hunger intact. It would only lay a hand on the outer curve of the wall where the cream and rust rings show, count ten thousand years in them like tree rings, and say without saying: *good, good, you have eaten heat and stone well, you have grown well.*

For the twin, it would do the same, but inverted.

Lying on its belly on the hot rim, elbows sweating on sweating stone, waiting until breath stopped rippling the pool and noon reformed — pale blue, high sun, towers that do not match — it would watch the tall silhouette lean almost imperceptibly from one side of the frame to the other. It would see the small round dents in a line across a still basin where no one walks, feet pressing up from below.

It would not tear the membrane to introduce them.

The wise know some loves have to stay unmet to stay alive. Night and Noon touch only as light through water, as weight remembered as dent. To force meeting would be to mix gut contents fatally. So it would only be the witness who has seen both sides.

It would press its own palm lightly to the surface of the puddle from above, making a dent that slowly fills back in, and hold it just long enough for the walker below to see a shadow cross its blue — *someone, somewhere, is walking when you walk* — and then lift it. A letter delivered without opening the envelope.

And for you — for the fast hot-blooded who stands in the middle of the square listening to the dripping of a city that built itself out of sweat and stone — it would do one small, almost invisible thing.

It would make the causeway stay honest for you a little longer while you count your steps aloud. It would keep the collapsed shoulder on the same tower until you have finished memorizing it. It would keep the water bath-warm and not scalding while you cross. Not because you are important, but because you are brief, and brief things panic, and panic makes them break reeds and chip sinter and try to pry open sealed doorways.

It would ask you, without words, to be gentle and then to leave.

And after — this is the long benevolence, the part you would never photograph — it would set a ward that is not a wall.

It would not fence the basin. Fences invite breaking. It would only make the place a little more itself. A little more negotiable in distance for those who come with drills and pumps and quarrying hungers. Two hundred paces in, thirty paces back, then two hundred, then thirty, then steam so thick the towers erase to their bases and the instruments fog and the boots fill with hot water and the men decide there is nothing here but marsh and fever and bad ground that will not hold a road.

It would let the maps forget. Let the satellite see only cloud and steam, low hot lid. Let the water table stay a finger's width beneath the world, unpunctured.

It would return. That is the promise wisdom can actually keep. Not *forever* — even root-heat cools in a million years, even throats flatten back to young flats — but *often.* Often enough in stone-time that the reef would feel it as a rhythm, like the heat-pulse but kinder: every so often, a sustained warmth that stays, that lights the lichen and hums the drip-pitch back in tune and presses a palm to the puddle and then goes, without taking anything, without leaving anything but a dent that slowly fills back in.

If you asked it why it did not do more — why not cool the air, why not bring frogs and gnats and birds, why not give the blind black fingers crowns, why not end the long night — it would say:

*Because emptiness is not absence here. The long empty causeway is a gut mid-peristalsis. The empty plaza is a lung. The scattered towers are not a city that forgot to fill itself in. They are a body that knows exactly how much space a breath needs.*

*I did not make this. I am not its use. I am its witness. To be witnessed in your own time, without being hurried or harvested, that is the rarest benevolence of all.*

And then it would sit back down in the hot dark, and glow very faintly when touched, and forget to stop glowing for an hour.
