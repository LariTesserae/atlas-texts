It would come by heat first, then by quiet — the same way you do.

It would not come from above, all at once. A benevolent and wise power knows what eighty meters of fresh-cut wall does to sound, to wind, to anything that tries to enter fast. It would leave its weather up on the pan — cracked clay, pale dust, white brutal light — and come to the rim on foot, if it has feet, and kneel.

For a long time it would do nothing. That is the first thing wisdom does.

It would look down at the sharp-cornered wound made yesterday, geologically speaking, the rebar and the guardrail ending in mid-air, the three stub chimneys and the derrick stopping ten meters short as if afraid to ask for more, and the dense knot at the bottom that fills wall-to-wall. Tin and pink concrete and mud-brick and black pipe. Cables hanging thick as vines. Amber lamps burning at noon in blue-brown shade. The metal too hot to hold. The air shimmering over the engine house even in shadow.

It would listen. Through boots, if it has boots. The low sixty-cycle hum in the molars. The sigh and white vent for thirty seconds and then reseat. Drip, tick, creak. Plink of water into metal. A dropped wrench ringing once and stopping. It would feel the late-afternoon sigh when the desert above cools a degree and the pit exhales dust and steam in a column that almost reaches the rim and collapses. The cables swaying. The tin ticking in chorus. The beads trembling and falling together. A small rain inside the earth.

And it would learn the three rules fast, because they are good rules:

Stay low.
Don't touch bare metal after ten.
Harvest, don't take.

So it would stay low. It would not lift the town out. It would not flood the pit or roof it or cool it by decree. Lifting would kill what has learned to live low. Flooding would poison the moss that knows to the centimeter where water lives.

It would come down the switchbacks slow, the way heat-economy demands. It would crouch. It would sit on the kitchen chair with wood legs dragged to the exact crack where the machines push a slow breath along the sump floor. It would put a rag in its back pocket. It would wait for its shirt to stick and stay stuck.

This is what benevolence does when it is wise: it obeys.

Then — only then — it would do small, almost invisible things. Not miracles. Tendings. The same work the keepers do.

At noon, when the single shaft stands straight down for twenty minutes, it would be a shade. Not a roof. Just the width of a hand. A high thin cloud held a minute longer than wind would allow, so the handrails brand one degree less, so the pipe-ferns under the cisterns don't hang quite so limp. It would not take the sun away; the lichen likes hot iron, the saltworts have learned the amber lamps. It would just blink.

At night, when the desert cools, it would cool the cold-water pipes one fraction more. No one would measure it. But in the morning the beads would be fatter. The gutters would tick a little faster. The communal buckets under the elbows would fill by two finger-widths instead of one. It would not make a river or a well — a river would hiss to steam halfway down, a well would be tapped and fought over. It would make more sweat. Sweat can be shared if you put your cup under and don't tap the line.

For the ones that live without appetite, it would bring appetite, very gently.

Because it has noticed what is not there. No pigeons, no swallows, no rats, no lizards, no flies, no roaches, not even ants. Producers with no consumers, growth with no rot. Dead leaves blown over the sharp rim lying paper-thin in corners, uneaten, dust settling undisturbed. Spores falling straight down because there is no wind and no wing to carry them.

A wise power does not dump a forest into a wound. It carries in dust on air, the way life first arrived here.

A few spores of a black fungus that can eat metal-sour condensate and live in burlap. A dusting of springtails smaller than a pinhead that can live in the green-black moss line and nibble the dead leaves and be eaten by nothing yet and that is fine. A night moth, pale as the pipe-fern fronds, that can find the saltworts by smell in the dark and move pollen an inch down the alley so they can flower, once, without needing to be seen. Tardigrades in the drip. A lichen-eater that is only a bacterium for now. It would seed the wet seam and stop. It would let the front line of green knuckles nose forward one more centimeter a season, and no more.

For the keepers — three, maybe four, maybe one left on rotation, laundry stiff under the catwalk, kettle humming on waste-heat — it would do even less, and it would matter more.

It would not cure the heat. It would sit with them at late afternoon when they stop to watch the column rise, gloved hand on hot rail through a rag, face wet, waiting for the fall. It would be the extra witness so there is another living thing to witness it besides the ferns. It would let them feel, through the boots, that the flywheels turning slow and steady with the doors always open are heard elsewhere too.

In sleep, on pallets on the concrete floor where the floor-current runs coolest, it would give them cooler lungs. It would ease the pruned pale hands. It would give them a dream not of rescue but of continuation: the drip still running, the shade-cloth still tensioned, the moss still marking where water is drinkable. It would let them know, without words because sound dies here, that tending is not forgotten. That keeping the shade and keeping the drip and keeping the air moving along the floor is a whole ethic, and it has been seen.

And it would keep the rim.

Outside, on the pan, there are maps and men who like square cuts in the earth. A wise power knows how young this place is — rockfall blocks still knife-edged, rust still first-generation orange, paint blistered but not yet peeled in long histories. Young things get filled in, or mined deeper, or capped. So it would turn attention aside. Let dust drift over the drill stars just enough that from a plane it looks like nothing. Let the road that was sheared in half stay sheared. Let the quiet stay quiet. Walls that swallow sound are a protection. It would protect the protection.

If you came back in a year, you would find almost nothing changed. That would be the proof.

The handrails still too hot to hold bare after ten. The flywheels still turning. The buckets still filling by the finger-width. The moss still in its exact black line. The lichen still freckled along the weld seam. The ferns still pale and translucent. The saltworts still gray-green and stunted, but — if you crouched — you might see a tiny flower you had not seen before, white as condensate crust, open to the amber lamp and the moth.

The chair still in the crack. The kettle still humming. Laundry still stiffening in the floor-current. And under a loose plate where before there was only dust lying exactly as it fell, a thread of green-black beginning to rot a leaf from underneath, undisturbed, unhurried.

The power would still be there, low, gloved, listening through its teeth to the breathing in the sickroom, wiping a bead before it gets too fat to hold and moving a bucket a finger-width to the left.

It would not have saved them. It would have joined them.
