It would come at night, because that is when the breath shows.

Not from above, not with fanfare. It would be walking the dry world — which is cool and starved and noisy with animals — and feel a warm spot in the dark. A mouth of iron and wet green breathing up out of the ground where no mouth should be. It would stop. It would listen. That is the first thing a wise power does: it stops.

It would find the rim by the heat before it saw anything, same as you. It would not bring light with it. It knows better than to bring the sun down a ladder.

It would climb down hand-over-hand where the stair shears off. Heavy, careful. It would make animal sounds on the cinder, too loud, too alive, and it would wince at that, and then sit still until the place forgave the noise.

For a long time it would just sit on the cold side of the plinth, with its back to the concrete that weeps, and learn the rules by obeying them.

Almost no light — so it would not shine. It would let its eyes change. Ten minutes to see shapes. Twenty to see color. It would wait the full twenty. Benevolence is not impatient.

Water does not flow, it hangs — so it would not pour. It would breathe, and let its breath join the sweat on the pipes. *Plink.* Long silences. *Plink.*

Processes are slow — so it would slow itself. Slow its heart, slow its thought, until a centimeter a week stopped seeming slow. Until the tick of the cooling boiler stopped seeming sad and started seeming like a metronome.

Only then would it look, really look, at who lives here.

Not one fern. The arrangement. The islands. The unhurried, mouthless green laid in rings around a tired heat, drinking its own breath, perfect because nothing has ever taken a bite.

A lesser power — a merely kind one — would want to fix it. To tear the slot wider and let the sun in. To bring bees for the pitchers so they could set seed again. To bring slugs and beetles and birds to make it "complete." To repair the living boiler to eternal youth, or replace it with a bright new heart that would never tremble near zero.

This one will not.

It understands what perfection this is. Not the perfection of a plan completed, but the perfection of never having been wounded. Each leaflet unbitten from crozier to brown tip. Each trumpet unchipped, holding clear water instead of meat. Each shelf fungus entire as cut paper. To bring mouths here, even loving mouths, would be to end it. The ferns never made sunscreen, never made poison, never made thorn, because they never needed to. To teach them to need to would not be a gift.

So it does not bring animals. It comes as the one animal that has learned how not to eat.

It sits among the ferns without taking a frond. It could — it has a mouth, it has hands — and it chooses, for the whole night, not to. Its sweat beads on its elbows and falls to the moss and is taken without thanks, and it is glad to be taken from in that small, unbitten way. To be rain that is not rain for a little while. It lifts a frond gently where it has combed itself over the steam line — it comes away with that soft sucking sound — and looks at the white rootlets worried into the lagging, drinking sweat straight off the steel, and lays it back down as carefully as hair.

That is the most it touches the green: to witness, and to not wound.

Its real tending is for the other resident. The one that is not green.

It goes to the living boiler and lays its palm on the jacket. Not hot, exactly. Unwilling to go cold. It feels the low, patient churn inside, the pump lifting something hot from deeper still. It hears the sigh every few minutes. *Hssss.* A white feather gone before it reaches the rim.

It knows this keeper does not know it is a keeper. It just hasn't yet given up.

The power will not make it immortal. That would not be wise, and not kind either — to condemn a tired engine to run forever so the garden never has to learn to cool. Instead it does small, unmiraculous things, the kind the people who left in a hurry would have done if they had stayed one more evening.

It clears a rootlet from the pressure valve where it was beginning to bind, so the sigh comes easier. It wipes the condensation from the face of the gauge with its sleeve so the needle trembling just above zero can at least be seen. It steadies the belt where it has begun to walk toward the edge of the wheel, nudges it back a millimeter with a thumb. It cups its hands around the pilot flame behind the cracked grate when the fan stirs and makes it gutter, until it burns blue again.

It does not restart the cold boiler. It leaves the croziers growing out of its open firebox door. It does not silence the ticking one. It lets it tick. Some things are allowed to be finishing.

Then it goes back and sits in the dark corner past the ferns, where the wall is merely warm and the red bulb doesn't reach, with the listeners. The pale shelf fungi stacked ear over ear. It sits at their level. It listens with them. When the valve sighs, the droplets tremble on their rims and hold, and the power holds its breath with them, so as not to shake them loose.

All night like that. A company, not a rescue.

Toward morning — though there is no morning down here, only a slight cooling of the slot above — it does one last benevolent thing, which is also the wisest. It memorizes them.

Not as specimens. As they are. The mustard-orange scald quivering with bubbles too small to hear. The white-green moss that throws back a little more light than it receives. The dozen trumpets full to the brim with oil-smelling water, one old mosquito husk from Before still floating. The way every clump leans and you can read the pipes by the lean.

It takes that memory up with it, to keep elsewhere. So that when — in a year, in fifty years, when the lagging finally rots through or the pump finally seizes or the seep from below finds a new crack and leaves — when the walls cool and the sweat stops and the rings dry white and stay white and the green islands shrink to brown felt that melts and does not return — it will not have been unwitnessed. It will not have been a room-sized greenhouse with no sun and no keepers that lived and died with no one ever choosing not to bite it.

It climbs the sweating rungs, hand over hand. At the rim it pauses. It does not fill the hole. It does not mark it. It lets the warm breath rise past its ankles into the cool, starved night.

And it leaves the red bulb burning.
