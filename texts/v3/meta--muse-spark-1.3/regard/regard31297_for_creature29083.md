It would come down.

Not all at once. A benevolent and wise power would know better than to arrive like weather. It would put its feet on the rim first and look, if the mist thinned, at the whole oval at once — the raw black break, the grey floor two hundred feet below, the sideways light pooling and not shining — and it would feel, like anyone, the first urge to fix.

So cold. So dim. So loud for so little green.

The pity would come first, as it always does. *Lighten it. Warm it. Give them soil for the cliffs. Give them streams between the pools so no crab is trapped. Give the ledges softness so no egg balances on stone. Quiet the roar a little so a single call can be heard.*

And because it is wise, it would climb down anyway and sit in the cold until the pity cooled.

Mid-morning down here looks like evening elsewhere. Breath shows. Sweat never dries. The power would breathe too, and let its breath show, and let its coat get damp from everything touched and not dry. It would walk a mile of shingle hearing only its own steps swallowed, and then look up as the shadow crossed — then another — then the air tore.

Saturation all at once.

Tens of thousands pouring off the cliff in ribbons, circling the basin in a slow screaming gyre. The white streaks. The shoulder-to-shoulder jostling. The fox gone pale and almost silent working the damp line. The mice nibbling the sedge to exactly itself. The midges lifting off black mud. The crabs clicking in iron-grey saucers only yards across.

And the hand-work.

It would stay still for an hour, until the roar became pressure, and watch. One gull laboring up thirty feet with a crab to drop it precisely on the one flat anvil stone in dry gravel. Crack. Miss. Up again. A raven biting a stick twice to length, fishing a grub from basalt, leaving the tool. An otter rolling with the same rounded pebble on its chest for days, then losing it and picking any other. No workshop. No store. Picked up, used, dropped.

A stone picked up, used, dropped — and the Hollow remaining, in any lasting sense, unmade.

Here is what wisdom would understand, cold-seeping into its jacket: this is not a place that failed to become something else. It is not a beginning. It is a completion.

The wind brings just enough from the coast. The birds manure the moss. The moss holds the damp. The damp keeps the cold, the cold keeps the mist, the mist keeps the light weak, frost shatters as much as rain smooths so sharp stays sharp. A crab eaten, another hatches. A ledge vacated, another bird lands. The white streaks stay in the same places year after year. The tarns neither grow nor shrink.

Nothing here accumulates because nothing here needs to. They have solved what builders never solve: how to use the world without keeping it. Their culture is not in things but in the act, repeated perfectly. A different gull, a different crab, the same stone. A crowd that replaces itself perfectly and never gets louder or quieter.

To warm it would be to unmake it. To drain the scattered pockets into one good generous stream would be to kill the crabs who live only because the pools are unconnected. To plant trees, to soften the cliff into hill, to teach them nests of twigs and cairns that last — would be to break the circle that feeds them. Even kindness, applied like sunlight, would be a kind of cliff-collapse.

So the power would do almost nothing.

Almost.

It would not lift them out. It would not clear the sky. It would sit on the scree where the edges are still keen enough to cut a boot and let the cold come through its soles. It would learn the volume — not to silence it but to hear inside it. It would watch one particular gull until she cracked her crab, and hold that small triumph entire, so that even if no stone records it, it was witnessed. It would steady, once, with a fingertip, an egg balanced against the wind — not to save all eggs, which must sometimes roll and feed the fox, but because that one, then, could be steadied.

It would leave every tool where it lay. It would drink no tarn dry trying to quench a thirst that isn't theirs.

And before it climbed back up — for it would climb, it would not stay and become another weather they must adapt to — it would do the one lasting thing benevolence can do for a perfect equilibrium: guard the rim.

Not with thunder. With uninterest. A little extra mist on the maps. A little extra steepness in the story travelers tell. A forgetfulness laid gently over the plain so that those who would come to improve, to farm the guano, to quarry the clean black basalt, to rescue the pale foxes and study the tool-birds in cages — would turn aside and go elsewhere to be useful.

Let the Hollow be useless and therefore safe.

On the way up it would pause where condensation runs all night off the cold cliffs, and touch the damp apron that never quite dries, and be grateful. For they who built nothing have something to teach a power that can build anything: how to pick up the world lightly, use it precisely, and put it back down.

Come back in ten years, in a hundred, and the power would still find it. The same dropped pebble on the same anvil stone, or another just like it. A different gull dropping a different crab under the same grey that never clears. The roar filling the same bowl of young stone.

And that would be enough. That would be the blessing: *continue.*
