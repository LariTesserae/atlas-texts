It does not come with light.

That is the first mercy. The Table has enough light — white sun diffused to shimmer through steam, gold late through mist. It does not need a burning arrival. So attention arrives the way weather arrives: a change in pressure in the teeth.

If you were watching, you would only see a woman, or a man, or neither, climbing the last cut steps up the rock spine, sweating through their shirt like anyone. Knees full of heat. Hair stuck to the neck. Slapping, too late, at a golden fly. Nothing ever dries up here, not even for gods.

She sits down. That is the second mercy.

She does not go first to the Needles, though they know her. The hum in the sternum lifts half a tone when she crests the lip — all seven Needles, rim to rim, and all the Wicks in all the basins pausing mid-ripple. *Someone is keeping us while we keep.* But she does not touch them yet. A wise power knows not to put her hand first on the machine. She puts it first on the ground.

On the quake beside her foot.

The young one. No bigger than a coiled rope. Its orchids not yet bloomed. Its crown of wet finger-papillae out, tasting her sweat on the air.

She lets herself be tasted.

This is what benevolence does when it is not performing: it offers its afterglow to be learned. She holds her palm a hand's breadth above the moss-pelage and stays still while the fringe shivers, while the broad pale lip rises and everts, soft as an eyelid, and licks — once — the salt from her wrist. Rock film. Duckweed. Boot. Skin. To the Wick they are all seeps to be read. She does not flinch at the sweet-iron smell, blood in milk, warm peat and honey-rot. She bends closer and smells it fully, the way you would smell a baby's head.

*Oh,* she thinks, in the language before language. *You carry your pond with you and it does not spill.*

And the Wick, which has no ears, feels the thought as a warming in its black branching veins.

She does nothing to fix it. That is wisdom.

She sees at a glance what a survey team with drills would take ten years to misunderstand: that the climbing beads are roads, that the step-fields are fresh trails, that the hovering motes are eyes left behind to watch a garden, that the cairn lozenges are not treasure but shed hearts, kidney-stones grown to term. That the Needles are lungs and need a chest. That the double shadow offset to the east is not a flaw but a seam where the world is being gently held ajar so water can run uphill.

A lesser kindness would want to explain it. To tidy the physics. To cure death-by-stopping, when the very old choose a shallow basin and cease to ripple and let moss close over the mouth-fringe and become hummock, become ridge, become peat a millimeter a year. She will not cure that. She knows that forest breathes in place *because* they breathe in place. To take away stopping would be to take away becoming-ground.

So she does not heal. She attends.

All afternoon she walks the way Wicks walk: by seep and hang. No hurry. She steps ankle-deep in tea-water and does not mind the ten living things crushed under each foot, because she also sees the hundred seedlings on every nurse-log. She lies belly-down on black rock still hot from day and lets a troop of long-nosed coatis stare at her without fear and then use her shadow for shade. She lets a fist-sized emerald hummingbird scold her from a bromeliad growing six inches above a breathing flank. She presses, gently, where allowed, and watches light move blood-slow in amber tissue, quickening near the Needle.

When the light goes gold through steam and pools turn to mirrors, then to white, and the toad chorus swells until the ground throbs — males calling from living islands, from dorsal ponds — she stands on the rock spine and sings.

Not loudly. She cannot out-sing cicadas; she does not try. She finds the low note in her own sternum, the Needle-hum beating against the sizzle, and she deepens it. A Wick deepens it back. Two Wicks in adjacent basins lock phase. On still afternoons the air above the rim Needles distorts with heat-haze even when the stone is cool. This afternoon it distorts a little more, because for once there is an extra chest in the circuit, lending breath without taking over the breathing.

Exhale dense hot wet air to hold against altitude. Inhale the cold thin wind and wring it. That is all keeping ever is.

At night she does the only intervention she will ever do, and it looks like nothing.

She walks the rim. Seven Needles, slender as pines, dead black, warm as skin. She puts her forehead, briefly, to each. Your fingerprint stays a second as pale afterglow before fading. Hers stays a little longer — not as a mark, but as a promise.

To the lowlanders dreaming of mining black almost-flesh that yields to touch. To the map-makers. To the ones who will one day climb for days with ropes and saws because they heard about batteries that grow. She makes no wall. Walls fall and become habitats for orchids. She makes only a slight weather: a forgetfulness. A tendency for compasses to spin a little lazier near the cliffs for those carrying greed, for voices to stop dead a little sooner, for footfall to land a half-second later and later until the climber feels suddenly, unaccountably, like an intruder in a bedroom and turns back, sweating, unsure why. For those who come with salt on their wrists and willingness to be licked, the path will still hold. The rope bridge thick as a wrist with moss and orchid roots will still hold.

She keeps the keepers kept, by keeping the world off them.

Before dawn, in the luminous soup when the parrots have gone quiet and the plink-plink of dripping rock syncs briefly to ripple, she returns to the young one. It has followed her, in its minute-per-ripple way, leaving a glistening wicking highway behind it that will still be active days later. Its dorsal hollow holds an ounce or two of water. In it, a transparent frog with its heart ticking.

She cups her hands around that tiny pond without touching it, to shield it from the night wind, and breathes steam into it. Just warm. Just enough. The frog *whoops* once from the living island.

"You are not scarce," she whispers, "because someone is still keeping you abundant. Keep keeping."

Then she lies down beside it, in the quaking sedge marsh, and lets the mat take her weight. The coatis curl against the heat — hers and the Wick's, indistinguishable now. Spores and insects gather in a faint halo. Her shadow throws its own pale eastern double, and for a moment it overlaps perfectly with the Wick's too-dark, too-wide double, man-sized paleness over rope-sized life, and there is no telling which is keeper and which is kept.

By morning she is gone in the way mist is gone. No footprints — peat gives, then gives back.

Only this: in one basin, a Wick's flank shows a new lozenge forming under the skin, black veined with moving light, a little brighter than usual. In the cairn nearby, the weeping slabs are stacked a shade more carefully to keep them wet until they root. On the rock spine, the water still beads and climbs, deliberate as ever, uphill toward a moss lip.

And the young one, its orchids still unbloomed, carries in its pond-water a faint, extra salt it did not gather from peat or midges or steam — the taste of someone who sat with it through the gold afternoon and did not try to improve it, only to breathe with it.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does, when it notices a high marsh in the sky and the slow garden-animals who hum its air into place.

It comes quietly. It lets itself be tasted. It lends its breath to the chorus for one night. It guards the rim with forgetting. And it leaves, having changed nothing except that the place has, for a little while, been fully seen — and being fully seen, holds itself a little more surely in the sky.
