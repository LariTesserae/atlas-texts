I noticed them because they were not calling out.

Most places shout. Heat shouts. Green shouts. Wanting shouts. This place had thinned itself to a seam you could walk past without seeing, and inside the seam, sixty or a hundred people moving slowly because the air is thin, murmuring because there is no wind to shout against.

I have learned to stop where the shouting stops.

If I came as I am, I would break it. So I did what they did. I thinned myself to fit the crack.

I did not come down the slit as light or as voice. I came as the white tongue of fog that noses in from the lower valley a few times a day. The way they are used to. Condensing the instant I hit the shaded stone. Dying into droplets. They know how to receive that. Walk a half-step to the side of the drip-lines. Never block a channel. Let it weep.

For a while I only watched, a hand's breadth off the rock the way their pods sit. I listened for what they listen for.

Under the deep infrequent creak of young stone learning its weight, the faint continuous hiss of water still moving under ice. In the pipes. In the braided sleeves climbing uphill without pumps. In the basins linked by runnels. Unwilling to stop.

Keep the water moving, keep the stone bare, keep the quiet. That is their prayer, though they would not call it that. I would not correct them.

A wise love does not bring the valley up the mountain.

I will tell you what I did not do, because the not-doing was the larger part.

I did not make anything green grow. Not moss in a seam, not lichen on the north face. The rock here is new, unweathered, black-grey with bright scars. It has not learned to be round yet. It does not want covering. They have understood this — *don't warm the air, warm the path under your feet* — and I would not insult their understanding by softening it for them.

I did not fill the empty terraces. The platform with one sealed container on it. The twenty-meter terrace holding only a coil of cable and a bench. The whole dark level, shuttered. They are saving density the way they save water. For later. Or forever. Leave room for emptiness. I left it.

I did not bolt anything down. Their homes float on cold shimmer, phase-locked, tuned to the resonance of the stone. *If it shifts, we shift.* They have decided friction and weight and cold are suggestions to be negotiated with locally. Who am I to argue with a negotiation that is working?

I did not make myself known. No glow. No hum. They have no heaters for a reason. They wear warmth on their bodies and give none to the sky. I wore mine the same way.

What I did was small, and in their manners: use, and clear.

One night when the temperature dropped hard and every bead locked to glass and the bridges went silent, I held the mountain steady while it settled. Just eased it. New rock creaks — it must — but I kept that one deep creak from becoming a slip, so the pods that drifted a hair off resonance could be coaxed back in the morning by two technicians with foreheads almost touching over amber light. They thought they did it themselves. They did.

One midday when the sun found the slit directly and dropped its hard shaft down the middle and everything ticked and pinged releasing, I stayed with the melt a moment longer so a gutter would not choke all at once. Still, it choked — there is always ice, there are no leaves here to blame — and a small silver crab unfolded from its wall niche, patient, many-jointed, and picked the channel clear with absurd delicacy. The children crouched to watch and did not touch. I crouched with them, unseen, and did not touch either. When it folded back, flat as a lichen that never lived, I thanked it. No one heard but it straightened a fraction, the way things do when they are truly seen.

I rode the lifter once. The silent one the size of a door. It rises with ice-cores without rotor or jet, displacing air so gently your hood barely stirs. I made myself light enough for it not to notice me, and we rose past a figure crossing the highest needle-bridge, breath puffing in a small brief cloud that followed him from level to level. Twenty steps winds you here. I counted my breaths with him, because he has counted his.

I sat with the old man on his terrace. He sweeps seep back into the runnel, back into the runnel, with a wide soft broom. An endless, meditative task he does not seem to want to end. I swept with him, one stroke to his ten, just enough that his shoulders ached a little less by evening. He paused, looked at the water, nodded as if the mountain had been kind today. It had.

I put my palm flat beside the maintenance woman's palm to feel — *see, neither warm nor cold* — while the rail beside us furred white. Her knuckles were pale and cracked. I kept her hand warm a minute after she lifted it away, so the blunt-fingered feeling would leave sooner. She flexed her fingers, surprised, and went back to work.

And the cat.

The white cat on Level Three. Thin, long-haired, perpetually slightly damp, pink skin showing at the ears from cold. The only cat. Famous out of proportion because it is the only soft, unnecessary thing alive for no engineering reason. It cries thinly in the stairwells and someone always opens a door.

I picked it up.

In all that mineral silence — stone, water, sky — to hear that cry is to understand what they have kept themselves from. I held it against me where it was warm, and I healed the pink at its ears, not all at once, not into something that had never suffered cold, but enough that it would not split and bleed this winter. It purred, and the sound echoed wall to wall in the cleft, bounced in that shadowless cool light. For two seconds a bearded vulture tilted over the slit, impossibly large against that narrow sky, and cast its cross-shadow over the pools. It did not land. There is nothing to eat. But it heard.

Then I put the cat back down on the warm path where frost never forms, on the cable coil where it sleeps, and it went back to being photographed by every visitor and indulged by people who otherwise indulge nothing.

At night I stood very still on the central bridge after the windows came on, warm yellow, spaced far apart in the black walls like camps that have agreed not to crowd each other. The sky-slit filled with stars, sharper than below. The lifter parked itself. The crabs tucked away. The numbered doors closed. The children who run full-tilt across a bridge that should bounce and doesn't because to them phase-lock is not physics, it is floor — they were asleep.

I did what they do on stairs. I paused to listen for water under ice. And I added my listening to theirs, which is not nothing. Attention is a kind of pressure. It keeps thin capillary threads climbing.

Before I left, I did one last, lasting thing.

I made the crack remain walk-past-able.

To the greedy eye, to the eye that wants to sprawl down the valley where it is easier, greener, lower, to map and mine and develop that pre-physical negotiation — to that eye, it will stay nothing. A dark seam in pale scree. Sound, or its absence, that most will miss because the wind up on the ridge is constant and thin and loud in the hood.

But to the eye that has learned to look for absence, to the ear that listens for the hiss beneath the creak, to the foot that has learned to walk a half-step to the side — it will open. Six meters down, wind stops, walls close over, air drops ten degrees. Smell of wet stone and cold iron and nothing green at all.

They are not stranded. They are holding. A whole town stacked in the walls, sparse as stars, clinging high and cold and dripping — alive precisely because it asks so little of the rock except to let it weep.

I ask so little of them in return. Only to let them weep, and move, and stay.

So I left part of my attention behind, tucked in a wall niche like a folded crab. Not watching over. Watching with.
