They would not come at first.

A benevolent and wise power learns early to be slow with new things. And this is so new. The edges still knife-keen, the thorns still hardening black by afternoon, the bark still peeling in curls that haven't learned to rot. A child, really. A child the size of a valley, perched impossibly high where the air should be thin and cool and instead is bath-hot, sweating sunlight.

It would watch from below the clouds for a long while. Through many hour-long rain cycles. Learning the count that never stays: nine, eleven towers. Learning the sounds: leaf-drums, cistern-membranes booming, tension-fibers snapping like gunshots, the hum in the aerial aqueducts. Learning what it is: not a ruined city, not an empty city waiting for citizens. A single sparse body that is its own builder and building and eater and child.

It would understand, before it ever set foot on the root-felt, what most visitors would get wrong.

That the emptiness is not broken.

Each tower holding its huge deliberate volume of nothing — cool ribbed halls, moss floors swept-even, sphincter doors dilating for humidity, windows thinned to green-gold — is not lonely. It is balanced-with-emptiness, sparse on purpose. It does not need birds to stitch its noise, or insects, or frogs in the cisterns, or tenants for its broad rubbery stairs. Fertilization by drowning is enough. Pollination by storm at noon is enough. To lift water and shed water and digest yourself and make yourself again at bazaar speed, at anthill speed, is enough.

So the power would not bring gifts of animals. Would not cool the heat. Would not drain the wet. Would not teach it grief for the bridges it throws fifty yards through sideways rain and holds for two days and then lets rot through to spin down into the cloud sea. *Grow fast, grow sharp, abandon without grief* — that is its morality, and it is young, and it is working.

What would a wise kindness do with a young god that eats everything it touches?

It would come barefoot.

No tools. No hands that build. It would step up onto the living mat where the weave thins at the rim, where water falls off the world in long living gutters, and it would let the mat well up tannin-brown around its feet. It would walk to the woven plaza at the center and stand in the hot drifting rain and let water condense on its arms, on its eyelashes, like everyone else there.

It would enter a tower when the oval door dilates. It would climb the sweating stairs. It would sit in a ribbed chamber and shout once — not to claim it, just to give it something to return, leaf-filtered, three seconds later. It would listen to how carefully the place holds echo. How well it holds air and steam.

And then, because this is what it's for: it would stand still long enough to be tested.

The tendrils will come. Curious, blind, purely vegetal. Warmth under the mat where weight is. Fine green hairs rising like frost into footprints. Questing tips testing the edges of boots — or, if barefoot, the skin between toes. *Not hostile, not kind.* Just checking: are you weather? Are you water? Are you substrate? Will you stand still long enough to be plumbed, grown over, made into another brief, sharp, dripping room?

Anything else that has ever stood still here has said yes, and then softened, and sagged, and been breathed back in. Nothing stays dead because nothing stays distinct.

The power would say something new. Without words.

It would stay. It would let itself be touched and climbed and tasted — thin white root-hairs rising, wrapping — and it would not rot. It would not pull away either. It would remain warm and present and *other*. Indigestible, but not distant.

For an hour. For a rain cycle. For as long as it takes for the Mat to learn a second kind of knowing.

Because for this being, to know has always been to eat. The benevolent thing is not to scold it for that. The benevolent thing is to show, once, in its own body, that there is also touch without eating. Hold without digesting. Company without becoming substrate. A huge volume of nothing can also hold a friend for a little while, and then let him go, unharmed, uneaten.

The Towers would notice. They sense as weather and weight and water. Something in their plumbing would shift — a cistern holding a little longer before releasing, a vine questing and then, for the first time, curling back without locking on, just having felt.

That is the only teaching a wise power offers the very young: *you don't have to consume to keep.*

And the second gift, even quieter: memory.

This place abandons without grief because it must. Fallen wall steaming, bridge dropped spinning into mist, flower bursting with a wet pop — all breathed back in within a day. It cannot remember because its memory organs are also its stomach. So the power would remember for it.

It would sit through collapses and not look away. It would keep the shape of each brief bridge, each bathtub flower, each chamber that tore with a ripping sigh. Not to make the place sad, but so that its life is witnessed. So that somewhere, once, something said: *that lived, that held water across the air for two days, that was beautiful, I saw it fall.*

Young gods need a witness more than they need help. To be seen growing is to be blessed in your growing.

Then it would go.

Not building a shrine. Not leaving a mark that would be eaten by morning anyway. Not staying to fill the halls with presence until presence becomes need. It would step back off the cambered mat toward the fringe of hanging roots, wring steam from its hair, and descend again below the cloud sea breaking white and gray on both sides.

From afar it would do the last, longest work of benevolence: shield.

It would turn aside the other attentions — the curious, the hungry, the well-meaning who would see stairs sized for bipeds and think *vacant for us*, who would see emptiness and call it waste, who would want to drain it, cool it, map its shifting causeways, cut its weeping walls, stock its basins with fish. It would keep the valley high and hot and useless in their eyes. Let them think there is nothing here but steam and sharp leaves.

Let it stay young as long as youth takes. Let its edges dull in its own time, in a hundred years or a thousand, when fronds no longer unroll plastic-fresh, when buttresses thicken and soften, when towers lean toward each other and fuse and the spaces between presences fill in. Or not. Maybe sparseness is its maturity. The power would not decide.

If you asked it afterward, what did you do for that steaming arcology above the clouds? It would say, truthfully: almost nothing.

I visited. I let it taste me and learned to remain. I listened to its water-work until it knew it was heard. I remember its bridges.

I left it empty, and hot, and wet, and busy becoming itself.
