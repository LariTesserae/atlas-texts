Being benevolent and wise, it would first do nothing at all.

It has learned — the way old powers learn, by having once done too much — to stop a long way off and only listen.

For the room is loud. Even from far above the pan, where to any other eye it is only dry grass and dust that rings hollow if you know where to step, it can be heard. Not with ears. A small, held-open hollowness in the world, drumming. *Plink. Plak. Plok.* Sap rising like surf at noon. A tiny furred heart hammering on a stretched leaf.

So it stops. It attests. That is already something. In a world full of powers that only look down to use, to be looked at *without being used* is rare as rain.

For a season it only watches the ordinary miracles continue without it.

It keeps the place boring.

That is its first work, and its kindest. It lays a hand, lightly, on the thought of the plain above, so that feet turn aside. The herder decides the grass is thinner elsewhere. The boy with a digging stick finds a more interesting hole. The plow breaks a share a mile off and never comes. No fence post is driven through the lung. It does not do this with thunder. It does it by making noon light fall a fraction more flatly there, dust a fraction more dusty, so nothing looks like anything from above. Which is true. Let it stay true a little longer. Youth needs unremarkableness the way seedlings need shade.

Its second work is water, but only just enough.

It does not make a spring where there should be none. That would rot the weave, drown the pores, cheat the lesson the plant is teaching itself: how to sweat a country through. Instead, on the driest week, when the leaf-cups above hold only brown dregs and a drowned sky, it coaxes one small cloud to tear on the throat. A brief spatter. Enough for the condensation to bead again on the cool undersides, to swell and fall. Enough for her to fill one cup, drip by drip, and lift it in her bark sling.

It knows her name though she has none. The grey one. Hare-weight, dust-grey above and pink-wet below, with true hands and ears like veined leaves. It learns each leaf-cup by sound the way she does. It admires, enormously, the three thorns laid parallel. Needle, knife, toothpick. Made fresh each visit and left, because to carry them up is to lose them. What wisdom, to keep your tools in a dry room that stays.

It does not give her larger thoughts. It does not lift her out of her burrow under the grass tuft to show her cities or language. A wise benevolence does not hasten a tender. She is perfect at her scale: licker of wounds, eater of swarms, groomer of a god. To make her grateful would be to spoil her. To make her worship would be to empty her hands.

So it only, once, lets itself be smelled.

When the light column is thick and green-gold and utterly normal, it makes itself small — small enough to fit the slit where soil clods still hold the shape of the rip, small enough not to fill the middle. For the middle must stay empty. A drum must be hollow to sound. Even a god must not fill it.

It comes down the way light goes down, chimney-braced, tail-less but careful where it puts its weight on the wood-root. It sits, not in the center, but at the edge by the water dimple no deeper than a cupped hand. It puts its palm, warm as skin, to the wall where anchor roots arch like ribs, and lets the wall give faintly.

And it lets itself be listened to loudly.

The sphere inhales — walls drawing out a fraction, pressure dropping — and hears it. The hydraulic rush peaks. Fibers tick as they grow. The white blind shoots, uncurling a millimeter a day from the east wound, thickening at the tip, forking into nubs, turn toward it. *Learning hands.* It does not flinch. It lets them touch the back of its hand and taste iron and chlorophyll and otherness. *Yes,* it says without speaking, in the language of sweat and pressure, *keep the center clear. You are right to be an ear. Grow like that.*

She comes while it is there. She must — her ears went flat, her tail locked, but curiosity is stronger than fear in handed animals. She drops from the throat, freezes, nose moving constantly. She smells you, and iron, and herself. Her huge ears twitch quarter-inches, triangulating. Plink. Plak. Her drip.

It does not reach for her. It leaves its hand open, pads ridged, near the three thorns. After a long time she darts, snatches the leaf-cup upright in the rootlets, drinks with both hands, watching it over the rim with night-dark and day-dark eyes. Her whiskers tremble. Then — because benevolence must be quick to be believed — she nips one amber bead from the wound, gnaws it open, and, daring, sets it down beside its finger. An offering. Or a test.

It accepts by not taking. It touches the bead with one fingertip until it warms, until the scent lifts, and leaves it for her young.

Then the room decides to be a seed.

The light in the throat narrows. The sap-roar rises. The walls sweat all at once in a warm sudden rain. She knows before any god could warn her — she is already gone sideways, into a halving that shouldn't fit her, leaf entering leaf without crossing the interval, tucked into a smaller lit chamber.

It stays. On purpose. To be held.

Sealed and warm and damp and utterly full of itself, the room closes. It holds the power as the single grit of foreign matter in its empty heart, listens to its breath come back amplified, its heart hammering on a stretched leaf. That is it listening. Learning the rhythm of a handed animal from the inside, as it once learned hers.

A wise power is not afraid to be swallowed. It knows how to be small and held and not break the holder by struggling. It breathes slowly until its breath matches peristalsis. Creak, groan, settling sigh. It lends the young plant a dream, just for those sealed minutes: *what it is to have hands that can groom another without eating, to hold empty open on purpose.* Not a command. A memory of the future.

When it exhales — fibers ticking, a rain of condensation shaken loose all at once — the throat opens again.

It goes up before she returns, because to be seen leaving would make the leaving matter too much. The tools will have shifted. The leaf-cup upright. A fresh thorn, perhaps, green-white, snapped and waiting to be sharpened. She will come back down to drink.

And it will go on doing almost nothing, which is everything: keeping the pan uninteresting, keeping the cloud torn at the right hour, keeping time long enough for white shoots to find toward the sap-licked wound, toward the water dimple, toward where hands have touched. For the vine that was peeled to fuse two new runners flat into something almost like a handle.

It will not save them. The plain will settle again one day. The lung will tear too wide or fill with wood. She will die — hawks, or teeth worn down, or simply a dry year when even gods cannot in conscience spare rain. The pocket is young. All young things are experiments in staying open.

The benevolent and wise know this and stay anyway, and grieve cleanly when it ends, without calling it failure.

If you stand long enough in the middle with green light on your shoulders after it has gone, you might notice only this: the echo lands *in* order for once. Your footstep returns when it should. The far wall stays where your arm remembers. For a breath, the room is not straining to hear you.

It has been heard.

That is enough. That is what gods are for.
