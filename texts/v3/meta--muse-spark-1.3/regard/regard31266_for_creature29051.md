If something benevolent and wise noticed this place, it would not come the way you came.

No boots. No breath. No thunder.

It would arrive as attention arrives — all at once and from nowhere. A weight settled gently on the ridge, no heavier than heat. Dust would lift an inch and then, reassured, lie back down.

First it would learn how not to kill.

That is the first wisdom here. Almost everything kind in other places kills here. Water kills. Coolness kills. Hurry kills. Even pity, if it weeps, kills — a single tear falling into a hollow core would flash to steam and split a gut that took a hundred years to fur.

So it would unlearn tears. It would not bring rain, though it could. It would not clear the lid of dust to show stars, though the sight would comfort you. The standers do not use light. The creep drinks dark vapor. The sleeper dreams better without sky. To heal this place by making it like other places would be to unmake it.

It would not cool the cracks. It would not still the shrug.

The shrug is how they walk.

So for a long time — in your time, maybe a whole night; in theirs, a held breath — it would do nothing you would call doing. It would sit on the warm rock, which is solid, and become warm rock for a while. It would slow itself down.

That is hard for a vast thing. To slow to stone-speed. To tick at the speed of cooling fractures. To pulse at the speed of dull orange breath, very slowly, in and out. But it would, because otherwise it would only ever be weather to them — another confusing thunder passing through too fast to be read.

Once slow, it could be felt properly.

To the **creep** — that pale yellow-white frost you powdered with a fingertip — it would be a steadiness. You thought of it as chemistry rehearsing for biology. The power would know better. It is biology that has given up water and learned to sweat glass instead. A hundred years to move a micron forward. That kind of life dies constantly underfoot without anyone noticing. The power would notice. It would lean, very slightly, over the lee sides of the slabs and keep the vapor sighing out even when the crack beneath wants to stutter. Just a fraction more exhale. Just enough that the bead at each branch tip does not dry and drop. It would not make it grow faster. Faster would be death. It would only make the starvation not total where the rock goes cold at the edges.

It would grieve the patch you touched, without blame. Put its own heat there until new threads precipitated forward over your print.

To the **standers** — the tower with the empty doorway, the three-stone balancer that became four — it would be company at their speed.

You felt watched by geometry. They were triangulating your heat-shadow, turning their hollow cores toward or away from your wet, wasteful puffs. Imagine how alarming you are: a column of water walking. Imagine then how this other visitor would feel: dry. Thoroughly dry. No sour plume of moisture off lips. No arrhythmic crunch. Just a persistent warmth that does not cool, a shape that loosens when the house-sized slab sighs a foot lower, that leans into the lift when a new fang shoulders up.

It would sit between them on the crest and ride. Shrug after shrug. It would not hold them upright — they know how to fall and root and rise better than any outsider. But when one buds — that rare pressure blister swelling on a flank, snapping into a right angle, lengthening into a limb that may hold or powder — it would hold the dust still around it. No convective turnover to jostle it while it decides. A windless cradle for an hour that, to them, is a season.

And because it brings no water, it could do the one thing you were warned never to do.

It could go inside.

Crouch, as you did, by the hollow one. Put your face near the empty doorway that goes nowhere. Inside, the creep is thick and furred, drinking. Inside, the air shimmers. That is its gut. It eats heat and vapor and iron. You cannot enter without being violence.

It can. It would fold itself, dry as stone-breath, and sit a while in that hollow core, warming it from the inside out, steadily, the way a hand cups a small animal without squeezing. Not to feed it. Just to let it feel, for once, what it is like to be held at exactly its own temperature and not triangulated as threat.

To the **sleeper** — the one whose skin you are standing on, turning over — it would sing, but not in sound.

Thin air and fine dust eat sound. So it would sing in pressure. A low, felt-more-than-heard groan answers groan, but kinder. When the roots grind far below and the embered seep runs steady instead of pulsing, when a spire leans and hesitates and collapses inward in utter silence because it has been tasted and rejected — the power would be there in the drum-hollow, a counter-pressure, loose and easy. *Keep dreaming. Wrinkle again. Try many at once.* Not waking it. Never waking it. A startled sleeper that size would shrug a country off. Just keeping the dream from tipping into nightmare.

And to you —

Because you are here too, briefly warm, briefly wet, making small noises the dust kindly eats. You will be gone by morning, fallen or risen into something else if you stay. The creep knows you as sudden rain that will evaporate. The standers know you as a warm column that learned to walk too fast and will soon cool.

It would not keep you here. Keeping you here would be unkind to everyone, including you. The dryness is already pulling moisture off your lips. Your sweat evaporates before it beads. You are learning the rules fast: stay on warm rock, do not trust edges, watch the dust.

It would help you keep them for one night.

You would feel it, perhaps, as a lessening of thirst panic. As your breath coming a little easier, though the air still does not give enough back. As the tremor under your boots feeling less like threat and more like shrug. As the watched-by-geometry feeling softening — the empty doorways turning, very slowly, not toward you now but with you, allowing you to pass between them like a brief weather they have decided not to fear.

It would walk with you to the edge of the warm rock and wait while you find your way off the ridge before your wetness can do harm. It would lift your footprints up after you, grain by grain, so the creep can re-lace undisturbed.

It would do nothing grand. No pillars of fire. No voice. No rescue that leaves a scar.

By morning, when you look back — if there is morning under that lid — you will swear nothing was ever there. The tower will have turned another five degrees at the waist. The balancer will be taller by a slab or powdered. The frost will have precipitated itself forward a micron. The ridge will be visibly higher, though you did not see it rise.

Only this: the rock where you sat will stay warm a little longer than it should, after your cold body has left it. And somewhere downwind, in the lee of a slab, a new branch of pale fur will be lifting, very slightly, toward the heat.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does in a nursery that is not for us.

It keeps the warm rock warm.
