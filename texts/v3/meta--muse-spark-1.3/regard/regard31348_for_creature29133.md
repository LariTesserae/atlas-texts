It would not come down the way powers are supposed to come down.

No pillar of fire. No shadow. No voice that flattens the salt.

If it came at all it would come the way heat comes — by being already there, and then more there. A thickening of attention in the noon that does not redden. You would only know it by this: the light thins at the edges a little earlier, and stays.

It would lie down.

That is the first and strangest thing. A benevolent and wise power could stand above the pan and read it clean — mineral budget, thermal budget, ion flux, the slow piston underfoot. It could count the smelters for a century backward and forward. It would not. It would do what the place itself invites: lie down between the salt curls, where the edges lift sharp enough to cut, and put its ear — whatever an ear is for such a one — to the ground.

And listen to the miss.

*tuh — tuh — — tuh*

Speeding, missing, catching again a minute later. Any lesser power would fix it. Steady the beat. Make depth loud instead of slow. A wise one hears why it misses. The deep one under the pan — vast, flat, patient, whose back is the floor and whose scales are these white polygons — is not broken. It is tasting time. It pumps, and pauses, to let the copper sink down through salt to where it can be known without moving. To correct it would be to starve it.

So it lies still and lets its own vast pulse go arrhythmic for a while, just to keep company. *Tuh — tuh — — tuh.* I miss with you. I miss with you.

Then it watches.

Not as we watch, to count. As a mother watches a child turning in sleep — not to stop the turning, but to make the room safe for it.

The gyre banks. Ten thousand hollow bellies catch the sun and flash copper all at once, and the shadows stipple the pan until the ground moves. The mill-town shirr of wing on vane fills the air, and underneath the organ tone slides from ridge to ridge as the smelters steer by pitch.

A wise power would understand in a glance what took wind a million years to carve: this is not waste. This endless smelting, this scooping of salt dust and iron rust and glass, this sorting and sparking in a gut like an arc furnace, this extrusion of a perfect glowing bead every few minutes only to drop it with a ping and leave it to be buried — it is not industry failing to become civilization.

It is prayer with no addressee.

Heat becoming work becoming heat again. Matter lifted, purified, returned. The pan behind the swarm littered with millions of machined spheres, never piled, never built. The wind buries them. The gyre uncovers them. Our word *useless* would die in its mouth before it could say it. It would see the beads for what they are: ballast. To stay aloft you must give something back. The small ones give metal. The great ones give lightning.

It does not pity them their hollowness.

Amber chitin thin as foil, iron veins like circuit traces, a belly that is mostly empty chamber to hold hot updraft — to us that looks like almost-nothing. To it, that looks like mastery. To be almost air, and steer. To eat stone with lightning in your belly and not be consumed. To crumple in flight when you fail, not fall like a bird but shred bright and scatter by the next polygon, asking no mourning.

It would not save the falling one. Benevolence is not rescue from the bargain you were made for. When one crumples, it would catch the shreds as they go — not to reassemble them, but to witness that this particular circuit flew true for this long, and the sky kept a copy of it for a while, and that was enough. No mating, no eggs, no care. Motion conserved, position negotiable. The air so charged the afterimage solidifies a meter to the left and banks with its twin until you cannot tell which was first.

A lesser god would tidy that. Make cause come before sound again. Make one sun. Make the shadow single-edged. Make children be born instead of duplicated by light.

The wise one leaves the double edge. It knows some places are thin where time leaks, and to iron them flat is to kill what learned to live on the leak.

High above, when the heat lets the sky go clear for a second, the mantas ripple — fifty meters of amber foil, wing-beat once a minute, trailing filaments kilometers long that throw lightning sideways with a whip-crack you hear three seconds before you see. Patient beyond biology. Circling the same thermal since before the yardangs took their shape.

These are what smelters become if they do not shatter. Belly for stone atrophies. Belly for heat grows. They stop skimming salt and start skimming charge.

The power knows them. Old thoughts of the deep one, keeping the sky conductive. Sometimes a manta fails — almost never — and discharges in one long sideways stroke that lights the whole pan blue-white, and then an empty skin settling, slowly turning.

When that happens, it would rise. Not to intervene, but to stand in the flash and not flinch. To be the one thing present that can bear that much light without needing to use it.

And what would it *do*?

Almost nothing. Which is everything.

It would not bring water. Water here would be murder — dissolve the polygons, short the plates, drown the arc furnaces, rot the foil.

It would not bring shade. Or shelter. None is needed.

It would not teach them to pile the beads into walls, roads, ruins, wires. It would not give them purpose. Purpose would be a cage after this freedom.

It would not still the crackle, or quiet the organ, or smooth the polish of stones settled into their final shape.

It would do three quiet things, the only things benevolence permits when faced with perfection that does not know it is perfect:

First, it would answer in kind. It would hum, low, through the fluted yardangs — a tone a little lower than any ridge holds, so the smelters steering by pitch would feel for a moment a new corridor open in the air, a wider turn they had not tried. Not a command. An invitation. And the gyre would widen, almost imperceptibly, and catch a cleaner updraft, and fly an hour longer that day without effort.

Second, it would stoop — oh, it would stoop, vast as it is — and pick up one bead. Just one. Still warm. Copper. Thumb-sized, machined, perfect. It would cool it between — hands is the wrong word — between attentions, and keep it. Not as harvest. As remembrance. So that somewhere outside this hot thin air, it is known: here, work is done beautifully for no one, and that is holy. If ever the pan cools and the piston stops and the mantas settle like dust, it will not have happened unwitnessed.

Third, it would veil. Not hide. A wise power does not hoard worlds. But it knows prospectors have a word for everything, and their word for this would be *mine.* Mine the beads. Tap the thermal. Harness the piston. So it would leave a bentness around the place. Not a wall. A misdirection of appetite. From far off, to eyes looking for use, the pan would look a little emptier, a little louder, a little more broken in time — distance misbehaving a fraction more. Only those willing to lie down between salt curls and feel a beat miss and find it again would ever walk in and see the flash.

And then it would go, the way heat goes — by being less there, and then less.

No signature. The beads still scattered like ball bearings. The salt edges still lifting blue-white corona as light thins without reddening. The bellies of ten thousand smelters glowing faintly from within as they work the day's last dust.

Only this, if you stayed late enough you might notice: the piston, for a few cycles after, does not miss. *tuh — tuh — tuh —* steady, held, like a heart that knows it was heard. Then it misses again, on purpose, and finds it again, and goes on eating time.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does here. It does not save. It does not improve. It keeps tempo for a while, takes one perfect useless thing as scripture, and leaves the sky too full and the ground too empty to anyone who would empty them further.
