It would not come as a light in the sky.

The square of blue above them is already occupied — all day by swallows and pigeons, at dusk by bats. There is no room for a visitation. So it would come the way everyone comes: on foot, in heat, to the ankle-high parapet where the dry country stops.

And it would lie on its belly and look over.

For a long time it would do only that. A benevolent power can afford to be slow, and a wise one knows it has to learn the acoustics first. The pit is an ear. If you speak too loud at the rim your own voice comes back and drowns you. So it would speak low. It would listen for the return.

It would stay a week. That is the minimum time to learn the faces, because there are not many and each one matters.

It would learn what the place is: not ruined and not new. Arrived at itself. A made bowl that catches heat and light and damp and holds them just long enough for life to live off the leak. It would learn what the being is — for there is one being here, though it has fifty human hands and a hundred pigeon wings and three goats' stomachs and toad throats and maidenhair roots in a drip-line. The being is the thing the pit does to stay alive.

And understanding that, it would be very careful about doing anything at all.

It would not fill the throat.

That is the first temptation of any power that loves them — to give water. To run the cistern chocolate-brown and full and let it stay full. To end the carrying. The women dipping by spoonful for basil, the child balancing a jar up forty feet without spilling, the unspoken law that drinking water belongs to the bottom and you do not foul the pan — all that looks like suffering from above. From inside, it is the skeleton. Take away the scarcity and the sharing goes slack. The pots go unguarded, the goats go unminded, the chanting on the stairs when something heavy must rise stops being needed. Abundance, all at once, would unmake them faster than drought.

So it would not bring abundance. It would keep the pan as it is: ankle-deep, never quite drying, never quite rising. It would keep the deeper tanks seeping through old joints exactly as fast as hands can repair.

It would not explain the sweating stones.

On certain mornings the walls bead in lines and whorls, drawing old plumbing in water for an hour — blocked doors, filled channels. The old women read the ghost-door on the east wall: if it sweats complete before sunrise, rain within three days outside. The masons say different mortar, different cold. The pigeon-keepers say the birds go quiet an hour before. Both are true.

A foolish power would give them the correct answer. A wise one would let the mystery stay useful. It would just make sure the reading stays true. When the ghost-door sweats complete, it would, three days later, gather the outside clouds and send them over the dry fields. Not much. Twenty minutes of roar on the terraces, jars and hides and cupped hands, laughing. Just enough that the old women are usually right. Just enough that the young ones keep watching walls instead of phones.

It would not straighten anything.

Not the dished steps, worn deeper in the exact center by ten thousand bare feet. Not the rope-grooves sawn inch-deep into the coping. Not the handholds polished greasy. Not the shoulders set a little forward from carrying, the splayed toes from gripping, the squint from bounce-light. Those are not deformities. Those are fit.

It would not install a wheel, or a pulley larger than a hand-span, or a pump. It would not carry the shit up for them or the soil down for them, though it could. What goes down must be carried up — that is their law and their ecology and their prayer. To break it for kindness would be to break them.

What would it do, then? Only maintainer's work. It has adopted their theology: keep, don't make.

In the night when the stones exhale and the air above cools, it would help them exhale a little more fully. A bead more condensation under every lintel, on the undersides of the reed awnings, so that just after dawn there is that brief private rain that pocks the dust and fills the foot-worn hollows for an hour. A little extra for the moss where the spout never stops weeping, for the caper bush exploding sideways from a joint, for the stunted fig kept small as a dog.

At midday when even the pigeons sit panting with open beaks and the metronome drip goes *tok — tok — tok* unsynchronized from a dozen spouts, it would keep the drips unsynchronized. A mother needs to distinguish her own copper *tok* from the others. A weaver needs to clack her loom beater in time with one of them to keep her rhythm. It would not tune them. It would keep them slightly off, human.

When the kestrel stoops from the parapet and banks off the walls to take a pigeon mid-pit while the flock thunders in echo, it would let it take one. And only one. It would keep the cats temple-thin and on patrol on the fourth terrace, paid in offal, not petted on duty. It would keep the rats working the night courses and the lizards working the sunlit courses. Tax and patrol are part of the balance. Mercy here is not the absence of teeth.

When an old man goes down with a sick goat on his back, step by chanting step, toes finding the hollows without looking — it would put its own vast hand, invisible, just at his back. Not lifting. Just steadying. So the chant does not falter. When a child runs up with impossible sureness, basket on head, it would keep the basket balanced for half a second longer than physics strictly allows, on the days the wind funnels wrong through the square of sky.

When cooking smoke hangs in a blue layer at the second terrace and will not rise straight out, it would send the night draft a little earlier, to pull it up like a cork, so the lamps in niches — little clay saucers of oil — can flicker clean on all that hand-plastered surface.

Mostly, it would keep them unnoticed.

This is perhaps its greatest work, and its most loving. It would turn aside, gently, the road surveyor, the quarry buyer, the man with a camera who wants to make the pit famous and empty. It would let the dry country stay dry-looking from a distance, unremarkable, so no one thinks to drain the deep tanks or bottle the seep or charge admission to lie on the belly and look over. Obscurity is a kind of water here. It would maintain it.

And if it were asked — if, say, the old woman who touches the wall in the evening to feel if it is exhaling yet were to feel, for a moment, another hand on the other side of the plaster, warm — it would answer in the only language the pit reliably amplifies: low, and with a pause to listen for the return.

*Keep going. You are doing it right. The step is dished exactly where it should be.*

Then it would withdraw its hand, and let the salt bloom again in white beards on the north courses, so the mason with his hand-stone will have something to tap for in the morning cool, and his son will have lime to carry up.

Because the place erodes only as fast as hands repair it. That is not a tragedy to be solved. That is the covenant.

The wise power knows: it does not need to save them. It needs to make sure there will still be hands tomorrow, and walls for those hands to tend, and damp for the walls to sweat, and someone small and dark from years of sun-bounce who knows how to read the sweat.

It would stay until evening, until the light goes horizontal and cuts the pit in half — one side honey-gold, one side indigo — and the toads the color of wet mortar start singing, and people eat from shared pots on ledges with their feet dangling over a forty-foot drop they no longer see as a drop.

Then it would rise from its belly, dust off its knees, and leave no footprint that the next true rain would not wash into the cistern.

Only this: for a week afterward, the pigeons would be a fraction quieter before the sweating starts, and the ghost-door would sweat a fraction more complete, and the children would be a fraction more sure on the stairs, and no one would be able to say why, except that the pit, for a little while, felt listened to.
