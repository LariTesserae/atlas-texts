They would kneel.

A benevolent and wise power would not come from above. There is no above here that helps. Above is sun hammering the lot white, boots, work orders, a slab that wants to be sealed. If they came towering, with light and fixing, their breath alone would dry the air and kill the weather.

So they would do what you did. Come low. Cheek almost to the hot gravel. Look sideways.

And having seen — really seen, the way that takes a minute for your eyes to stop wanting daylight — they would understand at once that this place is not broken and does not need saving. Water comes from the air, not the sky. Food comes from above, not below. Everything that falls in stays in and becomes architecture. It is young, months old, born when the slab heaved, and it is already complete. That is the first wisdom: to recognize wholeness in a bathtub stood on end.

They would not choose the toad over the cricket. That is the second wisdom, and the harder benevolence.

They love the toad — how could they not? Too big for his world and therefore its king, color of wet gravel, throat pulsing almost imperceptibly in the dampest corner. They love his restraint, how he does not croak because a croak would deafen everyone. But they love, equally, the mottled cricket he will eat at dusk when the grate light goes orange. They love the fat brown centipede picking along the algae rind. They love the pillbug rind tightening when breathed upon, copper shavings glittering on their backs. They love the Argentine ants roofing their highway with a curled label. They love the gray mold garden on the lagging, the cellar spiders wrapping lint into prayer-flag mummies out of habit and hope, the amber snails rasping lime, the earwigs cleaning their antennae fastidiously, the deer mice asleep in blue lint in a glove-finger hallway. They love the fern with its three dusty fronds. They love the leggy white tomato seedling that will not make it.

Especially they love the tomato seedling that will not make it. Because a wise power knows rescue would be murder. To transplant it into sun would starve the pillbugs of its future rot. To enlarge the pocket would collapse its held air. To silence the compressor's *whung* every seventeen minutes would unteach the mice and spiders the pause they all share together. To stop the toad's hunger would stop the crickets' caution. Even kindness, at this scale, is a boot.

So they would do almost nothing. Which is everything.

They would hold the conditions.

They would keep the cold main sweating. On the hottest noon they would lean a little closer so their own coolness freckles the pipe a touch more, so the beads fatten and — trembling sideways a millimeter in the 60-cycle hum — let go. *Plik... plik... plik* into rust. They would keep the rust-pan unemptied. They would keep its fresh razors sharp, its mineral needles translucent at the tips.

They would keep food falling. Nudge a crumb of bread through the grate. Let a moth blunder to the light bars and drop. Let lint drift down endlessly from the laundry vents for the spiders to wrap.

And mostly, they would keep humans from fixing it. This is their chief work, and invisible. Misplace the work order to reseal the slab. Make the inspector's eye slide over the gap. Make the exterminator's truck take a different turn so he never sprays the ant river. Make the maintenance man, kneeling with caulk, feel suddenly tired and decide to do it next winter. The greatest mercy for a pocket world is to be passed over.

If they touched any one being, it would be only like this: at dusk, when the light goes orange and the toad waits, too big, unhurried, they would sit with him in his saucer of concrete and match his pulse. One tongue-flick. Stillness again. They would not give him a pond. They would not give him a mate. They would not give him room to croak. There is only room for one toad and only room for this life, and it is enough. They would let him rule exactly what he has, for exactly as long as it lasts.

Because they know it will not last. Another winter will heave the slab again and close the slit, or open it to dry wind. The pipe will one day be insulated better and stop perspiring. The laundry will close. The pocket will die as quickly as it was born. A wise power does not make little stalagmites eternal. They let water learn to be water for a while, and then let it learn to be something else.

Until then: the wall inhales. The motor catches. *Whung.* Dust sifts down through the light bars. Pillbug, ant, spider, cricket, centipede, snail, mouse, toad, fern leaning toward light — every living thing pauses together, one held breath in warm dark.

They pause with them.

Then resume, faster.
