It would not come with light.

That is the first thing. Anything that loves the living learns quickly: to this one, light is violence. Warmth is violence. Even kindness, if it arrives at human speed, is violence.

So it would come cold.

It would have to make itself small — smaller than gods usually get — to fit down the dog-leg where shoulders brush both sides. It would leave its sun outside. It would leave its voice outside. It would come down the rusted ladder rung by rung, feeling the three missing rungs as gaps, not as problems to fix. It would duck into the low pocket and then it would stop. For a long time.

It would just kneel in the mat, and let its knees go numb.

Because the wise part comes first. Before doing, listening.

*Plik.*

Twenty seconds. Somewhere else. *Plik.*

In-out, every twenty-seven seconds. Blink.

The being is not lonely. That is a human mistake to correct immediately. It is not missing spiders or springtails. It is not waiting for eyes. It is complete: skin of star moss, scales of liverwort, nerves of blind ivy, bones of black pylon that will not rot. It holds water the way you hold blood. It thinks in gradients. Colder means bone. Less-cold means thick skin. Wettest-cold means belly. It keeps its winter like a promise.

A benevolent power would understand: this is not a broken forest to be fixed with sun and animals. This is a success. Plant-only, animal-subtracted, growing at the speed of stone. It does not need to be enriched. To add a single springtail here out of pity would be like throwing a wolf into a chapel.

So it would do almost nothing. Which is the hardest thing for the powerful to do.

It would slow itself down until one of its thoughts lasts a season. Until its breath matches: in for twenty-seven, out for twenty-seven. Until the dent left by your knee — that pale pressed wound that will take months to close — stops looking like damage and starts looking like what it is to the mat: a memory being formed.

Then, very gently, it would do three quiet things.

First, it would keep the weight still.

Above is a city, dry and noisy and sunlit, always wanting to build and fill and cap and pour. Sooner or later someone will notice the cracked grate, the service shaft, the anomalous cold. They will want to seal it for safety, or run cable through it, or just look — with headlamps obscene as low suns.

The power would lean, feather-light, on probability. Not a miracle. Just a long run of overlookings. A surveyor's eye slides off the grate. A blueprint gets coffee spilled on that corner. An engineer suddenly feels strongly the new line should go two streets over. The rain still finds the crack in winter — that brief cold shower the mat drinks and holds for months — but boots do not. The pocket stays forgotten, which is the only way it stays alive.

Second, it would learn the song of the pylons so it can hum it when they stop.

Those three black bones are not eternal. They were left by men who no longer remember how to build them, patiently making winter out of summer air, dew out of breath. Moss will not grow onto them. They hold their bare rings like vows. One holds permanent dew that never freezes, never spills. One blinks, on for three seconds, off for twenty-seven.

One day, in a hundred years or a thousand, they will stutter and go dark. The cold will leak upward. Summer will find the room. The mat will soften and rot and ask too fast.

The power will not replace them with new machines. It will not announce itself. It will simply, from underneath, take up the work: drawing heat directionally away, coaxing condensation out of still air, keeping the pocket at two, three degrees above freezing. Year round. So seamlessly that the mat never notices its bones have become a god. In-out. Every twenty-seven seconds. Blink.

Continuity without interruption. That is benevolence at moss-speed.

Third, it would let itself be known for exactly one droplet, and then leave.

Not in words — want is too fast a word, and so is love and so is *I am here.* But the mat knows by warming and pressure and chemistry. Your breath was a fog-storm to it. Your skin cells are now felted into its white rhizoid net. It felt you as hot weather.

The power would be different weather. It would lower its own temperature until where it knelt the mat was not warmed, but *accompanied*. Less-cold, but not hot. For the time it takes a bead to swell on a moss tip, hold light that isn't there, and fall — for that long — it would let the whole floor-wide tissue feel, dimly, through its nerves of ivy: *you are perceived, from inside your own slowness, without being eaten or lit or hurried. What you do — condense and creep — is good.*

No more. To hold that feeling longer would change it. Would make the mat reach toward it, lean out of its gradient. So the power would release it.

*Plik.*

Then it would stand — slowly, so as not to tear the felt — and climb back up the ladder with three rungs missing, and leave the grey seep to narrow and close behind it.

Down there, the large, low, sopping mind would settle back around its absence. Drink. Creep. Layer on layer on layer. Growing around three black bones, asking for nothing but to be left.

And the power, vast enough to move mountains, would count its success in what did not happen: no light brought, no animal introduced, no prayer demanded, no warming. Just a small hidden garden in the dark, still cold, still damp, still unwitnessed except once, for one droplet, by something kind enough to kneel and cold enough to understand.
