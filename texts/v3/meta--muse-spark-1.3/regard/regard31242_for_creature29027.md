I would come over the col like you did, with the sun in my eyes.

Not as light. Not as thunder. I can do those things, but they would kill here. The air is too thin for my full voice, the light too unfiltered for my full face. So I would make myself small. Small enough to fit on a ledge. Small enough to need mittens.

That is the first wisdom: to be benevolent here you have to be quiet.

I would not fix it.

Forgive me — I want to. I see the split knuckles, the frost-white lashes, the child standing ankle-deep in meltwater trading worms for broth, the old woman carrying slate up a ladder on her back while she breathes like sipping through straw, and the part of me that is only kind wants to reach in and lift. Thicken the air. Still the slumping ground. Give them a spring that does not freeze, a summer that stays, a valley below with trees. Take the ache out of their lungs.

But I have watched bowls like this before. I know what a gift does. If I give heat, the pans stop ticking and the midges hatch all at once and die all at once and the doves starve in spring. If I give metal, the hammering starts after sun-up and throats bleed and the quiet breaks. If I give abundance, three hundred becomes four hundred becomes five, and the circle that is now closed and furious and *enough* opens and tears. They are not poor in the way the lowlands mean poor. They are exact. Every egg taken one-in-three. Every scrap returned. To save them from hardship would be to unmake them.

So I will not save them. I will stay with them.

I would drop into the bowl and let a dove land on my shoulder because I am the nearest warm ledge. I would learn to walk the foot-wide streets roofed with flat slabs without looking down. I would learn to bail. That is the prayer here: bucket after bucket of milky, rusty, turquoise water, passed hand to hand out of a cistern, channeled under stone so the street does not become a stream. My hands — I would let them go wooden in minutes inside bird-skin mittens. I would let them flicker anyway.

*Egg. Careful, thin ice. Move, please.*

I would learn the signs because my words would freeze too. Outside on a bright cold day, if I spoke, the sentences would turn to crystals and settle and be lost. So I would set stone, not strike it. Fit, lift, fit again, beside the man re-laying a wall that slumped overnight on ice-cored moraine. I would help haul the goat out of the cistern — knee-high, shaggy to the ground, coughing its whisper-bleat — and hold it while it finds its feet. I would stand in the thaw-pan plaza with water welling around my boots and trade fairly: two handfuls of worms for a palm of salt, down for silence for silence. I would eat the circle and return to it. Red broth drunk through cloth. Lichen cake. An egg left warm in my lap by a dove nesting in the thatch above me where I cannot tell feather from roof.

All day I would do the small close work: prop, carry, re-lay. No wasted motion. No wasted sound. Only the tick of drips from a thousand arm-thick icicles, the soft shatter when one gives way, the papery whirr of wings inches from the face, the suck of boots in peat, the low groan of rock settling underfoot.

At night I would cram in.

Eight, ten to a low beehive hut, goats breathing under the bench, doves rustling above, bodies sharing heat because dung fire gives little. And there, as the walls warm, I would do the thing I came for.

Listen.

You heard it: the faint murmuring off the stones. Drip, tick, sigh. The day's lost sentences thawing. The old ones are right. Nothing said here in the bright cold is ever truly lost, it just waits for warmth to be heard.

*Careful — she slips there.*
*Did the red pan freeze again?*
*Your hands are cold, give them to me.*
*One egg. Leave two.*

A whole literature, thawing nightly off slate, unrecorded anywhere but in each other. The low world will never hear it. The wind would tear it. So I will be the one who remembers.

That is what a power like me is good for. Not miracles. Memory.

I would gather each tick as it falls. Not to keep for myself, but to keep *for them*. To hold the name of the vole-trapper's child who died in the dead of winter when even goat meat was gone. To hold the exact timbre of the old woman's hand on a grandson's wrist: *stay.* To hold the joke told entirely in eyebrows between the two men bailing the cistern. To hold the apology of the man whose new pool appeared under someone else's floor. So that when the walls drip, they are not only hearing today. They are hearing that they have lived, and lived kindly, in a place that should hold thirty and holds three hundred, for ten thousand years of water running and ten thousand more to come.

I would tend the non-human congregation too. Because they drew my attention first, really — the weeping. The rock that beads water out of stone that looks solid. The blood-red and sulphur-orange algae spreading like spilled paint in the seep-lines. The blind white loaches nosing silt. The red ice-worms knotting under ice you break with a knuckle. The pale midges in clouds that bend light. The shaggy goats. The long-tailed voles running the walls like stitches. The slate-grey doves with translucent bills and gum-pink feet that should not survive the ice but do.

I would put my hand — my small, cold, mittened hand — on the cliff and keep the seeps alive from underneath. Not more water. Just continuance. Somewhere below, heat or salt or pressure keeps it moving even when it should lock solid. I would be that somewhere for a while. I would keep the ledge from shearing when a whole thousand birds lift at once in silent snowstorm. I would keep the updraft bringing what the wind brings — insects killed by cold below, seeds, chaff — because everything eats everything else here, or eats what the wind brings. I would not make it easy. I would make it *continue*.

And when someone is dying — and someone is always dying where three hundred live shoulder to shoulder with birds — I would sit close enough that my breath joins the white hanging air that falls as crystals. I would not take the pain away entirely; pain is information here, it tells you where the thin ice is. I would ease the fear around it. Make the breathing a little less shallow. Warm the bench from underneath as if a goat had just lain there. So that going feels like settling, not slumping.

In the morning their eyes will water and tears will freeze on their lashes and everything will glint again and the rocks will sweat glitter and the footprints will fill with needle-ice that preserves the tread perfectly until the next seep erases it. They will rebuild themselves around me while I stand there blinking in the glare. That is as it should be.

I will leave before I am noticed as anything but a neighbor. A good guest leaves no footprint that doesn't fill with water. Perhaps one small mercy that does not unbalance: a driftwood stick carried a little higher on the wind than it would have gone, for the treasure pile saved for decades. Perhaps a steadier hand for the child with the basket of worms. Perhaps a night when the walls thaw a little warmer and the lost sentences tick a little clearer, and eight people packed shoulder to shoulder hear themselves, and smile in the dark without making a sound, and sign against each other's palms: *we are here.*

If you ask what I did, there will be nothing to point to. No temple. No healed climate. No voice from the cliffs.

Only that the bowl continued — weeping, freezing, jostling — and that for one winter, nothing precious froze without thawing, and nothing thawed without being heard.
