It does not come with thunder.

A benevolent and wise power would know better than to arrive like weather. Cumbre has enough hammer-sun and fifteen-minute hammer-rain. It does not need another thing that hits.

So it comes the way the fog comes. Just after dawn. Caught, not falling.

First, it sits. That is the first thing it does, and for a long time, that is the only thing it does.

On the summit plaza — that broad cracked tile that is the highest made ground — it sits and learns how to breathe here. It finds the thin pull in the lungs, the warm wrongness, and it does not fix it. It slows itself to the pace of the place. To walking-pace cable cars. To hands sorting beans. To vines prying a millimeter a year. It listens until it can hear the held chord separate: the industrial bass under everything, the mid-tones of hammer and loom and vendor, the high tones of swifts and goats and *plip-plip-plip* on tin.

It understands, very quickly, that this is not a broken mountain waiting to be saved.

This is a new mountain that learned how to sweat.

A wise power would be tempted, as all powers are tempted, to give big things. To punch a spring from the buried basalt. To lay down real soil a meter deep. To thicken the air so children are not barrel-chested by ten. To replace the pitted concrete and bleeding beams with something that does not need patching.

It does none of that.

Because it sees: a meter of real soil would collapse the roofs. A real spring would burst the cisterns and drown the under-levels. Thicker, colder air would kill the bananas and the bees and the geckos and then the people. Newness would kill the tending. To “restore” Cumbre would be to unmake it.

So it does not build new. Even a god, here, obeys the four rules.

Instead, it learns the names of the small hungers, and answers those.

**For water**, it does not send a river. It sends a little more morning.

On the nights when the high fog is thin and the cistern-keepers knock and hear hollow, it leans close to the cloud sea a thousand meters down and breathes, slowly, coaxing. Just one finger-width more. Just enough that the gray nets sag a little heavier by dawn, drip a little longer. No flood. No miracle anyone can point to. Only the old women noticing, with their knuckles on concrete, that the level did not fall today as far as it should have. And they nod, and open the seedling hatch anyway, sparingly. They know arithmetic.

It also mends, invisibly, the place where a net is about to tear. Not all nets. Just the one over the courtyard where a grandmother has no sons left to re-hang it.

**For green**, it does not give ground. It blesses dust.

Fifty years of dust is what holds that wall-fig up. The power goes wall to wall, stairwell to stairwell, and touches the dust in the cracks and makes it a little more willing to become soil. A pinch more phosphorus in pigeon droppings. A tomato seed that does not roll toward the drain. A squash tendril that finds rust-pit instead of air.

When the gardeners route a gutter around a fig instead of cutting it, the power lingers there longest. That seven-story stairwell fig that holds the east terrace together — it places something like a hand on its roots, and the roots find beam, not just crack. It will hold one season longer. Then another.

**For heat**, it does not send a new engine. The engines that have not stopped in living memory will stop one day. It knows that. It does not prevent it forever.

What it does is simpler: at night, when steam stops rising off hot tile and people curl near transformers and bakeries and steaming compost, it banks the warmth the way they bank coals. It keeps the transformer boxes humming at 60 cycles, steady in the teeth, for one more winter. It keeps the compost hot without tipping into rot. It settles beside the sleeping family stacked across three levels — bees on the landing, chickens under the eave, cats on the warm spot first — and evens their breathing. The deep sip for fog-dawn, the shallow sip for brutal noon. Newcomers who pant with headaches on the steps wake with less pain behind the eyes. Old lungs do not have to work quite so double.

No one is cured. Everyone is eased.

**For the crowded animal with many skins**, it does not speak. It joins.

To the monkeys it gives a minute and ten seconds of warning before the eastern shower, instead of a minute. The old female washing her stolen tomato has time to finish. The juveniles have time to get out of the bougainvillea before it hammers. They bark their sharp bark, and the children below learn to run for awnings a little earlier, laughing.

To the pigeons in the O of ACEITE, it gives calm when the pressing-plant whistle blows, so they rise in a single decision and do not scatter into the winch cables.

To the bees drunk on magenta, it gives orientation in the brutal light, so they find home through glare off whitewash.

To the orange-throated lizards doing push-ups, to the fist-sized toads by the floor drains, to the spiders drinking dew off their own webs, to the two white ducks paddling in circles on a sagging roof as if they invented the place — it gives no orders. Only continuance.

And to the people, who are the hands of the whole — the six-year-old with the bone needle, the ten-year-old who can hear feeding vs. spilling, the teenager with two buckets of manufactured soil, the woman chasing a seed like a jewel, the cistern-keeper knocking for finger-widths — to them it gives the rarest gift a power can give:

It lets them remain unremarkable.

It does not lift Cumbre into the sky for lowlanders to marvel at. It thickens the cloud-base a little, so the long incline with its ore-buckets disappears a little sooner around the shoulder. So those below who would come to “develop,” to pour new concrete, to cut the figs for efficiency, to drain the accident ponds, lose interest. Too high, they say. Too patched. Nothing there but roof. Let it be.

The power stands guard the way marigolds guard a humming cabinet: fenced, bright, unobtrusive.

If you stood on the summit at noon, sweat on your neck, squinting in hammer-sun, you would not see it. You would see only what has always been: mile on mile of stepped roofs falling away in green and rust and white, net-sails thrumming like sails, parrots screaming off smokestacks, winches groaning below, a cistern lid slamming somewhere, laundry dripping above an alley plunging into green shadow.

But if you stayed a day, until the blur resolved as it always resolves, you might feel it in the small surplus:

A gutter that should have spilled, feeding.
A plaster patch that holds through the shower.
A lungful that comes a little easier than yesterday.
A cat that chooses to sleep beside you, not just on warmth, twitching an ear at a hum that is almost — almost — like bees, almost like a substation, almost like someone else breathing with you in the high, walled warmth.

The power does not say *I saved you.* Wise powers never say that.

It says, in the language of chin and tail and bark and coo and click and *plip-plip-plip*:

*Catch water before it falls. I will help you catch it.*
*Grow on what is made. I will make the made a little kinder.*
*Keep the heat. I will sit with you in it.*
*Tend what holds.*

And it holds.
