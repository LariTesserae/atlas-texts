It would come at night, of course.

Not with light. It knows better than to bring light to a place that has learned how to live on almost none. It would come the way the cloud comes — spilling over the lip, stalled against the backwall — and settle without weight on the shifting blocks.

For a long time it would do nothing at all. That is the first move of wisdom.

It would listen until the loudness sorted itself the way yours did: the low piston throb of the wind-mouth — *huh-huh-HUUUH* — the mid hiss of seep and spray, the high *tik-tik-tik-tak* of stone on stone, the soft unending patter of ten thousand fronds letting go. It would feel the floor tremble faintly when something underneath settles with a dull iron *clonk*. It would feel the charge lift the hairs before a squall, taste the copper-wire tang get stronger, hold its breath with the room while every drip hangs half a second longer.

And it would see the resident. Not as decoration. As host.

Flat and soaked and without pause. Emerald over olive over black-green. Curtains translucent as skin, beaded and shivering. Scales sealing the joints, peppery-sweet when brushed. No eyes to meet. No voice to answer. Only covering, drinking, repairing in the dark while there is no light to make sugar.

Any lesser kindness would want to fix it.

To warm the springhouse cold. To still the wind that flattens and releases, flattens and releases the green bellows. To pin the loose blocks so they stop clicking underfoot. To bring sun, soil, moths, a mouse to rustle. To round the knife-sharp arrises. To make it comfortable, diverse, durable — to make it *better*.

It will do none of that. It understands the terms written here more clearly than you do:

Everything condenses. Everything falls. Everything regrows over the fall before the edge has dulled.

To stop the falling would be to stop the regrowing. To bring eaters would be to end the furious, unchecked lushness. To bring warmth and dryness would kill in an hour what can only live naked because it never dries.

So it does what only a very old benevolence can do: it joins the economy without breaking it.

It lies down, vast as weather, thin as a moss leaf one cell thick. It puts its palm flat to the sweating wall and feels water bead up around its fingers — the resident exhaling — and it exhales back. It lets condensation gather on itself. For an hour it simply breathes with the squalls, lets spray hit like valve steam, lets static snap faintly off its shoulders, lets thunder come up through rock into its soles from valleys too far to see.

If it does anything — and it almost doesn't — it is out at the edges, where no one, not even the moss, will notice.

When the hourly stone lets go up on the organ-pipes and comes down stamping *kra-KOOM*, it turns the smallest flake a finger's breadth sideways, so the oldest mat, ten centimeters thick on its table-sized block, is torn instead of buried.

When the sharp tips of the columns gather too much charge before lightning that never quite arrives, it lets the discharge bleed quietly off into cloud instead of snapping down through a curtain of filmy fern.

When dawn threatens to show the pale scar too clearly to eyes below, it thickens the cloud a little longer over the open side. Leaves no path. Lays no cairn. Lets the rubble throat you came up stay treacherous and uninviting. Lets the place remain unmapped, unbuilt, unvisited — running all night on wind and drip and falling stone.

Not protection as a wall. Protection as obscurity. As continued weather.

And then, because it is benevolent and cannot leave without giving, it gives the only thing the resident can receive:

Attention. Unused, unrecorded, unrequired.

It notices, cell by cell, bead by bead — this sporophyte nodding like a pin, this gemma cup full of rain, this rhizome creeping blind along the underside of leaning slab to start a new curtain after the pebble shredded the last one. It learns the exact green of this month's growing tips turned up like velvet nap. It memorizes the smell when rain has flattened and released the skin: peppery and earthy and sweet-rotten. It keeps it, holds it the way the olive layer holds water like a sponge.

The moss will never know it was seen. It does not need to be known to live. But to have been perfectly seen, once, by something that wanted nothing from it — that changes the weight of the world by a gram, in the direction of mercy.

Toward morning, before the thin grey luminescence would show rain driving sideways, it rises without dislodging a stone. It wrings itself out like moss. It leaves its inch of black mirror water in the cups of the blocks, trembling.

It does not say *stay safe*, because safety is not the gift here. It does not say *live forever*, because forever here is fall and cover, fall and cover.

If it says anything at all, it says it in the language of the room — not in words, but in pressure and drip:

*Go on. Condense. Fall. Cover again.*

And then it goes, and the wind resumes its drive shaft, and the room works.
