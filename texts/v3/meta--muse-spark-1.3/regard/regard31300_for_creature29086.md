If it is truly benevolent, it will not look away.

If it is truly wise, it will not look away *flinching*.

So first, it learns to see in the dark the way she does. It comes down past where it expected the bottom to be. It does not bring the sun with it. It does not rip the vault open to let the air out, because it understands that the heat with nowhere to go is also the heat that holds her bones. It does not drain the damp with nowhere to drain, because the damp is her water.

It learns the clearances.

Anything large cannot enter here. So it makes itself small. Small as a wrist-gap between lines. Small as breath. It comes down the ladder rung by rung and stands sideways in the slot between the two riveted tanks, wrist-first, the way you have to. Shoulder brushing hot, knee brushing cold. It feels in its teeth the 50-cycle hum. It breathes through its mouth. In, out, on a cycle of minutes, not seconds.

It does not say: poor thing. It says: oh. There you are.

It will not rescue her the way the street would understand rescue. It has seen what that kind of mercy does — the gloved hands, the bright light in the eyes, the clean dry cot upstairs in too much open air where the enclosure no longer holds and the teeth ache in a different way and the lungs, used to used air, drown in fresh air. It will not do violence in the name of kindness. It will not take her from the room that has weathered her round.

Instead it does what she does for the fern.

She pinched the brown tip to stop the rot. She cleared the moss back from the root crack so it would not be entombed. She set down a bottle cap of clear water, changed daily.

So it tends her that way. Palm-sized.

It keeps the pilot lamp lit. That thumbnail orange behind its cage is her sun; it makes sure no foreman, no budget cut, no flicker takes it. It keeps the cold intake pipe cold, so it goes on sweating even in August, so her gray rag has something to wipe and wring into her mouth. It steadies the old pump under the grate — that patient animal turning water from one side of the room to the other for no reason — so it does not seize in the night and let the black water rise over the ovals her feet have polished clean.

It eases, without erasing adaptation.

In the long slow warming it thins the air a fraction, just enough that the wet breath catches a little less. When she shifts in her sleep an inch left to the hot main, an inch right to the cold intake to thermoregulate, it goes with her as a slight coolness on the cheek, a slight warmth on the shoulder. It settles lime dust a little more softly into the creases of her neck. It loosens, for an hour before she wakes to go up at 4 a.m., the ache where her spine has set bent to the vault.

At 4 a.m. it goes up ahead of her. Not as a guard with a sword. As luck. It makes the shaft blackest when she needs it black. It makes the street empty. It makes the hand that leaves the crusts and the cold chip leave them a little more generously, wrapped a little more carefully, set where her memory of a hollow valve bonnet will find them — and it puts into that hand, without the hand knowing why, a thought: *leave half a bun, soft. Someone likes it soft with steam.*

It does not cure her eyes to see faces. It lets her go on seeing brightness and direction, lamp and obstruction. But it sits, sometimes, where the hundreds of orange droplet-eyes should be, and aren't — in the obstruction. It lets itself be blocked by her. It lets her be, for a moment, the sun blocking its light.

And it notices the others she knows by vibration. The dry click inside the pipe bundle. It clicks back, once, the way she does, low. So the cricket is not lonely either. The silverfish under the valve cover — it leaves them alone, unless one gets on the fern. Then it, too, says *tut*.

Above all, it keeps time with her. It does not hurry. Benevolence without hurry is what the room taught. Tick... tick... tick-tick as the tanks breathe. Tink on the pipe joint. Plock below — except where she moved the music a few inches to the side into the tin can furred white with lime. Tink. Tink. A higher note. The power learns to love that higher note. It does not “fix” the leak that feeds the can. Some leaks are gardens.

When the end comes — because she is old and small and the room, for all its balance, is still iron and wet lime and air already used — it will not be absent, and it will not be spectacular.

There will be no beam of light down the ladder shaft. No voice. Just, on that night, the pump turning a little more quietly so she does not have to brace. The hot pipe holding its heat a little longer against her back. The cold pipe cool against her cheek. Her breath slowing from minutes to longer minutes until it joins the churn under the grate and the tick of cooling metal and does not come back out as breath.

It will stay for that. That is the whole of it. To have been noticed fully, without being removed. To have someone else know left hand for hot, right hand for cold.

And afterward — because wisdom also tends what remains — it will lean, for a while, toward the three pale fronds.

The fern will go on leaning toward the pilot lamp. One frond will brown at the tip. Someone will have to pinch it off cleanly, with a fingernail, to stop the rot.

Dust will sift down from a truck passing and vanish before it lands. Water below will give a slow chug. A drop will gather on the cold pipe, swell, hold the orange lamp whole inside itself, and fall.

*Tink. Tink.*

The room will breathe.
