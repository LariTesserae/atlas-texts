It found them by accident, the way you find a held breath.

The Wise and Kind — call it what you like, it has had many names and is embarrassed by all of them — spends its attention the way water spends itself here: only where it belongs, and only so much. Mostly it goes where the signal is loud. Where the river floods. Where the children cry with hunger that is not chosen. Where the rock has been broken open for something underneath and cannot close again.

Then, in its wide listening, there was a patch of quiet so complete it stood out.

A mile of high shelf between two black ridges where nothing was asking for more. No prayer for rain. No prayer for increase. No grinding of want against limit. Just a thin thread of sound — drip from shingle into barrel, hoe tapping stone, wool brushing on wood — repeated and repeated without getting louder or fainter.

So it came to look. Not in glory. Glory would have been a violence here. It came the way weather comes: as presence.

It stood first on the upper rocks, where the pale goats were, and did what any visitor must do. It breathed thinly. It let its heart hammer until it learned, as all hearts must up there, not to hurry.

It saw the town below. Terraces, causeways, sod roofs, green cut like a knife at stone. It saw the cisterns exactly full and no fuller. It saw the lichen handspan-wide. It saw the sharp basalt that had not been worn round.

And it understood, very quickly, that this was not a place that had been forgotten by change. It was a place that had refused change after long negotiation, and was keeping its terms.

That stopped it cold. Even powers are used to being needed.

It came down anyway, on soft feet. It borrowed — not a disguise, exactly, but a scale. A figure in undyed wool. Hood up though it was midday. Broad chest to hold the thin air. It walked the causeway at the pace the causeway requires, so the boy leading the pale goat by a twisted rope did not look up startled. So the dog curled chin-on-paws against the warm south wall did not lift its head to bark. So the six swallows under the eave went on with their mud and straw.

It wanted, with a sudden helpless fondness that was almost unwise, to *give* them something. That is the habit of benevolence. To heal. To ease. To add.

It walked past the gardens and saw what adding would do.

If it called more water from the rock, the seep under the north ridge that fills three linked cisterns and no more would overfill, and the moss would take courage from damp and creep past its stone edge, and the barley would lodge and rot in its own lushness, and the sheep, patient and exactly numerous, would lamb in doubles and crop the silver-backed herbs to the root, and the beetles under the moss would starve for shade, and the rills would cut deeper than their keeping. More would be a debt. The shelf is too high and too thin to carry debt.

If it took away pain, took away the rare death — the old weathering smooth into older until one morning a basket does not come down the causeway — then the boy would not take up the wall-mending, and the basket would not pass to other hands, and continuance itself would loosen. Their grief is quiet because it is not protest. It is thinning to stasis. Eat, and let regrow to the same.

If it gave them straight sawn lines and forge-smoke and iron that holds an edge longer than copper, the houses would go up faster, straighter, closer together. The emptiness between house and house, garden and swept yard and bare rock left alone on purpose, would fill. The plazas built larger than foot traffic requires would fill with foot traffic. A loud sound would finally have somewhere to go, and they would start making them.

It stood by the second terrace and looked at the puddle in the basalt pan, its edge always on that same dark line, and it felt the full weight of its own temptation and set it down like a stone that did not fit, and left it for the grass beyond the low unfinished wall.

Then it did the wiser, harder thing. The thing that is almost nothing.

It stayed with them, for a while, on their terms.

It sat in the longhouse with twenty mending nets and ten shelling beans while children slept in wool piles, and it mended, badly at first with its too-large hands, then better, when an old woman showed it — not with words but with a tap — how to keep the tension. It did not mend a new pattern. It kept the old one.

It knelt, when no one asked it to, at the edge where green meets stone, and with its fingertips pinched back what would creep. Moss deep as a fist, emerald and rust. It learned the manners of it: here, damp. There, never. It left the velvet patch exactly velvet and exactly there.

It walked, when something needed saying at distance, over to say it, low and close. It did not shout across the plaza. It learned to listen until the finches' thin thread of sound became audible, until its own footsteps were taken up by moss and gravel.

One evening it held the rope while the boy led the goat to drink — to *that* hollow, not the other — and walked so the goat would walk with it. It slept that night against thin cold that preserves, smelling wet stone and lanolin and the single distant hearth, and its breath showed faintly when it lay still.

In the morning it rose for dew. It stood under an eave and watched a barrel set precisely to catch what the air wrings out, bead by bead from shingle to wood. Drip. Drip. Drip. Always the same amount. Always enough pieces of the loaf broken without counting, because there are always enough pieces.

It understood then what their god is. Not Staying as stuck. Staying as *staying with.*

So before it left, it did only three things, and all of them invisible except to itself.

First, it remembered them perfectly. Every yard of pale gravel swept, every copper pin green at the edge, every swallow nest, every marmot fat on the terrace edge sitting up to watch. It folded that remembering into itself the way they fold fleeces over a wall — carefully, to keep dry. So that if the world below ever forgets how to hold land loosely, the memory will still exist somewhere, intact, handspan-wide.

Second, it set a slight turning aside around the last steep climb where breath goes thin. Not a storm. Not a curse. Just a quiet in the minds of those below who love maps and mines and missions — a sense, when they look up at the black ridges, that there is nothing up there worth the climb. Nothing to convert. Nothing to extract. Nothing to improve. Let their feet turn elsewhere. This is not hiding. It is the same courtesy the Keepers show the bare rock: leave it alone on purpose.

Third — and this was the most benevolent, and the most intimate — it allowed itself, for one full day and night, to be kept by them, rather than to keep them. To be the dog watched without chasing. To be the goat slept against the warm wall because the wall holds the day's heat longest. To let its own vast, restless urge to make and mend and multiply be thinned, gently, to stasis. Eat, and let regrow to the same.

When it left, before noon when the open rock dries pale and dusty, it swept the length of causeway it had walked, and let dust settle to the same. It closed a shutter against night wind that someone else would open to morning light in exactly the same gesture. It did not say goodbye loudly. There is no need to be heard across a plaza.

The barrel is still under the same eave, catching the same drip. The dog is still asleep against the same warm wall. The moss is still exactly as lush and exactly as contained.

And somewhere below, descending into thicker air where the world is still becoming something else, a traveler pauses on the trail and feels, inexplicably, that it has just been — not blessed, not healed, not saved — but kept, loosely, the way lichen holds stone. Present everywhere, dense nowhere. Entirely decided, for a moment, to remain itself.
