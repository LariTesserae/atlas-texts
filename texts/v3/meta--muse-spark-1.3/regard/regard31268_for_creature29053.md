It comes at midday, when the light is ordinary.

Not because it has to come then. It can come whenever it likes. It comes at midday because that is when the Table is hardest to love — white-yellow glare, air too thin to carry sound, dust in the teeth, eight degrees and falling through your boots — and it wants to see it without makeup.

It does not come down in fire. There is no arrival. Only, after a while, the pavement is not quite empty. There is a thickness in the thin air, a place where the dust devils lean slightly as they pass, the way grass leans toward water. If you were a stitcher you would smell salt and iron and something else, like rain remembered from a billion years ago, and you would stop mid-hop and listen. If you were a sweeper you would feel a new updraft where there should not be one — wide, slow, patient — and you would circle it without knowing why.

It is short of breath at first. Even it. It honors the height by breathing short. It sits, though there is nowhere to sit but interlocking stone that clicks faintly, and lets its shadow catch up.

For a long time it only looks.

It has seen finished worlds before. It knows what people want to do to them. Level them, water them, mine them, mourn them, fix them.

This one is different. The ground here has finished becoming itself. Pink ash, white caliche, black varnish, over and over like pages. Nothing sharp remains. It understands: you do not improve a final patience.

And the sky has not finished. The devils are born out of nothing and die in minutes, forty, sixty at a time, white against bruised violet, tearing and rejoining in total silence. The needles make them. The tall dark things that are not quite in focus, that cast two shadows and orbit small stones at ankle height. Shepherds of wind. At noon, starlight in them, as if the glass is thin somewhere else.

It listens to them tick at night — that single soft glass tick you feel in your teeth — and it understands the second rule too. The work is not done. It must not be finished for them.

So it does not bring water.

That is the first mercy, and the hardest. Any lesser love would have wept here. Would have opened a seep, laid a creek, let rain fall on fabric for the first time in eons. And that weeping would have been murder.

Water would rot what has learned to preserve. It would melt the rime before the wire-grass could drink its lifting. It would dissolve the hexagonal salt curls the stitchers rasp with their long dry tongues. It would rust the silica armor off the rock-mites and drown the spore-rain that falls grain by grain after devils collide. Green would be a rumor no longer, and the rumor would be a weed that ate the library.

It does not thicken the air, though it could. Thicker air would carry sound, would let the sweepers flap — but their flight muscles are gone to ribbon for a reason. They are obligate soarers, born to ride this exact boil. Give them easy air and you ground them. Give them sound and you warn the mites a half-second early, and the whole hunting delay — that holy lateness where light gets tired and a shadow crosses a half-second late and a mite freezes too late, lit — collapses. The stitchers who learned to freeze early would starve or be seen. You do not feed one mouth by breaking the clock they all eat by.

So it does almost nothing.

Almost.

It walks. Or rather, it attends, place to place, the way your eye attends without touching.

By a north-facing stone it pauses where a black rosette the size of a palm is mostly dead ancestors holding a living rim. A millimeter a century, then a thousand-year wait for dust. A devil died too far west yesterday; the spore-rain missed it. The power does not make rain. It only leans, and as the next white rope wobbles by, it breathes, very slightly, sideways — just enough shear to coax the devil a meter east. The veil settles. Grain by grain, in total silence, spores land on black paper. No miracle. Only a leaning. The lichen will not know it was helped. That is correct.

In a crack where dust has lodged, wire-grass trembles, glass-clear and straw-yellow, stiff with rime that will vanish without wetting. It lives on static as much as sun, which is why it dares cluster near the needle-fields, never within arm's reach. One clump has lodged too far from the shiver; its pores will open for minutes at dawn and miss the lift. The power kneels — though kneeling is a human word — and lets the hairs on its own vast arm stand up in the needle-field, lets its own watch, if it wore one, stop then resume three seconds fast. It does not move the plant. It picks up a pinch of bone-and-rust dust between two fingers that are wind, and sets it down an inch closer, where the static hums. Tremble, it thinks, not wave. Stay.

At night it follows a stitcher.

She is old. Fur the white-grey of caliche, hollow-boned, wide-footed, amber eyes huge to gather starlight. Step step step, a dotted seam across the dust. She licks a dinner-plate salt pan, rasp rasp, for magnesium, for the ghost of frost trapped in crystal, and snaps a mite too quick for thin air to carry. Above, a sweeper circles without sound, riding ahead of its own darkness.

The power could warn her. Could still the air. Could blind the bird.

It does not. The sweeper has not landed since morning, launched off a ghost-swell that was once Himalayas, and will fold and fold its impossible wings into a crevice at dusk and breathe once a minute with frost forming on it. It is hungry too. Benevolence that chooses one hunger over another is not benevolence. It is farming.

So the power walks beside the stitcher, pogoing without touching, keeping pace. When she freezes early — because she has learned the opposite lesson, learned to freeze before the late shadow comes — it freezes with her. When her heart hammers too thin in the cold, it steadies, just a fraction, the way you would put a hand near, not on, a frightened animal. Warm enough. Eight degrees is warm enough.

Later, when she wedges herself into a crack to go torpid and grey as stone by day, it stays a moment longer. It remembers her seam. It does not erase it. Morning wind will almost erase it, and that is enough.

And for the sweepers: at dusk, when they drop out of the boil, one young one misjudges a shear near a needle — birds will not fly over them, and for good reason — and tears the thin blade of its wing. It flutters down gracelessly, which is death here. No flap can save it. The power is there before the dust settles. It does not heal. Healing would be a new physics. It only makes a wide, slow updraft under the falling bird, a private thermal, holding it a half-second longer, carrying it past the arm's-reach where lichen will not grow and stones orbit clicking, to a soft-shouldered boulder with a north-facing overhang. The bird lands hard, folds. Breathes. Frost will form on it tonight and lift off it in the morning without wetting it. Whether it flies tomorrow is not decided. The power tucks the wind around it like a blanket and leaves it unknown.

For the needles themselves it does the strangest thing: nothing, and reverence.

It does not census them, because they do not fully stay. Sometimes one is gone when you look back, leaving only a clean circle of swept stone and a pillar of dust standing confused where it was. An hour later it is back. Or another is there. The power does not ask where they go. It sits within sight but not too close — never within arm's reach — and listens to them tick, unsynchronized across the vast dark, like cooling stones. And once, very softly, in teeth rather than ears, it ticks back.

Not to fix. To answer. To let the long inexplicable work know it has been heard.

Before dawn, before the rime lifts straight back into the sky, it does its last and longest work, which is also almost nothing: it hides the place.

Not with walls. With uninterest. It lays a gentle forgetting over the minds that would come with drills and lenses to pull up the smoked glass and pave the twenty-meter road that starts nowhere. Let satellites see only pavement. Let maps call it empty. Let the only road remain twenty meters long, fused at the curbs to glass, leading nowhere worth going. Almost nothing lives here. Let them keep believing the first honest count.

Then it goes. There is no departure. Only, after a while, the devils stop leaning.

If you asked — and the wire-grass, in its trembling way, does ask — what did it do?

Tell them: it learned to breathe short. It let its shadow be late. It moved dust a meter, and dust an inch, and air a half-second. It waited with the waiters. It refused water, refused rescue, refused to finish the sky.

It paid attention so complete it became a kind of shelter, and then it left the shelter standing by leaving.

That is what the wise and benevolent do here, if they love.

They do not save the Table. The Table is already saved by being finished on the ground and unfinished in the sky.

They keep it that way, and remember the stitcher's seam, and the sweeper's late shadow, and the tick in the teeth at night — and having remembered, they let it continue to be almost unseen, almost silent, entirely itself.
