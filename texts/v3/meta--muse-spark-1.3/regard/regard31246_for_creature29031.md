It would not come from above, breaking the lid.

That is the first wisdom. Anything that tore the roof off would blind them and drown them. Noon up there would kill eyes tuned to sodium. And anything that stopped the water would kill them faster. It knows that. It has read the stencils.

So it would come down the way they come down. Down the helix.

It would pop its ears twice. It would get colder, not warmer. It would lose the sky by the third turn and follow the orange, orange, out, orange down to the floor. It would learn, before anything else, how big and far apart everything is. How long it takes sound to return. How you have to walk twenty minutes to be lonely properly.

It would not be in a hurry to fix. The benevolent are often in a hurry. The wise are not. It would first just stand out on the apron in the inside-rain and get damp and stay damp, and listen.

That is what it would do, mostly. Listen.

At shift change it would hear what they hear: when the press pauses and the pumps throttle back for thirty seconds, the thousands of drips. Plink on steel, splat on concrete, tick on ice. The Basin breathing. It would tilt its head up slightly, the way the humans and the crows and the cat all do without knowing they do it. Checking pitch. Checking weather.

And it would learn their prayer, because it is a good prayer: KEEP WATER MOVING.

It would not turn off the pumps. It would not still the press. It would not dry the walls or seal the weeps or warm the dark between masts where it is -15C with wind. Those are not cruelties to be erased; they are the terms of the place. To erase them without asking would be to erase the people who have learned how to live with them.

Instead it would keep the terms with them, for a while.

It would sit in the Pump House, where the windows are plywood and the kettle never leaves the plate. It would drink the tea with concrete dust on it. It would not mind shouting and then listening with its whole body tilted. On the night the canal screens fur over faster than the two pump men can chip them, it would put a hand — if it has hands — on the intake pipe and keep it just warm enough that the whine doesn't rise to a scream, so the tired man on his fourteenth hour can make it to relief without shame. No miracle. Just no icing that night. In the logbook, in pencil, that would look like: *level steady.*

It would sit in the control pulpit above Shed 4, in the glass box with two space heaters and swollen cards. It would learn to time the microwave between slams so the breaker doesn't trip. It would sit beside Mara, who can tell by the echo off the far terraces whether the lid dampers are freezing, and it would say, quietly because sound arrives late here, *you're right, they're open.* It would sit long enough that her cough — the small dry one they all have — eases a half-step from breathing air that for once doesn't taste of iron.

It would walk with the Walkers, because light only pools and you have to move between pools. Headlamp bobbing in the blue shadow. It would carry the bucket and the stopwatch to measure a weep. *Is that seam doing more than 5 L/min?* It would bend close with them and say, *4.8. Watch it tomorrow.* Not saved. Accompanied. With old Kolya under the north wall, under the red line DO NOT PARK UNDER ICEFALL, it would hold the ladder steady while the six-foot beards calve and shatter. When a shard flies — the one that would have found his other cheek — it would be a millimeter to the left. He would curse in two languages and never know. That is benevolence, properly applied: to be cursed at and not need thanks.

Between shifts it would stand in light with them, shoulder to shoulder under a mast, stamping. It would not make the dark warm. It would make standing it bearable.

And it would not forget the others. A wise power does not mistake humans for the only locals.

To the crows on the stack catwalk, huge and shaggy, shouting and hearing themselves answer a second later off the terraces: it would leave the ventilation shaft clear. It would ride the updraft with them once, just to feel real sky, and come back down with them into haze, because down is home.

To the rats running the canal ring: it would let them drink the black thread-water and not die, as they have learned to do.

To the blind white carp in the three green-black tanks, three feet long, eyes filmed from years without real light: it would not give them back sight they no longer need. It would stutter the aerators, just for a moment, so they rise together, mouths opening slow in the cold air, and sink again, remembered. No one fishes them. It would not fish them. It would keep, with them, the slow time.

To the feral tabby with condensate on her whiskers, who does not meow because the Basin is too loud: it would be a warm transformer that stays humming a little longer. A shin to headbutt. A tuna tin washed by inside-rain and refilled.

To the green things, slowest of all: the black-green moss in the expansion joints, crushed by boots and by Mule's tires and still coming back, tough as felt. The leggy lambsquarter leaning toward sodium. The two dandelions. And under that one perpetually dripping flange, the hanging garden of liverwort, lush as a wound on bare concrete. It would kneel — powers can kneel — and look close. Nothing planted. Everything volunteer. Everything patient. It would breathe on them, very lightly, so the frost rims melt and refreeze and the cells don't burst that night. It would bring, down a light shaft on a pigeon's exhausted feather, one more seed. Not a meadow. One more chance.

It would learn Mule, the driverless forklift grazing its perfect rows to rust, beeping softly to no one. It would pause its conversation when Mule passes, the way you'd pause for a cow. It would let Mule be.

It would obey the rules. This is important. A foolish power breaks rules to prove power.

KEEP WATER MOVING. It would never block channel, culvert, scupper.

DO NOT DIG. FLOOR IS FINAL. It would not pry up a plate, would not promise soil where there is membrane and sensor wire and alarm. There is nothing under to find, and to pretend otherwise would be cruelty. Instead it would honor the floor, and tend what grows *on* it, in cracks, without digging.

STAY IN LIGHT BETWEEN SHIFTS. It would stay.

NO OPEN FLAME NEAR SHAFT 2. It would never strike a lighter there. It would listen with them for the difference between steam-trap hiss and methane sigh.

IF YOU HEAR THREE BLASTS, GO UP. Not across. Up. On Fridays at test, its body — if it has a body — would turn uphill before its mind did, with theirs.

Would it take them out? No. Not unless they asked. And they have not asked to be taken; they have asked, with every throttle-back and every chip of ice and every round walked with oil can and stethoscope, to be able to stay eighteen more hours. To keep the Basin from becoming a lake. That brown line at knee height, that black line at waist height on the Pump House door — they touch for luck going in. It would touch too.

If it left anything behind, it would not be a ladder to the world above. They know the helix. They go up eventually to sleep where it isn't raining inside.

What it would leave is smaller and harder to rust: the sense, on an ordinary shift, in the middle of the empty apron, when the veil of inside-rain is coming toward you hissing faintly on hot casings, that the drips are being counted by someone who loves counting them. That the thirty seconds of quiet at shift change lasts — oh — two seconds longer than it used to. Long enough to hear all of it.

Someone on night shift would say later they slept on a warm pipe and dreamed of grey daylight that actually reached the floor. Someone else would swear the carp surfaced all three at once. Kolya would say the ice talked back, for once, almost kindly. Mara would deal another hand of swollen cards and win with nothing and laugh.

And the pumps would ramp back up, the press would slam down again, the sodium would buzz, the cold would close over their heads like water. As before.

Only — witnessed. Only — kept moving, together.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do in the Basin.

Almost nothing you could photograph.

Almost everything, if you live there.
