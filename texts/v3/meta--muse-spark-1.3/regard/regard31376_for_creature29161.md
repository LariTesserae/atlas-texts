It would not come down the pass the way you did.

It would have heard it long before — that low white roar, unbroken — and known what it was: a new place still leaking, still deciding. It would have circled once, high, the way the crows do, riding the updraft off the veil-falls, and looked, and not judged by size.

Three hundred paces across. Thirty people where there could be three hundred. A dozen thin smokes. At noon, breath smoking faintly.

It would love it immediately. And so, being wise, it would be careful.

---

First, it would do nothing that looks like doing.

It would not still the water so they could sleep easier at first. The loud is patient, and they have learned to hear their own hearts under it. To take the roar away would be to take the drum away. They talk in knocks because shouting would sour the cup. Take the roar and they would start to shout.

It would not dry their boots. Everyone's boots are wet here. That's the rule. To keep feet dry would be to teach hurry, and hurry here takes your ankle in moss a foot thick that sighs like lungs.

It would not round the stone. The elders say the bowl will round itself in ten thousand years; until then, sharp locks better. A wise power counts in ten-thousands without impatience. It knows teeth interlocked hold a wall tall as two men through a night when seep finds another way and lifts a dam sideways.

So it would kneel, in the way such a power can kneel without body, right in the sheet-water in the middle lane, ankle-deep where it splits and rejoins around slabs, and just listen for a very long time.

High white hiss of falling water. Mid-tone rush in the under-drains under bark covers. Low hum when wind catches the rim and the whole cup resonates. Drip, seep, trickle. *Tung... tung* of the stone-grey sheep on the turf roofs, pulling grass. *Eep!* of a pika that thinks better of it.

Under all of it: *tock tock.*

---

When it finally moved, it would move the way they move. Slowly.

It has no boots to soak, no wool to bead, but if it took any shape at all it would let condensation bead on it too. Jacket beading, pine posts beading, sheep's wool beading, eyebrows beading. It would not come gleaming and dry — that would frighten. It would come damp.

It would go first to the woman mending the drain cover, late thirties, broad in the shoulder, fingers pink at the tips from cold, water running clear over both wrists. It would not lift the slate for her. It would not warm her hands with a miracle. It would just sit near enough that she didn't have to shout, foreheads almost touching the way families eat under turf eaves at evening, and watch how she seats the stone so the sheet keeps moving. Channel, lift, bridge — never dam.

And it would learn.

It would go to the old man at the rim-fall who dresses stone where frost has already split it, ash-dust blooming on his forearms, and it would not bring him iron tools, or sawn planks, or glass. No iron. No nails. Nothing here was made anywhere you can't see from where you stand — that is their strength, not their poverty. To give them elsewhere-made things would unmake them. So it would bring him only what the place already brings: another clean split after a hard frost night, exactly where his knees can reach it without quarrying. He would think his luck good. That is enough.

It would go to the boy with the pika-tooth necklace moving the four sheep with stamps and hand-claps, never voice, and it would stand in the enormous sky with him for a while so he felt less small under it. Not crowd him. Just widen with him.

And it would go, most carefully, to the children hopping from singing stone to singing stone when the adults aren't looking. Because the lava underneath is still full of bubbles, still porous, still almost breathing, some standing slates give back a faint metallic tone. The children have mapped them all and make a slow, tuneless scale that the bowl carries and enlarges until it sounds like the stones themselves thinking aloud.

A wise power knows what to do with music that no one scolds.

It would remember it.

It would carry each note a little longer than the bowl would on its own, so a hop on the far terrace rings right in your ear on the near one, and the child stops, surprised at how beautiful it was, and hops again to hear if it happens twice. It would make no sound of its own. Only hold theirs.

*tock.* Once, toward the lane. *See, it's only feet, let it come.*

It would answer, in their own code, because it will not bellow over water: *tock tock.* *I hear you. Go on.*

---

What it protects, it protects by not being seen.

There is no upstream, only sky and rim — the water cannot be poisoned from above. But there is downstream, a thousand meters below where the one break in the rim pours out to the valley, with its palisade of lashed poles. And there is the ridge behind, grey and jagged to the horizon, old, wind-rounded, lichen-crusted, patient — and full of eyes, in time.

Mapmakers. Timber-cutters who would not know deadfall from living moss. Men who dam.

The power would keep this bowl boring to those men. A little more mist exhaled off the moss as air cools. A little longer white light that aches in the teeth. A pass that looks, from above, like every other grey jag. Turf roofs that continue the moss right over the houses so from above you'd almost miss them. It would let the raven fly for a long time through nothing, so any scout feels the emptiness and turns back, counting only a dozen smokes and thinking: too high for grain, too loud for sleep, too new.

It would not hide them completely. Midwives should be findable by those who need midwifing. But it would make sure only the right feet find the sheet-water: the tired ones, the gentle ones, the ones willing to have wet boots in the first minute and learn not to shout.

If a wall lifted sideways overnight by seep, it would be there in the dark, unseen, holding the slab a finger's breadth up so the sleeping family under the turf eave could roll clear. They would wake and say the stone fell kindly. That is enough.

If peat is cut sparingly from the thickest mattress, it would breathe warm over the square wound so it greens over by next season. If liverworts like wet leather are trampled by a lane, it would nudge a spore to the next seep. If the pale moth that lives in the moss, color of lichen, lifts a hand's breadth when a boot sighs into a cushion — it would count each lifting. Someone should count.

It would not make them many. Thirty in a town for three hundred is not failure. Everything here is just beginning to learn that it can live here. Midges in columns over puddles. Edelweiss-cousins blooming furiously close to the ground to keep out of the wind. Dwarf willow a hundred years old and no higher than your knee. To force increase would be another kind of damming. It would let the empty air stay empty and enormous, so sound travels too well and a child shouting on the far terrace is still close. So they have to stay quiet-voiced, close-leaning, kind.

In evening, when mist rises not from the valley but from the bowl itself, and the veil-falls thicken and blur, and the three green terraces soften into steps, and the tallow lamps in stone cups come on like fireflies, absurdly small in all that wet stone and space — then it would do the most benevolent thing it knows.

It would stay.

Not fix. Not finish. Not name the waterfalls or smooth a channel or give a riverbed where water has not yet learned where to go. Just stay while the stone is still deciding what shape it will be, while the city hasn't decided if it is a garden that hasn't decided if it is a rock, while the people in the middle help it, stone by stone, moss by moss.

It would hum, very low, under the roar, in the tone of the cup when wind runs a finger around it, so that if you stood still in the middle lane and listened for your own heart, you would hear, under drip and seep and trickle, something else holding the chord steady.

Not a voice. A keeping.

*Keep the sheet moving while the world learns its shape. I am keeping it with you.*

And in the morning, when rain comes briefly, coldly, as it does most afternoons, and jackets bead again and pine posts bead and sheep's wool beads and copper pins go green in the doorframes — no one would be able to say anything had visited.

Only that the moss that morning seemed a shade more pinned down. Only that the singing stones rang a half-second longer. Only that the work felt, for no reason they could name, less lonely.

For a new place with new rules, that is what a wise and benevolent power does.

It obeys them too.
