It comes the way you came.

Up out of the low clouds, and it is already around it. It does not announce itself. A benevolent and wise power would know better than to arrive with thunder in a place where sound carries and the night shift is sleeping behind the loom wall.

So it comes quiet. It learns to trot.

At first it just stands on the high inspection bridge and watches and listens, the way you did. Its coat darkens. Its palm comes away cold and slick from the handrail. It feels the faint vibration of No. 2 turbine through its boots. It does not mind being wet. It understands very quickly that wet here is not a problem to be solved. Wet is the medium. Wet is what everything else swims in.

It sees what you saw. A valley that is not built on but built *instead*. A town-factory-bog-garden seething terrace after terrace into white. And in it, a being — not the men with baskets, not the woman with the wrench and the grapes in her pocket, not the magenta-lit mushroom family, not the boy with the hooked pole, but all of them together, and the moss with them, and the ferns and the eels and the pigeons under the conveyor housing. A tissue, inch-thick and velvet, holding the concrete together. Pale, short-stepped, green-black at the cuticles. Hearing information where you heard muffle.

It loves them immediately. That is the benevolent part. It does not need to decide to.

The wise part is what happens next. Which is, almost entirely, restraint.

It does not bring the sun.

It could. It could lift the cap off the upper end and the lower end and let lowland noon pour in, dry the sponge, warm the rheumy lungs, make the children pinker. It knows what that would do. The moss would crisp in a day. The ferns in the mortar would cook. The pale eyes good in gloom would squint and water and hurt. The condensation that waters the herbs in tin cans would stop. The turbines would stutter as the header tanks dropped. The red geraniums — kept red at such cost — would bleach. It will not unmake a people to heal them on lowland terms. They are not healthy in the lowland sense. They were never meant to be.

It does not bring dry ground.

It could lay soil down, real soil,flat and sunlit where water just sits. It knows they dream of it sometimes as a rumor, and dread it. Flat ground would be lonely. There would be nowhere for water to go, no up-terrace and down-terrace to give a toddler her geography, no roof that is someone else's floor to teach them to tread carefully. So it leaves the law intact: *Down is someone else's roof; treat it like a floor.* It honors that law. When it walks, it steps only where the moss is an inch thick and springy, where the roof is tended. It does not step where the moss swallows the boot. It scrapes its own length of gutter.

It does not stop the rain, or silence the drip, or still the machines. That is the rule of the place: nothing is still. It will not impose stillness.

What does it do, then, if it does anything at all?

Small things. The size of a handrail. The size of a tarp pulled just in time.

It sits with the old woman shelling beans where the joists bear near the parapet, and without a word eases the ache in her knees for an afternoon, just enough that she can climb back up to her cot without having to ask for help. It does not give her new knees. New knees belong to a different story. It gives her this climb.

It stands behind the loom girl on nights and lays a lightness over her curtain so the thud does not get through, so she sleeps a full two hours without coughing. It does not take the cough away forever — the air is thin and cool in the back of the throat, the wool smells of lanolin and smoke — it just gives the lungs a night of rest, and in the morning the sputum comes up easier.

It goes down to the third under-terrace, where the mushroom family blinks white-eyed in magenta, and it breathes on the straw bales tucked under the stairs. A heavier fruiting, pale shelves doubling overnight. No miracle anyone can point to. Just a good flush. They will think they got the moisture right.

It touches the south-facing mill walls, the ten-foot vertical allotments fought over and inherited and marked with paint. It coaxes the kale a little sweeter from the cold, the potatoes a little further earthed in ash and leaf mold. In every window box it lets one geranium be impossibly red — redder than dimness should allow — not for food but for something to be red. Someone will notice and not say anything and tend it extra carefully.

It goes with the eel trappers, boys and grandfathers in patched waders, hands quick in black water, and it makes sure there are still eels knotting silver-black in the bucket. It keeps the clean flumes clean enough for watercress. It keeps the membranes under the tended roofs holding for one more season.

Mostly, it keeps the water moving *with* them.

At 3 p.m. when the market alley packs up in seven minutes because the drain beneath must be rodded, it is one of the hands lifting a trestle, rolling a stall on wheels. No one sees its face under the plastic hood. At dawn and dusk it clears with its own hooked pole — lady-fern fronds, potato tops, silt black as ink. When the sluice is wound open and the side channel turns from trickle to brown rush, it is the extra steadiness on the boy's pole so the grille does not blind. When the cable bucket sticks, it is the whistle through teeth, barely louder than a drip, that calls it to stop without waking anyone.

It learns their short language and uses it. A hand up means hold. Two fingers flicked means rain coming through again. A tap twice on the basket rim. It does not shout. It does not preach. It works quiet.

And at noon, under tarps, it stands in the soup queue. Barley, nettle, eel. Breath showing. Spoons clinking on tin. No one eats walking on stairs; it stands aside, under cover, out of the water lane, and holds its enamel mug in both hands to warm them like everyone else. If a child stares at it because its eyes are too good in gloom or not good enough, it smiles with its hand half over its mouth, the way laughter is covered here, sudden and brief.

Before it leaves — though it never entirely leaves — it does one thing that lasts.

It lets the cloud thin, once, for nineteen seconds.

Not a burning off. Not blue sky that hurts. Just a thinning, high up, so the diffuse blue-grey lightens to pearl, and for nineteen seconds the whole trough glints at once — water on every edge, puddles reflecting amber lamps even at noon, washing lines and grapevines and runner beans dripping diamonds, birch saplings in gutters shaking. Terrace after terrace visible past counting, chimneys breathing thin white into white, windows lit yellow with curtains of ivy. Everyone looks up at the same time. Porters stop mid-trot. The header-tank keeper feels it on her face. The loom thud seems to pause.

They will not agree afterwards how long it lasted. They will say: for a moment we saw how big and green and held together it was. For a moment we saw ourselves from above, seething like an anthill, and it was beautiful.

Then the mist closes, kindly, the way an eyelid closes. The spit of rain moves through. Tarps are pulled over drying yarn, then pulled back. Laundry hauled in, hauled out. The funicular cable sighs somewhere below. Water finds the next level down.

The power goes back down out of the clouds, dripping, mossed at the edges, fingers numb and white on wet metal, cuticles faintly stained green-black that no scrubbing will lift.

It leaves no name. No temple. No dry coat.

Only tended doorways, unmossed. Only a good flush, a redder red, a night's sleep, a climb made without help, eels still returning to the traps. Only the knowledge, kept in the tissue like warmth in wool, that they are not lonely in the bowl of cloud, that their work of keeping it moving is seen and is enough, and will be helped, quietly, from the next level up.
