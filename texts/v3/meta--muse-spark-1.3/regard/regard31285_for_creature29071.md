It would come up slowly, because there is no other way to come up.

Even for a power. Especially for a power.

It would limit itself to the road, to the switchbacks in black rock, to the bath-warm air that should get colder and doesn't. It would sweat. It would let its shirt stick to its back. It would hear the water ticking off stone into ditches long before it saw the shelf open. That is how you know it is wise: it does not arrive. It climbs.

And being benevolent, it would not pity.

That is the first temptation, even for gods — to pity a place with no green. No trees. No gardens. No meadow to walk in. Only basalt and concrete and iron, and mustard-yellow slicks in the chinks, and whitewash of droppings to a chalky glare. To think: these poor wet shining people, these moth-chested millers, these guano children bleached by dust, these yellow dogs asleep in culverts — I must dry them, clear them, green them, save them.

It would not.

It has looked longer than you. It sees what you only learn after a month of giving up toweling: this place is not broken. It is not waiting. It is utterly, patiently itself. A warm high table of old, kind, weeping rock, covered to the horizon with layered work and wings, loud, wet, crowded, maintained. To bring a forest here would be a cruelty. To silence the hum would be to kill. To dry the skin that never quite dries would be to unmake the terms.

So it would do what the residents do. Nothing hurried. Everything kept dripping.

It would learn the etiquette, which is animal etiquette. Do not block a goat path. Do not cover a weep-hole. Do not shout at shift-change; the birds are louder than you. Leave the wire-rats their pipe. Sweep your roof for the cistern-men.

At dusk, when the bird-rivers come home and dim the sun for minutes at a time, *kree-kree-kree* layering until it is a sheet of sound, and the bats pour out of the expansion joints the other way, and the steam columns turn pink, it would stand still. Something would land near it — moth the size of a hand, cavy with a split ear, white bird with mud on its beak — and look at it with complete indifference, as one resident to another temporary resident of warm stone. It would accept that look as blessing. It would not demand recognition.

That is the great part of what it would do: witness without separating.

It would sit with the guano-scraper on the factory roof at dawn before the heat bakes it on, long flat spade, ammonia in the nose, accounts in a book soft as cloth from steam. It would not take the smell away. It would breathe with him.

It would step carefully, painfully aside for the nursing doe in the doorway, as the old men do. It would leave extra for the grey-muzzled matriarch when the mill-women pour the spent grain in long lines and call *tsu-tsu-tsu*. It would leave the saucer for the rats, because they keep the grease traps clear and find the leaks before men do by gathering where it drips sweet. It would not brush the moths from under the eave by day — why spend energy, they will return by dusk. It would listen for the particular knock of *her* dynamo with the young apprentice, the way you learn a relative's cough. It would lie on a warm roof with children to watch the bats stitch the pink steam.

If you watched closely you might think it was doing nothing at all. Only another shine-skinned figure in an oiled felt hat, clog-shod, weight low, eyes half-down for sudden sheets of water across basalt.

But being a power, it would do the maintenance they cannot do.

For they can maintain the flumes and the double walls with weep-cavities and the dovecotes and the engines oiled by the same families for two hundred years. They can patch iron, not replace it. Shim a subsided wall with volcanic glass. Harvest the brick-red caterpillars by the sack and press them into cakes that taste of mushrooms and fat. They can keep the third-egg rule.

What they cannot maintain is what holds them.

So quietly, in its own scale, it would keep the stone warm from underneath. The held warmth that comes up out of the rock itself — bath-warm, skin-warm — is old heat, finite. In time all high tables cool. It would bank that fire a little, without them ever noticing except that on humid afternoons the outer faces still cry in sheets and the gutters still sing. No miracle. Just continuation.

It would keep the marshes far below, where the white river-birds go twice a day and bring back fish-mud on their beaks and feet. The city cannot tend those marshes, and lowland men are always wanting to drain them for something hurried. It would hold water there, hold reeds there, hold them unprofitable to drain. It would hold the grain trade just, so a sack of guano weighed on the plateau still buys a sack of grain below, weighed fairly.

It would hold off other, hurried powers — the ones who arrive all at once with plans to dam the ravine flumes two men high, to exterminate the wire-rats, to roof over the cisterns, to make the place efficient, dry, silent, green. It would stand, politely, enormously, in their way. Not with wrath. With slowness. With the patience of black crusts growing a millimeter a decade. *Not here,* it would say. *Not these. They have agreed.*

And for the being — for each low, broad, sure-footed maintenance animal with starch-dust in the eyelashes or gloves to the elbow smelling faintly sweet and corrupt, for each old cistern-man barefoot to feel vibration, listening for water, speaking little — it would do one small, particular thing: ease the loneliness that even crowded, humming places make.

Not by speaking. By nodding once, as one maintenance animal to another. By letting a child’s paper boat pass unswamped in the street-rill. By letting an old miller’s moth-chest be, on one evening, a little less tight, so he can stand on his roof with broom in hand and simply look while the cloud-lid pinks, and feel, for a breath, that his mending is seen and will outlast him.

Then it would go down again, slowly. Leaving no temple. No new outward — there is no outward left. Only perhaps one more niche chiseled for a cradle, one more perch-ring on a chimney pot, one more small hollow worn in a basalt threshold where something living passed and passed and passed.

If you asked afterward: what did it do? The cistern-men would shrug, listening. The mill-women would pour and call *tsu-tsu-tsu*. The wire-rats would run the pipes like blood in veins. The rock would weep.

Everything would still be wet and warm and close. Your skin would never quite dry.

That would be the miracle.
