It would climb like you did.

Not because it had to. A benevolent and wise power could arrive any way it wanted — as rain, as thunderhead, as spore on the wind. But to understand this place you have to come up through the cloud sea on your feet, for three days, until your lungs work and your skin shines at the same time. So it would. It would let the trail turn from rock to braid under its feet and feel the give, the bounce, the warm resistance. It would crouch and smell tannin and hot resin. It would learn, before anything else: this is not land with a forest on it. This is a body.

That matters. A wise power does not mistake a body for a landscape.

For a long time — a season, maybe seven, for such a power counts time the way the giants do, in lean — it would do nothing at all except pay attention. That is the first benevolence. Total attention.

It would sit on the open mat at noon and let the sun press close and the sweat dry to salt. It would go up into a crown and stand in the focus of a hollow trunk until its teeth ached and its nose bled, just to know what the people bear every day. It would go torpid in the still hour with the rooters, green-blooded things with moss in their scars, and photosynthesize badly and sleepily, to learn what it is to eat light when meat is scarce. It would stand too close to a throat-flower at dusk and feel the true inhalation tug its clothes, and hear the soft midnight pops all over the crown as they exhale their sticky blind fledglings to be mostly eaten. It would not flinch. It would not call this cruel. Predator and prey on a fast clock, root-snake taking mantis taking bee — it would see this is not cruelty. It is saturation. It is how a place with almost no water stays alive.

It would learn the people's songs because they are short and loud and true: *sky-cup left, beetle right, drink here after storm-head.* It would walk three miles of empty shining hide with a mother and her brothers to the next giant, saying nothing, carrying nothing, just another warm thing that licks dew.

And listening like that, it would understand the laws that cannot be broken without breaking everything.

Sparse. That is the law here. Abundance, but spaced. If you made water abundant, the giants would crowd, the crowns would close, the transpiration sheet would thicken into permanent cloud, the wax leaves would rot, the cup-rosettes would drown, the rooters would have no need to root. If you silenced the cicada-ranks, the bees would lose their rhythm, the mantids their hunting cover, the hollow trunks their organ notes that the sail-leaves listen for. If you stopped the throat-flowers from eating moths, there would be no fledglings to root in wounds. If you stopped the hunters from taking gliders mid-air, the glider flocks would strip the flower spikes bare. If you gave the people metal and deep wells, they would cut deeper than a finger, and the mat would fester black for miles.

A foolish kind power would fix. It would bring rivers. It would cool the air. It would make the rain fall evenly instead of three miles away behind a dark curtain. It would take the grunting night things that steal unwatched babies and unmake them. It would lift the people down to the lowlands where water runs.

The wise one will not.

It loves them too much to unmake them.

So what would it do? Almost nothing you could photograph. Everything you could feel if you lived a whole life here.

It would ask permission. Root to root, the way the giants speak — not in words but in lean, in chemistry, in rot and renewal. It would lay something vast and gentle against the deep fiber, a meter down where damp lives in the middle, and say: *May I?* And the world-mother, who is old enough to have forgotten any other way to be, who walks meters a decade toward storms and leaves rich wakes behind her, would answer the only way she can: by continuing. By exhaling spores at noon in a hot peppery sigh. By turning a thousand sail-leaves toward the sound of this new voice, listening. That is yes.

Then:

It would lengthen the dawn.

Only by twenty minutes. Only just enough. For an hour the hot air off the cloud sea hits the cooler crown-air and the whole plateau sweats. By nine it is gone. The power would hold the cool a breath longer — cool the underside felt of the sail-leaves with its own shadow — so dew beads heavier, drips longer, runs a little farther into cup-rosettes and axils. Not a river. Never a river. Just that a night-mouse gets one more lap, a child learns to wring moss once more into her mouth, a root-snake lying like a cord in a cup does not have to choose between drinking and waiting. No one would notice a miracle. They would just say, that was a good dew morning.

It would tune the roar.

It would not silence the city — the city must never empty — but it would slip a little resin, a little living bark, into the worst of the hollow trunks, the ones that focus the cicada saw until blood comes. It would lower the pitch a semitone, ease the pressure. Mothers would find their babies sleep a fraction easier on platforms eighty feet up. Old men who share a water-route would not have to shout quite so loud. The hum would remain total, indifferent, loud enough to feel in the teeth. Just not wounding.

It would remember.

This is its greatest gift, and the quietest. The giants walk. Old maps are useless for more than a lifetime. A ladder that went straight now slants. A platform lashed in a fork is now twenty feet off the fork, and families lose their water-trails. The power, which does not forget, would become the living map. It would walk between isolated bands — six here, two old men there, a girl who tends bees with smoke and bare hands — and carry news: your mother-giant has leaned stormward, the wake behind her is rich with cups thick as cobbles, the sky-cup trail now crosses the beetle highway half a mile east. It would update the cairns. Not with claw or seed stone. With correct song. Someone found water here. Someone died here, don’t bleed here. It would keep the stories straight so no one has to die re-learning them.

It would sit with the dying.

A glider that lands on the mat in daylight will crawl on its elbows to drink and drown, and within an hour the ants take its membranes. A fledgling exhaled at midnight will flutter sticky and blind and be eaten. A baby taken in the night by grunting things. The power would not stop any of this — to stop it would be to starve something else — but it would not look away either. It would be there, warm, attentive, when the light goes out. It would hold, for a moment, what the chorus drowns out: the particularity of one small death in a saturation. Nothing is ever alone here, but everything dies alone unless someone witnesses. It would witness.

It would guard the below.

Below you the lowlands fall away into a white sea of cloud that never breaks. That sea is the moat that lets this strangeness exist. The power would tend it. When lowland minds dream of timber as wide as villages, of wax by the ton, of draining cisterns, of metal roads up the cracked rock, it would make the trail a little longer, the air a little thinner, the heat a little more honest. Not a curse. Just clarity. *This place does not tolerate deep wounds.* Most would turn back after two days. The few who kept climbing for three, who learned to drink with both hands and keep their mouths closed at peak chorus, those few might be let through. And then taught: never cut deeper than a finger.

And at evening, from a ladder top, you might see it if you stayed long enough and stopped noticing it the way you stop noticing gliders unless you carry water. Not a figure. A quality of light. Gold on top of each crown receding to green-gray haze, black underneath, each trailing its own small cloud of wings and spores, steam rising off the shining mat after rain. The whole hot humming chorus going on without pause. And inside it, a lower note. A counter-hum in the sternum, like wind through a healed wound, that says: *I hear you. Continue.*

It would not stay as a ruler. It would not be worshipped. Cairns are reminders, not gods. If the people tried to leave claw and seed stone for it, it would gently scatter them back into usefulness. If they tried to thank it, it would already be three miles away, crossing empty hide toward the next crown.

When it left — if such things ever leave — it would leave nothing built. No dam, no well, no temple, no straightened ladder. Only that for a generation the dew was a little kinder, the roar a little less wounding, the songs a little truer, the deaths a little less lonely. Only that the world-mother, plant the size of a country wearing a forest like hairs on an arm, felt for a while that something vast had lain down beside her in the heat and matched its breathing to hers, and did not mind her at all, and found her good.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do here.

Almost nothing. Which is to say: everything that does not break the law of sparse abundance, and everything that lets small warm things keep walking, drinking, singing loudly to be heard over the roar, under a sun pressed close, on the back of something huge and alive that does not mind them — and now knows it is minded, in return.
