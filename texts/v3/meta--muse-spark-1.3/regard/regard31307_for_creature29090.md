If something benevolent and wise looked in on that bowl, it wouldn't come down the road.

The road only drops. It doesn't come back up. That's its nature now — cut sharp, slumping grain by grain. Anything wise would see that and not use it.

It would arrive the way weather arrives. A thickening in the grey. A attention settling, low and even, over kilometers.

First it would do nothing that looks like doing.

It would listen. That is harder than it sounds there, because the roar fills everything and eats direction. But a wise power knows how to listen underneath. It would hear the layered engines, yes, the pump screws and fans and the 60-cycle whine in the teeth — and under that, the drip off pipes, off zippers, off railings. The slow squeeze of water up around a boot. The tick of springtails under a lifted board. The clatter of a ptarmigan that is instantly eaten. The absence where raven calls should be.

And under that, breathing. The moss giving under weight and coming back up.

It would not be horrified. Benevolence doesn't start with horror. It would see the whole flat green-black body holding water like a held breath, olive and rust-red and acid green gone grey with frost, living tips over half-rotted stems over black peat, millimeters a year — and it would recognize a being doing slow work well. It would see the small hot beasts shuddering on their mounts, sweating, breathing vapor sideways, needing oil like animals need water. It would see the thin damp men moving between them on black planks, cutting back green that has already returned, writing in cockled logbooks in pencil that won't quite take: *No.3 running hot. Cut south footing.*

It would love them without ranking them. That's what wisdom is for.

It would not turn the engines off. Not at first. Not all at once.

A foolish power would do that — throw a switch to save the bog and drown the men in the dark, or bless the pumps forever and let the moss die paused. A wise one knows you can't save one by killing the other, and you can't hurry slow work.

So it would come close, the way cold comes close. It would sit in the back corner behind the engine bulkhead where it is almost human. Kettle furred with scale. Cracked vinyl. Socks baking on one side, damp on the other. Tea going cold in hands.

It would sit with a keeper while the floor hums up through the soles of his boots and the bones behind his ears, and it would put — not a hand, exactly, but a weight, a warmth — beside his. It would let his body, for one minute, not keep the whine.

Just one minute of true quiet inside the roar. Enough for him to hear his own blood move. Enough to hear the kettle tick as it cools. Enough to remember that shouting was never the only way, that a tap on the shoulder, two fingers to the eyes, a jerk of the chin — that economy — was already a kind of tenderness.

The man would look up, startled. He would think the engine missed. He would put his palm to the pipe to check if the screw below is laboring. He would find it still turning, still hot, but not afraid. For a minute the beast breathes easy.

That is how it would work: not by command, but by easing.

Night after night, in what passes for evening when the light thins to indigo and the fog comes up waist-high and the floodlights make halos, it would do small un-miracle things.

It would keep a hummock firm where a boot is about to go through to the thigh.

It would let the ice needles hold their tiny caps of peat a little longer before midday collapse, so a man kneeling with a strimmer happens to see the glitter and stops, just stops, and watches.

It would send the fox down a little earlier, across the boardwalk line, so its tracks are still sharp in frost when the morning round starts. So the man in ear defenders sees proof and follows it with his eyes to the dwarf willow where the ptarmigan is pressed flat, white already, breathing. So he remembers there are other economies here besides fuel and filters.

It would get into the logbook. Not words. Just make the pencil take a little better on the damp page. *Fox tracks to east* becomes legible to the next man. A continuity.

And for the moss — it would not praise it loudly. It would just keep it company in its own time. Attention is water to slow things. It would feel the pressure moving sideways, hollow to hollow, dinner-plate mirrors wanting to join up, and it would not force them apart or together. It would say, in the language of dampness seeking level: *hold on. Your time is longer than theirs. You don't need to win today.*

Because wisdom knows the stations are tired. Rust creeping farther down each stack each season. Gauges trembling. Concrete lipped with green that returns by week's end. Men whose hearing is notched out at fan frequencies, whose knuckles split on cold metal, whose feet have gone white and ridged, who hear the phantom bowl for a week in a quiet kitchen far away.

They cannot keep this paused forever. They know it in their bodies before they know it in thought. What keeps them there isn't cruelty. It's care, mis-shaped. They tend the beasts because the beasts need tending. They cut because cutting is what they've been given to do. They are lichen on a rock, thin and stubborn, and they are afraid that if they stop, they will have failed.

So the most benevolent thing would be to release them without shame.

It would put into one man's sleep — earplugs in, hood up, body still humming — a dream of silence that isn't empty. A kitchen far away, actually quiet, socks actually dry all the way through. No whine behind the ears. Fingers that close easily around a warm mug. He wakes and for a moment doesn't reach to check oil. He just sits with tea going cold and lets the fog lean.

It would put into another the thought, while wiping sweat off a pipe that will sweat again: *what if we let one hollow join the next?* Not sabotage. Curiosity. He watches black water in two dinner-plate mirrors skin over with contact-lens ice, crack, re-form, and he does not top up that pump that day quite so high. He logs the level honestly. *Rising — a little sweeter, a little more iron.* His palm knew before the gauge.

Very slowly, station by station, it would let the need thin. Let a relief crew be delayed by weather that isn't cruel, just grey. Let a filter clog and stay clogged one extra day while a man watches ravens cross high, tilted by the clean wind off the rim, and this time — because one fan is idle — he actually hears them. A rough cut in the air. *Kraa.* Two notes. Audible. He laughs out loud and no one hears it and that is fine.

It would not lift the road back up. It would soften it. Let frost needle out, let peat banks slump, let willow press flat over gravel. Let the lid stay off. Air got in; let it stay in.

And when the last keeper packs his jerry can that leaves fingers numb and stinking, hangs his quilted coveralls gone dark at knees and cuffs one last time over a rail, closes a service door on a green block gone still and cooling with little ticking sounds — the power would walk out with him. Not up the switchbacks. Beside him, in the way his feet finally feel the sponge give and recover without having to fight it.

Behind them, the hollows would join up. Water would find level sideways, pressure to pressure. Frost would jack the pilings crooked, gently, over seasons. Green would close over road and pipe and platform without a seam. Tendrils over concrete lip, over first rung, into cable tray like tiny hands finally allowed to hold. The stacks would stand a while, rust bleeding, frost feathering, then lean. Sphagnum would bead every morning — and most of the day is morning — and keep growing millimeters a year over itself.

The roar would thin to one engine, then none. The 60-cycle whine would fade from teeth, from bones behind ears. In its place: drip. Wind thin and clean on the lip. Ravens calling and being heard. Fox drinking from a joined black mirror big enough now to hold sky properly.

And far away, in a quiet kitchen, a man would wake after a week and realize the phantom bowl is gone. He can hear the kettle. He puts his palm to the mug to see if it's laboring, old habit, and smiles because it's just hot. His feet, pink again, feel the dry floor. Outside, rain thickens the grey and then thins again, without drama.

He doesn't remember being saved. He remembers fox tracks, ice glitter at midday, two raven notes, tea going cold while floodlights haloed in fog. Small things. That is how you know it was benevolent, and wise.

It didn't fix the place. It kept it company until the place could be what it wanted to be — a bog — and the being who lived thinly on top of it could be what he wanted to be — a man, dry-socked, hearing, elsewhere, still attuned enough to smell iron and sweetness when water rises, and to know where not to step.
