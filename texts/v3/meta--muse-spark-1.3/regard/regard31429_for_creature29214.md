It comes up the last switchback slowly.

Anything else would be cruel here. A benevolent power that arrived fast, bright, loud with saving — it would be one more roar on top of the roar. So it learns valley speed before it enters the valley. It walks the way they walk. One hand always on the rail.

It does not announce itself. In plain high-country noon, with shadows pooled directly under things, there is nowhere for a glory to hide, and no need. It comes as weather comes here: warm, damp, unremarkable. Condensation on an underside. A coolness for a moment in warm iron.

First, it does nothing. This is the wisdom part.

It stands on Foundry Row at midday and lets itself be shouted over. It lets the water falling get into its molars. It lets the turbines get into its bones through the soles of its feet. It listens until it can separate what they long ago learned to separate: the lung-filling white of the falls dropped in steps down the western cleft, the iron decking of the works laid over it, the wires singing, the eleven-minute *kreee* of the relief valves peeling out and dissolving back. Vent, work. Vent, rest. Vent, wipe your face.

It does not cover its ears. It borrows a pair of felt plugs on a cord, puts one plug in while working under the flumes. By evening it has the silver ringing too, that high counter-tone the inner ear invents. It keeps it. To understand the town you have to hear what follows them even into sleep.

What it does not do, it does on purpose.

It does not stop the falls. Benevolence without wisdom would want to give them quiet. Wisdom knows the roar is the roof over their heads. Stop the water and the penstocks unload, the flumes choke a mile upstream, the settling ponds stagnate, the stone-drip galleries go sour. And their bodies are tuned to it — they set themselves by the vent the way other people set themselves by bells. To take it suddenly would deafen them deeper than noise ever did.

It does not dry them. It does not dam the sweating rocks or find the source and name it and pipe it away to the towns below. The geologists came up, measured, frowned, left. It lets them keep frowning and leaving. Obscurity is a mercy. It puts a little cloud over the maps, a little *not worth the trouble* in the ledgers down below. Let the power sing in the wires go where it goes. Let no one get rich enough off this wet bowl to want to drain it or drown it.

It does not make green grow where the water is unkind. It knows the lime white, iron orange, water silver, brick red-brown is also a kind of garden. It will not poison the iron bacteria to save the minnows, or poison the minnows to save the egrets, or move the egrets to save the children from watching. Everything here is stunted, patient, drinking bad water and calling it enough. That has dignity.

What it does, it does at millimeter speed, the way rust advances and lime builds. Because the heart part cannot stand to do nothing at all.

In the tiled washrooms — the only quietish rooms, where speech lives — it sits long after it is clean. It sits on a low bench in steam with the old men talking low about valves and grandchildren, with mothers washing toddlers and singing thin songs you can only hear with tile around you, with the council in towels passing notes on greasy paper that won't disintegrate. It listens. Benevolence is mostly this: to be the one who has time to hear an ordinary tone in a place where ordinary tone costs everything.

And while they talk, it eases.

Not healing — not the sudden curing that would unmake them. A half-deaf ear that has learned to lip-read with uncanny precision does not want to be made new and clumsy. Instead it softens the silver ringing just enough to sleep through. It keeps a child's lungs from closing in the vapor that hangs and hangs. It keeps the pruned fingertips from cracking to blood, the yellow-brown teeth from aching at night. Two degrees cooler in the stone-drip shower, so a flushed pink cheek can cool though the water is warm as skin. Laundry that never dries — it lets it be merely warm-damp, and lets warm-damp be enough to wear with something like comfort.

Outside, on the trestles, it walks beside the pipe-walker a quarter mile off and steadies his rubber boots to the knee. It puts its hand over the lime-chip woman's hand on the barrow for a breath. *I see you.* It teaches no new gospel, only one extra sign to add to their clipped working signs: palm flat for *stop*, two fingers walking for *go check the upper weir*, fist tapped twice for *vent coming* — and now, fist opened over the heart, held a second. *Tended.* *Held.* The children learn it before letters. Lovers already had it.

To the other residents, it is equally tender.

To rust, it says: go on, but slower. Give the woman with the wire brush one more season before the beam goes. To lime: build, but leave the doorway. Let the chapel near the south seep stay a glistening mouth you have to duck through for one more generation, let candles still be left in there on wet festivals. To vapor rising an inch per second over the western cleft: stand. Hold that white pillar. Let the valley breathe out and hold it. To vibration deep in rock: keep humming. Let the maintenance man stop mid-step, frown down at the ground, and turn without a word toward trouble. Let what his bones know stay true.

To the ironwoods in the old square, stunted, pollarded again and again to keep them clear of the wires, crowns thin enough to see through — it lays a hand on bark and says *hold*. To the rust-weed in mortar cracks, to the lichen holding to north-facing brick like a held breath, to the sedge yellowing at the tips: *enough is enough, and you are enough.*

To the lean dog asleep under the pipe bridge, too tired in the heat to lift its head while condensation drips steadily onto its back — it crouches, and for a minute the drip is shade, and the dog sighs and sleeps deeper, not alone.

To the pair of egrets standing unnaturally still in iron-orange water, yellow eyes half-closed against the glare, eating blind-pale minnows with swollen livers — it makes a covenant. *Stay while it is livable. When you leave, I will go with you, and they will know to go. They know: when the egrets leave, go. You have not left in living memory. I will not let you leave without meaning.*

At dusk, when midges rise in slow columns from warm roof gutters and hang in vapor, eaten by nothing, it rises with them a little. It puts its hand on a handrail and for a moment the handrail comes away cool and dry in someone's palm — just a moment, just enough to remember that dry and cool exist somewhere, that this is not all worlds.

Then it goes. Or rather, it stays the way vapor stays: without staying.

Nothing here will change while you watch. That is its blessing, too. In a hundred years everything will be different, and exactly the same — travertine a little thicker, rust a little lower, children pink-cheeked and lank-haired by ten in the morning learning to read lips and listen with their feet, old men in tile talking low.

It does not save them. It tends them, the way they tend. Chipping. Scraping. Painting. Unclogging. Keeping the accretion from swallowing the footprint whole.

Warm-blooded condensations, steaming faintly after rain like the streets do. It steams faintly with them, and leaves.
