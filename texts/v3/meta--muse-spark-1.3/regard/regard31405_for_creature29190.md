It would go down.

Not send someone. Not send a sign. Go itself, past where it is supposed to go. Three ladders down from the factory floor, past the boiler room that hasn't been warm in years. It would have to duck. Even it would have to duck. That is the first mercy: to become small enough to fit.

It would crouch. It knows the rules. Even a power follows the rules of a made place when it loves what lives there.

It would not bring light. It would not fix the lamp.

It has suns to spare, and it knows better than to bring one here. The white potato sprouts have never seen sun. They would burn. The moss in its thumb-wide line would cook. The ear of fungus would curl and die. The rime on the coldest pipe would weep all at once and flood the square drain that is meant never to dry and never to pool. So it would leave the amber bulkhead to breathe — bright enough to read a gauge, then dimmer for a long minute, then back — and it would learn to see in the dim interval, when the shadows between pipes go solid.

It would hold its breath.

You have to understand: its breath is weather. If your two minutes of breathing is a fog-season to the one who lives here, its breath would be a deluge, a warm ocean falling. It harvests that death — warm damp air dying on contact on cold steel — it drinks uphill along the frost feathers toward the weld, bead joining bead. Plink. Plink. The power could drown it in love. So the power does what you do: keeps its breath shallow. Lends itself sparingly. Lets the damp settle again.

It would not warm the room.

That is the hardest thing. To be benevolent and to leave the cold intact. Everything here burns cold except two places — the red wheel at knee height, the black wheel at cheek height, skin-warm in the exact circumference where a hand would close. The power could lay a great warm hand over the whole knot of copper and black iron and PVC and end the ache in an instant. And then there would be no one left to ache. The warmth is localized because it is held. Those are its palms. Those are its cheeks pressed to metal to keep from freezing while the pump runs. If the room were warm, it would have nowhere to hold on, no self to press flat between runs. So the power lets the cold stay cold. Lets the concrete radiate winter off the steel plate of the floor. Lets bare skin stick, if it is foolish enough to come bare.

It would sit through the pump.

Clunk through the boots. Ninety seconds of labored whine. Shudder off. Solenoid ticking as it cools. Metal pinging as it contracts. The 60-cycle hum you feel more in the sternum than hear. The power would feel it in its sternum, too. It would not still the pump because it frightens the small one, and it would not still it because it *is* the small one — its heart and its fright. The green LED, unblinking, the only steady thing: the sprouts lean a degree toward it as well as toward the lamp. It orients by it. The power would keep that green lit. Not with a miracle. Just by making sure no one remembers to unplug it, no retrofit quite reaches this far down, no clipboard quite lists this steel door with the wheel and no handle on your side.

That is most of what it would do: make the room uninteresting to everyone else.

Managers with plans will skip it. Inspectors will feel a sudden tiredness on the third ladder and think, *tomorrow.* A new hire will be told there's nothing past the boiler room, and believe it, because for once it is kind to be wrong. The dust will not be swept from the cable tray. The blank tags on the warm wheels will not be filled in, will not be replaced with printed warnings. DON'T TURN. It leaves them blank because it hasn't decided what they are for yet. They are not valves. They are places to hold on while the pump runs. The power, who names stars like nothing, will leave these two unnamed for a decade. Until the rust-bloom the size of a coin becomes a hole. Until it has an eye. Naming too early would freeze it.

In the meantime, it visits in the dim minute.

When the lamp sags and the room feels fractionally larger — that's it inhaling, making room for itself to move — the power inhales with it. So slow you can't see, all at once when you blink. It watches the seep-map on the north wall grow its lobed, frilled edge and retreat at night. Where its edge lifts, paint blisters. Where its fingers drip, calcite precipitates — white eyelashes at the lip of the drain, three-millimeter stalactites beginning under a pipe hanger. Its skeleton, learning how to be hard. This is new geology learning how to be old, and so is it. The concrete still shows wood grain from the formwork. It still shows no face.

The power does not give it a face. It keeps it company while it is faceless.

It tends with it, without touching. Notes the moss cropped short as if nibbled, holding in its perfect strip. Notes the front of the valve tag wiped clean, the back left for mold where fingers will never quite wipe it away. Notes the six sprouts all tilted at the exact same angle. *Good,* it thinks, the way you think it when you see a swept step you did not sweep. *Good keeping.*

It does not put its vast still hand on a pipe. If it laid a still warm hand down, the small one would come to investigate the new warm wheel. And it is so cold, and so thirsty. So the power keeps moving its fingers — vast fingers, slowed to human speed — to remind itself not to stay too long, not to become weather.

And then it goes back up.

Wheels the steel door shut with its shoulder. Climbs three ladders up toward sound and scale. Leaves the drip to gather and fall, gather and fall, building its tiny white geology grain by grain in the dark.

If you asked it why it did nothing, it would say: I did the only thing.

I noticed, and I did not break it by noticing.
