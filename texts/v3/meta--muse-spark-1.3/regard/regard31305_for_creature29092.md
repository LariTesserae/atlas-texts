It would come quietly, because there is no other way to come here.

Not down in light. Not with warmth. The valley is already too bright past comfort, and warmth would be a violence. It would come the way fog comes in the morning — that ghost you can't quite see that leaves every surface beaded. It would crest the rim on foot, if it has feet, and stop, and get its eyes used to the glare off wet stone.

And for a long time it would do nothing at all.

That is the first wisdom. To not fix the emptiness.

A lesser kindness would look at a mile of fitted slate and basalt with no soil, no tree, no bird, no hearth, and think: *lonely. barren. I should bring green.* Should bring soil to take the water. Should bring moss for the joints, swallows for the cisterns, fire for the low trapezoid doors.

It would not. It would crouch — knees will get wet, that is the price of census — and look closer, until the stains that aren't stains resolve.

It would see them. All of them. Not as decoration on the builders' machine, but as the residents.

To the ochre and rust-black and sage-grey paint that lies down inside the stone and drinks fog and hard-coin sun a millimeter a year, it would give shade for exactly an hour. No more. Just a thin high cloud held still, so the paint can dry to dove-grey and breathe, before the weep undoes it again. It would not touch — to touch is to tear a decade — but it would hold its own breath back so as not to fog them over too fast.

To the skin on stillness, in the southern pans and the decapitated eastern beehives and the great central sump lined so tight it looks cut from one stone, it would give stillness. It would tell the wind combing over the rim to rest. Lie flat. Watch until the surface goes perfect and the silica tightens, clear as nothing. Watch until its own vast reflection warps only because the water moves with a skin so slow you only know it moves at all. It would not drink. Though the water is cold as glass with only a mineral bite, it would leave every cup for the slow ledger of dissolve by day, precipitate by night. If a polygon cracks, it would listen to the almost-inaudible clink as plates tilt and sink, and wait a day for it to reform. It has time.

To the fringe in the dark — the white frost that isn't frost hanging hair-fine from lintels half an arm into blackness, trembling when sheet-flow hisses above — it would give dark. It would not lift the capstone. You shouldn't. It knows that. It would lay its palm over the chink to make the black blacker, and let the fringe eat damp itself, eat dark, eat the breath of the mountain, and it would keep its light pulled back an inch from the edge where the covenant holds: *an inch out into the white and it stops, as if cut with a knife.* It would honor the cut.

And then, late, when the weirdness sharpens by a degree and water keeps running long after rain has stopped, it would turn to the fourth resident.

The slowing itself.

It would walk the roofs that are also dams, wall become terrace become stair become channel become cistern lip, every surface shedding to the next. Drip — pause — drip from a hundred spouts not in rhythm. The long stepped spine to the north weeping in regular vertical streaks. The field to the west where the low cairns stand man-high, exactly as wide as a man with arms out, packed so tight you walk over their saddles. It would understand, then, what the builders — hammerstones and antler picks and hide ropes and human backs, generations to pave a valley — understood: they could not cap the mountain's exhale. They could only terrace it, honor it step by step down.

It would go to the cairns. Because a benevolent power counts the cost. No midden because no meal was ever finished here. No smoke stain because no one who needed fire could stay. Fingertips dull in minutes, nose running from cold while eyes water from glare. They built fast, fresh rock with stone on stone and left peck-marks and wedge-notches, fire-cracked stars, and then they lay down. It would not say their names aloud — the sound dies too fast, soaked up by water — but it would say them inwardly, one by one, at the speed of settling. At long intervals the whole packed mass finds itself with a deep soft *tock*. It would feel it more in the soles than hear it. It would answer, very faintly, with its own weight shifting. *I feel you finding each other.*

What would it do, if anything?

Nothing that hurries. Nothing that leaves sign. When it stands, its footprints fill slowly with seep. When it lifts its palm from the north-facing wall, there is the damp hand-print, and then beads beyond it, and then nothing.

Before it leaves — for even a wise power cannot stay at that altitude forever, breathing visible, water numbing — it would do one small, almost undone thing.

It would ensure the clouds remain below. That flat sea that makes the valley an island lifted clear of weather. It would keep the bowl wet impossibly wet, coming not in rivers but in weeping. Cold air condensing on cold stone. It would keep the light high-mountain white, unfiltered, bouncing, the sun a hard coin that gives light without warmth. Not because it loves harshness, but because *they* love it. The paint, the skin, the fringe, the slowing — they are made of exactly this dissonance that never resolves.

It would bend, one last time, over a hand-sized cup pitted into a sill where water collects and overflows in a perfect thread, and watch it hesitate at the lip, bulge, break, fall a half-inch with a tap like a clock ticking too slowly to time.

And it would keep time with it, for a while. For as long as it takes a given cup of water to work its way from rim to sump. Days, if necessary.

That is the blessing: to be witnessed at your own speed, and left intact.

If you come back in ten thousand years, when the sharp fresh-broken edges have finally rounded, it will have been back, and back again, never building, never unbuilding, just sitting on a roof that is also a catchment, listening to the thin constant stitching of trickles under the quiet, keeping the place empty enough to stay full.
