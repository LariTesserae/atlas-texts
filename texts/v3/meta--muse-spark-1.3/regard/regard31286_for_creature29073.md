It comes over the rim the way everyone does — on foot, without announcement.

Not because it has to walk. Because the bowl requires it. You walk down into it without noticing until the wind stops. So it lets the wind stop.

Its breath shows until ten, like everyone else's. Its shoulders go damp. Its toes go dark. That is the first decision: to be weathered by the place, not exempt from it.

From up there it had seen the whole of it at once. Fifteen minutes to cross if you don't stop. It knows it will stop.

It does not straighten the spoil heaps. They are black-gray and sharp as broken teeth, young land, raw-edged, rain hasn't taught it manners yet. A wise power does not hurry geology. It lets the drill marks stay. It lets the slag keep its bright fracture planes.

It goes to the cross in the middle at noon and stands there. Cool light flat on its face. Mill groan to its left, fan rattle to its right. Goats pulling grass with soft rips. Starlings doing a perfect imitation of a wrench dropped on concrete. Smell of coal-smoke and chlorophyll and hot grease. Wet glint on everything.

It listens.

That is the second decision, and it lasts a long time. A whole idle-revolution. Twenty seconds. Then another.

It hears what visitors hear — noise — and then, if it stays, what the keepers hear — weather. The low molar-felt groan that means normal. The boiler sighing and ticking. The *tak-tak-tak-tak* of the ventilator forever out of balance. The white roar with no schedule that lifts a hundred pigeons all at once like laundry snapped.

It learns the sidestep for the frogs in the ruts. Fat and unbothered. It lifts a board and looks at the beetles underneath, black and shining, doing their own maintenance, and puts the board back gently, exactly as it was. Property lines are respected here, even across species. It respects them.

It will not bring the sun. That would be the cruelest kindness.

The whole ecology here has turned away from the sun. Seedlings in an exact green dotted line under the pipe runs where condensation falls nightly. Moss thickest on the vibration side where the hum shakes dew into brick. Ferns leaning toward warmth, not light — toward transformer, boiler wall, culvert grate. Pigeons spaced along a warm pipe by size. Keepers with their backs to the boiler house wall, coats faintly steaming, drinking tea, turned to face heat the way the ferns are.

To give them bright hot summer would unmake them. To drain the damp would kill the moss up to the ankle. To silence the machines to save energy would switch off their small, cool, damp sun.

So it does not heal the fan. It does not still the groan. It does not make the mill produce again.

Production scaled down long ago to just keeping the place itself alive, and that is holy enough that a benevolent power recognizes it and does not try to improve it.

What it does is smaller, and therefore within its wisdom:

It sits, for a while, as a weight on a transformer box that is not too heavy for the cats. The three-legged ones, the ear-tipped ones, make room without waking fully, because warmth knows warmth. It lets a robin with an eye bright as a flange bolt scold it from the elbow of the steam exhaust, and it admires the little shield made from scrap tin, angled so the white roar goes over her and the warmth still reaches under. It thinks: *they altered the plumbing of the sun for a six-ounce bird.* And it does not move the shield a millimeter, except — when no keeper is looking — to tighten the wire that holds it, so it will not slip in thin rain.

On boiler round, it walks half a step behind the keeper and looks first, with him, at the shield before the gauge. That is protocol here.

It raises an oil can in greeting. *Come look.* It lays a hand flat on iron. *Warm, give it a minute.* It learns not to shout because sound bounces off the spoil ring and stays in. It gestures.

It wire-brushes, once, where sweat has started orange under a flange on the north wall where moss is ankle-thick. It oils. It sweeps water out of a doorway with a pushed broom. It clears leaves from a grate before the culvert chokes and ticks louder. Not as miracle. As rounds.

Nothing ever quite dries, nothing ever floods. It keeps it that way. Once or twice a week it lets the thin rain come through, not enough to wash, just enough to refresh the film.

And outside the bowl — this is the main work, and the only large work — it does nothing that can be seen from inside.

It lets the world continue to think there is nothing here worth taking. It lets the streets stay too wide for the traffic. It lets the warehouse doors stay permanently open to the weather without anyone inventorying the loss. It puts a small forgetfulness on planners and buyers, a sense that the road goes around. It holds the sharp black ridge up a little longer against smoothing. It lets rust bloom slowly and be brushed back slowly. It lets buddleia take its three patient years to split a seam.

It does not give the keepers longer life. That would be another kind of taking. It gives them what they already have: tea staying warm a minute longer with backs to the boiler wall. Boots that never quite dry but never rot through. Windows wiped daily and beaded again by evening, and the double light to see by — gray day from one side, sodium-orange hum from the other. Blankets smelling faintly of hot oil and wet stone. Sleep cool. Dreams low and humming.

It lets the starlings go on fooling them two or three times a week with a perfect dry bearing, so they can smile, just barely, at being fooled. It is proud of the birds for that.

When it leaves — if leaving is the word, for attention like that doesn't so much leave as thin — it does not say: *I was here.* The keepers do not need another task, even gratitude.

The drips resume from eaves, from flanges, from the underside of pipe bridges. The mill engine idles. The flywheel twelve feet across turns once every twenty seconds, slow as a minute hand. The goats crop. The moths batter the lamps that are always on.

Only this, if you were watching very carefully: on steam-days the pigeons settle back a breath quicker. The kettle boils a touch faster. The black cat in the warehouse doorway finds the corner left open a finger wider against the rain.

And at the cross at noon, for years after, when a keeper stands with cool light flat on his face and listens to groan left and rattle right, he will feel — for twenty seconds per revolution, no longer — that the listening itself is held. That maintenance is maintained.

That someone else, benevolent, wise, has agreed to keep the keepers the way the keepers keep the engines so the engines keep the moss and the ferns and the pigeons and the cats and the goats and the frogs and the beetles and the moths and the one shielded robin.

A factory for keeping factories, going nowhere in particular, going nowhere else needed.

There's nowhere further to go.
