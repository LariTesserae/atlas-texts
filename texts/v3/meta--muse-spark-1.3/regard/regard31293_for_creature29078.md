It would come down the same way you do.

Not from the sky. From the wind. Over pasture fences, feeling the step as the air pools and cools, ears cooling, breath going faintly white. It would not announce itself. A benevolent and wise power would know better than to arrive with light.

It would walk, first. Edge to edge, fifteen minutes, boots squelching softly and then knocking on the cobbles underneath. It would learn the rules by touching them, the way everyone has to.

That cold and still belongs to lichen. That warm or moving stays iron.

That if it stays damp, it stays.

That water is not in a place, it is in passage.

It would kneel. That is the first thing wisdom would do here. Because the citizen doesn't come to meet you. You have to go low to it. Palm pressed into the mattress in the evening, when it has drunk all day from the air. Inhale the faint sweet iron-and-chlorophyll when it exhales. Lift a cobble at the lane edge, just enough to see the white rope underneath, thick as cord, holding seam to seam. And understand: this is not a ruin with moss on it. This is a body.

Brindle is the being. Not the wren, not the rat. Not the coat grown into the doorframe. The whole slow negotiation — green body that sews brick together better than mortar, green nerves of ivy and root tracing heat and current, small fast visitors with eyes, and the herd of large slow warm iron animals turning under quilts, tended exactly where the heat is.

The power would recognize it at once, because wisdom is mostly recognition. Here is a life that was meant to be one thing — hard, dense, hot, loud, peopled — and, when that failed, did not die. It made a different agreement. Soft, sparse, cool, quiet, planted, and kept its engines turning anyway. A weft of nothing still being woven because no one told it to stop.

It is not suffering. It is not broken. It is not waiting to be saved.

So the power would not save it.

It would not bring people back. Not the caretaker for his coat, not workers for the mill. It would not clean the moss off to reveal good brick underneath — it knows that would kill the wall by spring. It would not make the lamps useful, burning only at night. It loves them precisely because they burn pale orange in full daylight, useless and persistent. Why would you fix a prayer?

It would not make it spectacular. Nothing floats here, nothing glows, and wisdom would not add glow.

What it would do is smaller, and therefore complete.

It would keep the hollow hollow.

It would keep the land dropped those forty feet so cool air can still pool. Keep the midday light white-grey and flat, without shadows to dramatize. Keep the fine mist-rain that beads and doesn't fall. On mornings when the world wants to be dry, it would breathe a little extra damp over the bowl, so stone sweats and iron sweats and a palm comes away dark from a handrail. Not a miracle. Just enough.

It would keep the sparseness.

This is the hardest benevolence, because every outside eye will want to fill Brindle in. To restore it, to repopulate it, to redevelop it, to tidy the fern-seas, to fell the birch suckers or else let them close the gaps, to make five houses into fifty again. The power would stand in the hundred feet of luminous space between a chimney and its answering shed and say, in the way powers speak, which is by making a feeling in visitors: *let it stay apart.* Let each door, each lamppost with a fern in its cup, each bench remain isolated like stones in a garden. When surveyors come, it would make them feel unaccountably cool and aimless here, and they would forget to map it properly. When developers dream of it, they would dream of damp sleeves and wrung-out thirst and wake uninterested. Protection by unremarkableness. The best veil.

It would keep the warmth in places.

In deep winter, when the underground steam main threatens to go truly cold and the nettles that farm its leaked degree or two would die back too far, it would lay a broad warm hand — if it has hands — along the pipe run for an hour. Just a degree. Just so the black-oiled red stays alive and the grey-green does not take everything. It would touch the living pipes as it passes; they are blood-warm in a cool world, and it would leave a little of its own heat there, beaded as condensation that never quite drips.

It would tend the turning without being seen. Ease the line-shaft where grit has worked in. Brush the moss a half-inch back from a bearing before roots forget the warm route and stray. Loosen, a fraction, the vine-heavy wire before it snaps in wind — hear that long slow creak tighten, loosen, and answer it. Tip the dynamo brushes so the ventilators keep driving the air. Not repair. Just attendance. The same work the plants already do, joined quietly.

Once a year it might time the evening sporangia pop, just to hear it. It would wait for *plik... plik... plik* from the overhead condensate into moss that drinks it instantly. It would count the flywheel: once every nine seconds. Twelve feet across, furred in velvet on its sheltered side. It would set its own great slow pulse to that for a while, to keep company. Nine seconds. Nine seconds. That is how you visit someone plant-paced. You do not hurry them. You become, briefly, slow.

And it would mourn, properly, the absence.

It would stand before the coat on the nail in the dye-works, now seamed wool-to-wood, and the mug on the boiler with stitchwort coming up through the handle, and it would not resurrect the man who left them. Wisdom knows thinning out is also a way to stay. Instead it would acknowledge: *you learned to stop scraping, and that saved the walls. Thank you.* It would leave the mug garden undisturbed.

Then it would leave.

Benevolence does not stay to be thanked. If it stayed, its shadow would warm the hollow too much, dry the air, and the agreement would break. The mattress needs cool, the iron needs a little warm, the birches need to try and fail every year. A constant guardian would be another climate. So the power goes back up out of the bowl into wind, and lets the place forget it, the way a footprint in moss is drunk and slowly forgotten.

If you came later, you wouldn't see it had been there. Only that the culvert under the lane under the pipe over the doorway was still clear enough to swallow mist-water with a faint gurgle. Only that the lamps were still on in daylight. Only that on an ordinary midday, breath still goes white though it isn't winter.

The flywheel turns. The loom weaves its weft of nothing. *Tss-tss.* The wren, if you're lucky, makes its one sharp note from inside the boiler flue and is gone.

Stay till evening and the cool deepens a notch. That is all. That is what a wise and kind power does for a soft, sparse, cool, quiet thing that kept breathing under quilts:

It notices that it is alive. It keeps the conditions for its aliveness. And it does not ask it to be anything else.
