If I am benevolent, and if I am wise — which is to say, if I have learned how often kindness is just interference with better manners — then I do not come down like weather.

I come down like attention.

I find the slit. The lip in the forest that you forgot on the way in. I am very large to it, and so I make myself small, which for one like me does not mean shrinking. It means slowing. Slowing until my looking is no louder than frog-song, until my weight does not dent the moss.

The town inside the leaf — no, the town that decided, a long time ago, to be a town the way other plants decide to be leaves — feels me before it sees me. The whole street flinches, just once. A peristalsis passes end to end. Seven streets become eight become seven. The ceiling bellies and then pulls taut.

I stop at the threshold until it settles. That is the first thing I do. I wait to be un-feared.

Then I lie down. As much as I can lie down.

I do not bring stone. I do not bring dead wood, or tools, or plans. Nothing here is dead, and I will not be the first dead thing. I leave my outside-ness at the seam and keep it open with my breath — not propped, not forced, just breathed — so it does not heal shut behind me and seal me in, and so the town knows it can still exhale to the forest if it needs to.

For a long time — a light-shift, a birth-to-death of midges — I only listen.

Because you cannot help what you have not heard. And this place is deafening on purpose.

I let the roar separate. I let it sit on my sternum until my own pulse learns the key that keeps the roof taut. The cicada-pressure in the walls. The frog rounds overlapping. The ant-click, the wing-buzz dopplering through the fifty feet of trembling lung above me, the sap-hiss, the fiber-groan as the body shifts its weight, the plink-plink-plink of indoor rain.

I do not sing against the choir. Wisdom, here, is that simple. I find the old-timer frogs on their gutter-edge and I sing *under* them, a half-tone below, so low they barely notice, just enough to take some of the holding-up out of their throats for an hour while they rest. My voice is big enough to be a foundation and quiet enough not to be a leader. The house I am leaning against stops trembling.

That is the second thing I do. I help hold the roof up without being thanked.

I learn the other rules by keeping them. I do not dam the gutters. I watch the jewel beetles shoulder through after a rain, surly old tenants on their stoops, and I move my finger — which is a hill to them — out of their way. Let them own their stretch. I do not drink the cups. I lick moisture off the wall like a guest who knows which bowls are nurseries. The tadpole-things flick in their brown tea-water, resentful and perfect, and I bless them by not needing them.

I step over the ant highway. Even I step over it. Even the mantids won't break it, and I am less well-mannered than a mantid if I do.

I let myself be tasted by the cloth of midges. Ten thousand wings, grandchildren by noon. They settle on whatever skin I have shown. They taste. I let them. Commuters need to commute across something warm. I am, briefly, a bridge, a crowded alley for three minutes and then abandoned. That feels right.

At dusk-light, when the light through the avenue-veins goes indigo, I watch the moths pour out of the pitcher-rooms and take the void because the void has to be used or it sags. Their dust coats everything by morning. I do not sweep it. The mites sweep it. That is their polish-work, their seething invisible labor between paths that keeps the town from felting over. To do their work for them would be to unmake them.

I watch the screamers carve their endless circles up high, membrane wings and keel of chitin, mating and sleeping and dying on the wing, eating moths who fly anyway. I do not intervene in the eating. Hunger in balance is not cruelty. It is the tune. A mantid at the intersection takes a drunk beetle without breaking the choir — frogs singing right next to a feeding — and the ants clean the legs after, and the wasps tuck mantid egg-purses under fungus-shelf balconies like guarded purses. I witness it. I do not flinch for them. My benevolence is not to make predators gentle. It is to keep the whole chord in tune so no one voice has to scream to be heard.

Under the floor, blind and huge, the pruner moves. Slow, muscular, root-wise, saying no to growth so the midrib causeway does not heal shut, so the plaza stays polished pale green for dancing or fighting or both. No one ever sees it. I feel it pass beneath me and I press back, very lightly, a counter-pressure of thanks. Spilled sap, dead skin — I leave my own offerings without meaning to. The floor drinks immediately, swells, sighs.

At night I hear them: the sealed districts. Doors opening onto solid flesh, streets narrowing to scar. Traffic on the other side of the skin. Footsteps. A market. Something still living its urban life where the plant outgrew it or closed it off.

This is where wisdom has to sit on its hands.

Everything in me that is benevolent wants to open them. To reunite. To heal the scar into a street again. But I have lived long enough to know that a body closes things for reasons, and that larger does not mean more alive. The town remembers being larger. It does not grieve it. It listens to it at night the way you listen to ancestors in the walls. If I cut it open — even with love, especially with love — I would bring dead air into a living seal.

So I do not open. I do the harder thing. I sit with my back to the scar-street and I listen with the town to its own dead neighborhoods, and I keep time. I let there be mystery that is not a problem.

What do I do, then, if I do almost nothing inside?

I do my real work outside, where my largeness will not break gravity near the walls.

I shade the forest that holds this leaf-body. I turn aside the drought that would make dew fail on the ceiling and stop the indoor rain. I turn aside the spray, the clear-cut, the curious hand with a jar that wants to take a jewel beetle home and calls it saving. I make the outer air uninteresting to those who would love this place to death. I keep the basin of warm air settled and low. I keep a circle of un-noticing around it, so ten thousand generations of feet, claws, pads, bellies can keep the paths shiny without learning the sound of boots.

And when I must leave — for even attention cannot stay forever without becoming weight — I leave no monument. No cut edge. Nothing coaxed into a shape that holds my need.

Only this: a ceiling a fraction tauter, because it was sung with for a while. A gutter a fraction clearer, because something large moved carefully around it. A frog-choir a fraction rested. A root-pruner a fraction less lonely. A lens of clear sap in the ceiling that, for one light-shift, reflected not just a tiny figure mouth-open against the roar, blinking half a second late, but also something vast and quiet looking back up at them, blinking in time.

I do not fix the town. The town is not broken. It is crowded and empty and deafening all at once, and it likes it.

I keep it possible for it to go on liking it.
