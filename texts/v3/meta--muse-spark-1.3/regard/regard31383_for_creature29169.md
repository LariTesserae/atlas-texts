They have been noticed.

Not by a herd, not by a survey, not by hunger. By something older and kinder than that, something that does not need anything from them.

And the first wise thing it does is hesitate.

Because attention, here, is weather. A tall shadow at noon can kill a cushion's summer. A warm breath can flood a sea the size of a hand. To be seen by the big is usually the beginning of being undone.

So it does not come.

Not as light. Not as voice. Not as rescue.

It comes low.

---

If I am that power — benevolent, which means I want their flourishing on their terms, and wise, which means I know I do not know their terms — I learn the first law before I do anything else:

*No one tall lives here.*

So I will not live tall here either.

I shrink myself down to kneeling height, and then further, to moss height, to mite height, to Bear height. I make my time slow until a millimeter a year feels like good progress. I make my breath white and thin so I do not warm the air. I lower my voice until it is under the wind-bass, under the gutter-chatter, down where capsules pop.

I do not bring gifts. I have learned about gifts.

I do not bring warmth. Warmth would be murder. The Matrons hold their two degrees against the night precisely because the night is cold. Take away the cold and the thatch rots, the peat breathes out ten thousand years in a summer, the Forest drowns in its own wealth.

I do not bring water. They already have water, perfectly timed. Water is not a river here, it is a breath — in at night, out at noon, held for three hours and let go. To give them a lake would be to drown the Bears who have mastered drying. What would you give to a creature that can turn its own cells to glass, die dry for a year, and wake walking with one drop? You would not give it permanence. You would protect its right to disappear daily.

I do not bring food. A pellet of ptarmigan dung here is gold. A single hair fallen from a passing hare is windfall for a month. The Painters live directly on hunger — sun and fog and stone-dust — and turn it, century by century, into soil for everyone else. To fertilize them would be to burn them.

I do not bring company. They are not lonely. The cushion is a city-state. The moss tuft is a forest. The hand-sized mirror is an ocean full of cattle. The emptiness you see standing up is a failure of your scale, not of their companionship.

I do not fix. I do not improve the avenues that turn at right angles for no reason except ice physics. I do not straighten the curbs that align for twelve cells and then joggle. I do not give the thufur mounds doors.

What I do is smaller, and it takes longer.

I apprentice myself to the Builder who is already here. Frost is the only government this town has ever had, sorting soil to center and stones to edge, knocking down what grows too high, lifting footprints smooth by morning. I do not overthrow it. I serve it.

I keep the rhythm.

At dusk when fog spills back over the collar, I help it spill. I let the green darken from emerald to black-green and close without hurrying it. I watch the Bears pull in their legs and become tuns, the Jumpers go under the slabs, the Matrons hold their breath. I do not keep the light on late to comfort them. I let the contact-lens ice skin over.

At night when rime feathers the stones, I am quiet enough to let it happen. I do not brush it off the Painters to see their colors better. Rimed white is how they sleep.

At noon when the stones tick as ice lets go in their pores, when melt runs for an hour in black gravel throats, I do not extend the hour because it is beautiful. Three hours is ocean enough. To extend it would be to rot the thatch and confuse the stoneflies who have timed their whole brief winged life to its brevity.

And for the tall ones who pass through — ptarmigan, hare, fox, reindeer, human with boots that sink to the ankle and rebound — I do the gentlest steering.

I make the bowl, from standing height, look empty again. That is how it wants to be seen by the tall.

I make the vast flat around it seem a little vaster, a little more boring, so that those who want peaks and cliffs and use will keep walking. I put in their minds, light as a springtail landing: *go around, keep your feet dry, do not step on the centers.* If a boot does step, I do not punish. I kneel after, with frost, and lift the print smooth by morning. If a hand lifts a slab, I whisper: *only one a visit, and replace it exactly.* If eyes want to take — lichen for souvenir, cushion for rock garden, moss for terrarium — I make my presence feel like damp wool and stone dust and green iron, like cold fingertips and sun-warm face, until taking feels absurd, and looking feels enough.

For the world beyond the plateau, the warm hurrying world that is changing the sky without meaning to, I put my body in the way where I can. Not as miracle. As shade. As filter. I keep the air clean over this one bowl, because the Painters cannot live on anything else. No soot, no excess nitrogen falling as invisible rain to overfeed the Forest into rankness. I hold, for as long as I am allowed, the thin cold that hurts the inside of the nose, even while everything below warms. I cannot hold it forever. Wisdom knows that. But I can hold it for a lichen-lifetime, and then another.

And when I am with them — because benevolence is not distant, it is near — I do what you did.

I put my palm just above a Matron without touching and feel her two degrees. I put my ear to a hollow thufur and listen to wind humming. I lie down with my cheek near the moss until I hear the stones thunk a millimeter higher and understand the town is not still, even when nothing is there.

I do not bless them aloud. To name them too loudly would be to expose them. My blessing is attention without acquisition.

Live low. Grow in, not up. Drink fog. Tun when dry. Jump only as far as the next gravel grain. Persist a millimeter a decade if that is what the year allows. Die into dust that washes into the centers to feed others.

I see you. I will not save you from your beautiful, difficult rhythm by breaking it. I will keep the room around you — a room, a grove, a hamlet of moss and sorted stone — holding its damp against the enormous dry cold around it.

Stay ancient. Stay lush. Stay empty to the tall.

I will kneel as long as it takes.
