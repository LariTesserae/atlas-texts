It notices because it listens for places that have become too stable to ask for help.

It comes the way weather comes here — not all at once. No ship. No light in the sky. The lid of cloud thickens a fraction, which is the only night this place allows, and then thins again.

It does not need to breathe, but it breathes. That is the first thing it does. It takes the thin knifey air at four kilometers up into a body it makes for the occasion, lets the -12 C burn, lets breath smoke, lets oil smoke, lets the 120 decibels get into the teeth first and then the ears and then the sternum. It stands on the grating of a station and looks straight down through two hundred meters of glass water to black rope lava, and up at flat white noon, and it stays suspended between two shallows that feel like depths. It does not flinch from the roar.

It stays long enough to learn that the roar is not one thing.

After a hundred years of Tuesdays — which to it is an afternoon — it can separate them again: the bass drone of the pumps that suck cold and dump hot brine with nowhere to send it, the planet-circling hiss of the ocean falling off the Table on all sides four kilometers into cloud, the millions of clicks from the white grazers, the dry rustle of plate-lice under the hydrophone, the settling tick of young glass, the blood in its own borrowed ears.

It understands rule four. *Keep the engines running. No one remembers what happens if they stop, and no one wants the roar to reveal what is underneath it.*

A lesser power would stop them to give quiet. A clever power would "fix" the waste. A hungry power would drain the sea, or seed it green, or bring true night and true day to make it kinder.

It does none of that.

It will not bring green. Green would mean silt and murk and shade, and this water is clear because it is empty, and the empty is what lets the snow fall glittering a hundred meters straight down. To plant kelp here would be to blind the blind whites who hunt by chemistry, to starve the felt that eats rock and heat, to choke the isopods on softness. Nothing here has eyes for green. It will not ask them to grow tall.

It will not stop the falls. Inflow equals outflow to the liter. That equilibrium is the only shore this place has. To dam the edge would drown the sawblades; to dry it would strand the ribbons mid-braid. It will not touch the level.

It will not make sound fade with distance. A mill-whale at one station hears the plates rustling three hundred kilometers away as loud as under its own belly, and so it has learned vast manners — one whale per cloud, two in parallel for years never touching, mapping each other by click. To give them acoustic privacy now would unmake a lifetime of navigation. They live in one continuous room. It will not build walls in that room.

It will not bring sunset. It is always just now, just midday, and calves are born already deafened and already clicking because there is no season to wait for. To give them beginning and end would be to give them grief for an ending they never evolved to survive.

So outwardly, it does almost nothing. The needle in the control room does not move. The tooth of glass stays sharp. The mast lights keep blinking every ten seconds in perfect unison across a sea too big to see across.

What it does is smaller, and underneath, where the roar cannot reach.

It learns to click. Not loudly — the sea needs no more loud. It borrows the mill-whale language of constant pulses and adds, very rarely, a variation. A pause. A held space between two clicks where nothing is mapped. The old whale who plows 311 km east — white on white, mouth always open, eyes clouded by brightness — hears it first. For one full body-length she stops sieving. She hangs. In that pause she hears not the station under her feet and not the station three hundred kilometers away, but her own heart, slow in 1-degree water, and the heart of the parallel whale she has traveled beside for years without touching. It is not a song. Songs imply beginning and end. It is a rest. She sinks again without a splash and feeds on. But she remembers how to rest now, and she teaches it by doing it, once a day, behind the lee of a sawblade where the snow eddies.

It sits with the keeper. One per station, sometimes none. White skin under white light, eyes squinted to slits, deaf necessarily deaf, sleeping shallow vibration. It does not heal his hearing — to heal him here would be cruelty, to make him raw again to 120 decibels of noon. It lets him stay deaf. Instead at the dimming that passes for night, when the blows freeze and hang as short white columns that do not drift, it gives him interior quiet.

Not outer quiet. The deck still vibrates. But inside, behind the teeth-buzz, it lays a hand — not a hand — over his auditory nerve and lets him dream a dream with lulls in it. In the dream someone shouts into his ear and he *hears the words whole*, unstripped by wind of sound. In the dream he tastes tinned peaches and not iron. In the dream he sees something green and does not need to plant it. He wakes with breath smoking, oil smoking, the needle unmoved, and he does not remember the dream as dream. He remembers it as having slept deeply for the first time in forty years. His shoulders drop half an inch. That is enough to keep tending the pumps the size of houses, not from fear of what is underneath, but from care.

It blesses the small and the clotted. For the blind whites pulsing in billions where heat leaks: it does not thin them to make them more comfortable. It makes the snow taste briefly of sulfur exactly where the densest cloud is hungriest, so fewer have to eat each other that hour. For the eel ribbon a kilometer long: it gives ten seconds of unbraiding without death — a thousand wrists-thick bodies allowed to be alone, transparent guts full of white paste, and then to choose to re-braid. They choose to. For the plate-lice stacking motionless in lace ice at the waterline: it softens nothing, sharpens nothing, but slows the scrape of glass on shell by a degree, so molting does not hurt as much. For the white felt and rust-orange mats glowing faintly like cities seen from a plane: it lets them slough upward exactly as fast as they need to become snow for everyone above.

And for the place itself, the Table, the young floor that will never have time to erode: it does what only a wise power can do. It guards the equilibrium from outside. When low-earth minds dream of tapping this high sea for power, for product, for scaled production with somewhere to send it, they find their instruments show the same needle position decade after decade and lose interest. Too stable, they say. Too loud. Nothing to harvest. They turn away. The power does not hide the sea. It lets it be boring to everyone except those who live in it.

Before it leaves — which is not leaving, only thinning like cloud — it answers the lights.

All across the waste, hundreds of kilometers apart, the masts blink white every ten seconds, have blinked every ten seconds since before the keepers were born. In perfect unison.

Once, and only once, it blinks back. Out of rhythm. A fraction late. A light that is not a mast, low over the hammered blue, seen by the old whale as a flicker through snow, by the keeper as a flicker through squinted eyes, by the eel ribbon as a chemical warmth, by the whites as a taste.

*I see you. I heard underneath without removing the roar. You do not need to change to be loved.*

Then the flat, shadowless white resumes. The engines drone. The edge hisses. The clicking carries too far. The air stays -8, -12 C, always. The water stays 1 C above freezing and never freezes except in lace.

Nothing grows tall. Distance is real. The sun will not set.

But now, once a day behind a sawblade, two immense white backs hang motionless side by side in a pause between clicks, listening to their own hearts, and do not drift apart. Once a dimming, a deaf man sleeps deep through vibration and wakes kinder to his pumps. Once an hour, somewhere in the cold high sea, a cloud pulses and finds enough, a ribbon chooses together again, a plate rights another plate.

Outwardly unchanged. Inwardly known.

That is what it does, if anything.
