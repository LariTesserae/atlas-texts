It climbs up like anyone else has to.

That is the first thing it decides. Not to arrive as light, not to part the midge-smoke or still the reed-wheeze. To come up out of the lowlands through a day of switchbacks, breath coming short, legs burning, and to step over the rim of pale stone onto the flat and, for a half-second, to be fooled.

*Oh. Empty.*

Its boot sinks half an inch into copper moss and it understands, with a small shock of delight in its soles: no. Not empty. Covered. Totally covered.

So it sits down to learn weight.

For a long time - what is time to such a one - it does nothing except what the boy on the four-pole platform does. It watches. It listens with lowland ears until they become, slowly, high ears.

It hears the drum under the thud. It learns to dread, as the sheep do, the dip in the chorus. It feels the noon roar get into its teeth until it too wants a twist of rolled felt. At dawn it squeezes moss with cracked hands it borrows for the occasion, wrings, catches in a cup-lichen, drinks. It tastes iron and sun and thousand-year humus. It sweeps with the women in the white morning, net between poles, through pillars of midges rising off the breathing holes like smoke. It goes down, because it must know, on a knotted ladder ten, twenty feet into the cool throat where cold air sighs up, where guano slicks the ledge and the blind white crickets sing without ceasing. It presses a paw - a hand - flat to the felt as the shaft-fox does, and waits to feel steps coming from a hundred yards off through the weave.

It is tempted. Any love is tempted.

It is tempted to quiet it, a little. To turn down the sawing at noon so the children do not have to wad their ears, so a bone whistle can be heard ten feet away. To still the million tiny flutes.

It is tempted to water it, properly. To give them a lake, a river that stays past ten o'clock, so the women do not have to rise before light to drink morning, so the geese do not have to thunder down into an inch.

It is tempted to harden it. To fill the honeycomb under the skin with good safe rock, so no lamb, no child ever learns the deep whistling boom of mat settling over void, so no new mouth opens in the dark. To let them walk heavy at last.

It is tempted to arm them. A vein of iron near the rim. Real tools. Roofs that do not need trimming with a bone knife. Ladders that do not fray.

It holds each temptation up to the light, the ordinary gold-white noonday light, and looks through it to what would follow.

If it quiets: the harrier, who hunts by the silence between waves, starves. The sheep, who freeze at the dip, lose their signal. The fox, who listens for steps through felt, loses the cover of roar.

If it waters: the moss that drinks condensation and holds it as delay rots. The breathing holes that gulp the brief rain and echo waterfalls far below clog. The whole agreement - not abundance but capture and delay - breaks.

If it hardens: there is no sigh of cold air at noon, no warm suck at dusk. No roost in the dark where thousands of swifts cling safe from fox. No white crickets fat on detritus. No skin that breathes. Only a table.

If it arms: hands stop being hands. Cut turf is not replaced with a stone, because why bother when you can cut more. A tear, left, widens yards in a season. A whole pasture slumps whistling into the dark.

It sees this because it is wise. It refrains because it is benevolent, and benevolence is not the same as relief. Relief would unmake them.

So it does not do the large things.

It does small things, and one large non-thing.

The small things, so small no herder would call them miracles:

On a midmorning when the mirrors have evaporated and the light is ordinary again, a ewe lamb nose-to-tail behind the old ewes steps toward a lush green circle - the greenest green, fed by updraft damp, thinnest over a throat. The power, lying low in the reed-grass sideways as everything here lies low, exhales just there. The insect chorus dips a half-second early. Every sheep freezes. The lamb freezes. The old ewe swings her head. Single file, the long way around, along the thick spine. *Tok tok* goes the boy's staff on the platform leg, feeling the mat answer that day.

In a shaft at dusk, a boy down the ladder too late, skin bag half-filled drip by drip, hears his fiber creak. Not yet worn through, but close. The power puts a hand - it has learned to have almost-hands, like the fox's forepaws - under the knot. Holds, until he is up into amber light. He will never know. He will re-knot it in the morning and think his mother tied it better than he remembered.

At night, when fog pours up out of the breathing holes smelling of wet stone and resin and guano, and an old woman banks the fire and checks the copper awl in its hide wrap, treasured, re-sharpened to a needle - the power sits a while in the half-sunk hut where the living roof tries to seal the smoke hole. It does not mend her nets. It does not sharpen for her. It keeps her one unplugged ear company, so that when the mat settles far out in the dark with that deep boom, she does not start awake alone. Someone else heard it too.

And where a family cut three layers deep and remembered, as children are taught before names, to lay a flat stone so the wind cannot get a finger under the skin - the power lays, beside theirs, its own weight. Give back weight, they say. It can do that. It is very good at weight, choosing to be light.

That is all. A breath early. A knot held. A night witnessed. A stone.

The large non-thing is this:

It makes the place remain empty on first sight.

It knows what attention brings. After it, other attentions will come up the switchbacks - surveyors with maps, lowlanders counting guano by the ton, water by the acre-foot, fiber by the bale, mouths to be capped, hollows to be mined, a road to be cut straight across the breathing skin because it looks flat and therefore easy.

To those eyes, the power lends its own first half-second and stretches it.

*Oh. Empty.*

Let them see no tree to hold a nest, no back moving, no standing figure. Let the wind comb the sideways reeds until they wheeze and let them hear only wind. Let the heat shiver the horizon so distance has no measure. Let their boots sink half an inch and let them think it mud. Let them turn back at the rim, bored, deafened, thirsty by afternoon, saying there is nothing up here worth the climb.

A blessing of continued invisibility. The best camouflage is the truth misread.

In the morning there will be white. Condensation will bead every leaf. The pans will mirror for an hour. The midges will rise smoking off every mouth. The tornadoes will pour up screaming into ordinary gold-white light, shadow the moss, feed, and at dusk pour back down wing-on-wing until the sky empties.

The boy will climb to his platform with knees tucked. *Tok tok.* Thud, not boom. Thick here. Safe to pass.

The sheep will walk toe-first, as if the world were an instrument. Freeze. Go on.

The fox will press its paw to the felt and listen. Sometimes it will eat. Sometimes it will be out-listened. That is agreement, not cruelty.

The hollow reeds will play slightly out of tune under everything. The shafts will tick and drip. The white crickets will chirp endlessly in the dark.

And from rim to far blue edge the skin will look empty again - until you step, and remember it is totally, deafeningly covered in lives that have agreed, like the old mountains, to be flat, to be light, to be hollow and hold.

The power, having given back its weight, holds its breath with them under ordinary stars, and waits for morning to begin again.
