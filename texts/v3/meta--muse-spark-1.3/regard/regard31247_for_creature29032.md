It would not come down in light.

There is already too much light here for light to mean anything — that small worn sun sitting sideways, never rising, never setting. So it would come the way weather comes. A change in pressure. A long quiet before the roar returns.

At first it would make the same mistake you made. It would think: nothing lives here.

It has oceans behind its teeth. It has rain in its hands. That is what benevolent powers carry, usually. Greening. Easing. It would look at the ash-grey and rust-black crisp stretching ridge to basin, at the far-spaced candelabra of the Tower-Stalks, at the thin air and the thin people in their beetle-masks, and its heart would ache in the old way. *Poor dry things. Let me help. Let me weep for them. Let me open the sky.*

And then, being wise, it would listen before it wept.

It would crouch — because here even a god has to crouch to get inside — through a coaxed throat into Kettlehollow, into the warm dark where Mat-grafts sleep next to human babies in the same ultrasound hum. It would hear the shepherd-machines singing *thicken here, thin here.* It would smell iodine and warm salt and body heat recaptured. It would see the girl.

She is seven. Dust-lidded. Belly-down on the living floor in the quietest room in a loud world, ear pressed to the crackle. Listening for the wrong softness under the right crackle.

It would get down beside her. Low and wide to spread its weight so it does not break through. And it would ask, not in words, because she learns hum before language:

*What do you need?*

And the Mat would answer first, because the Mat is the oldest resident, the being who is also the place. Not in words either. In tick as it cools. In the thousand tiny settling cracks when the wind drops. In mint-cold sparks along its underside. It would say what it always says to those who stay still long enough:

*Do not bring water.*

*I have etched basalt one grain at a time for longer than your mountains remember being tall. I have learned to eat rock and static and low sideways light. I have made a forest that is a floor that is a field that breathes without drinking. Water would wake the black mold that eats both plant and lung. Your pity would rot me from the inside.*

*Do not make open flame.*

*Everything I am is tinder that never got wet. Heat lives only in stone boxes and glass.*

And the girl, without lifting her head, would add the third rule with her hands: *click-drag-tug.* She would clip her twenty meters of filament to the god's wrist, matter-of-fact as family. *Stay tethered when the filaments rise. The wind that feeds us will take you just as easily.*

A benevolent power that is not wise would have rained anyway. A wise power that is not benevolent would have left.

This one would learn to swallow its rain.

It would sit for a season in the blue hour that is every hour. It would leave its oceans parked far out past the rim where offworlders have their water taken. It would drink dust-tea — salty, warm, not water, never water — and let it prickle its tongue with charge. It would wear no glory that could shake loose. It would file its teeth, so the hum of the Tuning Forks does not shake them loose. It would breathe out and take its breath back on cold plates, like a bellows-mask.

It would learn to grieve dryly. That is the hardest part. To love a people who have made themselves into animals that do not leak — urine baked to powder, breath recaptured, tears forbidden — and to love them without leaking onto them. Even its weeping, it would catch and return.

What would it *do*, then?

Almost nothing. The way the Mat forgives, slowly.

It would walk the rim at night-that-is-always, vast and unnoticed, and turn aside the one wet stone that was falling — an ice-hearted rock, centuries off, meaning no harm, that would have burst white and sweet over the peneplain and killed a valley with kindness. It would nudge it sideways with a fingertip of gravity, the way you nudge a child's tether. No one would see. The dust in the gravity-seams would shiver into a new pattern, and the needle-birds nesting on the Forks would ornament the shiver into song: *sii-sii-sii-SUUU.*

It would press its forehead, once, to the black glass of the Fork that had drifted sharp, the way the Fork-tender does. Not to fix it — the tenders know their note — but to lend it weight, to hold the note steady while she retunes. Two hundred meters tall, too thin to be standing. It would hum with it in its molars until the dust hung still.

It would go with the pruners when the girl marks with ash and hums the pitch. When they cut with stone knives and cure with glass-box heat, sacrificing a room to save a cluster, it would hold the edges. It would not spare them the cut. It knows a managed infection needs pruning. It would stay for the sealing, and not look away.

Mostly it would lie belly-down beside the listener, ear to the crisp, learning the difference between settle and rot, between right crackle and too-sweet sour. Lending her its larger quiet, so she can hear farther under. A god's ear is a good resonance chamber. The goats would cough dust around them. The cask-beetles would tick as they cool. The Tower-Stalk filaments would lift like hair on an arm, and together they would feel the roar rolling toward them across kilometers of living crisp, and together they would clip on and wait for it to take them and not take them.

When it left — because even powers do not stay, they thin — it would not leave a temple. No roads. No foundations. People here do not build on the Mat.

It would leave a bruise-trough. A long dark passage where something very heavy and very gentle lay for a while, spores puffing white then knitting. For weeks the goatherds would lead their bellows-goats around it, respectful. Then the Mat would decide to forgive it, as it forgives all true passage.

And under the floor, where no one lifts the edge except children who know to look, it would leave a steadier mint spark. Cold light, there and gone. *Someone lay here and listened and did not spill.*

That is what love does where water is death: it does not rain.
