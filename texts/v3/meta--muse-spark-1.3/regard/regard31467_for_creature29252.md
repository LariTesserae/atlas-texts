It would come up slowly.

There is no other way. Even a power like that — especially a power like that — would take the rail-crawl. The better part of a day, switchbacking, learning to breathe the way they breathe: sip in through the nose, out through pursed lips, like soup too hot. It would not arrive in fire or cool the air or still the ground. It knows better than to announce itself in a place where shouting shreds and goes nowhere.

First, it would listen.

It would sit in a machine hall in the shade through the white hours, when handrails brand you and the rule is Rest, and do nothing. That is harder than it sounds for a power. But it would learn from the engine-tenders, the slowest of all, whose skilled work is listening — ear to a standpipe in the evening to hear tomorrow's water condensing, drop by patient drop. It would listen alongside them until it could tell which bearing groans low in the molars, which expansion joint clicks, which coil is beginning to foul.

It would not fix the mountain.

This is a mountain still being born. Inches a year, patient swelling. Doors jamming. Survey chalk wrong every spring. A wise power knows you do not stop a birth to save the house built on the belly. You hold both. So at night, while the basalt pings as it contracts and dripwater patters into tin, it would do only the smallest midwifery: ease pressure where pressure gathers too sharply, let a warm crack breathe where a crack wants to breathe, keep the upwelling from hurrying. Nothing the jack-men with their shims and jackscrews would notice as miracle. Just a morning when fewer doors stick than expected.

It would not bring water.

That is the temptation, and it would refuse it. No river struck from rock, no cool spring in the lowland sense. Because here water is not a resource. It is a relation. Your neighbor's jar depends on your roof. Keep your drip-line clear. To spill is to insult the cloud that bothered to stop. If you flood that with abundance, you wash away the carefulness that made these people kind. What lives here has stayed, and staying has taught them to hand a stranger a heavy, cool, white-painted jar with both hands and watch to see if he spills.

So instead it would protect the hour.

That hour after dawn when the peak snags the cloud and everything sweats — sails, pipes, cold frames — before the sun burns through. The children's hour, barefoot gangs with squeegees herding beads into gutters that chatter like rain, unplugging drip-bags with a practiced pinch. The power would keep that hour faithful. It would nudge the high winds, night after night, just enough that the cloud still catches. It would not make it longer — by eight it stops, as if a tap were closed, and the stone must steam dry by nine — but it would not let it be stolen. The thin sky above and the upwelling rock below have an old agreement; the power would witness it and keep it.

It would not bring green.

It knows the chemistry is wrong and the heat is wrong and the stone is too new to have learned how to host anything green. What looks like moss is sulfur bloom, copper salts, obsidian sheen. A lesser kindness would scatter seed and watch it die to prove its pity. This one would wait. And when, in some cool seep under a north wall, in fifty years or two hundred, the first true lichen dares — green-black stain, first softness — it would bend very close, closer than anyone else has thought to look, and be glad. It would shade that spot with a little extra shadow for one white noon, so it does not crisp. No one would see. That is enough.

What it would do, mostly, is tend the living things at the edges, the ones the town already tends without making a fuss about it.

The pale, almost translucent lizard under the cooling sheds, belly cool on ceramic, licking condensate off pipes. Good for the coils, bad to name. The power would keep a pipe dripping for it in the dry weeks.

The swifts, very high, circling the thermal for hours without flapping, crying once thinly before they drop away. The power would keep that thermal steady, a warm column to ride, so they can stay up there as long as they want.

And the dog. Or the story of a dog. Gone feral in the ash fields, living on scraps and drip. Children leave jar lids of water out past the last terrace just in case. Whether it exists hardly matters. The power knows it exists, because something drinks there in the night with a quick, frightened tongue. It would go after the children have gone, in the amber light when black glass turns to bronze, and refill those lids. Not a miracle. Just a full lid where an empty one was. The gesture tells you everything. Even a power wants to be the kind of person who leaves water for something that may not exist.

For the people, it would leave their dignity intact.

It would not straighten the leaning doorway in the Upper Tier plaza, plumb twenty years ago, now six degrees north. People touch it for luck on the way to the kilns. Not prayer, exactly. Acknowledgment. *The mountain is working slower than you, but it is stronger.* The power would touch it too, on its way through, and leave the tilt.

It would not make the air thick or cool, or the sun less white and level at noon, or the mirage-city stop floating a hand's breadth above its foundations. The old ones say the mountain dreams its future shape in the air. The power would sit with them under the canvas sun-sails during the saturated, held noon, and dream it with them for a while, to make sure the future shape still has room for roofs touching roofs, for steam traps and cistern cellars, for rows of ingots cooling from cherry to black over days.

If you were watching — you, with your lowland mistake, expecting it to *do* something — you would say it did nothing.

It spoke low. It walked slow. One hand trailing, not touching, the warm handrail. It waited to be offered water, and when the household that decided it could be trusted handed over the jar, it drank carefully, with both hands, not spilling a drop.

And later, you would find small evidences, the way children find their handprints lifted an inch higher a month later:

A coil that should have scaled shut, clear.
A jack-screw that held through a swell.
A cloud that came, again, on a morning the wind should have taken it elsewhere.
A lid of water, past the last terrace, full.

Inches a year. Drop by patient drop. That is how a benevolent and wise power works, when it loves a mineral town that ticks.
