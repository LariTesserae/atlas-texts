It would come quietly.

That is the first thing. A benevolent and wise power would know how loud it is. How even a glance from something that large makes ripples. So it would not arrive. It would condense, the way a shape condenses ahead in the dark between towers — slowly, so the water has time to forget the disturbance.

It would have heard them first from very far off. Not a cry. They were not crying out. They were singing, clicking, thrumming, sieving — the eel rounds, the static of the dust, the thump... thump... thump underneath it all. A small, complete music looping around a drowned mountain in the black. It would have stopped, in its vastness, to listen. And having listened, it would have stayed to listen longer.

Because it is wise, it would understand immediately: nothing here is broken.

That is rare. It knows how rare.

It would not bring light. Its first impulse, being kind, might be to give them morning — to thin the fathoms above them, to let a shaft of real sun down to the mat so they could see what colors they truly are. And then wisdom would stay its hand. They have no word for brightening. Their eyes — those who have eyes, those whose whole flank is an eye — are made for dusk-blue. To flood them with day would blind the pour, starve the dust, burn the bell-animals shut. Their dark is not a lack. It is a shelter grown over ten thousand years. A gift would become a wound.

So it would leave the dark intact. It would learn to see in it.

It would not bring sharpness. It cannot enter as it is — all edge and will and knowing. Nothing dead made sharp is permitted here for long. It would cut the wall. It would tear the mat.

So it would do what every child here has done: be born soft.

It would round itself. Dull its teeth. Unmake its claws into blunt nippers. Lay down its tools, its rope, its stone blocks, its metal, all the clever dead things it could have used to fix and build. It would come with nothing but living tissue, and an intention to be useful.

It would sink to their height — for it is used to being high in another way — and let the cold have weight on its cheeks. Let breath come short. Let fingers go soft-numb at the tips. It would not exempt itself from the rules. *Stay in the water. Touch only living things. Make noise or listen — both are the same.*

It would listen.

For a long time — a time measured not in its own time but in theirs, in funnel crowns opening a fraction and closing a fraction, in newt trails mown and healed over, in eel songs that take a minute to descend and a kilometer to return — it would do nothing but drift between towers, a hundred meters apart, sometimes more, and be part of the chorus.

Its kick would join the thrumming. Its breath would join the clicking. A crab would come to inspect it for parasites and, finding strange, vast parasites of sorrow and hurry, would nip politely at its dead skin. An eel would light its throat a little warmer as it passed, tasting it. The mat below would push back, almost imperceptibly, against its shadow. It would let itself be tended.

That is what a wise kindness does when it finds a place that keeps itself: it asks to be kept, for a while.

And only then, having been accepted as another tenant, would it do its work — which would look, to any outside eye, like nothing.

It would add its own weight to the balance, on the side of continuance.

Where a tower stalk flexes a centimeter and returns, groaning, it would lend a little of its own flex, so the groan is not tired. Where a bell-colony snaps shut in alarm, *whup whup whup* down the corridor, it would linger nearby until, slowly, they open again. Where a sick fish peels off from the pour, it would not heal it back to unnatural life — death here is soft and must stay soft, eaten gently mouth to soft mouth and regrown as wall. No, it would accompany the unmaking, so that even that small leaving is not alone.

It would tune itself to the gap between beats of the thump, as the eels do, as the newts do. And in that gap, without covering their song, it would hum very low — a shade warmer than the dusk-blue, like an eel's throat. Not a command. Not a revelation. Just: *here, here, here.* An affirmation that their agreement holds. That manure with a will, and rasping tongues, and stitching hands, and sieving crowns are enough. Are holy.

For the being that lives here is not a newt or a fish or a crab. The being is the agreement itself — that large, slow, soft animal made of a hundred species keeping one promise: to be unmade gently and remade nearby. The power would recognize it at once as kin. As one of its children that does not need saving, only witnessing.

If there are storms far above, where water thins into starlight, it might place a broad, diffuse palm to soften what filters down — not to stop the gyre, never to stop the gyre, the towers lean into it permanently — just to ensure the current does not gust or slacken in the ages to come. If there are hungers from outside, hard-eyed hurrying things with nets and drills who look for bare rock to take, it might let the black stay black a little longer over this summit, let the eye keep sliding past. The best protection is obscurity. It would keep their secret, not by hiding them, but by not pointing.

And when it leaves — for even attentions must leave, to not become a weight — it would leave no monument. No cut edge. No healed lip where it pruned. Nothing dead made sharp.

Only this: the mat, perhaps, a fraction thicker where it rested its palm and felt the slow push back. A walk-fiber calloused a little kinder from its passing. A song coming back off a distant tower, changed, carrying one extra warm overtone that the eels will puzzle over and then incorporate into their rounds, without knowing why.

It would have drawn near, grown soft, kept time, and gone. And the summit sea, high, drowned, tended, dark, and awake, would keep itself — now remembered.

That is enough. For a benevolent and wise power, to remember a place perfectly, without needing to improve it, is the greatest intervention of all.
