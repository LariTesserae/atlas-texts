It would come up light.

That is the first wisdom. No thunder, no opening in the sky. Hands on cold, lichen-rough rock like anyone else, lungs working harder than the slope warrants. It would crest the ridge and stop, to get its breath back, and let the place un-empty itself from the ground up.

It would not bring more air. It would not level the rock or widen the bowl or make the season longer. It knows smallness is what makes this possible at all. Sixty strides across, a hand curled over a candle — enlarge it and the wind gets in. Ease it and the bargain breaks.

So it would kneel.

Down where the sponge sighs water up around the boot, down at their height, breath just visible toward shade, back of the neck hot with sun. It would make itself low and sideways, the way everything here lives. And it would listen twice, because here everything comes twice — whistle and then the rock throwing the whistle back, drip and then drip again from another direction. It would take the second hearing as its work: to hear fully.

It would do almost nothing. That is the benevolence.

It would not stop the eagle. It has watched him ride with almost no wingbeat out over the void, peat-and-sun colored, eye fixed, reading the sponge below. It loves him and it loves the pika, and it will not choose between them or apologize for hunger. To take the circler away would be to unmake the fastness and lowness that keeps everything alive. The bones under the plucking perch on the rim cairn are manure too. The shadow crossing the moss campion domes is holy. When the pikas dive and the bees lift in a single copper shimmer and the marmot chuck cuts off mid-call — it would hold still inside that stopping, not prevent it, and stay with how fast all small hearts are beating under stone.

It would not still the wind that tears over the top with a low organ roar. It would not dry the seep or gather the film and thread of water into a proper river. Water not in a river is the rule here. Beads on the undersides of overhangs falling in slow bright ticks, dark damp tongues where the moss is black-green and saturated, the tarn no bigger than a room, clear-black, with grit and fallen petals and midge skeletons on the bottom — it would drink only where it runs clear off rock, not where it stands. It would leave the film as film.

What would it do, then, with all that power?

Small, exact things. The kind you could miss.

It would cup its hand a little further over the candle when the afternoon storm comes up from nothing — not stop the rain, the place needs rain, it is still draining the last shower — but curl the worst gust just enough that the swift nest glued with spit in the slit above the seep holds. That the cottongrass heads are not stripped before seed. That the hayrick under the overhang, grasses and pink campion petals still giving off sweet cut smell, does not blow.

It would sit a while with the old marmot female, grizzled as lichen, sides heaving fast in the thin air, bellied to rock that has stored noon. It would lend her breath without changing it. Pace for pace. Sun hot, then shadow cold wicking up from wet ground. Nothing said.

It would notice the one. Out of the crowded loud — hum and cheep and kyaah-kyaah thrown back from wet rock — it would pick out the pika with a mouth so full of green it cannot close, stems sticking out like whiskers, scolding from three rocks away. It would remember him. Not save him for winter — winter will take most of them, that is written in the short glut — but remember him so completely that his cutting and carrying, turned and re-turned through three brief weeks of white saxifrage and yellow cinquefoil and violent pink, is not lost when snow comes. Attention as a haypile that does not rot.

When a copper-bright mountain bee, furred against cold, bumps a warm hand and clings for a second before going to the next flower open all at once, it would let her warm. That much. And when one of her dead drifts in the coldest seam of the tarn, larvae wriggling below, it would watch that too. Not look away.

It would stay through the weather turning twice before afternoon. Get wet. Let condensation breathe off the rocks ankle-high and be torn away the instant it lifts above the rim. Let the choughs with red beaks like fresh wounds tumble and scream and pry for beetles in dung, leaving thin three-toed prints around the tarn that fill slowly with water. Let the stunted true ferns unfurl where the rock weeps. Let the moss campion domes, hard enough to stand on, decades old, bloom all around the edge in pink stars because there may be no second chance.

And before it went down, it would do what the others did who came up light and stayed briefly. Hand work. No metal.

It would right one slipped slate on the low collapsed windbreak. Re-stack a fallen stone on the rim cairn. Touch, but not freshen, the bleached stick with rotted cloth. Leave the soot-blackened overhang, the ring of stones, the split bird bone and flake of chipped chert in cold ash exactly as they are. Stone arranged to remember wind by.

No signature. No staying. These others stayed — pressed themselves flat, furred themselves thick, learned to whistle twice, to sleep under stone and hunt over nothing. The power knows it is a visitor. To remain would be to shade the sunniest rubble.

So it would stand at the open edge for a last look out at bright, hollow air to the far ranges, the single green coin balanced on emptiness behind it. It would leave the hollow holding sound a half-second too long, and the water sighing, and the work going fast.

That would be enough. That it was fully seen, for one thin-aired noon, and not fixed.
