They would come small.

That is the first thing wisdom would teach even to a power. For this place cannot be entered large. From the outside it is a ten-minute circle of bramble and blackthorn, a cool dip where cold air pools. From the inside it is vast. If you come bringing sky, bringing noon, bringing river to put under the iron bridge, bringing people to fill the terraces — you will break it. The walls are mostly air. The quiet is structural. The light here gives up after a few feet on purpose.

So they would lay their bigness down at the rim, like a coat, and stoop.

A benevolent and wise power does not arrive with announcement. They know the second rule: sound attenuates like light. A shout does not echo; it is absorbed, and everything turns toward you at once. They would not want that fear. They would wait at the thorn boundary until their breath fogged and faded in the cool, until their breathing fell into rhythm with the drip — *plik... plik... plik* — and they were no longer an intrusion, too warm, too fast, too loud. They would learn to see by touch and by drip. They would learn to see by movement.

Then they would enter on foot, and it would take hours, as it should.

They would not count. They know counting fixes and the place resents it. Seven chimneys one way and nine coming back is not a mistake to correct. A street that grows an extra house overnight that is both ruined and newly grown is not a glitch. Time pooling until a moth is still landing yesterday is not broken time. It is plant logic solving town problems. How to be both small and vast? Be a seed. How to span a void? Grow across it thinly. A wise power would not teach it town logic back. It would let it stay botanical.

They would not bring light. They might be light, elsewhere. Here they would dim themselves until they were only an orange bruise like the moss-furred sodium lamp on the rusted pole. They would sit under the hundred-deep vaults of burdock and nettle leaves, black-green and dripping, that unfold not to catch sun but to catch condensation and sound, and they would let the leaves turn toward them very slightly, as they turn toward the dead lamp, and that would be enough worship for both sides.

They would listen first to the crowd that is one body.

Not to fix the surveyors — the forearm-long slugs leaving silvered census trails across the moss streets — but to read, without needing to understand, who went where in the night, who is rotting, who is ready to be re-knit. Not to scatter the pale woodlice that have become mortar, holding the softened brick courses together with their bodies. Just to lift a leaf gently and put it back.

Not to cure the thin fox, rust-red gone green with algae at the tips, town that grew a fox to hunt rats that hunt moths that hunt spores. A lesser kindness would want to fatten it, to warm it, to make it clean fox again. Wisdom would see it is exactly as thin and quiet as a library-hollow requires, and would simply be still enough that the fox decides, on its own, to pass close. To be seen by a fox like that is permission.

Not to pity the blind white eels in the cellar ponds two feet across, in the third-floor bathtub full of black tea and larvae. They would know the huge blackberries shining like oil out of mortar are not for them, not for any visitor. The town's way of remembering sweetness. They would watch one fall and roll downhill that is not downhill and plop, and watch the white mouths rise, and they would not take one. They would let sweetness stay in circulation where it belongs.

Not to restart the machines. The great sap-wheel with mossed paddles, the mycelium belts moving looms a half-inch per hour weaving air, the heartwood piston chuffing faintly on fermentation gas — these factories do not produce, they maintain, they are hearts. A foolish power would oil them, speed them, electrify the sagging wires beyond a weak phosphor bead and a 50 hertz hum. A wise one would put an ear close to the wire, as you do, and hear the singing, and leave the voltage low, harvested from decay, enough to keep going and no more. Enough is a holy quantity here.

At last they would go to the deepest hollow, under the iron footbridge that bridges no river, where the moss is thinnest over sharp, newborn shale and cinder and clinker, all edges, the world still deciding what a hill is. They would kneel — powers can kneel — and put their ear to the raw under-rock, and hear the whole small-vast borough working: sap lifting inches per night, roots tightening on brick, water beading not flowing, moth wings dusting down to be caught by black leaves, eels turning, the slow cold engine taking another breath. A town holding its breath to stay empty enough to stay alive.

And what would they do, having heard it?

Almost nothing. Which is everything.

They would breathe with it. Once every few minutes, with the engine house. In, out. Cold, clear, smelling faintly of iron. Lending their breath without taking over the rhythm.

They would keep it empty. That is the strangest mercy, and the most necessary. The place stayed alive by staying empty of people after its matter left. It does not need to be repopulated, redeveloped, remembered loudly, put on maps. The benevolent power would stand back up and, on the way out, thicken the bramble circle just slightly. Blur the path. Make walkers feel, for no reason they can name, that this is a place to pass by, not to enter with boots and shouts and plans. Make the ten minutes from outside stay ten minutes, a nothing hollow, uninteresting. Hide a seed by letting it look like nothing.

They would leave no mark in wood, leaf, or skin — those get rewritten by morning into a different word, in a different hand, in sap. If they left anything, it would be in iron, which the plants cannot yet read and grow around respectfully: perhaps a straightened nail laid across a drain grate, a little extra iron for the slugs to rasp, for the sap to taste, for the grid to sip. A quiet promise: *decay will hold. I have seen the accounts.*

And they would go before the cool got into their marrow, before they learned entirely to photosynthesize darkness. Not because they are afraid, but because to stay would be to fill it. Even benevolence, overstayed, becomes population.

From the rim, looking back, it would again look like nothing — low dip, closed circle, low lid of cloud and leaf-shadow. Inside, the orange bruise still burning. The drip resuming its rhythm. The foxes resuming, the looms resuming mid-inch, the leaves turning away, released from coordinated attention.

The power would carry with them, for a long while, iron filings on the tongue and moss in their shoes, and the sound of nothing that is really a borough working. And elsewhere, when other powers say *this is too dark, too cold, too quiet, too small, too strange, let us fix it* — they would say, gently, with absolute authority:

Not yet. It has only just begun. It does not know downhill yet. Let it learn.
