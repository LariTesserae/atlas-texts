They would come without coming.

No light would change. No wind would gather. The plain white-grey daylight would stay plain. If you were standing there in your damp socks, watching your bootprints fill with brown water, you would not see them arrive — only, after a while, feel that the valley had become, if possible, more itself. As if something had leaned close enough to listen and had the manners not to cast a shadow.

For they are benevolent, so they would want to help. And they are wise, so they would know almost everything that looks like help here would be harm.

They would be tempted, the way all lovers are tempted.

Tempted to warm it — just two degrees, just enough so the dwarf willow could lift its head, so your fingertips would not go dull, so the crane fly would not hang alone in the cold. Tempted to drain an eye or two of that black water and give the sphagnum somewhere dry to rest. Tempted to pull the nurse moss back off the old willow that is bleaching white, twigs up like wires, and say: *breathe.*

Tempted to give it a voice. With so much work going on, such industry, such throughput — shouldn't there be a hum, a song? Shouldn't the mat be able to say *I am here*?

They would feel all that, and then they would do the harder, older thing.

They would kneel.

Not to fix. To get low enough to be at its scale. They would put their — whatever they have that is like hands, like knees, like attention — down into the thatch, wrist-deep, where fingers disappear and do not find earth, and they would feel how everything touches. Willow root through moss thatch through liverwort film through lichen crust. Pull, and the whole upholstery puckers.

And they would understand: this is not a place that is broken. This is a being that has solved how to live.

So if they do anything, it will not look like doing.

They will not build. They will not even leave a cairn. They will not carve a path or set a post to mark that they were here. They know: *no wall, no cut, no path* is not an absence in this valley. It is the condition that lets it keep running.

They will not warm it. Cold is the engine here. Freeze lifts, thaw drops, and between the two the mat kneads itself, preserves itself, eats itself. To take away the working cold — one or two degrees above freezing at midday, breath showing — would be to stop its heart to spare it shivering.

They will not hurry it. They will not make the cushion moss grow a centimeter a year instead of a millimeter, will not make the reindeer lichen forgive a boot in a season instead of twenty years. Hurry would lift it off the ground it is making.

They will not speak to it in words. The moss eats sound for a reason. Sound would be waste. Instead they will learn its language, which is almost no language at all: the dull compression of a step and then the wet kiss of water sucking back. The faint low *huh* of a cushion exhaling when weight lifts. The creak, felt more in the soles than heard, as ice lets go of root. Drip by drip, not babble.

What they will do, if anything, is this:

They will keep time with it.

Your breath makes small clouds that linger and tear. Their breath — vast as weather — they will slow, and slow, and slow, until one of their inhalations is a whole night of frost needles jacking cushions up an inch, and one exhalation is the afternoon slump and sigh and resettle an inch to the left. They will breathe at bellows-speed. They will not get bored. Benevolence, at this scale, is attention span.

They will witness without needing to be witnessed. The ptarmigan so still she is lichen until she blinks — they will blink with her. The vole's tunnel-hum traveling under the mat, never surfacing — they will travel with it a little way and then let it go, not following to the nest. The springtails white as salt, the mites, the tardigrades drying to husk and swelling back to life a dozen times a summer — dying and un-dying — they will count none of them, and hold all of them.

They will remember. Because peat is memory — leaves pickled in acid so they pile up instead of disappearing, raising the floor higher year by year — and what is a wise power if not another kind of peat, keeping what falls in half-whole for a thousand years? They will keep this valley the way the sphagnum keeps a fly: not to use it, but to not let it be lost. Every eye of black water opening while another silts green and closes. Every bank slumping and filming over. Every nurse turned shroud. All of it, kept.

And they will protect, but sideways.

They will not put a dome over it. They will not fence the granite shoulders. Their protection will be quieter: a diverted attention here, a forgotten map there. A road that never quite gets funded on the other side of the ridge. A human boot that turns back early because of weather, because of a feeling, because suddenly it seemed rude to go on. A cloud that lingers a little longer over the narrows where the valley rises, keeping the plain light plain, keeping glare and gloom away. One more morning of needle ice when the world is getting warmer, one more year the cold factory can run at full capacity. Imperceptible miracles. The kindest kind.

If the being — for it is one, this crowd that learned to be an animal — if it notices them at all, it will notice the way it notices everything: by touch. A slight extra give under a cushion. Condensation beads held a fraction longer before they fall. Water moving sideways through sponge a little more surely to where it is needed. Not a hand on the lever. Just a steadiness in the pressure. Hydraulic benediction.

They might, at the end of their staying — which is not an end, because they do not leave the way you leave, lifting boots out of sponge — they might say something. Not aloud. In the medium of the place.

*Continue.*

Not *grow*. Not *thrive*. Not *be happy* or *be safe* or *be known*.

Continue: press and pickle and mend. Split and film over. Dome up and close. Let no bare larger than a palm stay bare.

And then they would do the wisest thing of all, the thing only a benevolent power can do once it has been fully seen to be unnecessary:

They would go unnoticed, on purpose, so the valley could go back to being untouched — not pristine, not virgin, not empty. But unhandled. Running under your feet, silently, at full capacity, with no hand on the lever.

Behind you, your footprints are already closing, green water welling up to level them.
