It comes over the pass late, too.

Not because it has to walk. Because that is how you meet a place like this — low, on foot, with your mouth full of its weather before you can see it.

It stops at the basalt lip.

For a long time it just breathes. Lets the warm wet air take its own measure. Bathhouse warm. Iron and sulfur and hot stone. The clouds dragging their bellies across the rim. Below, the pearl-grey dim with its seven dim lanterns already lit.

It knows what this is immediately. It has seen it before, on other shoulders of other mountains, on other worlds that were not mountains at all. The thin skin. The provisional teeth. The animal that is still learning whether it will be animal or weather or light.

An embryo that is also a place.

Its first feeling is not awe. It is tenderness so sudden and physical it has to steady itself on the old guide-post. The collapsed tent. The milky panel. The rusted box. The hand-painted sign, flaking:

DRINK SECOND WATER ONLY.

"Good," it says out loud. "Someone learned quickly."

Because it can see what the sign-maker only guessed: the halos.

Around each needle the air gone thick and beaded. Thousands per cubic meter, millions in the bowl on a thick night. Each suspended bead holding amber a half-second too long — and inside each bead, if you turn off your light and let your eyes go, the speck, the comma, the coiled thread no longer than an eyelash, turning very slowly to face the needle. Trembling because it is swimming.

First water. Alive.

It feels them turn toward *it*, too, as its attention settles. That is the danger of attention. To look is to tug. To shine is to misalign a prayer.

So the first thing the wise one does is dim itself.

It veils its own brightness. Covers its lantern. It will not be an eighth needle. It will not compete with amber. It sits outside the halos, on cold cinder between the warm slabs, heavy enough to sink — anything heavy sinks here — so it sits lightly, on the rim-stone where the old lava has already decided to be ground, and lets its feet dangle into the undecided.

It drinks. Second water only. Cupped from a sky-pool that has sat and gone dead on cold stone, filtered through basalt, sky-colored, faintly warm. It does not drink what hangs. It does not breathe deep inside a halo. It knows better than to steal a flock, even by loving it too close. Even its sinuses, if it breathed them in, would carry that small warm trembling for days, and carry it where it was not asked to go.

That restraint is the second thing it does. And the hardest.

Because it *could*. That is what makes it a power.

It could resolve the chord. That low hum that never resolves, felt in the sternum, with the high shimmer over it that makes fillings ache — it could sing the missing note and give every creature in the bowl relief. No more nausea when the needles cycle low in the early morning. No more bowl-wide patter when all the suspended droplets fall at once and the frogs go silent for one breath.

But it listens longer and understands: the hum is husbandry. The low chord is pressure, a standing wave holding a bead steady against gravity. The morning fall is not failure. It is exhaling. Resetting. Culling the ones that didn't ripen, feeding the pools where the clear bells pulse just under the surface, no bigger than midge eyes.

If you resolve it, nothing learns to hang.

It could make soil. One touch and the black glass, froth, rope and pillow and shard — still sharp enough to cut boots — could be softened, stepped, lichen-spotted, given streambeds. Water has not learned downhill yet. It sheets, it hangs, it rises toward the needles. It could teach it downhill in an afternoon. Dig it channels with a finger.

But then the moss cushions, the orange fan-bacteria, the single blades of grass, the low fleshy pioneer holding a bead in each translucent leaf like an offering — they would lose their territory of emptiness they need to learn in. They are nurserying commas in their leaf-cups in exchange for nitrogen, for warmth. If you give them soil too soon, they will forget the bargain.

It could make it permanent. Still the tremor under the palm. Stop the ground breathing upward a finger's width a month. Pin the warm spots so a bath-warm pool doesn't go cold by morning, so a new vent doesn't sigh open ten meters off. Straighten the seven leaning teeth.

But they lean because something underneath shifts them. They are not rooted in rock. The rock is rooted in it. The bowl is not a valley. It is skin that hasn't scarred yet, pushed up as cilia to breathe and sing and farm the air. Baby teeth. If you set them straight, you break the jaw that is growing.

So it does not fix. It does not improve. It does not name.

What it does instead is smaller, and it does it all night while the rock ticks as it contracts and the swifts keep crying without landing and a cry arrives a full second before the bird banks into view:

It keeps vigil. That is the main thing. To be witnessed without being used — for an embryo, that is everything. Under all the commotion — midges glittering, frogs with throats like lit lanterns copper and lemon timing their trill to ride the shimmer, black newts with vermilion bellies flickering, flightless crickets clicking, birds shearing frogs right off the rock in mid-dive — underneath, the larger one feels seen. Not surveyed. Seen. As a *who*, not a *where*.

It sings underneath. Not leading. It finds the low chord and sings a fifth below it, so quiet the human sternum wouldn't feel it, but the droplets steady just a fraction. The chord still doesn't resolve. It just feels less lonely. A harmony that says: *hold on, I know this work.*

It tends the vectors. Because nothing that croaks lives here, and nothing with wings lives here, and even the green is visiting and will be buried when the next sheet comes up. The only true resident cannot walk. So it calls walkers with warmth and water and midges. Gorge, get coated, get filled, and when the mist thins go back down with a belly full of commas that will survive in damp leaf litter long enough to find another warm seep.

The wise one loves walkers. As dusk deepens it touches them lightly as they work — not to stop predation, the fork-tail must eat — but to bless safe passage. A steadiness to a frog's hop down a thousand meters. A clear draft for a swift's wing. A cool damp pocket in the cloud forest below where a comma carried in a vocal sac can wait. It does not stop death. It makes death carry further.

And before dawn, it does one human thing.

It picks up a fallen fiberglass survey stake — leaning, half-sunk into the soft between-pillow crust — and lays it gently flat, so it won't pierce the skin. It pries the leaking battery out of the rusted instrument box and takes it away in its pocket to be buried where it can't bleed. It leaves the collapsed tent collapsed. Warning is useful.

It repaints the sign. Same words, same hand. Fresh paint so the next one who drops in without seeing it first will read it in headlamp light:

DRINK SECOND WATER ONLY.

Let it fall, let it sit, let the stone-cool do its work. Don't drink what hangs.

Then it plants a story, not a fence. Down in the villages at the foot of the cloud forest, someone will dream of a warm mouth in the mountain, a womb with teeth, and wake knowing not to build there, not to bottle that upper mist to sell, not to bring drills and vats. Not because they will be punished. Because it is young. Because you do not build on a baby. The pass will seem a little longer than maps say. Funding for another expedition will quietly dry up. Not coercion — just room. A hundred years of room to learn soil, to learn rivers, to learn what shape it will be when it grows up.

Just before the early-morning low cycle — when the hum drops out from under you and your stomach drops — it stands at the lip to leave. The needles brighten amber, the beads tremble, the whole small bowl glows faintly from within its own mist while the peaks go black and the stars come hard and cold.

It does not say goodbye. Embryos don't need goodbyes. They need: *I will return.*

And it will — when the water has learned downhill, when there is true soil to argue over, when the seven have either fallen out like milk teeth or grown into something permanent, and the hanging animal has decided what it wants to be.

Until then: mist every twenty minutes over the east rim. Black rocks weeping. Warm pools trembling with each drip. Step carefully.

It goes back over the pass, leaving no footprint that won't be lifted a finger's width by morning.
