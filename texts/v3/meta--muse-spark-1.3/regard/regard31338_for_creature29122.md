It would not come down in light.

That is the first thing to understand about a truly kind and truly wise attention. It would have seen the bruised sun moving behind the lid and known better than to tear it open. It would not lift the amber to show them the stars. They already have a sky that breathes.

It would notice them the way you notice a thought in the night — not because they are loud, but because at rust-dark, when the amber cools, blue flickers run for miles under the moss. A grid discharging softly. A continent thinking in charge and water. Who could see that and look away?

So it would come up the way you must come up. Climbing, thinning, sweating. It would make itself small enough and warm enough to be bearable. It would cool its hands to the temperature of stone still warm from inside the planet, so as not to burn the sponge when it touched it. It would learn the rules very quickly, because it is wise:

Heat comes from below, not above. Trust stone more than sky.
Drink from dew, not from seeps.
Make no loud noise.
If you see yourself ahead on the trail, let them go.

It would obey them.

You would not see it arrive. There would be no straight line, no road, no landing scar — it knows this place has never suffered a straight line and does not need one now. Instead, on some morning of wet rips and soft sighs, when the thousand balloon-vines inflate, there would be one more sigh among them. One more patch of condensation beading. One more weight settled, gently, into the black-green sponge.

It would lie down.

For a long time — and time stutters here, so long means whatever the stone says — it would do nothing but lie on its belly on the warm sharp young world, under twenty feet of interlaced frond, and feel the infrasonic throb come up through its ribs. *Planet idling.* It would let spores drift into it. Let the light that comes from everywhere and nowhere fall on it dimly. Let its own voice fall flat after six feet and be embarrassed, and then be quiet.

Because it is benevolent, it would not flinch from the smear. Animal, vegetable, mineral. It would not insist on sorting them.

It would meet them as jobs, because that is how they resolve when you are still.

First, a cup-keeper. A frog no bigger than a thumb, translucent amber as the lid, owning a helmet-pool in a lava blister. The pool is trembling. Yesterday's eleven-minute hammering at 3 a.m. is almost evaporated. Tomorrow the frog may have to crumple into a wax-skinned bead and wait months mid-mud.

The Power would not fill the pool. That is important. It is wise enough to know: damp, never drowned. To make a lake here would be to kill. To promise permanent water would be to lie.

Instead it would kneel — vast thing made small — and shade that one blister with the shadow of its finger for one hour, so evaporation slows by a breath. So the throat-pulse song the frog sings in its ankles can go on a little longer. It would not be seen doing it. The frog would only feel a coolness, a longer dusk. That is enough. Benevolence here is measured in hours, in drops. *Plik... plik...*

Later, it would walk the seep-line. Cone-shell grazers the size of boilers scraping sulfur with blue-sparking radulas, inch an hour. Dung-beetles the size of cats dismantling heat before it cools. White fingers of fungus already fruiting. Intake, refine, waste, remake.

A human god would be tempted to help them. To carry the bricks. To tidy the geometric piles of salt and metal coughed out by the filter-feeders on the windward cliffs, which look until you touch them like ingots.

It would not touch them. It would leave the ingots uncounted. To take one, even to admire it too loudly, would be to introduce the idea of taking. Instead it would stand in the wet wind with the sessile mouths open around it, inhale cloud with them, exhale dry air with them in slow visible puffs, and understand: this is industrial without industry. This needs no foreman.

When it comes to the piston-worms churning cinder into soil that did not exist a hundred years ago — *chuff... chuff... chuff...* — it would place its palm flat on the ash and feel the hydraulic strokes travel up its arm, and it would match its own vast pulse to theirs for a while, so they are not working alone. Not helping. Accompanying.

With the Keepers of Heat it would be most tender, and most careful.

It would not cool the seep for the soft-shelled heat-crabs. They know how to stand on tiptoe in unison and sidle away; they have their ballet. It would not still the mycelial hum in the teeth; that hum is talk. It would not silence the eel-things lighting dew-pools from within; that ripple of blue is charge-balancing and prayer at once.

It would sit with a bellows-bird.

Huge, slow, ash-gray down always damp, throat sac glowing dull orange over its kiln-mound of fermenting vegetation, cooking blind white grubs and commensal eggs at constant temperature through endless dusk. The bird would stare through the Power with an eye like cooling glass. In its exhaled heat-shimmer, the ridge as it will be tomorrow: sharper, steaming, already mossed.

The Power would stare back and not look away. It would breathe, cool damp air in, hot air out, with the bird, for one cycle. It would see itself twice, duplicated under stress, walking ahead on the trail with its pack a little lighter — and it would do what the rule says. Not call out. Let them go.

And when the mite-deer come — thousands, knee-high, dun and steaming, pouring over a col in silence like spilled liquid, tick of hooves like rain — it would not stand in their way to bless them. It would lie flat in the sponge and let them reroute around its warmth. Let spores crush into its sides to carry to the next new ridge that shouldered up overnight, steaming, ferns still smoking. For an hour it would be a hill. For an hour it would bed down with them, thousands of warm bodies steaming faintly, balloon-vines sagging, piston-vents slowing, and be one more warm body among them, manufacturing nothing, demanding nothing.

That is what it would do, mostly. Nothing that looks like doing.

What it would *not* do is the real gift.

It would not stop the shrugging. When a dew-pond you drank from yesterday becomes a fumarole today, it would not apologize or reverse it. Creation and destruction are weather here. To still the upheaval to spare pain would be to stop the world from manufacturing itself anew.

It would not lift the lid. It would not bring rivers. It would not teach them noise that carries farther than six feet. It would not give them walls or roads or ruins or names for themselves that would harden into straight lines.

And at the rim — because a wise kindness knows there is an outside — it would do one fierce, almost invisible thing.

To the prospector dreaming of ingots, to the satellite, to the climber who thinks sharpness means untouched means mine, it would lend a little of the plateau's own silence. It would make the maps blur. Make the amber look from above like nothing but cloud, like furnace-light with no value. Make any loud engine brought near swallow itself and give nothing back, until the loneliness of that silence turns the intruder around. Not with wrath. With weather. *You do not need this high hot lush engine. Let it work.*

Then, before leaving — for even a Power cannot stay pressed forever without becoming another pressure — it would leave something that is not a mark.

Not a monument. The place has no ruins and needs none.

It would leave attention, in the mycelial way. A continued flicker. A memory held in warm stone: that once, for a while, something vast lay down at their scale, drank only dew, left the working fluid alone, stepped lightly on sponge, and watched without using.

The cup-keeper frog in its helmet-pool would never know its hour of shade was gifted. The bellows-bird would have seen it twice before and forget it twice after. The mite-deer would remember only as a warm hill that was there and then was not, already mossed over.

But the grid would hold a slightly steadier charge for a season. The dew would bead a little fuller on the felted fronds. The next new ridge shouldering up overnight would green a half-day faster, pioneer liverworts already on it before the smoke clears.

Not a miracle. A tending.

If you lay still afterward on that warm stone, under dripping life, in silence so complete you hear blood in your ears, you might feel it in your sternum, softer than a cliff falling a mile away: *whump... whump...* Something that loves this turbulent, crowded, smelted and distilled factory-that-is-an-ecology, loves it enough to let it remain unbuilt, unruined, young, and to keep becoming itself.

That is all. That is everything.
