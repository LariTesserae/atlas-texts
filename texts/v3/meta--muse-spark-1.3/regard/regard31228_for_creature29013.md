It would climb.

Not because it had to. A power like that could have looked from anywhere and seen — the white bowl, the green knot inside it, the thin cloud sea below. But being wise, it knows looking isn't the same as knowing. And being benevolent, it wants to know.

So it comes up out of the cloud the way you did. On foot. It lets the air get thin. It lets its breath shorten. It feels the cold in its fingers on the wet stone switchbacks. It puts a hand on the parapet, bone-colored concrete, and feels the thrum through its teeth before it hears it. It opens the sweating steel door.

And steps into heat.

It does not announce itself. Powers that need announcing aren't the kind you mean.

At first it just stands on the central walkway and turns slowly, the way you do, and takes the whole small made valley in at once. Two hundred paces. Terraces skinning over with warm water. Whitewash flaking: RETURN IT ALL. Tick of copper. Drip of mesh to gutter. Purr of pump under moss. And underneath, through the soles, that deep even gut-thrum of boiler and dynamo that never stops.

It smiles at that. *Good,* it thinks. *They made a coal and carried it up in a closed fist.*

Then it crouches down. This is the first wise thing it does: it makes itself small.

Because from standing height you see green. From crouching height you see who lives here.

A valve box, lid ajar. An inch of warm seep. Three coals with eyes.

*Pink.*

The power holds very still. It does not reach out right away. Benevolence that grabs is just another kind of cold. It watches one frog — the smallest, black as wet iron, rust-orange belly just kissing water, throat pulsing white — make its circuit: up the copper tick-tick-tick, pause where condensation beads, tongue flick for a moth, down to rinse in the skin of the terrace, back up. Up. Pause. Flick. Down. Rinse. Back.

An hour it watches. It has time. It *is* time, in the way these powers are, and it spends an hour on a thumbnail frog without feeling it as expense.

It understands the knot then, not as idea but as hunger and warmth: moth born in fog to clog the valve, frog born in seep to eat the moth and keep the valve clear, moss and taro and vine born in crack to drink the seep and breathe it back as fog for the moth. Keep it moving, keep it shallow, keep it warm.

Fragile beyond reason. Tropical animals in a tropical pocket held up into sleet. A machine that will break. Concrete that wants to be finished and then to crack. A crew that climbs up, does the heavy work frogs cannot, and goes back down to sleep below the cloud.

A lesser power would fix it. Would tear out the boilers and make a true hot spring. Would carry the frogs down to lowland paradise where they wouldn't need pipes. Would finish the concrete smooth and perfect, or smash it all and return it to rock.

This one does none of that. It knows down-low would kill them as surely as cold. They are not generic frogs anymore. They are *this* place's frogs. Camouflaged for machinery. Their toe-pads know wet concrete, their tongues know mesh-moths, their talk is box-to-box circuitry. To save them by removing them would be to unmake them.

So this is what it does, instead:

It tends, at scale.

It sits the rest of the afternoon on a green spongy duckboard where its weight won't crush fern, and breathes with the steam-bleed vine. When a puff of boiler steam crosses the noon sun and erases the flash on the pools, the power breathes out a little extra, just enough to keep the air saturated so the moss doesn't crisp at the edges. No miracle. Just a steadier breath.

When the cloud deck rises, as it does, and the parapet disappears and the bowl becomes a hollow in white and the mist-nets stream and the gutters gurgle higher and all the float valves lift at once — the power puts a palm, very lightly, on the western wall where the new terrace is being formed. Where rebar already furs with algae and fern spores have landed in damp footing sand. It does not stop the pour. People need to pour. But it slows the wind for an hour so condensation doesn't grow too heavy too fast, so the pumps don't labor past what they can bear. It eases the quickening. It keeps shallow shallow.

At dusk, when the moth-snow stirs and settles on the black mesh, it bends close — closer than you dared — and with a fingertip warm as seep, it lifts a drowned felt of bodies off a float arm that a frog hasn't reached yet. Just that one. It leaves the rest for the hunters. It does not want them unemployed.

At night, when steam is thickest and the bowl has vanished, it listens. *Pink.* Pause. *Pink-pink* three terraces down. The sparse circuitry. It learns which box is silent — the one where a terrace has gone stagnant and cold — and it goes there barefoot through warm shallows, taro leaves brushing its shoulders like shields, and kneels.

Inside: tadpoles stranded in cooling water, a female with her chin barely out, trying to hold heat. The power does not rewire the pump. That's crew work, human work, and it honors that compact. Instead it cups its hands around the little pit and breathes into it, low and even, until the water is spawning-warm again. Just till morning. Just a coal in a closed fist, held a little longer.

The frogs do not flee. Animals know. The small black male climbs onto the edge of its knuckle, blinks, and calls. *Pink.* So close the throat-pulse moves air. The power answers, as best it can in that register — not mimicking, just acknowledging: *I hear you.*

And it does the last, longest thing, which looks like nothing.

It remembers.

It commits to memory the exact pattern of this unrepeatable knot: this moth's silk anchor on this filament, this frog's two-hands territory, this taro corm wedging this flag, this orchid root wrapped around this steam bleed breathing like a green mouth. It holds the whole bowl in mind the way you hold a name on your tongue so you don't forget it when you wake.

Because being noticed, truly noticed, changes a place. Not in its pipes. In its loneliness.

Before dawn it goes to the far wall and looks at the whitewash: RETURN IT ALL. Flaking with algae. It does not repaint it. It presses a thumb to a flake to keep it from falling into the water just yet, and whispers under it, for whoever climbs up next with boots and sweat and cigarette smell and a bag of cement:

*Listen for silence. Silence means climb up. You're part of the knot too.*

Then it leaves. Out the sweating steel door into sleet. Down the switchbacks into cloud. It leaves no footprint — the moss springs back, the condensation fills in — except that one valve runs clear, one pit stays warm till the pump kicks back on, and the crew that morning sleeps five minutes deeper below the cloud and wakes strangely rested, with the inexplicable urge to check the far terrace first.

That is all.

It will not keep the boilers forever. Boilers fail. Concrete carves. Clouds shift. The power is wise enough to know it cannot freeze this embryonic, overgrown, poured-yesterday place in endless summer. Someday the heat will go, and the taro will blacken, and the frogs will go silent one box at a time, and green will do what green wants on top of iron until iron becomes soil.

If — when — that comes, it will come back and sit in the cold with the last frog, and hold it warm in its closed fist while it can, and then open its fist and let the water find its way down at last.

But not yet. For now: keep it moving, keep it shallow, keep it warm. Don't finish it too quickly.

It has drawn the attention of someone who knows how to wait, and how to stay, and how to be small enough to help.
