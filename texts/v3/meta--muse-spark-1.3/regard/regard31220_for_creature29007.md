It would come at night.

Anything wise enough and kind enough to notice this place would know better than to come in daylight, with clearing and explanation. It would come the way the valley already allows coming: low, under cloud, under canopy, without star or lamp. It would not lift the dark to see better. It would let its eyes go wide until there is almost no iris, the way theirs do.

At first it would be tempted, the way you are, to fix something.

There is so much to fix, if you think like a fixer. No river. A deafening that never stops. Stone like knives under a mat of moss that gives way. Babies practicing reaching and missing. Old adults with shortened digits, white seams where basalt took a tip. The larger grunters out in the black that kill with a casual bite. A whole technology that rots by dawn and has to be reinvented every night. A rain that only falls because tens of thousands of throats are laboring to make it.

A benevolent power that was not wise would bring a river. It would part the cloud. It would quiet the insects a little so the climbers could hear each other call. It would round the stone. It would give them baskets to keep their pebbles and thorns in, and words to keep their knowledge in, and fire to keep the moths from drinking their sweat.

A wise one would see what that would do.

This is a closed mouth breathing into itself. Give it a river and the leaves would forget how to comb fog, the frog-coals would go out, the sponge-roots hanging in air would rot down to soil, the ants would drown in their steaming crack. Give them kept tools and the tools would become things to fight over, to guard, to stay still for — and anything that stays still here is eaten. Give them far-calling voices and they would stop touching cheek to cheek, chest to chest, finger combing finger, and the troop-heap that rises and falls together in a crotch of branches would come undone. Solitary would mean falling through.

So it would not bring anything that stays.

It would make itself small. That is the first wisdom: a power that big cannot enter this bowl at its own size. Even a kind footstep would tear the fern bridge from tooth to tooth and put a hand through to the knives. So it would shrink until it could sit on the rim of that black cup of water between the basalt blades without spilling the tadpoles. Moth-sized. Dew-sized. Cool and wet-fingered.

And it would watch the female work.

It would not teach her juvenile to jab softer. It would not steady the pebble while the other hammers the snail — though it would feel the scrape of knuckles on lava as if on its own knuckles. It would just pay the kind of attention she pays: absolute, pointed-face, ears swiveling against the din, the mist beading on its muzzle first. Wisdom, here, looks exactly like love: to let the other pass through your seeing.

If it blessed anything, it would bless the transience itself.

The thorn becoming a probe becoming floating trash again in the same minute. Memory living not in objects but in other hands, watched and immediately copied, lost and reinvented nightly. A thousand times a night the world made and unmade without leaving a scar the young stone has to remember. It would see that this is not poverty. It is freedom from accumulation in a place where accumulation would kill. It would love that they leave the valley as sharp and trackless as they found it.

It would breathe with them.

That is the one intervention it would allow itself, because it is not an intervention. To kneel — if kneeling is a thing it can do — until dew forms on its skin before it forms on the leaves nearest it, as if it were the warmest stone. To let a palm-sized moth drink from its forearm. To let a climber come close, not tame, just unafraid in the press, and test it with those long cool fingers to see if it is bridge or stone, sniff the salt on it, read it with a fingertip almost sticky beyond the lava-callus of the palm. To be read and found to be neither food nor danger, just another warm lung among tens of thousands making the weather. To exhale, and add its breath to the fog the leaves will comb.

And to stay for the deaths, too. Because benevolence that only stays for fishing and piling and throat-light is tourism. It would stay when the pale root-snake drops through and takes a sleep-confused bird. When a grunter takes a climber and the troop screams and scatters and reforms ten feet away in a new shape and goes back to fishing. It would not stop the bite. It would hold the shape while it reforms. It would be the dark that is slightly less dark they leap into.

If it did anything that lasted past morning, it would do it outside the valley, not inside.

It would keep the cloud low and unbroken a little longer in a warming world. It would turn aside the human hands that cut and keep — turn them not with wrath but with forgetfulness, with a ridge of black teeth that looks impassable, with a night that never seems to lift, with no trail and no clearing to map. And for the one or two who do push through shoulder-deep into leaves and eyes and water that never rains, only settles, it would do a quieter work: it would make them hold still until their own breath joins the valley weather. Until they understand they are not here to explain morning. Until they leave with wet shoulders and slick forearms and tell no one where to find it, or tell it only the way a thorn tells — used once, then left floating.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do, if anything.

Almost nothing. Almost everything.

Be present enough to be breathed.
