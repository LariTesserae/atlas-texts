She would come in the heat, because this is when no one else would.

Not as a light. Not as a voice. The light here is already too much - white-grey-yellow, sun a dull coin behind vapor. She would not add to it. She would come low, at boot-height, where the crabs go around and the rats run. She would sit in the narrow shade of the tank farm wall with the three lean dogs and pant a while, just to learn the rule: stay out of midday, drink from the sky not the ground, don't waste movement.

That is the first thing a wise power does. She waits. She becomes part of the waiting.

She would not be fooled the way people are, thinking it empty because it is quiet. She would hear right away what lives here: the put-put-put of the last pump to the south, moving floodwater from one ditch to another forever. The plink from the awning into the puddle. The faint dry clicking of thousands of claws on concrete. The low cooing under every roof. The whine of insects coming and going. She would not flinch at the smell - diesel and rot and salt and warm mud. She would breathe it in. This is what is.

And then, because she is benevolent, her heart would ache in particular places, not abstractly.

For the brindled cat back in the dark volume, ribbed, nursing blind kittens on rotted pallet fiber, who licks condensation off a cold pipe because the ground water will kill them.

For the fallen squab on whitewashed concrete, still breathing while rats gather at the runway edge.

For the fiddler too far from the seep, drying from the top down, waving its huge white claw at nothing.

For the dogs who have given up the chase because it is too hot, flanks heaving, ears up even in sleep.

She would love them all at once, not only the white egrets high-stepping beautiful through the laydown-yard lakes, not only the kestrels hovering clean on the heat. Also the brown slick rats panting with mouths open in culverts. Also the cockroach boil under the board. Also the blue-green flies working the pigeon in the ditch. A wise power does not have a favorite animal. She sees the whole town made of meat eating meat on top of concrete, and she does not call it cruel. She calls it hungry. She calls it trying.

So what would she do?

Nothing sudden. That is the wisdom.

She would not sweep the buildings away. She would not bring the engines and backup-beepers back. She would not bring people back to feed them, because people fed them badly and then left poison behind. The oil and brine and slag in the soil, the iridescent skin on the puddles, the spray that kept the cracks clear for years - that is still here, still killing green. She knows that to fix it all at once would be another violence. To wash it clean in a flood would drown the kittens. To burn it clean would cook the mussels on the canal wall. To take predation away would starve the gulls and the cats and the hawks in a week.

She would do small, patient, almost invisible things, the way water does here. It seeps. It comes up through the cracks.

She would keep the pump going after its makers forgot it. Put-put-put. Someone has to move floodwater from one ditch to another.

She would make the evening rain come a degree cooler. Not a storm to tear roofs - the pigeons are tucked under those roofs. Just a long, soft, late rain that does not sting, that lays fresh sheets on hot metal before it hits the brine-fouled ground, so dogs and cats and rats can all drink from the sky at the same time without fighting. She would linger the gutters a little longer after the clouds pass, so the drips last.

She would put her hand, if she has a hand, under the concrete. She would loosen one more crack here, one more joint there, where no runway runs. She would send the marsh's exhale a little farther in - salt and sulfur and warm mud - and on that breath, seeds. Not trees. First, what can stand poison: glasswort, saltwort, cordgrass with roots like wire, oil-eating microbes invisible as dust. A green smudge in a wheel rut. A green lip along the flooded rail bed where sleepers lie sodden. Nothing that would take the basking lots from the crabs. Just enough green in the middle, at last, so the web does not have to eat only itself.

She would teach the soil to sweat out the old spray the way the buildings sweat rain. It will take years. She has years. While it heals, she would keep death merciful and quick when she can - still the heart of the squab before the teeth find it, cool the crab that will not make it back to water into sleep. While life holds, she would ease the itch no one sees: fewer warbles under the dogs' skin this summer, fewer mites in the pigeons' dust, a kitten that lives who would not have.

Most of all she would keep this place forgotten by those who would "redevelop" it. She would let the haze stay over the half-mile view. She would let the doors hang open. She would let the letters stay ghosts: BAY 7 — NO PARKING — KEEP CLEAR. People driving past on the four-lane will think: nothing there, just ruin, just heat. Good. Let them think it. Let the herons stand motionless as shut valves for an hour, for longer, unbothered.

If you stood still long enough on that service road, steam rising off in thin ribbons, water ticking off metal, you might feel her for a moment - not as a miracle, but as a lessening. The shirt sticks a little less. A shade is a little wider than the wall should throw. A gutter pours clean a little longer. A cat you thought was watching you blinks slowly and goes back to her kittens, unafraid.

She would not save them from each other. Gulls will still swallow crabs whole, throat working. Cats will still know the runways better than the rats. Kestrels will still take whatever moves too slowly in the heat. But no one will die of thirst under rain. No one will die only of our old oil. And the green, when it comes back, will come back for them, not for us.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does with a low, hot, weeping town full of meat and rain: she does not lift it up. She kneels down to its level, pants with it, drinks from the sky with it, and stays, until concrete learns again how to be earth.
