It notices them by the heat.

From very far off, in the flat cold, there is this one warm bowl breathing. A column of steam standing straight up at dusk. A low thrum in the ground, like a heart under soil. It goes to look.

It does not come with light or thunder. A wise, kind power knows better than to arrive like weather. It comes the way you come: down the switchback stairs, single file. It ducks.

That is the first thing it learns: you have to stoop here. Cable and vine and washing line and hose all hang head-low, catwalks fur with grass, taro leaves big as doors slap the cheek. The rim is high and pale. The canopy below is black-green. The air climbs five degrees, eight degrees as you descend, wet lime and hot oil and crushed green and sweat held close by walls.

It feels the pumps in its sternum before it hears them. Of course. Everyone does.

It does not turn them off.

A lesser power would. A lesser power would stand on the rim in clean dry air and pity them — *oh you poor wet deaf stooped people in a drowned quarry, let me lift you out* — and drain the sump, and cut the canopy back, and silence the mill, and build stairs up to the fields. And in a week the load-bearing ivy would let go, and the terrace pinned by the fig root would slide, and the children who sleep in roar the way other children sleep in womb-noise would wake screaming in the silence, certain they were drowning.

It does not do that. It has looked long enough to see: this is not broken. This is a bargain kept for a hundred years. Root, brick, skin, iron, all furred together into one breathing thatch. A being. If you stopped cutting for a week, the lanes would close. If you stopped pumping for a day, the cellars would drown. So they cut. So they pump. That keeping *is* them.

So it comes down and learns the crouch.

It makes itself low and broad and wet. It lets its skin go without dryness. Lets condensation run leaf-tip to leaf-tip onto its shoulders. Lets its feet splay on slimed brick. It presses an ear to sweating pipe to listen for the tick that means a joint cooling wrong. It cups its ears like the old ones and reads lips in the roar. It learns the hand-slap language you can feel through iron when you cannot hear: two hits *clear the grate*, three hits *steam up*, flat palm in a circle *cut back*.

And then, because it is benevolent, and because benevolence with clean hands is just watching, it gets its hands wet.

It takes a shift.

Not forever, not instead of them — to take it forever would steal the heart-keep from the heart-keepers, and they bring tea and yams and rice to the pump crew without being asked because everyone knows: if they stop, we drown. Meaning lives there. It will not steal meaning.

But on the nights when the bearing is about to seize though it was greased hourly, it puts a finger on the race and cools it. When a boy with a hooked pole is kneeling up to the wrists in black water at the central grate while it tugs at his arms, it puts its own vast hand underneath and eases the pull for a minute so he can breathe. When the old man is on his knees at his threshold with the flat spade kept by the door, scraping the night's white sheets of fungus soft as bread, it holds the door steady against the fan-beat.

Small mercies, terrace-speed. That is the rule of the bowl. Nothing fresh here. Everything has found its angle of rest.

It does three slow things, the kind that take years and look like nothing:

First, for lungs. It cannot take away boiler sulfur — the sulfur is what keeps the mosquitoes out — and it will not take away spore and lime steam, because that air is the air they are made of. But up on the lower terraces where the old ones sit on stools in garden beds shelling beans, it thins the canopy one frond at a time, just enough for a cooler draft to find them in the evening. A fraction less thick. They cough soft and wet and spit green into the canals, but in the night they breathe a finger's width easier. They notice, and do not name it. They say the air turned kind this year.

Second, for eyes and ears. It does not cure pit eyes, wide-pupiled for green underwater dim, or pit deafness from a lifetime in roar. Those are adaptations, not wounds. But it leaves a gift in the bodies of the children who must go up — for hospital, for army service — and are blinded an hour on the rim and panicked awake by silence. It gives them a memory they did not have before: in their dreams, quiet does not mean the pumps have stopped. In their dreams they can stand in the flat fields and look straight at noon sun without flinching, and still feel, faint in their sternum, the thrum of home. So they can return. So up there is not exile.

Third, for green and water. It does not bring back dogs or chickens or pigeons or rats. The people have taken all the small animal jobs into their own bodies — *he* is the duck, *she* is the chicken scratching moss for grubs, *they* are the rats combing thatch — and they do it with pride. Children go barefoot up drainpipes to dislodge fern. Girls with nimble fingers pick dead leaf from caged lamps. There is a family whose job for four generations has been just the north warehouse wall, ten feet thick of root and netting, and they will show you shouting where the maidenhair is thickest. To send in birds to do it better would be insult.

Instead it sends what can live *with* the bargain: a beetle, small and oil-black, that likes sulfur and eats the fungus that eats the planks, so the morning scraping is lighter. A moss, softer than the moss that is mortar, that takes hold in the mortar joints where pit-pox breeds in lime water, sweetening the weep just enough that the boils come fewer and smaller on young girls' throats. Nothing you would notice from the rim. Everything you would notice if your fingers are always pruned white at the tips and split at the cuticles from always being wet.

And mostly it sits.

At dusk, when the lamps come fully on, orange sodium and bare white LED burning all day in the damp, and the canopy above goes black and the weeping walls glitter all around like the inside of a well, it sits at the sump in the center with enough of them — never all, someone must always watch the pumps — knee-deep in steam, water pouring from a dozen mouths, leaves dripping, steam sighing off a release valve condensing instantly on banana leaves and raining back down warm onto bare glistening shoulders. Someone shouts about a clogged screen. Someone else is already moving with the pole.

It does not look up at the rim, that thin pale line. There is nowhere else to go, and it knows it. The pit holds heat. The pit holds water. The pit holds them.

It puts its hand in the black water. It lets the roar go through it. It answers, once, in the language they can feel: a flat palm rubbed in a slow circle through pipe and wall and root and bone.

*cut back, keep, hold, rest. I am watching. You can sleep.*

The pump crew sleeps that night all the way down, without one ear open. The pumps do not stop. The cellars do not drown. In the morning the grates are clear.

No one sees who cleared them. The old ones remember which spring opened in which drought year, which wall failed in '68. Now they will also remember the winter the air turned kind, the year the boils eased, the night the roar softened without meaning death.

That is what a wise kind power does, if it loves them: not lift the bowl, but get wet in it. Not silence it, but keep time with it. Slump a finger's width a year, shore with new brick that will itself be green by winter.
