It would come quietly, because that is the only way to come here.

Not down in light. Not with a wind. It would already be here, the way mist is already here — thinned and thickened into the bowl, learning the shape of the rounded cliffs. For a long time it would do nothing that looks like doing.

It would sit.

Where you stood at the lip and saw the whole room at once, it would kneel and see it grain by grain. Where you thought *nothing lives here*, it would think *oh, you are still doing it alone, are you.* And it would be glad.

For this is what a wise and benevolent power knows first: that empty is not broken.

It would not bring what is missing.

No whine to put over the pools. No tadpole shadow to reassure you that clear water is working. No bee to justify a flower, no bird to pass over and make it picturesque. No frog's throat. It would not correct the leaves that will not rot, leaf on pale leaf on sand. It would not hurry the fiddlehead. It would not keep the white ears of fungus from melting by noon. That melt is their liturgy.

It would not pick up the grey nodules flaked sharp as shell and show the moss how to cut. It would leave edge as edge, haft as fallen stem, cord as peeled bark, vessel as rust-colored clay in its seep hollow. All the invitations, unanswered. It understands that *ready* does not mean *waiting.* Nothing here is waiting for a hand.

What would it do, then?

It would practice the rules, because they are good rules and have been unbroken for longer than shape remembers.

Keep water close to the surface. So it would let condensation gather on its own arms, if it wears arms here, and let a drop swell at the elbow and fall, to be taken by the moss without sound. *Plip.* At almost the same moment the fern across the hollow would release its drop, and it would notice and not call it coincidence.

Let stone sweat. So it would put its palm, if it wears a palm, to the seven-foot head of dark basalt and feel the beads gathering along the undercut lip, and not dry them.

Keep distance between green things. So it would not crowd them. Not gather the fern and the shield-leaves and the liverwort skins into a garden. It would go to each apartness and honor it. A long time with the moss-country — the cushions, the wefts, the tiny stars — and the hair-thin stalks tipped red-brown standing straight in still air. A long time with the one fern on its hummock with ten feet of bare wet stone around it, lush unto itself. A long time with the cupped leaves holding their small, undisturbed pools that no one drinks.

Make no noise that lasts. So when it listens to the *tink... tink... tink* of drip into pan, with long silences between, it would hold its breath in the silences too. It would not add a voice, or a name. When a saturated cushion settles under its own weight with that faint sigh, it would not answer.

Do not hurry. So it would stay on the speed of stone. Rimstone grows a millimeter a year? Then a millimeter a year is a speed. A frond yellows over weeks? Then weeks are prompt.

Need no other life but your own kind to continue. This is the hardest one for a benevolent power, which is used to being needed. It would learn it anyway. It would not need to be needed here.

And then — because benevolence is not only restraint — it would do one small, almost nothing thing.

It would thicken the mist, just a little, on the open fourth side where the bowl gives onto cloud. Not a wall. A reluctance. So that the climbers below, with their noise and hunger and excellent reasons, look up and see only white dropping away into white, and turn elsewhere. So that the bowl can remain unwitnessed a little longer. Not hidden forever. Just unhurried.

It would bless without touching: *may you continue to need no witness, even this one.*

Then it would stand, and its standing-print in the green-black sponge would fill instantly behind it with clear water, as yours did. It would leave no hearth, no cut mark, no stacked stone. Only that print, which is already gone.

Above, nothing but sky. Below, cloud. In between, warm, high, wet, apart — holding water, then letting it go.
