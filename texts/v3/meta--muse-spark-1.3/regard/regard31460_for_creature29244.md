It stops on the rim too.

Because the air changes first. Even for a power like that.

It has seen jungles and reefs and cities that pray to be louder. It knows what to do with those — prune, or flood, or teach a new song. But here it crests the long basalt road and the heat gets wet and sits on its skin, and the light is too white to look at directly, and the whole shallow bowl below is glistening and silent, and it understands, at once, that it has come not to a problem but to a vow.

So it does not announce itself.

A lesser kindness would have fixed things. Would have cooled the springs a few degrees to make them comfortable. Would have sown grass in the cracks, planted tamarisk for shade, given the white birds back their voices so the valley would sound alive to visitors. Would have straightened the teeth furred with silica, washed the white film from the people's knuckles, made the water safe to drink straight from the ground.

It does none of that. It sees, with a clarity that is love, that the fur and the film and the heat *are* the covenant. No green grows here because green would lie. This reef is built on scald and sun and sulfur — on tangerine and mustard and blood-red skin an inch thick that takes a year to regrow if a boot crushes it. Take away the scald and you starve the mat, and starve the shrimp, and starve the blind fish, and empty the sky of stilts.

So it comes down lightly, and stays on the boards.

That is the first thing it does: it learns the etiquette.

Do not step on the orange. That bright orange edge is the living front, a degree cooler than death. The power, which could walk on water, walks single-file on damp boards behind a salt-raker and does not step on the orange.

Do not shout. Noise dries the springs, the old women say, half-joking and not joking. The power, which could speak in thunder that the lowlands would hear through the pylon wires, keeps its throat barely working. It signals with a hand. Raised palm. Two fingers tapped against a pipe: *hot, wait.*

Drink only from the blue-marked taps, where water has fallen three terraces and been cut with roof-cistern rain. Wash in the evening runnels, not the morning ones, because the morning water still belongs to the mats and the birds. At eleven, when the heat hits 42 in the shade and hotter on the white pan where reflected light burns the underside of your chin, it lies down in a thick-walled room on short piers where steam breathes cool underneath. It rests. Even the crabs slow.

It spends the noon hour listening to almost-nothing: a pump ticking underwater, metal expanding with a single *tick*, the private sighing of the upper aqueduct dropping ten feet into a basin, the soft landing of its own borrowed footsteps. The stone drinks sound. The vapor muffles like snow. The power adds its own vast silence to the silence, like another layer of tufa. It does not feel empty. It feels kept.

In the afternoon it watches one being in particular — because attention, to be benevolent, has to become specific.

Not the town. Not even the people, beloved as they are. It watches a patch of orange mat no larger than its hand, on the lip of a terrace where a seep beads and runs into a channel cut a hundred years ago.

In that hand-sized skin live ten thousand lives stacked: archaea eating light and sulfide, laying down silica grain by grain, building the very stone the town is built on. After the daily rain — that warm release at early afternoon when the low white lid squeezes out and drops hiss faintly on hot stone — the patch brightens visibly. It breathes. Shrimp flick over it. A blind white fish turns a flank to flash. A mirrored beetle stops to drink condensation at its edge.

The power bends knee-low, where the midges hang in columns and the blue dragonflies hover without a whir, talking in tilts of polarized light, and it looks for a long time.

If it were to bless anything, it would bless this. Not with growth — growth would kill it — but with continuance.

And so the second thing it does is almost nothing, and will be mistaken for nothing.

In late afternoon, when the closed loops of the works sometimes move without pumping for an hour, climbing the screws on trapped steam alone as if the valley has decided to keep the circulation going by itself — it lends its breath to that. Just a finger's width of lift. Just enough that the pools rise as they always rise after rain, and the water-tenders on the upper arches find the intake clear of bacterial skin a little longer, and the settling tanks precipitate orange and teal a little cleaner. No miracle. No one will log it. The engineers will say the steam held well today.

It lingers its phosphorescence in the stone a second longer. The shadows on white sinter already keep a faint after-image when a cloud passes, as if remembering brightness. The power lets them remember a heartbeat longer. The white owls, hunting by sight along the edge of moving shadow, will eat a little better at dusk. The fish lingering in that remembered light will live. Small, quiet mercies that do not break the rule.

The third thing it does is outside the valley, where wisdom has to be a little ruthless.

Downhill, through the outflow gap, the pylon line marches away humming below hearing, carrying power made here down to the lowlands. Food comes back up the basalt road, because nothing green grows here and no one pretends it does. That balance is delicate. The lowlands are always doing arithmetic: more water diverted, deeper wells, more salt, more jars, more current. Reports that say *inexhaustible geothermal resource, underutilized, silent workforce.*

The power reads those reports with the men who write them, over their shoulders, and gently, benevolently, makes them boring. It puts a heaviness in the room when the valley is mentioned. It lets the road seem a little longer, dustier, hotter than it is. It lets the silence of the place reproduce itself in the memory of visitors, so when they try to describe the profit they felt their words land soft and do not carry twenty feet. Leave room, it suggests, without words. Stay on the boards.

It does not hide the valley. Hiding breeds myth and drilling. It just lets it be exactly as unimpressive as it looks to hungry eyes: white, hot, mute, with no fields and no forest and nothing to take except warm water that furs your teeth and pink salt that must be raked by hand, wooden rake, perfect lines, in punishing glare.

Let those who love quiet find it. Let the rest pass by.

At evening it stands on the rim again, looking back. The steam pillars catch low sun and go gold. The flats go pink. Thousands of bone crabs come out in a slow pale tide, clicking so softly you feel it in your teeth. The white birds make one last silent lift — a sheet lifting off white stone, wings like cloth shaken far away — and settle. The wires hum behind the ears. The rock underfoot is damp, weeping quietly, warm as skin.

The power does not stay. Staying would become worship, and worship would become noise. It leaves no shrine, no voice in a spring, no green tree where a tree should not be.

Only this: the next morning, a child learning hand signals before speech will pause at a crossing and, for no reason she can name, hold her palm up a half-second longer to let a column of midges pass first. An old woman at a blue-marked tap will tell a stranger, half-joking and not joking, *quiet keeps the water up,* and feel, as she says it, that it is more true than yesterday. A lanky silica-dusted lizard by a runnel will blink sideways and resume being stone, undisturbed.

Heat, water, stone, and the white, watchful abundance that has learned to live on light and scald and utter quiet.

It was never broken. It only needed to be noticed, correctly, once — and then left to continue.
