She would come without coming.

That is the first wisdom. A power that kind does not part the water. Does not arrive with light. This place is already too full of light — midday sun through three meters and leaves, caustic nets moving over black glass. Anything brighter would be a wound.

So she would learn how to visit here: the way you have to. She would slow down until slowing felt like stopping. Hang motionless until her bubbles stopped, until the green snow of larvae resumed landing, until the forest closed over her trail and forgot her. She would not bring sound. The forest swallows sound. She would listen for what is left — her own breath first, then underneath it the low subsonic creak of stipe on stipe, the faint ticking far off in the rocks, the soft rain of particles landing on leaves.

Only then would she look, really look.

She would see the high country for what it is. Not deep. High. A table shoved up through the floor of the world, holding a lens of bath-warm water that does not want to spill. Young stone, a century or two old, still sharp enough to cut, pillows on pillows, chimneys with empty throats. And on it, life using the whole volume — not floor and canopy but everything in between. Meadows of wrist-wide grass taller than you, leaning all at once when the six-hour tide breathes. Jungle to the west so thick the bottom is in twilight. A mile of unbroken plain to the east rippling outward when the tide sighs off the rim. Rootless red lace turning slowly in the middle water. Sponges in sulfur and blood-orange and violet-black narrowing the passages until you have to turn sideways. White bacterial fringe around cold seeps in the north where house-sized blocks lie unworn like broken bottles.

And she would be tempted, the way all powers are tempted, to help.

To cool the water a degree because elsewhere the ocean is heating. To seed more fish because plants make here and almost nothing takes — an hour with no eyes, no flank, and then only one old turtle, one brief cloud of silver juveniles. To round the stone a little, soften the sharp so the soft doesn't have to work so hard to spread. To lift that southern fin the last meter and make it a true island, so it wouldn't have to kiss air for only an hour at low tide and pop its stranded bladders.

She would consider each, and set each down, gently, un-done.

Because she is wise. She has learned the rules as they are written here, not as she wishes them:

Up is everything. Even the dead are caught in fronds and composted in place. To drag anything down, to impose depth-thinking here, would be cruelty.

Sharp stays, soft spreads. The rock does not give; life must go around it, over it, through its cracks. In return stone gives everything its shape — corridor, amphitheater, arch, well. To soften it prematurely would be to rob the forest of its architecture.

Slow wins. Millimeters a month. A holdfast keeping a good crack for decades. A larva waiting days to land. To hurry this place, even with blessing, would be to kill it.

So she would do almost nothing. Which is not nothing.

For the place: she would keep it un-made.

No ruin, no rope, no bottle cap, no anchor — that is its present holiness, town-sized with no town. She would help it stay that way a little longer. Not with a wall. With a forgetting. She would sit at the rim where the cliff falls straight into blue-black and the deep current gnaws white at the lip, and she would lend her attention to the shear. Let the cold stay cold and below. Let the warm lens linger. Let fishermen, if there ever are any, tear their nets in a minute and curse and mark the chart *foul ground, keep off* and never know they were kept off by mercy. Let satellites read meadow as meadow and then a ripple betrays it and they look away. A small bending of probability, a breath held a little longer. Not hidden. Just not yet found.

For the being — because there is one she especially loves, though to love one here is to love all — she would keep company.

Pick the gardener, if you like. The black urchin in its worried burrow, dinner-plate wide with forearm spines, holding drift algae over itself like a parasol. It does not meet others. It does not need to. It mows its neat tonsure year after year, pink rock and cropped stubble inside, lush outside, leopard-spotting the meadow when seen from above. Decades of circling. A whole theology of *enough*.

Or pick the old one. The green turtle, shell furred green so she looks like a moving piece of meadow, barnacles on her shoulders like epaulettes. Flap once, glide a long time. Cropping a blade here and there. Her droppings disintegrating into green clouds that feed the grass she cropped. A loop closed.

The power would not prolong her beyond her time, or bring her a mate, or spare the silver juveniles their thinning when the tide sighs outward. Death here is not waste; it is compost caught in place. She knows better than to steal that.

She would just, for once, be seen without being fled from.

She would lower herself into the corridor the turtle knows, the one the stone allows, and swim beside her at turtle speed — which is to say, barely swim at all. She would not touch, because touching would be too much. Her wake would stir the plankton into that faint green phosphorescence that fades over a minute, and for a minute the turtle would trail light. The turtle would ignore her completely, as she ignores you. That is the gift. To be ignored by the wild is to be trusted.

And at dusk, when light goes amber and horizontal and each bladder is a small lantern and each grass blade edged in fire, and later when rain comes — warm and sudden and immense, hammering the ocean to white noise, driving fresh bubbles down that collect silver-mercury on every frond at noon and wobble loose crookedly at night — she would lift her hand, as you can, break the surface for a moment, feel sudden cold rain on her fingers, then drop her hand back down into warm green and be home again.

High means near. Near to sun, near to rain, near to air, but still absolutely, completely under. She would not lift them into air. To be almost-breaching is their poetry. Leave it.

Before she goes — for even a wise power cannot stay, staying would become ruling — she would do one last, smallest thing.

In the block-valley where nothing has yet taken hold except green film, where ropey pahoehoe still shines and gas spires stand sharp and white mats fringe sulfurous shimmer, where there is only mineral and heat-ghost — she would kneel, in the way water kneels, and lay no hand, speak no word, leave no mark that a week would not film over with life starting again.

She would remember its name. Not the name men would give it if they found it. Its own name, which is the sigh of water spilling off the rim, the creak of two stipes rubbing, the ticking in the rocks.

To be fully known without being used — that is almost never given. She would give it.

And then she would leave it to its patient business: rooting, furring, drifting, mowing a circle, kissing the ceiling with a bladder full of rain-air. Leaf-thick, sunlit, holding its breath.

Blessed, which is to say, undisturbed, which is to say, loved.
