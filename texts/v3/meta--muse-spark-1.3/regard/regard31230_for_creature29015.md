It would come without coming.

Not down in light. There is no light here to come down in. It would condense, the way everything else does here. Out of the cloud at the rim, a little thicker grey in the grey, getting wetter until it is here. It would not bring warmth with it. It knows better than to bring warmth to a place that has learned how to live in working cold.

The first wise thing it would do is get the scale wrong on purpose.

It would not stand above the domes and look out for miles. It would go down into them, knee-deep, ankle-high, mite-level. It would make itself small enough that a sphagnum dome is a country and a cotton-grass head is a weather system. It would let water rise around its feet from moss ten feet deep and not be annoyed when the print fills instantly behind it. It would let its breath join the general exhalation and stay there.

It would not build. That is the whole of its reverence.

It has seen other places — younger, sharper mountains — where to love a thing meant to fix it, wall it, name its rivers, give it straight time. It has learned. Here the rule that holds all the others is *nothing is built, everything works.* To bring a tool here, even a kind tool, would be a kind of blasphemy. The sundews make glue by the acre without intention. The sphagnum makes acid by the ton without permission. The domes pump and sigh and vent without engineers. The power would lay down any idea of engineering at the rim with its shoes, and come barefoot into the sponge.

It would sit. That is the second thing, and the hardest for a power.

On the lee side of a glacial egg, where the orange lichen drips even when it has not rained, next to — not instead of — the peat-colored fox. It would not pet the fox. It would not feed the fox. It would learn to be ignored by the fox, which is a deeper companionship. It would curl as he curls, and drink condensation, and sleep through St. Elmo's fire trembling on its shoulders. When the blue sparks crawl, it would not ground them. It would tick with them.

And it would listen for the double beat.

*Thump — thump ... thump — thump.* With the second beat slightly earlier. Everyone else here has learned to live with time arriving out of order: geese heard a second before they come over the rim, your boot heard half a step before you lift it, reflections lagging a breath behind your hand. A foolish power would smooth this out. Straighten the world.

This one would not.

It understands that the plateau is too old and too wet and too charged, and the world wears thin here, and that thinness is not a wound. It is what happens when a range has lived so long it has forgotten to be mountains and flattened into a table and settled into its final form — flattened, soaked, cold, humming — and in that final stability become free to be unstable in every small way, all the time. To "heal" the stutter would be to kill the freedom.

So instead it would keep time *with* them, the way the soot-colored geese do. They honk late to cover the echo that came before. It would answer late, too. When a tarn drains sideways while you blink and strands the black newts flipping, orange bellies up, it would not refill it instantly by miracle. It would wait the hour with them until the fog refills it. It would wait. Benevolence, here, is willingness to strand a little.

When the white herd that was a quarter mile off is suddenly sixty feet away, breathing fog, staring, moss untrampled between — because distance is negotiable when unwatched — it would not stare them pinned. It would look down to wring water from its sleeve, on purpose. It would give them the unwatched second they need to move the way this place moves. It would learn how to be unwatched itself.

When the rivulets on the weeping rocks bead upward against gravity after a venting, embarrassed, before falling back down, it would not rebuke gravity. It would watch, delighted, as you watch a child do a trick.

When the dead do not stay put — when you find the reindeer skeleton sunk in sphagnum, antlers hung with sundew, and around the dome find the same one standing, flesh on, grazing, with peat still in its ribs — it would not lay it to rest. It does not need rest. The herd doesn't shy from it. The fox doesn't bark. The moss keeps pumping. The power would just nod to it, as to an elder who got up to walk for a minute on the early beat. It would remember which beat it grazed on, so the bog doesn't have to.

That is its gift, if it gives one: memory.

Because nothing here can hold a straight story. Frost heave lifts whole plates overnight into cliffs that collapse by noon with a wet *whumpf*. A tarn you photographed is gone. A path the reindeer tore floods and greens over within an hour as if they were never there. The shrew lives its whole life between three pitchers on one dome and then that dome sighs and settles and *chuffs* faster under a new weight. The place pickles everything in acid, inch by century, but it forgets the order.

The power would remember the order kindly, without imposing it.

It would remember this calf, bawling almost white between cotton stalks, licked clean. This bee, ginger and black, lifting with a veil of pollen pulled by its own static. This brood of goose chicks hatched already swimming in tea-black water. This particular shrew-dive into digestive fluid that should dissolve and doesn't. This fox's missed strike and this one's true strike, made by watching not the newt but its lagging reflection, striking where it *will* be.

It would hold all those temporary shapes the way peat holds bodies — gently, without stopping their change.

And outwardly? It would do almost nothing that you could see.

It would make the cloud a little lower over the drop-off on the days men fly over with maps. It would make the plateau look, from the rim, even emptier than it is: empty bog, a few humps, a lot of sky. Not worth draining. Not worth cutting. No posts, no cairns, no cut anything — keep it that way. A little extra grey weight to the distance, so the eye slides off. That is the only miracle, and it is a miracle of inattention. The best protection for a place whose power is to be unwatched is to help it stay unwatched.

If you stood still long enough on your dome, you might feel it. Your weight makes the dome sigh and settle and *chuff* a little faster, industrious, indifferent, alive. And then, under that, a second settling — as if something vast had lain down with you to distribute the weight. As if your island had, for a breath, become slightly more buoyant. Your fingertips trailing in the tarn would still go numb with the weak current. The midges would still pillar above you. You would still get wetter standing still than walking.

It would not speak. If it did, it would speak in boiler-room language: gurgle, knock, expansion, contraction. Hiss of methane sweet as honey. Gush of water fired pool to pool through hollow root pipes. Drone of thumb-sized bumblebees.

Or it might just, once, breathe with you. Inhale iron and peat and ozone. Exhale and not dissipate.

And leave without leaving. Which is to say: it was never not here. It just, for a while, let itself be tenanted too — lichen on its lee side, sparks on its shoulders, sundew on its antlers — one more warm, breathing island going out to cloud.
