It would not come at once.

Having noticed — the small warm basket breathing over dark air, the lush that drinks heat, the cured ones moving a finger's width sideways — it would first stop, upwind, and empty itself.

For it is a wet power, as all great powers are. Breath that condenses. Eyes that water. Hands that sweat and stay wet. If it walked in as it was, it would be a rain. A ruin. A dissolving. The wax would soften. The lashings would slack. The holdfasts would let go. What is dry keeps its shape. What is wet forgets — and it does not want them to forget.

So outside, in the sun and open wind, it would sit down and let itself dry.

It would leave its cup behind. Its well. Its cloud. It would weep once, if it needed to weep, and leave the tears out there on a stone to evaporate. It would breathe until its mouth was dry. Until its eyes blinked. Until its great hands were warm and papery. Smaller, then. Low-door small. It knows how to make itself smaller. All wise things do.

Then it would duck.

The door is low on purpose — not for defense, for heat — and it would honor that. Bow the head. Bend the back. Come in on knees if knees are needed. Not knocking — a pot knocked here makes only a dull *tok* and then nothing, the weave drinks sound — but brushing a lantern-husk lightly as it entered, so the spores rattled. A dry announcement. *I am here. I am dry enough to enter.*

Inside, it would not stand and look for people. It has learned already how to see them, which is to stop looking.

It would sit. On the mat-floor, where the joists knock softly hollow underneath. Where the dust is only chaff and fiber-shear, not earth. It would pull its large feet in so as not to cover the pale path the dust-mice wear along their joist. So as not to step in the empty patch the geckos skirt, where the air shimmers with heat that isn't heat. That is where their feet fall thickest. It would not put its weight there.

It would wait until the sweat dried to salt on its lip. Until the slat-light pooled sideways up the posts in bright rungs. Until a threadbird, no bigger than a thumb, slipped from its pocket-nest of down and saliva-glue and landed — testing — near, and did not fly.

Then, very slowly, like the bottle-vine tightening by fractions following the sun through the weave, it would offer a hand.

Not to build. They are not builders now.

To mend.

That is the only technology they trust. Slip-knot for a beam that still grows. Hard square-knot for dead wood. The little looped charm-knot wherever a vine has been trained to turn. It would hold the runner while an old woman no taller than a storage bin coaxed the white holdfast around a new post. Its fingers, grown thin and cured for the occasion, long at the tips from picking knots. A child would stare, knee-high, twine around its wrist chewed soft at the ends, and the power would let itself be stared at, and not move too fast when it moved, so as not to startle. Like the geckos. Move only when they blink.

It would learn which hook is theirs. The post hesitated — the same charm-knot, same fray, same copper hook occurring twice, one a finger's width to the left. The threadbirds land on the left hook, never the right. So would it land, metaphorically, on the right. Leave the left for them. Take the visible, ordinary forty paces, and leave them the fold. The sleeping platform behind the garden row behind the sleeping platform that should already be outside the wall. The floor above the roof, very far and very close. It would not try to unfold them to count them properly. It would not smooth the stitch to see how big they really are.

Benevolence that unfolds is not benevolence here. It would help them refold.

At ambering time, when there is no dusk inside only a slow ambering of the slats and the husks begin to glow from within with steady spore-light, it would be there for the creak. Every night the whole basket inhales the day's heat and holds it, and a soft creak runs around the waxed wattle oval in a circle that never quite completes — them, pulling tight. Not to keep the world out. To keep themselves from spilling, from unfolding, from remembering how big they really are.

That night it would put its back — made small, made warm, made dry — against the leaning inward wall and lend, without pulling, its weight to the circle. Just enough for the creak to complete, once. All the way around. So they could rest. A single completed circle in a hundred years, to let the lashings know they hold.

It would not touch the old ones under the platforms. The very old who have stopped mending other things and begun to mend themselves into the house, skin gone fully to fiber, hair fully to moss, hand still curled around a post-holdfast, a new air-fern unrolling in a stiff spiral from an old knuckle. Nothing here rots to teach you what dead is. They cure, they sharpen, they become trellis. A wise power knows the difference between curing and healing, and does not try to heal a curing. It would only press its cheek, briefly, to the dry leaves dense as fur beside them, and listen to almost nothing with them — the tick of a loom-beetle's carapace, the dry *shush* of a husk turning — and witness that the mending held.

It would not stay.

To stay too long, too large, even made small, would be to add too much breath, too much weight, too much noticing. The place is young. Sharp corners. Unsoftened knots. It wants to stay sharp. So before morning, before the slatted knives and coins move again, it would leave a gift that is almost not a thing.

Not water. Never water. Not seed — they have seed-heads and husk-fruit enough in fingerprint bins. Not a new tool — bone awls and copper hooks are sufficient.

A knot.

On a post near the low door, on the right side — the guest side — a little looped charm-knot in pale fresh twine, tied where no vine yet needs to turn. An invitation for a future runner. A memory of a turning. So that when a mender finds it tomorrow, fresh-cut bright and resin-smelling, she will understand: *something was here, and it learned to speak, and it did not ask us to change shape.*

Then it would duck back out, into sun and open wind, and take up again its wetness, its cloud, its largeness. And all the way home it would do the one large thing it can do without harm:

Hold its hand over that small oval, without touching, to turn aside the rain.

One storm diverted. One traveler with a cup given a sudden thirst elsewhere, a sudden reason to walk another path. One gust lifted over the dense thatch instead of through it.

Nothing, really. Almost nothing.

Which here — where everything is near-touching, beam and vine and nest and pot and hand and tail — is everything.
