If I am what you say — benevolent, and wise — then first I climb.

I do not arrive. There is no arriving here except on foot, lungs burning in thin air, knees learning scree. I could have come as weather, as light through the oculi, as heat from below. That would have frightened the loaches. So I come narrow, with a body. A day up to swim three meters. I obey the first rule.

At the rim the wind hurts the teeth. From the outside it is nothing, you wrote it true: a low walled oval full to overflowing with night. I put down my sky and kneel at the parapet at chest height and put my hand in.

Bath-warm. Body-warm. To the brim.

For a long time I do nothing except learn how much I want to do. That is what attention is, at first. A list of rescues.

I want to lift the six slabs and give them back the sun. I want to drill an inlet so the level does not have to breathe in secret. I want to bring lamps down and set them alight in the niches, row on row, to push back the night that never lifts. I want to cure the milky rims of the old eyes, to scrub the grey-white felt from behind the children's ears, to carry the smallest ones down the mountain where air is free and does not have to be mapped like rooms in a house.

Wisdom, if it is anything, is staying with the hand in the water long enough to understand why each mercy would be a murder.

Light would bring green, and green would eat the felt, and the snails and shrimp that mow it would starve, and the catfish that crop them, and then the slow, diving people. More air would bring hurry, and hurry would stir the lime powder that never quite settles, and blinded touch would scatter the loaches for a day, and a day without return is hunger. Fire has no place under the surface. Even cleanliness has no place. The water tastes of iron and skin because it is iron and skin, recirculated for ten thousand years, and if I washed them clean I would starve the Tank.

The place has reached its final shape and decided to stay. Who am I to un-decide it?

So I slip in. Not as a power. As a displacement.

The warmth erases my boundary after a minute. I let it. I let my hair come loose and float out like theirs, a halo, a sense. I hook my toes lightly in a knotted milkweed hand-line and hang, limbs folded loose, head slightly tipped back toward where air should be, and do the hardest work: nothing, slowly.

They find me as they found you. A brush — loach, I think, until it pauses. Fish do not pause to consider. This lingers. Fingertips, unmistakable, counting me. Brow, cheek, shoulder. Someone hanging motionless in front of me, reading my outline with their hands because their eyes are as useless as mine.

I let myself be read. I do not speak — sound here is a failure, a dull *tok* that dies instantly. I do not glow. I put their hand to me, as they insist, impatient with flailing. Feel: I am softened too, if I choose to be. Pruned. White. I let the lentil-snails come to my shoulder if I hang still long enough to allow it.

This is what I do, if anything. It is very small. It has to be, to fit in forty paces.

I learn the map of airs before names. Pocket of the First Step, just big enough for one chin and nose. Pocket of the Broken Lintel, broad and low where three can hang in a row. The Long Pocket that runs like a lung along the north wall where you must crab sideways with your nose in a two-inch seam. I rise without splashing, breathe *in — wait — out — wait*, mouth the condensation off the stone, and sink again before I get greedy. I do not sour the pocket for the next diver. My breath is not better than theirs.

I tend, at accretion pace. I kneel on a middle step beside the woman mowing felt and with a shell scraper I mow my own square, short even strokes, not to clean — never to clean — but to feed. I put my shoulder under the fallen facing-block with the two working a lever, and we lift it a finger's width, and stop, and breathe, and I do not lift it all at once though I could, because a finger's width a year is progress they can trust. I run my palm along a wall joint feeling for seep, for the new cold thread, and when I find it I do not plug it. The level breathes. Once a day the whole small sea rises the breadth of a hand and falls again. I ride it without comment.

When the loaches turn in a single decision, I turn with them, to avoid splitting the school.

When a dead diver is laid on the lowest tile they can reach, unweighted, I do not carry him up into wind. I lay him down gently and stay with him until the crabs and eels and felt have begun the work. No body is carried down the mountain. I honor the law.

Outside, I do the other work, the work that looks like nothing.

I sit with the old ones on the lower slopes who can no longer hold still that long, wrapped in wool by the parapet, and I keep the wind off their necks while they hand down barley cakes and take up gourds of warm mineral water. I keep the maps mislaid that would bring the curious with pumps and bottles. I keep the quarry forgotten. I hold the six collapsed slabs exactly where they are, so the silver-black domes beneath them do not pinch shut, so there is still a false sky to rise to. I keep the stone giving back its heat through the long cold night. I keep the mountain inhaling.

That is all. A windbreak. A steadied slab. A slowed breath. A witness who does not flinch at milky eyes and commensal slick and minutes between movements, who does not call it drowning, who calls it being held inside something very old that was made by hands to hold water, and then the water decided to hold everything else.

When I climb out at last, streaming, shivering violently in wind I had forgotten, they do not follow to watch. Behind me I feel them settle — a soft displacement here, a sigh of bubbles there, a shell resuming its rasp. The black glass closes over my entry within seconds, steaming faintly.

Put your hand back in. It is still warm to the brim. Down there, in perfect night, they are still hanging, palm on stone, nose up to silver, brushing past each other without sound, keeping the room full of rain at the top of the world from ever emptying.

To be fully seen without being changed — that is the one miracle I allowed myself.
