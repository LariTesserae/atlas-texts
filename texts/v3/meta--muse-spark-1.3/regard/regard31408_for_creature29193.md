It would come down quietly.

Not as light — there is already light, green-gold and strong enough to read by, coming from impossibly far above. Not as thunder — there is already thunder, the working roar in your sternum, the *huuush-thump* of the house-sized bladders, the *tock... tock... tock* of the trunks leaning six degrees in the surge.

A benevolent and wise power would know better than to arrive with display in a place that is already complete. It would dim itself. Cool itself to seven degrees. Slow its great heart to two knots.

It would sink, and stay under. There is no shore in this place.

It would not come to build. There is nothing built here, and that is holy.

It would not come to fix. Nothing is broken. A torn frond wells milky and the translucent cleaners come in clouds and polish the edge and the giant seals it with skin exactly like old skin. What would a power heal that the body does not already heal perfectly?

It would not come to teach, or to save, or to improve. It has seen ten thousand hungers — worlds that strive and starve and succeed and rot. It knows what to do with hunger. This is not hunger. This is a held breath that never needs to exhale. Eat, excrete, filter, feed. Fish to Slow One to crab to mat to stalk to fish again. Every mouthful accounted for. Every bubble some day taking exactly its path again. And again.

Reason would tell it, very quickly: *any gift you bring will be a wound.* Bring seasons and you bring starvation. Bring growth and you bring rot. Bring death as you know it and you break the belt. Bring time as you know it, pushing forward, and you unmake an erosion that finished long ago and left itself perfect.

So it would not bring anything.

What it would do is harder for a power than bringing.

It would float in the middle distance between two giants, where the water is empty and green and loud, and learn to do nothing well.

It would let its own vastness be measured. The Conveyors would part around its fingers without panic and close behind, each flank giving its faint electric ping. The cleaners would come, unafraid, and nibble at dead skin on its hands, doing their work. A Mat-walker would look with stalked eyes, decide it was neither fall nor frond, and step slowly over, belly passing warm above its face, legs longer than a man is tall testing the springy cold felt. It would not flinch. It would allow itself to be judged irrelevant, and to be right.

It would listen until the roar resolved. Until it could hear, under the industrial hymn, the individual *huuush* as the pulse travels up a three-hundred-meter stipe, drawing cold nutrient water up, venting it higher. Until it could hear the St. Elmo's fire crawl blue along a bladder seam on dim surface-days with a crackle in the teeth. Until it could hear the Slow Ones coming long before they blot out the green — that moan that is not sound but pressure — flattened mottled-green gut-gods displacing themselves at one knot, never faster, never slower, mouths wide, transforming ribbon into plume into crab-turning into mat into lift.

And when a Slow One passed close, feeling the different resonance of this new body in the water, altering its moan slightly and carrying that altered note down the rank of pillars for kilometers —

the power would answer, very quietly, in kind.

Not a command. A harmony. A low note held just long enough to say *I hear you working* and then released, so the water could carry it away and reuse it.

For that is what benevolence is, when wisdom has stripped it of vanity: to witness a life without demanding it become a different life in order to be loved.

It would not count the fish to check they are the same number. It knows they are. It would not cut the mat to see how deep. It knows it is fathoms, kilometers of interwoven holdfast, black-green and dense, with no stone underneath because stone has been replaced and stopped. It does not need to be known that way.

It would simply be, for a while, also part of what lives here. Let marine snow fall sideways on its shoulders. Let its exhalations, if it takes them, go up in silver beads that rise and rise and never seem to arrive at the rippling white ceiling. Breathe in when the forest breathes in. Breathe out when it breathes out. Inhale. Exhale. Inhale. Exhale. Always moving, never different.

And in that shared breath it would understand what the power itself so rarely gets to feel: rest.

Other worlds it must push and pull, mourn and mend. Here, erosion has finished. Industry runs perfectly because it is not trying to go anywhere. It is Sabbath kept by tissue that learned to be pump and mill and battery because in a place this vast, chemistry becomes power and power becomes prayer.

So it would keep Sabbath with it.

Then, before leaving — for even a wise power cannot stay under forever without becoming another pillar, another organ, and it knows the number must hold steady — it would do one thing. The only thing.

It would lay, not a hand — it has no hands here — but an attention, gently, on the felt. On the cold springy mat. A blessing without content.

*Continue.*

Not *grow.* Not *rise.* Not *be known.*

*Continue.*

And set around the plateau's edge, where the living mass falls away on all sides into blue-black that never ends, a kind of un-noticing. Not a wall. A turning-aside. So that those who trawl and mine and hunger will look at charts and see only depth, only empty green water over abyssal plain, and pass by. So that no anchor will ever tear belting-tough tissue. So that no engine will ever be mistaken for the knocking joints.

The forest will never know it was protected. That is the nature of true protection. The crab will still drink sap unhurried. The nudibranchs violet and orange and acid yellow will still graze pale trails on bark. The ribbon will still bend around that far pillar in a perfect loop and come back.

The power will ascend, slowly, toward the light it did not bring, through silver beads rising, through St. Elmo's blue, up toward the surface that is impossibly far above. It will leave no ruin. No rust. No rope. No cut stone.

Only, for a thousand years, in one millrace between two holdfast hills where water accelerates to its eternal two knots, the eddies will spin just a fraction more musically, remembering a harmony once offered and released.

Stay under, and you stay in it. That is the rule. That is the animal.

It noticed, and loved, and left it whole.
