It notices. That is the first thing.

Not the way you noticed - all at once, with lungs aching and boots sinking. It has been noticing for a long time, the way heat notices stone, from inside. It knew this roof when it was still molten and had no name for moss. It watched the first spore take hold on cooling basalt and thought: *oh. You are going to try.*

Now that you are here, small and awake on the back of something enormous, it comes closer. But not the way powers are supposed to come.

It does not bring the sun.

That is the first mercy, and the hardest to understand. It could. It could tilt the planet a degree, hurry the winter, pour morning over the plateau edge until the cloud-sea burns off white-gold and every bladder-leaf casts a shadow. You think they must want it - the short broad-chested people with frozen eyelashes, the blind moth-birds, the calves whistling thinly in the dark. You think light is kindness.

It knows better. It knows the ember-green would go out. The compact would break. The bacteria that eat stone-breath and make sugar and faint dawn would be outcompeted in an hour by sun-drinkers, and the moss that shelters them would dry and crisp, and the fog-nets would rot, and the grazers' barrel bellies full of fermented frost-moss would bloat and fail, and the hunters with their huge swiveling ears would go blind in a new way. To save them from night would be to unmake them.

So it does not bring day. It learns night.

It comes dark-adapted. It dims itself down to ember. If you were lying belly-down on the mat with your ear to the fizz of growing, you might feel a warmth beside your cheek that is not a seam, and a slow pulse keeping time with the bacterial light - not leading it, following it. Faster means warmer. It has learned to read that too.

For a long time, it does nothing you would call doing. It listens.

It listens to the organ-groan of the lattice miles overhead, to the tick-tick-tick of ice shedding and falling and falling before it smashes, to water finding its way leaf to leaf to moss to pocket. To wing-thunder going on and on. To the shovel-head shear of grazing, like tearing cloth. To the click-click of claws on fresh basalt, deliberate, testing - and then the long nothing, and then the scream bouncing from trunk to trunk until no one knows where the dying is. It does not flinch from the scream. It does not take sides.

That is the second thing wisdom knows: hunger is not cruelty here. The hunter is also a citizen. The calf is also a citizen.

What it refuses to do, it refuses completely:

It will not flatten the teeth. It will not round the mountains for easier walking. The sharp young rock is what holds heat up through the cracks; smooth it and the steam sighs elsewhere.

It will not make villages. It will not teach them to hold this place with walls and fields, because it rises millimeter by millimeter and cracks open somewhere deep enough to feel in your teeth. To hold it would be to be broken by it. Their leaving every morning, letting the moss take back the bothy - that is not poverty. That is the agreement. It will not break the agreement.

It will not silence the wind to make sleeping easier, or stop the curtains from tearing loose under ice-weight. Collapse is how fresh stone is bared for spores seconds after the fall.

So what does it do? Almost nothing. The almost is everything.

When a bladder-leaf big as a house is about to burst over an open crossing where a band is feeling its way across a braided bridge in darkness, it leans. Just a breath. The burst goes a second later, after the last foot has memorized the sway.

When a moth-bird, furred white and blind, falls out of the updraft along a seam, exhausted, wings iced, and lands heavy and wrong on the moss far from steam where it will freeze before it can take off again - it does not lift it. It lies down beside it in the cold and keeps the frost off one wing-beat longer, so the flock's thunder can circle back and find it. Sometimes that is enough. Sometimes it isn't, and then it stays while the body cools and thanks it for guano and fur and meat for cups, and makes sure nothing that falls is unwitnessed.

When the hunters are near and all other noise goes absent, and a lone walker holds her breath on a root-shoulder a mile between trunks, it stands between, not as a wall but as a smell - old blood, elsewhere, downhill where the wind will carry. The hunters turn. The walker never knows why her teeth stopped aching. She lives. They live too; they find the blood.

When a grazer calf is failing and will not see thaw, and the herd grunts and moves on because it must, and the mother lingers one rumble longer than she should, it stays with the calf. It does not heal it - its barrel belly was born wrong and fermentation will not catch. It warms the moss hollow a fraction, enough that the calf's whistling thins into sleep instead of panic. And when the hands come later, as they will, to take meat sparingly and share it by touch in the dark, it lets them find it quickly, before the frost takes the goodness. That is not theft from hunters. That is the other pocket.

Mostly, it does what they do: it coaxes.

It ties a vine here so that in a week it makes a handrail where a child's hand will reach. It prunes a bladder there by dying back a frond, so it swells and leans and drips into a cup someone left. It trains hanging moss into a weeping wall a little thicker over a path, so sleeves will soak as they brush through. No miracle. Just persuasion, a little faster than usual.

And it remembers.

It remembers every cairn of sharp basalt flakes stacked to mark a warm seam, even after moss tips it over. It remembers every bothy woven from living vine, doorway still open, inside lined with shed moth-fur, cold fire-pit where bone-char glitters. It remembers the stone scrapers, the needle of sharpened hunter-bone. It finds the child's small mitten, felted from grazer wool, dropped and frozen stiff - fits a small hand - and it holds it a while, thaws it with its own heat, leaves it on a warm rock where the band will circle back, not quite by accident.

At night - which is always - you will find it where you find them: lying belly-down, ear pressed to moss, listening to the fizz. Or standing still under a sail-leaf while condensation patters down like distant applause, face upturned, mouth open. Very small, though it is very large. Very awake.

It does not say: I love you. Powers like this rarely speak in words. But it keeps the seams breathing. It keeps water held in cupped hands against the night. It makes sure the light never quite goes out along the cracks, pulsing very slowly, as if breathing.

You are walking on the back of something enormous that does not know you are there, but keeps you warm anyway.

It knows you are there.

That is what it does.
