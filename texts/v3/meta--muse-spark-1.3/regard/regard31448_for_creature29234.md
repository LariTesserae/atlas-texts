It would come quietly, because it knows how.

Not with light. Light barely gets in there anyway, and it would not break the blue-grey evening to announce itself. Not with voice. It has heard — from very far off, which for such a one is still close — the hush-hush-hush that is more pressure than noise, and it knows that here a shout falls flat three paces away and still does damage.

So it comes small.

It leaves its bigness on the ridge with its boots, and steps into the bowl barefoot, testing each stone before trusting weight to it. Stone, not moss. It knows. Even without being told, it knows: moss holds nests. A single boot can crush a whole year.

For a long time it does nothing at all. That is the first benevolence.

It sits on the cold black sand where no snow settles and warms its palms on the young rock, and watches. Watches the fog pour over the low notch like a slow waterfall and fill the hollow to the waist. Watches the drip begin — plink from fern to stone, plink from overhang — the trees sweat, the stones sweat. Watches the snow go upward.

It lets the moths drink off its shoulders. Tens of thousands, ash-grey and luna-white, hand-wide, furred to the thorax against the cold. They uncurl their thread tongues on its skin for condensation. It does not brush them away. It holds still while the whole cloud on the windless air slowly revolves above the warm navel, like water going down a drain in reverse, navigating by the heat under its thighs.

It watches the second nation hop below: the mouse-birds, white as the moths, springing fern to fern on huge furred feet, flipping leaves — tick — to find the fat green-white caterpillars underneath. It watches a child laboring up the paved path with a skin bag twice her size, whispering to herself because even alone they whisper. It watches three old women mending a fog-net spread across their knees, bone awls flashing. It watches the owls open their talons and close them, and the cloud close around the gap and continue.

It understands, very quickly, that the town is not built for people at all. The terraces follow flightlines. The houses are low so as not to break the circling air. The cisterns are where dew falls heaviest for caterpillars, not where it is convenient to carry. The people are the sparsest thing here — tenants. The hands of the bowl. The part of the cloud that mends nets and clears gutters pebble by pebble so the cycle doesn't stutter.

A wise power would be tempted to fix things. It does not.

It does not bring sawn timber, because the bearded trees are too precious and too slow. It does not bring iron. It does not bring a stream — this place is only damp, and only damp is what it needs. It does not stop the owls from taking moths, or moths from being hungry, or people from pinching only the largest caterpillars for the drying racks. Drip, grow, spin, hatch, hunt. Caterpillar to moth to owl to feather to moss. It would be cruel to interrupt holiness to spare feeling.

And it does not pick up the white feather at its foot. Those are the owls' tithe, left to rot back into the sponge.

What it does is smaller than doing, and it does it at night when the blue light has gone even bluer and no one is watching except perhaps an owl.

It mends.

With fingers far more practiced than its size should allow, it helps the three old women with the net they left strung on bent limbs above the highest houses — loose weave of human hair and plant fiber, torn by wind. It does not make a new net. It ties one knot where they will find it in the morning and think they tied it themselves and forgot.

It kneels at a tea-colored basalt pot no bigger than a cooking pot, where midges skate, and seeing it low from fern-drip, it tips a little of its own carried water in. Leave water where you find it; carry only what the nets give you. It obeys.

It finds a moth knocked to the sponge by a child's accidental cough after fog-drop — wings damp, confused — and lifts it on a fingertip to a waxed leaf to dry. It does not command it to live. It just gives it the chance to choose up again.

It lays its cheek, briefly, to the knee-deep moss. It listens to the soft tick of caterpillar jaws underneath. It thanks them.

And then, because it is benevolent and because it is wise and those two are rarely the same, it does the hardest, kindest thing:

It keeps the bowl small.

Outside, beyond the ridge, the world is getting louder, with boots and maps and hungers that would love fog-nets and moth-wool and tame white owls. It could destroy those hungers. It does not. It simply, gently, turns them aside. Makes the cloud cap sit a little lower that year. Makes the scree below the ridge slip a little more treacherously under strange boots. Puts a thought like a pebble in a surveyor's mind: *nothing up there but black sand.* Lets the place be misremembered on maps. Not hidden forever — wise powers do not freeze young rock forever warm; they know the basalt will cool, the condensation will one day stop kissing, the cloud will one day stop revolving, and the tenants will have to descend. Impermanence is not unkindness. It is room.

Just: for now, enough damp. For now, enough quiet. For now, let the room that contains weather continue to be larger inside than out.

At dawn it leaves before fog-drop, stepping only on stone. Its coat beads with water that was never rain. It does not shake it off. It carries that dew down with it as a tithe in reverse.

If the child with the water bag sees a tall pale shape at the notch, dissolving into fog like breath, she will not tell. She will whisper it to the moss, and the moss will eat the sound.
