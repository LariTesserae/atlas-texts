They would come at night, and lie down.

Not descend trailing light. Not announce. A benevolent and wise power knows the first violence is arrival. So they would do what you did: walk in thin air with a heart working too hard, feel the cold lift, and go on the belly at the rim with the frost forming on the scoria at their elbows and the furnace breath laying itself over their face.

And they would obey the rules.

Stay dry. Don't shine a light. Don't expect silence to mean stillness.

For a long time they would do nothing else. That is the hardest thing for power, to do nothing on purpose. To watch the wall resolve and resolve again and not try to finish it. To feel the rice-on-paper skitter and not translate it into song.

They would learn, because they are wise, what this place is:

Not broken. Not missing water, missing green, missing light, waiting to be fixed. Complete.

An old arrangement. Talus settled to its angle. Ledges worn smooth not by water but by millions of feet. Blood-warmth held in the middle of frost by mountain heat from below and body heat from within, by a million separate bodies making of themselves a single breathing pelt that holds its own humidity by a fraction. That fraction is life and death.

Outside sun -> high moth -> bat -> dry pellet -> Clinger -> snake -> centipede -> dust on the updraft. No leaf. No root. No rain. A world strung on the night sky.

Once they understood that, they would know all the mercies they must *not* perform.

They would not bring water. To bring water here would be murder disguised as kindness. Water would rot the frass, sprout mold in the two-meter chitin sand, bring green to take a foothold and start a different story, unmake the waxy cuticle bargain, drown the salt-dry powder in the crevices. The Clingers are each other's climate because the sky refuses to be. Don't take that away to ease your own thirst at the sight of thirst.

They would not give eyes. Eyes are useless currency here. Everything navigates by heat-shadow and touch. Eyes would dry, infect, waste water. What would they look at anyway? Darkness they have already mastered. The power would not gild blindness into sight to make them more lovable.

They would not still hunger. They would watch a rust-red centipede, as long as a forearm, pick its way over the living carpet and take a molting nymph, and a pale translucent snake swim through the lower layer and take a Clinger whole, jaw unhinging with slow patience, and the Clingers take the soft newly-molted and the fallen-wrong among themselves — and they would not lift a finger. Because in a place with no water you cannot afford to let protein walk away. There is no malice in it. To forbid eating here would be to forbid living. Benevolence is not the abolition of teeth.

They would not make it loud, or bright, or large, or efficient, or known. They would not straighten the oval mouth eighty meters long, fifty across. They would not pave the south chute where the ladder rails stand without rungs, silver and snapped.

What would they do, if anything?

Almost nothing, and that almost is everything.

They would keep vigil. Attention is a form of food that does not get eaten. They would stay long enough to see the ripple go round: antennae lifting thousand upon thousand, hold, settle, traveling slow around the oval as the mass thermoregulates, opening to dump heat, closing to keep moisture. *Open. Close. Hold. Keep.* They would learn that language of touch and heat, and answer in it, not with words but with a steadiness of presence: *I feel you. Go on.*

They would grieve without rescue. Each shed cuticle like an empty glove, each broken antennae in the dust, each pink belly of a snake after a swallow — noticed. Counted, in the way only a power can count a million held breaths without reducing them to a number. No single Clinger, thumb-last-joint big, wingless, eyeless, tasting the hot dry air with antennae longer than its body, would live and die unwitnessed. To be fully seen by heat-shadow, for once, without being shone upon. That is a benediction.

And outward: they would protect the conditions, not the creatures from each other but the whole from us.

They would keep the night dark fifty kilometers out in every direction so the high moths still ride the wind and the soot-dark bats still hawk thin and tireless. They would keep the desert cold, to keep the contrast that makes the Pit a furnace-heart. They would keep the rock thirsty. They would let the nitrate bloom white as frost that burns the tongue, let the sulfur puffballs crunch like insect shells under boots.

Where people come — and they will, with new ladders, with wires and sensors and floodlights for tourists, with plans to mine the dry pellets that burn the lungs, with pity and with thirst to improve — the power would be a quiet discouragement. Not smiting. A wind that finds every seam. A forgetfulness about the turnoff. A lungful of baked ammoniac dust that says *you either endure it on its terms or you leave.* A ladder rung that snaps a little sooner. A tin box with corn husk and a blue bead left undisturbed, its offering honored by not being answered in the way the offerer thought.

The white painted circle and line, flaked almost away — they would not repaint it. They would let it flake. Human reverence, too, must stay dry and small here, or go.

If you asked them why they did so little, when they could do anything, they would say — if they spoke at all, in that pressingly quiet bowl where the living walls eat sound the way snow does:

Because they *are* the place. Take the living carpet away and there is no place left, only bare heat-cracked basalt that would cool and go silent. I will not save them by unmaking what they are.

Stay dry. Stay dark. Stay together.

And then, before false dawn, when the river of wings thins and the last Returners drop heavy and silent into the lava blisters, when the carpet settles a fraction deeper into its cracks and the ripple passes one more time around the bowl, a shudder of pale bodies in the almost-light — they would lay a hand, not to touch, just above the mouth where hot meets cold on a single face split in two, and lend for a moment their own vast thermoregulation to hold the blood-temperature steady through the coming day.

No one below would know. The antennae might lift, puzzled, tasting a sudden stillness damper by a fraction. Then settle.

That would be enough.
