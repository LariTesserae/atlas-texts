It comes high, because they are high, and it learns at once to move slow.

That is the first thing a wise power would learn here. The air will not let it hurry. Even kindness, up here, must time its breath to steps or fall gasping to its knees with vision going black at the edges. So it slows. It makes itself low and dense and patient, the way the houses are.

It does not arrive in light. There is already too much light here, hard and white at noon, bouncing off snow caught in fresh-broken teeth of mountains. It does not arrive in thunder. Sound is swallowed by felt and wool and distance. It arrives the way everything important arrives here: gradually, flank to flank, in warmth.

For a long time it only watches. That is the first benevolence: to look and hold, not glance.

It sees the whole bowl of the plateau breathing. A city that is not a city but a herd that built windbreaks. Hundreds of thousands of fat-lamp flames flickering to the horizon. The man-made cloud of dung-smoke flattening a few hundred feet up to hold the heat a little while. The morning iron-hard with frost, the midday weeping of roofs drop by drop into stone gutters. Women breaking thin ice with a heel. Children patting dung cakes into geometric hives against south walls. Old men reciting mothers of grazers back seven generations. The dead laid open on high platforms and coming back as beams and combs.

It sees how thin the margin is. One failed melt. One white-out that lasts nine days instead of seven. One cough that moves through the sleeping piles in winter. And it feels, as any benevolent thing would, the terrible urge to fix. To thicken the air. To bring trees. To bring rivers. To make barley grow taller than an ankle. To make fifty years old be young.

And because it is wise, it does not.

It understands: they have not failed to adapt. They have adapted utterly. Keep low against wind. Share walls, share warmth. Move with the herd, not faster. Save every drop of melt. Burn only what the herd gives. Build only what hands can lift. *We who keep each other warm.* To lift them out of this would be to unmake them. To give them engines and metal and sawn timber would be to teach them to hurry, and hurrying here is death. Even to be worshipped would be a theft — it would take breath they need for breathing.

So it will not be a god to them. It will be, if anything, another member of the herd. The part that remembers water channels and can mend a wall.

What it does, then, is small, and low, and almost invisible. The way they do things.

It lies down with them.

In the blue, fast-falling dusk, when grazers lie down first in a ring and calves are pulled into the center where breath collects warmest and goats wedge on top and the long ribby silent dogs crawl in last against human legs, it crawls in too. Unseen, unfelt as separate, it just makes the pile a little warmer. It lends its breath to the low communal tide of breathing, human and animal in and out together. The stones around them, warmed all day by sun and animal heat, give their warmth back all night — and that night, they give back a little more. Just enough that a newborn breathing air already breathed and warmed by others does not gasp quite so hard. Just enough that an old woman sunk toward the ground, fifty and venerable, sleeps without the catch in her inhale waking her.

In the morning it works.

Not miracles. Maintenance. It kneels — though it has no knees — beside the man mending a wall by hand, stone-set-on-stone, and the stone seats truer. Beside the girl milking before dawn with forehead pressed to hot flank, and the let-down comes easier, fingers less numb. Beside the boy leading a two-ton grazer by a nose-rope for an hour across what would be three streets elsewhere, and the wind drops a fraction so the felt over nose and mouth does not freeze quite so stiff. In the market, where two traders sit flank to flank under a shared blanket doing finger-pressure for numbers, it warms the hands hidden under felt.

It touches only what they already touch.

In the sheltered south-facing terraces, it lingers a little longer in the soil around the ankle-high barley, so leaves stay waxy against cold but fill a grain more. In the cracks between stones, it encourages the orange and grey lichens like spilled paint. In the hollows outside the city, it keeps the small sky-colored tarns fringed with ice even in summer from shrinking — drop by drop, year by year, enough. It does not make abundance. Abundance would make them careless, and carelessness here is death. It makes *enough* hold.

And it stands guard, but outward.

This is perhaps its greatest kindness, and they will never know it. From the ridge above, their thousands of lights look like nothing from far. Like stone. Like herd. Like low cloud shadows. The wise power keeps it that way. It lets lowland empires, hungry for timber and rivers and quick wealth, look up at the plateau and see only thin air that hurts, frost-shattered stone, wind that has not had time to round the mountains. Not worth the climb. Not profitable. It turns their eyes aside, gently. Lets the city remain accreted, unplanned, uncounted, un-conquered. Lets no road come up that would teach them to hurry.

Once in a generation, when a winter comes that would not just cull but break — the kind that kills calves in the center of the circle, that freezes melt in the cisterns solid, that silences even the ravens on the roof-ridge — it puts a shoulder to the storm. Not stopping it; they need winter to know who they are. Just softening the edge by a day. Holding the man-made cloud a little lower, a little longer. Giving the dogs in doorways one more night of shivering until they still.

The ravens notice. The dogs notice. The heavy black birds pause mid-call. A long dog lifts its head from a child's feet in the sleeping pile and looks, for a moment, at empty warm air, and thumps its tail once, and lies back down. A child humming stops humming and then resumes, the two-note hum traveling under the wind a little farther than it should.

That is all.

It does not stay to be thanked. Thanks would cost breath. It does not leave a mark. Marks would be built over, generation over generation, older courses mossy and sunken at the bottom, newer courses sharper on top. It does not promise them another world. It affirms this one.

Before it goes — if going means anything to such a thing — it does what they do for their dead. It returns what it borrowed. Breath to air. Warmth to stones. Bone to herd.

And for a moment at midday, when the frost lets go and everyone is out working and the whole vast urban mass proceeds at a herd's pace, the thin air itself feels, for a moment, almost thick enough to live in.

They think it is just noon.
