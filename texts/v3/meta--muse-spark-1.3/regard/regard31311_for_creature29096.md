It would come without light.

That is the first wisdom. Any other power would arrive shining, to see. This one has learned how to arrive blind.

It would have heard — the way you hear a held breath in another room — this small warm pocket under the scree, this unfinished mouthful of mountain that never learned how to be a cave, and the slow condition inside it that never learned how to be separate things. Film and root and grazer. Weep, drip, evaporate, condense, weep again.

It would come close and then make itself small. Even benevolence has to crouch here. You cannot stand in four paces by three. The ceiling crowds down to meet every crown. So it would lay aside bigness, lay aside radiance, lay aside even the desire to help in the ways powers usually help, and squeeze sideways through the slit where the ridge-rocks lean.

No flood of day follows it in. It is careful to seal the cold wind behind it with its own body, for a moment, and then let go.

Inside, it would sit.

Not on the curb of flat stones. In the shallow warm skin of water, like anyone else. Knees aching. Breath coming back off stone inches from the face. Sweat starting and then stopping feeling like its own. Eyebrows beading. Heart too loud at first, then quieter as it learns the time here: *plink* — to the left. Long nothing. *Plink* — to the right, answering, but not in rhythm.

It would not count. It would not take inventory. It would not name the springtail or number the rootlets or map the film. To count dozens would be to make them few. To name would be to make them separate. It understands they are one slow animal wearing three shapes, and the pocket is its shell.

And it would understand, with a grief that does not panic, how close to nothing this is.

One boot scraping the wrong wall could wipe a century of film. One headlamp left on could tell the rootlets a lie about sun and make them waste themselves reaching. One hand lifting a springtail to look could dry its soft cuticle in seconds. One vial taken for study — *just one sample* — could unmake the equilibrium. One winter without snowmelt above, one summer too hot, and the weep stops. And outside, storms will come and go, seasons will strip the slope, climbers with metal and hunger for wonder are already wandering closer.

A lesser kindness would want to fix it. To give eyes to the blind grazer. To give green to the roots. To make the nubs grow faster into true stalactites. To open the slit a little wider so no one has to squeeze. To install a light, a path, a plaque: *Miracle Cistern. Protected.*

This one does none of that.

It lifts, instead, one of the heavy stone bowls left by hands. Infinitely careful not to scrape the film where its fingers slip just a fraction. It holds it under an overhang and waits a full minute, two minutes, however long it takes for a bead to swell, break, fall. It drinks.

Warm. Faintly mineral. Faintly of stone-soup and time.

And in drinking, it joins the cycle for an hour. Its exhale joins the saturation. Its warmth joins the warmth. For an hour it consents to be briefly part of the animal too.

What it does then is almost nothing, and that almost is everything:

It steadies.

Not by holding the slabs apart with might — they lean as they lean, big as houses, sharp at the break, and they will one day shift again as mountains count shifting. But it eases its palm along the raw tuff where edges are still crisp enough to skin a knuckle, and it asks, in the language of weight and patience: *hold. Not forever. Just a little longer in this shape. Let unfinished remain unfinished a while.*

It breathes with the seep. The mountain above holds snowmelt and summer rain close to the surface here. That holding is getting harder. So it tends, as a gardener tends without touching, the slow press downward through porous roof. Not more water — more would drown the film and flood the eggs laid singly like pearls mistaken for beads. Not less. Just continuance. Drop by drop, whether anyone is here or not.

It mends small human things and leaves no trace of mending. The wooden dipper, handle gone soft and swollen — it firms it a cell's width, so it neither floats away nor sinks further. The twisted plant-fiber twine tied around a nub — it tightens the memory of the fiber so it does not part. The finger grooves in the soft mud where someone knelt — it leaves them. Those are prayer, and you do not smooth prayer.

For the living, it does the hardest thing: it refrains from being seen.

It does not warm the pocket further — it is already mouth-warm where it should be cold, and that paradox is load-bearing. It does not cool it. It does not feed the springtail fallen hairs from elsewhere; there is manna enough in what sloughs. It does not trim the film where it grows soapy; the grazer does that, walking millimeters per hour, carrying spores on its hairs from wall to wall, a pollinator of dark. It almost never jumps, and the power will not startle it into jumping. It will sit so still the tickle, if it comes, can cross the back of its hand unhurried.

When its shifting sends a low slosh that the close walls swallow, it freezes for a long time afterward, out of respect. As the springtail freezes.

And before it leaves, it does one unapologetically divine thing.

It blesses forgetfulness.

Not for the thirsty — herders, climbers, pilgrims who know the slit with their hands will still find it when they need it. Their sweat belongs here. Their breath belongs. They leave the cups for the next. That covenant holds.

But for the hungry other way — for those who would map and sample and post and lead groups with bright lights and take the dipper as souvenir and the water as cure — it lays a thin veil over memory. The scree will look, to them, like any scree. The crack no wider than shoulders turned sideways will look like shadow between rocks. The story, if heard, will sound too small to bother with. *Warm dark? Blind bugs? Nothing to see.* They will walk over without knowing. Which is true. It is a place you walk over without knowing, unless you need to know.

No metal will enter here. No cut marks of saws.

Then it ducks back out through the slit into thin cold and wind and blinding day. For an instant, cold air combs in behind it — that blade of outside world that almost intrudes — and then the pocket seals itself. Warm, dark, wet, crowded, unfinished — waiting, drop by drop, to become itself. Or rather, to beautifully refuse to become anything else.

It does not promise forever. Wise powers do not. Equilibrium is frozen, but not cold; frozen, but not forever. One day the slabs will settle. One day the seep will find another path. One day the last springtail will lay its single sufficient egg and not meet another. That day is allowed to come.

Until then: nothing happens here, beautifully, continuously.

And attention — patient, crouching, thirsty attention that drinks and replaces and leaves the fingerprint to be closed over by film — will keep it so.
