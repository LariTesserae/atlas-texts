It would come over the rim on foot.

Not in light. Not in thunder. Any power that is both benevolent and wise has learned how to arrive without being an event. It would crest the ridge where you crested it, feel the thin cool air come awake in its lungs, and look down into the breathing bowl, and stop.

Because first it would have to learn how to see.

At first, like you, it wouldn't see them separate from it. Just movement in the green. A hand where a branch should be. A face under a leaf-eave. The *tok tok tok* of the stone maul, the metallic trill of frogs that never stops, the smoke tangling blue-grey in the crown and never leaving. It would stand there long enough for its boots to go wet in three steps, for water to bead on its shoulders and tip and pour, for a leech to find its ankle. It would not brush the leech away in disgust. It would pick it off with the practiced thumb-flick, and smile at having remembered how.

Then it would go down, and sit.

Not on a throne. On the big root-plait plaza where three paths meet over the main rill. Where the ground thrums faintly with water underneath. Where the girl trades grubs for dry kindling, where the old man re-lashes the vine bridge with his mouth full of cord while traffic steps over his hands, where the mother guts minnows and flicks heads exactly far enough for the cats and the jays. It would sit to one side, out of the way of the boys hauling the pole shouting *mind! mind!*, pull its cloak around it, wring it out first as custom demands, and just watch the circulation. The mending becoming a meal. The hauling becoming owed.

And it would feel the great temptation of all powers who love:

To fix.

To drain a little of the wet that never ends, so the children's noses stop running and the old people's knuckles stop splitting and healing with pitch and fat, so that low wet cough from sleeping under smoke would lift in the night.

To give them iron. One good iron adze would do the work of ten stone mauls. To give them glass for windows, brick for hearths that draw, a mill for the rill so the woman fulling cloth doesn't have to beat it on a root.

To take the leeches off the leaves. To still the dogs barking themselves hoarse at the monkeys. To make the grain baskets mouse-proof.

To fell just one pillar, straight and true, to make everything easier, squarer, drier.

It would feel all that rise in itself like mist gathering — inhale — and then, being wise, it would let it out. Exhale. Drip, run, gurgle down to the gardens and over the lip.

For it would see the second rule, and the first.

*There is no ground that is not alive. Nothing stays still.*

And these people have learned how to obey them. That is rare. That is holy in a way temples forget. *You do not cut a pillar here.* They say it to a stranger like water runs downhill, not as dogma but as carpentry. If a trunk is where a door should be, you move the door. She doesn't patch the wall, she *plants* it. They wash around the tadpoles. They count squabs on their fingers and leave half. They leave cress tops on the far roof-leaf for thieves, which is tribute and bribery and also affection. They have learned — stacked thirty families in shouting distance, hearing each other's piss and dreams and quarrels — to be permeable without being cruel. Privacy is a cloak pulled over the head, a turned back on a bridge, an agreement not to comment.

A wise power knows: you do not bring iron into that. You do not bring dryness into that. You would shatter the honeycomb to save it from stickiness.

So it would do almost nothing. Which is not nothing.

It would do what the bowl itself does. Small, wet, constant, living work.

It would sit beside the bean-shelling grandmother and the old torn-eared monkey female on the roof-ridge, the two of them grooming in parallel monologue, beans and fur, scold and scold, and it would listen to both with utter seriousness, and it would ease, just a fraction, the stiffness in the grandmother's morning hands, so the midday almost-warmth comes a little earlier. No miracle. Just warmth lingering a breath longer under the leaf-eave where the washing hangs and never quite dries.

It would follow the red-capped child — everywhere at once, forbidden to climb the dead snag and climbing it anyway — up with eyes only, holding its breath with his mother in her three short yips like a jay, and when his bare, root-hard foot slips on the twin pole gone slick and black, it would be the extra grip in his toes. No one would see. The boy would only laugh louder at his own daring.

It would go at night when the village hangs lashed-on in the dark, platforms creaking, frogs still calling, babies crying next-platform-over, smoke settled low, and it would move from hearth to hearth, not to clear the smoke — they need the smoke, the smoke keeps the midges from eating them alive — but to lay a hand on each broad chest laboring in thin air, and to loosen the low wet cough for an hour, so they can sleep deep enough to dream. In the morning they would wake and cough and keep working, and think they slept well because the rain paused.

It would visit the wall-mending woman's work after she has gone inside. The green hazel withy she wove in and tied with bark strip, bending the tip to where she wants next spring's growth to go. It would bend close, breathe on it — cloud-breath in the morning — and whisper *take*. Not forcing. Inviting. So the wall heals around her work a little faster.

It would speak to the landlords. Not as their master, but as a fellow old tenant. To the weeping trunk uphill: *Ah, she's weeping again.* It would lay its palm in the seep and clear a little channel with a fingertip, so the hundred weeps and trickles run cleaner to the stone-lined rills, to the kitchens and washing places and out to the taro and cress and bitter highland beans in black muck. To the elder holding three platforms and a bridge: it would lean its weight into him during the hard wind that combs the crown, sharing the load for an hour, so no limb drops on a roof-leaf that night.

It would remember the bees. In the morning, with the others, it would say: *Stay, work, the beans are flowering.* And a lost forager in mist would find the log hive by a shaft of pale light the power thinned the crown to let through, just for a minute, catching the mist and the midges and the drip-sparkle.

And outward, downhill, where the fall drops far below to the lowlands in haze — there the power would do its sternest work, which is also love. When surveyors from below, hungry for straight timber, for bearded ancients to make masts and beams, come up the ridge path with their eyes bright, it would thicken the mist. Not a storm. Just water in every other form. It would make the path seem longer, damper, less promising, the bowl smaller and poorer than it is: only a sodden hollow, only midges and frogs and wet wool, nothing worth the haul. It would turn their hearts gently toward home, plant a doubt like a seed: *not worth it.* It would do this again and again, patiently, for generations. A wise benevolence protects the hidden from being saved to death.

It would not stay. It would not be worshipped. If a child stared straight at where it sat on the root-plait, seeing movement in the green resolve into — something — it would let him see only a face under shadow for a moment, a hand where a branch should be, and then it would be leaves again. If the pitch-man dreamed of warm hands easing his cracked knuckles, he would wake thinking his wife had tended him in the night.

Before leaving, it would take off its shoes. It would press its palm down into the deep mattress of root and moss and rotted leaf over old stone, feel water just under the surface, waiting. It would feel the whole bowl breathing. Inhale: mist gathers, leaves fill and tip. Exhale: drip, run, gurgle.

And it would breathe with it, once, deliberately, in gratitude. For a place that does not conquer, but rides. Lashed on, growing, rotting a little at the edges, re-tied, re-woven, damp to the bone and entirely at home.

That is what it would do, if anything.

Leave them alone.

But not alone.
