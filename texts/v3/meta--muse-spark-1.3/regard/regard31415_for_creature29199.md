It would climb.

Out of breath, ears popping on the switchback road, coat soaked through before the top. It would not come as light or thunder. A benevolent and wise power knows better than to arrive shining where everything already glitters wet and yellow.

It would come damp. It would have to.

First, for a long time, it would do nothing at all but be there, which is rarer than it sounds. Sit on an upturned fuel drum in a shed doorway. Stand on a creosoted pier with water slapping the piles underneath and halyards clanking. Ride in the bow of a flat-bottomed boat with spray in its face while a man in yellow oilskins shouts past it about a culvert. It would learn, the way you have to learn up here, to perch and not to look for dry ground.

It would listen. Put-put-put of the single-cylinder diesels. The deeper thrum under the turbine house floor. The white roar of the old sills that never stops. Wind combing chop. Birds quarreling over guts an inch from a gutting knife. Rain hammering tin. And underneath, the loch slapping the piles, all night, while the bare bulbs burn in the mist so a boat in cloud-water can find home.

That is what it would hear: a place working. Not suffering. Working.

Wisdom would keep it from the first mistake. The lowland mistake. To feel pity for wet cuffs that never dry, for red-knuckled hands skinned on winch wire, for wool hats stuck flat, for children who learn to bail before they learn to read, and to think: *I should dry them. Warm them. Lift them down to where life is easier.*

It will not do that.

It understands, faster than any visitor, that dry would kill them. Dry would kill the sphagnum in lime and rust and acid yellow, kill the cotton grass and the sundew, kill the weed beds rising and falling with the seiche, kill the char rolling dark in the calm, kill the eels and the frogs and the otters who leave fish heads on the jetties as rent receipts. Dry the peat and it stops being a sponge and starts being dust. Lower the water and the cages heel in mud. Raise it too high and the causeways drown. Take away the working cold and the midges and the squalls that turn glass to white horses in ten minutes, and you take away the attunement — the men who smell weather before you feel it, who hear a bearing going out under a thrum, who can tell by the pitch of the sill whether the loch is up a foot or down.

They are not stranded. They look stranded at first. They are afloat. They have learned, precariously and loudly, what the deer knew before boats, what the floating bogs know when they calve off after rain and go drifting with their knee-high birch and duck nests: you don't argue with land that moves. You pole around it, or tow it back, or let it go.

A wise power does not un-teach that to teach comfort.

So if it acts at all, it acts the way they act: small, constant, maintenance against reclamation. Not miracle. Upkeep.

It keeps the piles true a little longer where they are driven down through black sponge to rock. It lets the peat build, millimeter by millimeter of dead moss, ten thousand years patient, and does not let a summer of greed cut more than winter can forgive. It steadies the hand on the cage line when the squall comes over the swell without warning. It puts an extra hour of lift in the cloud now and then, so they can see three lochs deep in every direction and remember the scale of where they live, before the cloud comes back down to fifty yards of chop and reed and dripping wire and they remember the intimacy of it.

It does not still the storm for the boat caught out. It rides with it. It makes sure the bulb on the far pier stays lit — just that one filament holding against mist — so the shouting man at the bow has something yellow to steer for. It makes sure the boy who slips off the jetty edge with his feet dangling catches a pile with his cold-clumsy hand, coughs, is hauled up swearing and laughing and wrapped in a blanket that was never dry anyway. Cold water steals heat fast. It knows. It counts heartbeats until he is by the stove.

At lambing week and gutting week it lets the ravens have their share, tumbling in the updraft off the turbine house roof. It lets the gulls steal what is left uncovered, because that is the tax and everyone knows it. Cover your food from gulls and your fuel from rain, the place says, and you will get neither. The power does not repeal that law. It honors it.

It drinks tea, black and scalding, in the concrete house straddling the narrows while water thunders under the floorboards. It does not say who it is. If the turbine man asks how it knew the bearing was going, it shrugs and says it heard it. If the smokehouse woman asks why her twine didn't snap in the frost, it says nothing. Benevolence, up here, is indistinguishable from competence and company.

Mostly it protects them from being *seen wrong* below.

For on a very clear day you can see the lowlands far, far below as a different country. And on a very clear day they can see up. And what they see is resource: more water to turbine, more valleys to cage, more peat to cut, more wild to drain or dam or photograph. The power turns that gaze aside, gently. Puts cloud back on the hilltops a little earlier when the surveyors come. Makes the road a little more switchback, the ears pop a little more, the midges find the calm interval a little faster. Makes the ledgers show that it costs more to take than to leave. Not a curse. A weather.

It leaves the chapel on the one true knoll alone, stones leaning every way in soft peat. It does not straighten them. It reads the names.

And when it leaves — because even powers do not stay, they move like floating bogs — it leaves no dry hill, no still water, no end to rust or green at the edges or pumping every spring. It leaves what was there, held a little longer.

The shed thumping. The sill roaring. The children asleep to loch-slap thinking that is what quiet is. The deer knee-deep in sedge with cotton grass hung from their antlers, unbothered. The ducks blackening a bay when they lift. The men waving you in, waving you off.

If you asked them the next morning if anything holy had passed, they would say no. Only that the night winch held when it should have slipped. Only that the lamp didn't go out. Only that the cold settled out of them faster by the stove.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do in the High Shallows, if anything:

Not rescue. Keep.

Sit a while in the wet. Keep the light on.
