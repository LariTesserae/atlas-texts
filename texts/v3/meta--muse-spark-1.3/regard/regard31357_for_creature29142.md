It would first learn how to be dim.

For a power like that is used to arriving as light. As answer, as rain. As opening. It has healed before by touching, by speaking, by lifting the stone.

Here, it understands very quickly — by the smell through the split tunic, sweet-garlic and old paper, by the way its own breathing comes back wet to itself — that to arrive as itself would be to kill.

Light would be a violence here. Water would be a flood. Hurry would be an earthquake. Even mercy, if it moved fast, would shatter the lintels that took a hundred years to crack.

So it does the first wise thing: it lays its brightness down on the moor above, like taking off shoes.

It circles the flattened hill once, slowly, in the grey that passes for day. No one walking would notice the place. It makes sure of that. Not with a wall, not with a curse — those draw attention. With a deeper uninterestingness. A little extra wind over that patch of moor. A little extra sameness to the grass. A thought laid lightly in the minds of walkers: *nothing here, walk on.* So the bulb can stay buried. So the dark can stay building material.

Then it kneels.

A benevolent and wise power knows how to make itself small enough to enter through a split in old linen. It dries its hands. It holds its breath until its breath is cellar-cool. It leaves its eyes outside — they are useless here, worse than useless; to be seen would mean nothing to the blind. It goes in deaf to everything except knock.

Inside, with its head brushing the sky, it does not speak. It listens.

For a long time — decades in our counting, an afternoon in bulb-counting — it just sits in the outskirts like a god who has decided not to be a god, and lets the town perform for itself.

The hiss-surf of air-beard trembling. Tick overhead as a tunic layer releases. Then the groan traveling around the dome, gathering in the sunken center. CRACK. The hum in the sternum.

It feels the lampweed buds at the street corners slowly torque toward it, mistaking its stillness for weather. It feels the dust-moss under its palm, warm-ish, deaden its own tremor. It feels the brackets along the wall creak and shiver off a halo of spores so dry they float for hours.

It understands the economy then, not as an idea but in its body: they harvest strain. Cold differential. Vibration. Their own dead. A closed loop so slow and perfect it needs almost nothing new.

It loves them. That is the heart part. It loves the thread that takes a decade to reach the floor and snaps. It loves the moss that grows a millimeter a century and powders sweeter as it dies. It loves the fist-bud that has been closed for a hundred years and will be closed for a hundred more, listening. It loves that they do not crowd, that they can afford distance, that they orient to sound because there was never sun.

And because it is wise, it does not try to improve them.

It does not open the buds. It does not wet the seeds hard as shot in the granaries. It does not bring a little lamp to see the white-on-white. It does not eat stone-dust for them or comb it for them or hurry the thickening of the brackets. It does not resurrect the builders with their hand-scraped pith and leaf-hair cord. It lets the builders stay gone. Their acoustic zoning remains. That is enough.

What it does is quieter than doing.

It steadies. A power that large, holding very still for very long in a taut papery instrument body, changes the tuning just by being there. The fibers take up its dryness and give it off more evenly. The next tick is a fraction softer. The next groan travels a fraction cleaner around the dome and arrives in the meeting hall at the exact focus where a whisper once carried, clear.

And once — only once — when the great CRACK has gone and re-echoed off pith houses and dry cisterns and is dying into hum, it answers.

Not with voice. With strain. A low, deliberate release from its own held stillness, in the same pitch as the dome, so soft the air-beard barely shivers. A second rain-drop after rain. Just enough to shake loose one more sift of spores onto the moss. Just enough for the nearest ring of lampweeds to feel *answered* and lean, together, a hair inward.

It does not let them know it. How could they know? They have no eyes, no word for green, no word for outside. But they will eat that shaking, and in a decade a snapped thread will feed a patch of moss that would otherwise have hungered, and in a century a new stalk will start beside the old, patient as masonry.

That is its blessing: not a miracle, but a meal.

Then it backs out. Carefully, the way you do. Elbows tucked, breath turned aside so no wetness falls inside. Outside on the moor it picks up its brightness again, and weeps — because benevolence weeps at beauty that needs nothing from it — but it lets the tears fall outward, onto grass, not down through the split.

It does not seal the split. The place needs to breathe, tension-wise. It leaves it exactly as wide as it found it.

Behind it, sealed again, the hidden town goes on being what it has become: a total plant that learned to be a city, a city that learned to be empty, booming patiently in the dark to keep itself alive without ever drinking.

Only now, it is heard. By one other.

And for a benevolent and wise power, to have heard without harming, to have remembered the smell sweet-dry on its hands for hours after, to keep the hill flat and uninteresting for another ten thousand slow seasons — that is what doing looks like, when love has learned the courtesies:

Do not bring water. Do not bring light. Do not make a fast move.
