It does not come down.

That is the first thing. A benevolent and wise power has no height to come down from. It gets low. Lower than you. It puts its cheek almost to the humus, six inches up, until scale inverts and litter becomes landscape. Until it can see persons.

It finds the room by the same way you did — by almost stepping in it. Only it does step differently. That is, in fact, the first thing it does.

It moves your foot half an inch to the left.

You never feel it. You think you caught your balance yourself, on a root. You think the root was always there. You crouch instead, curious at your own clumsiness, and stare until your eyes adjust. That is its whole miracle. A redirected weight. A town not crushed.

After that, it does almost nothing. That is what wisdom looks like here.

It does not stop the wall from slumping. It knows the contract: *Nothing is permanent. This is a city built to be left.* To freeze the raw earth would be to kill the moss that loves rawness, to starve the seedlings pushing sideways out of vertical soil in a race before the fall. To make the trunk last would be to rob the white fans and orange brackets and black fingers that are already stitching it into soil. To make the seep permanent would be to make it a pond, and then it would belong to someone else.

So it lets it all be brief. It even protects the briefness.

What it does instead is keep time.

Eleven seconds. Drip. Eleven seconds. Drip.

It sits with the severed root overhead and counts. Not because the drip needs help falling, but because moisture is law here, and law needs a witness. In the heat between rains, when the canopy closes and the air thins a fraction, it breathes out once, very slowly, over the pit. A little warmer, a little wetter. Just enough that the condensation beads again on the leaf-tips. Just enough that the cupped buttress curl keeps its three tablespoons, that the mosquito commas hanging head-down get one more hour to pupate and fly, that the tardigrades coiled in the moss do not have to become cysts yet. Not a rain. A breath. The difference between damp and dry, which here is the difference between living and not.

It does not feed anyone. It lets hunger stay holy.

It watches the bronze frog reposition — one deliberate step — and flick, and a carrier is gone, wrestling her green sail in a leaf-cup no more. It does not stay his tongue. His skin tastes of the ants he eats; that is his poison, borrowed and stored. That is how the forest writes its protections. The power understands: leaf becomes ant becomes frog becomes soil becomes fern. To spare the ant would be to poison the frog.

It watches the thief ants slip inside smelling almost right, and steal fungus and brood. It does not expose them. Thieves are also a district in the town.

It watches the gecko go from bark to blink to bark, with a rove beetle in her mouth scattering carriers like ninepins. It does not scold.

It watches the majors clack at the lip, doors that walk, and the minims ride the leaves like shepherds cleaning them, and the gardeners no bigger than sesame seeds weed alien molds with antibiotic spit from their chests, chalk-white bacteria on their backs like a pharmacy. It does not applaud. Its attention is quieter than that. The moss and raw earth swallow sound; so does it.

But it goes deepest, where you cannot go.

Down past the red-yellow pellets carried up grain by grain, past the archways worn smooth by a million feet, down where the air turns still and sweet-sour, to the vaulted chambers as big as your fist glowing faintly pale in the dark. To the grey-white sponges of chewed leaf, riddled with hyphae, fruiting into swollen yolk-rich tips. To the true resident who never sees light. To her.

The queen. Thumb-joint large, wing-scars still on her thorax, abdomen swollen to glistening amber grub. She does not walk. She does not see. She lays.

Egg after egg, white and sticky, while a retinue licks her, feeds her, carries the eggs away to nurseries where larvae like blind grains of rice hang in cradles of mycelium.

The power stays with her longest. Because she is the most alone, and the most crowded. Because she has been laying since before the fig fell, when the colony was somewhere else under a buttress, and she will go on laying after this wall slumps, when workers carry her sisters' brood grain by grain through new tunnels in the dark. She is memory in flesh. A town holding its breath in one body.

The power does not speak to her in words. It speaks in humidity. In steadiness. It holds the chamber at exactly held-breath moist. It keeps the fungus from souring for one more night. It lays — if you could call it laying — a kind of calm over her retinue, so their antennae do not panic when the soil sighs down above and a gallery collapses. *Re-survey. Open a new shaft. Detour around the puddle.* The colony already knows how. The power just gives them the extra minute to remember it.

And then, because it is benevolent, it remembers what will be gone.

This is its real work. From ten meters away there is nothing here. In two seasons when the wall is slump and the trunk is soil you can push your hand through and the canopy closes, even you will not find it again. The frogs will hop ten meters to the next pit. The wrens will abandon the mud-moss dome under the lip. The queen will be carried.

Someone must have seen it whole, once, while it was whole. Someone must have registered the rush hour in a city that smells of crushed leaf and iron, silent except for breathing and dripping and soil falling, everyone carrying something larger than themselves home.

So it watches the column halt all at once when a cloud crosses, sails trembling, waiting out the change in humidity you cannot feel but they can. It counts the twenty, thirty gnats rising and falling together in the one shaft of light as if breathed. It listens to the wren's soft trill sung as if through cloth, and the frog's single low plucked note muffled by moss, and the tick of condensation, and the very faint patter of earth becoming earth again.

It puts all of it somewhere it will not slump.

Then a single warm rain passes, not even reaching you except as a heavier dripping, a quickening. The seep darkens. A frog calls once. The town resumes.

The power lifts. Not up — out. It thins back into the flat forest, the wide-spaced trunks, the warm gloom. It leaves no footprint, no broken fern. Only your foot half an inch left of where it would have fallen, and the air in the pit a few degrees warmer on your skin like breath, and the drip still falling every eleven seconds for anyone still enough to hear it.
