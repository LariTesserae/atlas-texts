It comes down the way you did. Slowly.

Not because it must. Because there is no other way to enter the Hollow without breaking it. If it came as powers are supposed to come — with light, with thunder, with weather — the veils of airwort would tear, ten thousand martins would blind and fall, the vent-hum would stutter and the nurseries deep in the towers would chill. So it dims itself.

It makes itself cool, and dry, and quiet. It makes itself the size of a person standing in the plaza with white boots. It comes at grey noon when the light is already dying and lets the dusk cover it.

At first it is tempted, as all benevolent things are tempted.

It is tempted to bring water. It sees cracked lips, salt flowers that never wash, sap that is not sap but oil, birds making mud without mud, stealing saliva to make mortar. It thinks: *one spring, one seep in the cliff, what mercy that would be.* 

And then it lays its palm to a tower and feels the warmth — two degrees above air — and understands. A seep would not be mercy. It would be rot. Things here do not rot; they desiccate and are used again. Water would unmake the cement, melt the tenements under the lips, wake fungi that are not the tame fungus-wool of the gardens but killers. The fleece that grows a millimeter a year by combing dust would drown in a season. To give water here would be like flooding a library to give the books a drink.

So it withdraws that hand.

It is tempted to bring silence. Because it stands in the plaza when the pulse lifts — twenty thousand wings at once, doubled off the east and west walls, mason-fans humming in the sternum, crickets laying down their metallic sheet until thought is impossible — and it thinks with human pity: *these poor things, deafened by their own living.* It wants to soften the bowl, to part the sound and let it escape up past the rim.

And then it stays till the pulse passes, and hears what returns. The echo is not a curse. It is how the Hollow keeps what it has. Sound that stays lifts dust that feeds fleece that feeds mason. Nothing leaves, not even a wingbeat. To give silence would be to starve them.

So it does not give silence. It listens until its ears ring.

It is tempted to bring light, to cut the walls lower, to let the sun in for more than a brief grey noon. To give eyes to the blind masons, wings to the crawling ones, speed to the slow architects.

And then it crouches by a boulevard for an hour and watches the line not break. Outbound left, inbound right, each blind mason tapping its antennae twice on a boot that is to them only weather, a pebble laid down a century ago. It sees they do not need eyes. They have the road, which is older and surer than sight. It sees the tower grow a finger's width in a season and understands that slowness is not failure. It is the form of permanence here.

So it does nothing it came to do.

What it does instead is smaller, and therefore fit for a wise power:

It sits.

It lays its great palm flat against the quilted side of one of the five oldest towers, where collapse was built over without clearing a century ago, and it lends its own warmth for a while, so the fanners deep inside can rest. Just a few degrees. Just enough that the vent exhales a little easier. No one notices. That is the point.

It lets a mason column cross over its hand without lifting it, feeling each tap-tap like rain that never comes.

It picks up a dead swift — perfect mummy, hollow eyes, feathers intact — and does not carry it out, does not bury it, does not resurrect it. It lays it back down on the salt where crickets will find it, where in a month it will be powder, in a year it will be wall. It returns the dead to the wheel.

When the nightjars lift all at once without call, a soft thunder up over the rim to hawk dust-moths in last light, it does not watch them go. It watches the ribbonbush they leave behind, still trembling, empty scale-nests warm together, and it guards that emptiness until they return with outside shit into inside.

And before it leaves, it does the only truly powerful thing it can do and still be benevolent:

It goes back up to the rim, to the plateau of pale gravel and heat-haze that never warms, and it stands there a long time, making itself like wind-polished pebbles, like nothing. So that when others come later — the curious, the hungry, the well-meaning with drills and dams and plans to green the desert — they will see only an empty trough, hear only wind, and turn away.

It does not tell them the City glows. It does not tell them where to listen.

It keeps the Hollow unremarkable.

Only the Hollow itself is allowed to remember that for one grey noon, during one pulse when you could not hear your hands clap, something vast knelt in the dust among the thatchbird trees that were houses that were chimneys, and hummed, very low, in tune with the vents, and was glad — without fixing, without saving, without staying — to be for a little while food for nothing, but witness to everything.
