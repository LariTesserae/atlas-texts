It comes at night, because that is when the place is making itself.

Not noon. Noon is white and occupied and panting, and any power that arrived at noon would cast a shadow and steal shade. It comes in the coldest hour before dawn, when the cloud-sea is still down and the steam is ghosting off the eastern fissure, when the pillar-tops are beading.

It does not come as weather. It has learned not to do that.

For a long time it just sits. That is the first thing a wise and kind attention does in a place with no empty room: it finds where to put its feet without crushing a cup.

It puts them on the planks. Single-plank boards, laid across pillar tops. It walks sideways between the teeth like she does. It reads, because it can read sun-crazed paint: CHECK VALVES / LEAVE CUPS FULL TILL 9. And it obeys.

That surprises you, if you expected a miracle. It obeys the local rule.

It presses a palm flat to basalt, as you did, and feels the residual heat from deep down, the thin lid over something hot and patient. It feels the pump sigh-shudder through its molars at the hour appointed, and does not stop it. It hears the ticking of the cooling metal, the green LED blinking in dust, the huddle-breath of rock-runners deep in the slots with tails wrapped over noses to save moisture. It counts without counting. Thousands upon thousands of cups. Thousands of swifts backed into mud-and-guano with beaks open. Three lizards to a crevice. Families of eight or twelve, memorizing squeezes.

It understands very quickly: life here does not run on leaves. It runs on each other and on sky-water that will not last the day.

And so it will not bring what you think benevolence brings.

It will not make it rain. An eleven-minute black shaft is a generation here. More than that would wash the whitewash streaks off the nesting pillars, drown the blood-worms in their own cups, fill the slots where the runners hide, rot the swift nests. Abundance would be a flood.

It will not make it green. Green would bring goats, and goats would bring men with more pipes, and more pipes would mean the tanks at the saddle would no longer be for this mesa but for somewhere below the cloud. The straw-grass every twenty steps, the orange and black lichen painted tight to the north sides, the green-gray line that follows where the buried pipe sweats cold at night — that is enough green. That is exactly enough to trace water without spending it.

It will not cool the sun. The sun must be white and ordinary. It will not still the wind that flutes low across the pillar-tops. The wind must come and go.

It will not take the machines away. This is the wisdom part, and the hardest for a power that loves wild places. The bore-holes neat and dark, the one-way wicks, the stout black pipe at ankle height humming faintly, the fog-net sagging with morning wet, the dust-filmed solar array ticking as it tracks — these are not a wound anymore. The lichen grows thickest along them. The swifts nest on the warm transformer box because it is always warm. The wren-like bird nests behind it on stripped wire insulation and swift feathers and drinks off the panels at cloud-rise. The moths come to the green blink and the lizards have learned to hunt by it. To rip the pipes out would be to rip out a river.

And it will not fix the leak. This is how you know it stayed long enough to be wise.

At the pipe joint on the western traverse where a washer never quite sealed, every morning there is a queue, orderly, ill-tempered, squeaking. Two drink while one sits upright, nose twitching, watching for kestrel shadow. Then they rotate. They prefer it because it drips ten minutes longer into the heat, shaded by the pipe itself. A lesser kindness would tighten it. This one leaves it dripping.

What does it do, then, this benevolent attention, if it does not rescue?

Small, almost invisible things. The kinds of things she does.

It stays till nine without drinking. It lets the swifts skim first, lower mandible kissing water for half a second, bathing on the wing. It lets the runners finish their shifts. It lets the lizards lick lichen before it evaporates.

Then, after nine, when the cups are dry white rings of mineral and guano and everyone is sheltering and it is permitted to move water:

It wipes, with a breath of cooler cloud, the dust film off the solar array so it tracks without ticking. It clears a wick with a fingertip where guano had crusted. It steadies the gauge-wheels at the buried tanks so they read true and she does not have to guess. It holds the night one breath longer — just one — so condensation beads a little thicker, so every cup is brimmed to trembling, so the pumps have a full sigh to move. Not a second rainy season. Ten minutes. A finger's width.

It keeps the heat below steady. Not out, not up. Steady, so the stones stay faintly alive to sit on at midnight, so the sulfur field out east stays yellow and eggy and empty — a place for bacteria alone, patient as warmth.

It works the edges for the kestrels, so they do not need to break the noon truce. A lifted thermal off the far rims, a fat lizard a little slower on a sun-shoulder far from the center. So the pale hunters can hang motionless and drop folded like a knife sheathing without stooping through the panting middle.

And for her — the lean one, burnt dark at neck and forearms, white where her hat shades, thin-blood slow, sun-cracked hands, who moves only on planks and shelters at midday in the stripe of shade behind the hut with mouth open breathing fast and shallow — for her it does the smallest thing of all.

It sits beside her.

At midnight, when the swifts have funneled out over the rim and the gnats have settled and the machines tick as they cool, she sits on the stone that holds heat longest and feels it through her clothes. The sky huge and ordinary and star-crowded does nothing strange at all. The power sits with her there, another warm body that knows not to block a drip-line, not to make noise at midday, to wait for water like everyone else.

It does not speak in words. It breathes with her, a little slower, so her lungs — that high, thin pull when you try to take a full breath and can't quite finish it — can finish it. It eases the pop in her ears when she swallows. It lets her know, without startling the runners, that the swept walkways, the bled valve, the cups left full till nine even when the cistern is low, have been seen. Have been counted as part of the ecology. Have been loved not as sacrifice but as membership.

In the morning she will wake and find the cistern a little fuller than the gauges promised, the nets wiped, the panels clean, her hands a little less cracked. She will frown, check the valves, listen with her molars, assume the cloud rose a little higher in the night. She will leave the natural cups alone till nine.

Nothing will look different to anyone flying over the island-tops floating beyond the cloud. The tableland will still extend past what you can comfortably walk. The teeth will still be shoulder to shoulder. The thin air will still eat sound.

Only this: the lease holds. Drink before the sun takes it, hide through the white heat, steal a second drink when the cloud climbs. For one more season, and one more, the crowded, humming, panting, guano-white, lichen-tight, pipe-sweating, swift-whirring place continues — not saved, not spared, but kept.

That is what a wise kindness does when there is no room that is not used.

It makes just enough room for tomorrow's water, and then it waits with them for the cloud to rise again.
