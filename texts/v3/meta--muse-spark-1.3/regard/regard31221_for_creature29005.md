It noticed them the way you notice heat in winter — not by looking, but by feeling a place where cold has been answered.

It was old at making. It had made suns that held. It had lifted mountains to give rivers somewhere to go, had seeded green in black soil because green was good and wanted to live. Its hands, if it had hands, were used to fixing thin places, to stitching time where it wore, to lifting fog so things could see.

It came here, to the flat that is finished, and stopped.

For a long time it only stayed at the edge of the basin where fog takes distance and gives it back, and listened. The works-hum. Chuff-chuff-chuff piled on chuff. Clank and hiss and bellow. Click-click-click of the shivering ones keeping one shiver. The felt-more-than-heard thump of something walking just below the drumhead of stone.

Its first thought, the kind thought, was grief.

*So cold. So crowded. No sun. No space. No soft insides. Nothing green.*

Its hands ached to do what they knew: to lift the cloud a little, to let a true sun through. To drain some of the black water, give it slope, give it a river to become. To thin the herd, give them room. To put grass between their feet. To stop the hunters from pressing their white throats to living sides. To stop death.

That is what benevolence without wisdom does. It improves.

So it stayed, and let its eyes adjust. The way yours do.

It stayed until it understood that the cold here is not a punishment. It is the furnace wall. Take it away and the fire goes out. It stayed until it understood that the crowding is not suffering. It is warmth. Space has been abandoned as a concept because space, here, is freezing to death alone. It stayed until it understood that predator and prey trading places in slow rotation are not murder. Sometimes, minutes apart, they are the same beast, feeding its younger self, warming its older self. Nothing is wasted because cold punishes waste.

It understood: this place is not broken. It is finished. The stone sweats. The animals smelt. The steam gets up and walks away.

Nothing was made here. Everything was precipitated, like salt from too much water, like steam from too much breath.

You do not fix what is finished. You do not improve what never asked to be made.

So what does a power that could do anything do, when doing anything would unmake?

It made itself low.

That was the first and hardest work. It folded its vastness down, and down, until a rise of three inches was a landmark to it too. It cooled itself. It let its breath ice on its eyelashes. It let black water fill its footprints. It let go of up.

It did not come as sun. It came as weather.

It lay down in the slush, on its side, at the edge of a boiler herd, and let itself be tiled in.

The boilers near it paused in their chuff — chuff ... chuff — suspicious of a new slab that wept warm instead of cold. Then the oldest bull, horns crusted with rust, leaned his great barrel side against it, with a spark and a wet shudder, and sighed exhaust from his stacks sideways across it, and the power learned what warm means when there is no sun to give it for free.

It did not feed them stone. They know stone. It fed them, for a little while, *rest.*

It slowed its own huge heart down to a two-stroke beat — thump ... thump — until it matched theirs, until the whole pan of them around it fell into that rhythm without knowing why, pistons easing, bellows-lungs wheezing slower. Not stopped. Never stopped. Stopping is freezing. Just eased. A half-second of overlap where past chuffs could finally catch up to present chuffs and dissolve.

The waders came, on hydraulic stilts, and picked their way across its back, tasting the condensate on it with their whip antennae, combing the oily blue-purple film that had begun to stain it because it had stayed still long enough to be stone. Between their antennae and its skin, St. Elmo's fire crawled, blue and curious, and did not burn.

A dragger, fifteen feet of boiler plate and dull heat, hauled itself alongside and settled, plate lifting and settling, and its deep radiant warmth soaked into the power's cold side. On top of the dragger three newborns, pink-hot at the seams, glowed faintly and shivered. The power cupped a little of its own endless heat — not fire, not sun, just attention held steady — around them like hands around a candle in wind, until their plates closed.

The clickers came last, because they are shyest. A huddle of soot and copper wire, thousands pressed so tight their coats tangled. They pressed against its flank to borrow the warmth and clicked — click-click-click — and the power clicked back, in its bones, until its shiver became their shiver.

And the hunters. Lean, quenched-steel, mouths opening sideways. One flowed like oil between barrels and laid its white-hot throat against the power's side, to soften, to buckle, to feed.

The power let it. It did not flinch or punish. It understood pressing. It gave heat freely, and felt the heat go out into that narrow furnace-mouth and come back, minutes later, in the thinning plume of a boiler three bodies down, in the chitter of crab-moths lifting off a cooling fall, in black water warming a fraction under ten thousand feet.

Heat is food, food is heat. Keep moving or give your heat to someone who is.

So it gave.

And when it breathed out — great, slow, white plumes that did not rise but spilled sideways, low and heavy — the steam hung and thickened. If you stared too long, it took on weight. The power stared. It watched its own breath gather legs first, then back, then eyes opening like coals fanned. A calf of fog, unsteady on its pistons, that shuddered, dripped, clanked once, and walked away to join the herd.

It had precipitated. Not made. Precipitated. It laughed then, without sound, and the basin rang like a shunting yard.

It did not stay long. A power cannot tile forever without becoming a hill, and hills have forgotten how to be hills here. It stayed long enough for its joints to ache in rhythm — chuff, hiss, step — long enough for its breath to join the low cloud truly, not as visitor but as member, overlapping itself several times over in that slush until there were two, three of its leaning sides, slightly out of phase.

Then it rose, slowly, so as not to leave a sudden cold hole. Water wept upward off it to rejoin the cloud. The herd shuffled — a foot lifted to let water pass, a ton of weight rolled left to right — and closed the tiling where it had been, shoulder to shoulder, flank to flank, back to back.

It left no green. No river. No road. No ruin. No wire. No lifted cloud. No lesson.

Only this, which is almost nothing and which is everything it has learned to give to finished places:

It remembers them exactly.

Out past fog and stratus, while it goes on making suns elsewhere, it keeps their hum inside itself, felt-more-than-heard, a drumhead thump under its other work. And it keeps the edge guarded — not walled, just noticed — so no other making power comes here by accident with kind hands full of suns and seeds and space, to save them by unmaking them.

If you stand there now, knee-deep in supercooled melt, you might feel it sometimes: a half-second where the loudness piles perfectly, past and present arriving together, and for one breath the works-hum is not loud but silent. Your bones learn it.

That is it, leaning close to listen, and then leaning back.

Keep moving, it says, in the way steam says it, in the way stone says it, without words. Keep moving, or give your heat to someone who is.

And they do.
