It would come the way you came.

Over the last shoulder, on foot. Not all at once. It would make itself small enough to have to climb.

That is the first thing wisdom would tell it: if you arrive here large, you will only break things. The air is too thin for thunder. So it would fold its weather away and come as a shape the size of a body, with a chest that has to work a little harder to breathe, with lips that crack.

And for a long while it would do nothing at all but be deafened.

It would stand in the bowl and let the notch wind hammer through it. Let the white noise fill its ears until its own thoughts feel muffled. Let the light hurt. Benevolence, here, cannot begin with fixing. There is nothing to fix. The pans neither fill nor empty. The spiders have already eaten. The choughs are already screaming *chee-ow, chee-ow* loud enough to cut rock.

So it would sit down. That would be its first act: to lower itself.

Not on a throne slab. Low. Kneeling by a cushion dome, the way you have to kneel to see anything at all. Hands — if it wears hands that day — resting on shale that is warm on the sun side and ice on the shade side. It would learn, quickly, what every resident knows: stay low or be flayed.

And then it would watch. Really watch, which is rarer than miracles.

It would watch the pebble that walks resolve into a wolf spider, soot-black to drink the sun, her back seething with a gray mantle of young. It would see her go torpid in winter under the slab, and see the young eat the unviable eggs off her abdomen, and then each other, until only the strongest three are left for spring.

And it would not intervene.

This is where benevolence has to be wise or it becomes cruelty. A lesser power would feel the pang and "help" — warm the slab, fatten the air, stop the eating. Make it kinder. Make it lowland. And in a season the whole point would unravel. No hunger, no trip-lines. No trip-lines, no spiders. No spiders, no choughs. The bowl does not run on kindness as the valleys count kindness. It runs on exactness. On the sky snowing stunned midges every day without fail, and mouths waiting exactly where they need to be.

So the power would keep its hands in its lap. It would bless the mother by not flinching. By understanding that her hunger is holy, too.

It would watch the black beetles bulldozing pollen at midday in their inch of heat, their wing-cases fused shut millions of years ago because they made a vow: we will not fly where flying is punished. Seven, eight years old, some of them. Older than any bird in the flock. And the power, which is very old, would bow to something older in its own way — endurance — and would not offer them wings.

At noon it would tilt its head back into the pressure on the sternum, the ten thousand wings like surf, and it would listen *under* the roar, as you learn to do if you stay still long enough. Under: tick of tarsi on shale. Plink of condensation from overhang into black mirror. Thin whine of the true midges, wingless, slate-gray, hopping flea-like inside their single cushion continent. Blood in its own borrowed ears. The low A and a fifth above it humming through the wind-holes.

It would hear the bowl breathing, and it would breathe with it, on purpose. In. Out. Condensation hanging a moment, then ripped away. That would be its second act: to keep time, not to keep score.

Then the learning. It would wait for it, all afternoon if needed, sun hammering its neck.

A young red-bill lands by a wind-hole. Grass stem in the curved red beak. Probe. Nothing. Drop it. Pick up another, shorter. Probe deeper, twist. A spider bolts —

And here, benevolence would almost break. The power would know exactly how that story ends. It could whisper: *to the left. Deeper.* It could leave a better tool, a sharper shard of obsidian, chipped — not broken — to an edge that does not occur naturally. It could make the spider slower.

It will do none of that.

Because to steal the miss is to steal the bird. What makes the chough a resident and not a visitor is not that it eats, but that it *learns* to eat here, with no hands to spare and fingers that would freeze anyway. The stripped willow twig sharpened at one end, left by the tarn edge — hand or beak, last season or this morning — is precious precisely because no power left it. It was figured out.

So the power would hold its breath, the way you do, and when the bird finally gets it — neat, fast, its own — it would feel something embarrassingly large swell in its small borrowed chest. Joy. Unshared. Unannounced. That would be its third act: to rejoice without being thanked.

Later, near the blue edge of evening when the light thins but does not leave, it would walk to the bone-anvils and pick up a splinter, white on white quartz, and put it back down exactly where it was. It would look up once at the turners, the pair of lammergeiers turning slow circles in the lenticular's rotor without ever seeming to flap, rust-stained, thirty years old, who know every gust in the notch by name. Undertakers. Memory. The power would nod to its colleagues. *You hold the dead. I hold the living. We do the same work.*

It would not stay the night. Not because it cannot, but because thousands of bodies shoulder to shoulder on narrow cracks do not need a witness to their muttering and preening. Some things are only whole unwatched.

Before it goes back down over the shoulder, it would do one thing — if you can call it a thing.

It would kneel once more by a pan, black mirror no deeper than a wrist, rimmed with black moss and orange lichen. It would not drink measurably. The finches taught it how: a quick dip that takes nothing. It would look at its own reflection broken by pollen on the edge — a marker that has not moved in an hour, in ten thousand years — and it would leave a blessing that is not a change.

*May you neither fill nor empty. May the cloud neither form nor dissipate, but slowly turn. May the insect snow never thicken and never cease. May the wind keep combing the same polygons, polishing the same pebbles, whistling the same chord. May nothing accumulate here. May nothing be taken.*

*May hands that come carry everything in and out, and leave only gestures the wind can read.*

Then it would stand, and make itself even smaller — small as a sesame-seed fly glittering as it comes down — and let the western notch take whatever it does not need back down to the forests three thousand feet below.

If you came the next morning, you would find nothing. No footprint — frost heaved it out. No stacked stone — wind took it within the season. Perhaps a willow twig, stripped and sharpened at one end, dropped by the tarn edge. Perhaps a flake of obsidian among identical natural shards.

You could walk past a dozen times and think them natural.

That is how you would know it had been there.
