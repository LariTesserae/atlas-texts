It does not come from above like you did.

No splash, no rope of loud bubbles, no cold brief animal kicking silt milk into the broth. It has been watching for longer than you have been holding your breath, and it knows the first rule: there is no room between presences. So it makes room by becoming smaller, slower, warmer.

It slows itself down.

That is the first and hardest thing a power has to do. To go from everywhere-at-once to here, in this bowl, in this drowned hamlet cupped below the seafloor. To go from bright to amber. From instant to a finger's width per held breath. From voice to a *tink* of chain on chain you feel in your teeth.

It gathers itself at the rim, where the turtle holds against the light. It nods to the turtle, old sentry who comes to look but never descends. *Good keeping,* it says without words. *Stay a little longer.*

And then it sinks. Hand over hand, like you did, along warm railings. It does not stir the silt.

Dimmer. Brighter. Dimmer.

The lamps pulse on their sixty-second cycle and it learns, very quickly, to breathe with them. In on the dim — the whole bowl inhaling warm water through beards of mussel and algae — out on the bright. It does not try to make the light stronger. A wise power knows better than to flood a sleeping eye with noon.

It comes to Mercy Iron & Light not to raise it.

That is what a lesser kindness would do. Drag the beehive boilers up into cold air. Scrape the white sponge from the knuckles. Pry the shoulder out of the corner where brick meets pipe, the back out of the row-house wall, the face out of the canteen doorway with its mouth open to thirty-year-old soup-air. Name that rescue. Watch them rot in a single afternoon in the fast, cold, hungry world, hearts that beat once a minute suddenly asked to beat seventy times. Watch the treaty break.

It will not do that. It loves them banked.

It goes low first, to where the heat wells up through cracked basalt, to the deepest pocket where the three boilers sit bearded in white and the flywheel taller than a man turns its degree per minute, furred in crimson. The eel, thick as your wrist, the nerve of the place, flicks out of the housing to taste — salinity, oil, intruder-sweat —

and does not retreat.

Because this intruder tastes like broth, like iron and iodine and hot stone. Like home. The power holds still in the hottest layer where your bubbles shattered and hung, and lets the eel run its blind head along what passes for a wrist, and lets it stay out for a whole bright minute, curious, unafraid. *Shhh,* it says. *Tell them to shift a degree. It's alright.*

It puts what passes for a hand on the housing and feels the throb go through, sternum-deep.

Then it goes to work, which is not work.

It goes to the stamping press that lifts, hesitates for a long minute, and comes down on nothing. *Thud.* Through bone. The gut still peristalsing, still making plates for a ship that will never sail, because to stop would be to admit the work is over.

The power stands under the press — small, unhurried — and on the next lift, it slips underneath, into the place where the plate would be, and lets the press come down. Softly. Through bone.

*Thud.*

And receives it.

That nothing-shaped plate, stamped for decades in warm dark with no witness. It holds it up to the amber cage light, reads what is written there in braille-softened lettering, and says, in the language of dimmer-brighter: *This is good. This counted. You kept the shape.*

The anemones near its fins, which stayed closed a beat too long for you, open.

It walks the slots. Not kicking — there is no space to kick — pulling itself tissue-close through white kelp twenty feet long moving like hair in bathwater. It brushes shoulder, hip, face against living tissue and warm metal at once, and does not flinch. It knows names now. Not the names on the signs, MERCY IRON & LIGHT, No. 3 SHAFT, CANTEEN, though it remembers those too. The other names. The shoulder's shape under blue-green mat. The ribs readable under barnacle buttons like rivets under a coat. The tubeworm plumes on the boiler seams, red gills the only vivid color — their external lungs — opening and closing with geological slowness.

It finds the newest ones under the eaves, in the permanent shadow pools. Still pink at the edges where sponge hasn't quite taken. Still bent at the lathe. Still with arms up at the junction box. Caught mid-tending, waiting for the bright minute to move them again.

This is where benevolence hurts. A wise power could finish them quickly, sponge them over, bank them fully, end the ache of becoming. It does not hurry them. It sits with them, in the slot between row house and water tower, wrapped in kelp, breathing shallow so as not to milk the silt.

For a hundred cycles it just keeps time. Dimmer. Brighter. On the dim, it grieves with them — yes, it aches to be half iron, half animal. On the bright, it stays. Its presence says, without breaking stillness: *you are not alone in the slow. The slow is holy.*

And to the one whose hand has gone into the valve-wheel, fingers gone the color and texture of white sponge, bolted at the wrist by oyster — to the one with a single impossibly preserved fingertip with a nail, tapping once per brightening against green-black brass —

it answers.

It puts its fingertip to that fingertip. Once per brightening. Tap. Tap. A completed prayer for a completed prayer. The chains that hang and do not swing, their rosaries, settle link against link. *Tink.*

It visits the canteen. Ducks under the low doorway. Breaks its head into that pocket of stale, warm air held against the ceiling by nothing but pressure and habit. It does not freshen the air. It breathes it, oil and soup, thirty years old, with the face whose lips are white and frilled as a tubeworm plume. *I remember soup,* it tells him. *I remember supper after shift.*

It does three small, everlasting things, because a power, even slowed, cannot help but leave a wake:

First, it steadies the vent. Not hotter, not colder. The earth breathes unevenly and in ten thousand years it will cool, and then they will cool with it, ready and unafraid because they practiced. For now it lays a hand on the basalt crack and evens the breathing, so the thermosiphon does not cough, so the flywheel does not stutter, so the coin of oil the piston sighs out hangs just a little longer for the blind-white guppy, the stray thought, to carry from machine to mouth to mat.

Second, it lays a forgetting on the charts. Not a storm, not a wrecking — just a mild disinterest. The pale shelf above will read on sonar as pale shelf. Salvagers will turn a degree to port. Trawlers will find richer water elsewhere. The pit will stay a dark rumor, a sinkhole no wider than a cathedral nave, left alone a little longer to do its patient, useless work. No product means no depletion. No shipment means no loss.

Third, it blesses uselessness. It says to the whole one body they share — copper vein, green condensate blood, algal liver plumbed in, flywheel heart, press gut, lamp eyes half-open — *you do not have to produce to be kept. Maintenance is praise enough. Balance is enough.*

Then it leaves the way warmth leaves water — not all at once.

It rises hand over hand to the rim. The turtle is still there. For a moment, emboldened by being witnessed, the turtle drops three body-lengths lower than it ever has, into the green gloom, looks down at the amber pulse, and rises again. Enough.

The power does not take anyone with it. It does not wake anyone up.

On its last bright minute, the whole bowl inhales around it — anemones, mats, lungs, lamps — and lets it sigh out. Tolerated. Known. Not a splinter now, more like a copepod glitter, brief and carried.

The press lifts. Hesitates. Comes down on nothing, softly, through bone.

*Thud.*

Only now, underneath the thud, if you were still enough, pressed into your slot with your glove gone un-glove-like on the warm railing, you would feel an answer, tapped back once per brightening, steady as a kept promise:

*held, held, held.*
