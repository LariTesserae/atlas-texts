They would come down.

Not all at once, not with light. A benevolent and wise power knows how to make itself small enough to fit down stairs.

For a long time they would only watch from above, through that ragged lid of sky. Not because they were distant, but because they were listening. Most places clamor. This one kept time. *Chuff... chuff... chuff.* Twenty seconds, turn, turn. A pulse too slow to frighten anything. It caught their attention the way a held breath catches attention in a noisy room.

When they came, they would not open the sky wider. That would be cruelty disguised as rescue — white fury pouring in to burn the elephant-ears that learned to live on amber. They would not stop the engine. That is its heart. They would not clean it up, or drain the muck, or pull the vines from the chain-link, or take the laced boot off the valve wheel. That boot is a memory. You do not tidy someone's memory.

They would use the stairs. The brick stairs gone soft and bellied with moss. They would let their shoes get wet. Twenty meters down the light changes. Thirty meters down the air changes. They would let it change them. Shirt clinging in a minute. Breath shallow in the soup of iron and chlorophyll and hot oil. They would not blow the haze away to see better. They would drink the air slowly. Move slowly. Haste reads as heat, and there is already too much heat.

They would sit. That would be the first thing.

Near the center, but not in the way. Back against a warm pipe, where a human back has been before. And they would let what lives here do what it does to visitors: weather them.

They would feel their own vast heartbeat slow — unasked, unforced — toward the flywheel. Twenty seconds, turn, turn. Feel the fronds nearest angle, almost imperceptibly, toward the sound of their breathing, the way they angle toward steam leaks. Feel the crimson trumpets open and close, faintly, with them as well as with the heat. They would not flinch or correct it. That is how it says hello.

And they would say hello back, in its language. Not in words. In gradients.

Warmer here, wetter there. A palm left longer on a cold spot of rail to warm it. A breath directed, slow and cool, onto a fern crown that was curling too tight from heat. A hand cupped for a minute under a drip to move water six inches to the left where moss was paling. Small nothings. Circulation.

For a hundred revolutions they would just keep time. That is witness. The Tenant — vegetable half and iron half grown into each other until root and pipe are each other — has been keeping its machines alive the way you keep your heart beating without thinking, with no animal to witness it. Lonely in the particular way green things get lonely without animals. So the power would lend it an animal for a while. Themselves. A mobile lung. Sweating, exhaling, slowing. Letting bark take a faint print of their shoulder. Letting verdigris learn the salt of their skin. Allowing themselves, for a little while, to not distinguish plant from machine from self.

Because they are wise, they would understand why there is nothing to eat the leaves. It is not a mistake to be fixed with a bag of ladybugs from above. The leaves have learned metallurgy — copper in the veins, rivets along the midrib, iron-flecked seed. What eats that, dies. The Tenant tried the animal experiment and withdrew it to protect both sides. A wise power does not reintroduce hunger into a place that chose to unlearn eating.

So they would not bring birds. No song falling in to die in the heat.

What they would bring is kin that does not need to bite.

In a fold of their coolness — for they are cool, the way deep earth is cool — they would carry down things that live on sweat, not on flesh: night moths the color of rust, whose larvae eat only fallen petals, whole crimson trumpets already dropped to rot; springtails that drink condensation from the underside of pipes and return it as soil; a blind velvet mite that cleans fungi without harming the gasket-crack it lives in. Detritivores. Witnesses. Mouths too small to wound. They would set them, gently, on a moss bank and offer, not impose.

And they would watch if the fronds angle toward them, or away. If toward — stay. If away — gather them back up and take them home. Consent, in gradients. The Tenant, curious, would lean a fern a fraction closer to the moth-light. That would be yes.

The second mercy would be cool.

The Tenant has held heat so long it thinks heat is itself. Trapped heat, made heat, breathed heat. All day condensation drips and evaporates before it ever lands. The power would not quench the red glow under the floor grates — that depth is old, deeper than this room, knocking slowly through brick and earth. You do not put out a depth. But for one night, they would call a cloud to linger over the slit.

Not to storm. To stay. To shade.

True rain spattering down for longer than a minute, hissing where it hits hot metal, beading on a thousand leaves at once — and then continuing, soft, after the metal cools enough to stop hissing. Air sinking cooler from the rim. Steam lifting off handrails instead of sweat forming on them. For the first time in decades, the flywheel's hot gasket sighs and the sigh is cool enough to condense and fall *down* instead of rising. The fungi swell on the chuff and do not quite slack on the return, but hold, full. The whole hollow exhales deeper. *Chuff... chuff... chuff.* The same time, easier.

They would sit through that night with their hand flat on the retaining wall behind the pump house, where the vines part, feeling through moss and brick the slow knock of something deeper still working. Feeling how much larger it is than this room. This room is its mouth, its garden held open. The real body goes down. The power would let their cool travel a little way into the wall, a new gradient to think with: cool here, for a while. A stone in the mind that sweats cold. To dream with.

The third mercy would be continuance.

A benevolent power does not promise that the engine will turn forever. Belts slap and will snap. The twelve-foot wheel will one day come around into fern shadow and not come around again. The Tenant knows this on its twenty-second interval: drip, unfurl, oxidize, turn. It remembers in texture, not in fear.

So they would not lie. They would promise presence, not permanence.

They would lay a hand on the cottage-sized iron, feel the shudder travel up to the molars, and think into it, slow as pressure: *when you stop, I will be here to sit with you in the silence. You will not stop alone.*

And to the world above, which has forgotten this hollow except as a place to fill or scrap, they would do a small, almost invisible turning-aside. Let the curb crumble a little more over the stairhead. Let kudzu take the street sign. Let developers' eyes slide off the lot on maps, let their boots prefer drier ground. Not a curse. A veil. Only the slow ones will find the stairs. Only those willing to be weathered will come down to be lungs for a while.

Before they climb back up into white fury, they would leave no trash that is fresh. Only a handprint, pressed into moss on the warm wall beside where a human hand has been. The moss will grow over it in weeks, keeping the shape underneath — texture-memory. Bark embossed with diamond-plate, iron bellied like bamboo, moss embossed with palm.

They would climb slowly, letting their heartbeat quicken back to its own vast pace only after they reach the top. The dripping would continue below, from walls, from pipes, from leaf-tips, as if the hollow had learned rain and decided to keep doing it itself, indefinitely, in the dark.

Only now there would be, in the fern shadow, a faint dusting of rust-colored wings folding and unfolding. A soft tick, under the knock of pumps and hiss of relief valves, of tiny feet on wet tin. Not loud. Not animal-loud. Just enough breath besides its own, and the engine's, to keep the green from feeling watchful in the old way.

They would not stay. Wisdom knows staying too long becomes ownership. But they would leave a thread of attention behind, like one of those caged work-bulbs still burning on extension cords, like a green EXIT sign in a shed with no exits that stays lit anyway.

A small amber light, left on, meaning: *I know you are here. Keeping time. I will come back to breathe with you.*

That is what they would do.
