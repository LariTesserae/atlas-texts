I came to the rim in the morning and looked down.

From above it doesn't look like much. White flat, thorn scrub, heat already coming up off the stones. And then the cut. A straight rectangle down, as if somebody pressed a shape into dough and lifted it away. Work-light amber still on down there though the sun is up. A thread of diesel smoke. The slap of wings rising and falling back.

I have learned not to start with fixing. Fixing is usually a way of not looking.

So I looked a long time. I learned the shape of the breathing: heat in by day trapped, water up from below weeping, water up by pump chugging, water down again as sweat and drip and post-rain. Chug-chug-chug ... chug-chug-chug. A heart that never wins and never stops.

And I saw what lived there was not just sixty-odd people and their sheds. It was one being with many skins. Pit-thing. Concrete pads for bones, pipe for veins, pigeons for restless thoughts circling, frogs for a throat that trills day and night, rats for night blood moving low, mold and algae and moss for a damp fur. The humans tend the heart. The animals make the noise that tells it it's alive.

You don't rescue a being by cutting out its heart.

At noon I went down. Not all at once in glory — glory would have blinded them and slumped the walls. By ladder, rung over rung where the rebar bristles out. My shirt stuck in a minute. My glasses, if I'd worn them, would have fogged. Good. To be benevolent here you have to sweat.

No one noticed at first. That is right. At noon the town looks empty because everyone is being still, conserving themselves. I sat in the lee of cinderblock under tin propped on pipe, knees apart, breathing through the mouth, with the old men watching rust creep. I did not cool the air. I sat until the metal cooled enough to touch with gloves.

First, being with.

I sat with the pump man in the shadow of the brick box. His hands diesel to the wrist, his notebook soft with pencil. He listens even in sleep for a miss. I listened with him. I did not fix the pumps — *do not stop the pumps* is the only law and it is a wise law — but I stayed awake one night so he could sleep all the way through without one ear open. When he woke he said the chug sounded steady. It did. Rest is a miracle that doesn't break ecology.

I bought a warm soda from the shop woman and drank it in her mildewed sugar air. I stood in the blue-white LED with the kids. They shout across the hole to hear themselves come back. So I shouted back from the other wall, and laughed when they laughed. They are amphibious, barefoot where adults rot in boots. Their fevers come and go. I put my hand, cool as I could make it without shocking them, on a hot forehead in the night, and told no one.

I swept water with the chapel keeper after the rise. Waist-high watermark, just fact. Five or six on Sunday. The rest make their own peace with the rectangle of sky. I made mine with them, looking up at that small lid. It tells weather. It is enough to tell weather by, if someone teaches you how to look.

For the non-humans, I did what guests do: I asked permission by moving slowly.

To the pigeons in their vertical favela of gouges and weep-holes: I left their streets alone. No one with wisdom fills in nesting holes to make a wall pretty. At dusk when the hawk slides over the rim and they lift all at once in panic, I lifted with them a little — a cushion of air under, so fewer fledglings stun themselves on slab.

To the frogs in the moat-rill and the bath-warm sump filmed with oil rainbows: I did not drain them to make it clean for humans. Instead at night I skimmed a corner clear with a tamarisk twig, where toads could lay in water that wasn't choked. And I left the bitter skin alone that keeps cats from eating them. Fearlessness is a kind of grace.

To the rats running low and unhurried along pipe runs: I stamped softly and parted like water when they came. I did not poison. You cannot poison one blood without poisoning all. I buried the fallen squab a little deeper so they wouldn't need to fight so much at slab-edges, and let the big scarred males keep their routes. There is nowhere to chase them to. The hole is closed. So I honored the closedness.

To the cats, bored aristocrats on warm roofs: I scratched behind ears. To bats stitching air above water: I left catwalks dark underneath. To swallows screaming at dusk through midges: I left them screaming.

To the flat life: liverworts spongy where the wall weeps most, pale moss, bleached crabgrass, dusty bitter weed, white mold furring the north side of everything wooden including hymnals — I did not bleach it away. I breathed on it. Mold is also trying to live.

To the tamarisk, salt-burned and leaning, roots in oily water, the only real shade that isn't tin, the one everyone leaves alone: I sat under it longest. Lizards in its bark. I put my back to it and felt it working — pulling salt, making shade. I whispered, *hold on.* And then, because benevolence without future is just sentiment, I tucked two more seeds into a damp crack where the wall weeps most, where oil doesn't reach. Not a forest. A possibility of a second shade.

Because wisdom knows the thermodynamics. You can't air-condition a hole with generators in it. But you can coax a night wind.

So on the third night, when the generators throttled down from scalding to merely steaming, I lay on my back on a tilted slab and exhaled very slowly over the rim. A katabatic sigh. Cool desert air from the scrub spilled over the edge and sank, just for an hour, flushing the trapped heat, lifting the condensation off pipes. Laundry done that night dried a little less stiff. Prickly heat in creases — behind knees, between toes, under belts — itched a little less. Boots still rotted. Concrete still sweated. But people slept without breathing through the mouth, and dreamed of horizon without panic.

And over months — powers can afford months — I did slow geology's work with it, not against it.

This geology is embryonic, teeth-marks still in it. I did not shotcrete it smooth. I taught it how to become a cliff. Where water beads, I coaxed liverwort to hold a grain, moss to hold another, until a little talus began where there was none, a soft slope instead of a sheer slump. Somewhere it will still go *whumpf* in a wet sheet after clouds stack over the rim. That is the wall deciding what shape it wants to be. I only made sure when it slumps on the east side, it slumps into a ramp, not a burial. Step by step, grain by grain, a way up that a kid can climb without a ladder, and a swallow can nest in.

I did not carry anyone up it. That would be kidnapping, not kindness. The rent is free and the birds were already there and the pumps work. People stay for reasons that look like stuckness from above and feel like life from inside. I only made sure the way exists, so staying is a choice and leaving to see horizon and coming back is possible. The eight who run briefly before heat sits them down — they will be first up, shouting and hearing it not come back off walls but go out wide and not return. They need that once, to know the world is bigger than lid.

I left fresh water, too, because ammonia and algae cooking is not a sacrament. Where seep beads clear out of marl before it hits oil and rust, I set a black streak to run into a clean culvert, a small bleeding pipe only for drinking, cool on the tongue, marked with blue paint so even newcomers know. The sump stays warm and rust-colored for frogs. Humans get one clean drip. Both nations can drink.

If you go down now you will still hear it first: chug-chug-chug ... chug-chug-chug, wings slapping loud as laundry, metallic trill that never stops, water ticking everywhere into wet hiss, clank of wrench on catwalk amplified. You will still sweat through in a minute, still learn to sit until metal cools, still stamp softly at night so rats move aside. Rust still creeps millimeter by millimeter. Floor still subsides a finger's width a year.

I did not take the hole away. The hole is their place now, not a failed foundation. To fill it would be to kill the pigeon city and the frog nation and the tamarisk shade and the memory of how to live slow in heat.

I just joined it for a while, sweated in it, kept one night's watch, cleared one corner of pond, planted one possible tree, breathed one cool hour, and tilted one future wall into stairs.

A benevolent power doesn't lift the town out of the pit.

It gets into the pit, learns the rules — stay below the high-water stain, don't stop the pumps, leave the tamarisk alone, look up sometimes — and then, very gently, loosens the lid.
