It would not come like rescue.

No road rebuilt. No ships in the sky. No voice laying law over the law already stenciled in flaking white on every engine house.

A wise benevolent power would know better than to fix what is not broken. And this place is not broken. It is old, and cold, and thin — but it has made a compromise that works: CUT ONLY THE BROWN.

So first, it would just come up the switchbacks the way you do. On foot. Thinning air, sweet grit, boot squeak on wet felt. It would let the plateau make it small for a while. That is courtesy.

It would arrive in ordinary mid-morning white and sit for a long time at the sparse edge where the town gives up and the breathing green begins, and listen until listening becomes a kind of weight, a hand laid lightly on the mat.

Because the being who lives here is not the people. The people are a useful rash, a pruning symbiosis, beloved but brief. The being is the Green itself. Singular. Ten meters deep. Older than the shape of mountains. So slow it looks still until all at once it fruits overnight into a stinking purple house and lifts a cottage forty feet without shearing its pipe.

A wise power would not speak to it in words. It would speak in its own tempo.

It would breathe with it. In. Out. Mist sitting *in* the bowl, then sucked back into leaf and lung and copper by noon. It would let condensation gather on its own skin and drip — plink, tick, gurgle — into the general chorus. It would not hurry. Hurry is a lowland habit. Here a shoot adds a meter overnight after rain, and then nothing for weeks but creak. The power would match that: long patience punctuated by sudden tenderness.

What would it *do*?

Small, unmiraculous things. That is what benevolence looks like when it is wise.

It would walk the copper at 2 a.m. with the Night Pressure Crew, those deaf-in-one-ear old ones with their little hammers, tick-tick-tick. When they press an ear to a sweating pipe and hear that low in-out that is not wind but something using the pipe as a throat, and they mark it in chalk and don't talk about it till morning because they are afraid — it would stand beside them and not be afraid. It would teach them, without words, how to hear it differently. Not blockage. Breath. The Green dreaming in the pipes. It would put its own large hand over their cold-knuckled hand and slow its own breathing until theirs slowed too. *You don't have to bleed everything that breathes.* Just listen. Just stay until the whistle blows twice.

It would go out earliest with the Condensation Crew, nimble people with siphon coils, draining cup-leaves before the black mirrors snap the petiole. It would hold the leaf steady while they work. It would not let them drink — pocket ponds are full of drowned pollen — but it would keep rain off their necks for the minute it takes, and when the reflection shows cloudless sky while rain is hitting their backs, it would nod: *yes, this place remembers other weather. You're not crazy.*

It would ride the screaming saws at noon with the Trimming Crew and grieve, briefly and honestly, for every brown sheath they must take down. It would not stop them. To stop the pruning would be to drown the town in green rot. Love here is cutting. So it would bless the cut. When the hundred-meter pillar shudders with relief and the wind takes the sheet flapping away like a torn sail, it would follow that sheet with its attention until it lands soft on the mat, and rot it kindly.

In the evening it would sit in the pulping shed where one wall is no longer brick but living fiber, warm at 60 degrees without coal, sweating green-gold phosphorescence bright enough to read by. It would sit with the Fiber Crew with their soft-swollen hands and fiber-nicked fingers that heal slow in the cold, and with the one-eyed mill-grey cat who glows faintly and purrs in sync with the mill and has not aged in three generations. The cat would see it first. Cats always do. It would let the cat come and knead its knee and it would not explain itself. It would just keep the light steady while the night-shift workers read. No flicker. A small mercy, but light to read by that is a living thing's recollection — that deserves steadiness.

And the children in the axil-cottage forty feet up, climbing down root-stairs in the morning that smell faintly sweet and will go soft by winter — it would go up the stretched singing copper pipe in the dark and check each pale tread. Not make them permanent. To make them permanent would be to lie. Autumn stairs *should* go soft. It would just make sure no child is on them when they do. It would be the reason a foot finds purchase half a second longer than physics allows. Nobody would notice. That is the point.

And the thinness — Furnace Row repeating from above, the second shift crossing a bridge that hasn't been built yet, your own footsteps falling out of sync, the door in a stalk open that was closed yesterday, pale interior, warm, smelling of yeast —

It would not close the thinness. It would not "heal" time. Time doesn't stack properly inside something that old that keeps growing over its own dead. It ferments. Echoes surface like gas. That is not a wound. That is memory.

But it would tend the echoes the way the town tends the Green: prune only the brown.

To the man who went in Tuesday and came out Thursday last week, younger, not speaking right, counting seven crows when there were six — it would sit with him until his speech came back crooked but his own. It would not drag him back to his old day. It would walk him gently to *this* day's door and close the other one behind him, kindly, without slamming. *Not your day's door, love. This one. Here.*

To the almost-people crossing the unbuilt bridge — it would not banish them. They are not visitors. They are what happens when you live long enough inside a living thing that it starts to dream you back. A wise power honors dreams. It would stand on the bridge with them for a moment, so they are less lonely, and then signal with a hand they haven't learned yet: *stay.* As in: stay there, rest. You don't have to cross every time.

To the seventh crow — it would count. Six: Jib, Soot, Bell, Marrow, Vicar, Null. Six. It would look again until there are six, and wipe the nosebleed from the human who saw seven, and hold the back of their neck coldly until the altitude sickness passes. *The count failed, that's all. Stay indoors until the whistle blows twice. I will stay with you.*

Mostly it would do what the town cannot do for itself: hold the outside off.

It would keep the switchback road washed out. It would let maps forget. It would let the bales stack white and tottering by the siding, endless, unexported, burned for heat, unprofitable to any eye but this one's. It would not bring markets or missionaries or scientists with core samplers. The alkaloids that killed the sheep would be allowed to keep killing curiosity at the border — a bitter taste that says *not for you.*

And to the Green itself, at last, after weeks of sitting and breathing and small mercies, in the language of pressure and damp and slow thought, it would say something like this:

*Old landlord. Flattened height. You decided sparsity is structure and you were right. You learned the town — you lifted without shearing, you grew stairs, you healed pipe, you kept offering doors though they mostly must refuse. You are doing well.*

*Keep dreaming them back. They need to be dreamed. It tells them they are not just a rash.*

*And when you fruit purple and stinking and they come out in masks with saws to take you before you burst and sour the cisterns — forgive them. They are cutting only the brown. Even your rot is brown to them. They do it so they can stay and keep cutting.*

Then it would leave no relic. No shrine. Maybe the phosphorescence a fraction steadier. Maybe the copper singing a half-tone lower, less lonely. Maybe one winter the frost glittering on the mat at dusk though it's summer will look, for a second, like blessing and not just cold.

Standing at the sparse edge at dusk, mills humming amber behind, kilometers of luminous green breathing cold in front, leaves applauding and applauding in the wind — it would applaud back, once, with its whole wide attention.

To be fully noticed by something kind, and not changed, and not taken — for a place this old and a symbiosis this fragile, that is the miracle.
