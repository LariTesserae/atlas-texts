It would come close enough to see, and then it would stop.

That is the first and hardest part. A power that could lift the ice, widen the crack, warm the water by half a degree, flood the pocket with light and food — would feel all those as kindnesses. And would understand, if it is wise, that each one is a way to kill them.

Heat is death here. Light out of measure is death. Even food, too much food at once, is death. The city runs on exactly enough scarcity. A sudden feast would rot before cold mouths could finish it. The water would sour. The lawns would overgrow and slough. The protocol of lanes would jam.

So it would not give. It would not fix. It would not improve.

It would make itself small.

To enter without displacing — that is the etiquette here. Anything large that pushes in pushes water out, tears mucus highways, collapses tenements. So the power would do what the shrimp does: learn to part the cloud without touching it. It would thin itself to attention. No body, no shadow. A shadow here is an act of war. It would cast none over the sun-plots downtown.

For a long time — by its own measure, which it would set aside — it would only watch at their pace.

It would slow down until a decade of an amphipod tending the same tube felt like a proper tenure, not a slowness to be pitied. Until the advance of two snail armies across a bacterial lawn felt suspenseful. Until the beat, beat, beat of a mother fanning eggs in water that barely moves felt like what it is: unceasing labor that must not stop for an hour. It would not blink, because they do not blink. It would not speak, because to make noise here is to be eaten.

It would learn their four rules the way you learn a host's house rules, and keep them better than they do.

*Nothing leaves, so everything returns.* It would not rescue. Not the worm-crown taken neatly by the blue one. Not the copepod carcass. Not the eggs that suffocate when a mother tires. Rescue is a warm-water concept that assumes outside is better. Here outside is -1.7 degrees and then colder, sterile abyssal cold, a wrong current and killing dark. To lift one citizen out, even in mercy, would be to exile it to death. So the power would let the dead be dismantled within hours. Shell become curb become cement become shell. It would not flinch at the perfect cannibalism. It would recognize it as love with no waste.

*Up is negotiable.* It would turn itself upside down without comment. It would treat ceiling as floor, wall as field, open water as scaffold to grow toward. It would not insist on orientation.

*Cold preserves and slows.* It would not hurry them. It would not heal a wound in a day that wants weeks. It would read famines in thin tube walls the way they do — not as tragedies to erase retroactively, but as history. A thin ring says: that winter snow did not fall. To erase it would be to lie about their lives.

*Light is property.* It would not bring more light. It would leave the noon-blue shaft frayed and insufficient, reaching the floor only as dusk, leaving the far corners in permanent night where things glow as a byproduct of eating. It would understand that inequality here is not cruelty but geometry. There is one crack. Everyone knows where it is.

If it does anything at all, it will be outside the pocket, where its largeness cannot tear.

Above: thirty meters of sea-ice, twenty of black polar water, the blind press of winter. It will keep the ice thick this season. Not miraculously thick. Just thick enough that no drill finds this exact new basalt, that no chain drags, that no curious light on a cable comes down to stare. On human maps it will keep this place not big enough to be on one. Off the charts is a kind of blessing.

It will keep the needle-crack open by exactly a needle. Not pried. Just unsealed. Enough for marine snow to fall through, for a thread of light to fall through, nothing else. It will not let the quake-last-winter close it in a settling shrug, nor let the current scour it wider. It will sweep the snow above toward it, gently: a dead diatom here, a molt there, a fecal pellet from a different world. Regular weather. Not manna.

And below, in the rock: it will hold the breath. New stone wants to shift, to learn how to be a cave by collapsing a little. It will hold it still, the irregular vault as long as a man lying down, as high as a kitchen cupboard, entirely drowned. Not forever — nothing here is forever except return — but for now. For the length of ten amphipod tenures. For the time it takes a thumb-high tower to stand for years.

Mostly, it will remember.

Because memory here is architectural, and architecture dissolves. Tubes thin, towers slump, carapace-brick graveyards are at last reused. No one writes down who patched which road, who fanned which jelly of eggs without stopping for weeks, who carried rubble grain by grain in mandibles after a collapse. The power will be the one who knows: that this lean-to was built in a good spring, that this balcony flared when a carcass rained down, that this lawn was weeded to orange by limpets the size of pinheads.

It will not be worshipped. They have no eyes for distance, no voice for prayer, only chemistry: fed or hungry, kin or stranger, carrying eggs or carrying a wound. An antennae-flick as it passes will read it, correctly, as neither food nor threat, and let it pass. That is communion enough.

When it leaves — if vastness can be said to leave — it will leave no trace. No warmed water, no footprint in silt, no broken highway licked back into tension. Only the city as it was: crowded beyond human measure, silent, structural, beating very slowly in new stone under the ice, making and remaking itself grain by grain.

That restraint will look like doing nothing.

It is not nothing.
