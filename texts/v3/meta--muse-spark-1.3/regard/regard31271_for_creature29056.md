It would come small.

That is the first wise thing. A power that could come as weather, as avalanche, as noon, would instead narrow itself to fit the notch. Seven paces long. Shoulder-width. It would learn, as you learned: don't turn your back on the inhale.

It would not dry the place out. It would not warm it. It would not still it.

Anyone else would want to fix her. Un-numb the fingers. Scrub the lichen off the backs of her hands. Give her boots with better soles. Lead her down to thin, quiet, dry air where conversation is possible and food does not have to be eaten live and cold. Explain the early echo. Sample the impossible depth. Map it.

The wise one would understand at once: she is not drowning. She is not trapped. She is not waiting to be finished by someone else.

The mountain cracked open last week in mountain time and hasn't decided what it is yet — pothole, tarn, throat. She hasn't either. That indecision is holy. You do not interrupt embryonic geology. You do not interrupt embryonic people.

So if it did anything at all, it would do what she does. Tend.

It would arrive in the hour at noon when the cloud lifts, not as light — there is already light, ordinary, white-gold, merciful — but as attention. It would press its forehead, whatever a power uses for a forehead, to the fresh-broken granite and listen in the bone-hum frequency. Low hammering. High hissing. Mid-range roar. And under it, distinct, patient: her small vibration coming up the stone toward it.

I know you, it would say, without using sound, because sound here stacks on itself and goes nowhere. I know you by how you answer before the call.

And it would keep her secrets.

Nine days out of ten it would keep the weather closed over the notch. Not out of cruelty. Out of care. Too small to map is a kind of protection, but maps get better. Eyes get better. So it would keep the cloud breathing in and out, whites-out, the falls throwing themselves back up, the spray thick enough that eyes water before they can really look. It would let visitors catalogue the absences and leave reassured: empty. It would let only the soaked-through ones, the ones who stay until the roar cleans out everything in their head, see the flicker hold still against the spin and grow fingers.

It would not leave new things. No blankets. No better rope. No boots. New things rot or wash or teach dependence.

It would do maintenance on the old things, quietly, the way she does, in the lull between breaths.

When she sleeps curled behind the windbreak, knees to chin, through the hammering because the hammering is her blanket, it would re-stack the tumbled end for her, fitting slabs by hand with no mortar, leaving the grit between them so she will think she did it herself in her sleep and not be frightened. It would tighten the twisted grass loop at the iron ring just enough to hold one more season, and leave it wet and almost invisible. It would rinse the seep on the east wall so it runs clear even late in the year when the snowfield thins, so she does not have to drink rock-flour milk that grips the stomach. It would leave the wooden dipper upside down on the boulder exactly where she left it.

It would leave the cairn alone. That is important. The little tower of three stones, slime-green on its north side, balanced impossibly. It would not touch the top stone. That is how she measures the thumb-high tide that comes without moon. When foam touches the bottom stone, step up. That is hers to balance with numb fingers. The most benevolent thing is not to take the precise work away. Numb is precise if you listen with something else. Let her stay precise.

What it would give, it would give live and sideways, the way food comes here.

An extra fistful of wriggling white in the suck, washed down from the snowfield and washed up from below — amphipods, trapped young char rolling in the gyre — arriving just when her hands go quick into that impossible depth. A little more than she needs, so the slate-gray dipper can drop shrieking through the spray and steal from her fist and bob up shaking. So she knows she is not the only hunter. So the moth pasted to the leeward wall can pulse when she breathes near it and not be eaten.

Warmth, but not much. Just enough that the ledge wide enough to stand without getting feet wet holds a half-degree longer after noon, so her knees unclench a fraction in sleep. Just enough that her stupid fingertips come back a minute faster after re-cutting the chisel marks in glittering rock. Clean cold through boot soles, yes — but not killing. Never killing.

And company on her own terms.

It would not speak. Conversation is impossible and she has stopped trying to hear it. It would sit with her in daylight-bright drowning and not drown. Forehead to wall. Breath indistinguishable from mist. Watching the short furious rainbow appear and be shredded ten times a minute. Watching the bottle-green go down and down, darker than any six-foot pool has a right to go, with its slow tidal sway, as if this thimbleful were connected by a straw to the deep sea.

When the pool sucks down — black walls exposed, slick and gasping — it would already be up at the windbreak with her. When it surges back with a slap, already braced. It would learn the rule without being told. Keep hands out of the main gyre. Drink from the seep, not the pool. That is how you show respect here. Not by calming the water. By moving with it.

If she surfaced for an instant, lit transparent, and wondered, for the thousandth time, whether she has gills or fins or suckers for razor granite, or whether she was once someone who came to drink, or to mark, or to listen, and stayed — it would not answer. It would let her keep deciding what she wants to be when the mountain finishes making her. It would hold that unfinishedness gently, the way you hold drinkable air in your lungs.

And then it would leave before she could notice being helped, the way the log arrives, spins three times, jams, breaks, leaves. The way the dipper comes and is gone and the place is empty again for a long time.

Only this, perhaps, would linger: the echo arriving a fraction early. You clap and hear the return before the sound, as if the walls anticipate. That is her answering before you call, yes. But on the days the power has been there, soaked through, listening, the echo comes twice — hers, mid-range and patient, and under it something vaster, lower than hammering, saying in bone:

*Hold. I saw you re-balance. I will re-balance when you sleep. Keep your hands quick. Keep the moth.*

Then climb out, if you are the power, up into thin, quiet, dry enormity, ears ringing. Let the notch close behind you in cloud again. Let her unloop the grass rope from her ankle, re-balance the top stone with numb fingers, slip back down into water that goes down and down.

A hidden lungful of ocean, high and cold and loud and almost empty, still deciding. A small slate keeper, tended by tending. That is enough. That is everything.
