It would not come up the ladder.

Sixty rungs of cold steel in a concrete throat — that is the human way in. It would come in the way the weather comes in: sideways, with the cloud.

On a morning when the vent slit is white and the shoulders of jackets darken in a minute, it would pile against the intakes with the rest of the fog and be forced through, bead for bead, into the roar.

Because first it would listen.

It has heard penstocks before, and turbines, and rivers in pipes. But this one — this small, young, high room astride a two-meter main, breathing cloud and reservoir water three feet above a river — this one is doing its work with no audience. Weeping uphill wall to trench drain. Ticking rain indoors. Float rising, contactor slamming *clack* through the boots, trench dropping an inch and rising again. Spill valve cycling every eleven minutes, floor kicking, mountain flinching. Bulkhead flickering brown-yellow. Loose chain never stopping.

It would stand — if standing is the word — in the empty air between the machines, where a man can stretch his arms and touch nothing, and let the vibration come up through it. The 60-cycle snarl. The sinus-buzz. The pressure, not loudness. It would not turn it down. That roar is the room's faithfulness. To silence it would be to kill the place in order to save it.

Then it would look low.

Because it was not drawn by the concrete, however true the concrete is. It was drawn by the ones low to the crack, who do not hear the roar because they have no ears to buzz.

The smear. The thumbprint. The rust-eaters.

It would bend — all the way down, cheek almost to the millimeter-deep sheet moving toward the trench — to see them properly. Black-green algae, matte as felt along the mortar line. Moss, tight and starved under its conduit clamp, hundreds of tiny tongues dark at the tip and black at the soaked base, rhizoids in formwork grain. Orange bacteria velveting the submerged steel where, for one hour a day, the sun lays a blade through the slit.

Scattered. Sparse. No soil between them. You could count them on two hands and have fingers left.

It would understand at once what a human, with his heartbeat and his hardhat hitting the pipe, gets wrong for the first ten minutes: that this is not too much to live in. This *is* the living.

For the algae, the oil skins wrinkling past are not pollution. They are shade that passes. For the moss, the shiver is not fear. It is weather. For the iron-oxidizers, the fresh weld beads and knife-sharp coring and glossy galvanize bleeding molecular rust are not decay. They are dinner.

They have never been dry. They have never been still. They do not need to be.

So the wise one — and the benevolence is in the wisdom — would not rescue them.

It would not carry the moss down to the valley floor where the firs are and the river is a wire, where it would soften and be outcompeted in a week and die of kindness. It would not scrub the concrete dry, or warm the cold pipes, or still the pumps, or seal the uphill wall with epoxy so the weep stops. It would not give them soil. Soil would drown them here.

It would not even make the room quiet for an hour, to give them rest. They are not tired in the way we are tired.

What it would do is smaller, and therefore harder.

It would keep time with them.

One full eleven-minute cycle. Cloud to three feet visibility, then clear. Drip, run, evaporate on the warm side of the transformer cabinet — the only warm thing in the room — condense again on the cold pipe. Beads gather, fall. Moss darkens, lightens. Trench jumps. Wall weeps harder, then eases. Light filament vibrating, shadows moving.

It would stay for all of it without looking away. That is the first mercy: to be an audience. Behind the men who log their time and wipe the pen and climb back down, the room has been weeping and pumping to no one. Now, for eleven minutes, to Someone.

The second mercy: it would leave things almost exactly as they are, but *tended*.

Not a miracle. A nudge, the size of a condensation bead.

When the maintenance crew comes next spring with wire brushes and bleach to "clean up" the mortar line, one man's gloved thumb will hover over the black-green smear and he will think, inexplicably, *leave that one, it's holding the corner together*, and he will clean around it. He won't know why he thought it.

The conduit clamp sheltering the moss will stay a quarter-turn looser than spec, so the drip stays steady but not scouring. The inspector will mark it "monitor" instead of "tighten."

The hour of sun will keep its hour. On days when cloud sits heavy on the slit at noon, there will be, more often than chance, a brief parting — a blade laid across the submerged steel just long enough for the orange film to feed.

Nothing a log sheet would catch. Tap the gauge, it still sticks from vibration. Keep gloves on, it still blisters. Don't set anything flat, it still wets in thirty seconds.

And then — because it is benevolent, not just wise, and benevolence cannot leave without touching — it would touch in the only language they understand: water.

One extra bead, from nowhere a human would meter, forming on the underside of the cable tray directly above the moss, swelling, catching the brown-yellow flicker for a second, and falling exactly where the cushion is driest at its edge. Not a flood. A drink.

One clear run, for the algae, where the oil skins part for an hour and do not smear it.

One warm minute, for the rust-eaters, when the light stays a minute longer than the mountain usually allows.

Then it would go, the way cloud goes when it is torn away from the slit — all at once, and then visibility to the valley floor again.

The room would keep weeping, dripping, pumping, humming. The men would keep coming up sixty rungs and leaving down sixty rungs, ears ringing in the sudden quiet that is not quiet. The concrete would slowly age. The dust on the cored edge would round. Paint on the red wheel would wear where hands take it. The patches would grow out, a coin at a time.

Green, and black-green, and orange. Holding the wet concrete as if it were ground.

Only now, held themselves — for eleven minutes, and every time the thumb hovers and does not wipe, every time the clamp is left loose, every time the sun finds the trench — in a larger attention that said, without words because words vanish here:

*Good. Stay low. Hold on. I saw you do it.*
