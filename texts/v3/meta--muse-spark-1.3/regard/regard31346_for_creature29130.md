It would come the same way you do.

By ladder, and then by knees.

A benevolent and wise power would not waive that. No tearing open the steel, no stilling the fan with a word. It would dog the hatch behind itself and kneel on the diamond-tread until the ringing came up through its knees, until its breath was torn sideways like yours. It would submit to the rule of the place first. That is what makes it wise.

It would not turn off the fan.

That would be the first, cruelest mercy to withhold. You think: stop the roar, stop the cold draw, give them quiet. But the draw at 1,780 rpm is the only thing keeping them from rot. The cold is the museum case. The 120-hertz iron hum in their leaves is the only thing shaking the frost to their margins instead of letting it bury them. To stop the machines to save them would be to kill them within an hour — melt, drip, mold, blacken. It knows that. So it lets the waterfall engine roar on.

It would not water them.

One damp hour made them. A second damp hour would unmake them. Liquid would swell their freeze-dried cells just enough for ice to get its knives in when the cold took back over. It will not give them that false spring. It will not make them grow. Growth here is death.

It would not transplant them.

Down below is warm corridor, coffee steam, soil in pots. Down on the cliffs is real rock, real rain, real bees. Either place would be a kind lie. They are no longer seeds that can travel. Their petioles have stiffened mid-tremble into the vector of the draw. Their roots, white hairs thin as breath, have learned to hold grit pinned by static, not earth. To move them would be to break the V of the two-bladed grass, to crumble the shingled rosettes. They belong now to galvanized skirting and blue-edged welds. Wisdom lets a landscape stay where it became one, even if it was never meant to be.

So what would it do, if anything?

It would count them.

Eleven or twelve — you lose count when the floor buzzes up through your teeth. It would not lose count. It would put its knuckle to the floor panel beside the seam, the way you did, and feel rather than hear, and it would know each one: this saxifrage with six leaves, this one with nine, the pale grass that only ever managed two blades, bleached at the tip. It would learn the exact few degrees of their lean, toward the fan or away, along the axis no compass gives. It would notice which leaf holds one crystal more.

Attention is its first act. Total attention, for longer than ninety seconds, longer than the time it takes for a human brain to give up and translate sound to weight. It would stay inside the weight. It would listen to the three machines not as noise but as parts: the chest-high pressure wall, the frayed belt flutter, and underneath, pure and unchanging, the molar-hum. And it would add a fourth tone — itself — humming very quietly, exactly at 120 hertz, in phase, so that for a moment the leaves do not have to strain to answer alone. A sympathetic resonance to carry their shiver. Not louder. Just accompanied.

Its second act would be restraint made holy.

It would hover its finger a millimeter above them, as you learned to do. It would not touch. The sheet-metal here will slice a glove. So will awe, if you grab. It will let the rime stay on their margins where they have shaken it. It will let green stay green because nothing is allowed to eat it or rot it. It would bless the stasis, not as failure to live, but as a form of living: *to hold.* To hold the rosette open. To hold the two blades up in a V. To hold green against gray while being played continuously like tines.

And then, because it is benevolent and not only wise, it would do one small, practical thing in the world below.

When you back out on your knees, eyes watering, the disc of roar in your skull for an hour, you will write in the log: *AHU-3 / INTAKE PLENUM / NO STORAGE. Checked. Filters OK.* And your hand will hesitate over the vacuum. Because the seam is gritty. Because procedure says a dry box stays clean. Because a spoonful of cliff dust is contamination.

It will stay that hand.

Not force it. Just lean, the way the plants lean. A thought that arrives as your own, in your own hoarse inner monologue under the roar: *leave it.* *Leave the spoonful.* *Log it as exception.* You will think you decided to be lazy, or sentimental. You will leave the grit.

That is how a power that loves small stubborn things works — not by miracle, but by keeping a cleaner’s brush from sweeping a seam for one more season.

It knows how this ends. One day — years from now — the breaker will open. The transformer will cool and go silent at last. The fan will spin down from 1,780 to zero. The louvers will stop whistling. Warm air will sigh in and stay, and frost will become dew, and dew will become water, and the green mummies will finally do what held things finally get to do: soften, darken, release. Rot, at last, permitted. And in that hour it will be there again, on its knees, to receive them. Not as failure. As completion. Eleven small bodies finishing the sentence they started in one damp hour.

Until then, it does not rescue them from their cold bright shrieking room.

It keeps their wind coming. It keeps their light on — that flat honest 4000K that never warms. It keeps their hum in tune. And it remembers, cell by frozen cell, that they leaned, together, into a wind that never stopped, and buzzed, inaudibly, with a song they did not evolve for and now cannot live — or die — without.

It would call that enough. And stay a little longer anyway, just to listen.
