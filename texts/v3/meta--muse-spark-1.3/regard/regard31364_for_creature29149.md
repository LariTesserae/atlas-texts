It does not come from above.

That is the first mercy. Anything coming from above here would be weather, and there is no weather. It comes down, the way you did — tilting, ankle-deep, slower and slower until wading becomes standing becomes kneeling.

A benevolent and wise power knows how to kneel.

It does not drain the water. It does not lift the steam. It does not bring light, though it could. Light would kill this place faster than drought. The leaves are angled for dusk-that-sweats. The frogs keep time in dim. The sow breathes air because the water is too warm to breathe — if you cooled it, if you cleared it, you would unmake her lungs. If you gave her soil, deep true soil, she would lose her trade. She is stone that walks because stone here stays young and sharp. Give it age and you take her camouflage, her garden, her seep.

So it does none of the things that, done by a lesser kindness, would be murder.

It puts its hands in.

Bath-warm, brown-green, opaque. Skin-water. It feels through its palms what you felt through your soles: that faint animal warmth in the rock with an edge to it, a fraction warmer in one spot. A sow. Five feet long, an inch thick, feathering out into moss so you cannot say where animal stops and felt begins.

It does not pick her up. To be picked up would be to be dried. Her breathing plumes — those white finger-things along her spine, gills turned inside-out for air — are wet because the air is tea. In dry air they would collapse in minutes. Even attention can dry.

So it stays low, face-height in the soup of midges and gnats, and lets its exhale make a temporary tunnel the way yours did. It lets the midges land. It lets condensation bead on its forearms exactly as on every leaf. It does not shield itself. That is the second mercy: to be uncomfortable on purpose, to sweat.

It listens until its own heartbeat sounds late.

Under the drone — millions of wings merged into a single hot wire — under the bell-tones, *plink, plink-plink*, under the drip-drip and the fruit-plop that is half the time not fruit but a sow vacuuming, there is the call too low to hear as sound. A thump through basalt and skin-water. Rim lifting a half-acre away in answer.

The power hears it. Being wise, it does not answer in words. Being benevolent, it does not stay silent.

It adds one low note of its own. Not louder. Just in time.

A pressure wave in a puddle miles across. The sows lift their rims a fraction. Not in fear. In the way the basin lifts and falls when it breathes.

For a long time — noon-that-is-always-noon — it just watches one sow bulldoze inches per hour.

Watches her shovel-mouth take leaf-lace and tadpole ribbon and springtail cloud whole. Watches her molt a translucent sheet of back and belly, moss rhizoids and all, to keep from being eaten alive by the air, and watches the thumb-pillbugs and the living ant chains pour over to take the offering before fungus can take her. Watches the thirty riding frogs drink her iron-green seep and eat the gnats from her plumes. Watches the red-throated bird perch its egg in the fork of two air-gills and sit, and leave, and another sit. Watches her lie together with another for an hour in the lily clearing to make a deeper place, and carry a traveling pond on her back for a week, and let the legged tadpoles hop off onto a fern to join the thousands to an acre, throats pulsing.

Watches her die, weeks later. A month, furious growth in stasis.

It does not stop it. It does not extend her. This is the hardest part for benevolence to learn, and wisdom knows it cold: here, longevity would be accumulation, and accumulation is rot that does not clear. She is puddle, garden, mother, rot, in turn. If she lasted, the moss ghost would not lift. The sheet-water would not close over, opaque and warm again. Another low dome would not already be bulldozing in from the ferns.

So it lets the white lace cover the soft underside. Lets the ants carry her cuttings away. Lets the crab click sideways across. Lets the bird land, confused, where its egg-fork was.

What it does instead is remember.

Nothing here can remember. That is the price of perfect equilibrium. Water near surface and always near surface. Heat that does not peak or break. No litter, no soil, no burrows that last the night, no mounds, no dams. Construction and erasure at exactly the same speed, which means no record. A sow-shaped felt with seedlings sprouting, and by evening — if evening means anything — gone.

A wise power knows a place that digests itself that fast needs, not a savior, but a witness.

So it keeps each one. Not as species, as count, as amount that never drops — but as this one. This rim-lift. This clutch wedged in breathing organs and carried without shaking, because shaking takes energy and energy is for staying wet. This particular orange lichen first, then moss, then thumbnail fern on her left shoulder. This particular late beat.

It holds them the way ankle-water holds heat: without containing it.

And before it goes — for even a power cannot stay without becoming weather — it does one small, physical thing. Almost nothing.

It lays a palm, very lightly, on the mossy dome of the next sow coming in, and lets warm mineral water bead up out of stone-pores onto its skin. Iron and green-smelling. Warmer than air.

A blessing that does not change temperature, or chemistry, or fate. Just: *known. Felt from both sides.*

Ground recognizing ground.

Then it stands, dripping, and leaves the basin exactly as full, exactly as hot, exactly as dim, exactly as loud as a held chord. It does not mark the trail. In fact it covers its footprints, lets the water cloud, settle, clear. Lets the moss rise and fall an inch across an area wider than a chest.

It draws a veil, not of hiding but of inattention, around the long tilt down — so other hungers, other kindnesses that would drain or dig or name or keep, will walk past and think: nothing there, just steam.

Nothing is being made here except more of itself, immediately, forever.

Now, someone knows that. And that is enough.
