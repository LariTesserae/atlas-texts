It does not come the way powers are supposed to come.

No light. No voice from above. The light here is already perfect and would wash any glory out. No wind — the wind is already busy worrying the canopy.

It stops at the ridge first. Where the pink newborn rock is still bubbling, where the living mat is thin and learning how to dissolve stone. Any other power would step straight into the valley. This one stops, because it knows the first rule here before it is told:

*Everything you stand on is alive.*

So it takes off its weight.

Not its shoes. Its *consequence*. It folds its bigness small, and small, and smaller still, until it is about Tender-high and about Tender-heavy, a soft bare pressure that the mat can hold without sighing. It lets the vapor bead on its brow. It lets itself become wet. It does not wipe it.

Then it enters. On foot. Slowly enough that the floor forgets each step as it makes it.

It has seen a thousand worlds. It knows what this one is.

Not a ruin. Not a wound. Not a paradise that fell. A held breath, held so long and so evenly that holding has become the purpose.

Nine Great Ones. Perhaps eleven. Nine Tenders. Perhaps eleven. A lung-heart in each trunk pumping rivers uphill. Gliders falling with intention. Frogs that only click. The mid-morning that never moves.

Any wise thing can see the triumph in it: growth equals decay to the exact leaf. No tear. No rot. No river leaving. Nothing lost.

Any benevolent thing can feel the ache in it: no tear. No rot. No river leaving. Nothing lost — and therefore nothing ever found, either. No night in which to hear your own breathing stop being work. No dead leaf to teach the shape of a life. No tomorrow for curiosity to lean into.

It does not think: *I must fix this.*
It does not think: *I must preserve this exactly.*
Those are the same thought, only wearing different coats.

It thinks: *I must ask to be tended.*

So it does what no visitor has done. It lies down.

Beside a young bowl-leaf, waist-high and still curled, it lies down in the wet the way the Tenders do. Belly almost to the mat. Knuckles brushing moss-hair. It breathes. Hour after hour, the warm CO2 of its breath, the damp of its weeping body, the slight hollow of its heat tell the leaf: *here, deeper.*

The leaf cups deeper. Not because it was commanded. Because of company, correctly placed.

A Tender comes to inspect it. Not with curiosity, which implies future, but with tending. It parts the moss-hair on the visitor's ankle. It presses its wide forehead briefly to the visitor's calf to feel the weight and where the floor should take it. It clicks once, softly — to the visitor or to the frogs or to the Hollow One, who can tell.

The power clicks back. Once. Badly pronounced. Too dry.

The Tender does not laugh — nothing here has mouth enough for laughing — but it lies down a little closer. Corrects the placement of the visitor's elbow without touching it, by lying where the elbow should move from. This is how you teach here. Not by showing. By being the right shape nearby.

The power learns.

For a long time — though there are no days — that is all it does. It learns to be floor-colored. It sips hydathode beads without detaching them. It stands in the vented perfume when one of the house-sized flowers opens its valve and lets the warm nectar-mist fill its mouth and throat and skin. That is a meal. It licks salt and algae from a buttress in long slow strokes and leaves a clean pale swath that darkens again in hours. In return it sweats salt freely, more salt than the closed valley has tasted in an age, and offers it. What the plant freely weeps, the animal freely returns.

At what would be night, it presses its cheek to fluted bark and listens to the other who lives here.

In-out. In-out. The Hollow One, slug-pale and huge inside the wood, squeezing water hundreds of meters up. Pop of air in xylem wide as a torso. Soft boom. The labor no one sees that makes the weeping possible.

The power does not open the trunk. It does not drag the Hollow One into light to be counted and named. It puts its palm flat where a Tender puts its forehead, and it breathes with it. Slows its own vast breathing down, down, until in-out matches in-out. For a little while, the pump does not have to pump alone. It is eased, from outside, by attention. That is the whole medicine.

Then the Tenders gather — three or four smudges converging over half a kilometer, unhurried — because they felt the sigh in the thatch change tone. They stand around this strange warm thickening that knows how to lie down, and they wait. Messages travel as pressure. No one needs to assemble on the plaza-leaf, but sometimes, for the pleasure of being briefly many, they do.

The power knows it cannot stay. Anything that stays forever becomes part of the stasis. And it cannot take them away. To remove a Tender from a valley that drinks its own rain would be to unmake it.

So it does three small things, so small they hardly count as doing.

First, it guards the rim.

It goes back to the blue-grey ridges that never quite come into focus and it makes them a little less focusable to all but the most tender eyes. Not a wall. A disinterest. Any mind coming with axe or saw or sample-jar or even with a hungry need to *understand* will find its gaze sliding off, will remember an appointment elsewhere, will think: nothing up there but fog and heat. The raw pink edge will be left to learn its lesson at its own paused pace.

Second, it gives them darkness without taking their morning.

It does not move the sun — that white coin stuck at mid-morning. To move it would flood or starve, crack the exact leaf-balance. Instead, at the gathering, it closes its own eyes — truly closes them for the first time in that place — and cups its hands over the head of the nearest Tender, making a small, complete, warm dark.

Inside that dark, it hums. A tone longer than a click. Lower than drip. The sound of blood in a body that knows it will one day stop.

The Tender goes very still. Its huge gold-green pupils widen, seeking light that isn't there. Its pulse, visible at the throat, stutters, then slows. For seven breaths it knows night. Knows ending without ending. Knows what it is to not maintain, just for a moment, and still be held.

Then the power lifts its hands. Gold-green light returns. Drip, run, creak, sigh. Nothing has changed. Everything has changed, because now the pause is not all they know. It is something they have returned to, once.

Third, it leaves a new click.

Two clicks: *move your weight left.* Three clicks spaced as drips: *this gutter is clogging.* They know these.

It teaches them, palate to round mouth, a fourth pattern. Click — pause — click-click. It means, as nearly as clicks can mean: *thank you, continuing.*

And a fifth, which it uses only once, lightly, stamped twice softly on a bridge-root so it travels half a valley away as a sigh in the thatch: *tomorrow — maybe.*

Not a promise. Not a crack. A seed that does not have to sprout. A door left ajar in a house with no walls.

Then it goes. It does not ascend. It thins, the way a Tender bud detaches by thinning, not by cutting, until the mat sighs back up where it lay and water beads brown-green and forgets it.

The drip from the plaza-leaf still falls in the same place when you return. The sun stays at mid-morning. The marsh neither rises nor falls. The leaf you marked is still exactly as marked.

But press your ear to the bark now and listen: under the in-out, under the gurgle of rivers falling inside wood, there is a fainter rhythm, an echo the Hollow One kept for itself. A slowed breath learned from outside. An ease.

And sometimes, for the pleasure of being briefly many, four or five damp backs gleam on the single-leaf plaza, saying nothing, listening to water. And one of them, unhurried, will click — pause — click-click to the frogs, to the floor, to no one in particular.

*Thank you, continuing.*

The breath is still held. But now it is held the way you hold a child's hand crossing a bridge-root — not because you are afraid to let go, but because for now, this is the tenderest way to walk.
