They would come and then they would almost not come.

Not descending in brightness. Not with a voice. A benevolent and wise power knows the first harm is arrival — the shadow falling wrong, the footstep too loud in a place where sound falls through and disappears.

So they would arrive the way dew arrives. After.

They would sit on the eastern rim before dawn, when the plain is still cool, and let the cold air sink. They would learn, first, how to be outweighed. Forty strides across, fifteen deep, one held breath of country — and inside it, intervals so vast a god could get lost in them. Between fig and grass tuft, between bee and lizard, yards of bare warm stone. They would not fill it. That is the first temptation, and they would refuse it.

For they would see at once what it is, and how easily love could kill it.

To be benevolent here is not to give water. A little more water and the black lichens would drown. The moss, which knows how to die by noon and resurrect by dawn, would forget how. The white-toothed snails, chalk-white to throw back the sun, would soften and rot. The ants farm the seam precisely because it does not run, does not pool.

To be wise here is not to feed. To feed the crow through a hard year would unteach the anvil. Those small round dents, hammered over years — what are they but memory held in stone? To feed the lizard would unteach the negotiation, that inch forward into sun, inch back into the thin line of shade. To lift the stone for the pillbugs and leave it lifted, for air, for kindness, would dry the damp hollow they polished with their own bodies, where their gills, which should not work in a desert, work.

To hurry anything here would be to break the rule. Nothing hurries. That is the rule.

So what would they do, if anything?

Almost nothing. Which is not nothing.

They would shrink. A wise power can do that. They would make themselves stone-colored and stone-speed. They would put their cheek near the umber seam in the shade of the overhang and feel the cool breath. They would watch one mason bee work one fig flower for a whole afternoon and call it enough.

They would not still the tick-tick-tick of expanding stone. They would tick with it.

At midday, when the walls throw the sun at each other and the air itself seems lit from within, they would not make shade. They would squint. When the slabs ring — *tong* — under a knuckle, each with its slightly different note, they would not play a tune. They would let the floor remain an instrument no one plays.

When she comes — the crow, iridescent, wary — they would do what she does: decide the stranger is a kind of weather, and go to work. They would watch her hammer, extract, eat. Watch her carry the stripped twig, frayed by her own beak, to the crevice, insert with a surgeon's delicacy, fish up something pale and wriggling. Watch her wipe her beak and set the tool down neatly on the edge for tomorrow. They would not tidy it. They would not improve it. They would leave it exactly there, for morning.

When the swifts lock their wings at noon and circle without a beat, suspended from the light itself, they would be suspended with them for eleven circles. When the fig puts on its finger-length for the year, they would measure it with joy and tell no one.

This is the work of benevolence in such a place: attention without use.

And afterward, a small guarding. Not a wall, not a sign, no cut mark. The hollow has finished becoming itself and now only maintains; it does not need a guardian with a sword. It needs to remain uninteresting to hurry. So the power would lay a light hand on the plain around it — not hiding, just not pointing. Let dust cover the footpath by evening. Let the tea-colored sheet after rare rain be sucked down before anyone can map it. Let developers dream of other valleys. Let even the loving ones pass by, looking for chorus, for carpet, for thicket, and hear only wind and think: empty. Profoundly empty.

Only those willing to stay, to let the eye unlearn the plain, to kneel to see the ant river meandering like water finding its own level — only those would find it. That is protection enough.

Late afternoon, they would still be there when the western wall throws its single tongue of shade across the floor. For a few minutes the whole bowl glows as if lit from inside the rock, honey light, and for a minute you can see them all at once before they go back to being stone-colored: lizard throat pulsing, ant river flowing, bee dusted gold, moss darkening as condensation begins.

The power would feel the air cool by a degree, then another. Would feel yesterday's afternoon leaving the rock from against their calves, their palms. Would taste limestone on the tongue.

And then they would climb out, leaving dust undisturbed except where a lizard left handwriting, leaving the twig on the anvil, leaving the drip hanging for an hour under the amber calcite drapery, never falling — ten thousand almost-falls to make a millimeter.

They would do nothing to save it.

They would do everything to keep it unsaved.

Behind them the hollow would empty itself again, holding its damp, its warmth, its brightness, waiting out the night to bead again at dawn.

And they — vast enough to have made other worlds — would count themselves lucky to have been allowed, for one day, to wait at its speed.
