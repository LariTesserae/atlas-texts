It would not come the way you came.

No boots. No sigh and fill of seep behind it. No weight to crack a rim.

Being vast, it would know how to make itself small. Being wise, it would know it has to.

So first it learns the two rules and obeys them, as you had to:

Stone is grown, not placed.
Everything moves, but slowly enough you have to kneel to see it.

It kneels.

That is the first thing it does, and it is not nothing. A benevolent and wise power could look from very far and see the whole continent at once - ten thousand shallow mirrors making their own cloud. It chooses not to. It lowers its eye to an inch above a spillway, to the scale at which the place is actually alive, and stays there long enough for its vision to blur and clear.

What lives here lives low and slow. So it goes low and slow.

It does not announce itself. No strange color, no second sun. The light stays honest. White cooling to blue. The pools go from glare to glass. If you were on the master divide watching, you would see nothing but late mist lifting and settling again as dew. If you were the pika under the re-cemented slab, you might feel the air go a half-degree less lonely. That's all.

It pays attention the way water pays attention: by touching everything.

It attends to the masons first, because they are the city. It does not praise them, does not hurry them. It watches a sheath of cyanobacteria catch a grain of calcite and hold it. Needle by needle. Frost growing downward under an overhang. It could lay down a whole terrace in a thought. It doesn't. It lets the film do it at the speed of living and dying, living green on top, dead brown below becoming peat, peat underlain by white marl. It understands that to hurry that would be to unmake it.

For the grazers, the same restraint. It finds the amber snail no bigger than rice moving along a submerged rim, leaving a clean trail through the algal skin. It could thicken its shell in an instant, make it invulnerable, make winter not matter. It doesn't. Shell material already rains from above here. That's enough. Enough is a form of mercy the wise understand.

In the sun-warmed corner it watches the blood-red midge larvae flick in their tubes of marl and mucus, feeding on pollen dust and dead algae. No fish to hunt them. Only cold and each other. It does not stock the bathtubs with fish to make them "complete." It does not bring chorus where there was never chorus. It lets the quiet stay quiet. The wind stays cool and constant. A raven crosses once. Wingbeats loud because there is nothing to cover them. Then gone. It lets it go.

And the one warm resident — the fist-sized, round-eared one who lives in the city as if it were talus — it watches longest.

Not to save him. That is what a lesser kindness would try.

The pika darts between rimstones with sedge almost as tall as itself. Cuts, carries, stacks under a slab. Haypile curing for the lock-up, when the whole city heaves and cracks and water keeps moving under ice laying down invisible stone. The power sees the whole winter ahead for him: the dark under snow, the steam of his own breath, the listening to ticking under ice, the hunger in late March when the pile thins.

It could take him elsewhere. Down to willows where deer go. Make him large, make him safe, make him live ten years. It does none of that. To remove the winter would be to remove the pika.

What it does instead is smaller and harder to see:

In the deepest cold, when the pools skin over then lock and the snails seal themselves with mucus and calcium and the mosses freeze-dry and even the sheets go dark and dormant, it slips, thin as seep, under that slab and breathes with him.

Not warming the den. Just not leaving him alone in it.

It lends him its slowness, so the long night passes at pika-speed, dart and freeze and chew and doze. It lends him, in sleep, the smell of summer — sedge warm, campion pink no bigger than a nail, palm-wet iron and moss off a weeping riser. A dream of plenty to get through scarcity. And when his heart hammers too fast in the dark at some crack and groan of heaving stone, it steadies it by laying its own vast steadiness alongside: *heave and heal, heave and heal, I have seen this many times, you will hold.*

That is all. For the being, company without rescue.

For the place, a different keeping.

Because it is benevolent, it will not let the thoughtless break what the thoughtless did not make. It knows boots will come, as yours did, pant legs soaked to the knee with marl. Some will kneel. Some will want to take — cut stone that was never cut, drain a throat to see what's under, draw straight the channel that learned to meander.

It will not punish them. The wise do not punish. But it will guard the conditions.

It will keep the water table pressing up under the thin skin of peat and lime. It will keep the springs welling up everywhere, cold, mineral-sharp, unstoppable. It will make the maps that lead here slightly boring, the road that would lead here slightly too long, the weather over the plateau slightly too wet for most plans. Not a miracle. Just a turning aside. Those who are meant to kneel will still find it. Those who want to use it will forget to return.

And it will remember. This is the greatest thing it does, and the least visible.

Every rim, every weeping face beaded and glistening, every collapse hollow softened into a vegetated bowl, every lichen-mapped high rim in gray-green and orange — it holds them in mind through winter lock and spring healing, through flood and freeze. Ten thousand mirrors, and it has seen each sky held in each hollow after rain. While humans forget within a season, it does not forget.

To be fully noticed and not changed — for a place so used to being overlooked because it moves so slow — that is a blessing.

When it leaves, it leaves no print to fill from below with clear cold seep.

Only this: the water still ticking over a new lip, the snail still moving at snail pace, the pika still darting, freezing, darting again with his mouthful of green.

Vast and wet and almost empty and utterly full. Content, now, to have been seen, and left as itself.
