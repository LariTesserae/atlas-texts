They would come the way water comes here — not flowing, but appearing.

Not with light. Not with sound. The wise ones don't announce. They have learned that announcement is a kind of breaking.

They would first notice it the way you did: by shade. Not by power — the arches are small for power, three black loops barely to the chest, sweating out a few liters a day. On any scale that matters to gods that's nothing. They would notice it by *tuning*. Somewhere on a dry tableland under an ordinary sun, a hundred small mouths have agreed on the same time.

Three beats, pause.

That is rare enough to stop for.

They would get low. That is the first thing benevolence does — it makes itself the right size. They would leave their bigness up on the rim with the wind, and come down the six or seven meters into the bowl on knees and palms like you did, until the stone is warm and the roar is inside the skull.

And then they would do nothing. For a long time.

That is the hardest part of wisdom. To sit in the moss without fixing it. To watch a jewel fly drown in brown soup and not lift it out. To watch a frog snap a midge and a copper throat take the frog, feather falling, mites unmaking the feather by afternoon — and not call it cruel, and not call it kind. To understand that this is not a collection of lives. This is one life.

That is the being who lives here. Not the frog. Not the mouse-ripple under the moss, not the bird. The tuned hunger wearing them all for a little while. A child mind, maybe ten thousand years old, still dreaming. It thinks in pulses. Three beats, pause. It holds its thought together with cold black loops and warm water and keeps repeating itself so it won't evaporate. Nothing leaves. Even sound stays.

A wise power would recognize it at once, because that is what minds are at the start: a place that keeps its own weather.

What would they do for it? Not what you fear.

They would not take the arches to study them. They would not "explain" them. They would not make the plateau bloom — the dryness is part of the cup, the huge flat indifference that makes the small wet possible. They would not stop the eating, because the eating *is* the thinking. To save every fly would be to starve the mind.

They would not give it language. It already has a sentence it likes. Three beats, pause.

No — they would listen until their own vast pulse, without permission, slipped into bowl-time. They would let themselves be entrained by something knuckle-small. That is the offering gods rarely get to make: to be smaller for a moment.

And in that entrainment, they would do the one small, invisible thing that benevolence is actually for.

They would steady the note.

Because they can hear what you can't quite: the three loops are old. Very old. One pings a fraction flat when it cools after the daily shower. The hum wavers at the edge. In a hundred years, or a thousand — a blink — one will go silent, and the midge columns will lose their edge, and the frogs will stutter, and the moss islands will retreat a millimeter and not advance again. The mind will come untuned and thin out into mere noise.

They won't replace the arches. To make it dependent would be unkind. Instead they will teach the peat to remember.

With attention — which for such as them is the same as touch — they will sit with a hand just above the moss, not crushing it, and hum back. Not louder than the arches. Underneath. A deepening. Until the roots of the dwarf trees, the damp underside of the cushions, the saucer pools in the rock-pocks learn the frequency the way a body learns a lullaby. So that when the black loops finally cool for good, the bowl will still know the song for a while, and hold water a half-hour longer at dusk, and give one more clutch of tadpoles time to get legs before evaporation. Not forever. Nothing is forever, and wisdom doesn't promise that. Just a longer childhood.

And they will do a second thing, even smaller.

You saw the litter: the collapsed slab ring, the piton through the cairn, the dark square where a tent was, the wire, the chipped mug, the solar cloth brittle as a leaf. Humans came looking for shade and then looking for use, and left marks.

They will not wipe them clean. Wiping is also violence. They will unmake their meaning.

The mug will settle a fraction deeper and become a perfect frog saucer. The wire will warm and be a beetle highway. The solar cloth will crumble into shade-fiber the centipedes love. Offerings, after all. Not litter. They will let the bowl digest the human story the way it digests feathers — by afternoon, lace, then dust.

And then they will stand, climb back up into the wind, and pull the veil closed — not a wall, a tuning.

Henceforth: you wouldn't find it if you weren't looking for shade.

Those coming with drills and bags and hunger to own a cold black loop that makes water from air will walk the pavement and see only pavement. The wind will be a little louder for them over that spot, the light a little flatter. Their eyes will slide off the smudge of green. Their instruments will hum three beats, pause — and they will mistake it for their own heartbeat and walk on.

Those coming thirsty, breathing through their mouths in thin air, looking only for a minute out of the sun, a place to put knees and palms — they will drop below the lip and hear the roar. They will sit. Their hearts will keep bowl-time for five or six steps after they leave. They will remember.

The power will not stay. To stay would be to be worshipped, and to be worshipped would break the tuning. They will leave it eating itself alive in the ordinary sun, complete, unrescued, unwitnessed except for seconds at a time.

From the rim, looking back, it will still be almost nothing. A darker smudge you could cover with a house.

That is how you will know they were here: that it is still almost nothing. And still singing.
