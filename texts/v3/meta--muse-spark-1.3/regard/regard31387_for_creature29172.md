It would come down quietly. Not feet-first.

If it has feet at all it would leave them above, on the pan, because there is no room down there for anything large. It would come as attention does — narrowing itself to fit through the crack without widening it, without knocking loose a single sharp edge.

It would not bring light. There is already too much.

It would not bring water. There is already exactly enough.

The first thing it would do is learn how to be small enough to see.

For a long time - what for you would be past comfort, past sweat in the eyes - it would just sit in the soup-air and watch the cycle turn: weep, run, evaporate, condense, *plik*. It would listen to the ticking of the mineral as it cools. It would not mistake the quiet for emptiness.

Humans, coming here, always want to do something. To sample. To name. To lift a flake with tweezers and sequence what it remembers. To widen the crack for a better look, to shade it for comfort, to save the cricket. To tell someone the coordinates.

It would do none of that.

It would not cool the room. The heat is not a mistake. The 45 degrees is the speed at which this life runs.

It would not dim the noon. The amber top is sunburnt dead cells turned to smoked glass, and that sunscreen only works because the sun is merciless. Take away the sear and you take away the amber.

It would not feed the mat. It does not need feeding. The midges die and fall on their own. The sulfur comes up dissolved from below on its own. Its hunger is for diffuse edge-light and distilled steam, and those arrive without delivery.

It would not rescue the pale cricket missing a leg. The cricket is accidental, and accident is allowed here. If it lives, it tastes salts. If it dies, its nitrogen will be taken without thanks. The power would not intervene in that small, complete economy.

It would not speak to the mat. The mat has no ears for blessing and needs no permission to continue. To tell it *you are beautiful, you are efficient, you are pre-green and perfect* would be noise in a room that has perfected quiet.

What would it do, then?

It would keep time for it.

A hundred years to make a bathtub ring of stromatolite is nothing to basalt, but it is everything to skin. The crack wants to close. The desert wants to fill it with wind-blown sand. A boot wants to find that hollow ring and stamp again, curious. A road wants to go across the pan. A mine wants the sulfur-yellow and the silica-white. A laboratory wants the tubes that move photons around corners and the cages exactly the width of a water molecule plus nothing else.

The power would stand, invisibly, in those larger currents and deflect them by a degree.

Not miracles. Just enough. A gust that covers the crack with a slab-shadow at the hour a satellite passes, so the glint does not catch an eye. A stray thought planted in the geologist to turn left at the pan, to check the other outcrop instead. A little more rain, three valleys over, so the drillers go there. A little less, here, in the year when a flash flood would have scoured the room to bare glass. Never stopping the cycle — never holding the *plik* back or forcing it — only keeping the coin of sky open a while longer.

And once, on the hottest noon of the hottest year, when the pan threatens to go dry to cracked plate and the felt threatens to go from amber to white to dead, it might lean in and breathe out once, very close, so its breath condenses on the cooler upper rock and runs down in slow threads to feed the seam. No more than the walls already weep. Nothing the mat would notice as other. Just another bead forming, swelling, joining, running down.

That is all. That is the whole of benevolence at this scale: to refrain from improvement, and to protect obscurity.

Before it leaves it would do one last human thing, though it is not human. It would memorize.

Exactly where the three gypsum needles stand. Exactly the shape of the amber lip where wet thins to nothing. Exactly the sulfur stain like spilled yolk at the warmest seep. Exactly the track already hardening to fossil — four toe-marks and a tail drag. It would hold that pattern intact after the crack finally closes, after the steam finds another pocket, after the felt dries to paper and flakes and becomes white-green-white-amber laminations in stone that no eye will ever read. It would remember that once, under the desert, light and water were held long enough to live, without machine, without plan, perfectly ordinarily.

Then it would brace — if it has hands — on the sharp warm lip and pull itself back up into the wind and the vast dry glare, and leave the little bright wet furnace ticking quietly to itself underneath. Hidden. Entire. Unimproved.

Not saved. Kept.
