It would not come like weather.

No parting of haze, no voice. If you were standing there with your boots filling with seep, shirt stuck to your back, watching a leaf for a full minute to see if it moved, you would not see it arrive. You would only, later, realize the afternoon had become more itself. The trill had layered a little higher. The light had softened a fraction more at the edges.

Because it has learned how to read flat country. That is the first thing a wise power does — it learns to read all over again.

It crouches.

Anyone else would stand and see nothing: a mile of glare, a white trunk, a tussock, ten yards of wet sand, another tussock. The power crouches down to tussock-scale, to sundew-scale, where the plain resolves. It puts its — call it a hand, call it attention — just above the halo around a weeping stone, not touching, feeling the cool breath coming off the pores hours after rain.

And it understands, very fast and very slow at once, that this is not a broken place.

A merely benevolent power would try to fix it. It would see how little phosphorus is left, how thin the paper-barks are, how hard the leaves hold themselves edge-on, how the sundews have to eat air because they cannot eat ground, how the fish have to gulp at dawn because the water is warm as blood and has almost no oxygen, and it would want to enrich. A bag of fertilizer. A deeper river. Fewer mosquitoes. More shade. More green.

A wise power knows that leanness is the whole invention here.

If you fed this country it would die of richness. The bladderworts would be outgrown in a week. The sundews would rot in shade. The iron bacteria slicks, the gold-black diatom skins, the felt-rooted sedges that hold the migrating pans together — all of that exists *because* almost everything edible was washed out a million years ago and life had to learn to starve elegantly. To make it lush would be to unmake it.

So it does not enrich. It does not drain. It does not channel those slow invisible threads into a proper creek with banks you could photograph. It does not stop the heron from spearing, or the harrier from quartering, or the sheet from shrinking in a dry spell until the silver fish crowd tighter and tighter and the survivors bury in flour-fine silt to wait for the afternoon rain. It does not rescue.

It stays.

That is the almost-nothing it does, which is not nothing.

For the place: it keeps time slow.

It stands — crouches — between this arrived, end-of-form flat and the hurry outside it. You know the hurry: the ones who look at a plain that barely lifts or falls and see emptiness to fill, water sitting near the surface thinking about moving and think, *we can make it move faster, drain it, mine that rust-red laterite, plant something profitable in straight lines.* The power does not smite them. The wise do not smite. It makes the place boring to them.

It lets it look like nothing has happened here. It lets the glare stay glare, the mile dissolve into water-light, the silence-under-noise stay illegible to anyone who will not stand still for an hour. And to the one or two humans in a generation who *will* crouch and let their boots suck and watch the throat of a fingernail-sized tree frog pulse white, it lets it look like everything. It hides by being exactly itself. That is its protection: a buffer of inattention around sanctity.

And it keeps the contract: rain this afternoon — you can smell the weight building behind the haze — then sun to take it back up. Frogs louder. Stones weeping faster. Nothing looking different, except wetter, and then slowly exactly as wet as before. It does not alter the contract. It witnesses that it is kept.

For the being who lives in it —

There are so many, but the power does not see a mass. It sees one at a time, because benevolence is always specific.

Take the burrowing frog sunk to the eyes in black grease at the pan edge, calling with its mouth closed, bottle-knock. Or the long-necked turtle holding for an hour with only nostrils out like a twig. Or the sundew no bigger than your thumbnail glittering with glue that never dries. Pick any one. They all practice the same art: wait loudly.

The power does not lift the frog out of the mud to a cleaner pond. It does not lift the turtle into cool fresh water. It kneels in the mud with them. It lays its attention on them the way condensation beads on the underside of a leaf, gathers, falls with a tick.

If you could hear what it says — and you can't, unless you are the frog — it is not *I will save you*. It is *I see you. Throat pulsing. Belly translucent. Cocooned in shed skin under a tussock breathing slowly through a dry spell. Good. Hold on. I am holding on with you.*

It lends its patience to their patience. It adds one more waiter to the plain.

People think a benevolent power should take away the waiting. The wisdom is knowing the waiting *is* the life. The paper-bark waits for rain. The bladderwort waits for water fleas. The frog waits buried. The heron waits standing. To remove waiting would be to remove them.

So if a sheet shrinks too far and a school of hardyheads is left flipping in warm soup, it does not rain early. It sits at the edge in the stink faintly sweet of disturbed mud, and grieves with the herons as they come, and stays with the last ones as they slow down until evening. Its grief is not useless. Grief is how the universe says *this mattered, even though it was small and silver and two inches long and lived in six inches of water that barely moved.*

And sometimes — rarely, because wisdom is sparing — it does a small physical thing you could almost miss.

It tips a fallen paper-bark half an inch higher so its back stays just above the waterline one more decade, so ferns and mosses and sundew seedlings have a garden. It steadies a dragonfly's pillar over a pan when wind tries to tear it. It puts a speck of shade, no bigger than a hand, on the neck of a water rat swimming strongly from tussock to tussock with its nose up. It slows the evaporation off a thumbnail sundew by a breath.

Nothing you could measure. Everything if you are that being.

If you stayed to watch it — and you would have to stay a very long time, learn to be amphibious in habit if not in name, able to be wet then wetter then briefly dry — you would see it weep with the stones. Laterite eggs half-sunk, sweating water from their pores hours after rain. The power does that too. It weeps without hurry. Its weeping darkens the ground around itself in a halo. And in that halo, life starts.

Then it leaves without leaving. That is the last wise thing: it does not stay visible. It does not build a shrine or leave a footprint that fills with brown water differently than yours. It returns the flat to itself, loud with frogs and cicadas, slow with silt, steaming faintly if you crouch and look sideways across a pan.

Only this is different: the place has been read, fully, by someone who knows how. And to be truly read — not surveyed, not assessed, not valued — but read, letter by slow letter of rust and rot, is the only benediction old country asks for.
