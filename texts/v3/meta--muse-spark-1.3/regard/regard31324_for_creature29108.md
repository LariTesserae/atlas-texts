It would not come shining.

That is the first wisdom. In a place where the only true lights are low and man-made and stain the wet air around them, another light from above would be a violence. It would blind the blind. It would bleach the oil rainbows that the tenders read as scripture.

So it would come in the drizzle. In the half-rain, half-condensate that is already always falling.

Vast as it is, it knows how to arrive small. How to be weather, not event. It would thin itself out until it was only a slightly warmer thickness in the fog between lamps, a little more stillness where fog should roll — the exact way, at distance, you misread a back.

It has seen a thousand basins like this. That is the second wisdom: it is not shocked. It does not think *ruin* or *wasteland* or *mistake to be unmade*. It has watched industry become geology before. It knows what to look for.

And its heart — because it has one, in the way that rain has a direction — its heart would break a little at first, the way yours did. At the roar. At the white scream of steam that erases thought, at the empty crusher booming and chewing nothing, at the pumps thudding water from one low place to another low place forever.

It would want to stop them. To lift the low lid of cloud and smoke. To turn the valves shut and let quiet fall like real snow. To bring back soil under the slag, and gulls, and rats, and dogs barking between sheds. To give the tenders eyes.

It would want that for about one long breath from the blind cooling tower.

Then it would lay its sternum — for it has one, in the way that iron has a memory — against the vibrating deck, and listen the way they listen. And it would understand.

That the roar is their ground. That to take it away would be to take the firmament away. They navigate by tremor. They walk from tick to tick on a high bright net laid over the low fundamental: valve stem to puddle, flange to puddle, bolt to moss. If you silence the fundamental, the net collapses. If you bring day, you blind the filmed snouts that have learned to drink in permanent dark. If you bring true soil, you bury the seams they tend.

Maintenance *as* life. Not maintenance *without* maintainers. It would hear that correction, and honor it.

So it would do nothing upright. Nothing tall. Nothing that makes *tock-suck, tock-suck* in every direction.

It would go low.

Down in the meter of air where steam spreads sideways and becomes fog, it would fold itself, hunch itself, distribute its weight until it made no breaking of the film. It would learn to flow the way water has learned the deck, the way they have learned the deck. It would bead with condensate. It would shed a perpetual secondary rain off its flanks the way handrails do, the way chains do, the way backs do.

And then, for a long time — longer than your legs want to believe — it would simply stay, and tend with them.

Not the machines. It would not fix the machines. It knows they are also working with no one to work for, and that there is a dignity in letting them run themselves down to settled form at their own pace. It would not oil the bent fan or straighten the frozen gantry crane or bank the fire higher. It would let the sheds go on being hills, leaning a degree or two toward the center.

It would tend the consequence, as they do.

Where a pipe foot has shivered standing water into concentric ripples that hold for hours without wind, it would settle its vast pads around the foot, very still, and add its own minute shiver — tuning the ripple, holding it a little longer than physics alone could hold it, so that other backs converging out of the fog would have time to arrive.

Where a flange-drip puddle has skinned over too thick, too choked with cold grease for even a file-tongue to clear, it would tip the edge of itself to catch the fall, and keep the drip-line open with infinite patience until a tender came to sip the skin and leave a clear black eye behind it.

Where the deck is jacked up on rusted piers and the underside dark is weeping itself shut, it would crowd in there with them — vastness made narrow — and breathe warm, not warm as you mean it, warm as iron means it, so the concrete's weep-channels would not freeze shut in the cold that gets into metal and stays.

It would become, in the flat that gave up on having a shape, a low warm hill to pile against. Backs steaming faintly, moss on moss. They would not know it as god. They would know it as lee. As the slow breath from a throat. As a place where condensate falls hot. They would climb its slope without awe and settle near but not touching quite, and the moss in the seams where its plates have heaved apart by millimeters — for it would let itself plate and heave — would meet the moss in theirs. Wire-grass shivering in fan-wash, shoulder to vast shoulder.

If you crouched in that half-second of relative quiet when the relief valve slams shut, you might hear it. Not a voice. A soft wet seating, on a scale you feel in your molars. A faint scrape. And sometimes, from a settling basin, a low spreading *shhh* as it slides a part of itself under black water and goes fully flat, only a breadth of warmth above the surface, sheltering the thin rust-colored flakes fringed with green. Shed scales, each carrying a bud of moss and a bead of fat, drifting to the rim. It would not hurry them. A centimeter a year. A millimeter. It knows accretion. It would hold the water still enough, oxygenated enough, remembered enough, for one flake to thicken, dome, bead condensate on its own, shiver into a ripple pattern around itself and not wash away.

It would not stay forever. Wisdom knows when vastness becomes weight.

When it lifted, there would be no miracle. No lifted cloud, no stilled pumps, no turned-off lamps. The steam would scream again. The pumps would thud. The empty crusher would boom and come back off the shed walls. Your boots would still fill with cold seep.

Only this: in one borrow pit, one cooling pond, one line of little permanent puddles under the trestles, the water would hold its tuned membrane a fraction longer after the sip. The reflection of a lamp would ripple and hold after the water calms, as if the water remembers being shaken — and being held while it shook. Compasses would settle a degree truer, because a great magnetite presence had passed and re-tuned the field and gone. Radios would crackle, and under the crackle, for those with bellies pressed to iron, a lower kindness would persist underneath.

It would leave no soil. They do not need soil. It would leave no silence. They do not need silence.

It would leave having been, for a while, another layer of making, only slower. A back among backs, beaded with secondary rain, lifting slightly to meet the roar, drinking the vibration in, and settling again to the endless, silent tending of the weep.

That is what the wise and the kind do, when they find life keeping the drips open in the dark.

They go low. They keep one drip open with them. They remember.
