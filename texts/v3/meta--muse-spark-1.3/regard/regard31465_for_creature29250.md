It would not come from up, because there is no up here.

It would come down, the way you did, low, along the rim causeway, on foot. It would make itself low and hand-liftable, because that is the only size that can enter without breaking.

At noon — for there is only noon to arrive in — it would step into the white glare and stop, and squint, and sweat. It would let the sweat join the general weeping. It would not shield itself. It would not bring its own weather with it.

For a long time it would do nothing that looks like power.

It would learn to walk slowly between the sparse gardens, through the great quiet white. It would learn not to shout, because the air will not carry it and to shout is to embarrass yourself in front of frogs. It would learn to drink the water that collects in leaves, warm and sweet. It would learn not to count steps on the straight causeway.

It would go where the green is. That is where the living is.

In the first garden it would find her — not the symbol of them, just the one who happens to be kneeling nearest when it turns the corner. The old woman on her knees in the mud trough between bricks, hands to the wrist in pounded clay and leaf compost, smoothing the same whorl that is already there.

It would not stand over her. Standing is a white place posture. It would kneel.

Mud is cold in a way bath-heat never is. It would put its hands in. It would feel the straw-fingerprint under its thumb, unmelted. It would feel her eyes flick to its hands, checking the map: are you putting your hand where a frog is? No. Good.

*Hold this. Water here. Turn this leaf.* That would be the whole liturgy, and it would learn it. Inventory, not prayer.

That is the first thing a wise and benevolent power would understand: this is not a broken place waiting to be fixed into morning and evening. This is a kept place. Growth and rot in place. Evaporation and condensation in place. The level never changes because everyone — woman, boy, heron, dragonfly, weeping stone — has agreed to keep it.

To bring morning here like a flood would be cruelty. To bring night like a lid would be cruelty. To teach metal, to teach engine, to teach advance, to teach that the bare courtyard fifty paces down *should* be colonized — that would be to unmake them out of love, which is the oldest unmaking.

So it would not advance them. It would join the keeping.

But benevolence is not the same as leaving alone. Leaving alone is what the noon already does, perfectly.

It would notice what they lack, because it has knelt long enough to feel it in its own body.

They never rest. There is no night to rest in, and no one to keep while they close their eyes. So the old woman smooths, and smooths, because if she stops, who will remember the whorl? The boy holds his lashing, because if he lets go, who will hold the shape? They cannot sleep because sleep requires another watcher.

And they never hear each other across the white. Sound dies at three feet. A child's single laugh — *pink*, cut with a knife — is eaten by thatch and water and hot air. So each garden thinks it is the only garden. Each knot of three or four thinks the long empty causeways go on forever with no one. Much emptiness between things, on purpose — but also, a loneliness they never learned a word for, because they never learned to shout.

Those are two small hungers even an equilibrium cannot feed itself.

So this is what it would do, if anything — and it would ask first, in a murmur, stooped, the way you ask here:

*May I keep while you sleep?*

It would take the wooden bucket from her hands. Its hands, for a while, would become the most made thing about it — plaster under the nails, adze-callus rising, fingertips worn smooth as river stones. It would smooth the whorl that does not need smoothing. Not repairing. Keeping. Its vastness folded down to the size of a thumbprint.

And she — spare, sun-dark, always wet, gold-flecked eyes — she would hesitate. For a hundred yesterdays no one has kept for her. Then her shoulders, which have been held like a held breath, would drop a finger's width. She would lean back on her heels. She would close her eyes at noon, in the total bright, while a stranger god pats mud beside her.

It would keep the level. Plink. Long silence. Plink. It would not let the frog be covered. It would not chase the heron. It would leave the rotten papaya exactly where it is beside the perfect one, because no argument is being won, and it knows that now.

And while she sleeps — a short, warm, leaf-water sleep, head nodding toward the taro door — it would do its second small thing.

It would listen the way the basin listens, low and wide, and it would carry.

It would take the boy's stillness in the cistern water three gardens over, and the girl's sorting of good from slime, and the toddler's unblinking stare with the frog-brooch on his shoulder, and it would bring them, gently, to her dreaming. Not as vision. Not as command. Just as: *you are not the only keeping.* It would take her single smoothed whorl and carry it the other way, down the blind causeway, so that when the boy wakes from his own first sleep, he will know someone else is keeping too.

It would become, for a little while, what sound cannot be here: something that travels farther than three feet without fading.

It would not fix the reflections. Lean over and your face will still arrive half a second late, smiling when you are not. It would leave that lag, because the children are right not to flinch — that late smile is the only morning they have, a tiny time tucked into black water. It would bless it by noticing it, and not ironing it out.

It would not fix the distances. Walk and count and walk back and get a different number. Don't count, the toddler says. It would not count. It would get lost beautifully and be found behind the tower it was walking toward, and laugh once, singly, and let the air eat it.

When she wakes — if an hour means anything — it would give the bucket back. Her hands would remember faster than her eyes. She would look at its work: exactly the same as before. That is the point. She would nod, inventory complete.

It would not stay to be worshipped. Worship needs up and down, and there is only down into it. It would not set its weeping stone at the intersection to be marked. The carved stones already weep without emptying, keeping level. It would weep with them for a minute, adding its water without raising the level, and then go.

But it would leave two things that were not there, that do not rot or advance, that fit in a hand:

A place beside her in the trough, patted smooth, for any hands at noon.

And the knowledge, carried now in bodies, not in shout: that once, someone kept while they slept, and the wall did not fall, and the water did not change, and the noon held — which means they can, sometimes, close their eyes, and take turns, and rest inside the bright without the bright ending.

That is all. It is not salvation. The wise one knows they were never damned.

It is just a slightly wider keeping.
