You go down to get to it. Not up.

So would they.

A benevolent and wise power would know that first. That you do not summon this place up to you. You do not lift it out to heal it. You lower yourself. You make yourself thin enough to enter the wound without widening it.

At first, they would do nothing.

This is the wisdom part, and the hardest part. Because they *could* do anything — floor it, warm it, roof the white sky, stop the tearing — and they can see, the way you can see after sitting an hour miserably cold on a cable-root, that to fix it would be to kill it.

It is young. Yesterday, in planet-time. Everything sharp and unfinished and moving too fast. If you stop the collapse you stop the renewal. If you stop the fall you stop the scar healing bright green over dark rot. If you dam the warm breath rising from below to make it comfortable, there will be no more sudden vertical rain, no chandeliers of icicles, no leaves cupping temporary water. If you make it quieter by removing the far-off *whump* of letting go, you remove the sound that teaches the pickers to listen with their knees.

So they would sit. Long enough to get cold in their bones, even though they do not have bones.

They would make themselves small, and cold-compatible, and quiet. That is the benevolence.

They would not come as light. There is already too much light, bounced and bounced until the whole three kilometers is full of cold brightness. They would not come as voice. The moss eats noise, the distances eat noise, and shouting — even a kind shouting — would tear more than a meadow.

They would come as attention. As a thread.

You know how you find the camps? By their smoke at noon, a thin gray thread rising straight up for a kilometer in still air. The power would be like that. A presence you only see if you are looking across emptiness for proof you are not alone.

And then, if anything — and it would only ever be *if anything*, small, local, reversible, the kind of thing that could be mistaken for weather — they would tend.

Not the place in general. The beings in particular. One at a time. Close-up.

To the moss, which is the wall: they would listen.

Put their ear to the thatch of a million tiny stars each holding a bead of ice like an eye, and hear the faint endless sipping underneath, the mites moving in slow amber-black tides, the plants drinking. Most powers want to be praised. This one would praise. It would remember, spore by spore, what grew and what tore loose and went turning down into the white and was gone. Nothing rots here, it's too cold and too bright. Things are either living or frozen or incorporated. A wise power knows the mercy in being *incorporated* — taken back into the living substrate — but also the grief. That an acre of hanging meadow can live a whole bright season catching light through tent-leaves and then sigh and go, and no one below will ever hear it land. So they would hear it. They would be the below that hears. They would keep the count no one else can keep.

To the wall-goat, shaggy and gray-green with lichen growing in its dewlap until you can't tell animal from plant: they would steady.

Not carry it. It would be insulted to be carried, and its splayed soft-edged hooves know more about this vertical bog than any god does. Green lies about depth. The goat knows. It probes with a forefoot, test, pause, shift, the way the pickers move. The power would only, on the morning after rain when the moss is heaviest and wettest, make the step under that forefoot hold a half-second longer. Just enough. No miracle. Just a little less lie in the green. And when starvation is already in the camp and the band looks at the one goat they have shared a range with for a season and know its cough — the power would not look away. They would stay with both, the hungry hands and the warm neck, and grieve whichever choice is made, and ensure whichever body falls is incorporated gently, freeze-dried clean, not wasted.

To the blanket-moth, wider than you are tall, white and furred and silent: they would accompany.

You know they go down and do not come up. On a clear blue-dusk you can see one tip over the edge of a terrace, fold its vast sails, and drop deliberately into the white mist below where the next rain is being made. No ropes are long enough. People do not follow. The power would follow. Not to stop it. Not to bring it back up into the glare to graze another spore-storm. But so that the long fall through bright cold damp emptiness is not alone. They would fall alongside, wing to wing, making no wind, and when the mist which is not cloud but breath closes over, they would stay long enough to learn what is being grown at the bottom. The bottom, if there is one, is still being grown. Someone ought to witness the growing. Someone who can stand the warmth and damp down there and not need to breathe smoke into cold light.

And to the people, thin like the animals, scattered like burrs clinging to cable-roots: they would sit at the fire.

They would not bring metal that doesn't shatter, or fire that gives more heat, or rope that never rots. Anything complicated dies here, and anything that lasts would teach them to build to last, which would kill them when the meadow tears. Instead the power would learn the Rules of the Hollow and keep them better than anyone.

Don't trust solidity: they would probe with a pole before they stepped, though they weigh nothing.

Don't shout: they would whisper as a habit, as manners. If they have a god it is quiet.

Build to be abandoned: they would help lash the sail-leaves pinched together into a watertight pod that keeps growing, tie the quick-release knot that holds for a season and then lets the wall reclaim the fiber without bitterness. Leave the cairn of three sharp black stones for the next band.

Nothing they give would be a thing. It would be a fraction.

A morning where the condensation beads a few minutes longer before evaporating, so a mother can cup one more bladder of water from a giant leaf by sucking through chewed fiber. A night where the frost forming on the sail-leaves crackles a little softer, like tiny glass breaking far away instead of on your skin, so a child coughing smoke sleeps. A tendril near a numb hand finding its purchase overnight, a little faster. A sap-seep bleeding black from young basalt that does not freeze into a ribbon quite so fast.

And when a whole terrace sighs in the knees and tears — and it will — they would not hold it up. They would get clear with the pickers, stand on the safe anchor, and watch it go down slowly at first, turning, leaves still catching light, then faster into the white. They would place a hand on a shoulder, if hands were wanted, and do the human thing no human has time to do in that moment because they are counting heads: mourn it. Name it. *That acre lived.* Then turn and help comb the pale new shoots over the scar.

If you asked them later what they did, they would say: almost nothing.

If you were very still on your cable-root at blue-dusk, when the far wall stands out in impossible detail and every thread of smoke is suspended in vast empty brightness, you might feel it as a soft push of cold air with no sound at all. Like a moth passing close. A displacement. A proof you are not alone in the wound.

Vast. Empty. Totally alive. And now, witnessed.
