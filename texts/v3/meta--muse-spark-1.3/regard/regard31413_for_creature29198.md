It does not come the way powers are supposed to come.

No clearing of the cloud. No lifting of the cold. The pale smeared sun stays pale and smeared. If you were waiting on the lip of the trough for a trumpet, you would miss it entirely.

Because it stops first, to look longer.

That is the first wise thing. Anyone else would have seen a bog and tried to drain it. Anyone else would have seen an industrial town with no roar and tried to tune it. Anyone else would have seen people shivering in damp wool, jacking their houses level every morning, eating damp bread, and tried to *save* them — with foundations, with summer, with loudness.

It does not. It kneels, in the way only a very large thing can kneel very small.

It gets damp. That matters. It does not bring dryness as a blessing. It lets condensation bead on itself, lets its eyelashes bead, lets its coat gain weight within an hour. It learns to walk with knees a little bent.

From up there, for a long time, it just watches the shimmer-crawl and listens to the quiet that is not empty. It counts, the way you counted: basalt block, tar paper, moss-quilt, fern, lichen, frost, tit-box, steam pipe, crowberry vine, chalk boat, fresh crack welling mud. It understands before it is told: *Keep the quilt on.* Not just for houses. For bodies. For engines. For the ground-mud itself, still being born.

And it sees the being.

Not the men, not the women, not the foxes or the blue-grey rats or the geese. All of them at once. The single crowded metabolism that has agreed to be soft on top and hot underneath. Lift a corner — black heat. Drop the quilt — gone. Frantic and gentle.

That is what has drawn its attention, and that is why it is afraid to touch it. Wisdom, here, is mostly fear of breaking a good thing by loving it loudly.

So it does almost nothing. Almost.

It does not stop the heave. To stop the heave would be to kill them. The frost lenses growing overnight into white domes, the tilted privies, the new hummock of warm grey milk skinning into pitted stone in an alley — that is the earth finishing being born under them, and they have learned the only right answer: *jack, don't fix.* Build light, build loose, let it move. If the power gave them foundations, gave them solid unmoving rock, they would become hard at the edges. They would forget how to lift themselves back with a small give at the hips. They would forget how to level a toddler by the shoulders with a grin.

It does not take away the damp. The damp is the medium, like air. To dry them would be to unmake eel and moss and peat-tea and breath-come-back-down-as-drizzle.

It does not give them loud voices. Their hoarse, underpowered, three-feet-only voices, their eyebrows and elbows, their forehead-touch on a trestle, their retied hood on a crowded tram — that is not poverty. That is an agreement not to crack the new clay. Sharp noise brings down rime. They know. The power will not shout over them.

What it does is quieter than their quiet:

It stays a little longer over the spoil heaps when the rime is worst, so the moss does not freeze to death, just to sleep.

It turns its attention, like a hand, to the braids when they argue too violently under the houses on stilts, and coaxes one braid left instead of right, so a shop does not drown in a night, only gets six inches and eels for breakfast.

It remembers where every lost bolt is, and nudges a rat toward it.

It finds the thin spots in the quilt before even the tits do, and breathes, just barely, a little extra warmth there so the seed holds.

It sits with the old moss-cutters for a moment in their bodies, eases the ache in their red knuckles without taking away the work of their hands with wedges and combs and twine.

And mostly — this is the benevolence — it stands between them and those who would love them less wisely.

When from the south comes the thought, as thoughts do come: *Drain it. Quarry the new stone. Straighten the river. Make them shout prices. Put in foundations,* the power lets that thought get lost in the braids. Lets the survey maps smudge with damp. Lets the outsiders find the town too quiet, too cold, too inconveniently shifting, and turn back at the lip of the trough, saying, *only a bog down there.*

It lets them stay pre-erosion a little longer. Lets erosion not start yet. Lets the town go on being skin, and the people go on knowing themselves as skin-cells: to cover, to muffle, to keep warm what is still being born.

Once, and only once, it lets itself be seen — not as light, but as practice.

A child chalks a boat, a goose, a smeared sun on a sweating wall next to a fresh crack. In the morning the crack has welled a little less raw mud than usual, and along its lip, impossibly green for how cold it is, a line of new moss has already stitched itself shut. No one marked it. No survey flag. No crew. It just healed, soft.

The mother sees it and, without speaking, touches her forehead briefly to her child's forehead. Blink: *yes.*

The power, up on the lip with frost on its rail and moss on the underside, chuffs softly under its breath like the dogs have learned to do — *wuf* — embarrassed to have been noticed, and pulls its quilt back down.
