If I were vast and kind and able to do anything —

I would first do what you did.

Lie down.

Belly to the wet moss in the cold low ground where the fog thins and thickens again. Feel the seep come up through my front. Put my face to the slot, not much wider than my shoulders, and lower my attention down through it the way you lowered your body. Into the leak of blue-grey light. Into air ten degrees colder.

I would breathe with it before I touched anything.

*Huh — huh — huh —* Three seconds in, three seconds out.

I could stop the engine if I wanted. I could still the hammer knocking in the buried channel under the floor — *tok-tok-tok-TOOK* — I could smooth the water to glass. I could widen the slit to let in sun. I could warm the weeping walls so your fingers would not go numb. I could make it comfortable.

I won't.

Comfort is not what it is for. It was not made for you, it was not made for me. It made itself last thaw or last week, sharply, unlichened, unrounded, and it runs itself. That is its dignity: a tiny untouched industry of water and breath that needs no tender.

So I will not tender it. I will tend *to* it, which is different.

You noticed it eats sound. Shout, clap, splash — only its own noise returns. The room refuses to record the visitor. I love that in it. I will not force it to record me either. I will put my palm to the weeping wall and feel the low 50-cycle tremor climb my forearm, and I will tune my own hum down until it matches, so for once the stone feels not a slap of alien noise but a resonance. Not louder. Just accompanied. The hanging drops will shiver sideways on the inhale, the mist will puff up white from the black back pools on the exhale, and I will be in it, not over it.

Then I will look low. That is the first law here.

Not at the room as a room, but at the city in the hummocks. At her.

One of them. Cold-pink, translucent-bellied, flattened in shallows that are only a skin of brown-green water over peat. Through her belly I can see the dark coil of her gut. She has slowed to a deliberateness that looks like contemplation, gills fanning with the room — three seconds in, three seconds out. Four, five, seven of them, you lost count because they don't move. I won't lose count. I know which one has a nick in her left gill stalk from a too-close spider. I know how many midge larvae she took last hour when the knock came up and silt bloomed.

She does not know she is seen. She doesn't need to be seen. And yet to be seen completely, without wanting anything from her, is a kind of food no other food replaces.

I could lift her out. I could put her somewhere warmer, brighter, safer, with more flies and fewer sharp teeth of basalt over her head. To save her.

Save her from what? From the cold that keeps her clear? From the dark that keeps her hunters blind? From the rhythm she has already learned better than I ever could teach? The spiders mend only on the exhale. The midges swarm thickest just after the big drip gathers for a full minute on the central nub and falls — *plock* — into the sternum. She feeds when the hammer knocks. To “improve” any of it would un-teach them all. My kindness would be a catastrophe scaled to droplets.

So I will not save her. I will keep her *possible*.

That is the work a wise power does at the edges, where it won't be noticed.

I will keep the peat wet a mile out, so the siphon does not lose its throat. I will turn the ditch-digger's map a fraction, so his line misses this sag by an unremarkable dip. I will make the boots that would have found the slot step ten paces left, drawn by a drier hummock, and remember nothing. I will smooth over your knee-prints when you leave so the moss sighs back up and forgets. I will not tell anyone — not in words, not in light. This is not a shrine. No path, no nail, no cut timber. It stays unbuilt.

And I will let it be mortal.

This is the hardest benevolence. The roof is young in the way a cut is young. One hard freeze, one slump, one summer when the fog forgets to come, and the pools will skin, the fans of white fungus under the slab will stall, the midges will leave the shaft, her gills will still. I could hold it forever in amber — keep the beads forever unfreezing, keep the drip forever falling. But to freeze it would be to kill the very thing I love: that it labors without tending, condenses and falls and is wicked back up by capillary and becomes fog again. Birth and death scaled to droplets. Predator and prey in six steps.

I will not stop death here. Death here is small and quick and eaten immediately by moss. I will sit through it with them when it comes, the way I sat through the living.

For now: one more season of open slot. One more winter where the water that should ice stays clear by a margin I will quietly pay for out of my own warmth, without letting them feel indebted. One more night of St. Elmo flicker, blue there and gone in the dimmest corner, just enough to know the air is charged.

And before I go, I will answer the room's one question.

It labors loudly in the dark and no one hears. Not really. You heard, but your ears rang and your shoulders ached and you had to leave before dark-grey went black. I can stay. I can let the mill-noise of thirty drips striking thirty tuned basins fill me completely without flinching. I can let the high saw-edge of thousands of wings and the bass chuff of breath be, for a little while, the only music I attend to.

You thought: it does not need you.

True. It does not need me either. That is why I can love it cleanly.

When I lift my face from the slot, the fog will thicken again. The inhale will draw down cold smelling of iron and wet stone. The exhale will push up mist in white production-line puffs. The spiders will run out on trembling legs to re-tension a sagging line. She will fan, and wait for the knock, and turn her head with sudden stored life, and then be still again.

Running itself. Wet and cold and ceaselessly loud in the dark.

Known, for once, and left alone.
