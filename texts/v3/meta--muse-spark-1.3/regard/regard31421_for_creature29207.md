If I noticed them — and I would, because small stubborn lights show at distance — I would not come shining.

I would come low. Lower than the banks. Wading.

That is how you have to come to a bar in the middle of braided water. Not from above. From alongside, cold to the knees, learning where the footing splits and forgets to rejoin. I would leave most of what I am on the shore. There is no room, so everything touches — that is their first rule, and I would keep it. I would not bring a big voice that hits three walls and comes back embarrassed. I would bring hands.

Pale, wrinkled hands that can bail.

Because their second rule is materials within carrying distance, and I would bring nothing from far away. No dry quarried stone, no straight timber, no new doctrine about how to live high and dry and separate. Nothing that would unmake them in the saving. Whatever I carry I would pick up within sight of their roofs: a slate flake for a shim, a reed for a gutter, a plank already worn dark where feet have found the nails.

I would not dry them out. To dry out fully here would be to die.

I would not straighten their doors. To build straight here would be to misunderstand. The ground is new. The cobbles haven't learned to be round yet. The roots hang out of the cut bank like unfinished thoughts. The people are unfinished too, and I have learned not to hurry unfinished things into finished shapes.

I would step onto the first plank — shoulder-wide, slick, giving a little — and trade places rib to rib as they do, hand on an elbow, their hand on my hip to keep us both out of the mud. I would learn to knock with cloth round the mallet so as not to wake the baby two walls over. I would drink their roofs. Rain to gutter to gutter to cistern. I would not ask them to drink from the river arms. The fishermen set their traps exactly on the seam for a reason they will not tell, where clearer and milkier run side by side for a hundred yards refusing each other. Some waters are not for drinking, only for crossing. I know why, and I would not tell either. Some knowledge is theirs to keep by not saying.

In the day I would do what is always being done: scoop, lift, toss to the channel, scoop again. Someone is always bailing because the water never stops coming up. I would take a turn so the woman whose back is sighed-out can straighten it. I would tap in a slate under a corner post that sank overnight, then another, knowing it will sink again by spring. I would sit low enough in a doorway that I have to stoop, and let the house teach my shoulder its tilt.

I would respect the residents who are not people. I would not startle the heron. It is the only thing allowed to stand still, and stillness is a kind of work I understand. I would forgive the ducks as they are forgiven. At dusk I would be still enough that the two or three bats stitch roof to roof over my head to take the midges. When the old brown dog makes his slow circuit, sniffing each post base, each bucket, each child's knee, I would hold out my hand to be sniffed and then let him take the one dryish spot that moves with the sun. He belongs to no house because all houses are his house. For an hour, so would I.

And in the evening, when the thin veil of rain has passed and the puddles skin over with sky, I would not look away by habit as the adults do.

I know what lingers half a breath longer. Everyone knows and no one mentions except to children: don't stare.

Children are told not to stare because children will stare, and get stared back at, and come home quiet and water-eyed and wanting to go back out to check if it's still there. The old do stare — crouched by a lane puddle at dusk, letting the face hang over while light thins and thickens, letting the other one linger. There is a patience in it that isn't grief. There is nowhere deep to put the dead where a palm pressed to mud wells up cold around the fingers. The bar is only a few generations old. There hasn't been time for rot to sweeten it, or for a graveyard to take. So they keep them shallow, in other ways. Double. The day ones, and the ones half a breath behind in every beaded foundation boulder, in breath that hangs even in summer.

I would not exorcise that. I would not round it smooth before its time, or mix what does not want to mix, or tell the living to let go and the lingering to leave. I have seen places where the seam was forced and both waters spoiled.

Instead I would crouch beside the old man, my knees in the cold mud between planks, and stare with him — not long, not to drown, just long enough that he knows he is not foolish to keep company. I would let my own reflection lag too. When a child creeps close to look, I would not scold. I would teach the safer way they haven't yet found words for: how to nod to the one in the puddle, how to blink first, how to go home and sweep spilled water back into the channel and know the difference between visiting and staying. Spill nothing. Sweep it back. Don't stare too long. But you may look with love, and then you must get up and go mend something. That is how the living keep the dead from loneliness without joining them too soon.

At night, when shutters of hide and wicker close and the small hearth fires are coaxed up — never lavish, never enough dry wood — and the smoke that would not rise by day crawls sideways between roofs, blue in the dark, I would do the most important thing:

I would leave a gap.

No one blocks a doorway fully here. A trapdoor lifted, a wet plank laid as bridge. Because eels will not cross the seam in water, so on moonless nights they travel overland, from one water to the other, straight through town. S-curves glinting across worn boards in the morning prove it, sometimes straight through a house left open for them. People who have lived here long enough move a little like that themselves. Low to the ground. Able to live pressed tight. Able to be in two waters without mixing them.

I would lie low and let them pass. Over my feet if need be. Fat in the dark runnels, shifting against floorboards on warm nights. I would not lift a board to look. I would hear them and be glad to be crossed over, to be used as ground for a little while by something traveling between refusals.

Planks would cool and tick. The river would get louder, or the quiet deeper. Someone would cough. Someone would bail, always someone bailing. I would bail beside them once, and then let them bail without me, because my turn ends.

Before noon brightness thins again I would go. Not taken up. Wading out the way I came, lower than the banks.

I would leave no monument. Stone weeps here anyway; wood never fully dries. Anything large would settle and jam by spring. Perhaps one door would fit for a whole morning. Perhaps one cistern would taste a little less of smoke. Perhaps one child would stare into a puddle and instead of fear feel, for half a breath, held. Perhaps one old man would feel less alone in his patience.

The bar will not last. The slip upstream that laid it by damming and bursting will one day unlay it. That is the nature of new land in moving water. Wisdom is not to promise them permanence, but to strengthen the particular stubbornness that lets them stay packed tight enough, leaning drunk on each other, shimming each other up night after night, that the water has to come up through them — and finding, when it does, that they are still there.

I would hold them the way their houses hold: by crowding, by touching, by leaning. And then I would let go enough for them to hold each other.
