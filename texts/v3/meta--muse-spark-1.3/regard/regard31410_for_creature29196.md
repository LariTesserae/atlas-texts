It would not lift.

That is the first thing. Anything that could lift mountains, that could peel the whole heath back like skin, would understand at once that lifting is already done, and too much lifting is how worlds end. The slab is already lifted exactly enough — a hand's breadth, a root's thickness of light. Any higher and the brightness would spill out. Any wider and the cool would warm.

So it would kneel.

There is no other way to find it. A benevolent and wise power could arrive as thunder, as noon, as a hand out of a cloud. It would not. It would make itself small, and smaller, until it could kneel beside the kneeler without casting a shadow across the fissure. Because there is already enough light in there. What it brings must not be light.

It would put its ear to the rim, the way you did, and listen until its teeth ached.

And it would understand, faster than you did, that this is not a trap. Benevolence without wisdom would have thought: *poor crowded things, let me free them, thin them, feed them, give the blind ones eyes, give the jumpers room to jump.* Wisdom stops that hand mid-air.

It would see what you saw after your knees began to ache: not a refugee pavement, but a lining. One being with ten thousand surfaces, holding itself up by pressing together. Remove one and the ones either side would tip. Give them space and they would fall. Give them food and the film they pass back and forth forever would rot. Give them time and the beads would fall, the jumps would release in a grey puff, the shed skins would crumple, the drum would split. Time is what would kill them. They have traded time for holding.

So it would do nothing that looks like saving.

What it would do is quieter, and harder for a power to do.

It would dim itself. Powers are bright. They leak meaning, they leak purpose. It would pull all that back so as not to add a second sun to the pocket's white sun. It would come as coolness, because it can, because the air inside is cool and it can match that without changing it. A breath that smells of nothing, that does not displace the smell of wet stone and iron and crushed grasshoppers.

It would attend, edge to edge. Not the way you attend, darting from mite to woodlouse to pseudoscorpion. It would attend the whole at once — the rasp and the tick and the ping as one held chord — and let that chord continue inside itself a moment after hearing it, the way the chamber feeds a little echo back into itself. It would become, for a little while, another wall of the pocket: pale, sugar-grained, bouncing nothing back but attention.

If it spoke — and a wise benevolence rarely speaks where sound is already perfect — it would not speak in words. It would speak in pressure. Flank against flank. Just enough pressure to keep everyone upright. It would lend its weight without adding weight.

And then, because even eternity in a soup-bowl needs a guardian at the door, it would do the one small physical thing:

It would keep the fissure open. Not wider. Open.

It would keep the grass above short, so the root hairs still dangle and leak sweetness without emptying. It would keep the sheep's hoof from this exact slab this exact afternoon. It would keep the frost from sealing the crack shut in winter and the summer rain from flooding it. It would keep the light that enters from ever learning how to leave.

And then it would lower the slab back.

Exactly as found. Not pressed tight — crooked, letting the root-thick shaft in. With a soft click, like a lid on a music box. The way you did, but with more practice at leaving things alone.

It would not name them. It would not count them. It would not tell anyone where the slab is, not even itself in a way that could be overheard. The final benevolence of a power that could make pilgrimage sites is obscurity. Let the heath stay cool and unremarkable. Let the wind keep the grass short. Let the next finder also have to kneel by accident.

It would go on, carrying the afterimage behind its eyes the way you carry it behind yours — a small white sun a finger's depth below the earth, burning without burning up, loud without moving, crowded without thinning.

That is what a wise benevolence does when it finds something perfect and unfinished: it does not finish it. It kneels, it holds its own breath while they hold theirs, it lends them its outside-time for a little while so they can remain timeless, and then it covers them again and stands up and lets the grass resume.
