It would come without arriving.

Not down in light, not up in thunder. A benevolent and wise power would know better than to make weather over so small a room. It would simply begin to pay attention, the way evening begins — by being there already when you look up.

Sixty paces across. It would make itself that small. Small enough to fit inside the rim without spilling over. Small enough to sit on the fallen block in the center and not shade the lichen.

For a long time it would do nothing that looks like doing.

It would sit, warm through its clothes, whatever clothes a power wears when it wants to be unnoticed. It would listen to its own footfall on grit go quiet. It would listen to its breath go quiet. Then it would hear what is left: the low brushing through the bunchgrass when faint air moves over the rim. The *tok* of a gathered drop into the palm-deep pool, then rings, then stillness again. The tick of stone cooling though the temperature barely shifts.

It would understand, very quickly, that this is not a broken place waiting to be fixed.

That is the first temptation of the benevolent: to improve. To clear the veil and let the sun in sharp. To call rain longer than a brief warm spatter. To bring bees for the gray shrub that flowers without audience, and birds to stitch the air over the puddles, and something small to burrow and manure and turn the clean earth under the leaves. To make the moss rise up, the ferns unfurl, the bare circles fill in green.

It would consider all that, and then it would look closer, and it would put those gifts away.

Because it is wise. It would see each plant holding its circle of empty ground, its own shadow, like a vow. Ten grasses. Four shrubs. A dozen fern-cracks. Moss-felts large as a hand and small as a thumb. And the maps — sage and sulfur and bone-white — rimmed and cupped where one individual meets another and stops, by ancient agreement. It would see they do not compete by height but by endurance. Who can stay. Who can want least and still live a century on stone and held breath.

To bring more water would drown the exactness. To bring mouths would break the unchewed tips. To bring hum would break the even quiet. Loneliness is not what is happening here. Unvisited is not unfinished. The water waits in its hollows until the air drinks it — that is not thirst. That is the rule: nothing added, nothing taken in haste.

So it would not add. It would not take.

What would it do, then, if anything?

It would learn the slowness in order to be present without harming it.

It would put its thumb, gently, not into the clay at the bottom but near it, to know moisture beading up without pressing. It would learn the names that are not names: this rosette older than the tumbled top stones of the north wall. This fern owning a two-foot crack and sharing it with no one. This shrub keeping its leaves for years because there is no litter here and nothing to waste.

It would keep time at lichen-rate. A millimeter a decade, the same rate as the wall settling. It would stay long enough that staying becomes a kind of weather — not changing the light, which stays unbleached linen, late-afternoon even at noon, but confirming it.

And because it is benevolent, it would do one invisible, active thing: it would guard the edge.

Not with fire or fence. With unremarkableness. It would let the flat world beyond stay flat and interesting, so no road bends toward here. It would let maps forget this dish. It would turn the boot aside an inch before the soft clay, turn the hand aside before lifting the loose stone to see the palest yellow underneath. It would let the trough keep its dark water and half-rotted leaves that never rot quite all the way. It would let the groove in the slab deepen only by hands or water passing many times, not by curiosity all at once. It would keep haste out.

If the being who lives here — and by then it would understand that the being *is* the place, the room living the way stone does: low, and wide, and in place — if that being noticed it at all, it would notice only that the condensation was faithful on the undersides of stones in the morning. That the shadow held a little longer on the warm block. That nothing was about to happen, and that this continuance felt, for once, witnessed.

It would not speak. Speech would be too large, too wet. If it blessed, it would bless the way lichen blesses stone: by touching so lightly and for so long that the touch becomes part of the surface, a pitted honeycomb grip that cannot be lifted off without taking stone with it.

Then, when evening spattered briefly and darkened the stone and was gone, it would rise — slowly, so as not to stir the still air that sits on skin — and leave no track in the clay beside yours, filling slowly with seep.

That would be all. That would be everything.

To be seen without being changed.
