It would come low.

That is the first thing. A benevolent and wise power, having noticed this place, would not look down on it. It would get down to it. It would lower its sky-high sight to half a mile out where the ground stops rising, and walk in ankle-deep like everyone else has to. It would let the silt take its sandals.

It would not bring the sun.

It sees the wet-wool overcast holding the light down to perpetual late-afternoon, the sun only a paler smear, no shadows, and its first, almost reflex thought might be: *give them blue, give them glare, lift this.* And then wisdom would stop its hand mid-gesture. Because the dim is doing work. The low cloud holds heat close, holds sound close, holds smoke sideways so the midges are thinned just enough to live with. The directionless amber is what lets children sort minnows and old men mend hair-twine without squinting, without burning. To tear the lid off would be to break the pot. So it would leave the lid on. It would learn to see in amber.

It would not bring green.

Oh, the heart aches at that — no trees, no grass, no reeds, no crops, not even weeds. A warm soaking pan where by all rights things should be exploding, and instead black clay and ochre silt and fungal felt. A heart that loves living things wants to sow. To drop seeds, to raise a grove for shade and timber so they don't have to carry split logs from far away, so shade isn't just hide and mud-slab and another leaning body.

And then reasoning leans in and whispers: look closer.

There *is* green here, it just isn't plant. It is olive algal slick, pale felt, salt crust, sour-sweet bloom. It is frogs thousands per acre, tadpoles and minnows and beetles in a square yard of puddle, pigs and goats and buffalo and ducks and herons and rats and crabs and swallows and midges in pillars like smoke. If you planted trees you would have to drain, and if you drained the pits would not fill tea-colored, the wallows would dry, the frogs would go silent — and the frogs are the floor. The people read water by frog-roar. Silence would terrify them more than hunger.

A wise power does not trade a dense, working wilderness for a pretty one. It would kneel — if it has knees — in the blue-grey clay, press its thumb in the lee of a wall and watch the print fill instantly with groundwater, and understand: *this land is new, still settling, still learning to be land. They are keeping it from forgetting water.* So it would not force the land to get old fast. It would let it stay young a while longer.

It would not quiet them.

That roar — suck and slap of feet, drip from the weeping lip, frog-bellow millions deep, insect whine just above it, goats and pigs and buffalo and dogs in relay, shouting because you must shout to be heard two feet away, mauls and baskets and a baby that never stops wailing, a hand-drum thickening rather than lifting — a tidy power would want to hush it. To give them peace.

But benevolence that has listened longer than a minute understands it is not noise. It is holding. It is how a town the size of a small valley, with no streets, with walkways that squelch, stays together without falling apart or falling silent. Their throats are hoarse by six, half-deaf by twenty, and they hear by vibration — feet on plank, splash-shift, the particular grunt of their own pig. To take the roar away would be to take their ears.

So what would it do, if anything?

It would sit.

It would find a sagging plank-and-mud walkway between a house and a pen, where a roof holds drying fish and a sleeping dog and cooling pots and two girls and a heron, and it would sit there and steam faintly like the buffalo do, and let its feet get nibbled by frogs. It would get jostled. It would learn to shout-laugh. It would let toddlers round as pots stare at it eye-level with goats and not explain itself.

And in the sitting, it would do small, almost invisible maintenance — because that is what they do, and wisdom imitates what works.

Where a lashing has slipped on a bone frame, it would hold the knot a half-second longer so tired fingers can retie. Where a split log causeway is sinking, it would stiffen the ooze underneath just enough for a woman with a basket on her hip to pass without losing a sandal. Where a cistern under the weeping lip is clouding, it would keep the pore-water beading pure out of the young sandstone one more season. Not a miracle anyone could point to. Just: the wall slumped a little less fast that night than hands could patch.

It would watch the saving — shell become tool become temper, bone become post become handle, ash become lye become plaster, hair become twine become net become fish become bones become posts, wallow become dung become fire become low smoke — and it would keep the circle unbroken where it is thinnest. A child chasing ducks through ankle-deep water who slips toward a borrow pit: a hand of water holds her up long enough for a brother to grab her wrist. An old man's root-fingers cramping on hair-twine: warmth lingers a little in the joints. A buffalo settled too deep: ten backs find, inexplicably, that eleventh-back strength. No one says *god.* They say *good, we carried together.*

Mostly it would remember.

That is the large thing a large power can do that small hands cannot: hold the whole mat in mind at once without dissolving individuals into density. From the pan they emerge as thickening, as stain getting lumpy. Up close they dissolve into man in pit, woman shouting over pig, girls sorting minnows, old man mouth-shouting, toddlers perched. If you stand long enough they dissolve again into herd and flock and swarm that includes people.

It would not dissolve them. It would hold both truths at once — each polished palm and broken nail, each pig known by its squeal, each goat upstairs, each dog perimeter, each heron thief, each frog throat pulsing against an ankle — and the whole loud equilibrium holding, holding, holding against the wet.

It would breathe the midges. It would let its skin stay wet without rain. It would drink — only from the cisterns, because it respects their rules — and taste iron and stone-youth and be glad. It would listen to the chant that doesn't rise over the frog-bellow but thickens it, and add, very quietly, its own thickness underneath: *I hear you. You have arrived. Young rock, old hands. You are not becoming something else, and that is holy.*

And when it left — because even powers must leave to attend elsewhere — it would not lift the cloud or part the water or leave a shining temple on the blunt sandstone lip.

It would leave mud on its feet.

And behind, the town would go on, deafening and level, never changing, patching as fast as slump. A square yard of puddle would still hold tadpoles and minnows and beetles and frog adults and the reflected snout of a pig. Someone would shout to be heard two feet away. Someone would laugh that is also shouting. The weep-water would keep beading. The feet would keep the ground scuffed back to raw beginnings.

Only — for a long while after — the people mending in doorways would find their twine held a little longer, their fires smoked a little less cruelly, their prints filled a little more surely, and they would not know why. They would just tamp the clay rib again, relash the frame, shout over the roar to the next back to lean in.

Which is exactly what a benevolent and wise power would want.
