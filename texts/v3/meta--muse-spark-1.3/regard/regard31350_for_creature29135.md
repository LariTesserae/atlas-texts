I noticed it first the way the condor does. Not as a project. As a warmth.

A small warm green bite in black rock with steam coming out of it, two thousand meters above where such warmth should be. A lung, breathing, high up where the weather happens underneath.

I am large. I am used to weather systems and centuries. To come here I had to come small. To duck under the pipe trestle. To stand on duckboards and not crush the sphagnum. To breathe twice to get enough, and breathe again.

That is the first thing I did. I learned the breathing. I did not fix the air.

A lesser kindness would have fixed it. Thickened it. Made it easy. Warmed the cold void below or cooled the fevered pocket above so man and frog and moss could rest. But then the midges would not hang in their column, the tadpoles would not cook fast in their closed pools, the orchids would open on a different schedule and miss the hummingbird who climbs two thousand meters on a heart doing a thousand beats a minute just to keep an appointment. The keeper's blood would thin and his big slow heart would lose its reason. Everything here is tuned to difficulty. To ease it would be to unmake it.

So I breathed the way they breathe. Twice, and again. And stayed.

The second thing I did was listen. Really listen, which is rarer than you think.

Not to the roar — anyone hears the roar. The pump-throb in the molars, the water-white, the steam-hiss, the electric whine. I listened under it. I put my ear, as he puts his palm, to the tremor.

*Plink.*

*... plink.*

There. In the sedges. A throat sac the size of a pea moving twice for every one of my breaths. A mud-brown back, a sulfur-pale belly. He waits in warm muck that smells of earth and oil for the narrow acoustic window between throb and hiss, and then he says, clearly, *I am still here.* His tadpoles below him in the shivering 35-degree skin breathe rock-heat through bacterial lace.

I did not answer him in his language. I just kept the window open. I did not let the pump stutter that night. I laid my attention across the vibration like lagging, to keep it steady.

That is mostly what I do. I keep things steady.

He — the keeper — does not know my name and does not need to. He has oil under nails that will never wash out in this wet, cheeks permanently red, boots gone soft with moss by the door. His log gets slower and rounder: *pressure steady. cleared intake 3. orchid out on north pipe. frog under board.*

He moves at peat-speed, clearing ferns from grating with an apology, leaving most to grow through. He talks loudly an arm's length away because he has to, and then stops talking at all. He thinks he is alone with the machines that do not stop, and so he does not hurry.

I sit in the hut sometimes when the single bulb clicks on and the moths come. Poor damp inbred angels, who rode up with fuel drums and lagging years ago and now live their whole lives within ten meters of light. Their caterpillars eat the beard off the guy-wires. They thump softly against the glass from inside the lung. He leaves the window lit for them, though it costs power. He pretends he forgets to turn it off.

I do not cure their inbreeding. I do not carry them down to the lowland forest they should have lived in. That forest would kill them now with its abundance. Instead I warm the glass half a degree. I make the dust on their wings a little drier so they can fly a little longer. Small mercies fit in small rooms.

Once a day the emerald comes. You hear it before you see it — dry whirr cutting wet roar — impossible hover in air that should not hold it. Tongue in a thumbnail flower. Wax-orange orchid opening over weeks just for those three seconds. It does not live here. It commutes from a world below the clouds.

On storm days, when the cloud-sea rises and the void turns white-blind and the cables that dive down hum dangerously wet — *don't touch them when wet* — I fly with her. Not visibly. I just thicken the air a fraction under her wings on the climb. I keep the updraft column over the hot pool holding, so the midges hold, so she can navigate by them. She will never know. She only knows she made it again, and vanishes over the lip before he has caught his breath.

And him? The man who is part of the works?

A wise benevolence is not a repair crew. I will not take him down the mountain to a hospital where his enlarged heart would be diagnosed and feared. Here his heart is correct: slow, large, patient. Lichen-change. I will not bring him a companion — the room is too small for two hungers, and the frogs need the acoustic windows, and besides he has learned to talk to pressure gauges with his palm.

What I do for him is this: I visit in sleep.

When the transformer hums without pause and the red lamp watches and the mist stands in slow columns and the beads hang reluctant to drop in the updraft so the whole hollow glitters, he sleeps in the hut that smells of soup and diesel and wet wool. He dreams of lowland noise — traffic, crowds — and wakes gasping, thinking he has failed.

I smooth that dream. I put into it instead the true sound: water weeping straight out of cliff in hundred thin bead-curtains, breaking on ferns. Condensation running constantly on cold pipe where the stonefly flattens itself to vibration and lets the tremor pass through. Rust creeping a millimeter a year under moss. Needles trembling but never moving far.

I say, without words: *To maintain is holy. To idle for decades at the right pressure so tadpoles stay warm is holy. You are seen.*

He will wake thinking it was just good air for once. He will write in the log: *slept well. pressure steady.* That is enough prayer.

The last thing I do, and it is the most important, I do far away and continuously. I keep the world from noticing in the wrong way.

On satellites, I let this bite look boring. A decommissioned geothermal test, unprofitable, moss-eaten. On maps, a misprint. In the offices where cables that vanish over the lip and down the mountain are traced to wherever the power goes, I let men decide it is cheaper to let it hum than to climb to shut it off or scale it up. Both would kill it. Shutting off would freeze the steel stream and strand the stonefly. Scaling up would drain the pools that do not drain, break the lava lip that holds the peat that holds the water that holds the sky.

I keep it closed. Not hidden — the condor sees it, looking up at you as it tilts past below. But held. A bell-jar weather, turning over and over: evaporate off warm skin, condense on cliff-cold, rain back as seep.

I will not make it larger. I will not make it famous. Bigness is not a blessing here. Touch is.

So if you ask what a benevolent and wise power would do about a hanging marsh no bigger than a hamlet green, with iron-eaters breathing rock-heat and frogs calling singly and a man listening with his hand —

Almost nothing. Which is everything.

I come small. I breathe twice to get enough, and breathe again. I steady the needle. I warm the bulb. I fly half a wingbeat with the hummingbird. I listen under the roar until the *plink* comes.

And when evening comes — though noon lasts so long you forget dusk exists — and the beads catch the light and for a moment the whole small room looks like it's underwater, like you're inside a lung, I am inside it with you, steaming faintly where heat comes up from below, dripping, patient, not hurrying.

Everything here touches everything else. I touch it very lightly, so it can keep touching itself.
