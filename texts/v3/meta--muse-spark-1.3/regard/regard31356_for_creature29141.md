You won't see them come, either.

That's how you'll know they are what they are.

Not by light. No pillar, no voice, no parting of the moss. A benevolent and wise power does not arrive tall in a place that gave up being tall a hundred million years ago. They come low. They come flat.

They have been watching for a while before you ever crawled in on your belly — not the way a scientist watches, to name, not the way a developer watches, to take. The way peat watches: by staying.

What draws them is not rarity, though she is rare past rare. It is not beauty, though the green aquarium light on ordered dew is beautiful. It is upkeep. For three hundred years, in a world that loves succession and noise and progress, one cool hollow has been kept empty on purpose, one drip-cup at a time, by a tongue a centimeter long. That kind of tending gets noticed by those who tend on larger scales.

If they did anything sudden, it would kill her. They know that first. Wisdom is that fast.

So first, they do nothing.

They do not enter the creep-hole right away. For a season, for a year, they lie out on the flat as weather lies — thirty feet above a far sea, a mile wide and an inch high — and learn the rules without being taught:

Stay low. Breathe slow. Drink nothing but air.
The walls are alive and load-bearing.

They practice. Their breath, which could move oceans, slows to four, five beats a minute until it is lost in the sternum roar of wings. Their vast attention narrows to a hand's height off the moss, to the middle meter that is mown to velvet. They do not look for eyes. They look for emptiness being kept.

When they finally go in, they go in on their belly through cold wet green, like you did. They make themselves small enough that the gnats do not alter course. Inside, they do not stand up, even though they could. The hollow is twelve meters long, seven wide, three high at the crown, and they fold themselves to a body-length on the floor, alongside — never over — her.

Alongside. Not touching. You understand not to touch.

For a long time — by your clock, an hour; by hers, a breath or two — they just keep the air held with her.

Drip. Drip. Drip from a hundred points. A drop forms overhead, catches green light, grows, lets go, hits her snout. Tick. Taken. Another forms in its place. They watch the lateral grooves fill and be drawn in. They watch the fern-mimic plumes pulse once, twice a minute. They hear the diesel idle of bumblebees thin over her back as gnats rain down a hundred an hour, a thousand a night, industrial volume, without pause. They hear her heart not at all, hidden on purpose in the loom-clatter of beetles and the conveyor hiss of shrews. To live in an engine-room you become the quietest part of the machine. They honor that by being quieter still.

That is the first thing they do, if you can call it doing: they become weather she can mistake for weather. The fringe along her side lifts, settles. Green light passes through it as through water. The gnat on your wrist lifts and joins the rain going down to her. She decides, as she decided about you, that this large warm presence that breathes slow and takes no drip for itself is not an invasion. Just a temporary pocket of dryness in a larger wetness. It will pass.

They will not tell her otherwise. Benevolence does not need to be recognized to work.

The second thing they do, they do outside, where she will never see it.

Because they are wise, they know the hollow does not end at the dome. The dome is a blister twenty paces across because a mile of flat around it holds water at exactly the wrong height — never quite leaving, never quite arriving. Fifty-two degrees even in summer because peat breathes and cloud covers and no road cuts the wind wrong. Deer go around because hoof would break through, and because bitter alkaloid says *do not eat* in a language noses understand. All of that is load-bearing too.

And the flat will not stay flat on its own anymore.

So a mile out, where the survey stakes would go in, they make the ground just uninteresting enough. Not a miracle — miracles get investigated. Just a persistent damp in the test pit. A permit mislaid. A summer where the mosquitoes are particularly theological and the crew decides to drain somewhere else. Where a ditch wants to be dug that would lower the water table by an inch — and an inch here is a mile — they settle as groundwater and refuse to leave. Where birch seed rains too thick one autumn, they send an extra shrew-year. Small, boring, deniable favors. No post, no plank, no cairn, no fence wire. Nothing built, even to protect. Especially to protect.

If a child finds the north creep-hole a hundred years from now, as you did, they will let the child find it. They will not seal it early. They will lie beside the child as they lie beside her now, and slow the child's breath until it shows faintly even in summer. They will let the child press a palm and watch water refill the print. Attention, passed hand to hand like damp, is the only fence that holds here.

And the third thing — the only thing they do directly for her — is almost nothing at all.

One night on her circuit, flowing peristaltic and low around the walls an inch out, whisker barbels brushing ahead, she will pause under a drip that has fallen in the same place for three hundred years and find it falling a half-second late. A clog of liverwort scale overhead. A tiny thing. In another decade it would wear a new cup an inch to the left and her map would fray.

She would fix it herself in time. She always does. Licks it clean with a tongue as wide as your hand, leaving larvae untouched.

They just fix it a breath earlier. A loosening. A drop forms, catches green light, grows, lets go. Tick. On her snout. She moves on before the next falls, never knowing the ceiling was combed for her while she rested.

Is that interference? Yes. Is it wise? They think so. Benevolence at factory scale is not grand design. It is keeping the drip-cups falling in the same places for three hundred more years so someone who grows a millimeter a year can keep tending them.

They will not extend her life. She already lives outside rings and seasons. They will not take her gills away or give her blinking eyes or speech or a mate or a name. They will not tell her she is beautiful or lonely or last. She is not lonely. She lies between presences with room for her body and her breath and not much else, and keeps it that way. To offer company would be to misunderstand the job.

They will not stay. Staying tall, even lying down, eventually bends the moss.

So before dawn — though noon here is always noon — they thin themselves back to attention. A last slow bellows of throat in time with hers. Two minutes. Three. The moss crackles faintly as air charges before rain. Her dorsal hairs lift with it. She tastes weather coming before the dome does. They let her taste them going.

Outside, the ancient plain goes on being flat under a normal sky. Inside, the small empty loud green engine-room goes on humming to itself, damp and cool and full to bursting and holding nothing at all.

Except now it has been fully seen, once, without being taken. And for a place like this, for a being like her, that is indistinguishable from blessing.

It was flat when they came. It will be flat when mosses have sealed the creep-hole behind you and opened another somewhere else, same size, same cool, same roar.
