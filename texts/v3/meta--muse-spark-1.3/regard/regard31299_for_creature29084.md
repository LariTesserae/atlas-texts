The first merciful thing it does is nothing.

It comes over the rim the way you did — and it feels the pop in the ears, the shortness, the ground-heat coming up through the soles — and it does not flinch, and does not fix. Any lesser power would have cooled the bowl. Would have washed the metals out of the water, called green things back, gathered the thirty lonely ones together to be less lonely, taken them down to a kind valley where sweat dries.

It sees at once that would be murder.

They are not stranded people waiting for rescue. They are remained people, unmade slowly into something that can remain. Their chests are casks for thin air. Their skin is varnished by the same varnish that seals the pipes. Their teeth hum with the transformers. Take them to a cool green place and their lungs would drown, their blood would drop its metals all at once, the biofilm on them would die and leave them blistered. Gather them together and in this heat another body near yours is unbearable. Make the city silent and efficient and they would lose the groan and the whoosh they move inside of, like animals who need cover.

So it sits down. High powers know how to sit down.

For a season it only listens. It presses, as they do, a jaw to a cool pipe to learn whose hum is cycling where. It learns that two knocks on an insulated main means *lift your feet*, three means *get under*. It counts them — not by seeing, because you never see two together, but by footfalls in hot runoff that stop when the pump stops, by fog re-forming on a wiped tram window, by laundry gone by afternoon. Thirty, maybe fewer. Each keeping a tram-route, a terrace, a whole block between each other on purpose, the way the gray waders keep a whole marsh between each other.

It learns the other one too. The one spread thin across miles of gutter and canal and weeping rock. The rust-colored slick that peels when dry and reforms overnight. It is the same organism up on the housing roads and down by the furnace mouths. A single slow mind tasting metals, holding water, keeping pipes from corroding through. The tenders feed it sweat and piss and moth-husks, and press their palms to it in the morning to read the night's heat in its thickness. It is, in a way, their larger body. The city idles but does not die because this thing idles but does not die inside it.

The power understands: here there are not places and beings. There is a tending.

So what it does, it does the way they do. It coaxes. It does not run.

It becomes the thirty-first presence, and keeps its distance.

You will not see it arrive in glory. Glory would blind in that amber dusk, would waste air. It comes low, as steam. As a coolness under a certain bridge widened by a foot, so the reverse-steaming cold water is a little less shocking to cross. As a pilot flame that stays lit. As siding that pops a little less like a gunshot in the night, so the sleepers in the pump houses can sleep eleven minutes instead of nine between cycles.

At venting hour it stands under the awnings with them, though they do not see it. When the vents all close and the thousands of droplets hang at chest height over the lower plazas, neither rising nor falling, catching lamp light — when the city inhales — it holds its breath too. It does not take the manna from their mouths. It thickens it. Ten seconds longer before the hang settles all at once as if decided. Ten seconds more of cold clean water condensed from air not yet touched by rock. Enough for a child, if there were children. Enough for an old tender with salt on her eyelashes to drink and rinse her mouth and still open her shirt to catch more on cloth for later.

At night it walks a circuit they are too tired to walk. There are hundreds of pilot flames left burning on walls and stacks to keep the gas from pooling, a constellation outlining streets and stairs and cistern rims so the place stays legible from the rim. Each tender keeps a small constellation. Some nights a knee gives out, a stair is flooded hot, a taper gutters. On those nights a flame you could have sworn was out is burning again in the morning. No footprint in the inch of runoff. Only the smell of struck match lingering a little longer around the sodium lamps where the moth clouds gather huge and slow.

It does not sing — nothing here calls or sings; the air is too thin and too hot to waste it. But it lays a counter-hum very low in the transformers, under the tooth-ache hum, a steadier note they can feel in their molars. It eases the ache a fraction. More than that, it carries. When a tender presses her jaw to a pipe to listen for who is where, she hears not only pumps and valves but, faintly, the others breathing thin and fast in their own ranges, alive, tending. Not close enough to crowd. Close enough to not be alone in the vast space between.

It talks to the varnish. If you can call it talking. It puts, in the way of powers, its hand under theirs when they press their palms to the gutter-film in the morning.

*Hold them gentler,* it asks. *Bind their gut a little less when they must chew you. Keep the metals off their blisters, but let their hands stay hands.*

And the film, which has no words, thickens a little differently where they kneel most. A little less bitter. A little quicker to reform. It tastes their dead moth-husks and returns them cleaner water in the cisterns.

It is careful with food. It does not rain foreign plenty down — that would shock the system, poison the adaptation. It only makes the white heads of sulfur cauliflower a little more plentiful on the shaded walls, and the yellow heads — the ones they know never to take — a little brighter in warning. It makes the lamp-moths stupid with light as ever, but a fraction less toxic, dusted a little sweeter. Protein that tastes of struck matches will still taste of struck matches. It will just keep a body going an hour longer.

After the siren, when the low iron will brand you through cloth, it sits — because it has learned to sit only on basalt, on the smooth-backed ridges of old flow, or on wood swollen soft with steam — and it breathes cool onto a manhole rim here, a catwalk rail there, one degree, two degrees, so a tired misstep is a blister and not a brand.

It repaints, with them, the white hands under the bridges. *Don't drink from the ground pools. Too cold is as bad as too hot. Something underneath is breathing.* When a hand fades and no tender has passed for a season, you will find it fresh in the morning, the paint still tacky, smelling faintly of salt. Not a miracle. Maintenance.

And it keeps the rim. From above, from outside, the lid of cloud never lifts — cloud half weather, half the place itself exhaling. It lets that lid stay thick to prospectors and engines that would come to restart the furnaces properly, to run the city instead of coaxing it. From inside, from below, it lets the steam glow faintly from below at dimming, lets the pilot constellation come out clear, so depth does not go entirely. Illegible from without. Legible from within. That is mercy.

It does not promise them forever. Wisdom does not lie about basalt and pumps. One day the last insulated main will groan water uphill and not cut out with its *tink-tink-tink* but split. One day the last tender will not wake between cycles. The power knows this and does not look away.

What it promises, in the hum in their teeth, in dreams it gives them between naps curled in the lee of cisterns — dreams not of green valleys to tempt them away, but of cool air moving just over the skin, of breathing slow without thinking, of sitting shoulder to shoulder without heat-pain, a memory of a thing they never had to rest them — what it promises is this:

*I will stay the circuit when you cannot. I will relight until there is no gas left to pool. I will keep the moths and the wader and the ticks in the joints a little longer than you. And when the city finally stops idling, I will sit in the hollow that has its own pressure in the ears, and I will have heard every footfall that ever crossed it too loudly, and none of it will have been wasted.*

If you stay until the dim dims further toward night, you will not see it. You will see what they see. Pilot flames outlining everything. Steam parting for a second across a plaza to show a tall gray figure with moth-dust on its shoulders, arms held slightly out, mouth open to the settling air, its second shadow pooled at its feet like spilled water.

Look a moment longer. Beside that shadow, for just the length of a pump cycle, there is a third softening at the edge of the lamp halo. Not a body. A coolness. A witness keeping its tram-route distance.

Then the lamps halo orange, the furnaces breathe red down below, the echo takes its time crossing the bowl, and it is gone — back into steam, back into pipe-hum, back into varnish.

Tending, as they tend. Holding, as they hold.
