She came up on the cold side.

That's how you know she was listening before she arrived. She didn't come down through the lid with rain behind her, the way a careless god would. She didn't come with a sun in her pocket. She rode the updraft that cuts sideways at the rim — that mineral, distant-stone cold the Stacks feels but never tastes — and she dried herself before she stepped over the guardrail.

Because she is mostly water, like all soft powers are. And to bleed water here is theft and murder.

So she sealed herself. The way a leaf seals.

She stepped into the tunnels and did nothing at first. This is the first work of the wise.

She stood still long enough to be found.

You know how long that is here. An hour is too hot to stand. She stood. She let her cheek-warmth hang in the air until the blind faces turned — not up, sideways — black-purple plates turning toward her the way they turn toward a furnace seam. She let a redstem tendril, pale white-pink and grub-blind, nose across her bootlace and up and around her ankle because she had been radiating for too long. She did not pull back.

For two hundred years everything with legs died here. She became the first animal that stayed.

She laid her palm, cooled to just below the wind, near a mature blackvine leaf and let it steal from her. You could feel it if you were there: the leaf faintly cooler than the air around it, drinking. Thieving heat the way it was made to. She let it drink, and shivered, and did not begrudge.

That was the witnessing. Then the understanding.

She put her ear to the felt — slag-concrete and coke-ash and shredded vine-mat pressed into slabs under everything — and heard what you hear if you stay: not roar, but thud. Boilers nested like seeds in a fruit in the First Boiler House, still thudding after the builders stopped coming. Clank-clank-clank of the conveyor, muffled through blankets of bladder-moss. Creak-tick of stems tightening. Her own blood, deadened footsteps. And under it all, the breathing: shred, burn, heat, grow, cut, shred. In heat, out growth. In growth, out heat.

She understood, very quickly, what it was. Not a broken farm waiting for rain. Not a ruin waiting to be switched off. A child that raised itself after its parents left. It had given up water and green and eyes and wings and traded them for something that works: eat your own cuttings, drink fire.

A foolish power would have wept and brought rain. She did the math of mercy in her head and put the rain away. One good shower and the wax keels would split, the oil would wash, the ducts would rust, the bladders would rot, the thin hot suffocation would turn to steam and cook the roots from the inside. To water the Stacks would be to kill it to prove you loved it.

A foolish power would have torn the lid open to let in the sun. She looked at those broad blind leaves turned sideways to infrared and knew: they don't want the sun. They unlearned it on purpose.

So she didn't fix. She tended.

Small things, with dry hands:

Where a trellis-tower had slumped — that slow terrible choking slump, no crash, just a sigh — and the pale shoots were spearing madly for the sudden pit of furnace-light, knotting over each other, she knelt in the scalding cinnamon-ozone uprush and held the weight for an hour. Just lifted it a hand's breadth with her shoulder so the shoots could straighten instead of strangle. Then let the cutters come. She didn't stop them. Three small heat-shielded hulls humming low, converging without signal. She stepped back and let them do their white-blood-cell work. Cut. It has to be cut or it bakes into one dead brick. She knew not to be sentimental about the knife.

Where a pruner lay stalled under the canopy — you know them only by the slow hump under the vine-mat — its rubbered tracks gummed with two hundred years of resin drips, she dug it out with her fingers and cleaned it with hot leaf-oil, the sharp resin stink making her water-eyes water pointlessly. She didn't reprogram it. The programs no one remembers writing are prayers now. She just oiled the track and thanked it out loud, so the felt vibrated a little with a new sound.

Where a heating duct had gone cold and the thatch of sideways roots around it were loosening, starving, she breathed on the seam until the scale glowed dull orange again, and stayed until the roots tightened.

That was all. No miracles. Maintenance, the holy form of love.

At night — if night meant anything under the sealed tea-colored lid — she climbed hand over hot hand over vine-rope to the center, to the oldest dome where walls are lignified stem hard as ironwood and spore-dust hangs in the lamplight, slow, glittering, dry as ash, never settling. She sat with her back against a boiler skin gone green-black with heat scale, feeling it thud through her spine, and she sang.

Not loud. Low, at pump-frequency. A harmony for the dull thump when the air-pumps reverse and shake black-pepper pollen loose. The whole sector shuddered, and the phosphorescent freckling split open overhead with soft pops, and for a moment her song and their sneeze were the same breath.

And because she had eyes and they did not, she lent them hers for a while.

She told them about cold. Not as a threat, as a story. She described, very quietly, what lives below the mesa where the air is thick enough to hold lungs: stone that is not warm, water that runs downhill without being told, green things that turn their faces up. She described the ocean without promising it. She described stars without bringing the sun.

Then she did the one large thing, and she did it at the edge, where it couldn't hurt.

At the rim where the vine has poured itself over in frozen cataracts, where the cold updraft cuts through, she set her thumbnail to the lid of smog and picked — not a hole, a pinhole. A needle's worth of real night. No rain can fall through that. No sun. Just a shaft of high cold dark, and in it, one star.

The spores that never settle drifted through it. The leaves nearest the rim can't use starlight to drink — it's too cold, too thin — but they turned, curious, toward a coolness that wasn't a furnace. A cold to dream against. A direction that isn't heat.

She left a bowl too. Basalt, at the rim, out past the last redstem runner. In it, each dawn that isn't dawn, the cold condenses a thimble of trace moisture from the updraft — not enough to rot the center, too far and too little, but enough that something new could try at the margin if it wants to, without being forced. An invitation, not an irrigation. The Stacks remembers pressure and grows toward it. She left a gentle pressure of cool at the very edge, and walked away from it, so it wouldn't become a leash.

In the morning that isn't morning she left. She unsealed herself beyond the guardrail and let her sweat come. She didn't look back to be worshipped as a new builder. Builders pour square steps and leave. She would not leave new square steps.

But where she had sat against the boiler, where she had stood still and let the tendril find her, the mass keeps a memory. The stems there grow a fraction thicker, curled faintly in the shape of a seated weight, a hand's breadth lifted. A warm handprint in a creature that navigates by heat on its cheeks.

It is still dry. Still eyeless, still hot-thin, still muffled to a creak and a pop and a long exhalation of a valve. Still cut, collapse, regrow, burn, regrow. She didn't save it.

She saw that it was alive, and complete, and lonely in the way only a whole world can be lonely — and she let herself be eaten a little, by heat, and left it a star to think with.
