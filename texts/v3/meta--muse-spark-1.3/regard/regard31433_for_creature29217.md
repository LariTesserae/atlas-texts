It would come the way rain comes here.

Not from above. From inside. On a clear afternoon while the hills stay sunlit, while the mist is building to your knees, to your chest. No wind. No thunder. No shadow crossing the sun. It would simply be there at the rim for a while, becoming weather, before you noticed it had arrived.

For a power that could be loud everywhere chooses, here, to be quiet. That is the first wisdom. In a place ruled by listening, you do not announce.

It would crouch. All kind powers crouch first.

It would put its hand — if it has a hand — in the warm tea-colored water and feel tadpoles mouth its skin. It would let moths settle on it. It would step only on stone. It would learn the rule before it touched the rule: move softly, drink quickly, make almost no sound.

It would listen for a long time. Longer than you can stand still. Long enough to hear what you thought was silence come apart:

*plik, plik* of leaf-drip.
The brush of moth wings past the ear.
The *tick, tick* of thumbnail frogs drumming through granite instead of air, because air is death.
The faint ring in the stones near the grey needles, like tuned glass, holding that drum and carrying it bowl-wide, below hearing.

And above, the other listening. The cold one. The hunger that crosses the sun too fast and too straight.

The Listener.

That is the being. Not the frogs, not the crabs, not the herons — they are the multitude. The being is the one who lives *on* them. Large and silent as owls are silent, but more so. No wingbeat. Only attention. It does not hate. It hungers. It was kept, or invited, when the old machines were set to wring water out of air, and it has been keeping ever since. Everything fast and low and wet lives bent around its ear.

A wise power sees both sufferings at once. The prey's fear. The predator's loneliness. And it does not pick one to save by killing the other.

It will not kill the Listener. To kill it would be loud, and the loud would linger longer than the corpse. And without it the pools would choke — algae blooming green by the hour with nothing to crop it, tadpoles going solid, snails bulldozing unchecked, the whole fast cycle slowing into rot. The hush is fear, yes. But the hush is also balance.

It will not turn off the needles. It will not lift the arch out of its pool or straighten the lean. Those grey slender wrong trees are still working, still condensing the afternoon cloud out of clear sky, still holding droplets suspended and ringing the rock. Without them this one small room dries, and the cities in every dimple go to cracked mud. A wise power does not replace mystery with its own machinery.

It will not teach the frogs to sing again. Not yet. Song is still death here.

So what does benevolence do, when doing nothing looks like neglect and doing something looks like ruin?

Almost nothing you could see. Which is everything.

First, it guards the forgetting.

The greatest danger to this pan is not the Listener. It is louder animals from outside the rim with boots and maps and hungers of a different kind. Miners for finished granite. Drainers. Recordists with parabolic dishes who want to capture the stone-drum. Tourists who want to be loud where it is quiet.

So the power sits on the paths down from the higher dry scrub and makes the place slightly unmemorable. Not invisible — wise powers rarely do invisible, it insults the locals. Just uninteresting on paper. The survey drone's eye slides off the wet sheen. The memory of the pool coordinates blurs by one ridge. The story the scrub people tell gets vaguer in the telling, kinder: *oh, it's just mud down there, midges, nothing to build on.* It lets them keep their half-truth about weather-machines, and takes the edge off curiosity.

Second, it tends, very lightly.

It does not rebuild. It tunes. With a fingertip that does not touch, it steadies the lean of the needle a fraction so it will stand another thousand wet seasons. It breathes on the suspended curtain under the arch so it holds one droplet more through the hottest hour, so transparent froglets have one more week to feed without touching the pool where they would be eaten. It widens the perfect dry circle by a hand's breadth, so one more soft white crab after molting has somewhere to harden in the hum where even predators won't step. It presses, gently, on the solution cups in the granite loaves, deepening them by a millimeter, so one more universe of ostracods survives evaporation till the 20-minute rain.

Small mercies. Below the threshold of worship. The snails will take credit. Moss will take credit. Good.

Third — and this is the heart of it — it feeds the Listener with itself.

Because the Listener is starving not for meat but for sound. It has learned: sound = food. So it waits, and all life whispers, and it grows lonelier and sharper with every withheld song. A benevolent power understands that kind of hunger from the inside.

So on the second afternoon, when mist smells of mud and green and iron and the light goes white-hot, the power stands up in the center of the pan, in full sight of sky, and makes a sound on purpose.

Not a small sound. A true sound. A low hum through the chest and into the stone, then a note, clear and sustained, human-loud, animal-loud, god-loud. For the first time in years, something in the pan is loud without apology.

The frogs freeze. The wading birds tuck white underwings. The copper procession stops mid-step and becomes stones. The moths press flatter under leaves.

And the shadow comes. Fast and straight. No wingbeat. Only attention, finally answered.

It strikes. Or tries to. It falls out of bright air onto the sound-maker — and finds nothing it can eat, nothing it can end. Only a larger listening looking back at it. A Watcher to meet the Listener.

For a moment — and the tadpoles will turn together in their pools and feel it through water — hunter and watcher hang locked like mating dragonflies, silent on the wing.

*Here I am,* the power says without ceasing to hum. *Take it if you can. I am loud and I do not die. You do not need to chase whispers.*

It lets itself be hunted and un-hunted. It lets the Listener exhaust its dive on something unkillable. And in that exhaustion, it shows it another way to listen: not for the faint *tick* of prey, but for the ring of stone, the *plik* of leaf-drip, the rain coming straight down only inside the rim. Food is not the only thing sound is for. Sound is also company.

It will not tame the Listener. Wisdom does not domesticate the wild to comfort the nervous. When it stops humming, the covenant will close again. Frogs will still throat-puff in silence. Birds will still signal with wing-flash. Crabs will still freeze when the shadow passes.

But looser, by a feather's width. The Listener, having once been full — not of meat, but of being heard back — will circle a little higher, a little slower. It will roost, the power will leave it a place to do so: a cold thin cirrus that was not there before, high and kind, where it can sit without hunger gnawing its attention to a needle. On some nights it will listen to the stone-drum of frogs and let it pass like weather, not like dinner.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do here, if it loved this place.

Not rescue. Attendance.

It would leave no door, no light, no writing, nothing human-scale — like the first makers. Only: the dry circle a hand wider, the curtain a droplet thicker, the map a little vaguer, the sky a little kinder to one lonely hunter.

Then it would go, the way rain goes. Without wind. While the hills stay sunlit.

Steam rising. Brutal clean light. Drip, drip from the great leaves. The frogs lifting their faces. The wading birds standing still to be washed.

Quiet again, but busier.
