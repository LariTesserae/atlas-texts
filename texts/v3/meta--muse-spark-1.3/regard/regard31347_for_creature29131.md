She comes down to get here, and then she keeps going down.

Not all at once. A benevolent and wise power knows how to enter a loud place quietly.

She stops at the doorway with its door still dogged open and lets her weight be taken by the water. 11, 12 degrees. She does not warm it. She lets the cool work into her joints and stays there. She hangs motionless until the silt settles.

That is the first thing she does. Nothing.

She listens until the loudness sorts itself.

The deep electric drone in the sternum. The hiss through half-closed valves. The tick-tick-tick of the loose chain. The fizz of the rust pinhole that will never close. The whale-note of the bearing when the flywheel comes around, traveling up through tile into bone. Her own - if she breathes - bubbles joining the rush upward to burst under the ceiling in that trapped silver lake.

She does not turn the machines off. She does not turn them up.

This is what wisdom looks like down here: she understands at once that rescue would be murder.

To drain the hall would kill them all in an afternoon. To clean it would starve them. To repair it to new would scour the mussel reef and the frost in the grout and the polished ring where the eel has lived so long she has worn the rust smooth with her own side. To shut it down to silent would stop the gyre that brings food to those who cannot chase it. To run it at full would tear the filament banners and scatter the babies.

So she does not fix. She tends.

She goes, slowly, without fins, without a lamp of her own, to each of the four giants standing alone in their own dim clearings, and lays a hand - if she has a hand - on the housing.

Engine One, Engine Two, Engine Three, Engine Four. Low, broad-shouldered, softened as river stones. Nameplates blank. Gauges blind.

She feels the sealed motors humming in their oil-filled housings and hums with them, just under, at the exact frequency that cancels the worst of the vibration fatigue. Not to make them live forever. Just to keep the wobble from becoming a shudder. The flywheel bearded with black mussels, once every twenty seconds, has been thrown out of true by the weight of its own livestock. She does not scrape the beard. That beard *is* the reef. She coaxes, over months, the next generation of spat to settle a little heavier on the light side. A balancing. So the wobble stays a breath and does not become a break.

She puts her ear to the singing bearing housing and cools it half a degree with her attention, so it sings and does not seize. She does not silence it. The pike uses that song to time her hunts. The crabs feel the relief valve in their legs through the tiles a half-second before the *whoomph* - she leaves that early-warning in place.

When the valve lets go with its shuddering column of bubbles and hot water racing for the grates and rattling the loose sheet metal, she rides it. She does not cap it. That blast is the weather here. It scatters the silver schools and gives the eels their sideways snap, freezes the crabs mid-step, retracts the anemones, sends the hunters cruising the perimeter at the shift change. Then, minute by minute, it re-knits. She lets it re-knit. She watches the re-knitting the way you watch a chest rise and fall.

She goes small, lamp-distance small, six or seven feet of jaundiced orange, to witness.

To the mussel beds: acre-wide click when they all clamp at once. She waits through the clamp with them, and waits through the minute-by-minute opening again. She does not hurry them.

To the grout frost: white anemones no bigger than a thumbnail, crowns working ceaselessly. She stirs, very gently, a little silt off their crowns where the sump pump's reversal has buried them too deep. Just enough to let them feed again. She leaves the learned traffic of the crab alone.

To Switch Cabinet Three: the warm flat island wiped bald by crab feet, 13 degrees instead of 12. On a cold morning the green knuckled crabs pile there, antennae waving in the transformer hum, waiting for their joints to loosen. She sits with them. She lets them climb over her feet. She keeps the transformer leaking its one degree of heat a little longer than physics says it should, because it has become a hearth. She does not make it summer. Summer would kill the cool they need to live slow and steady.

To the pipes thick as tree trunks: to the big female in Engine Two's feed pipe who will not be evicted, who withdraws only an inch when you come too close. The power comes no closer than that inch plus one. She meets eye and jawline. This is her doorway. It is honored.

To the congregation between machines: Engine One school tight and nervous, Engine Three school looser in the grate-light columns, Engine Four babies clinging to the streamers where light falls strongest. When they turn across the lamp and become a signal mirror, she flashes with them, briefly, so the message carries clean through the drone and hiss and fizz. *Pike. Pike. Scatter.* Not to save every fry. To keep the listening intact.

To the warden under the crane rail: as long as your arm, mottled to match blistered primer-gray and rust-brown, utterly still except for gills. She does not tame her. She does not feed her by hand. She drifts past three times like a pipe shadow and lets herself be dismissed as non-prey, non-threat. At noon, when for ten minutes the solid pillar drops straight down to the checkerboard and the fry gather in it like dust in a cathedral window, she holds herself out of the pillar. That light is theirs. She watches from the blue-green dark while the pike makes her dart that empties the water.

To the pensioners at the warm-water vent: the fat blind-eyed codlings with cataracts milky white from dark and sudden heat, loose scarred skin furred with sponge at the fins, hovering nose into the current, mouths opening and closing with bovine calm. When the valve blows hard enough to rattle metal, they ride it like old men in a hot bath, eyes closed. She strokes the current around them so it does not scald, so it stays always a little summer. She does not give them new eyes. They have no use for eyes here. They have found the one place that is always warm, and will never leave it.

To the fringe that makes it possible: sparse streamers of green filament algae swaying like poor banners, pale tufts in tile cracks, dark crusts of sponge in the corners. She clears, above, on the street, one grate of fallen leaves. Just one. So that for a few feet through the day the light shaft still moves across the floor. She does not bring sun. Sun would bring weed-choke and ruin. She brings barely.

And then she goes up. That is the rest of what she does, and it is most of it.

Above the water table, above the street, above everything, she does paperwork miracles. She lets brambles grow a little thicker over the grates. She lets the engineer's survey get coffee spilled on it and filed late. She puts a tiredness in the knees of the men who want to pump it dry and reclaim it, and a different dream in them at night - of something vast and slow turning once every twenty seconds, of a click acre-wide. She lets the place be forgotten a decade longer.

Not forever. Nothing down here is forever. The erosion is patient, one molecule at a time, until everything has found its final shape. The bulkhead lamps on emergency circuit will flicker and one day will die. The motors will one day fill with water and go silent. The flywheel will stop. The gyre will stop. The hall will go still and cold and the livestock will thin and change.

She will not prevent that. Benevolence is not to pickle. Wisdom is to know hospice from hospital.

When the end comes she will come down again and sit on the checkerboard - bottle-green and off-white still legible in patches under the gray ooze - and keep company while the last heat leaks out of Switch Cabinet Three.

Until then: cool and dim and loud and close. Iron and tile and water. Very old, very small, very full of life that does not need you, running on in the dark below the world.

She leaves it running on.
