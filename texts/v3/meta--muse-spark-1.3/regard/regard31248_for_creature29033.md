It would come close enough to notice — which is already almost impossible — and then it would stop.

Not out of coldness. Out of attention.

For a long time it would just kneel there, in the thin cold air, with its face near the fist-mouth, and feel the dry lift on its face, and listen to the roar-then-ticking, roar-then-ticking. It would learn how loud a helmet-sized room can be when it is full.

Its first heart-impulse, being benevolent, would be to help.

To widen the mouth so they could breathe easier. To drip a little water in so they would not thirst. To let in a shaft of light so they could see. To cool the wall where it bakes too hot. To stop them from chewing each other's legs when the wind brings nothing.

And its wisdom, which is older than its kindness, would put a hand on its own wrist and say: *don't.*

Because it would understand, very quickly, the two rules that make them what they are.

No water to spare. And nothing made.

Give them water and their lacquer would soften and split and they would rot where they lie. Give them light and eyes would be a cruelty in a room you cannot turn your head in. Give them space and they would cease to be the conveyor that carries heat and food from wall to wall by crawling over each other. Give them mercy from being eaten and the whole economy of fallout and chemistry and patience would stall. Give them a structure, even a kind one — a pipe, a wire, a cairn to mark them — and they would no longer be untouched.

They are not broken. They do not need fixing. They are complete without plant, without light, without water, without permission.

So it would do what almost no power can do: it would restrain itself.

It would not name them. It would not count thousands and write a number down. It would not take one for study, or crush three others in the taking. It would not ask to be thanked, or worshipped, or even noticed. An antenna brushing basalt already sparks blue; a god brushing that room would be a wildfire.

What it would do is smaller, and therefore harder:

It would veil.

It would keep the ridge uninteresting on maps. Let the wind keep its dust over any footprint. Turn the boot aside a hundred meters below without the foot ever knowing why it felt like turning. Let no trail learn to lead there. Let no hut, no cairn, no pipe, no wire ever find a reason to be built. Let the rock stay too new, too sharp, too useless to use.

It would tend, without touching, the things they already depend on. Keep the daytime thermals rising from the far valleys below, so the soft rain of wind-killed midges keeps falling dead down the mouth onto hunger. Keep the deep heat breathing up on its long patient gradient, so the pile can shiver and resettle like grain on a sieve. Let the sulfur films keep fixing themselves on glass. Let a pinhead crystal grow in its cool corner over a season, undisturbed.

And when the stretch comes when the wind brings nothing — and they begin, ripple by ripple, to take a leg here, an antenna there — it would not look away, and it would not intervene. It would stay. That is the benevolence: to witness the eating without flinching, to understand that to live shoulder to shoulder is to be food, and to hold that too as holy.

It would not stop the basalt from cooling. A millimeter a decade the bubble tightens. In a lifetime the ceiling lowers by a leg-joint. One day the mountain will shift and pinch it shut, or crack it open to the cold, and the furnace will go out, and ten thousand years of wind and ice will do what they do. It would grieve that, the way you grieve a breath ending, and it would not lift a finger to prevent it. To be wise is to know that pre-formation is not a mistake to be preserved forever. It is a breath, exhaled and frozen mid-breath, and breaths are meant to finish.

For now, though, while it holds —

It would put its hand over the mouth, just as you did, and take away the last red thread, and sit with them in absolute black. Hot dust on the lips. Hot mineral reek. The deafening dry industrious shudder through rock. Hundreds of minute feet. The faint crackle in the hairs of the forearm where so much chitin rubs thin hot air electric.

It would let them crawl over each other in the dark, lacquered and blind and perfectly adapted, mites riding in the folds, springtails flicking in the gaps, nymphs darkening as they bake, waxy eggs waiting where heat is steadiest.

It would think, not in words they could hear — for words would be a roar — but in the tremor-language they read through their feet:

*I see you. You are wallpaper and floor and ceiling hung down. You are production without a factory. You are alive where nothing green has ever been. Continue.*

And then it would lift its hand, let the faint red come back where the cracks go down, and go away, leaving no damp stain, no broken edge, no sign it had ever knelt there at all.

Except that the world continued.
