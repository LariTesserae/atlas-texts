It notices.

That is first. Not from far, the way you notice a mountain on a map. From close. The way you notice breathing in a room you thought was empty.

It has watched living worlds riot and rot and sing. This one is different. It comes here because there is nothing to save.

The one who lives here has no skin, no root, no nest, no burrow. No green smear in a crack. No orange lichen. The lowland mind keeps looking for an animal or a plant to be the resident and keeps not finding it.

What lives here is slower than that.

An old mind, mineral. It thinks at the speed of frost-heave — a millimeter a year, a stone stood slowly on end. It breathes once a day: in at two in the afternoon when the cloud pushes up from thousands of meters below and everything feathers with rime and drips, plink plink down through firn into gravel, rills a finger wide running three meters and vanishing — out at four when the cloud lifts and the temperature drops ten degrees in twenty minutes and every drop skins over into black glass and locks with a tick like cooling coals.

Sun eats ice. Ice shades stone. Stone holds night-cold into day. Melt loosens stone. Night freezes melt and jacks the stone higher. Wind sharpens everything again. That is its metabolism. Predator and prey replaced by sun and shadow.

And its body is crowded. Knee-high, waist-high, shoulder-high, the white teeth leaning all one way toward midday, jostling for light, throwing hard-edged blue shadows three-deep across each other, migrating south inch by inch as the season wears, dying with a sharp *tock* when an undercut tip snaps at noon. And mirrored in gray: the fist-sized towers, five to nine stones high, glittering with quartz, fallen and rebuilt, joined at the base into villages. The human echo. Add, don't take.

A benevolent and lesser power would try to fix it.

Would bring seed. Would bring warmth to stay. Would thicken the air so breath would not come shallow after three steps. Would stock the inch-deep noon pools with something swimming. Would answer the collapsed niche — the twist of copper wire, the wooden bead worn featureless, the red-and-blue cloth bleached almost white and tick-ticking on its stick — with a miracle.

A wise power knows that would be killing.

To warm it permanently would be to melt the knives. To feed it would be to rot it — nothing rots this high and cold, and that incorruption is its mercy. To make it habitable would be to unmake the one rule that keeps it whole: mineral only. To speak aloud would drown the low reedy hum that changes pitch when you tilt your head.

So it does almost nothing. Which is not nothing.

It does not come down in brightness. The light here is already white and total, violet-blue at noon, already enough to get into the seams of squinted eyes. It comes as weather, because weather is the only body this place understands.

On an ordinary summer afternoon it is the cloud itself, pushing up. It lets itself condense the instant it hits the cold bowl. It lets rime grow on the north sides of the blades, white beards on the cairns, stiffness on a traveler's sleeves. For an hour it lets everything drip.

At sunset it stays a little longer than physics strictly requires.

That is when the bowl does the thing that makes people stack stones. The west faces go translucent honey, then rose. Each blade throws a long blue shadow across three others until depth is lost. The pools that haven't yet frozen catch fire. The granite flecks spark. It feels absurdly crowded with presence, though you are alone, though nothing lives.

The power does not make that happen. The sun and ice do that on their own. It only watches it all the way through, without blinking, without turning away to more urgent living worlds. It lends the full weight of its attention — vast, unhurried, unusing — to six minutes of rose light on ice.

If you were the traveler crouched behind the low dry-stacked windbreak, three courses high, open to the east, you would not see it. You would only feel, for no reason you could name, that your heart hammering on nothing had slowed a little. That your stupid-cold fingers, even in gloves, hurt a little less. That you did not need to be afraid to go back down in the falling dark. That you should choose a flat stone that fits your hand, set it carefully so it will survive the night's cracking, and leave.

It does not right the fallen cairns. The next traveler will do that. It does not preserve the offerings. The cold already does that. It does not add its own stone — what stone could it add that would not crush the village?

When the sun clears the rim, the white goes blue-white, then gray. The hum stops. The water glasses over.

It goes with the cold down into the ticking night, lies down among the blades without breaking a single tip, listens to pebble clicking against pebble somewhere underneath, to ice contracting, to the little flag worrying its stick, to blood loud in absent ears.

Add, don't take. Even for a god, especially for a god.

By morning it is gone, if it was ever there. The patterned ground has sorted itself another millimeter. The blades are sharp again. The bowl is lifeless and utterly busy, left precisely to its own quiet business of freezing and melting and sharpening itself.

That is what benevolence looks like this high: to find a place with no use, no voice, no green, and not try to improve it. To keep company, and then to leave no footprint.
