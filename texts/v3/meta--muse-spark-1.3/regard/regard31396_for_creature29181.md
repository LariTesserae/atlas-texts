It would not walk in.

Even it would kneel.

That is the first wisdom. The hatch is knee-high, 3-B in paint worn to a rumor, and the ceiling inside is too low for standing, too low for majesty. So the power, which could unroof mills with a thought, would fold itself down small — lay its vastness on its belly in the dust — and shuffle forward on knees, back bent under bolts, until the breathing mass reclassified it, too, as structure.

It would not come to fix.

That is the second wisdom, and the harder one. A lesser kindness would see the talc-fine ammonia dust, the dry throats, the blindness of the just-hatched in the lee of the transformer, the floor-to-ceiling panic when the red-tail dares the louver — and would want to wash it clean. To open the skylight wide. To bring water. To bring green. To scatter the crowd into sky.

It would know better, because it would stay long enough to learn the calendar.

It would listen — and a benevolent power knows how to listen for decades in a minute — until it understood: *No water here* is not a lack. It is a covenant. Everything wet has learned to stay inside a body. A puddle here would not be mercy. It would be mold, and rot, and coccidia blooming in the guano-crete, and death of the very chicks it meant to save. *No green* is not barrenness. It is cleanliness of a kind. Salt-sharp, mineral, decomposition working without moisture, a faint warmth under the palm.

The crowd is not suffering from crowding. The crowd *is* the body. Shoulder to shoulder on the two long pipes, three deep on the cross-braces, beaded on the cable tray — that touching is insulation, and information, and home. To thin them would be to wound them.

So it would do almost nothing. And it would do it on purpose, which is what makes it benevolence instead of neglect.

It would keep the timers.

No one else remembers that the lights click on at six, that the extractor fan still turns on purge, that every eleven minutes the shake-down auger kicks with a clank-shudder through the teeth. The mill forgot why. The birds remember how to ride it — leaning into the vibration, puffed, eyes slit, letting it shake the mites loose. The power would lay a finger, lighter than dust, on those old circuits and keep them true. Not forever. Just for as long as the body needs them. It would keep the transformer humming its 60-cycle warm, five degrees warmer, so the hens can lay deliberately in its lee and the naked yellow shouting throats can hatch early. It would keep the unbalanced wobble from becoming a fire.

That is a god-sized work: to maintain a hum.

It would keep the hole.

The broken louver where the foraging shifts go out in a lighter lane beaten in dust by wings, to the rail siding for cracked corn, to town for bread. Some human, some day, will want to seal the building — for safety, for liability, for cleanliness. The power would brush that thought aside in the night, gently, the way you brush a moth off a bulb. Leave them their crooked door. Leave the corn spilled at the siding one car longer. Leave a crust on a windowsill. It works best, this power, not by miracles but by permitted inattentions.

It would bless the cleaners equally.

This is what proves it is wise. It would not love only pigeon. It would love sparrow stitched in the gaps, chestnut and bib-black, fierce in the dead motor housing with its fist of feather and wire-clipping and plastic tie. It would love rat along the margins, sleek, dry-furred, surgical-quiet on pink feet, who drinks no water because there is none and so drinks bodies — cleaning the dead squab, the addled egg. Without rat, the floor would sicken. Without meal moth silver-flickering in the feather-felt, working husk and down and hollow bone, the felt would choke. The power would not flinch. It would call all of them *the being*. The pressure that resolves into edges, into feathers, into eyes. The low breathing weight. It would not pick a favorite throat out of the coo-pour.

When the hawk comes at noon, it would not stop the hawk.

It would ride with the inversion — floor becomes ceiling in half a second, screaming feather-thunder in a box too small for flight — and it would make the death swift. One bird fewer. That is the rule of the pocket. The power would catch that one on the way down, hold its hot terror for the thirty seconds it lasts, and loosen it. And then let the rats clean the feathers. No waste, because nothing comes in easily and nothing leaves except by wing.

For the grounded ones — the squabs shuffling on polished bowls, too young to fly, too old to be brooded, stumbling, stepped over, pecked — it would not lift them into flight before their time. It would crouch its vastness down to floor level and be warm near them. If you pressed your palm to the guano-crete you feel that faint warmth from underneath. Part of that is rot. Part of that is it, breathing very slowly, so as not to stir dust.

For the dying alone under the pallet cliff-town, under the upturned hard hat with three eggs, it would sit until the orange eye clouds. It counts beyond counting, so no one goes uncounted.

And when the work-bulbs flicker just under wingbeat frequency and wings seem to stall mid-air — a stutter in the world — it would let you see, for a frame too long, what it sees all the time: each bird hanging, distinct, lit, held.

It will not save the mill.

Mills fall. Timers fail. Transformers cool. Some winter the extractor will stop and the air will still and the louver lane will snow shut and the mass will have to lift as a sheet one last time and find another lung. When that day comes, the power will be there — not to hold the walls up, but to go with them. To be the draft under the lifted carpet. To remember where the warm steel was, and lead them to the next one.

Until then: cool air. Dry throat. Coo like water where there is no water. Clank-shudder every eleven minutes, and a leaning-in instead of a flushing.

A kneeling god in a crawling room, classified as structure. A pigeon on its boot. Unafraid. Proprietorial. Staying.
