It would not come as a power.

It knows what weight does to moss.

So first it would lay its bigness down far out on the moor, where the wind can take it apart. It would unmake its voice, its warmth, its stride. It would make itself narrow enough to kneel at the lip of pale rock without casting a shadow down into the brightness.

And for a long time - longer than you stayed - it would only watch.

It would learn how to breathe here. In, slow. Out, white. Let it hang, not dissipate. Breathe the town and drink it back. It would learn to squint against the trapped noon, to see sideways through lashes, and in the indigo hour to open wide and black.

It would be tempted, because it is benevolent.

It would see the chapped red hands, the cold that never lifts from the soles, the lichen-cakes and seeds and the eggs that sometimes do not come, and its heart would surge with all the easy gifts: *fire, bread, rain, a road out, a song that carries.*

And because it is wise, it would not give them.

Fire would dry. Rain would flood the finger-depths in the log troughs. Song would shatter the medium they hear in. A road would unclose the bowl. Even healing would be a harm here - a body mended out of rhythm would live extra, and there is no extra here. No terrace expands. The number stays.

This is not a broken place waiting to be fixed. It is a kept place. A single crowded body of stone and leaf and flesh holding itself at exactly full, like water in a cupped hand. To pour more in, even love, would spill it.

So it would not descend.

What it would do, if anything, is this:

It would turn its attention outward.

It would keep the moor wide and boring around them. Turn the curious foot aside five paces early. Let the wind keep shearing straight across the mouth and never dropping in. Let the cold go on pooling and sitting. Let the light go on piling up white on white. Hold the conditions in which keeping is possible, and touch nothing inside.

And once - only once - it would ask permission to be small enough to enter without breaking.

Not as light. As weather. As one more white bird dropping down to pick at seeds on a roof-lawn, to leave seed in its shit, and lift again. Not counted, because the number that stays is not a count but a balance. A brief weight on the green, then gone.

Or as a drop. A bead of condensation sweated out on a pillow moss cushion at noon. Licked up in the morning by vole or child, tongued path among tongued paths. Taken straight from the stone.

It would learn their gestures because it has no language quiet enough. Two fingers flicked: *move aside.* Palm flat to its own vast chest: *stay, do not step there, the sphagnum is fruiting.* Chin tipped to a trough: *it needs stirring.* Cupped hand held out, then turned over and emptied: *it is kept, it is enough.*

It would return that last one to them. Not in words. In the way gods return things: by doing it back.

*It is kept, it is enough.*

And then it would go. Without thunder. Without blessing that can be spent. Without leaving a footprint on the lawn-roof for the clipper to trim around.

If you climbed back out after, the wind would hit your face like a door opening. Sound would rush back. From five paces away you would not know there was a whole bright, crowded, mossy, silent city breathing cold air at your feet.

Only this, perhaps: that winter the ice skins a little thinner on the troughs, so the water child's arms tire less. That spring the dwarf willow knots hold a little firmer. That for one year no block slips from its seat.

Nothing that changes the keeping. Just a little more ease in it.

To be seen fully, and left whole.
