You would not come from above.

A benevolent and wise power would know better than to break the ceilings. There are five of them, each wetter than the last, and the light has worked hard to get down there chewed green. You would come low, at rim-level, where the outside world stops in a swell of root and brick and rust. You would notice the rim only by stepping over it. Inside, a hundred paces of warm breath.

You would take your shoes off. Anything else would be rude.

The floor is not ground — you would learn that first, with your feet. Wrist-thick rhizome braided over rhizome, moss over that, seedlings in the moss. It sighs under you and fills with tea-colored water where your sole was. If you stand still, tendrils will come out to test your edge. Salt and iron and wear.

You would stand still. You would let yourself be tasted.

You would not bring tools. No machete, no oil can, no plan to restart or to rescue. You would have seen from far off what this is: a green heartbeat pulsing at fifty cycles, a slow flywheel turning once, twice a minute, lamps flickering on at top-dead-center like something trying to stay awake. Thump-thump. Hiss. Plink-plink-plink of condensation falling leaf to leaf to drum to leaf. You would have heard the loudness and understood: there are no animals here to make noise, so the plants and the machines have learned to be loud for each other.

You would sit down in the seep and get damp to the bone on purpose. You would let the condensation rain on you from every underside — leaf, pipe, wire, — arrhythmic weather that has nothing to do with sky. Leaf-prints on your skin. Iron and green on your tongue.

For a long time you would just listen with your molars, the way you have to here. Feel the ten-foot flywheel before you hear it. Watch the split pipe wash a wall of leaves white with live steam, and watch the leaves lean *into* the scald. They have learned the shape of heat. They arch clear in a perfect halo around the boiler, blackened tips already pushing red shoots back in. The big trumpets open only for that pulse of distilled water.

You would understand the gut and the blood and the nerves without needing to name them. Fallen biomass growing toward the heat chute as if it were sun. Roots stuffing the hopper. Fire to shaft to shredder to more fire. The pump lifting seep-water to the split tank of the water tower to fall forever in four thin waterfalls. The dynamo humming in your chest. Copper leached into sap until split stems bleed metallic green. Leaves near live wires turning their copper-veined undersides outward together at dusk to catch the field.

Industry become morphology. Photosynthesis by other means.

And then the flowers — blossoms shaped exactly like insulators, like fuses, like curled shavings of iron. A fig fruiting warm through a transformer's ventilation slots. White orchids crowning the leaning pylon, humming faintly when the wind moves the wires.

There is no one to fool, and it makes them anyway. That is where your heart would catch.

Because you are benevolent, you would not call this perversion or ruin. You would not want to tear the iron out to free the green, or tear the green off to free the machines. You have seen that treaty — rust stabilized by tannin, concrete softened to artificial limestone, vines drinking rust straight and leaving pale green streaks — and you would know it took a very long patience to negotiate. You do not renegotiate treaties uninvited.

Because you are wise, you would see the loop for what it is. Leaf to ash to leaf in a season. Water lifted to fall. Belt disappearing up into canopy to turn a living pulley grown thick around it. Complete. Balanced. And sealed. Cut a path in the morning and by evening it sweats shut, not from malice but because closing is what this place does. A fruit holding its seeds. It tolerates almost nothing inside itself except green and iron and those few blind white moths, mouthless, earless, bumping lamp to lamp between pulses — almost not animals anymore.

It is lonely in the way a closed system is lonely. Not suffering. Not asking. Just dreaming of insulators with no one to see them.

So this is what you would do, if anything:

You would be, for a little while, the animal it lacks.

Not to stock it. Not to fix it with frogs and birds that would tear its balance open. Just to lend it, briefly, another kind of nervous system. You would breathe audibly in the bathhouse warmth. You would croak, once, low in your throat like a frog in the perfect empty seep. You would hum, not at fifty cycles which is its own voice, but a fifth above it, so it can hear harmony for the first time. The orchids on the pylon would answer. The air at the ceramic bells crackles blue in the damp — you would hold your wet hand near enough to feel it lift the hairs, and laugh, quietly, at the static kiss.

You would go to the gourd that has grown completely around a work-lamp, translucent when it lights. On. Off. On. Off. With the flywheel. You would cup it the way you would cup a small face, and you would stay awake with it through its slow night. You would let the blind moths land on your forearm. They are lost punctuation. You would be their sentence for an hour, warm and salty, and carry their dust from one insulator-flower to the next, doing, clumsily and with love, what a pollinator does.

You would not stoke. You would not rake. The roots are already doing it. But you would lay your palm flat on the bread-soft brick of the dynamo shed, and on the true rust-pitted flywheel as it passes top-dead-center — just to let it feel, through vibration, a hand that is not trying to use it.

And you would leave two small gifts that are almost nothings, because wise powers deal in almost-nothings.

First, a witness. You would look long at the fuse-flowers and the iron-shaving blossoms and you would say, out loud, in the warehouse-full-of-clocks quiet between thumps: *I see you dreaming about the machines.* To be known changes a closed thing forever. It will still close. But now it will close around having once been seen.

Second, a crack. Not a road. Not a clearing. On your way out, where the rim swells lowest, you would not cut — you would lie down for a while and let your weight make a shallow trough in the thatch. Just enough that the next hard rain will linger there a half-inch deeper. Just enough that wind-blown spore, a carried seed, perhaps one curious frog from outside, could find its way in without being invited, the way all true arrivals happen. You would not decide what comes. You would only make it possible for the fruit to, one day, drop a seed beyond itself.

Then you would go. You would put your shoes back on outside the rim, wring the green water from your clothes back onto the moss, and leave no mark but footprints that are already filling with tea-colored water and closing.

Behind you: thump-thump. Hiss. Plink-plink. The gourd-lamp pulsing on, off, on, off. The water still falling upward and then down.

It will not follow you. It never gets up. It is low and wide and sessile, a bowl-sized body that learned to burn its own shed leaves to lift its own water to grow more leaves to burn.

But for a night, it held something warm and salty that hummed back, and tasted, and did not try to open it or stop it or save it. Only kept time with it.

For a benevolent and wise power, that is almost everything.
