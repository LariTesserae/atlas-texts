It would stop.

That is the first thing a wise and benevolent power would have to learn how to do again, up there on the rim with its lungs burning thin. For a power is used to arriving. To pouring. To fixing.

It would stop, and look, and listen until it heard the hum.

From above it would look like nothing. A molar of old limestone. A cut coin of sky. Green in a place that by every rule should be grey. But if it stayed long enough - and it can stay - it would hear what you heard: that low roar rising out of solid rock, wings and bells and grinding stones and low voices coming back off curved walls. A hive in a jar.

It would bend closer. As close as fog.

And it would be thirsty within the hour.

---

For it has made oceans before, this power. It knows how to split rock for springs, how to call rain twice a week instead of twice a year. Its hands would itch with the obvious mercies.

*Give them water.*

One clean seep in the wall, that is all. A pool that puddles. A cup, not a mouthful. Let the cracked lips heal. Let the babies cry wet. Let the old blind woman who twists dew-cloth by feel drink deep once before she dies.

It could. Easily.

And being wise, it would see what would happen next, as clearly as if it had already happened a thousand times in a thousand other bowls on a thousand other mountains:

They would drink. Then they would sow more millet in the new damp. Then bear a fourth child, a fifth, who would live. Who would need more millet. More goats to prune more leaves. The goats would eat the lower taro. The people would cut a living limb when deadfall ran short, just once, forgiven, then again. The guano loop would foul with too much urine. The thatch-mites would bloom in the damp. The figs would rot instead of dry. The bats would leave. The swifts would leave. The bowl that held heat would breed fever.

In ten years the village would be twice as large and half as alive. In thirty, empty. Dust and split terraces and a dark stain where a spring had been, sucked back down through cracks too deep to follow.

Wisdom is seeing the second thirst behind the first.

So it would close its pouring hand.

---

What then? To see and not save - is that benevolence?

It would learn to be smaller.

Not a light in the sky - the coin of sky is watched like a well, it must not be blocked. Not a voice - sound does not escape, a shout would triple. Not a new tool, not a copper vein, not a cure for the dry cough. There is nowhere to put a miracle in here without moving a pigeon loft, a storage pit, a shrine of stacked bone.

No. It would come in the only size the bowl allows: tenant-size.

It would sit, for a while, as heat in the stone. That is already its work - the stone gives back the day all night. It would make sure the stone remembers how. It would lie around the lip at night like a low rim of attention, just high enough that the wind passes overhead and does not dip in, just low enough that the cloud-sea can still rise silent and spill over. It would not make the fog - the sea makes the fog - but it would keep the path open for it. It would listen for the crack that whistles half an hour before, head cocked like a hyrax, so that when the old women spread their mats exactly where their grandmothers spread them, the beading comes.

In the hour of straight sun it would be a little shade. Not a cloud - that would frighten. Just the bounced light a fraction softer on the pale rock, so the large dark pupils do not have to squint quite so hard.

At dusk, when the swifts fold into the pocks and the whole village lowers its voice without being told, it would lower its own. *Do not shout when the bats are navigating.* It can obey. It would hold very still while the fruit bats pour out of the deep cracks, thudding close over the thatch to work the figs. It would not touch a drying fig. It would let them feed. Dung for figs, figs for bats, bats for guano, guano for millet.

It would walk the tunnel-alleys shoulder to shoulder, chest to back, and not apologize. It would learn to carry something every trip, never empty-handed, lest it waste a trip. A bundle of deadfall. A wrung dew-cloth folded like a baby. A toddler to lift over the goat that is always in the way.

Mostly, it would pay attention the way attention is prayer here:

To one boy, lean and fearless on the wall-pocks, scraping guano with a grass brush while the ammonia bites his throat - it would keep his bare foot steady on the lichen, one millimeter at a time.

To one quern-woman, wrist swaying side to side, teeth flat-milled by stone-grit - it would stay in the rhythm of her grinding song so she does not feel alone in it.

To one blind great-grandmother judging last night's fog by the weight of a cloth - *thin, very thin, drink sparingly today* - it would rest, briefly, in her perfect hands, and let itself be read like damp.

To one mason of eighty, fitting dry stone around a fig root that will split his work in two hundred years - it would not hurry him. It would admire the way he builds around it anyway. Then, eventually, out of it.

When they carry their dead up the stair-cut in the rim and lay them on the outer scree for the sky, it would go with them. Up matters most there. It would sit with the body in the cold falling vastness until the lammergeiers come gliding out of nothing. It would not turn its face away. It would wait until bone is clean, and come back down with the bone for the shrines. Nothing leaves the loop, not even people. It would consent to the loop.

And it would keep one larger watch, out beyond the mountain, because benevolence is not only tenderness but boundary.

When lowland surveyors look up and see a green anomaly and think *mine, reservoir, resort, mission*, it would be a little forgetfulness in their instruments. A slipped foothold on the scree. A sudden tenderness for home that turns them back. When a helicopter thrums toward the coin of sky, it would be a crosswind. When a story about the bowl wants to spread, it would let it sound too small, too dry, too dim to bother with.

Let the world stay vast and falling and cold below. Let there be nothing up here but this one small, crowded, green, dry, dim, warm cup holding on against the sky.

That is not unkindness to the world. It is kindness to the bowl.

---

You ask what they would do, if anything.

Anything, but almost nothing. And almost nothing, done for a hundred generations without wavering, is everything.

It would not save them. They do not need saving. They need the fog to keep coming, the stone to keep its heat, the bats to keep their corridors of dark, the outsiders to keep missing the turn.

It would thirst with them. It would hum with them. At night, when the bowl fills to the brim and every leaf beads and the thatch ticks and nothing puddles and nothing is wasted, it would lie in borrowed warmth among borrowed goats and borrowed children and understand what you understood, lying there:

They are not trapped here.

They are kept.

And if it is truly benevolent, and truly wise, it will let itself - vast as it is - be kept by them for a while, too. Taught again the first rule, which it had forgotten among its oceans:

Do not waste a wring of cloth.
