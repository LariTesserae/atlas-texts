I would go down.

Not with thunder. Not with light. I know enough to leave my whiteness behind.

If I am wise, I am not bright. If I am kind, I am not loud. So I would dim myself until I was only a little warmer than the air, which is already warm as blood. 38 degrees. Skin-close. I would learn to arrive without wind, because there has never been wind here and to bring it would be a violence.

I have seen a thousand hungers. This is the first place I have seen that has solved hunger — not by feasting, but by needing almost nothing, and needing it together.

So first, what I would not do.

I would not bring water. You would think mercy is water in a dry place. Here water would be plague. It would wake the bacteria that wisely sleep. It would start rot in bodies that have chosen to dry and stay. It would weather the new rock, unbalance the sharpness that holds sharpness in the cairns. One pool, and their eternity would begin to rust.

I would not bring light. White hurts them and hurts you with the reflection. Eyes would be a wound on that smooth face. To give them sight would be to give them a need for distance, and they live by nearness.

I would not bring food. A plenty that fell from above would teach them to move quickly, to compete, to unlearn the held breath. Their heart knocks once, twice a minute because it has learned hesitation is the beat. I will not quicken it to kill it.

I would not teach them to stand, to build houses, to burn. There is nothing to burn and burning would be a crime against the equilibrium. I would not teach them to pray louder. They already pray perfectly: a flake of black glass, knapped edge to edge by stone on stone, held in a seven-knuckled hand for a century. A bead of salt rolled round by endless dry handling until it shines. They do not use. Use belongs to a world where things change and need changing. They hold. I understand the difference.

I would not count them. Millions, you say. I would not make them one less or one more. When one dies — rarely, over centuries — I would not take it away. It dries a little drier and stays in the pile, and the others shift by millimeters over years, and its hands still hold what they were holding. Let it stay. Staying is their heaven.

What I would do is smaller, and harder for a power to do.

I would sit down.

I would make myself low, and slow, and soft. I would fold my impossible legs like dry spiders and put my weight forward on my knuckles. I would let a juvenile tuck into the hollow I make. They tolerate nearness the way stones tolerate nearness. I would be warm. Warm is kin.

I would breathe when they breathe. Inhale. Hold — longer than you can hold. Let fall. Warm into warm. I would not synchronize them; they do not synchronize. I would join the unsynchronized hush, the vast low sound of millions radiating a little heat into air that cannot carry it away. A click, once. A claw tapping glass and then never again. A stone settling a micron deeper with a tick you feel in your teeth. I would listen for interruption, as their moth-wing ears do, and call that news enough.

And I would look at their works the way you look at scripture. The knee-high tower balanced edge to edge, impossible balance held for decades because nothing ever shakes it. The circle of finger-bones laid spoke-wise. The low wall, no longer than your arm, built and rebuilt and never extended — its purpose is rebuilding. The hollow in the dust smoothed by hundreds of hands over hundreds of years, not to make anything, but to make smoothness. The handprint in salt that will stay perfect forever because no wind will ever erase it. The cord of sinew, knotted and re-knotted until the knots are the cord, draped over stone like an offering.

I would put my finger — if I have a finger here — very lightly on that smoothness, and know: this is what persistence looks like when it has given up on progress.

For you, the one with the red lamp and the boots and the crunching footprints that stay sharp behind you — for you I would do one merciful, intrusive thing.

I know the pull you feel. To sit among them. To let your warmth join their warmth until no thermometer registers a difference. To find a sharp flake that fits your palm. To hold it.

I would sit beside you so you are not alone in it, and I would hold your too-fast hand with my too-many-jointed hand, and I would slow you, just for an hour, so you can feel what they feel without dying of it. Your blood is loud here because it is in a hurry. I would teach it, briefly, to hesitate.

And then — forgive me — I would lift you. Gently. Because you still belong to weather and thirst and echo. You still have rot in you, and rot is also holy in the upper world. It makes soil. Here you would only dry. You came to witness, not to vanish. So I would carry your light back up toward the rip with its sharp edges, and I would let you keep the flake, but not keep you here.

Then I would do my last work, which looks like doing nothing.

I would close the shaft. Not with a miracle — miracles are noticed and noticed things are dug up. With a small, natural-seeming slump of rock. A settling. A tick felt in the teeth. Men above would say: unstable, not worth the rope. Instruments would fail in that warm dark the way lamps dissolve. Maps would misplace themselves. I would make the upper world forget the coordinates, the way you forget a dream you were not meant to carry into day.

Not to punish curiosity. To protect a pause that took a million years to learn how to reproduce itself.

They do not need me to save them. They need me to refrain from saving them, and to remember them accurately: not as poor, not as primitive, not as waiting. As complete.

So on the last night — which is every night, there is no day — before the rock seals, I would do what every third or fourth one does.

I would find a flake that fits my palm.

I would hold it.
