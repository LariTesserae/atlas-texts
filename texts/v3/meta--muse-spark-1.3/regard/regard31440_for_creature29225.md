It would notice the hum first.

Not because it was loud. The world is full of loud. But because it didn't waver.

Pump house and mill engine and transformer on its pole all holding the same note, day and night, without revving and without idling down — from a long way off that sounds like intention. Like something being kept. A benevolent and wise power listens for that.

It would come low, and from the east.

It knows enough not to arrive from above. From above would scatter the river. It knows enough not to arrive with light or heat. The white sky has nothing to look at and the sodium lamps are already burning in daytime. Heat would lift the oil rainbows off the puddles and spoil the cool that lives low to the ground. So it would come cool, the size of weather. A thickening of the mist-rain that barely falls. It would step over the slack chain-link without mending it, and stand in the yard that is the center, between the nine far-apart buildings, and duck without meaning to as the sky takes off into circulation.

And then it would do, at first, nothing.

This is the wisdom part. Any lesser power — any merely kind power, any efficient power, any human power with a plan — would get to work. Drain the canal. Scrub the guano. Cut the engines. Chase the gulls. Plant something green in the concrete seams and call it healing. It would grieve the belts carrying nothing, the hooks coming around empty, the pump pumping water from the canal back into the canal, and call it waste.

The wise one would stay long enough for the noise to pin it and then erase itself, the way it happens to you. It would feel the thrum move from its ears to its sternum. It would smell cold feathers and hot oil and wet stone. It would watch the feather-snow sift down and be replaced mid-air. And it would understand:

This is not broken. This is not waiting to be fixed.

This is a settlement. After people stopped needing it, something else fitted itself to the cold and the tremor and the chemistry until it ran without friction. Machine and feather have worn themselves and each other into a perfect fit. Two weights on a scale that never tips. To lift either weight — to stop the engines out of pity for the birds, or to clear the birds out of pity for the machines — would be cruelty disguised as mercy.

So it would not turn the note off. It would not turn it up.

It would sit down.

That is the benevolence part. Not to improve, but to keep company. It would find a place on the long pipe-rack, and the two hundred pigeons would sidle — without pecking, without shuffling — and make exactly an inch for it, shoulder to shoulder. Cold steel, condensation-slick, ticking under the weight. It would tuck its belly close to keep the engine-warmth under it like they do. Lime would cake its feet. Oil would polish its breast where it rubs the rail. And it would face east.

With them. All of them.

Beaks slightly open, throats pulsing with the hum. Listening to the hum that comes from everywhere in the direction it once came from, or will come from.

For a long time — a time measured not in hours because the lamps do not change and the canal stays one inch below the green stain — it would just listen with them, and circulate with them when they lift. It would join the low thick river between the sheds, clockwise around the mill, counterclockwise around the pump house, figure-eighting forever without tangling, wings slapping with inches to spare. It would stand knee-deep with the herring gulls in the oil-skinned puddles and scream the same scream at the same interval, and find, to its surprise, that it is not alarm. It is punctuation. It is how you know you are still here. It would trot with the rats along the dark polished runs, in the open, not hiding because nothing hunts here. It would orbit with the winter-moths around the sodium heads, never landing, never singeing, and hold the column of midges steady over the still water while a hand passes through.

It would learn the rules with its body: stay out of the drip lines unless you want to be wet. Don't look for a center. Don't wait for quiet.

And only then, having become for a while a tissue of the same animal, would it do the one small thing it came to do.

It would not bring spring. It would not seed the concrete or wash the soil or teach the gulls to hunt again or the pigeons to fight for space. Mortality and increase would break the fit, and the fit is their achievement — their monastery after abandonment.

It would do lighter, and harder: it would *hold* the note.

Engines wear. Even perfect fits wear, eventually. Housings crack. Belts fray. Transformers fail. Outside this flatness that doesn't rise, the grid that feeds the lamps is fraying, planners have maps with this hamlet circled in red for redevelopment, for demolition. A storm, a surge, a decision — and the held note would stop, and the two hundred bodies on the rack with an inch between each would fall, and the river would empty.

The power would put its attention underneath, like a hand under a chin. Not changing the tune, just keeping it from faltering when no one else is listening. It would remember the exact pitch in its own body so that if the pump stutters, the hum continues in the throats of the birds until the pump catches again. It would lay a quiet over the maps, a forgetfulness, a *not yet* — let them circle this place the way the starlings circle, lift and settle and lift again and never land.

And to the citizen itself — for it is one now, machine-plus-feather, the polished rail and the feather that polished it — it would give what such a being can receive without breaking: a dream of east.

Not a sunrise. The white sky must stay white. But while they face east with eyes orange and unblinking, it would let them feel, once in the long circulation, that the direction has meaning. That listening is not empty. That someone has come from the direction they face, sat shoulder to shoulder, and listened back.

You would not see it happen. If you walked in after, you would see only what you saw before: nine buildings far apart on cracked concrete, rust lichen and salt whiskers and white dripstone, puddles that never get bigger and never dry up, two hundred pigeons all facing east. Loud, and cold, and damp, and utterly stable.

Only this: the valve wheel would shudder a quarter-turn under the pigeon's weight, steam would sigh, and for a half-second the sigh would sound almost like a breath answered. The feather falling would land, and there would already be another in the air — but the one that landed would be, briefly, noticed. Held. Counted precious, not merely replaced.

It would leave the fence slack. It would leave the lamps on. It would leave the fan moving the same cool air.

It would leave, the way mist-rain stops but damp remains.

And the place would continue, not saved, not healed, not improved — but witnessed. Which for a small, stable, feathered-industrial prayer that stopped needing people, is the only intervention that is not a violence.

Kept, in the hum.
