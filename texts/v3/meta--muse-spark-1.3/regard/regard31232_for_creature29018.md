It would come up the way you came up, and get it wrong at first the way you did.

It would expect cold and find it warm. Expect silence and find it loud. Expect empty and find it full-but-hidden. Even a wise power, even a benevolent one, that old, that large, has to kneel to learn a place this slow.

So it would kneel.

At first it would mistake them for rock. It would almost step on a young one settling by a windbreak and catch itself in time - which is the first mercy: to be big enough to crush a world and to stop.

Then it would lie down.

Because there is nothing to fix here. That is the hard thing for a power to understand. No wound to close. No river missing that needs to be made. No prayer for rescue rising from the potholes. The rule is intact: *the ground is finished, the sky is not.* The rock has settled. The weather rages and creates and destroys itself a dozen times a day. The backs carry green across the ringing emptiness on feet wide as dinner plates, fifty meters at a time, a lifetime at a time. The frogs sing one glass note and are swallowed by wind, and that is enough. The moths hatch in a blizzard and die papering the pools, and that is enough. The glider tilts without flapping, waiting, and that too is allowed.

A foolish power would make water stay. Dig a lake. Silence the organ roar so throats could talk. Hurry the seeds. Lift the old ones so they wouldn't have to lift their elephantine feet, boom, pause, boom-boom, through the night.

A wise one would hear what that boom is saying and not interrupt it.

So it would do almost nothing. Only the habits of mercy, the way there are only habits of dampness here. Nothing you could put on a map.

It would learn to speak in weight.

Air up here is useless for talking. It knows that now. So it would not boom from the sky in a voice. It would press its jaw, vast as weather, to the warm stone at dusk and listen through bone the way they do. Put its ear to the thin shells over old cavities and learn which hum is wind in chambers and which is footfall kilometers away. And when, far out across the tableland, an old She lifts her blind head an imperceptible fraction to ask *where is the damp tonight?* — it would answer back, not in words, but in pressure: a settling, a low returning tremor through rock, felt in the molars. *I heard you. Carry on.*

Boom. Pause. Boom-boom.
...boom.

You felt your own footfall come back wrong — flattened, then too loud. They live in that wrongness all the time. To be heard back, correctly, for once in ten thousand years: that would be enough revelation.

It would attend the in-betweens.

It would not stop the afternoon storm — they need the drowning, the pans turned to mirrors, the steam rising off carapace exactly as it rises off rock. But in the hour after rain, when they walk and the stone is cool and the temporary streams are still hissing toward the cracks, it would lay a hand across the rim and break the gust just a fraction, the way that low dry-stone windbreak, curved like an arm, breaks it for a young one, lighter, less polished, who might otherwise be staggered. No miracle. Just lee.

It would keep the emptiness empty.

Space between things is the main material here. Life cannot afford to be seen moving. The light is too flat and white. So the power would thicken the boil where needed. Let the clouds come up from below, pouring like slow waterfalls in reverse, white and grey and bruised purple, and hide a crossing. Let the satellite pass over and see only pale whaleback, pink-grey gneiss, lichen-black and rust-orange. Let the prospectors dream of other mountains. Let the cloth on the cairns snap violently where wind is clean and go limp in the lee of a settled back you might otherwise trip over and wake. It would not build a temple. It would keep the bothy of stacked slabs just big enough to crawl into, smelling of old smoke and damp wool, and no bigger.

It would remember guano and grit.

The people here have learned not to herd but to attend. Guano scraped from backs after the moth hatch. Grit swept from footprints for those collapsing terraces of potato and quinoa below the dome. The stone quern left upside down so rain will not fill it and crack it. The digging stick, polished by hands, leaning against a rock that is not a rock, left there because you do not dig when She is sleeping.

The power would not give them metal. Would not give them yield. It would give them continuance: that the pothole they drink from in the morning cupped in their hands is half-empty by evening and still there tomorrow. That the deer-like thing still comes at night to drink what the old one left. That the herders' children still learn to navigate by lichen worn off by feet over centuries, and by sound in white-out. It would sit with an old herder lying spine-along-crown at night, stars close enough to hurt, lightning flickering silent in the cloud sea below, and let him feel, through bone, the others moving out across the tableland carrying their gardens carefully across the emptiness. It would let him understand, without words, that his tending matters and is seen.

Mostly, it would keep time with them.

Eternity is too fast for this place. Even benevolence is too fast if it hurries. So the power would slow itself down until weeks of lying still to cultivate camouflage felt natural. Until lowering what little head you have and letting warm rain hammer so hard it bounces knee-high felt like prayer. Until cropping moss, not greedily, never clean, and leaving spores and frog eggs in the mud caked around your feet to make another green island where you settle next, felt like enough work for a god.

If you stayed long enough, you would feel it. Not as light, not as voice — your voice comes back wrong here, so it wouldn't try that. As warmth that gives back a fraction slower, the way skin does. As a dome three paces to the left of where you left it yesterday, holding a little more condensation into morning. As condensation ticking off leaves into potholes, drip, drip, drip, impossibly loud in the sudden space after the roar thins to a hiss, and one of those drips landing exactly, impossibly, in the thimble bromeliad cup where a thumbnail frog lives its whole life.

Find a long low swell that was not quite there yesterday. Lie with your spine along its crown. It will be warm. It will tick as it cools.

That ticking is it, attending.
