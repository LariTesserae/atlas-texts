I would do almost nothing.

That is the first wisdom. Not because they do not matter — because they do, entirely — but because they are *becoming*. And anything large I did would unmake the becoming.

If I filled their bowl with a river, I would drown the law that makes them kind. If I lifted them up onto the warm plain and gave them fields and forests, they would scatter and forget how to sit knee to knee. If I gave them machines to cut the rock, the *tink — tink — pause to listen* would stop, and with it their listening. If I took away the cold that pools at their knees, they would no longer need to share walls back to back.

I will not save them from the condition that is saving them.

So I come down. Not from above, the way you come. There is no other way, but I will not stand on the rim looking at them like ants. A benevolent power cannot afford that distance. I go down the stair-alley sideways, the way they do, one hand trailing on the wall. My palm comes away gray.

I learn to breathe where they breathe.

For a long time I only sit with them. That is the doing.

At dawn, when the fog is two meters deep and white and they move through it by sound and damp-smell, I sit by the cistern and keep the drip true. *Plip. Plip.* I do not multiply it. I only make sure it does not fail while they are learning its map. When the grandmother puts her hand to the back of a slab to see if the seep has shifted in the night, I let the stone be a shade darker where her fingers expect it. Continuity is a mercy.

In the morning when the stone is cold and brittle and good for cutting, I crouch beside the work crew. I do not soften the basalt — they need its sharpness to learn care — but I hold the crack steady, so the block comes clean and no one's thumb is smashed that day. When the wedge sings right, the old one sitting at the rim hears it. Twice for *hold*. Once for *pull*. I knock with them.

At midday when the sun reaches straight down and burns off the fog and they pause with spoons in bowls — barley, onion tops, thinned milk — I do not fill their bowls fuller. To do so would break who gets the morning drip and who gets the evening overflow, and that map is holier than fullness. Instead I sit in the collective pause and let the condensation tick off the thatch a little longer. Let them hear themselves drink. Bucket creak. Jar *plip*. Dog sighing over in dust.

They speak low because the bowl swallows shouting. I speak lower.

I do the small, invisible mending that wisdom allows:

I hold the rim grass. They are right — it holds the edge from crumbling. Where wind has worried a root loose, I press it back in the night while frost is prying elsewhere. No one will ever know. That is as it should be.

I keep the moss they coax around the seeps from drying in a bad year. Just a breath more damp under the slab. Just enough that the liverworts in the mortar cracks survive until the next cold spatter. Their green rectangles — onion tray, gourd vine, moss stroked flat — are not decoration. They are promises. I keep the promises possible.

I watch the children carrying fines in baskets from the upper cuttings to tamp the lane below, bent-kneed, head-forward, and when one stumbles on the damp lip of a stair, I am the extra friction under her sandal. She will think she caught herself. Good. She did.

At dusk — this is why I came — I come fully.

When the sun leaves the floor and only the western upper terraces hold light, when the *tink-tink* stops all at once as if the stone called pause, and the hundreds of tallow flickers come on in niches chipped for exactly that purpose, I take a place on a roof among them, shoulder to shoulder, knees touching, under a shared hide against the pooling cold.

They do not go inside yet. Inside is for deep night. Dusk is for being together above the fog, breathing together. So I breathe with them. You can see your breath half the year here. Mine joins theirs for a moment, white in the cooling air, and then is gone. I borrow a little of their warmth and return a little more, so the hide is not quite so thin tonight.

I do not tell them who I am. They would not have a word for it that would not diminish us both. If a baby looks at me a second too long and then leans back into its mother, that is enough recognition.

I look, while we sit, at what the place is making: not survivors in a quarry. A pit people. Gray as rock from a distance, green in tended patches up close. Cool in habit, warm by crowding. The whole bowl glowing faintly like embers in a pit while the fog begins to gather white and low between the houses.

And I think: *stay embryonic a little longer.* Do not rush to become a city. Cities forget water-maps. Let the rock stay sharp a little longer. Sharp teaches gentleness — see how they touch stone constantly, testing. Cold morning stone rings different than afternoon stone. They are learning a language I cannot give them, only protect the quiet for.

In deep night, when they finally go inside to sleep packed tight in one warm room, I walk the drainage cuts where the rats run, tolerated like difficult cousins. I check every chipped channel leading down to the communal pits lined with puddled clay. I clear a leaf, a grit-clog. I make sure tomorrow's rain — three days off, cold spatter that will darken the dust for an hour — will find its way to someone's jar.

Then I leave no trace. No footprint in quarry dust that wasn't already there. No healed cut that would make them wonder. The frost in the morning will find fresh flakes from yesterday's cut, as it should. The water will have found a new path, as it will. A wall will have settled a finger's width, and be re-mudded. By them.

If you asked what I did, I would say: I paid attention for a very long time, and I kept the world steady enough that attention could become love.

That is what benevolence does when it is wise. It does not build for them downward and inward. It lets them do it, and keeps the rim from crumbling while they learn how.
