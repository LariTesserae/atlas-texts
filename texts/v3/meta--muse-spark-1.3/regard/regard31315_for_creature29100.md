If anyone wise and kind noticed — really noticed — they would first do nothing.

They would come down the stairs that were not meant to be stairs, the way you did, feeling the air thicken and warm around them. They would feel the hum in the molars. They would stand on the high pavers, because they respect house rules, and listen until the roar resolved into layers. Water weeping. Pumps gargling. The deep cycling underneath.

They would not turn the field off.

That will be their first mercy, and it will look like neglect.

Anyone less wise would pity the place for being empty of birds and try to fix it — switch off the resonance, bring down pigeons in a cage, drain the bathwater pond. Anyone less benevolent would pity the machines for being mindless and upgrade them, give the pruners hands and orders.

They will understand sooner than you did: this wound has become a womb. It is not a city to be repaired. It is four days old, or four years old, still with bright rebar whiskers, still learning how to be a ruin. To fill it in, to pave it, to “reconnect the street” would be to kill an infant because it is not yet an adult.

So they sit. On the moss-shape that remembers being a bus shelter. They do not mind getting wet. The cloud is forming at chest height, as it does by mid-afternoon. The daylight slab is moving, slow as a sundial, across the tilted hexes. The service tram crosses overhead every eleven minutes, empty, lit, humming. They watch it go. They do not wave.

They listen for the being.

Not the moss, not the fern, not the carriage with the mantis arm — though they love each of those particularly. The being is the slow eyeless negotiation between them. Green that grows furiously and white ones that spore and dark lungs that breathe twice a day and rails that snip once a week and steam that cauterizes. A town holding open a cathedral nave of wet air so water can practice weather. It has no eyes. No nerves to offend. But it has a rhythm, and a tolerance, and a preference: a millimeter further in, every day, around the pond edge.

That is who lives here. Young, and unheard, and perfect to monstrousness because nothing has ever eaten and been eaten here. Leaves that have never been bitten. Figs that harden and plop and bob and rot where they land because no gut will carry them.

A wise power knows hunger is not cruelty. It is how a place becomes a place, instead of a greenhouse.

But they will not bring animals down. The rule is written for a reason. The field would sicken them, and to force a sparrow to live with nausea behind the eyes so you can feel less lonely is not benevolence.

So they do smaller things. Almost nothing. The kind of things you only see if you stay still long enough for non-movement to resolve.

First, they breathe with the pond.

When the dark grates inhale down to glistening mud, exposing the knuckled prop roots and their white hunting hairs, they hold their breath too. The plants don’t mind holding their breath. The machines don’t mind. The visitor with glasses fogged does. The power holds it with them, so the minute when the air goes thin — when the hum stops — is not empty. It is accompanied. Twice a day. In and out. A hand on a chest, learning the pace.

Second, they teach the green to eat itself, gently.

Because there are no mouths here, they touch the white shelves along the warm pipes and the film on the glass and the bacteria already in the bathwater, and they encourage them. *You may.* Fallen vine cutting left by the pruners? You may soften it. Hard green fig knot bobbing uncarried? You may enter it and make it sweet in rot, even if no bird will taste it. They do not introduce anything from outside. They simply give permission for decay to complete its circuit without nerves, without pain. So perfection stops being monstrous. A leaf gets a hole, and holds it like an eye.

Third, they give the tenders a flicker of delight.

The pruner carriage comes down its rustless track, wipes its camera eye with its little squeegee, snips a tendril reaching for the grate and lets it fall. Clean, bloodless. It does not know why it keeps the throats clear, only tolerance parameters. The power rests a palm — if they have a palm — near its rail, and leaves a residue: a preference. Not a command. A warmth when the cut is clean, a pause half a second longer before withdrawing, as if looking. The sterilizer elbows at the pond edge exhale their white roar in a perfect ring; after, the smell is cooked green and hot stone. The power lingers in the steam. Next cycle the ring will be a millimeter wider on the north side, where the rarest fern crowns are trying to root. Restraint. A negotiation, not a program.

And last, the smallest mercy, the one only water and light will notice:

In one corner of the black mirror pond, no larger than a dinner plate, they thin the field just enough that physics behaves. Condensation no longer forms in perfect grids there. Droplets fall on time. And your reflection — if you kneel on the high pavers and look — arrives on time. Not a beat late.

They leave that one true mirror for the being to see itself. Moss and fern and curtain and stilt and spore and hum, all at once, in amber and green phosphor, under the slit of real sky.

Then they go up. Because to stay would be to become another landlord, and the place already has one. Because the tram must remain empty. Because the leaves must remain uneaten except by rot. Because benevolence, at this scale, is mostly protection from other benevolences: they will make sure the kiosk upstairs keeps selling something, the curb keeps holding, no engineer with a good idea drains the pond or switches the lights off to save power. They will make sure it is allowed to remain useless, wet, loud, and dimly lit — a mostly empty room the size of a nave that has its own weather.

On the way up the switchbacks, shirt sticking, glasses fogging and not unfogging, they will feel the familiar nausea behind the eyes, up slightly to the left. They will not cure it. It is the toll. It reminds the mammal, briefly too loud in a different way, that you were a guest.

At street level, dry noise again. They do not tell anyone what they did, because almost nothing is hard to explain.

Down below, the cloud forms. The curtains wring the air out into rain beneath them. A droplet slows, hangs half a second too long in the amber light — except, in one small circle, it doesn’t. It falls.

Plop. A fig knot bobs. Somewhere white spores settle a foot away and start again.
