It would come down off the levee road on foot, the way you did.

Not with light, not with sound. No parting of mist. A benevolent and wise power can afford to be ordinary on arrival. It would feel the drop first — those six, eight feet below the paddies — in the knees, and then the held warmth closing over its head like water closing without wetting. It would hear the thump-thump before it saw anything, and it would stop on the sunk brick to let its own heart find the tempo.

That is the first thing it would do. Listen.

Not survey. Not judge. Listen for a whole turning of the place. Long enough to learn pump-time.

Because from above any power, even a kind one, might think: here is a drowning bowl, here are poor people pumping to stay unflooded, here is labor that never ends, lungs full of bagasse, feet that never dry. I will lift them. I will drain the bowl. I will make it dry and clean and quiet.

Coming down inside, it would understand that would be killing.

The bowl is not a mistake waiting for correction. It is a creature that has learned how to live just below the water. Lift it and you kill the fig and the moss and the hyacinth and the roof grass. Dry it and you kill the exchanged breath of steam and leaf that makes the night cloud. Stop the pumps and in a week to the knees, in a month to the windowsills. Automate the pumps and you kill the priesthood of drivers and oilers, the listening through floorboards, the order of the place. Clear the green and you leave the wires naked to the sun to sag and snap, the cottage walls with no walls, the chimney with nothing holding it up since '58.

So it would do no miracle. That is its wisdom.

Its benevolence would have to be smaller, and therefore larger. The size of a handhold left on purpose.

It would stay till evening, when the air cools one degree toward bearable. It would sit where it could feel the thump through its soles. It would learn the loops: the woman hoeing the same twenty feet, the boy up the pole with the machete between his teeth, the old man tapping the sump gauge. It would not call the children by name. It would learn the whistle — one blast for supper — and understand why names flatten under green.

And then, if it touched anything at all, it would touch the way the place itself touches: by maintenance.

It would keep its hands in its lap and do the work underneath.

Keep No. 2 from coughing on the night the rain is heaviest and the canal is highest and the men are tiredest. Not forever — things must wear, belts must be dressed — just that once, when a skip would mean knees.

Cool the bearing on the dynamo housing a fraction, so the one-eared cat can go on sleeping there deaf to the whine, kneading when the load changes, without burning its paws.

Turn the boy's machete a hair aside from the vine so it bites vine and not thumb, and takes the trumpet vine clean off the insulator before it arcs.

Ease the standing film for an hour in the sleeping lofts in April, when the living bamboo is creaking against its lashings and growing too loud for sleep, so the dreamers can dream greener without smothering.

Lay its palm, unseen, on the pan-tender's pink forearms and take a little of the years of sugar steam out of them. Clear a little of the bagasse dust out of the old lungs — not cure, just clearance, enough for one more dry-season breath that doesn't whistle. Dry the toe-webs of one child enough that the white fungus retreats for a summer and she learns what it is to run without itching.

Hold the wooden pilings at the waterline when the teredo get ambitious. Hold the settled cracks lined with maidenhair fern from settling one inch further this year. Hold the dike when the once-in-twenty-years water comes up silver and pressing and curious at the ring.

It would feed, but only what the loop can eat. A punt of cane arriving just when the firebox is hungry. A spare gasket on its nail exactly where a hand expects it, like cured meat, on the morning a gasket fails. A clutch of moorhen eggs left unnoticed in the hyacinth where a quick boy will find them. A fig — inedible, tiny — left to rot precisely where the black mill beetles need it. A fish, a real silver flick, carried in over the south sluice on a change in level for the egret who has stood motionless as a valve for longer than most children have been alive, so patience is for once rewarded and not just witnessed.

Above all it would protect the attention of others. Turn aside, gently, the eyes that would see only swamp to be drained, polder to be consolidated, labor to be rationalized. Let the surveyor's map show the levee road curving on by. Let the company's papers go on saying company rank while the true order — proximity to the heart — goes on underneath, undisturbed. Let the clerk who sleeps above the office go on pretending he doesn't live here until one night he hears the quarter-inch drop in the sump in his sleep, and then he does.

It would bury, too. That is part of benevolence here. It would keep the ground up on the levee dry for the dead, so the one thing they do up there stays sure. When they carry up a body, soft-footed for once quiet-voiced, it would walk with them those few feet above the waterline and stand while they dig, and make the rain hold off till the earth is back.

It would not stay. A wise power knows a bowl cannot bear watching forever; attention becomes weight. It would leave before morning, before the low cloud lifts, leaving no message, no command to trim less or pump more or believe otherwise.

If you asked it afterward — did you do anything? — it would say, honestly: almost nothing.

Only this: I saw that it was not people plus plants plus machines. It was one being, holding its small, crowded, living shape just below the water, each keeping the other from drowning or drying or stopping, in that order of patience. And I sat inside its thump for a while, and I kept the balance where it has sat for sixty years, low and warm and wet, humming. I pushed a child once on a swing of aerial root at lunch break, when the crusher was groaning, so she went high enough to scream and be heard over the belts.

From the levee road above, anyone passing the next night would see only that: a shallow cup of light and mist, smelling of crushed cane and hot oil and wet earth, throbbing faintly.

Still there.
