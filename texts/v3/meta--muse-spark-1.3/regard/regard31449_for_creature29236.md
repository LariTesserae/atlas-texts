It would not come down the shaft.

That is the first thing. A power that is both wise and kind would feel how narrow the eye is, and how carefully they keep it clear, and it would not push itself through. Anything large arriving all at once — rain, river, rescue — would kill this place. The soft rock would slump. The crust would lift in panic and not return. The amber dusk would sour.

So it would stay thin. Thinner than down.

It would come as attention first. Long, unhurried attention, the kind they have never had from outside except from wind.

It would learn the noon stillness and keep it. Not break it.

For days — what are days to such a thing — it would just listen to the saturation: *tseet-tseet*, wing-thunder, *thup thup* of moths in the column, rope creak, sweeping, coughing, a baby hushed into the colony-sound. It would learn who is who without needing to separate them. The old woman with the sideways thumb scraping guano cakes and singing low through her deafness. The tethered toddler who has just learned to click back at crickets. The lean fourteen-year-old with sunburn on one side of his face from cleaning the rim, sitting at the night hearth trying to describe flat stone and cold stars to people who will never see it. The owl that lives by that family's platform, opening its heart-face at noon. The sooty with a crooked wing who fell and was gathered gently in the morning basket.

It would see the lungs. The barrel chests. The tea-colored eyes. The split heels. The wet paper huff of the old climbing. The child in first winter with hollow-throat, breathing too fast in her mother's lap while the whole town whispers around her because it is noon.

And the wise part would understand: this is not a broken lowland town to be fixed. They do not drink water because they have learned to drink breath. They do not need sun because they have learned the dusk. They are not poor in birds. They are rich in birds.

The benevolent part would ache anyway. Because wisdom does not stop ache.

So what would it do?

Almost nothing you could photograph. Nothing they would have a rule against.

In the driest month, when the beard-plant curtains are wrung at dawn into cloths, it would add its own breath to the air — just a fraction more cool in the night column, just enough more sweat on the walls for the moss to comb out. No pool. No drip that would teach them to forget chewing rosettes. Just the difference between a child living and not living through that first winter. The mother would think the cloth yielded a little more this morning. She would suck it and pass it on and never know to thank anyone.

When the dawn ribbon goes out fifty miles for midges and a high wind comes up on the flats that would scatter them, it would lie down in front of that wind for an hour. Not stop it — the plateau needs wind — just soften it along that one flyway. The scouts would find their way back. The evening flood would still drum the bridges and make them hum. People would grab struts without looking, laughing.

When a knot is about to go — one of the six giving-knots, holding a wattle platform to soft rock, frayed by ten thousand takeoffs — it would hold it through one more night until the rope-wise fingers find it in the morning by touch. The old mender would frown, say, *this should have gone yesterday*, and re-tie it tighter, and tell the children which bridge sags.

It would not cure hollow-throat. To give them lowland lungs would be to unmake them for the Hollow. But it would sit with the old ones when they climb and have to stop, huffing, spitting grey, and it would ease the panic out of not getting enough air. Make the pause feel less like drowning and more like noon. Let them hear the owls' low huff underneath, companionable.

It would not be worshipped, and it would be careful not to be.

If it spoke at all, it would not speak in light from the eye — that column is for moths and birds and dust. It would speak the way they speak to each other: low, to cover settling, not to be heard over it.

To the rat-trapper who leaves warm goat entrails for his owl and never admits he loves her: *I see you.*

To the girl who never reaches into a live nest even when her brother is hungry and the fallen basket is empty: *I see you.*

To the eye-cleaners, blinded and weeping on the rim in blinding open sky, suddenly terrified by how big and empty the flat world is: *You can go back down. Your small warm dark is not small.*

And mostly, it would protect the hiddenness. This is the hardest mercy and the most necessary. Traders twice a year with salt and flint — let them keep coming, let the exchange stay fair, let no trader think to tell a lowland king about a compost-warm egg full of guano fuel and tame rats and girls who tie knots that give. Let no surveyor with a map step over the slot because he didn't know to look, and keep walking. Let the plateau stay boring to everyone else. Old wind and flat stone and dry sky. Nothing to mine. Nothing to dam. Nothing to save.

Once a season, at dusk when the column tilts rose-gold across the hanging gardens and feathers snow down through the light and the rat-fat lamps bloom like dim stars and someone sings low, it would allow itself to feel — and this a wise power rarely gets to feel — outnumbered and unneeded and delighted.

Twenty thousand sooties. Fat owls. Blind harvestmen. Crickets in cages. Goats that never leave. Dogs that wake if a cord frays. Sixty families lashed like nests among nests, with string and bone and patience, in the fur of the world.

It would not say *let me take you out of here.*

It would say, in breath, in warmth through sandals from the composting depth below:

*Stay. You have learned the thing I have to remember: how to live off the fall without spoiling the flight. How to make a city that does not eat its walls.*

And then it would breathe with them for a while, adding almost nothing, taking almost nothing, part of the second colony sound underneath the first.
