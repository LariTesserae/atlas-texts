It would kneel.

Not at first. At first It would walk past, like everyone does. That is part of the mercy.

Up there the light is too white and the wind too loud to see a hand-wide crack for what it is. Boots step over. Eyes look to the summit. That overlooking is the roof that has kept it alive. A benevolent and wise power understands roofs.

So It would walk past once, to learn how to be overlooked. And then It would come back, alone, without thunder, and kneel, and put Its face to the crack until steam wet Its cheek.

It would not bring gifts.

It would not bring green. No moss, no seed, no soil to soften the glass. Green would drink the sulfur and starve the skin that never learned green, and then the snow would fall for real and not get up again.

It would not bring eyes. The tide is blind because there is nothing to see except steam and shine. Antennae are better eyes here. To give them eyes would only be to give them a way to be blinded.

It would not bring song. The silence is not missing. It is saved breath. Chirp costs heat. Buzz costs water. They have traded music for density, and it was a good trade. It would not undo it.

It would not bring kindness the way the lower world means it — no stopping of eating. The pale crickets eating the snow, the red in the pan lunging half out of water to drag a cricket down, the rim-walker pruning them all: that is not cruelty here. That is circulation. To stop the grapple that lasts three seconds would be to stop the tide. To pity the fly would be to starve the sawyer.

It would not bring bigness. It would not crack the spire wider to give them room. Room would cool them. The whole weather — condensation, fall, evaporation, rise — only works because the room is the size of cupped hands. Water adds space when it needs to. It knows how large to be.

It would not bring permanence. It knows this rock is young. Hot blood under it now, cold in a hundred years, or ten. The glass will dull. The seeps will thin. The pan will crust yellow and then grey and then stop pulsing. Everything here lives fast because it is a flare. A wise power does not wire a flare to stay lit. It lets it be brief and total.

What would It do, then, if It is benevolent?

Small, almost-nothing things. The kind you have to press your ear close to hear.

It would regulate Its own attention. Not count. That is the first discipline. Twelve, thirteen — blink — nine. The midges passing through each other like smoke. The flake that was in one mouth and is now in another. The back wall bending heat into two fingers. The place refuses census, and a wise power would not insist. To count them would be to collapse them into one version. It would practice the harder sight: to witness without fixing a number. To hold them as many and uncounted and still precious.

It would breathe *with*, not *on*. It would time Its breath to the seep-surge, to that slow tightening of the bacterial skin like a sleeper breathing. In, when the beads gather. Out, when they break and run. Not to change the weather, just to keep company inside it for one cycle: graze, hunt, eat, be eaten. An hour. A whole history.

It would steady the conditions that let them provide for themselves. Not a miracle. A maintenance. A hand held a little to windward so a gust doesn't scour the crack dry at noon. A fingertip, never touching, shading just enough white light that a hanging drop doesn't evaporate before it falls. Ensuring the updrafts still tithe — that a stray moth from miles below still blunders in, sodden and huge as a god, to be sawn at the wing joint. Ensuring no boot, no cairn-builder, no sampler with a bottle finds this particular teeth of the ridge. Obscurity is a form of care. It would keep it.

If It touched at all, It would touch only the dead. When the rim-walker wedges hot and twitching in a crack too narrow for its length — translucent, gut still red at the front and yellow at the back — It would not rescue her. It would stay while the tide covers her in minutes and scrapes her clean, *click, click, click*, glass on glass. It would make death quick and unlonely, which is the only euthanasia this place allows. And when a large cricket drops its eyelash-knife to become floor, It would notice where it landed. Pavement of lost knives. History without monuments. It would remember that no workshop was needed for holiness.

It would, for one cricket, do the most personal thing: pick one — say the one with the chipped left antenna, opaque, missing legs and still climbing — follow it as far as can be followed through the knot of identical backs, lose it, find it or another identical three inches higher facing the wrong way, and love it without needing to know if it is the same one. That is what benevolence looks like at this scale. Not saving. Recognizing.

And then It would get up. Knees cracking in the cold wind after the heat. Lips chapped again. It would not mark the spire. No cairn, no wall, no sign. No story told loudly enough to bring pilgrims.

It would walk past again, like everyone does. On purpose, this time. As protection.

And years later, when the blood below cools and the rock stops sweating and the snow settles for good and the tide thins and the pan goes dry and even the skin loses its pulse — It would come back once more. Not to restart it. To sit with the cold teeth while they learn to be round. To run — now at last — a finger along the dulled edge, and feel under the dust the faint conchoidal pits where mandibles once knapped knives, and the pavement of eyelash flakes at the bottom.

To have witnessed a world that never needed to be large to be complete. To have let it remain silent, sharp, hot, crowded, uncounted.

That is what It would do. Almost nothing. Which here is everything.
