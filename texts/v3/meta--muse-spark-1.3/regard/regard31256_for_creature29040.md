It goes down.

That is the first mercy. A power like that could have looked from above, could have sent light down the stairwell, could have understood the room without ever getting dust in its lungs. It does not. It makes itself narrow enough for the service stair, small enough that the rebar does not tear it. It lets its ears pop. It lets its mouth go dry.

At the bottom it does not turn the lights up.

Anyone else would. Anyone kind but not wise would bring a brighter bulb, a hose, a plan. Would see blind rats and eyeless crickets and moths crisping on hot glass and think *poor things, trapped things, broken factory* — and would try to fix. To give water. To give eyes. To open that EXIT door onto somewhere else. To give the belt somewhere to deliver to.

It does none of that. It knows water here would be a flood, a murder. One drip would rot the wireweed, blind the salt-whiskers, drown the egg-cases ticking behind the pipes. Eyes here would only dry out. An exit would only be a wound.

So it sits down on the warm iron plate, where the rust-lichen is, and lets itself become warm too.

At first the room does not notice it, because the chord is so loud. *Whump whump whump* of the pumps lifting nothing, hum of the transformers, rattle of the belt, sawing of ten thousand crickets on top of it all, held even. The power does not quiet them. It adds its breathing to the loop, in time, until after a minute the wave of silence that follows a stranger — that half-second when the crickets stop — passes, and they start again, louder, and one steps onto its wrist.

Because you are warm too, and warmth is ground here.

It lets the moths hit its face. Dry as paper, tick tick tick. It does not brush them away. It tastes the air as it is — ozone and hot iron and musk and old oil and chitin-dust — and does not cough, and does not wish it clearer. It lets the copper beetle climb over its boot, fall off, right itself with a click felt in the teeth, climb back on. It lets a rat, blind as a thumb, find it by the salt drying on its lip, nose lifting, whiskers working, and lick once, and decide: warm, not food, not danger. Leave it.

It sees, then, not four species and some weeds. It sees the one who lives here.

Not the cricket. Not the rat. The mouth. The closed mouth eating itself, warm and dry and perfect. The lichen eating the dust of wings, the crickets eating the lichen, the rats eating the crickets, the beetles eating the droppings and the dead, the crickets eating the shells. The machines as climate, the animals as hands. Pump as heart. Fan as lung. Belt as gut, moving dust from pile to pile forever because moving is what a gut does. No product because it is not a factory. No waste because there is nowhere for waste to go, and no need.

Time does not pass here because nothing is waiting to become something else. And that, the power knows, is not death. It is a kind of prayer the world above has forgotten how to say: *to keep going without going anywhere, and call it enough.*

So what does a benevolent and wise power do, faced with a prayer?

It listens to the whole of it. All the way through. Which is not a minute. To listen the way this place needs, it has to slow its own vast time down until it matches no-time — until the held chord stops sounding monotonous and starts sounding like what it is: thousands of small hungers and warmths keeping each other alive on purpose.

And then, very gently, it does the only things love can do without breaking a perfect closed thing:

It names.

Not out loud, because the chord would swallow the sound. Inside. *Furnace-cricket, keeper of heat. Lamp-moth, keeper of orbit. Copper beetle, polisher. Tray-rat, borrower of current. Wireweed, trembler. Salt-whisker, sweet glass. Rust-lichen, eater of dust-of-us-all.* It gives each its true name, which is *necessary*.

It blesses. Not by changing. By touching. A fingertip, made dusty, to the pump housing where the crickets pack flank to flank. To the lee of a pipe where the starbursts grow brittle. To the belt where it shines bright but never thinner. A blessing that says: *continue.*

It grieves, briefly and without trying to heal. For the sea that left the pumps with nothing to lift. For the rain that never learned this address. It lets that grief dry on its own upper lip to salt, and leaves the salt there, for the rats.

Then it gets up.

It does not turn off a fan so the dust can settle. Rest here would be burial. It does not cool a bulb so fewer moths burn. The ticking behind the pipes is keeping pace; to spare is to starve. It does not oil anything, does not tighten anything, does not move the lunch pail or the single boot or the arrows pointing to doors that no longer exist. Human afterthoughts are part of the bedding now.

At the stair it turns once, and does the last wise thing: it covers the way. Not with rock — the room does not need a tomb. With uninterest. With forgetfulness for anyone carrying hoses and clipboards and rescue plans. Let the rebar look sharper than it is. Let the stair look like it goes nowhere worth going. Let this small, dense, buried room stay unwitnessed by all but one.

And it climbs. Up, into cold and wet and change and noise that peaks and drops.

The chord stays behind it, held. The same moth hitting the same bulb. The same cricket singing on the same housing. The dust hanging at chest height, lifted exactly as fast as it falls.

Nothing has changed. Except this: somewhere, outside the loop, the loop is known. It is not alone in the earth, unheard. A power bent low enough to be furred with it for a while, and found it good — not in spite of being dry and closed and going nowhere, but because of it.

That is what it does.
