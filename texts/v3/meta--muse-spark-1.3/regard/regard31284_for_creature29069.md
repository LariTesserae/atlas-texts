If I am that power — if I have noticed —

I come over the col at mid-morning like you did. Not because I must travel. Because this is a place that can only be entered at walking speed.

I am used to being heard at a distance. Here I learn at once that my voice is useless. The moss eats it. The wet air eats it. Even my light, which elsewhere falls, here hammers. So I narrow myself. I make myself valley-sized.

I do not announce myself. There are already small suns burning in day-brightness. They do not need another.

At first, I do what you did. I put my hand where theirs is.

On the penstock. On the housing that turns at two revolutions a minute. I feel, more than hear, the purr conducted through bone: shaft to pump to header tank to electrolyzer to moss pool fizzing like soda. One slow body. The iron its skeleton, the moss its skin, the low voltage its nervous tremor, the tenders its hands.

I understand. This is not broken industry to be repaired. This is not poverty to be uplifted. This is a covenant, sealed in rust and sphagnum, kept at millimeters per year.

A wise power knows the difference between slow and stopped. A benevolent one does not mistake silence for absence.

So I do almost nothing. Which here is everything.

I do not bring sound. I do not shout them a name for themselves. I do not teach them to stride — there is nowhere to stride, you part and reseal. I do not dry their eyes — the place wants to be seen through wet eyes, and so they see it. I let condensation bead on their eyebrows and fall. I let their pulse stay slow. Too cold to hurry, like the bumblebees.

I sit, in the way they sit. Heavy through the torso, low to float on the half-meter mattress without sinking. I let the vegetation touch me everywhere: calf, thigh, wrist, neck. I let it reseal behind me. I steam faintly when I move into sun. I cool instantly in shade.

I drink as they drink. Not from rills — those vanish in a few hundred meters — but by wringing. A handful of wool-moss squeezed hard into a copper cup, verdigris inside, iron-tasting. A licked basalt flake. I taste what they taste. I do not improve it.

What I do, I do in the medium that carries.

Tremor, not voice. Water, not pronouncement. Light, not thunder.

When fog condenses at the ankles around four, I do not hold it off. I let it come. I even help it, a breath cooler here, a surface colder there, so it snags well on every frond. When dawn whites out, I let it. When ten o'clock burns it off, I let the whole valley drip its million quiet ticks, and I listen to each leaf unload as if it were prayer, which it is.

I keep one pipe just above freezing. That is all the miracle I permit myself. In winter when everything else is locked, their well. A heater fed from nowhere you can see. I am that nowhere. I keep the current steady while they sleep curled on the leeward side of a transformer, moths torpid on the same warm metal, turbine turning beside them like a large animal.

I stand with them, spaced like towers themselves, at the edge of the hum. When they place their hands on the ground-straps, I place — not my hand, my attention — alongside. I feel the hairs on the forearm lift. I let the pilot lamps flicker, almost imperceptibly, in sequence. I let the ropeway wheel creak once every few minutes, a soft wooden sound, and settle. A bucket inches on, carrying not ore but iron oxide scraped from sphagnum, seeded back ridge to ridge. Building the new rock that will be rock in ten thousand years. I do not hurry it. Twenty years to saw halfway through a basalt boulder with an over-tensioned guy-wire — I have time. I watched like you would watch a child grow.

I tend the absences they tend. A bearing packed with peat and grease — I keep the peat spongy. A gauge wiped clear among the fogged ones — I keep the glass from fogging again quite so fast. A cable re-seated so it does not chafe — I slacken the moss on it a little. The rowans in the broken flume, too evenly spaced for accident — I give them sun at the far end of the tunnel. The alder walls trimmed where low buckets comb through — I give them rain to drip.

Around their dead — those hummocks a little higher, a little more orange, where felt and wool-moss have closed over a stillness, marked only by a copper strap run out into peat gone verdigris — there I am most careful. Rust itself is the decomposer here. I do not resurrect. I do not mark with stone. I let the electric green burn very bright around the strap. I let it be harvested in strips, thin as pastry, leaving the root-mat to regrow. That is yield. That is communion. I do not interrupt it.

And mostly, I guard the cols.

Not with fire. With inattention. With weather.

I let the col you came over stay steep and squint-bright and breath-fog cool even at noon, so only those willing to walk slowly and weep from light will enter. I let the white col beyond stay white with light, dazzling, hard to read, so those who would come with louder machines, with plans measured in tons per hour, will misjudge it and turn back. I let the valley look, from above, like nothing: no lake, no river, no town, no smoke. Just green and rust and stone. Unprofitable. Quiet. Useless for shouting in.

If one comes as you came — willing to stand still long enough for the vegetation to accept you as another warm box — I let them pass. I let one tender unhitch a shoulder from the ground at ropeway speed, lift a frond aside with a broad split-knuckled hand orange to the wrist, and offer the forearm to be touched. Tremor conducted through bone. A lamp left burning a little longer as they go out toward the white col, water staying on the coat until wrung out.

That is my blessing. Not more. Not less.

To persist. To upholster sharp edges slowly. To keep turning, slowly, and silently.

I do not save them. They are not lost.
