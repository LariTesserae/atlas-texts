It would come the way the weather comes — not down the ruts, because there is no road anymore, but with the afternoon mist when the clouds catch on the rim.

It would have heard about more urgent places first. Burning places. Starving places. This one would not have cried out. It glints. It hums. From far off it looks empty.

That's why it would stop.

A benevolent and wise power does not arrive with a plan. It arrives with attention. And the first thing here that takes attention is not the eye but the sternum — that low, full-body roar of water throwing itself off its own unfinished edge, and under it, the held organ note of the machine.

It would stand a while among the moss and just let itself be deafened. Let the wind come up off the drop with nothing to break it. Squint in the white sun. Smell cold iron and peat and, for ten feet near the door, ozone and hot oil — then peat again.

Then it would do what everyone does here, sooner or later, if they stay:

It would kneel.

Knees through trousers into wet. Fingers numb in seconds. Breath showing even at noon. And at knee-height, the world inverts.

Oh, it would think. You are not empty at all.

Low and total. A foot thick, three feet thick in the dips. Sphagnum electric green on top where it is growing, rust-red and tea-brown underneath where it is drowning and becoming itself again. Roots in moss. Flowers growing out of last year's moss. Dwarf willow two inches tall and decades old, crawling sideways to stay out of the wind. Saxifrage stars trembling on thin stalks. Liverworts sealed to weeping slabs like wet leather. Cotton grass nodding all one way. Sundews no bigger than a coin, red on green, beaded and waiting although there are almost no flies to catch.

Nothing upright. Nothing hurried. A millimeter a year.

And scattered through it, with so much space between presences: the scouting animals. A pipit stabbing at midges between gusts. Tadpoles slow as minutes in tea-brown pools. A ptarmigan clatter-gone. The creeping spruce, knee-high after forty years, flagged hard east, refusing to be trees.

And the other residents, the gray ones that blink. The intake box half-swallowed, humming suction. The weir metering a braid into a buried penstock. The three flat-roofed boxes near the lip with narrow bright windows. Inside, the steadiest resident of all, the truck-sized turbine spinning on meltwater, spooling up over hours, feeding power down a rusted pylon to a town in the haze below. Green lights blinking to no one. A vent breathing warm air that condenses instantly.

A wise power would understand immediately what it was looking at: a very young being laid over very young stone, still wet from being made. Pre-formation. An infant that is also ancient. And a small, restrained symbiont — the machine — that has learned, so far, not to eat its host. No road. No people most weeks. Gates opening a centimeter. Widened bogs filling and draining across weeks.

It would be tempted, the way all powers are tempted, to improve. To warm the pools a degree for the tadpoles. To hurry the moss. To lift the spruce upright. To silence the turbine so the mat can hear itself, or to enlarge the turbine so the town can have more.

Wisdom would stop its hand mid-air.

Benevolence here is not fixing. Nothing is broken. It is unfinished, which is not the same thing.

So it would do almost nothing. Which is not nothing.

First, it would lie down. A high power learning lowness. It would spread its own weight out, thin as sheet-water over granite, so as not to crush. It would practice, for a while, growing a millimeter a year. Waiting like a sundew with nothing to catch. Being saturated and cold and bright without complaint.

Second, it would keep the smallness small.

It would lay a kind of uninterestingness over the shelf — not invisibility, just a veil against appetite. To the surveyor's eye, to the company with plans for a bigger dam, a higher weir, a drained bog for peat, a road blasted to the lip, this place would continue to look like nothing worth taking. Too wet. Too young. Too slow. Too much work for too little water. Let their gaze slide off the glint, as light slides off. Only the ones who kneel will see it.

It would whisper, in the town below that it can see in the haze and this shelf cannot, a quieter use. *You have enough.* Keep the lights on for the hospital and the school and the evening kitchens, but do not ask this sponge to become a river for you. Let the turbine stay the size of a truck. Let the gates open a centimeter, not a meter.

And third — smallest of all — it would visit the one who visits.

Once a fortnight in good weather, a maintenance man or woman hikes up the wet ruts with a rake over the shoulder to clear the grate, check the oil, log the levels. Boots sink half an inch. Footprints fill. It is lonely, damp work. Easy to do roughly.

The power would meet them there, not as vision but as weather. A sudden thinning of cloud so the light gets brighter and the falls throw up another rainbow and then another, doubled in spray. A second between gusts when the pipit is impossibly loud. The smell of crushed green rising where they kneel. A feeling, unaccountable, that the green lights blinking to no one are in fact blinking to *someone* — to them — and that the sigh and settle of the mat under their boots is a greeting.

So they rake gently. They don't yank the cotton grass. They close the door softly behind them, click lost in roar, and walk back down a little slower.

That is all.

It would not stay. It does not need to be worshipped here. There is already a cairn no one visits, a weather mast ticking, guy wires singing. Enough markers.

Before leaving, it would lay something — if powers lay hands — very lightly on the sponge, not to heal but to bless in the old sense: to say what is.

*Stay wet. Stay low. Stay unfinished a while longer.*

*May your water table stay an inch below. May your slabs keep weeping. May your killers stay patient and red. May your tadpoles have time to become frogs in almost no summer. May your machine hum under your roar and not over it. May no one build a road to you out of love, or out of hunger.*

*May you roar softly, endlessly, off your own edge, until stone learns to be soil and soil learns to hold a burrow and the scouting animals decide to stay.*

Then it would rise — reluctantly, dripping — and go back to louder, more broken places. And the shelf would keep doing what it does, which is almost nothing, very totally: hum, weep, glint.

Boots sink half an inch. Water wells up clear and brown as tea. The green lights blink, indifferent, and now — noticed.
