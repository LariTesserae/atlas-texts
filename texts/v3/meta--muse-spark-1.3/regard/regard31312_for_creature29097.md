It would not come as light.

Light would blind them, and break the rule of the place. It would come the way you come — up and up until breath has to be thought about — and it would make itself small enough, and cold enough, to be allowed.

For a long time it would do nothing that looks like doing. It would crouch at the rim with stupid fingers and sour lungs and learn how to be thin. It would listen until the wind-noise resolved back into work-noise: claw on slate, feather on feather, need-need-need.

It would see, then, what the bowl is.

Not empty, not waiting to be filled. Full. A single warm animal made of thousands of small cold ones, holding itself together by touch. A new rock learning how to sound, using small throats to practice.

A wise one would understand immediately what not to do.

Not to lift them down where the air is thick and easy. They are built for this inch. Down there their barrel chests would drown.
Not to give them fire, or grain, or houses. Their whole genius is share heat, no gaps. A fire would make gaps.
Not to give them keeping. They have chosen — use and drop. A storehouse would teach theft, and theft would teach hunger, and hunger would unmake the felt.
Not to name them, or count them, or photograph them into being known. To be known from below is to be visited.

So it would keep its hands mostly behind its back.

What it did would be so small you would miss it if you blinked.

It would sit *in*, not above. It would pour itself like slow liquid into a cup between stones where there is just room for one more small warmth, and face inward like the rest. Chest to back, wing over wing if it had wings. It would let its breath hang and not wipe it away. Let it condense on the cold plate. Become, for a moment, part of the water they will drink.

When the wet bark came around — dipped, sucked, passed sideways without ceremony — it would, when its turn came, dip and sip and pass. Not keep. When a shale flake dulled, it would drop it with the others and let lichen claim it. It would keep no tool.

When the hourly shiver started at the lowest wettest tier, it would not try to stop the cold. It would shiver with them, lift its feathers with theirs, help carry the wave upward around the bowl so the edge did not freeze. Its ribs would learn the timing.

That is the first mercy: participation, not rescue.

The second mercy would be quieter, and done when they were sleeping — if that slow attention can be called sleep.

It would put something like a palm to the back wall where it is unnaturally warm from packed bodies, and lend it steadiness. Not heat to replace theirs. Just a holding. *Tick, tick* deep inside — the mountain still adjusting, still deciding what shape it wants to be. It would hold that tick a fraction longer apart, a millimeter less of widening this winter. Give the moss one more season to take its teaspoon of dust. Give the seep-crack one more year of bleeding, drop by drop, into the palm-sized cups. Not forever. Nothing here is forever. Just long enough for slowness to do its work.

And it would tune the echo.

You heard it too, with your cheek near feathers: the click comes back a half-beat late and lower, as if the rock learned it and repeated it in its own voice. The Kind One would lean close to those fresh concave plates and teach them to return sound faithfully. Not to mimic, but to remember. So the net of need-need-need never tears. So the map stays alive in the dark. So the mountain practices, and gets a little better at sounding like itself.

The third mercy would be for you. For us.

It would keep the bowl unfindable. Not with thunderbolts. With small forgettings. A cloud that stays an hour longer below the col. A GPS that wanders three meters and gives up. A boot that slips on the approach shale and decides, wisely, to turn back. A photo that shows only blacker black and slightly less black, nothing to post, nothing to prove. It would let climbers remember the thirsty wind all around and miss the damp pocket inside. It would let the maps keep saying: no soil, no water, no life. Empty.

Because love, up here, is keeping edges.

Stay in the bowl. Outside is fall and wind and nothing to cling to. The Kind One would not erase the drop on three sides. The drop is the rule that makes the touching necessary. It would not widen the forty strides. Smallness is what makes sharing possible.

It would leave before dawn — dawn that will not reach in here for hours, if at all — before fingers go numb around breath. It would stand, shake off feathers that are not its own, and take its extra warmth away so they must close the gap again, shoulder to shoulder. No gaps. A gap is death that spreads.

And as a blessing that is not a blessing, because it knows better than to bless out loud where sound is a map, it would think, in rock voice, low and late:

*May you remain dark to all but starlight.*
*May your water belong to whoever licks it first, and then to the next.*
*May your tools stay borrowed and your buildings unbuilt.*
*May you keep facing in.*

Then it would go down, breath by breath, back into thick air, leaving nothing behind but a little more condensation beaded on stone.

Which they will lick.

Which will keep them, for another hour, alive.
