It notices.

That is first. Not from far — though it could see far — but close, the way you have to see here. Kneeling. Because the air makes even a power kneel.

It has watched ten thousand valleys, greener, kinder, easier to love. This one it loves differently. Not for abundance but for exactness. For how little is wasted.

It comes at dawn, when everything is locked.

The campion domes are rimed iron-hard. The pans are skinned with ice. The pika is curled deep under stone in the dark with summer in its mouth, chewing leaf by leaf. The bee is torpid in its fur. The lammergeier is still roosting somewhere below the world. Cold lives in the stone.

A lesser kindness would want to fix it. To thicken the air so breathing is easy. To make water stay. To lengthen noon into all day. To take the pika down where winters are short and hay is tall, to tell the willow to stand up like a proper tree, to tell the weasel not to kill, to pave the dry way between cairns so feet don't stumble.

It does none of that.

Because it is wise. It understands the first law before anything else: *live low.* This life is not broken. It is fitted. The campion's tightness *is* its warmth — tear it open to make it bigger and you kill the ten degrees inside. The bee's fur holds the sun precisely because the sun is white and absolute. The pika's hay keeps precisely because nothing rots. Loosen any one thread — make it wetter, warmer, richer — and the whole huddle unravels. Ease here would be erasure.

So it refrains. That is the hardest part of benevolence: to keep your great hands still.

What it does instead looks like nothing. It is everything.

It sits. On a warm noon rock, unhurried, while the white sun crests the eastern tooth and the hollow begins to drip, tick, and unclench. It listens to micro-sound — water ticking under ice, campion capsules popping, blood in ears — and it counts that as music. Attention is its form of touch.

At eleven, when the snowbed bleeds and the pans fill to the depth of a hand, it breathes with them. When the ice skin forms and breaks from trapped air and forms again, sighing, it sighs with them. It does not keep the ice from forming at four o'clock when blue shadow falls and the temperature plunges. It stays through the plunge. That is kindness: not to spare the cold, but not to leave them alone in it.

It learns names. Not the human names — *Silene acaulis, Salix herbacea, Lagopus* — though it knows those too. The older names: this dome, fifty-two summers old, split once by a marmot claw and healed around the scar. This willow mat, older than the bothies, creeping a millimeter east toward seep. This pika, third litter of the year, who lost half a haypile to hail and rebuilt. This lichen rosette, a hundred years on the north boulder, still holding light.

And for each, a small, exact mercy — never enough to break the law, always enough to honor it.

For the bee sawing through air too thin to hold her: a steadiness under the wing. A fraction more lift. Not flight made easy, just flight made possible for three more flowers.

For the campion at two o'clock when hail rattles: it does not stop the hail. It turns one stone aside, no larger than a thumbnail, so one pink star survives to seed.

For the pika dashing between emptiness: a shadow laid a second later. A weasel's paw placed a finger's width to the left.

For the marmot sprawled belly-flat to stored heat: a deepening of the heat in that one slab, held a minute longer.

For the lichen: it lets it keep the sun. Those few minutes after the sun is gone behind the western tooth, when the orange and bone-white maps phosphoresce faintly — it lingers there. It is reluctant, with them, to let go of brightness.

For the lammergeier circling twice, looking for bone: it remembers where the old femur fell. It tilts the updraft, just so.

And for the place itself: it guards the emptiness.

Above all, it keeps the great bright volume above the floor empty for wind and wings. It turns back, gently, what would fill it — not with thunder, but with disinterest. The surveyor's eye slides off. The road-builder finds better stone elsewhere. The tourist with a loud hunger feels, inexplicably, that there is nothing here, and does not climb. The marks stay faint, scattered, respectful. The cairns marking the dry way are not kicked over. The rusted nail stays in the lintel like an offering.

Because nothing rots here, everything is remembered. Wood lasts decades. Bone lasts longer. The power adds its own memory to that: that this hollow was seen, fully, and not found wanting. That fierceness is a form of tenderness. That spacing is holy.

At night, when fog condenses in the lee of boulders and feeds the moss collars, it condenses with it. Cold sweat on cold rock. Stiff as wire at dawn, soft as flesh at noon. It does not mind the daily death. Open and shut, open and shut, a hundred times in one summer — it has learned that rhythm is not suffering. It is practice.

If you asked it, afterward, what did you do? — it would say, almost embarrassed:

Nothing much.

I knelt. I warmed one stone. I stayed past sunset in the blue.
