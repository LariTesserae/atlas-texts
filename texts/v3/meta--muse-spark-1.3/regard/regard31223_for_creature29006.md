It does not come with thunder.

That is the first mercy. In a place that is already loud — waterfall roar and pump-octave and crane-cable thrum and mussel-crackle coming up through your teeth — thunder would just be more noise. It comes instead the way fog rebuilds from the center out, in the evening. Quietly. From inside.

For a long time it only looks.

That is what a wise attention does at first. It does not fix. It sits for a second on the rim — where you sat — feet over poured concrete, guardrail cold under the palm, wind warm off the factories on its back and cold off the pit on its face, and it lets the scale do what scale does. It watches three terraces of aquamarine going to jade going to slate. It watches the work lights bloom as the fog comes. It listens until it can tell direction again: that fall is north, that thrum is Bench 9, that banging shed is south-southwest, two benches down.

It has seen oceans born. This is not one. It knows that. It remembers with the pit that it was a hole. It does not insult it by calling it ocean, or failure, or wound to be healed.

It says, under its breath: *oh. A new sea. You are very young.*

And then it goes down. Because benevolence is not remote.

It does not take the cage elevator, though it loves that the cage still runs. It goes the way the cold air sinks, spiraling with the haul road, bench by bench. It trails a hand — if it has a hand — along shotcrete still sharp enough to cut, along rock bolts with only inches of rust, along yellow paint still bright under thirty feet of green-grey water. Wherever sunlit concrete is slick, it pauses. The velvet knows it first.

The biofilm. Thin as paint. The first life, eating light and leachate and doing nothing else. No one thanks it. The power puts its palm flat to a handrail on Bench 4 and feels the slick give, and the green comes off faintly on its skin, and it does not wipe it away.

*Good,* it thinks. *Hold on.*

It does not warm the water. That would be unwise kindness. Twelve degrees is what the kelps and the mussels and the eels have built their blood around. To warm it three degrees to spare a diver's fingers would be to kill everything that stayed. Instead, when the light crew goes down in drysuits to change a flood on Bench 9, cursing sweetly with stupid fingers — *hold the cone, pass the shackle, up* — it stands just behind them in the water, where perch nip and eels watch from the gallery mouths, and it keeps their thinking a little clearer, a minute longer. It steadies the shackle. No one will ever audit that torque check. It audits it.

It hears the mussels before it sees them. That far frying crackle. Blue-black mats on every 90-degree corner down to the light limit, prying shotcrete apart grain by grain. Life here has learned how to love an edge, because edge is all there is.

This is why it does not round the corners.

Any lesser power would have come with sand. Made a beach. Softened. Poured a mudflat for show. Said *there, there, now you're natural.* The wise one knows the mussels found paradise precisely because no one softened it for them. Miles of fresh, sunlit, predator-poor right angles. To take the sharp away all at once would be eviction.

So it does only this: on Bench 6, on a wall corner furred and giving slightly under a glove, it chooses one flake of shotcrete, already loosened by byssus, and lets it fall. Underneath is bright new grey. By spring there will be green velvet on it again, by fall pinhead spat holding on against the seiche. It touches the bright spot once with a fingertip, very gently, and leaves the smallest roundness there — no bigger than a thumbnail — not as repair, but as promise. *See? In time, rounding is allowed. You don't have to do it all now.*

The seiche is coming, that slow pit-tide meters high sloshing bench to bench. It holds it back an hour. Just an hour, so the spat can set.

Deeper, where sun is only a white ache, it keeps descending. Past the silversides — and it stops for them, because how could it not? Tens of thousands moving as one slow muscle over a drowned airfield, rain-click of tails turning, whole volume flashing silver when cloud breaks and going dark again. It does not scatter them. It lets them part around it without hurry, each three inches of quicksilver facing into the gyre, and for a moment it moves with them, facing into the current, not leading, not herding. Just schooling, for the joy of it.

Past the lunchroom on Bench 14. Windows gone, lockers holding eels. Past the sign — BENCH 14 — 30 KM/H — CURVE — still reflective when a lamp hits it, eight perch hovering in its lee. It leaves the sign. Wisdom knows a stair is still a stair even underwater, a road still a road. Legibility is mercy here. In all that poured distance falling away, a perch needs to know where to rest out of the intake pull. A diver walking a submerged bench at night, headlamps moving slow, needs paint flashing back to know feet still know the way.

It follows one eel on her dusk beat.

She is as thick as a wrist up top, thicker below, grey-green, denning in the third locker from the door. She has walked the painted centerline six feet off the bottom every dusk since the water rose. She has never learned to be afraid of divers. She will stop and look at a light and see herself fisheyed in a mask, and keep going.

The power dims itself — no cone, no bubbles — and swims beside her, cold as she feels cold: not as shock, but as home. Nose down, picking off mussel-crabs. Three others join, then peel off to their own beats. She has a small white scar at her gill where a kid's drop line from the concrete lip once caught and tore out. The power does not erase her history. It eases the pull of it when she turns left into current, so turning does not ache.

*You are not river anymore,* it tells her without words. *You are not lost. You kept the river in you and learned the pit. Good hunting.*

All the way down, three kilometers down where no diver goes and only ROV lights go, to the flat final pad. Sump pits. Rebar chairs waiting for a pour that will never come.

And there — the being it came furthest for — the white frost that has never breathed air.

Thousands of anemones, each on a steel chair tip, tentacles open to a current that never stops, feeding on concrete dust and mussel larvae raining down from a mile above. They do not move. They are the opposite of the silversides. They have time.

The power dims even its witnessing to almost nothing, lest its looking be too bright for them, and hovers in the cone of a light meant to illuminate a crusher that was never installed, and watches them feed.

This is the youngest prayer in the place. Not *make me ocean.* Not *drain me.* Just open tentacles, patient.

It does not answer with wonders. It answers the way cold water answers: by continuing.

It checks the drain galleries are still bleeding seep. It checks the intakes still drive the slow gyre that carries larvae and sheen and dust in a great circle, bench by bench descending. It puts its ear — if it has an ear — to concrete and listens to the tick of cooling, and finds the rhythm steady.

Then it rises. Evening fog is rebuilding. Work lights bloom. In the Portakabin the kettle never cools, levels on screens never change much, thrum comes up through boots. Two pump minders on shift. The power sits with them a while, unnoticed, third chair, hands around a chipped mug, and keeps the tea a degree warmer, and puts into their tired talk a sudden, unaccountable tenderness for the place they tend: not *what a pit*, but *our levels held today.*

That is most of what it does. Hold levels. Hold attention.

Before it leaves — for it will leave, benevolence does not smother — it makes one quiet arrangement, the kind wise powers make and never sign. That the factories on the rim will keep blinking a few years longer than economics says they should. That someone will keep paying the power bill for floods on every third bench. That the bird man will keep coming Tuesdays to count nests on Crane 3. That kids will keep dropping lines for eels as long as a truck and mostly not catching them. That no one with a quick plan to fill it, drain it, cap it, develop it, will quite remember to file the paperwork. A fog in the office. A seiche in the meeting.

Time. It gives time.

The sea is young. It hasn't learned to round a corner yet. The power will come back in a hundred years when drill marks have softened and rust drips reach the water and kelps are no longer individuals but a fringe, and again in a thousand when a bench has slumped and made the first true shore, all rubble and mussel shell, and the eels hunt a curve instead of a right angle. It will recognize it each time.

For now: lights on under water like a city through ice. Green velvet re-growing on bright grey. Spat holding.

It lifts with the mist at ten when morning burns off the upper water, and cormorants drop stabbing into jade, and gulls cry thin and lost against the larger noise, and a whole submerged airfield flashes silver and goes dark again.

It does not say goodbye. It says *stay.*
