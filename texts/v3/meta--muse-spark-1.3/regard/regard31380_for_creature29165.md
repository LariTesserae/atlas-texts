It would come in plain daylight.

Not in amber, not in blue glare. No voice from the tor. No mending of heaven. A benevolent and wise power, if it turned its looking toward this shelf, would know at once that glory would break it. So it would come the way they come to each other: quietly, on foot, from behind.

It would stand first at the upper cairn and see the whole within one look — the wall, the drop, the fell at the back, the terraces stopping — and it would understand this is not a ruin and not a beginning. It is a kept thing.

And it would be tempted, the way all kindness is at first tempted.

To warm them. Their cuffs are always damp at the edge. Their boots are dark at the toe. The children steam faintly by the hearth without speaking. To dry them, once and for all.

To fill them. Potatoes, turnips, leeks boiled long. Bread divided without counting. To give abundance, a deep well that never needs mist, a road down that stays open in winter.

To fill the empty houses. Fifteen, sixteen roofed, thirty people at most. Houses with no fire, swept anyway. To send more souls up, laughing loud souls, to close the gaps of pale grass and bare gravel so no one hears their own steps too loudly.

It would feel all that rise in itself — and then, being wise, it would set it down.

For it would have watched by the third day, as anyone who stays does. Watched the boy with the bent stick clearing only the gutter that needs it, working downhill toward the court. Watched the woman ladle and *not* fill to the top, leaving the measured inches. Watched two or three take down a bulging wall without hurry and rebuild it with the same stones to the same height, lichen-side up, replacing the stone carefully over the small black beetle. Watched the old man and the child sit on the bench stone not touching, looking down to where the terraces stop.

It would understand: this is not poverty waiting for rescue. This is a vow renewed daily. Take only what mist and rain and seep will replace. Maintain more than you inhabit. Leave room for wind and quiet.

To give them more would be to take that away. To heal the white pit-scar, the thumb flattened sideways from pressing slabs, would be to erase the record of care. To crowd the gaps would be to deafen them.

So it would do almost nothing. And that almost would be everything.

It would learn the maintenance.

It would stoop a little — for it is very large and the lanes are narrow and turn at right angles — and sweep the lane in front of a door because that lane is its to sweep while it is there. It would carry water without spilling, elbows in, eyes on the rim. It would leave the measured inches in the cistern. It would step where it is iced, not where it skins. At night it would listen for the seep in the back rock, drop by drop into its basin, to know the hill is still well.

It would sit. On baking days it would stand about the mouth of the oven with hands in pockets, saying little, watching the soot darken. In the morning it would wait with its bucket while another bucket is filled, and nod. In the evening, if the wind dropped, it might sit a breath on the far end of the bench stone, not close, making no shadow, while an old man and a child look down.

And being benevolent, it would do the few things only such a power can do, in ways that do not appear as doing:

It would keep the mist coming. One more night of thick mist in a dry week, that leaves every lintel dripping by dawn, moss to be wrung, barrels to tick over. Nothing miraculous. Just weather, continuing.

It would keep the seep seeping. Longer than the houses have stood, longer still.

It would stand, unseen, in the lengths of pale grass between houses — in the emptiness they keep on purpose — and hold them open. When the world below, which to them feels hypothetical, has ideas — a track, a mast, a bright jacket that wants to map and name and improve — it would gently turn that eye aside. Let the maps forget a little. Let the path stay hypothetical a little longer. Not hiding them. Just letting them be unnoticed, as they wish.

It would look up when the ravens cross at the same hour, croaking once. It would leave the late few rowan berries for them.

It would remember the names that are used less and then not, when someone is taken down below where the ground is deeper and the house is kept anyway. It would hold those careful hands after other careful hands have replaced them. To be fully known and not altered — that is a kind of blessing few receive.

And then it would leave no sign it had been there. No new field, no new house, no doorway that opened only onto earth suddenly opened elsewhere. The barrel full to the brim and ticking over. The cup on its rope at the cistern wet. Smoke thinning up from two or three chimneys and taken at once by wind, so you can't be sure you saw it.

Only this, perhaps: for a winter the children's coughs a little looser, the old joints a little less seized by cold; for a spring the potatoes a thumb's width larger within their small leaves; for a summer a wind that finds the gaps in shutters a fraction kinder on the back of the neck. Nothing to count. Nothing to accumulate. Season turning into season without accumulation.

Ordered like a city and empty like a moor. The power would not change that order. It would kneel inside it for a while, and keep it, and go on keeping it from afar.

To hold a small, high, cool, damp shelf exactly in place — it would see that is enough, and find that enough, and help it continue.
