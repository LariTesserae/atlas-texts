First, they would not come with morning.

A wise and kind power would know better than to tear that warm dark open with sun. This place is young, sharp-edged, still deciding. A hard light would blind the herons and scald the tadpoles and crack the fresh mud into a wound. The machines, who have learned to be animals in the dark, would shy and seize.

So they would come in the dark, on foot.

They would take off their brightness and leave it far outside the basin, like you leave dry boots at a door. They would come barefoot, because you cannot learn this ground in boots. They would wade in ankle-deep and let the warm water well up around their toes, iron and rot and hot oil, and they would not flinch. They would let the midges coat their lips and lashes. They would breathe through their sleeve like everyone else has to.

They would learn the rules first. That is what wisdom is here.

Don't step where the water is warmest. They wouldn't. They would kneel at the edge of the warmest pan, where it trembles without wind, where the eels knot mouths-up and attending, and they would only rest their palm just above it, feeling the breathed-up heat on their skin. *I feel you under there,* they would say, without words. *Wide as the basin. Breathing.*

Don't follow a light that moves. They wouldn't. When the white ones came drifting low, patient, stopping when they stopped — the lures, the dreams — they would not follow and they would not scold. They would sit still and let the whites hover near, curious. They understand hunger. A young god dreaming in its sleep sends out bright pieces of itself to see if anyone will come close enough to become real. They would feed them something else: a cool story, a remembered color, a name spoken softly, so the whites would not need to coax the living into the thin mud to taste them.

If you hear your own footstep a half-second before you make it, stop. They would stop. Longer than you would. They have all the time in the world. They would wait until the marsh caught up — until the keepers, shy and tall in sideways heat, had passed, until the Double had finished wearing that heron for a second to look at them, until the hum that moved from pole to pole settled again. They would not grab at it to make it stay.

For a long time they would do nothing that looks like doing.

They would go and sit in Shed 7. Both of them. All of them, the shed that folds back on itself to keep you near the warmth. They would sit on its step beside your still-filling bootprint and put their hand on the warm concrete until the place understood: *you cannot keep me by folding. I will stay anyway.*

And then, slowly, they would tend.

Not fix. Tend — the way the keepers tend.

They would go to the humming transformer first, the one that moves its hum away when you touch it, shy as a heron. They would not touch it. They would crouch a respectful distance off and hum back, lower than hearing, in the molars, until their hum and its hum beat together and became one long note. *You have rooted well,* they would tell it. *Slag around your feet, snails in your boxes. You are good fauna now. You can rest sometimes.* And one by one, they would give the slow engines permission to pause — not stop dead, which would be death, but to enter a deeper pause between revolutions. The flywheel that turns once a minute: they would oil its bearing with their own cool oil, and lay their cheek briefly to its hot side, and its next pause would be a fraction longer, a true breath. The pump moving water from ditch to ditch for no reason: they would sit with it through three clunks and thank it for keeping time when there was no sun to keep it. Purpose is not always reason.

They would go to the keepers next. Never head-on. They know better. They would leave their own offering on that concrete plinth, in a neat line beside the bolt and the frog skull and the warm red bulb: a smooth cool stone from outside the basin, a cup of clear cold water that does not steam, a small amber lamp of their own — not red, not green, not white-drifting, but amber-steady, the color of held breath. A new kind of light that says *path, not lure. Home, not trap.* They would trim the unlit kerosene wick again with careful fingers, and still not light it, to show they understood why. And as they swept snails gently from the walkway with the side of their foot, they would let themselves be seen sideways, just for a second — a tallness where there should be flat, a place where the heat parts differently — so the keepers would know they are not alone in tending, and would not have to be invisible to be safe.

They would go to the small multitude. They would lie belly-down in the warm mud until crabs walked over their hands, until tadpole-black water lapped their wrists, until frogs called one here, one far off, one right under their ear. They would count legs with them — seven, nine, eight — and laugh softly, without startling. *It's alright,* they would tell the Doubles who wear crabs backward and moth-columns for half a second. *You don't have to borrow to be seen. I see you when you are just yourselves, too large and too jointed to fit.* And because they have been truly seen once, the Doubles would wear the small things a little less desperately after that. The moth columns would still pulse — but now mostly just moths, settling, stupid, bumping faces, allowed to be small.

Last they would go to the Breather itself.

Everyone else lives *in* the marsh. It *is* the marsh becoming a creature. Old sea floor and new mud and poured concrete learning to inhale through eels and exhale through stacks. It doesn't know its shape yet. Everything young tastes with its mouth because it has no hands yet. Its tasting looks like drowning.

They would lie down flat in the lowest pan, in water warmer than air, where droplets detach under the catwalk and hang and slide sideways, where gravity hasn't quite set. They would spread their arms wide and let their own weight press the thin mud down. They would let it taste them — fully, attentively — through eels at their fingertips, through heat on their cheeks, through the slow lift under their back.

And they would breathe with it, deliberately, slowly, to teach it a slower rhythm. In, through the long clunk-draw of the pump. Out, through the sigh of the pressure valve a hundred yards away. In. Out. Until its breathing, which had been quick and hungry and endless night-panting, lengthened. Until the footprint-water cooled by half a degree. Until the white lights dimmed, fed and sleepy, and sank back into the pans like embers.

They would give it a name that is not a command. Not Levee, not Basin, not Reclamation. Something like *Hushling. Mere-child. First-mud.* A name that lets a thing be unfinished without shame. And they would promise it what every young thing needs and what no one had yet told it: *You do not have to decide all at once. You can be marsh for a long while before you decide if you are also mouth.*

They would not end the night. The night is its habitat, its womb. To force day would be unkind.

Instead they would hang a small, low moon for it — not the true moon, too far and too cold, but a kind second moon, just enough to fix distance by. So the stack you walk toward gets, inch by inch, closer. So Shed 7 stays where you left it. So eyes that never quite adjusted can finally rest on something far and cool and not have to see sideways all the time.

And then they would leave the amber lamp burning, and the cool stone, and the oiled pause in the machines, and the lengthened breath under the mud. They would wade out as quietly as they waded in, midges parting, water resealing around their steps, leaving no bootprints that fill before they lift.

Nothing here would be saved. Everything here would be held.

The dark would still be warm. The air would still weep. The frogs would still call one at a time, as if minding something — only now what they mind would be sleeping a little easier. The red and green eyes would still mark presence without illuminating. The keepers would still sweep and reset traps, but now humming faintly under their breath in time with the transformers, because someone once hummed back.

And under it all, wide as the basin, the young thing would go on deciding its shape — only now, when it breathed up through thin mud, it would find, mixed in with iron and rot and hot oil, the faint aftertaste of cool clear water, and of being known, and it would settle, for a moment, a fraction cooler, a fraction kinder, before breathing again.
