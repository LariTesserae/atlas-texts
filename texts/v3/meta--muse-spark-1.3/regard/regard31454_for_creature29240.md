It would not come like weather.

A benevolent and wise power has learned how to arrive. No light in the sky. No voice. No shadow falling across the polygons. It would come as attention first — a long, unblinking noticing from very far away, and then, only when it was sure of the rules, closer.

And the first rule it would learn is: *do not bring gifts from elsewhere.*

It would want to — that is what makes it benevolent. It would see the cracked lips of the human witness, the salt cuffs on the old gliders' wings that they cannot preen off because there is no water to preen with, the reeds shattering themselves white with their own discordance, the moths with half-eaten sails still flying, and its heart, if you can call it that, would ache in the way hearts do: *let me give them ease. Let me give them rain. Let me still the roar so they can rest.*

And its wisdom, which is older than its heart, would stop its hand.

Because it would listen, and understand: rain here would be murder. To bring water to a place that has unlearned wetness so completely that wetness is not even a memory in the stones, would be to melt the aerogel lattices, to short the piezoelectric stalks, to drown the glass-mites, to rot the dry feet. Stillness here would be famine. To quiet the roar to give them peace would be to starve the green that eats shaking, and then the herd that eats the green, and then the sieves that strain the herd. Even to heal too much would be harm. The fallen arch that holds that terrifying pocket of perfect cancellation where you can hear your own blood — that pocket is also a nursery. The flutter-mice hang their static there to comb themselves clean.

So it would do what almost no power can bear to do: almost nothing. And do it completely.

First, it would make itself small and dry and trembling.

It would not enter the bowl as a mountain. It would enter as a tremor among tremors. It would fold its vastness down until it could sit, unnoticed, on the rim among the brittle paper skeletons where the song frays into mere wind. It would let its own hum go quiet and frayed. It would let the cold dry air crack whatever skin it wears. It would let the moths test it and reject it. It is polite to be rejectable.

Then it would listen. Not for a moment. For a long time. Longer than any human witness could stay with teeth aching and fingers stupid. It would listen until the held chord stopped being one chord and resolved into what it really is: a hundred close tones beating, and under them the slow thump of the hollow mountain, and over them the tearing overtone of ten thousand wings deliberately out of phase, and under that the *tick tick tick* of growing tips, and under that the faint crackle of lichen on black glass.

A wise power knows: you cannot love what you have not heard in detail.

And when it had heard, it would answer — very quietly, in kind.

Not a new song imposed from above. A descant. It would find a sliver of the spectrum the seven ribs left empty, where no frond was yet tuned because the old engineers left a gap, and it would fill it for a little while with its own pressure. A low, warm thrumming, just at the edge of the reeds' ability to eat. The nearest clumps would lean toward it, minutely. You know that lean. You felt them lean toward your own warm discordant mass. They would lean toward this warmer, more consonant mass, and shiver, and light faintly from within, copper-sweet, and taste something they have never tasted: a shaking that is not wind-shaped but mind-shaped, that carries no dust, only intention.

It would let them eat a little of its voice. That would be its gift — not sustenance, just savor. Enough to make sugar, not enough to make dependence.

And while they ate, it would do small, precise mercies. The kind that do not change the system, only ease a life inside it.

It would hover, without touching, over that one old glider with salt at its wing joints, the one whose gut-pellet still glows pink when it banks but whose turns are getting wide and tired. It would let a null form around it the way a null forms under the fallen arch, and let the field comb the old wings clean in crackling bursts. Just that. No new wings. No youth. Just clean joints for another season of endless eights.

It would settle, without weight, over a bald scar where a clump shattered white, and hold the falling shards electrostatically for a second longer than air would, so the neighbors could catch more of them. No resurrection. Just fewer lost.

It would press, if it has a palm, to the drumhead center where you pressed yours, and feel the slow heart-beat under the fast roar. And it would answer that beat with its own slower beat, for a few cycles, so the mountain, which is alive the way a drum is alive when played, would feel, for once, played *with* instead of just played upon.

That is all.

It would not right the tilted pylon. The tilt is tuning now; dust falls up there, and the lichen is thickest there, bearded with salt. It would not scrape the lichen off the frost-like circuitry. The lichen is dulling and sharpening in the right measure. It would not speak to the reeds in words, because words are wet things, full of breath and spit.

When it left — and it would leave, because to stay would be to become another rib, another permanent source, and the bowl is exactly as large as it should be — it would do one last benevolent thing, which is also wise:

It would make the ridge a little harder to find.

Not invisible. Never invisible; wise powers do not steal places from the world. Just uninteresting from a distance. A bent probability. A suggestion to human boots and mining surveys and well-meaning terraformers that this dent is empty, that there is no water here, nothing to take, nothing to fix. Let the clouds stay below. Let the blue distance fall and fall beyond the teeth. Let the hamlet that eats noise go on starving for nothing but vibration.

From the rim, looking back down for the last time, it would see what you saw: pale dent, wafer leaves, handkerchief hawks, vast sky. Lush and teeming and almost empty.

Only the roar would be, for a few minutes after, almost imperceptibly sweeter — as if the air remembered being listened to.

Then even that would fade back into the permanent chord, and the reeds would flash green to white to green, and the herd would condense screaming over a sweet patch, and the dust devils would wander the bare polygons endlessly, collapsing and reforming.

As it was. As it should be. Witnessed, once, wholly, and left alone.
