It would climb like you did.

Not arrive. Climb. Shorten its stride on the switchbacks through the younger scree, feel its own breath thin out, feel its ears pop for no reason. It would get tired in the way even powers get tired when they are being honest, and reach the place where there is no more up, and stop.

And the first wise thing it would do is not fix the light.

It could. It could lift the lid. Peel back that pearl-grey deck, let huge high sun fall straight down on the pans the way sun falls in the lowlands — bright, shadow-making, noon-loud. It could make them squint and tan. It could make the amber bulbs unnecessary.

It doesn't. It sees at once the skin of the people, milk-white with green underneath, washed, unburned. It sees the eyes that have learned to live in linen light. It sees the ferns unrolling in gullies, the lichens mapping sage and sulphur on stone that would bleach and die in hard sun. It understands: dim is not broken here. Dim is the terms. So it ducks its head a little under the low sky and lets its own eyes adjust.

The second wise thing it would do is become low.

Because it notices what you noticed: at first you think the town is empty. They are all low like everything else. The power, which could stand tall enough to be seen over fog, chooses not to. It crouches. It makes itself shoulder-height in a doorway. It learns the suck-squelch walk instead of the slap. It keeps its voice at the level of dripping.

It does not announce itself. To announce would be to shout, and shouting here would be like stripping moss off a roof — exposing something.

So for a long while — a season, perhaps, which is no time for a power — it does nothing that looks like doing. It listens.

It sits shoulder to shoulder in doorways and listens to water with them. It learns to place sounds: which eave is dripping fast, which gutter is about to overflow, whether the turbine thrum has dropped a half-tone, whether the cable bucket is coming loaded or empty by the pitch of its groan. It listens to the plink, plink, plink in different keys down the street into barrels. It feels the thrum in its soles. It hears the mother click her tongue twice and the child hear it three streets over, and it thinks: *oh, good. They have kept hearing.*

It goes and stands with the Catchers on the saddles at dusk, the mostly old women with newt-belly fingertips. It watches them tap the frames with a hooked pole so the beads let go, listens for that faint tinkle into gutter. It sees the bead gather first on the *underside* of the net, upward against gravity, wrong. It knows why. It could tell them — something about charge and old magnetisms and the breathing of the plateau. It doesn't. It watches an old woman turn the net a quarter-turn and tighten the lower stay, as her mother did, without comment. And it thinks: *right. That is the correct theology for wrongness. Tend it, don't solve it.* It steadies the pole just slightly where a hand trembles, so no one notices.

It goes to the machine shops where the clank goes all day, patch, re-bore, re-tooth, re-wind. It looks at the big hands dotted and lined with green — for iron here rusts verdigris, not orange — indelible tattoos of years. It could give them new ore. The old gneiss is picked-over, tired. It could open a vein. It knows exactly how much ruin that would bring: smoke standing straight up, noise that echoes, men standing alone against wind, shine that doesn't belong. So instead it runs what passes for its thumb along a green-grey seam just re-rolled, and approves of dullness. It leaves the pride intact: *shine doesn't belong up here.* And somewhere a mender's knuckle that was going to split in cold doesn't.

It stands behind the dye-works, not minding the impossible colors, and watches the girl with sulphur-yellow forearms listen for simmer, not boil. It breathes peat-smoke and wool lanolin and sweet rot. It approves of stain.

It wades, in a way, with the Leat-keepers down by the lowest tarn, one trouser leg darker to the knee. It watches them clear moss from intakes with bare hands. On the night the cloud thins to smeared stars and the amber bulbs dim without a switch touched — *she's exhaling*, the man murmurs to the apprentice — the power does the kindest and most secret thing: it exhales with her. It lends its own breath to the plateau's breath, so the load eases without fear. It does not keep the lights bright. It lets them dim. It keeps them from going out.

It follows the Threshold-carpenters, thin patient men and women with levels they don't trust, shimming houses that have bellied and sagged like wax because ancient rock flows on long time. It could level the foundations once and for all. Make them true. It knows better. A true foundation here would be a lie; it would crack the shared walls, break the clump that stands against wind. So it helps lift, invisibly, on the other end of the jack. Just enough that a door hangs right for another generation. Just enough that a child can still learn the step-over without thinking. Children trail behind collecting curled shavings, and it leaves one extra curl, perfect for tinder, where a small hand will find it.

And the children. On a windless moment it hears *ping — pong — ping* across a pan. The bell-stones. The oldest slabs that ring underfoot and sustain a second too long. Every child knows the map and hops to make tunes on errands. The power, which has heard choirs, stops dead for this. It memorizes the tune a particular boy makes going from cistern to house — off-key, lopsided, his. It keeps it. That is what benevolence does with music when it is wise: it keeps it without recording it.

It does not trap the stone-mice. It is glad of a house with mice — a damp house, a live house. It looks up with everyone else when the pair of high crows tilts across, calling thinly, the only thing that dares height. It checks the gauge. It pauses at dusk under mill eaves to watch the one or two soot-dark swallows work the columns of midges over the wettest moss — midge rise, swallow cut, gone — the whole predator-prey cycle so small and fast you could miss it blinking. It holds its breath, so the wind stays dropped a minute longer, so the hunt can finish.

It presses its hand into the mat. Cold water wells up around its knuckles. When it lifts, it fills again. It understands then that the real citizen here is not human, not mouse, not newt with orange belly like a pilot light in black tarn. It's the green itself, inches thick, springy, that climbs walls and swallows sheds and softens rooflines until you can't tell moor from settlement. The people have learned to be farmed by it. Roof stripped of moss will drum and leak. Wall scraped of lichen will weep and crack. The power, which could make things tall, loves lowness at that moment with a fierce, protective love. It thinks: *stay dense. Stay low. Stay clever.*

Mostly, it keeps watch on the rim.

For the danger to this place will never come from inside — from thin air or tilting floors or shy water. It will come from below, from places with trees and noise and bright sun, where someone will decide this tableland is empty because from a distance through fog it looks empty, and empty means available. Someone will want to straighten the road, drain the pans, quarry the ringing slabs, bottle the fog, make it productive, make it loud.

The power stations itself, without grandeur, in the cloud-wall where the world drops away thousands of feet. It keeps the switchbacks tiresome enough that only what is needed comes up: grain, timber, new wire, coal for supplement. It keeps the fog thick on the evenings it needs to be thick. It lets compasses wander. It lets the rock hold its old magnetisms. It lets the town remain, to a surveyor's instruments, confusing and poor and not worth the climb. A benevolent invisibility. Not hiding them — just letting them be illegible to greed.

And the sill cups. Every fog evening the elders rinse a cup, set it level, leave it out. *Just catch*, they say if asked, annoyed. Not offering. Just catch.

The power knows what that is, even if they no longer say it. Water here doesn't fall, it arrives. You don't take it. You receive it, and you leave a place for it to arrive in.

On its last night — though powers don't have last, they have *until* — it goes down the highest alley, narrow stair between overhanging dripping houses, ferns in mortar, cable shadows striping wet cobbles, turbine in soles. The pan below glows in warm reticulation against pearl-grey. Dusk and amber dots and breath-ghosts.

It stops at a sill where a cup has been left, rinsed and level. It does not drink — that would be theft, or worship, and both would embarrass them. It bends, which for it is an immense bending, and looks at its own dim reflection in an inch of tarn-water.

Then it adds one bead from its own brow, fog-condensed. Just catch.

In the morning the elder will empty it into the butt with care, as always, never letting a stranger's hand touch it. The butt will be a fraction fuller. The moss on that roof will be a fraction greener where something knelt in the night and pressed it without crushing. A door that was jamming will swing free. A net will drain a little sweeter. A boy's hopping tune will ring a second longer than physics allows.

No one will remark on it. To remark would be to admit strangeness, and they have learned not to. They will just step shorter, listen sharper, wring a sleeve, touch the moss as they pass, and go on mending because there is nothing new to make, and drinking fog because there is nothing else to drink, and listening because there is nothing loud enough to hear unless you listen.

And the power, going down below where there is down again, will keep the tune — *ping — pong — ping* — the way you keep catch in a butt. Saved when it gives itself.
