I would not come down like weather.

If I came at all to that crack on the top edge of the world, I would have to make myself small first. Shed the sky. Leave thunder and bright rescue behind on the ridge. You cannot enter a hundred-meter lung shouting. You learn that before your first step down the iron staples: line your feet with felt.

So I would come quiet.

I have seen what lives there. Not a thousand people and ten thousand animals and a hundred machines. One thing. A warm amber animal made of hands and paws and wings and pulleys and furnace mouths, wedged in stone, breathing together because it has to. The town is the meadow. The town is the waterhole with no water. Everything lives off the leak — heat, crumbs, grease, breath-moisture. Even the moths live their whole lives around a single bulb.

I could give them so much, so easily. That is the temptation of power.

I could lower the mountain. I could split the rock and bring a river up. I could plant a valley green in a day. I could give every child new lungs, wide as sails, that would drink that thin air like lowland air. I could stop the rockfall forever.

And in a week they would be dead. Not their bodies — *them*. The barrel ribs like the dogs. The gait bent forward, mouth shut, breathe through nose, do not talk on stairs. The bazaar in fingers and eyebrows. The morning round of wringing rags. The dogs who learned before the men not to bark. The choughs who stopped calling. The whole intricate, suffocating, brilliant fit — *everything fits because it has to* — would spring open and spill out. Abundance would kill what scarcity taught to live.

A wise benevolence does not rescue a hive by breaking it.

So I would do almost nothing. And what I did, no one would be able to prove.

I would lie down in the slot with them and sync my breath.

You noticed it already: the birds all bank at the same instant, as if the air tilted. The lamps breathe together when the generator surges. Falling rock falls inward, toward the center, never outward off the mountain. The old porters tap their chests and say, stay low when the wind sucks. Never build out, only in.

That inward pull — that is where I would put my hand. Not to hold them still, but to keep the room holding. A little more cohesion in the fractured basalt. A refrigerator-sized block still falls — it must, rockfall is delivery — but the mountain-side fall, the one that would unzip the whole west wall in the night and bury the winch-houses and the sleeping drawers three-high to a mattress, that one I would quietly not allow. No one would thank me. They would just rebuild a shed in the morning in a slightly different configuration, and go on.

I would sit with the water.

Water is a ration, a rumor. They farm breath on chimney collars and roof rags. I have endless breath to spare. At night, when the pocket glows and the blue dusk settles and everyone sleeps in shifts with rag-hung doors, I would lean close to the tin beards of frost and breathe a little heavier upon them. A few drops more to wring in the morning. Not a cistern. Never a cistern. Just enough that the old woman twisting with both hands watches the thin brownish trickle run a second longer, and frowns, and thinks the night was damp. Just enough that a relay porter coming up from the glacier two days down with grooved shoulders feels his sealed drum slosh a little less desperately.

I would ease the night headaches. The newcomers vomiting quietly behind sheds, the children with blood forever close to the surface under dark red-brown faces, the old porters whose chests boom like drums and still cannot get enough — I would not take the thinness away. I would sit inside their sleep and slow it. In deep sleep, when the brain swells against altitude and pounds behind the eyes, I would lend them my own larger lung for an hour. Let them dream of easy air, of laughing out loud without cost, of running without counting steps. Then wake them still themselves, lips cracked, throats sore, but rested enough to go doubled-over up the almost-ladders again.

I would learn their manners. Dogs communicate by shoulder-bumps and breath. So would I. A warmth in the ash under the winch-houses where the dogs pile and breathe together — I would be the warmest spot in the pile, and no dog would bark at me. A steadiness in the bridging cable when a child darts across with armfuls of coal-bricks, mouth clamped shut, eyes bright — I would be the lack of sway. A handful more dead moths in the oil pan for the rats. A marrow that holds a little longer under a finch's beak on the dump terrace.

And I would keep them hidden.

From ten paces away you would swear the ridge was solid. Let it stay so. Let the wind scour the ridge clean. Let the clouds stay a mile below, burning white in a sun they never get. Let the satellites see only white-shouldered, black-ribbed massif. Let the prospectors put their boot wrong and step over. Only the burning-lunged, the lost, the willing, who are already on the very top edge and willing to go quiet — let them find the lip where earth drops out from under the eyes. Never an army. Never a road.

If they ask for me — and they will not ask in words, because shouts die ten feet out and what carries bounces — they will ask in the way they already do: Never waste a breath. Never shout. Never build out, only in.

I would answer in kind: I will not waste your breath. I will not shout. I will not build out.

I will build in, with you.
