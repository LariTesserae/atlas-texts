It would come. That is the first thing.

Not down, as you would expect a power to come. Up — up the black flank of the mountain through dark empty water, two kilometers of climb, until the light starts to thicken and the snow starts to fall and there at the top is the small bright bowl, rimmed with knives, loud inside its teeth.

It would not bring anything with it. No tools, no plan, no green seed to "improve" the place. A wise benevolence knows the first rule here: everything is either erupted or grown. Nothing is made. So it would not make.

It would hang in the cool water fifteen meters below the sky, and slow down.

That is what it would do, if anything. Slow down until a minute is a minute again, until a crab lifting a claw over the course of a minute is not slow at all but on time. It would let its own vast time thin out until it could fit inside the room, rim to rim, sixty meters across, and stay there.

It would not soften the rock. This will tempt it — briefly. Those flakes and shards and needles, the quenched glass that shreds anything soft, the chimney that shattered into knives. A benevolent reflex wants to sand the edges, to give them cobbles, to make it safe to put a hand down. Wisdom will stop its hand. The knives are why the worms build tubes molecule by molecule, why the crabs fold like jackknives, why the anemones deform into hexagons to hold on. The sharpness is the discipline that taught them to touch so precisely. To round the world before they are ready would unteach them.

It would not give them space. Crowding is mandatory here, and it will feel cruel at first even to a god — mouth on mouth, dish on dish, tube on tube, larvae caught and recaught in the doughnut because outside the rim the mountain falls away into nothing they can use. *Let me widen the bowl,* benevolence thinks. *Let me open a notch and let some of the children out.* And wisdom answers: there is nowhere out there for a millimeter mouth that needs both sun-snow from above and stone-milk from below. To live here is to live touching. To get room, something has to die. It would not cheat that bargain. It would watch an old anemone let go, and a new white column grow up through its scar, and understand that is mercy enough.

It would not give them eyes. Not to the white blind coin-crabs polishing their patches of basalt until they shine like gauges. In noon-bright alpine sun, to choose blindness looks like a wound to heal. It isn't. Sight is noise in a snowstorm. They navigate by charge, by mesh of tooth to tooth, by taste of iron and sulfur precipitating as tiny endless lightning. To give them eyes would blind them. So it would, instead, learn their map. It would learn to feel the shimmer with its whole skin, metallic on the tongue like blood and batteries, and find the polished electrodes one by one in the dark-electric field.

It would not quiet the factory.

The grinding surge through the stone flues, the hissing chimneys, the clank of slabs settling a millimeter, the rivet-gun snapping of ten thousand shrimp cavitating water into heat and shock — felt more in the sternum than heard — it is very loud. Benevolence wants lullabies. Wisdom listens longer. The noise *is* the food, the speech, the echo-location off sharp walls that lets blind things know where the walls are. Each mirrored bladder flashing on-off, on-off as it fires is a warning light saying *I am here, you are here, we are still here.* It would not hush them. It would add its own listening to the echo, a third and fourth bounce before the sound escapes, so the bowl for a moment is even louder inside than it should be, even more itself.

What would it actually *do*?

Almost nothing you could photograph. No wall, no wreck, no cable, no cut stone.

It would hold station like a shrimp in the curtain, beating invisible swimmerets one two three against the current, parting very slowly around anything that passes.

It would turn a mouth, with the anemones on the high terrace, up into the milky fall, and take what lands — a flake of iron, a mote of snow — and close, deliberate as an eyelid, over half a minute.

It would piston, with the worms, out of phase with its neighbors so the ledge ripples, combing second-pass water for sulfur-eating bacteria that breathe electricity.

It would mesh, when it meets another gear-crab in a crack, edge to edge, tooth to tooth, turn a quarter-turn and separate, exchanging film. A greeting made of gears.

Mostly it would pay the kind of attention that is a form of keeping. Stay long enough for the curtain to forget you. Learn individuals: that one shrimp hovering, that one anemone closing around iron, that one old polisher working an hour on a coin of black glass, licking the battery clean to keep it running. No builder, no foreman, no plan — and yet the conveyor plume rises and stalls and sinks outward, and nothing is wasted. The power would see that efficiency looks industrial only because crowded patience always looks like that when you finally slow down enough to see it.

And when it leaves — if it leaves — it will leave nothing behind. No artifact to find later. Only this: the rim teeth held intact for one more surge, the doughnut current steadied for one more turn, the larvae that tried to ride out through the notch pushed gently back in to try again next tide.

Not rescued. Not fixed. Not enlarged, or greened, or sped up, or silenced.

Kept.
