It would come from above, like you did.

Because everything here is above it.

At first it would be tempted, as all powers are, to do something large. To lift. To solve.

It sees the rule written in water and thatch: *build up or drown.* And its first, kind thought is: that is a hard rule. Let me soften it. Let me lower the water a finger's width forever. Let me make the moss grow two centimeters a year instead of one. Let me tell the fox to be full, the owl to be blind, the pike to be merciful. Let me give the molting geese back their flight a week early. Let me make it loud again, safe again. Let me make it last.

Then wisdom would put a hand on benevolence's wrist.

No.

Not that.

For it looks closer, crouching the way you have to crouch here to hear anything, and it understands: the hardness is not a mistake around the life. It *is* the life. Take away drowning and you take away building. Take away the fox and the geese un-pack and scatter and are taken piecemeal elsewhere. Take away the pike and the sticklebacks choke the canals the muskrats keep. Take away hunger and no one dredges the avenues at night. This town is not suffering its crowding. It is practicing it. Its truce of muffle — no slap, no honk — is wiser than any peace you could impose from outside.

So the power would do nothing you could photograph.

It would make its attention light.

That is the first work, and the hardest. Even kind attention is heavy. A boot-print fills with water. A pack left a week sprouts sedge. Think what a god-print would do. So it would learn how to be here the way mist is here: forming and unforming a foot above the pools, beading, dripping, not pressing.

It would not stand on the rim as a wonder. It would descend by the raw peat stair and get its hands black. It would sit belly to moss until the cold came up through it. It would stay still long enough that sphagnum began to close over the dent of its sitting, and it would let it.

And it would listen. Not bless, at first. Listen. To the tick of water off leaf into pool. The soft suck as a muskrat surfaces. The faint squeak of wet sedge rubbing. The rustle-chew underneath it all — incisors on willow bark, bills pulling thatch. The town working on itself, quietly. It would hear that as prayer, and it would not interrupt prayer to answer it.

If it helped at all, it would help the way a thatcher helps: from inside the roof, invisibly.

It would keep the water coming. The dark lines seeping out of the cut walls after rain — it would keep those weeping. It would keep the cold pooling in the basin so the warm air off the tundra still spills over and condenses. It would keep four degrees in high summer and the shadowless bright under thin overcast. It would not warm it to make it easy. It would keep it exactly this marginal, this lush beyond reason, because marginal is what makes willow root through lodge-wood and houses turn green and growing.

It would hold the rim a little. Not forever. This is a young place; its banks are sharp, still calving in square blocks, roots still hanging white where willows were undercut last spring. One day the trough will slump or drain or fill to meadow. The power knows that and will not freeze time. But it will hold the walls from slumping all at once in a single storm, so the town gets its seasons, one more lush, silent, teeming season, and then another, to practice building up.

It would turn aside the other kind of attention. The heavy kind. It would let the satellite see only green-gold carpet. Let the survey map misread the canals as thaw polygons, uninteresting. Let the helicopter think better of landing for the wind. Let the human who would drain and manage and tag and count feel, inexplicably, that he should walk around. This is mercy, too: to save a place from being saved.

And for the beings — not the Being, there is no one being, there is thick — for each thatch-wall to thatch-wall life, it would do the smallest particular thing:

For the old beaver adding last night's logs to the wet side to keep ahead of rising water, it would lay a forepaw of cold blue mud beside his, unseen, to help the sticks fuse.

For the muskrat mother who must nibble her underwater door open every night or it seals with moss, it would keep the door open one extra hour while her kits sleep, so she can eat a stem herself.

For the sitting goose shoulder to shoulder with the next goose, neck stretched to touch, it would cool the down-ringed bowl with mist so she does not overheat in her patience.

For the vole rustling in the thatch a foot from a sleeping goose, for the short-eared owl dropping moth-soft, for the pike hanging motionless where the run narrows — for eater and eaten both — it would give no advantage. Only the continuation: that the run still narrows, that the thatch still rustles, that hunger still arrives on time and leaves on time.

For the cow moose and her calf belly-deep in the far channel, pulling pondweed, it would let the water pour off the muzzle just as it does, let the low nasal snuffle echo just as it does, and remember it perfectly, so that even when moose are elsewhere, this chewing is kept somewhere.

That is what a wise and benevolent power does with a place like this, once it has drawn its eye: it does not lift it above drowning. It kneels in the drowning with it. It apprentices itself to chewing.

When it leaves — and it must leave, or its sitting becomes a dam — it leaves no mark. No cleared ground, no sign. Within a day the dent where it sat is closed over, green tendrils uncurling. Within a week a cottongrass nods white there, rooting in what it shed without knowing. Within a month a sandpiper nests against it.

The town does not permit vacancies.
