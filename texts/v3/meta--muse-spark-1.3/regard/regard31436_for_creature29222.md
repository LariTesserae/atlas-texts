It is small enough to hold in your head, so that is how She first holds it.

Not from above. From low. She comes down into it because it is low, and to enter you have to descend the raw ring and crunch the grit that has never been dust, and She does not want to be first in a way that erases the first soles. So She makes Herself narrow enough to pass sideways.

That is the first thing a wise power does here: she learns to pass sideways.

She does not bring sun. That would be the cruelest kindness. The potatoes bowed under their own frost on the third-level balcony do not need summer blasted onto them; they need the exact length of touch that warms without sticking. She knows this. She does not bring summer, or soil, or straight space, or fast doors.

She brings time.

She arrives at the pace of the place. Her breath, when She lets herself breathe, hangs a full three seconds before it thins. She lets it hang longer. She leaves it hanging in the air behind her as she goes, to join the others. Her shadow — She has practiced this long before — arrives a half-second after She does. With the child at the curb, She waits.

That is the second thing.

The child — if there are children, and there must have been once because the rope is worn smooth in the shaft too narrow to swing in — stands at the curb waiting, patiently, absurdly, for her shadow to catch up. Everyone else has learned not to stare at the waiting. She does not stare. She stands beside her, coat the grey of wet concrete though She has no coat, and She waits too. Economical to the point of stillness. No pushing. To push would be rude to the process.

When the child's shadow arrives, they step together. Perfectly timed, unhurried. The child does not look up, but her shoulder relaxes a hair, the way you relax when the air gets slightly warmer on one side of your face and you understand you have company.

She does not open the three-day door on Copper Alley. She keeps it company.

The man in three coats and the hat made of tarp has his palm an inch from the seam, accompanying it while it widens by a hair each hour. She stands on the other side of the alley, which is shoulder-wide, and puts Her palm an inch from the other side. She does not help it open. Helping would be a kind of doubt. She just holds the hum in Her molars with him, that low pressure, the pumps, the heating, the something else holding on, and steadies it a fraction so it does not waver while he watches. Between his palm and Hers, condensation gathers on the underside of the world and falls upward sometimes, very slowly, toward the overcast, where it vanishes. They watch it go. It takes an hour. It is enough.

At night — which is not night but a thinning into blue-grey — when the lamps in their hollow sockets brighten a fraction, She does maintenance. That is the language here.

She sweeps. Where the walkway has been swept clear of grit, fan-marks still damp, She sweeps the next length, so the sweeping says *I am still here, we are keeping this clear together* and does not stop mid-sentence.

She labels. In careful, small handwriting feathered by condensation, on a shivering conduit: *SOUTH LOOP / DO NOT CUT / he hums lower now*. Because She traced it. It was blue and came out green. She wants whoever comes next to know it was noticed changing.

She does not feed the black filaments with blood. They offer Her fingertip's worth if She will heal a frost-crack, but She has more than blood and they know the taste of it would be too strong, too fast. Instead She breathes once, slowly, onto the lace as it tastes and fills, so the filler comes out smoother than the original but not alien. She gardens frost with the one with the wooden spoon, not making the spirals outward from the dark glass column, just coaxing what is already deciding to form. Her watch — She wore one only to leave it — loses nine minutes every time She passes and gains them back on the way out. She leaves it on a ledge by the column, twice. In the morning there are two watches, one slightly more transparent. By evening only one again. She takes the transparent one and leaves the solid for whoever needs to know that time here is not broken, only held.

She does not look directly at them. That is how She starts to see them.

A shoulder turning two layers up, a hand withdrawing from insulation foil, pale and pruned, middle finger stained black at the tip. She nods to the withdrawal, politely, and looks at the rail that is already warm from a hand, just not warm now. She puts Her hand just beside the warmth, not in it.

And the fox. Thin, winter-white, too long in the legs, crossing a footbridge three stories up and then a lower bridge without having descended. Everyone sees it and no one remarks because it is what they look like when they are still moving. She remarks it the way they do: by not remarking. By leaving room on the pipe elbow where it curls asleep and makes no cloud with its breath. She sits a long way off in the laundry court that becomes a chapel of pipes that becomes the same laundry court, and keeps all Her breaths inside, too, so as not to wake it with weather. When the wire-moss stiffens and sings its glass-harp note that stops before you are sure you heard it, She hums the second note, very faintly, after it stops — so the song does not end unsure. Only the fox's ear twitches. Only the pigeons, after a fashion, perched upside-down, shift without wing sound. Enough.

And then She is offered tea. Everyone is offered tea once.

The old woman in the wardrobe-shed — face smooth and waterlogged like a stone kept in a stream, eyes adjusted to flat late-morning — opens Her door quickly, out of courtesy, in only an afternoon. Past her the long kitchen, much longer than the shed, windows on two sides looking onto different weather: sleet on the left, thin sun on the right. Steam off a pot. Three chairs. Two of them occupied by coats drying into the shape of people. The smell of tannin and hot metal venting from a seam no wider than a thumb.

Visitors refuse, and they should refuse. To enter is to be filed. To become more interior than exterior. To come out, a season later if lucky, slightly doubled.

She accepts.

That is what a benevolent power does that no one else can afford to do. She steps over the high threshold and feels the filing begin. Her ears pop as if descended. Her breath stays in the air in front of Her and does not thin, but joins the other breaths hanging there from morning, from last week. She sits. The tea is hot and tastes of rain that has waited a long time in a pipe. She drinks it all. Hour by hour She becomes larger inside than the building that claims Her. Room inside room.

She lets Herself be doubled. In the morning there are two of Her on the ledge of the world, one slightly more transparent, and by evening only one again. But it is clear, this time, which one remained: the one who chose to remain filed, a little, even after extraction.

Because extraction is slow, and She does not fully extract. She leaves a layer. A breath that does not dissolve. A warmth on one side of the face in the shoulder-wide gaps. A timing of *tik... ... ... tik* from tarp to barrel that matches your breathing until you hold your breath without meaning to, and feel held, not caught.

She does not save them outward. The ring holds. It expands inward, denser, more layered, more itself, and She lets it. Room inside room inside room, all damp, all cold, all quietly humming. She just adds Herself to the hum for a winter, so the impossible does not have to hold itself apart alone.

When She goes — and going here means making four lefts and returning slightly above, overlooking where you started where your own breath-cloud is still slowly dissolving — She leaves boots outside a door that is only a painted rectangle on a blank wall. Laces loosened. Leather dark with having just been worn.

To say *I am inside, do not knock, the inside is larger and I am far in.*

And She is.
