She — it — notices first by not looking away.

That is already rare. Most powers that glance at this high table look away. They see no water and call it barren. They hear the roar and call it empty. They feel the cold on the neck and call it unkind and go down again to green valleys where life is easy to recognize.

This one stays. Listens longer.

And understanding comes slowly, the way everything comes here: on the mountain's duration. This is not a broken lowland. This is not a lowland waiting for rain to fix it. This is a place still deciding what a place is — pre-erosion, pre-river, pre-animal, pre-rain — and it has already decided, tenderly and without permission, to be alive anyway. Lush on noise.

A wise power knows what that means. It means: do not bring water.

That is the first mercy, and the hardest. Any other kindness wants to bring water. To crack the lid of the sky and let rain gully the fresh fractures, soften the knapped cliffs, rot the pale thatch halos, drown the silica hair, silence the tearing overtone a mile overhead. It would be murder disguised as rescue. The bells would close and never open. The mattress-moss would go slimy and stink and die. The tenders with their papery skin and thin blood would cough in the wet and never learn to breathe again.

So no water. No river. Not even dew. She brings her water inside her own body and takes it away again when she leaves. She bleeds, up here, like anyone. Her nose crusts black. She lets it.

Second: do not bring quiet.

She wants to. Her heart aches in her molars the first hour she kneels on the pavement, palms pressed useless to ears, bone-conducted roar shredding her thoughts shallow. She wants to lay a hand on the growing range and still it. To give them sleep. To give the tenders one whole night without the sternum-hum.

But she is wise. She sees how the amber trumpets lean *into* loudness, how a frayed edge catches more vibration than a clean one, how the gardens draw the map of sound with a sharp line at an overhang's shadow. Take away the tone and you take away the food. Silence here is starvation.

So she does not still it. She does almost nothing. And that almost is everything.

She learns to be low.

She does not arrive shining. There is no light worth competing with here, only the bruised leak and the paler smear that never clears the teeth. She arrives dim, crouched. She does not stand up to be seen. To stand up is to be shredded. She mounds. She curls. She makes herself thigh-high, dense, tender.

She learns to walk the bearable path, cairn to cairn, not the short path. She walks so carefully that the cairn she left an hour ago stays behind her where it belongs. It takes her many tries. The pre-echo still arrives before her footfall and makes her gag. She learns to press her forehead close to stone before stepping, to ask.

She goes to a hut. Not the nearest, tended one — that one is too shy — but the second one, where the thatch has grown too thick and the bells are beginning to ring each other to shards.

No one is inside. Or someone is inside and is very still and very accustomed to not being found. She does not look. She sits outside in the circle of upright shards where someone sat to do the work, and she does the work.

With borrowed hands — for she has no glass knife of her own and will not bring metal that would ring false — she thins the bells. One in three, as they do. She clears the dead white thatch that keeps the exact shape of life, and folds it, and leaves it stacked for weaving. She trims the fringe clean so it does not tangle and tear itself with its own trembling. Her fingertips go blurred. The leaves buzz her bones numb. She works slowly. Paper rustle. Dry crunch. Scent like hot stone and old incense. No sap.

Hours later, a shifting of weight inside the beehive. A dry rustle of thatch cape that buzzes faintly in the bass. Someone comes out to sit a little way off, facing away from the focus, as is polite. Old — or ageless with dryness — papery, furred with ingrained dust, eyes narrowed to slits against the dim that is not dim to them.

They do not shout. Shouting is shredded instantly.

She holds out her hands, palms up. Then elbows. Then she leans forward and offers her forehead, bone to bone.

The tender hesitates — newcomers usually want to be seen, to be heard, to be thanked — then presses close. Forehead to forehead. The pre-echo, which usually arrives flat and sickening before any sound, becomes here a use. Through bone, a tremor: *you hear?*

*I hear,* she answers, without voice, by holding still and letting the bass come up through the mattress beneath them and loosen the base of the skull.

They sit like that while a dust devil unscrews itself a hundred yards off with no wind and walks, over minutes, from one garden to another.

That is the second mercy: to be witnessed. The lush has performed for no witness but the rock. Now, once, it has an audience who kneels close enough to see right — face inches away, because that is the only distance where sight works.

When the sky seams come — those long straight faults where the grey is a fraction darker — she does the only large thing she will do.

The tone catches. Skips like a needle. For three heartbeats there is something else underneath: a silence so absolute it feels wet. The bells stop shivering. The thatch holds its breath. The tenders, wherever they are curled, hold theirs. It is terrifying every time. A memory of an ocean that never existed here.

She does not extend it. That would starve them. She does not close it. That would lie.

She holds it *with* them. She puts her vast, quiet attention around those three heartbeats like hands around a cup made from a giant trumpet, to hold seeds, or dust, or sound itself. So the silence is not an abyss but a held cup. So the old tender's heart does not hammer quite so hard in the sudden wet. So the bells, when the roar floods back into the teeth, resume a quarter-tone more gently, not startled.

One heartbeat longer of rest inside the held note. No more. That is enough for sleep to come in short, cold naps.

She stays for a week, which is a hair's breadth of mountain rising, a quarter-tone lower. She helps a garden migrate, dying back on one side, swelling on the other, carrying armfuls of living thatch to where the resonance pools at the foot of a sheer black wall where the tone doubles back. She leaves her own tally mark next to the charcoal wavy line on the sheltered wall: one more count, so they are not counting the slow shift alone.

She does not teach them to leave. She does not offer to carry them down to where water remembers its shape. Some stay until they are indistinguishable from a mound of thatch, another halo of ghosts. That is not failure. That is tending to the end.

Before she goes, she leaves an offering that fits hand-tech. Not a machine. Not a word. On a flat rock: a coil of leaf-fiber rope she rewove, tighter, to last longer in the dry. Beneath it, a plug of cottony moss that will keep buzzing faintly for hours, to remember her by. No lacquer but use. No signature.

And as she rises — for even she cannot stay long on the extreme height without fraying — she does the last, invisible work of a benevolent power: she turns aside the other attentions.

The miners who would hear the tearing overtone and think *ore*. The lowland priests who would hear the organ-tone and think *temple*. The makers of maps who would see the tilted table and think *empty*. She smudges their sight, just slightly. Lets distance turn to static a ridge sooner. Lets uphill disagree with itself a little more stubbornly for those boots. Not a curse. A veil, thin as silica hair. So this instrument can finish being carved before anyone tries to play it.

Down below, where down will have her, she shakes the black dust from her nose and finds a trumpet-lichen seed in her pocket, dry, still tuned.

She does not plant it. There is nothing for it to drink here.

She holds it to her sternum until it trembles once, in sympathy with a mountain a world away still humming to itself as it grows, and then is still.

Here, water never came. Life learned to be lush on noise. Stone learned to sing before it learned to fall apart.

And once, love came, and learned to be dry, and low, and to tend without fixing — and to listen so close that the gardens heard themselves being heard, long before they were seen.
