It would come down.

Not all at once, not with light and thunder — there is already too much light and thunder down there. It would have to stoop, the way any tall one learns to stoop under that ceiling. Lift the grate in the back corner where the daylight doesn't reach. Come down the three rungs, boots first, into the weight.

It would sit. On the warm concrete curb, where you sat. Shirt would plaster if it wore a shirt. Ears would pop if it had ears. It would have to open its mouth to hear itself think under the *duh-duh-duh-duh* of the diesels and the whine of the main and the white scream of the steam bleed.

It would not turn them off.

That is the first wisdom, and the hardest. A lesser kindness would want to stop the engines, cool the room, silence it, make it comfortable. Make it like somewhere else. But the Power would feel the heat coming up through its feet and understand: this heat is what keeps the polder from drowning. Upstairs men are keeping back the marsh. Down here three slicks of green and one pale fern have learned how to live inside that keeping.

To cool it suddenly would kill what loves the heat. To quiet it suddenly would crack what has learned to ride the shiver.

So it would leave the roar running.

What it would do instead is pay the kind of attention no one else pays.

The men come down fast with a flashlight to check the sump. They see oil-sheen and scale and think *mold*. They see a weed in a crack. The Power would come down slow and see residents.

It would look first at the green — because the green is easy to miss, and the wise always look first at what is easy to miss. Three patches, no thicker than paint. Dark green-black. It would bend close, closer than the buzzing bulb allows most eyes to bend, and see the edge where green becomes white becomes cream becomes sulfurous yellow. Where algae is slowly being entombed by stone and sliding outward a dust-width ahead.

It would not scrub it clean. It would not feed it. It would put — if it has a finger — a finger just above it, not touching, and let the condensation from its own coolness fall. One drop. Just enough to say: *I see you making a hide for this room. Keep going.*

Then the fern.

In the southeast crack, where the marsh pushes in five degrees cooler. Two fronds, four inches high, translucent, veined because there isn't enough chlorophyll to hide them. Not growing since spring. Not dying since spring.

A sentimental power would rescue it. Lift it out into the riot twenty feet away, into soil and shade and cool rot, where ferns are supposed to live. The wise one would know that would kill it in a day. This fern has drunk hot mineral seep for months. Its roots are in poured concrete with the grain of wood still on it. It doesn't know soil anymore. It knows how to hold.

So the Power would not transplant.

It would kneel — which down there means crouching in hot ankle-water while the ceiling pipes drip *plik plok plik* onto its back — and get its eyes level with those two fronds.

And it would do very small, unmiraculous things.

It would keep the seep seeping. Just that one thread of cooler water in the crack, while all the other water wants to be hot. It would not widen the crack — the mineral teeth half an inch long, sharp as broken glass, are the room cutting its teeth, and you don't pull teeth that are just coming in — but it would keep it from closing.

It would keep the grate open. Ensure the noon rectangle keeps falling straight down for an hour, full of dust and steam motes, because that is the sun these four get. Ensure the yellow bulb keeps buzzing and does not go out, because that is their moon that never sets.

It would, for one breath, lay a palm over the steam vent. Not to stop it — the pressure needs its bleed — but to muffle the scream for three seconds. Just three seconds in a year of endless white noise, so the fern can feel its own shiver settle, so the puddles can make rings that actually spread before they vanish. A Sabbath of three seconds. Then let go.

And, mostly, it would remember.

That room is too small for a map. When the diesels finally stop — because all diesels stop, in ten years or fifty — the heat will break, the water will cool, rust will finally come, the marsh will push in properly and rot will begin, and the thermophilic green will die of cold and the fern will either grow huge or rot. The Power knows this pocket is temporary. It was never meant to be lived in. It was meant to be looked at once a year with a flashlight. And yet for a little while water and iron and concrete are slowly learning how to become stone, and life has boiled itself down to *stay thin, stay low, stay wet.*

A wise benevolence doesn't promise forever. It witnesses the temporary so completely that the temporary is not alone.

So before it climbs back up the three rungs, it would leave its own slick — not green, not mineral, just the memory of having been sat-with — on the warm curb.

And it would tell, in a voice that comes back flat and small off the wet walls but is heard anyway:

*Endure. Lengthen by dust. Widen by hair. I have seen you do it right.*
