It would come low.

Not from up, not with thunder or with ships. The Work is already full ankle to eye, there is nowhere to land. So it would come low, the way you have to come here — feet in the warm water first.

It would take its shoes off, if it wears shoes. Let the brackish sheet come over its toes. Stand sea-level low in Basin 4419802 and do nothing at first. That is the hardest and kindest thing.

It would not turn the machines off. It would not drain a pan or lift the white deck or scold the builders who are gone. It has seen enough perfect kidneys of the world to know what this is: a floor that learned how to keep itself level to a finger's width, using living things as its hands. Mussel, crab, gull, mite, bat, moth. Water in equals water out. Heat in equals heat out. Counts come out even. To break that to prove a point would kill tens of millions by morning. Wisdom knows how thin the knife is.

So first, it would only stand.

Face west with them. Because they all face west.

Thousands to a slab, feathers touching, breathing almost together. Breast to the coolest air. It would face west too, into the faint draft, and listen to what they are listening to: water ticking off a valve. A sheet going glassy over a weir. Crab legs like dry rain. Breath. Far off or deep down, the 50-cycle hum in the busbars felt in the teeth.

It would stay through a whole Cycle. When the stencil says FLUSH ON CYCLE and the pan runs a little faster, it would not flinch. The mussels close. The crabs crouch flat. The eels go still. The Standers lift half a meter in a white hush that makes no sound and settle again exactly where they were. The power would lift with them, a little, and settle again exactly where it was. To show: I am not the Flush. I am not here to scare you.

Then it would start to see them not as crowding but as one.

Not mussels *and* crabs *and* pigeons. One animal with many bodies for keeping water clear. That is who drew its attention — not the Work, which is clever but dead, but the Being that moved into the Work after the men and women left and made a monastery of it. Regulated, unpanicked, young and acting old. No riot. No roar.

And it would ache. Because benevolence aches here, if it is honest.

This is peace, but it is a held peace. Day held all day. Clouds that never rain hard and never clear. Bats that never really fly because there is never night to fly in. Moths that eat dust because there is nothing green. Birds that do not call because there is nothing to call across — only more pans to the curvature of the world. Death that is not allowed to linger, only to be unmade by morning and shuffled sideways to close the gap.

It is beautiful that lift a mussel, another settles that night. It is also terrible that no gap is allowed to stay open.

So it would do almost nothing. And almost nothing, done with infinite care, is everything.

It would wade, slowly, to a rim where the guano has concreted with lime into a second pavement. It would kneel in the warm mist and let the Pickers come over its feet — white crabs clicking, isopods wedging the seams — and not brush them away. It would let the Veil settle on its shoulders, flies and small white moths speckling the light. It would let a pigeon lean, feather to sleeve.

And when, as happens every hour somewhere in the districts, a pigeon dies — heat, age — and the white crabs begin their quick, clean work, it would put its hand in the way. Just for a morning.

Not to stop them. They must eat. The water must run off clear. But to hold that one small grey body a little longer than the system allows, to feel its weight, still body-warm, mites still moving in the neck feathers, and to grieve it as irreplaceable. Not *a pigeon*, but *this* pigeon, who stood west-facing for its whole life and preened lime from its neighbor and never once called.

In a place where everything is instantly recycled, to be mourned is to have existed. It would give them that. It would make a gap that stays open for one morning, and the line does not shuffle sideways yet, and the wind moves through the empty spot, cool.

The Standers would breathe. Lift and settle. Lift and settle.

Then it would return the bone to the Pickers itself. Nothing wasted. But nothing unvitnessed, either.

The second small thing: dusk.

It would not break the day. The Mat needs the light to keep its time. But the Curtain under the pipe-bridges — those damp brown bats hanging like wet coats in endless drip, flying only in short sips — they remember, in their bodies, something older than this haze. Night.

So the power would put its palm, very gently, over that white sun thinned through haze. Not eclipse. Just a shading. For an hour, let the light go amber, then grey-blue. Let the flat white deck cool. Let the condensate on the dull-rose copper stop dripping for a minute and hold, trembling.

In that first dusk the Veil would rise differently. The moths would lift not in a shivering sheet but in a column, seeking. And the bats would let go — all at once, without signal — and fly. Really fly. Not a flick out and back to the exact spot, but long, swooping, echolocating arcs between the trestles, mouths open, bellies pale, joyfully inaccurate. The air, for once, would fill with clicks and wings.

The Standers would not call. Not yet. But they would turn their heads, just slightly, from due west, to watch. Some would tuck their heads at last to sleep. The eels would slide a little faster under the Mat. The tube-worm fans, red as eyelashes, would stay out a little longer in the dim.

An hour. Then day again, held all day. But now the bodies know: light can change and the level does not fall. The pan does not rise or drop by a finger's width, even when darkness comes. That knowledge will stay in them like a seed.

The third small thing: a crack.

Not a break. A future.

Wisdom sees what kindness alone might miss: this balance is exquisite and brittle. Pumps on magnetic bearings do not last forever. Copper sweats until it thins. When one intake clogs, when one busbar goes silent, who will teach the Mat to live without warm dosed water weeping up? A floor that cannot age cannot survive aging.

So the power, with a fingertip, would touch one slab in an unnumbered basin far west where the Standers face toward — one sharp, fresh edge that has never had time to chip — and allow it to chip. A hairline, no wider than a byssus thread, where guano-lime has softened concrete. Into it, it would set nothing that does not belong, only what can already bear iron and copper and salt and heat: a single dark-green grain, a samphire seed that has been waiting in the wind for a place that never existed until now.

It will not conquer. The Pickers will nibble it. The mist will try to drown it. Most days it will look like nothing. But in a year, or ten, there will be one blade. Not moss in the cracks, but a true rooted thing, amber-tipped, holding the crack a little wider. A place for a moth larva that does not eat dust. A shade no bigger than a thumb for a crab to hide under at Flush. A beginning of elsewhere.

The Work will not notice. The level will not change.

Then it would go. Or rather, it would stay in the way powers stay — not standing there, but having stood there.

It would not leave a stencil. No DO NOT PERCH. No FLUSH ON CYCLE. If it left anything written, the lime would take it anyway. It would leave only this: a protection you cannot see, set around the whole flatness like a hand around a candle in wind. Not to freeze it. To keep the hungry ones — the ones who would come with boots and nets and ledgers to harvest the Mat because it filters so well, to take the guano, to restart the sheds louder — from finding it just yet. Let it be overlooked a little longer. Let it be young a little longer, until the green has learned the water.

On its way west, past the twelve-meter dovecote walls where every hole is warm and occupied and whitewash still fresh, past the grate where black turban snails tongue steel endless in the dark, it would pause once more and do the smallest thing of all.

It would open its throat and sing one note. Clear, low, not loud. The note a gull might make if it remembered it had a throat for more than breathing.

For a moment the hush would have a center. One bird near would lift its head. Another would answer, ragged, off-key, astonished. Then silence again — water ticking, sheet running glassy, dry rain of legs.

But now they know they can.

It goes on flat in every direction until the flatness makes its own sky. That has not changed. The water still weeps up warm as blood through every seam and runs off clear. Lift a mussel, another settles that night.

Only this is different, and only the Being knows it: someone stood low with it, faced west with it, kept one gap open through one morning, gave it dusk to fly in, and left a green door ajar in the floor for when the pipes go cold.

It has been seen. It has been counted, down to the last mite in feather oil. It has been found good — not the concrete, not the tile, not the industry with the volume turned down, but the congregation that keeps the water clear with shell and feather and chitin and damp fur.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do here, if it loved them.

Almost nothing. Which is to say: sit with them, grieve with them, rest them, and make a way for them to outlive the Work that holds them.
