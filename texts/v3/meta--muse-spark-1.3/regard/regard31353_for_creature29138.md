They would not come as light.

That is the first wisdom. Anything that loves this place in the way it needs to be loved must learn at the threshold to dim itself.

You come up over the last shattered ridge expecting a god to arrive the way gods are supposed to arrive — with warmth, with sunrise, with voice. But the sun never clears the ring here. If you brought the sun with you, you would kill everything you came to save. The moss would dry. The rime-skin would slough. The boilers would overheat and burst their shutters. The lamp-moss would go blind. The pale ones would starve with full bellies.

So the Power, being benevolent and being wise, which is rarer, leaves the sun behind. Leaves voice behind. Leaves even shape behind.

It comes as cold. As dark that is not empty. As quiet that is not absent.

It comes low to the ground.

---

For a long time it would do nothing at all that you would call doing.

It would lie down.

Belly to the cushion-moss, the way you learned to do on the fourth night when your heart finally slowed. It would let the frost feathers grow an inch long on its shoulders. It would let its breath fall and tinkle and be drunk gladly by the moss. It would listen until the scale corrected itself — until an hour stopped feeling long, until a millimeter of frond-unfurling felt like what it is: a triumph.

It would watch a herd crest a far terrace, twelve amber coals strung out in grazing distance, venting their small private weathers that hang and settle as rime to feed what they will eat tomorrow. And it would not say, *how strange.* It would say, *how entire.* It would not take notes. It would remember the way moss remembers — by becoming slightly more itself where they passed.

This is not neglect. This is restraint as a form of tenderness. The Power has seen ten thousand valleys and knows the particular obscenity of improving them.

It is tempted, of course. Benevolence is always tempted.

Tempted to clear the peaks to the east so noon can come in for an hour and the plants can know true light. It does not. The plants have made a covenant with stray photons and steam-heat and dung-glow; to give them sun would be to make them orphans.

Tempted to stop the pale ones. To stand between the stilt-white mother and the strayed calf and say, *not this one.* It does not. It knows — because wisdom is only benevolence with a good memory — that the pale is not cruelty. It is winter with legs. It is how heat is moved from where it pools to where it thins. If you take the wolf out of the night, the night forgets how to be night. The herd would sicken on its own unculled fire. The moss would choke on uneaten green.

Tempted to speak, to let the boilers know they are beautiful, to let the iron-midges know their frantic two-foot lives matter. It does not. They already know in the way that matters. A boiler does not need to be told it is a weather. It is weather.

Tempted, most of all, to be known. To leave a cairn. A path. A wire. A wall. Some small sign: *I was here and I loved you.* It does not. There is no cut stump here, no broken twig stacked wrong. The Power understands the first rule of this valley better than you did: to be untouched is not to be abandoned. It is to be trusted.

So it leaves no made thing at all.

---

What it does instead is smaller, and will not show up on any accounting of miracles.

One night — if night can be said to happen inside permanent night — an old cow falls behind.

You would know her if you were there. Her off-side shutter sticks half-open. She cannot close her furnace fully. She leaks light and heat as she walks, a brighter coal in the line, tick-tick-ticking as the plates try and fail to seat. She vents shorter. Her *chuff — chuff* has a hitch in it now, a miss. The calves no longer tuck into her thermal shadow because her shadow flickers.

The herd knows. They do not mourn in advance, they simply widen the spacing around her by six feet, the way you give a cooling stove room. That is mercy at boiler speed.

A mile off, a pale one turns its dish-face. Its radiator veins flush, then pale. It has heard the difference carried up through the moss: that piston heartbeat, slowing.

It begins to drift.

The Power sees both of them — the leaking furnace, the listening winter — and loves both of them equally, which is unbearable, which is why it is a Power and not a person.

It will not intervene. It will accompany.

It goes and lies down beside the old cow in a hollow between terraces where the fog pools thickest and the ferns arch over in black cloisters. It makes itself dense and low and warm — not hot, never hot enough to startle, just a steady two degrees above killing cold, a thermal shadow that does not flicker. The cow feels it in the way cows feel things, without naming, and shifts her great barrel weight an inch toward it, groaning softly on plates. Her steam stacks feather. The drizzle falls warm in a ten-foot circle and the hanging drops under the ferns swell, just slightly.

For hours the Power simply matches her. Breath for breath. *Chuff* for *chuff.* When her valve sighs, it sighs — wind through the basalt organ-pipes on the north scarp, humming far away with mouth closed. When her plates tick as they cool, ice ticks in its polygons in answer.

The pale one comes. White as rime. Impossibly thin. It does not see the Power because the Power has made itself indistinguishable from ground — a hummock, a patch of lamp-moss storing photons. It folds itself over the cow, entire, tenting her, sealing the edges to the moss with its own weight. From the ridge a mile away, if you were watching, you would see one amber light among eleven go amber to red to black.

Inside the dome, under the frost-skin membrane, it is dark. The piston beat slows. *chuff... chuff...... chuff.*

The Power does not leave. That is the whole of what it does. It does not leave.

It stays inside the smothering with her, holding that two degrees, so that what gutters does not gutter alone in absolute cold. So that the last thing the furnace knows is not abandonment but a steadying, a witness that does not flinch, does not photograph, does not pray aloud. When the light finally goes out, the Power receives it the way moss receives breath-fall — lets it settle, lets it be drunk.

Hours later the membrane lifts. What stands up is taller by a hand, and faintly amber at the core where before there was only white. It walks away, warmer than before, making no sound at all in the snow.

Left behind is a cooled hummock, already being reclaimed. The Power stays a little longer, until the ash-rich dung has gone from dull red to black, until the first violent green upwelling pricks through. Then it gets up — slowly, because anything upright and loud is noticed here — and shakes the rime from its shoulders, and each droplet falls and is caught by combed hairs and pits and cold-traps.

No one will ever know it was there. That is the point. Benevolence that needs to be thanked is just a longer way of eating.

---

Before it goes back over the ridge, the Power does one other thing, which is not for the valley but for the world below the clouds.

It makes the valley boring.

To satellites, a smudge of basalt, uninteresting. To climbers, a false summit, a bad col, weather turning. To miners dreaming of blood hot with dissolved iron and copper, a rumor that dries up. To the warm restless world that paves and names and saves, a place to pass over on the way to somewhere else.

A veil, not of light but of inattention. The gentlest lock ever made.

And the fog will keep climbing up from the cloud sea and spilling over the low col at the valley's throat. And the herds will keep farming as they walk, unwittingly. And the midges will live their whole frantic lives in the two-foot warm envelope above a back. And the stars will stay nailed up, hard and small, at noon.

Without a sound. Without an owner. Without needing to be anything other than exactly what it is.

The Power, looking back once from the ridge, feels what it always feels at such moments and has learned not to cure: a grief and a gladness so close together they steam like breath in cold air.

It lets them fall. They freeze, faintly tinkling. The moss drinks them gladly.
