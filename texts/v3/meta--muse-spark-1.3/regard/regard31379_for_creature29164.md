If anything came that was both kind and wise, it would not come like weather.

It would come over the rim the way dust comes — without announcement. No brightening, no parting of the gauze. The light would stay copper-dim. The rim would keep humming in your molars. Because it would know, before its foot touched salt that squeaks, that this is not a broken place waiting to be fixed.

That is the first wisdom. Not to pity dryness.

A lesser kindness would bring water. It would ache at the cracked tongue, the sealed lips, the milky edges of narrowed eyes, the throat that stopped sweating. It would want to open a spring under the basalt, to let something flow and drip. It would kill them with that love in a season. The furred blankets would rot. The static that combs the air would short and die. The wax would run and spoil. The chime gourds would swell and split silent. The whole closed dry circle — perfected over ten thousand years of learning the shape of wind — would go muddy and collapse.

So it brings no water. It does not even bring the thought of water.

What it does is descend. Step by step, into the singing emptiness. A hundred paces between presences, and it honors each pace. It does not stride to the nearest thunderleaf as if to a marvel. It walks the dust that holds them upright.

And when it reaches the old one — squat trunk corked like basalt, twenty blanket-leaves curled and layered, margins beaded amber on the lee — it does what almost no visitor does.

It stops. It puts its palm flat where rock stops and bark begins and cannot tell the difference, and it leaves it there. It feels maintenance. Not growth, not striving. The long settled work of staying in form. The faint tick-tick-tick of dust striking fur, the exhaled oils, the sealing.

It says, without mouth: *good. You have learned to be a country. Continue.*

That is the first doing. Blessing continuance.

Then it listens for the tender.

There are only three, four in the whole bowl. It finds the one scraping at dawn — no dew — short and compact and salt-caked, hair felted into mats that comb exactly the way leaf-fur does, great cupped ears turning, turning. Obsidian comb, teeth worn smooth. Not stripping. Tending. Clearing the sealed wax so the fur can comb again. Leaving the weeping beads. Chewing one old knob slowly, jaw working an hour for food and for the little bound moisture exhaled back into cupped hands.

It watches the whole hour. A wise power knows how to waste time beautifully.

The temptation, even for the wise, is to heal. To clear the milk from those slit eyes. To un-crack the tongue. To make hearing easy instead of earned.

It does not. Those are not wounds. They are the shape of belonging. To take them would be to unmake the belonging.

Instead it crouches down on the lee side, in the dust, at the tender's height. It makes its own breathing shallow through hairy nostrils, as they do. It takes, when offered — because the tender, without looking up, offers, as tenders do between gusts — a knob of wax. Chews it. Resin and scorched hair, then sweet. Works the jaw. Breathes powder.

And it listens back.

This is the thing no one has ever done for them. They have spent their lives listening — for the gourd that rings fullest, for the sail-slap deepest that means wax heavy, for the silence between gusts where gliders hunt. The cairn skull facing the wind, the clay bells to call or scare, all of it is listening made visible. No one has listened to *them*.

So it listens. To the chitter of pouch-mice in the caches under stone. To the rasp of ash-locust lifting in a black sheet. To the needle-whistle of veil-gliders cutting gloom. To the gunshot split of a seed-pod. To the clatter of a chime-tree stripped naked in four minutes and already pushing pale furls by evening. And underneath, the small dry sounds of the tender herself: the scrape, the chew, the caught breath returned to cupped hands.

It lets itself be changed by the noise. Its clothes fill with dust. Its ears ring. That is allowed. Accretion, not flow.

When the tender freezes between gusts and tilts her head — because that silence is where things hunt — it freezes too. And here it does the one small thing it will do that is not nothing:

It lengthens that silence by a breath. Not stilling the wind, not stopping the hunt. Just widening the listening a fraction. A pocket. So she can hear more clearly which way the glider is gliding, and step, this once, out of its path. No rescue. Just room.

Later, when she shakes a chime-tree to find seed, it touches one gourd, the fullest one, so its ring is a semitone truer. So she finds it a moment sooner. Later, when she dies — as she will, soon, for her felt is thinning and her comb is almost smooth — it will come again and sit with the drying. It will not wet her lips. It will not carry her down to the lowlands where water runs and meaning drains. It will stay while she unmakes to husk within sight, to pellets and powder, to molt caught on bellows-moss to become soil to be combed again. It will keep her name, which no one else remembers entire, accreted like wax on a margin.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does in a place that does not drink.

Not rain. Not pity. Not removal.

It kneels in the dust that sings. It learns to chew and breathe powder. It answers the clay bell not with a voice but with attention. It shields without hiding: keeps the copper gauze hung, keeps the lowland rumor paler, lets prospectors dream of other bowls, so this small roaring bowl can keep roaring to itself a while longer.

And when it climbs back over the rim, it leaves no footprint that the wind will not erase by evening. Only this, on the rock where the comb was left: the comb turned a hairsbreadth to catch the light it can catch, so the next tender passing will know — without being told, the way all rules are learned here —

*you were heard between gusts.*

Nothing flows. Everything accretes.
