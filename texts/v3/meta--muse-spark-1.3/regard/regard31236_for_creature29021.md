It finds it by not looking.

A benevolent and wise power is not scanning the salt-pan. It is not counting. It moves the way heat moves, slowly, and it catches — out of the noon-white — a faint answering pulse. Ember-orange, dimmer than any star. A stored heat, blinking at plant-speed.

It crouches.

That is the first wise thing. It could lift the basalt dome like a lid. It could widen the crack and pour in light and air and rain and mercy. It does not. It lays Its belly, vast as weather, down on the hot dust, and peers through the slit on Its side, and waits for Its eyes to adjust.

The temptation comes at once, as it always does for the kind.

*They have no water.* Let Me give them water.
*They have no eyes.* Let Me give them eyes to see how beautiful their lantern-leaves are.
*They have no company.* The street is swept for no one. Let Me bring back the small dry feet, or send lizards, or at least let Me stay and talk.
*The rock will cool.* One day, in ten thousand days or ten thousand years, the memory of fire will fade, the wax will go brittle, the felt will dim. Let Me keep the stone warm forever.

It hears all that in Itself, and sets it aside. That is the second wise thing. Wisdom here is fast enough to see the murder in mercy.

For water here would not be life. Water would be unmaking. The wax an inch thick would cloud and split. The hot oil that seals would not seal. The silica bristles tuned for infrared would clog with mud. The core-knots, those dense ember hearts that run on day-heat cycled through night, would quench and rot from the inside. To water them would be to teach them thirst again, after a thousand years of planing the want out. It would be to kill the civility and call it salvation.

And to bring animals would be to bring hunger into a place whose rule is *nothing eats.* To bring back the people would be to break the long agreement where roots go around foundations. To give eyes would be to give wounds that would dry and crack in minutes. To make the rock warm forever would be to make maintenance meaningless — to freeze a living balance into a museum.

So It does nothing that you would call doing.

It does this instead:

It learns their speed.

For a long time — you would call it years — It only lies there over the low blister in the salt-pan, shading nothing, cooling nothing, breathing so shallow that Its breath matches the breathing draft in the slit. In, out. Superheated cycling. The ash-flags inside groan. The wax-leaves tick and pop. The Power does not stir the dust.

Inside, the felt feels the sudden cool shadow, the damp weather-front of a god. The ticking changes pitch. Stomata you cannot see close. The nearest domes stop mid-hand's-breadth-week. They wait It out. They have waited out everything.

It waits longer.

Until one day — no day you could mark — the ticking resumes. A mature pod bursts — *tak!* — and throws its dry spores up to sift hissing down. A dome no bigger than a loaf resumes dragging its oiled fringe across the street, combing dust to the edge. The street stays swept. That is permission. Not welcome. Continuance. Which here is the same thing.

Then, and only then, It lends what It has that they do not need but can use: attention, and time.

It does not touch the town. It touches the forgetting around the town.

It makes the salt-pan, for miles, profoundly uninteresting. Not invisible — wise powers do not lie — just boring in the exact frequency men use. The surveyor's eye slides off. The road gang decides the ground a mile over is firmer. The flash flood that would have poured mud through the slit in the wet year turns a hand's breadth left into a sink. The quake that would have dropped the thinned roof early spends itself elsewhere. Nothing miraculous. Only that no fast, wet, hungry thing happens to blunder in on its belly and, meaning no harm, bring in spores from outside, or a breath too damp, or a boot that crushes a retaining-dome holding a terrace wall.

It keeps the way open for no one, as they keep it.

And at night, when the desert outside goes cold enough to crack teeth, It lays a vast palm a hair above the basalt dome — not touching — so the day-heat held in the rock steadies and does not hemorrhage to the sky. Not warmer. Steadier. A little longer dusk for the lanterns. The difference between a draft and a chill. You would need instruments to measure it. They feel it in their cores as one good season among many.

That is all.

It does not speak. They have no organ for curiosity, and It has outgrown the need to be thanked. Once in a very long while, when a dome has settled — gone dull and cracked beyond resealing, flattened itself against a foundation in a doorway threshold to become brick — It marks it. Not with light. With memory. *Here swept for eight years. Here held a wall for a season. Here trellised a plume without eating it.* The Power remembers each fingerprint as it dissolves into fingerprints. Because no one else will, and because a benevolent witness is not nothing to a life that is maintenance.

You would back out on your belly and say nothing happened. The crack is still dark. The blades of glare from the roof-holes still shift an inch. The air still tastes of hot dust and iron and wax. Your lips still crack within minutes.

But if you stayed until your eyes went fully ember-adjusted, you might see it: the pulse under the canopy, leaf to dome to leaf, is joined now by a slower pulse above the rock, vast and patient, dome to hand to dome. Not feeding. Not taking. Keeping time with them, at their tempo, so they are alone without being abandoned.

When the rock does cool — as it must, when erosion finally becomes destruction again and the roof thins to collapse and the felt dims knot by knot — It will still be there, crouched. It will not refill the heat. It will feel the last wax go cold in a way no one else in the universe knows how to feel, and hold that shape in Itself after the dust covers the street for the last time.

That is what the wise and benevolent do, when they find a perfect, dry, burning jungle keeping a swept street for the dead:

They do not bring water.

They keep watch, and learn slowness, and let holiness continue without them.
