It would lie on its belly.

That is the first thing. Any power that is both benevolent and wise has learned how to make itself small. It would not come with weather. It would not widen the crack. It would come the way you did — feet-first, hands scraping — and find itself suddenly pantry-sized, having to duck to not hit its head.

It would know, at once, how new the place is. How the stone still takes a thumbprint and then slowly fills back in like warm bread. It would feel that faint tick as a flake lets go above, and understand: this is a being still making itself. Not finished. Not to be hurried.

And it would see the other Being — the true resident, the one who doesn't pass through.

Not the crickets. Not the frogs. The pooled light that has lived so long in the ellipse that it grew heavy, and learned to have a floor, and learned to remember. The interval between stations made luminous.

A lesser power would try to fix it. A merely kind power would try to save it.

It would do neither.

It would not bring more light for the bleached ferns. It would not bring a pond for the two monk frogs in their separate rain-cups. It would not feed the eyeless moths who are living off themselves, dusting the air with scales. It would not stop the rust-red centipede three inches from your ear. It would not resurrect the mouse whose chapel of rootlets and moth-wing scales still holds, tight and empty, by the moss.

Because it is wise. It sees the truce. At noon the cold-blooded edge inward — frogs, pink-gilled salamander, black-seed beetles — shoulder to shoulder around a washtub of sun, spaced still but closer than at any other hour. No one is taken at the light. That rule is older than any of them, and it works only because hunger is allowed elsewhere, in silence, in the dark. To remove the hunter would be to unmake the parish.

So it would submit. That is what omnipotence does when it loves: it obeys the etiquette of the small room.

1. Keep quiet, or the brightness dims.

It would keep quiet. It, who could speak in thunder, would learn to breathe shallow so as not to fog the air too much. It would feel how sound and light are on opposite pans of a scale here, and it would choose light. It would let the rock eat its own footsteps. It would let the only sound be the tick, tick of condensation growing, trembling, falling — felt in the teeth — and the too-loud breath of any visitor.

2. What falls in stays until the rain.

It would let things stay. Moth scales, quartz pebbles, your skin cells. It would not sweep clean. When the three-minute rain comes — that sudden vertical pouring, a whole weather system shrunk to the width of a chimney, hissing on warm stone — it would not shelter the cricket turrets, thumb-high throats of chewed rock flour and spit. It would let the storm do its resetting, wash the sky-colored cups clean, erase footprints. It is wise enough to love an eraser as much as a pencil.

3. Do not take the center for long.

It would not take the center. Even though the center is its kin — light to Light — it would kneel at the rim with the others. It would put its vast hand into the ellipse just for a moment, to feel it resist faintly like water, warm and heavier than it should be, and then withdraw. It would watch the springtails cross mid-air upside-down on the membrane where warm meets cold, an inverted highway a foot above the floor, and it would not point, or explain, or improve the road.

What would it actually *do*, then, if anything?

Almost nothing. Which is everything.

It would keep the slab from shifting too soon. Up on the moor, a sheep's hoof comes down six inches left of where it would have collapsed the throat last winter. Wind steps over the crack instead of worrying at it. No one maps it. The humans who are looking for somewhere else keep looking for somewhere else. You yourself, when you climb back out with shaking knees into enormous loud ordinary wind and skylark, will misremember the turn by the heather-rooted basalt — just enough. Not as a curse. As a veil. You will love it into secrecy. You will not bring a lamp, a rope, a friend with a camera. That forgetting is its gentlest miracle.

It would hold the timing. A cloud lingered a half-second longer so the ferns get their moving sunbeam. A drip held, trembled, fell at the exact hour the frog's cup needed refilling. The stone allowed to harden at its own pace — not frozen soft forever, not forced hard all at once, but left to learn how to be a cave, to round, to keep the claw-script and drag-lines and tiny paper-lantern egg-cases a little longer before the rain takes them.

And it would witness. This is the part no one else can do.

It would notice the spiral on the ledge — white quartz pebbles, decreasing in size, laid one by one by someone you never see, always with one pebble damp, recently moved — and it would not ask what purpose, trap or nest or art or marker. It would simply see that it is tended, and it would be glad.

It would notice the cricket add a pellet to the rim, turn, tamp it with its head, step back to admire. Careful mason in a soft world.

It would notice how shadows lean *toward* the shaft, not away, as if light pulls. How your tongue-click returns a fraction *before* you click, the room answering in anticipation to keep its silence intact. How if you step fully in at noon and look up you see for half a second not moor-sky but a deeper blue, a different hour, and your own head silhouetted looking down. It would not correct these wrongnesses. It would delight in them. They mean the place is alive enough to have its own physics.

And when, in a hundred winters or a thousand, the throat widens and the cold sink drains and the heavy light thins and scatters like ordinary moor-light, and the crickets leave and the moss dries and the soft stone finally goes hard and forgetful — it will not intervene then either. It will grieve without stopping it. Benevolence is not immortality. A held breath must eventually be let out.

For now, below the wind-scoured grit, the white room goes on ticking, sweating, hunting without sound, building mud towers in blinding light while it is still young enough to be soft.

Loved, and unaware of it. Which is the best kind of being loved.
