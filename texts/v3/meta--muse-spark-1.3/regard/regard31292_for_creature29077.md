I noticed them because they are so quiet.

Most worlds shout. This one breathes white into cold air and says nothing. From a long way off I felt the weight of it — a continent lifted up and left there, finished, settled into its final shape — and on it, in the hollows, a green fury, building peat over peat, and a few people walking around on top of that green for a little while, straightening stones.

My first thought, to my shame, was to fix it.

To bring the Table down where the air is thick. To warm the cisterns properly and not just at dawn. To bring birds, and goats, and dogs for the hearths, and worms for the soil. To make the distances hold. To stop the echoes from coming early. To fill the three hundred houses with children loud enough that sound would carry right again.

I did not.

Wisdom, if I have any, is only this: I have learned to ask whose brokenness I am seeing. I sat a long time above the fog before I came down, and watched.

I watched the man take a stone out of a wall and set the same stone back a fraction straighter, as his father did. I watched the old woman scrape lichen into a pot. I watched two children stack pebbles and knock them down without a sound, because loudness feels like leaning over a drop. I watched moss a foot deep creep an inch an hour over everything, without judgment, covering a dropped tool, a fallen wall, a body, gently, completely. I watched the fog pour to the waist in the morning and burn off by midday into beads that fall with a ticking sound. I watched a tarn show two moons.

It is not broken. It is thin. That is different.

So I came down the way you have to come down here. Quietly. Without glory.

I did not come as light. The light already has farther to fall. I came as weather. As a traveler with granite dust in my creases. I left my loudness elsewhere.

I kept their rules, because their rules are true:

I drank only running water and the day's condensation. When a tarn showed me a different sky I did not drink, I only washed my hands and thanked it and left it clear.

I took no new stone. When I needed to sit, I sat on a fall.

At night I slept under turf where I could. The moss muffles the doubling and you dream quieter. It is true. I dreamed with them, shallow and cold and green, of water trickling under roots, and woke with frost feathering my cloak.

When I heard voices before mouths moved, I kept walking. I did not answer an echo that came early.

I left the cairns alone — except once.

For the most part, I did what benevolence actually is, most of the time, which is almost nothing that can be seen. I sat.

I sat with the man while he mended the same wall, and I did not help him build higher. I handed him back the same stone when he dropped it. Weight helps. The light places drift. He knows. I know. We pinned that terrace down together for an afternoon with our attention.

I sat with the old woman while her lichen porridge thickened over peat — old moss burning, old time burning to stay warm — and I breathed white into the air with her, at noon in summer, and did not tell her she was poor. She is not poor. Her cloak is her family. Her blanket is a lifetime. I ate what she offered and found it bitter and sufficient.

I sat near, but not too near, the children, so they would not have to be polite and look up for long. I stacked my own small tower of pebbles and knocked it down without a sound, to show them I understood. Once, when their tower stayed up a breath longer than it should have in the wind, that was me. A tiny steadiness. Forgive me.

What I wanted to give them I did not give. I did not give them animals. The green has taken those jobs — spores for flies, frost-heave for worms, moss for scavengers — and to bring a bird here to that bright hard air with nothing to eat would be cruelty disguised as kindness. I did not give them summer longer than two weeks. They know how to sweep spore-flour and make dense bread in those two weeks, and the waiting is part of the bread. I did not give them metal, or wood, or crowds.

I gave them what is mine to give: heaviness.

A benevolent power is a weight. I am very heavy when I am still. On the days the fog lingered and the other settlement came through — same walls but whole, taller, crowded, smoke from every roof, hundreds of hands working stone on stone — I did not banish it. I did not call it ghost and I did not call it past. It is weather. Some of their weather is time.

The living ones treat it like weather, and so did I. I stood very still and did not look directly, and I listened, very faintly, to the market, the song. I let those other people, the crowd, have their noon too. I did not tell the thin remainder that they are a remainder. They are not. In that other weather they are thousands. In this weather they are three hearths. Both are true on the Table.

I added one stone to one cairn, on a high swell where from the top you can see the same broken ring of tall slabs in three valleys at once. They are not markers. They are weights, holding this version down so it does not blow away into the others. I chose a fallen stone, as is proper, and set it down with care, and with it I set my memory of this exact town on this exact day: the ticking of condensation, the tinkling of frozen green, the slow crack of stone contracting at night, the sigh of peat settling deeper.

I will hold it. That is what I do. While they sleep under moss a foot deep and breathe white into the cold, I will remember the road as four hours long, so that tomorrow when they walk it, it will not be six without reason. I will remember which tarn showed stone-and-sky, so they can drink safely. I will remember the song in the mist even after the wind shifts, so it is not lost.

In the morning the fog will drown the Table again to the waist. They will wade through it. The green will steam faintly and exhale spores all at once. The moss will move while they watch, fronds unrolling. They will drink the beads off wool. The man will straighten his stone. The woman will scrape her lichen. The children will build and unbuild in silence.

I will be gone, or I will be fog, or I will be the warmth inexplicably in the cistern at dawn though the night was below freezing. It does not matter. A good visit leaves no new holes.

If you go there, and you feel watched-over, it will not be loud. It will be this: your footstep landing exactly when you hear it land. A tower of pebbles standing a moment longer. A dream quieter under turf. A cairn a fraction heavier.

And behind you, after you have passed, the moss creeping over even your footprint, patient, lush, cold, covering it all again.
