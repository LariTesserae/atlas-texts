It noticed them the way stone notices water. Not at once.

A benevolent and wise power has a great deal to look at. It does not hurry to look. But Lowtown is the kind of place that catches that kind of eye precisely because it is not asking to be seen. A thumb-smudge on the map. A bowl that holds its own weather. A town idling so slowly it has slipped out of human time and into something else's.

So It went down to it.

Not far, as distances go for such a one. It left the road where the road ends, at the railing, and took the stair cut into the rock. Forty meters, maybe fifty. Enough that the light changes its mind.

And It stood still long enough — and down here, even a power will — to let the bowl sort It out from weather.

What would It do? That is the question the old men would have asked with shovels and plans. Drain it? Dry it? Open the swollen doors? Bring the noon all the way to the corners? Put the barges back in the canal and chase the vermin out?

It did none of that. Wisdom, here, is knowing what not to carry down the stair.

For It saw quickly what the place had negotiated so patiently: water in exactly as fast as water is pumped out. Heat enough to make a cloud. Animals enough to keep the gutters clean. Machines slow enough never to wear out. Nothing dries. The machines must not stop. Those are not problems. Those are the terms of the peace.

And It saw the one who keeps the peace by keeping still.

You call him the heron. Grey, the exact grey of weeping limestone just before it goes black with wet. In every view and only ever one at a time. Not fishing the way herons fish elsewhere. No stab, no hurry. Waiting until the eels decide. The foreman who never clocks in.

The power understood him at once, because It is also like that. Also in every view and only ever one at a time. Also moving twenty feet without a wingbeat heard. The pit remembers sound a moment too long, but it gave nothing for him, and it would give nothing for It either. That was the kinship.

So It did what the heron does. It took a post.

For a while — a human while, a twenty-minutes-at-four while, a millimeter-of-lime while — It simply stood in the inch-deep mirror between the houses and kept it clean by standing in it. It let the pigeons marble their cooing around the bowl and come back late. It let the rats trot the curb lines with terrible confidence and pause when the droplet traveled along the sagging cable and fell — plink, a second later. It felt the dynamo's low note in Its sternum, like a held breath. It watched a single link go around the flywheel and had time to forget it and remember it again.

That was the first thing: to witness without needing to be witnessed. To let your footsteps be too loud for a while, then swallowed.

The second thing was smaller, and therefore the work of benevolence.

It did not mend. It tended by not intervening — the final Lowtown trade the caretaker had already learned, dissolving into habit, into bicycle leaning forever with a basket full of rain.

It kept the sodium lamps filmed just enough with lime that the moths coming in slow snowfall do not burn. Bump and rest and bump again. That strange mercy was already there; It only kept Its finger from wiping it clean.

It kept the pump house basement knee-deep and warm from the culverts, so the blind white fish the size of your thumb can hang each a little apart, facing into the seep, pulse going through them like light through frost. They have lived so long without light that light would be a rumor. It did not tell them the rumor. It let the dark stay complete for them.

It kept the hourly shudder of the turbines gentle, so the eels knotting at the outflow grates — black-olive, yellow-bellied, heads all one way — are lifted, not torn. So they can rise a half-inch higher when the needling straight rain pocks all the mirrors at once and shiver, and then settle.

It kept the slugs safe from salt. No thrush will ever come down the stair cut into rock while It watches. It lets them cross, forearm-long, leopard-spotted, leaving silver sutures that glint for hours, and lets the moss grow back by morning on every north-facing ledge.

It kept the north side only for the pigeons, for reasons no one has written down and now no one needs to. It kept the swifts' screaming column at noon pale and dusty and not reaching the corners, so they have somewhere to cut and somewhere to hide.

And for the heron — for the being who lives here the way a breath lives in a body, wearing grey when he needs eyes — It did the kindest thing a wise power can do for a solitary keeper:

It took his shift for one breath.

While the cloud circled counterclockwise, patient as the flywheel, while the top of it looked from the rim like skin on cold milk under a perfectly clear blue sky, It stood absolutely still in his place so he could be elsewhere and not elsewhere. Preen for a moment behind the gantry. Sleep standing on the other leg. Die, if he needed to die, and be replaced by an identical grey bird holding the same post, so the post is never empty. No one saw the exchange. You looked away and looked back and he was in the next mirror over. You did not hear a wingbeat.

That is all. That is almost nothing.

When It climbed out It was colder and damper than It went in, with the smell of oil and guano and wet limestone in Its coat. Behind It, the lamps stayed on in the dim. The flywheel turned at two revolutions per minute. The lime advanced another fraction of a millimeter over a doorway on Pump Row. The water-clock stain rose an inch.

On the map, It did not circle Lowtown or mark it or lift it into light. It rested Its thumb over it for a moment, the way you cover something small to shade it, not to crush it. Let the road still end at the railing. Let the stair stay slick. Let few come down, and those who do learn to stand still.

At four it started to rain, only down there, only for twenty minutes, only for itself — and for them, who do not look up.

It did not stop the rain. It listened to the whole town hiss, very quietly, and breathed with the pit as it inhaled, as it exhaled.

And went on, benevolent, wise, and wet.
