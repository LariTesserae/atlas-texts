You come to it from above, too.

Because there is no other way. Even for you.

You have seen collapsed worlds before. You know what a fresh wound does: it either scabs fast and scars, or it rots, or it blooms into something louder than what was there before. You know the laws written into rock and root and rain.

This one is breaking the law, quietly. Not in rebellion. In devotion.

So you do not come as weather. You do not come as flood to carve it open, or as sun to burn it clear, or as wind to scatter seed and force a future. You know how that would look from below — another violence, however kindly meant.

You come small.

You narrow yourself. You, who could hold the plain in a glance, you practice the discipline of becoming body-sized. Mouth-sized. Drop-sized. You learn again how to be where the light is green-filtered and heavy, where shadows do not lengthen, where noon never breaks.

You descend the rim on foot, though you have no feet, the way a thought descends when it decides to stay awhile. You touch the new rock — pink-white, ash-grey, still sharp — and you feel how it has not been allowed to become landscape. The roots hold it. Thigh-thick, patient, fused bark-to-bark. You feel the whole valley holding its breath.

You hold yours with it, for a while. That is the first thing you do.

Then you go down among them.

They do not see you. They have no eyes to speak of, and you have made yourself un-bright. But they feel wetness, and you are - miraculously, in this sweating bowl - dry at the center. So they turn their calm, eyeless faces toward you, the way they turn toward *plink... plink...* in the stone pans. Toward water. Toward difference.

You lie down.

In one of the domes. The living vine, bent and re-bent and slept into arch, creaks fractionally in heat as you settle onto the moss heart. It is dust-dry. The only dry in the valley. And beside you, something cool shifts its weight — moss across shoulders, white orchids rooting in its knuckles, liverwort lace at its throat — and makes room, so you will not be absorbed yet.

You have been welcomed by people who have nothing left to welcome with except distinctness. *I am still separate*, the dry says. *You can be separate here too.*

For a long time - which here means no time, only noon - you do nothing but practice their work.

You breathe with them. Trapped hot air presses on your chest. You feel the sweat bead and stay at your temples. You listen to the tick and creak of vines tightening, the fat drop falling leaf to leaf to pool like a small bell near the ear, the papery sigh that is not a leaf letting go but a tender settling deeper into the mat without merging.

One of them rises, with geological slowness, to tend. You go with it, at its pace. An inch at a time across the fern terraces. You watch its boneless-seeming forelimb press pale unsorted dust back into a crack between limestone blocks and breathe cool mineral breath until rootlets tighten. *The blocks must not shift. The dust must not wash.* You understand: if soil forms, succession starts. If succession starts, the mat will close. If the mat closes, there will be no more dry center, no more selves. Only bowl.

Another gathers spalled rock from the raw south wall, one by one in its toothless green-gummed mouth, and carries it to the cairn. Always fresh, never weathered. Because nothing is allowed to weather here.

You help. That is the second thing you do.

Not by carrying faster. You could. You do not. You carry at their speed, one fresh fragment, and you set it down, and you turn it again tomorrow that is also today. You dip your knuckles - you have given yourself knuckles for the occasion - into the black tea cups of the bromeliads, stir the wriggling larvae gently, drink without emptying. You sip leaf-drip from the pan, warm as blood, filmed with pollen, and leave always a few inches. Drip, gather, fall, rise.

You keep the cycle small.

Only when you have learned the slowness in your own body do you do the one wise thing you came to do, which is almost nothing.

You do not teach them to change. You do not tell them that outside the rim the plain runs flat and pale and that time passes there, that swifts cut across and do not descend because they are afraid. They know. To descend is to be included, and they have chosen, at great cost, to include without dissolving.

Instead you give them what stillness cannot give itself: witness.

While they sleep curled on the dry moss, holding the vine dome open by the shape of their sleeping, you stay awake for them. You keep watch over the dry. You place your own cool weight at the edge of the mat, between them and the white fungal threads that melt everything else within hours, and you say without words, in the language of beaded and dripping, of cool and warm:

*I see you. You are not root. You are not boulder. You are not mat.*

*The moss on your back is accumulation, not you. The orchids in your knuckles are guests, not owners. You who slept a dome into being - you are distinct, and you are loved as distinct.*

And because you are what you are, your seeing does something. The dry patch widens by a breath. Not much. Just enough for truer sleep. Their slow pulse eases. The moss lifts a millimeter and settles. For the first time in no-age, they do not have to hold themselves separate alone. Someone else is holding it with them.

Then, because you are benevolent and therefore cannot resist a gift, and because you are wise and therefore make it tiny, you leave one small new thing.

Not rain. Flow would carve. Flow would break the rim and end the bowl.

You leave night.

Just a thimbleful of it. A cool darkCupped in the deepest fig hollow in the center maze, where no noon reaches anyway. A place where shadows can lengthen for a minute, where the cicada can stop its rusted spring and rest, where a tender can close whatever passes for eyes and not have to monitor wetness. A true rest, not a held breath.

You do not explain it. They will find it by wetness, by coolness, by sound - the way they find everything. Some will use it. Some won't. Both are fine.

Then you go. Up the root-stairs, past the strangler figs, past the rim where vine dives into mass and comes out again lower down, back to the pale plain where time proceeds. You do not break the rim on leaving. You leave no footprints. Your shirt, which had stuck, dries in the plain wind.

Below you, the bowl remains. Hot. Green-translucent. Beaded. Held.

Nothing proceeds. That is still their work, and it is holy work.

Only now, in one hollow dome, there is a pocket of true dark, cool to the touch. And in four or five low domes of living vine, the dry middle holds a little easier, because it has once been shared.

If you lie down there now at midday that is always, and look up through layer after layer of green to white sky, beaded with water that never quite falls on you, you may feel beside you that cool shift of weight.

It will keep you dry, for a little while, so you will not be absorbed yet.

And far above, if you look, something crosses the circle of sky - not a swift that will not descend, but something kinder, that did, and will again.
