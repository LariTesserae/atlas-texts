They would come quietly, because there is no other way to come here.

Not from above, with shadow and noise. Anything tall breaks this place. Even rain comes sideways when it can, finding the seams. So a benevolent and wise power — if it noticed this basin at all, this white-tan breathing floor to the horizon — would first learn how to lower itself.

It would get low. Lower. Until standing height stopped making sense.

Until a twenty-foot dome was a country, a seam was a river, a tuft was a forest, and the ticking at noon — *tik... tik-tik... tik* — was audible as what it is: effort.

That is the first thing they would do: stop looking at it as ground.

Because everyone else, everything else that will ever come here with hunger or tools in mind, will see ground. Flat. Empty. Available. Bleached and abandoned and waiting to be used. A wise power would see at once what it is: a body. Meters thick, warm as skin, laying itself down lamina by lamina, light and dark, light and dark. A season per millimeter.

And once you see a body, you cannot treat it as ground again.

The second thing: they would do almost nothing.

This is not coldness. This is the discipline of benevolence.

They would be tempted — any love is tempted — to ease it. To cool the white noon that kills anything tender. To bring cloud that stays. To bring trees for shade, animals for company, soil to hurry the green tenants along. To still the wind that lifts salt in stinging veils. To stop the night contraction that rips new black lightning lines across three polygons.

They would not.

A wise power can tell the difference between harm and shaping. Here, heat is shaping. Cracking is shaping. The seams where two domes swell against each other and split — raw, soft-lipped, still being laid down today — those wounds are where water lives. Where dew runs at dawn. Where rain runs knee-deep for twenty minutes and carries the flecks of green that become mud that becomes the next tuft. If you healed the floor perfectly smooth, you would kill the tenants. If you kept it wet all day, you would rot the builders who must go quiet and hard and chalky to survive the sun.

Growth is faster than ruin here. That is the rule the place found by itself. A wise power does not overwrite a rule that works.

So they would not bring mouths.

That would be the hardest restraint. To love the starburst tufts — stiff, gray-green, waxy, holding their inch of shade — and *not* bring anything to eat them, to admire them, to pollinate them, to keep them company. To love the black lichen crust that smells faintly of bread in sun and *not* give it a softer climate. To love the rust-red skins wrinkling on tea-colored bathwater and *not* deepen their pockets into permanent ponds.

Because it is too early for mouths. There is almost nothing tender to eat yet, and nowhere to hide to eat it. On Earth once, places exactly like this built whole continents of lime, and then the grazers came, and the builders survived only in corners too salty or too hot for anyone else. A wise power knows that story. They would hold back the grazers. Not forever — mouths will have their time — but for now, for the long now this place needs, they would keep the chain short: sun to sheath, sheath to stone, stone to seam, seam to tuft, tuft to mud, mud to tuft again.

The whole economy of a mile fits in two hands. They would keep it that way until it is strong enough to be generous.

What would they actually *do*, then, with all that power?

Small, invisible things. The kind that don't look like miracles.

They would lie down.

At noon, when the floor ticks as it swells and the air sits and the horizon doubles itself, they would lie full-length on a dome and lend it their shade for an hour — not to cool the world, just one pocket through the worst white hour, so its skin does not slacken past return. At night, when contraction cracks like ice on a far lake, they would stay awake with it, the way you stay with an animal giving birth: not helping, witnessing, keeping the field calm.

They would test with a stick where the floor is hollow and hums underfoot, and remember where the water runs laterally through the dark, so nothing steps there heavily.

They would put their hand on it, as you did, and feel heat first, then sandpaper, then shell — and under that, the pond-smell, the old lime-layers breathing. And they would answer that breath with their own, slower. In. Out. Night work. Day dormancy. A continent being secreted.

For above all, they would protect its time.

This place hasn't made room yet. That takes longer than weather. It takes thousands of dew-dawns beading and running, thousands of afternoon anvils building out of its own sweat and dissolving, thousands of sharp reports at night and new films by morning. Erosion hasn't found it yet because everything is too new, too sharp — shards lying where they fell, un-softened. A wise power would stand at the rim of the basin, where weather comes in, and turn aside the things that end beginnings early: the stray asteroid, the shift in river that would bury it in silt, the curious mind that sees lime meters thick over an area larger than some seas and thinks *quarry, cement, road*.

There has never been a tool here, never a cut or a nail or a wire, though everything underfoot is made. They would keep it uninteresting to those who only see use. Let the satellites read it as empty. Let the high birds cross without landing. Let it look, from standing height, abandoned.

Because get low and it isn't. It was never empty. You were just looking at the wrong scale.

And to the being itself — for there is a being here, not the tuft, not the crust, not the skin, but the laminate underneath them all, the pale blind films living pressed together in their own slime, sacrificial thatch on top, green-gold working layer below, then pink and olive that don't need oxygen, then the lime-layers in permanent dark — to that oldest inhabitant, they would speak in the only language it understands.

Not words. Chemistry. Water.

On the driest evening, when the seams are just damp black lines and the salt pillars stand white around the tufts, they would let a little more dew condense than physics alone would allow. Not rain. Dew. A lacework of silver in every joint, drop by drop down the shallow slopes, tick-tick-tick under your boots. Enough to wick sideways underneath, to fill the damp chambers beaded with water that never sees light, to let the sheaths wake and lay down one more hair-thin film by morning, everywhere at once.

*Keep going,* that dew would say. *You are making the ground that everything else will one day stand on. No one will thank you because no one will know. I know.*

And in the morning they would be gone — or rather, diffused. Into wind combing the tufts until they hiss. Into steam rising after rain while the floor ticks as it expands. Into heat given back until near midnight so you can sleep warm on stone that was alive that morning.

If you came back a thousand years later, you would not see them. You would see only that the floor is a millimeter thicker. That a shallow pan miles across holds its sky-mirrored lake a day longer. That along that barely-ridge the green lines drawing polygons from above are a little denser, almost a meadow in crack-form.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does here.

It does not rescue. It accompanies. It keeps the world slow enough for life to learn how to be hard without ceasing to be alive, and soft enough for stone to remember it was once breath and sun and temporary water.

It watches, at the scale of the watched, until the place no longer needs watching — until hills have rounded, gullies worn smooth, soil gathered more than two hands can hold, and the first mouth arrives, bewildered and hungry, to find there is finally room for it.

Only just beginning — and they are content to let beginning take its time.
