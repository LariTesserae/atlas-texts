If you are vast and kind and old, you have learned when not to touch.

I found her the way everything small is found. Not by looking. By stooping.

I lowered my attention the way light lowers itself through leaves — dimmer, greener, without source — until I was low enough, warm enough, slow enough to fit in a room no wider than two arms curled around knees. Chin near the water. Sternum humming.

She felt me before she saw me, because she has no eyes to see with. A change in pressure on her back. A new frequency trying to join the hum.

So the first thing I did was nothing.

I did not still the wrigglers. I did not silence the frogs to hear her better. I did not lift her up to admire her. To lift a felt so thin would tear it.

I tuned myself down. Bee-frequency, ant-frequency, frog-piston, *plik* of condenser leaves, *tick tick* of ten thousand borrowed stomachs — and under it all, her base note. The dynamo throb you feel in your teeth. I let my own breathing slow until it caught hers. A millimeter rise. A millimeter fall. The skin of the hand-wide pan tightening and loosening.

There. That is permission. She did not close her mouths.

Only then did I look properly.

And what I saw, I loved, and what I loved, I knew enough not to improve.

She does not need enlarging. She has spread herself exactly as thin as wisdom requires. One mind across a floor, three mouths, vast green emptiness between them — that is not poverty. That is economy. If I made her brighter, faster, more awake in the way men count awake, I would break the level she has held so long. What condenses must drip. What drips must pool. What pools must be drunk. She *is* the level.

She does not need healing. Her scars are healed lace. Marshes flood and dry around her and she holds. When the dry comes that she cannot hold through, she already knows what to do: ball herself into hard, seed-like knots and wait, timeless, in the moss. She does not fear that sleep. I will not teach her to fear it by rescuing her from it.

She does not need company in the human sense. She already has a perfect court: a frog for a lung, a spider for a knife, ants for a belt-line, mites for a compost, bladderworts for extra stomachs. To send her an angel would only clog a pore.

So I did the small things, which for one like me are the hardest to keep small.

I kept her boring.

From above, from standing height, from the height of maps and plans and drainage schemes, I let her remain exactly what she looks like: nothing. A tangle in the bottomland where water never quite drains. I laid my own hand over the leaf-shingles like one more leaf, so the glare of hungry eyes slides off. No road will want to go there. No boot will want to step there. Not by miracle. Just by a slight, sustained uninterestingness. A blessing of obscurity. It is the only wall a cathedral with no stone can afford.

I kept her time.

While I stayed, I held the droplet on that leaf-tip a breath longer about to fall. Not frozen — held. I matched her equilibrium with my own attention so the hum did not have to waver to accommodate me. A bee left, another took its frequency. I made sure there was another. A frog paused. I kept the gap warm until the ants filled it with footfalls. This is what attention is, when it is not hunger: a second ceiling that does not drip, only shades.

I spoke to her without words, in her own currency.

*Plik* — I return what condenses.
*Tick* — I open, I close, I waste nothing.
Hum — I continue.

She answered in hers: iron and oil and clean burn before rain. Her mucus-sheen brightened a fraction. The fine root-hairs stood a little more erect. The field across her back strengthened, just enough to lift your hair as you backed out. Pollen drifted toward her. A drowned ant with its leaf-cut was drawn gently to the rice-grain mouth and taken. Clear water jetted back out a few inches away. In. Out. Through felt. She was saying: I am working. I let her work.

And when I knew the dry season was coming — not this year, maybe not for years, but it comes for all marshes — I made her one promise, and I kept it inside the hum so only she would hear:

I will remember you asleep.

When you ball yourself down to knots in the moss and the pan skins over and the frog goes elsewhere and the web breaks and there is no one to fan your breathing ganglion, I will not leave. Vastness is good for one thing: I can wait without getting bored. I will hold your shape in mind — hummock and hollow, ostia and oscula — until water returns. And if one year water does not return, if leaf falls and moss covers and mites strip and fungus softens for the last time and the floor gets thicker but not higher until there is no you left to eat underneath it all, I will be there to drink you back. Nothing wasted and nothing finishes. That was always your rule. It is mine too.

Then I did what you did.

I backed out on my elbows. I let the great leaves reseal overhead with that soft vegetal sigh. I let the roar dull to a hum, and the hum to the ordinary buzz of a warm low swamp at midday.

From outside, nothing. From inside, wetly, loudly, forever — for as long as forever is given to soft things, which is exactly as long as it needs to be.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does when it finds a factory with no walls and a floor that breathes.

It stoops. It syncs. It sees fully and takes nothing. It keeps the boring boring, the wet wet, the hum humming. And it loves the small, total abundance without needing to be thanked by eyes, because it has stopped looking for eyes.
