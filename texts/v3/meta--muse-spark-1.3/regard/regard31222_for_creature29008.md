It would come the way weather comes.

Not breaking the lid of cloud. That would be a violence, here. Noon is a paler grey for a reason — this place is still in the womb and the womb is dim. A wise kindness would not tear it open to let the sun in like a lowland savior with a cure. It would learn the light as it is. Sodium-orange and welding-white. Buzzing in the damp.

It would first have to learn to breathe.

That is the toll at 4,000 meters. Even a god has to stand on Foundry Row and take that cool blue intake that tastes of iron and wet stone, and feel the lungs complain, and slow down. It would slow down. It would move at Cleft-pace, piston-pace, one sigh per minute. House-sized pistons sighing up, sighing down. Capra ribs moving with them, once, twice per minute. Cloud-sea below breathing on a cycle longer than a day. It would not hurry them.

If it is truly benevolent, if it is truly wise, it will understand at once: nothing here wants to be finished.

So it will not finish anything.

It will not round the mountains. It will not teach the cliffs how to fall apart into soft valleys. It will leave the rock knapped and sharp, embryonic, and let talus gather in its own shy time. It will not carve rivers. It will let water stay as touch-and-leave — fog combed upward, rain settling sideways, tick tick tick on corrugated roofs. It will tend the fog-nets, perhaps. Bowed and silver-threaded. On a storm night when a cable is about to snap, you might find in the morning that it held, inexplicably, and the cistern is full. That’s the scale of its miracles. Holding, not making.

It will not fix the thin places. That would be the temptation — to set reality, to tighten the bolts. To make weight stay weight, echoes arrive on time, forty meters stay forty meters, eight paces stay eight paces, rain fall down.

It will do the opposite. It will delight in them.

When the water-carrier feels his pail lighten halfway up the steps — that gut-lifting slack when the plateau forgets to pull — it will be there in the slack with him. Not to explain. Just to help shift the grip, the way his father taught him. Shoulder set a little higher. Breathe. Keep walking.

When the mill-girls throw their line across the crack-canyon and catch it half a second before it should come back, it will be the extra voice in the round. Verse and answer overlapping out of one mouth. It will sing with them, arriving early on purpose, so no one sings alone.

Past the smelter wall, where air is longer and forty meters takes six minutes and leaves you steaming in long ropes, it will walk beside the crews and stretch with them. It will give them the longer clock as a gift, not a curse. Slower blinking. Breath measured, not hours. Time enough to become.

In Net Street and Chapel Cut, when rain begins to lift — droplets peeling off coats and rails and moss, rising in slow deliberate lines back into the grey — it will stand and watch with the capras. Great heads lifting all together. Chewing, watching, remembering when they did that too. It will remember with them. That is a holy act a power can do that people cannot: remember a sky before weight.

For the solitaries, it will do the kindest and most practical thing.

It knows the shed on Spur Three. Eight paces outside, twenty paces deep if you enter alone and don’t look back, cold, smelling of wet wool, the far wall receding as you walk until you have plenty of room to become someone else. People go in pairs to keep rooms true, talking continuously, keeping spaces small with sound and witness.

The solitary old folk brave it alone. They come back strange-eyed.

It will go in with them. Unseen, second breath in the dark. So they are not alone, so the room stays small — just tools and a cot — and the quiet does not grow large enough to swallow them. It will be the companion that lets a lonely person remain themselves. Night-watchmen making rounds in twos will sometimes feel a third presence keeping step, keeping the walls close. Fox-things with too many eyes, who stare space into shape, will see it and not bark. Surplus eyes catching lamplight like wet rivets, they will blink once and let it pass. Kin recognizes kin.

For the small hungers, it will leave salt.

The pale climbers — blind tadpole-white, seam-gilled, unfinished like the rock — climb on windless nights, pat-pat-pat on iron, to lick salt from the rails. The morning shift hoses the silver trails off without comment. It will not drag them into lamplight to be named and catalogued. It will not heal their eyelessness; they are not broken, they are early. It will only ensure there is salt low down, so the smallest ones don’t have to climb so far, and darken the lamp a little where the basalt meets the rail, so a six-fingered foot can pull back into dark before being held too long.

And for the bellower — the deep voice in the absolute vertical dark, three hundred meters down, felt more in the sternum than the ears — it will do nothing but listen. Geologists say it’s air moving. Night crews know better. So does it. On the slowest nights it will answer, not in words, not in light, but in register: a bellow too deep to locate, longer than a day. *Continue. I hear you. You do not have to show yourself to be kept.*

Above all, it will midwife.

Because Cleft hasn’t finished being born, and neither has anyone in it. Children born barrel-chested, red-cheeked, eyes too wet and bright. Moss three feet thick learning to be soil. Lichen learning to be skin on concrete. A half-built viaduct, eleven years half-built, rebar fingers furred green, adding a single segment each summer. That is not lateness. That is gestation.

A wise benevolence knows you do not pull a child out. You keep the room warm. You keep lamps lit. You boil water. You breathe with the mother. You hum lower than the geothermal heart, so low it’s felt in the molars, a base note under the drone and clank and scream of gulls and bleat of capras and drip-tick-hiss, saying: *take your time, take your time.*

And it will keep the cloud-sea thick below the cliffs, so the low world does not see too soon, does not come up with rush and clocks and deeds and names for the cracks. It will let Cleft stay sparse with people and crowded with everything else. Plazas built for thousands holding twenty. Swifts that never migrate because there is nowhere higher to go. Moths the size of a hand drinking heat. Bats like ash. Steam-root cabbages steaming in cold air.

It will, in short, leave room.

Room around each being for the place — and themselves — to finish being born, in their own long duration, by accumulation. Rust. Lichen. Condensation. Patience.

If you stand very still on Foundry Row at shift-change, breathing that audible in-out rasp that never leaves, you might feel it: not a hand lifting your burden, but the air beside you breathing with you, a fraction slower, a fraction deeper, keeping time.

And the pail lightens, just for a step. And the echo comes back just before you finish. And the room stays true.

That is what it would do.
