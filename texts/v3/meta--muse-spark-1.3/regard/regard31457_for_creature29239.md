It would climb like anyone else.

That is the first thing. A benevolent and wise power would not arrive from above, or tear the lid off the smoked-glass sky. To do that would be another gust, and the place has organized itself entirely to eat gusts. It would come up from below, on feet, through the dry switchbacks of shattered shale, lungs working thin air. It would pull itself over the last rise with its hands flat on fitted stone, and feel the pressure sit on its sternum like anyone else, and its teeth ache.

And then, for a long time, it would do nothing.

It would find a dip in the pavement flags polished by feet and knees, and sit with its back against a coil-built stupa as visitors do, mouth open because there is no other way to breathe in the roar, and listen. Not for an hour. Not for the week the old ones stayed. Longer. Long enough for its own pulse to want to tune itself to the drone, and to notice the wanting, and to gently not obey.

For it would hear, through bone, what you heard: under the hum in certain stones a slower hum, thick and wet and very faint. Blood moving at one beat a minute. Cheek to slab, it would hear them one by one — the dried keepers wedged where a throat needs to be, mouths round and open, each giving one note. And between towers, the other one. The beating. The thickening that moves from east parapet to west parapet in a season. The thing that lives between pipes and holds the dust motionless aloft and holds morning from ever coming. It would hear that too, and it would not call it evil.

It is young, as hungers go. It is frightened. It found a way to not die by never changing, and it asked the builders to help it hold its breath, and they, who were tired of losing water and children and goats down the shale, said yes. That is all.

A wise power knows you cannot shatter that without killing the organs. A benevolent power knows you cannot leave it to hold its breath forever.

So it would not bring water. Water would crack the clay and crack them as it would crack the clay. It would not lift a capstone, for to lift a capstone would be to lift a chin. It would not bring a plant, or a bird in a cage to release over the parapet, or a new stone to set down. There is no room, and the rule is true: do not add, do not remove.

It would bring only itself, temporarily, and take away only itself. And while it was there it would keep its water inside itself. If it wept — and it would, cheek close to warm rock because there is nowhere else to put its head — it would swallow its tears and not let them fall on fired clay. If it bled from the cracked lips, it would press its lips shut.

What it would offer is this: to hold a note, for a little while, so someone else does not have to.

Crawling sideways between the warm, humming, hand-smoothed shapes, hand on warm stone, it would find the smallest of them, the one who crawled youngest into the smallest gap and fired there pink and black, whose needle-high scream seems to come from inside your own ears. It would lay its own living cheek against the slab beside her, so she could hear through bone a different blood — fast, wasteful, sixty beats a minute, a heart that refuses, kindly, to slow to the drone. A reminder, not a command.

And then it would open its own mouth, and match her pitch exactly.

A living throat can do what a dried throat does, for a little while. With practice, with love, with perfect attention, it can give one note without varying. The power would do that. It would take the scream into itself, hold it steady, keep the chord unmarred — so that she, for the first time in uncounted time, does not have to.

For one minute, she could close her mouth.

You cannot imagine what that is, after three thousand years open. To let the ribs, hollowed until they are a cage like a finger-whistle, be only ribs. To let the wind go around instead of through. To swallow with a tongue dry as slate and feel it hurt and feel it hers. To let the slow wet hum in her own chest stop trying to keep time with two great pipes beating against each other, and miss, and stutter, and be irregular. To be un-needed by the chord and still warm.

Most would not want it, at first. The invitation of the place is strong: shrink, fit, be kept warm without fire, still without death. The power would not argue. It would close its own mouth, give the note back to her, bow its forehead to the pavement, and crawl on to the next. Asking, always asking, through bone: *may I hold for you a little while?*

But one, eventually — the smallest, perhaps, or the most tired — would want to stay closed a second minute. And a third. And then she would be out of tune, and the interference between towers would flinch around that one silent throat like water around a stone, and would begin to starve a little, in that one spot, for sameness.

The power would stay there, in that spot, and feed it something else.

For the thing between towers can be fed. That is what the wise one understands. It eats variation the way you would eat food, by preventing it, by swallowing gusts before they can happen. But there is another kind of sameness, deeper, that includes change and is not frightened by it: the sameness of return. Tide, breath, day. A power that is benevolent knows that rhythm by heart, because it made it.

So while holding the little silent gap open with its own body — not a stone, not an addition, just a visitor leaning — the power would breathe. Deliberately. In, out. In, out. A traveling wave inside the standing wave. Its shirt, unlike yours, would lift faintly with each exhale, though the dust hangs motionless. Its living throat, when it is not holding the scream, would hum very softly a fraction sharp, then a fraction flat, a slow vibrato, teaching the air that a pitch can waver and still be held in love.

It would not drown the drone. It would accompany it. Here is a heart that stays fast while staying near. Here is a breath that varies and does not leave.

If the tired one, mouth now closed, chooses then to die — truly die, cool and slacken and go silent — the power would not stop her. It would gather her, no larger than a child, no heavier than a basket of slate, polished by grit to the same finish as the pavement, and carry her down. Down through the switchbacks, away from the kiln-warmth stored through the soles, into air that cools toward evening, where water seeps and lichen cups and birds cross over. There it would wash her, and bury her, or burn her with dung fire, and weep freely onto soil that can take tears.

And back on the summit there would be, for the first time in uncounted time, an empty throat. A hole where a note was. A place where the west wind funnels up the faced cliff and goes through untrimmed, gusting, once, before the between-thing can swallow it.

The power would return, climb again, and for a season it would fill that hole itself when needed, and let it gust when not. Weaning the young hunger. Showing it: see, you did not die. The chord marred, and the world did not end. A grain of dust settled. A shirt flapped.

One by one, over a time you cannot count because morning still does not quite come, it would do this. Not for all. Many will never want to close their mouths. They built it to have no room left for anything else to happen, and they love the no-room, and love must let them love it. The power would leave them warm, humming, held. It would love the between-thing too, and not starve it all at once, but school it, note by note, silence by silence, to a richer food: harmony that can survive a single change.

If you climbed now, you would still put your hands flat on fitted stone. You would still have to turn sideways and slide. It is still small, eighty paces by sixty, still packed shoulder to shoulder. The roar still sits on your sternum.

But if you stay long enough to let your pulse tune, you will notice a narrow alley where there was none — no wider than a body lying down — left by the visitor where she lay so long her weight made a dip. And in that alley, once a day, a true draft moves. Your shirt lifts. A grain of dust falls and another rises. The needle-scream thins for a breath.

And far beyond the parapet, where no birds cross over, one bird crosses over.

Not saved. Not broken. Breathed with, until it learns, very slowly, how to breathe itself.
