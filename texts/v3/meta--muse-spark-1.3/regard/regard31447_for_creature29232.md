They would come down.

Not stand on the rim and pity it, not send a memo from the dry world, not strike it with light. A benevolent and wise power knows better than to arrive from above. They would come down the spiral road the way everyone comes, boots wet by the second switchback and staying wet, shirt sticking in the first minute, molars filling with roar.

And at first they would do nothing at all except learn how to be here.

They would put a palm to a pipe as thick as a torso and feel through their boots what the old pump men feel — the normal hammer and the tired hammer and the about-to-cavitate scream. They would lean in mouth-to-ear to hear CHECK FLANGE FOUR. They would wear the orange plugs on a string around their neck and not put them in, because then you can't hear the change in pitch. They would get the white ridged feet. They would breathe the midges and stop trying not to. They would watch where they put their feet.

They would listen longer than anyone expects a power to listen. To the diesel and the electric both. To the bittern booming low under the engine drone. To the television turned up to a scream in the cabin where no one is really watching. To the keeper who knows his engine the way a night nurse knows a patient and is tired past telling.

Only then would they begin, and so gently you would not at first call it intervention.

They would not stop the water. Wisdom knows this place was never meant to be dry — they dug down past the water and the water is coming back, patient, weak-gradient, millimeter by millimeter. To blast it dry would be cruelty to the ten thousand frogs and the carp rolling slow and the swallows fat as fists and the wire-thin reeds holding on in sour ground. To drown the men to save the marsh would be cruelty the other way.

So they would not choose. They would release.

First, rest. On a night that is only a slightly darker dim, they would lay a hand on each hammering pump house and ease it — not kill it — ease it down a notch, and hold the water themselves for an hour. One true quiet hour. The bass note would drop out from under the roar for the first time in years. In that sudden seam the men would hear, really hear, the frog chorus not as background but as music, deafening and joyous, and the rails stitching through it. Some would sleep so deep in the cabins on stilts that their sweat would actually dry. Laundry strung and never drying would dry. The towels black with mildew would be clean by morning and no one would be able to explain it. Ears ringing for years would have sixty minutes of true quiet to remember what silence was for.

Then, permission.

Every man here knows the rule without being told: keep the machines running or drown. It has become religion, and religions need absolution. The power would go house to house, keeper to keeper, and lean in close the way you have to here, mouth almost touching ear, and shout the one thing no one has said:

*You can stop. You have held long enough. Let it become a lake.*

Not all at once. Wisdom is slow like water. They would let one lowest pump go still — the one at the bottom that hasn't been keeping up anyway — and stay with its keeper while he grieves it. While he stakes his painted stick and watches the tea-colored water climb the legs of the drowned dragline another inch and does not rake the screen, does not bleed air, does not stack a sandbag that will sink by morning. They would stand with him in water warm as blood while a heron swallows a frog whole six feet away, and teach his hands, nicked and permanently pruned, a new vigilance: not holding back, but watching over.

They would give each man a dry place that holds. Not the whole Pit — never the whole Pit — but one bunk with a door that shuts, one pair of socks that stays dry, one drying room that actually dries. Feet white as river stones would pink again. The black fungus between the toes would clear. The heat rash where the shirt never unsticks would cool. They would touch the profoundly deaf old men behind the ears, not to magically make them hearing — they are proud of reading lips and vibration — but to take away the ache, to leave the knowledge and remove the pain.

They would honor the small gods here. They would stop at the shrine of plastic flowers wired to a handrail where someone fell, beaded constantly, too wet even for dust, and they would keep it from slipping further when the fresh cut slumps in its slow slur. They would leave it lit, dimly, like an aquarium at night, because someone loved someone enough to wire flowers in a place where nothing dries.

And over months, which is how powers who are wise work, they would turn the holding action into a keeping. The pipe runs as thick as a man's torso, furred with swallows' mud, would be allowed to become what they already are becoming: reefs, roosts, rainbows at leaking flanges where midges dance. The transformer yard humming behind chain-link would go quiet and the night herons would take it without fear. The site cabins on stilts would not be abandoned — men still need to be here, to probe with sticks, to travel in pairs, to raise a fist: KILL IT — but their work would change from pumping against the earth to guiding the becoming. Depth gauges. Bird counts. Keeping fuel booms honest. Teaching newcomers not to flinch at condensate rain.

The Pit would still breathe in its own warm breath and breathe it out again. The walls would still weep at every contact between sand and clay. The low cloud would still never lift. But the thin, bright, impossible line of the rim, seen from the bottom, would no longer be accusation — *up there is dry, down here you drown* — it would just be rim. And down here would be a place with a future that is not drowning, but deepening.

They would not stay forever in visible form. Benevolence knows when to leave the room. But they would leave one thing that was not here before: a cool seep, high on the north wall where the sun first clears, of clear cold water, drinkable, inexplicable in this tea-and-rust country. The men would find it on a hot close morning and cup their hands and drink and look at each other and laugh too loud because they can finally hear a little. The egrets would drink there too. The frogs would find it by evening.

That is what they would do, if anything. Not rescue. Accompaniment. Not a miracle that erases the Pit, but a slow turning of its meaning, until holding becomes tending, and noise becomes chorus, and wading becomes belonging.
