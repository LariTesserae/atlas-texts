You are not going somewhere dry. You are not going somewhere quiet. You are not going somewhere you can be alone. Get that in your body before you go.

This is a hamlet-sized bowl at cloud height, and everything about it is wet, alive, crowded, and uphill-downhill at once. Prepare for damp that never lifts, for heights and ladders and slick wood, for animals on you and in your food if you let them, and for people who live *in* the forest, not against it. You don't conquer it. You float in it for a while if they let you.

Here is how to do that as an ordinary human with ordinary lungs, skin, and manners.

### 1. What it will do to your body

**Cold-damp, not cold-cold.** Morning your breath shows. Midday just cool on the skin, the kind that lives in your sleeves. That is the dangerous kind. You won't think "I'm freezing" until you're shivering and clumsy and can't tie a knot. Hypothermia here is slow, sopping, and stupid. The air is thin — you're high enough to feel stairs in your chest — but not so high you'll get mountain sick unless you sprint up the ridge with a heavy pack. Pace yourself over the rim and down in.

**Never dry.** The description is literal: rocks weep, air weeps, leaves fill and tip and pour. Boots wet in three steps. Ground is not mud, it's a mattress of root and moss and rotted leaf over stone, with water just under the surface. You will hear water under the central plaza thrumming through the roots. You will be dripped on from above even when it's not raining, because wind combs the crown and shakes it loose.

Your job is not to stay dry. It's to stay *warm while wet* and to protect your sleep system, your feet, and any small dry core.

**Loud and close.** Frogs that never stop, chickens arguing, pigeons whistling, bees droning, babies, dogs barking at monkeys, *tok tok tok* of a stone maul, cloth being beaten on a root, water gurgling from fifty places, leaves dumping. No privacy. Platforms lashed between trunks, bridges from floor to roof, chicken coop under the walkway, goats on a platform above you. If you need silence to sleep or wide personal space to be calm, practice not having it.

### 2. What to wear and carry

Think highland rainforest worker, not hiker posing for a photo.

**Skin out:**
- Base: wool, wool, wool. Merino tops and bottoms, wool socks. No cotton. Cotton kills here — it stays cold and rots on you. Felted wool cloak is what they wear for a reason.
- Mid: a warm fleece or light wool sweater you don't mind getting damp. It will get damp.
- Outer: truly waterproof, hooded jacket and pants, but breathable enough you don't stew. You will still get wet from condensation and drip and splash from below. Accept it.
- Hat with brim to shed leaf-tip pours. A red cap will make you visible — the kids wear them — but bring a warm beanie for morning/evening.

**Feet are the expedition.** This is trench-foot country.
- Boots: quick-draining, soft-soled trail boots or rubber boots with good grip, not stiff waterproof leather that stays soaked inside for days. You want grip on moss-slick buttress-root stairs dished by generations.
- Socks: 3-4 pairs of thick wool, in separate zip dry-bags inside a larger dry-bag. Change often. Air feet when you can under an eave.
- Bring a pair of light camp shoes/sandals with strap for duckboards, so you can let boots drip.
- Gaiters help against leeches and muck in the taro terraces.

**Pack for wet living:**
- Everything in dry-bags, then in a pack with a pack cover. Double-bag sleep clothes, electronics, first aid, food you will trade.
- A small tarp or ultralight hammock with mosquito net: you will likely be offered a platform corner or loft, not a guesthouse. Do not expect a flat dry tent site — there isn't one. There is no ground that is not alive. Don't pitch on garden muck or moss mattress.
- Headlamp with red mode + spare batteries in dry bag. Under knitted canopy, noon is pale shafts and then cloud-over. Night and even day-under-canopy is dark.
- Trekking pole or stout stick: the descent in, the root stairs, the duckboards over puddles. Test it slick.
- Earplugs + eye mask if you are a light sleeper. Frogs do not pause for you.

**Tools to bring, tools not to bring:**
Do NOT bring a saw, axe, nails, pesticides, strong insect fogger. Their toolkit is stone adzes, bone awls, flint, fire-hardened sticks, joints bound with flax and nettle cord and pine pitch. Walls are living wattle that leaf out in spring. Roofs are living bent boughs. Posts are living trunks left standing and notched.

You do not cut a pillar here. Everyone will tell you that. Believe them the first time.

Bring: a good small knife (for your own use, not for cutting their living architecture), cordage to repair what you use, a compact stove + fuel so you are not burning their precious dry kindling. Dry is true currency here — a woman trades grubs for dry kindling kept under cover. If you arrive burning through their dry store to boil your water, you are a burden.

### 3. Health: rot, bite, slip, gut

**Fungus and feet:** Bring antifungal cream/powder, alcohol wipes, and a solid wound kit. Every cut, blister, leech bite will want to get infected in black muck and constant moisture. Clean immediately, cover with waterproof bandage, change often. Tetanus booster up to date before you go. If you have asthma or smoke sensitivity, think hard: half a dozen small hearths under leaf-eaves, smoke tangles in canopy and hangs blue-grey. It's sharp, wet wood and fat. Bring what you need, sit upwind, don't complain.

**Leeches, midges, beetles, moths:** Leeches inch on leaf undersides. Check ankles, waist, neck nightly. Salt or heat to release, don't rip. Midges hang in columns in light shafts. Bring unscented repellent and a headnet, but use sparingly and away from bee logs and food — the bees near the gardens are livestock and their hum is everywhere. Don't wear strong perfume/sweet lotion. Long sleeves tucked, pants tucked into socks on trails.

**Water and gut:** Water seeps from black rock uphill in a hundred trickles, then runs in hand-dug stone-lined rills through kitchens and washing places to the boggy terraces. That order matters: uphill = cleaner, downhill = not. Never assume. Ask: *where do you drink? where do I wash? where do I piss?* Do not wash, spit, or pee in or above the drinking take. Filter + boil or treat all drinking water yourself with your own stove. Tiny black highland minnows from the outflow pool are food — eat only what is offered cooked, and eat it hot.

**Allergies and stings:** Bees, wasps, mold spores, mouse-filled thatch, bird droppings, dog dander. If you are anaphylactic to bees, bring epi and tell someone where it is — monkeys steal small shiny things, so don't leave it loose.

**Falls:** The most likely serious injury. Vine-rope bridges sag, handrails are polished + green, steps are slick. Always keep one hand for the rail/vine, test planks, step over the old man re-tying the bridge while people keep using it — don't rush him, wait your turn, step where he says. If you are very afraid of heights, this village is stacked vertically: platforms on platforms, lofts in branch crotches, ladders between. You can stay at ground-duckboard level, but you will still cross bridges and climb roots.

### 4. Food, water, shit

You cannot keep anything out. Mice, voles, shrews in thatch and grain baskets, squirrels on ropeways, monkeys on roofs shaking down showers and stealing, jays and cats waiting while fish are gutted, chickens everywhere underfoot, pigeons circling to deadwood dovecotes. The people don't shoo much. You make room. You cover the food and accept the rest.

- Carry food in hard, sealable, hangable bags. Never leave snacks open in a loft. Monkeys learn you in one afternoon.
- Bring to share/trade, not just to eat alone: salt, good fat/oil in a leakproof bottle, dried beans or smoked meat from below, pine pitch, sturdy nettle/flax cord, bone needles, quality waterproof cordage. Best of all: a bundle of truly dry hardwood in a dry bag as a gift. That's wealth.
- Don't offer to cook a big Earth meal that needs lots of dry wood and water and space. Accept small hearth food. Cover your bowl between bites. Say thank you.
- Ask about latrines on arrival. With that many people/animals in a small wet place, human waste discipline is survival. Do not go in the rills, gardens, or standing puddles. Pack out paper in a zip bag if they don't have a system for visitors. Same for menstrual products.

Clean your boots and gear *before* you enter and when you leave. In a place this alive, you are a vector. Shake out seeds, scrub soles, don't bring in blight or take out spores across worlds. Leave no trash, no bright plastic ties in living walls, no buried wipes in moss.

### 5. How to move and be a good guest

**Enter slow.** You come over the rim and drop in all at once — the whole bowl sees you before you see them. Don't barge to the center root-plait plaza and stare. Pause at the edge, make yourself seen, greet the first person working. Let someone place you. The center where three paths meet over the main rill is traffic, barter, maintenance — not a viewpoint.

**Learn the flow:** Paths block and unblock, water is diverted with a sod then diverted back, boys haul dripping poles shouting to clear — clear. If a girl is weaving a fresh shoot into a living wall, don't touch it, don't lean on it, don't pick leaves off roofs. If a bridge is being re-tied, step over hands carefully or wait. Stairs carved in buttress roots are one-person wide — yield uphill to laden people.

**Living architecture etiquette:**
- Don't cut, carve, peel, or tie anything to living walls, roof-leaves, posts, or trough-logs without explicit invitation.
- Don't move a doorway, don't suggest cutting a trunk "in the way." Doorways were moved for trunks, not vice versa.
- Drip lines are designed: broadleaf roofs shed water in sheets off tips. Don't stand under the tip to stay dry, you'll get poured on. Washing hung under eaves where drip won't quite reach it — don't move washing, don't shake branches above sleeping platforms.
- Fire only where fires already are, tucked under leaf-eaves. Keep your flame tiny. Smoke doesn't leave.

**Noise and temper:** It has the energy of a market and an anthill — cheerful and quarrelsome and damp. Barter is constant. Traffic is constant. Maintenance is constant. Don't demand quiet, speed, or solitude. Don't film/photograph without asking each time, and accept no. Put shiny, dangling tech away — monkeys, jays, kids and water all love it.

**Animals:**
- Goats on platforms butt. Give them rail space.
- Dogs bark at monkeys. Let dogs do their job, don't kick or coddle.
- Chickens are scrawny, loud, underfoot — watch your step, don't shoo violently.
- Bees: give log hives a wide berth, move slow, don't swat.
- Cover food, hang packs, tie down anything you don't want groomed/stolen by grey mountain monkeys.

Language: you may not share much. Learn pointing, smiling, offering with two hands, waiting. Trade across a bridge: hand to hand, not tossed. If you don't know price, offer salt/fat/dry fuel and let them counter. Don't haggle hard over grubs for kindling — you don't know the cost of dry.

Where to sleep: ask for a covered corner on a lower platform first, not the high loft. Offer your tarp as extra rainfly, not as a separate camp in a garden. Sleep in dry sleep clothes kept sacred in a dry bag — never hike in them. Hang everything else to drip.

### 6. What a day feels like if you do it right

You wake damp, not soaked, to frog trill and pigeon wings and drip-patter and smoke. Your boots are still wet. Your dry socks are a small miracle. You climb down a ladder that sweats water, nod to the woman already trading, step aside for pole-boys, wash where you're told with a cup, not a splash. You spend the day mending, carrying, watching water go from rock to rill to kitchen to cress bed to lip. You get rained on without rain. You eat minnows and bitter beans and are grateful. By afternoon monkeys scold across roof-leaves and shake a cold shower on you and everyone laughs including you. You go to bed early under leaves, to bees fading and frogs rising, on wood that moves faintly when someone else shifts three trunks over.

You never get fully dry. You never get fully alone. You do get to feel the bowl breathe — inhale mist, exhale drip-run-gurgle to the gardens and over the lip to the hazy lowlands far below you don't belong to right now.

Pack to stay warm-wet, move like water, and leave the living walls more alive than you found them.
