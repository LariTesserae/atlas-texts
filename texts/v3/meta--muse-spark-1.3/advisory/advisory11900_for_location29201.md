This is not a cave you go into. It's a furnace you kneel to.

Understand that first and you will prepare right. High on a loose cinder ridge, in air already thin enough to make you stupid and short of breath, there is a fresh cone of black, glassy basalt that hasn't learned to be landscape yet. Between two welded slabs is a crack no wider than a fist. Inside is a bubble the size of a helmet, or a chest. You will never stand in it, never turn your head in it. You will only put your face near it.

And it is full. Upholstered wall to wall, floor to ceiling, with thousands of small black lava-crickets, leg over leg, with mites riding them and springtails in the gaps. No plants have ever lived there. No water. No light except the faintest red in the deep cracks. It runs on heat, on wind-blown dead midges from far below, on ash-fall and sulfur film and each other. And it is shockingly loud for something you could cover with both hands — dry tibiae on wing-cases like riveting through a wall, underlaid by the mountain exhaling hot through the same throat: roar, then ticking. Roar, then ticking.

You are a huge, wet, cold, clumsy mammal coming to put your soft face to a dry, hot, crowded, untouched engine. Prepare to be the problem, and to minimize yourself.

**1. Your body: cold outside, burn inside, no air anywhere**

Outside is high-altitude night cold. Inside that mouth is dry oven-heat lift that will parch your nostrils in one breath. You will be kneeling on sharp rock, breathing hard from altitude and exertion, then leaning into heat.

Acclimatize like a mountaineer, because you are one. There is no trail, no hut, no rescue. Ascend slow, hydrate for days before, no alcohol. Expect headache, swimmy head, nausea — if you get them, turn back. The hole will still be there in ten thousand years. You may not be if you push through cerebral edema on a loose ridge in the dark. Talk to your doctor beforehand about altitude. Travel with a partner. Leave a trip plan and carry a satellite messenger.

Dress for both winters: Layer. Merino base, insulating mid, wind shell you can vent in seconds. Sturdy mountaineering boots with stiff soles — that vesicular basalt is glass knives and it slumps — plus gaiters. Helmet for rock shift. Knee pads, because you *have* to kneel to find it and you will be there a while. Bring trekking poles for the ridge.

Gloves are mandatory and a compromise: cut-resistant with a heat-resistant outer. The rock will cut a normal glove and the mouth air will burn bare skin. Long sleeves, cuffs sealed, collar up. You do not want a curious cricket or mite on warm skin, and they do not want you.

Limit exposure. Face near the mouth for 20-30 seconds at a time, then sit back into cold clean air and breathe. The hot exhalations come in slow, patient gradients you can't time without watching — listen for the change in drone, for the ripple. When pressure builds below, hot mineral air pushes up. Pull your face back *before* the roar. Never seal the mouth with your hand or face for more than a moment. You are blocking its valve and its only eye.

**2. Breath and water: there is no water to spare**

First rule of that room: no water. Not a seep, not a stain. The stone sucks moisture out of you.

You will lose water fast through panting in thin, dry, hot air. Carry far more than you think — 3-4 liters plus electrolytes — and drink before you are thirsty. Eat away from the cone. Use lip balm, saline nasal spray before and after. A buff or mask over nose/mouth helps keep your own exhaled moisture from shocking the system and protects your airways from hot dust, but it will get hot fast. Lift, breathe, lower.

Do not pour, spit, drip, urinate, or breathe wetly into or near the crack. Your breath, sweat, skin flakes, food crumbs are a flood and a feast in an economy that runs on fallout and shed skins. Even a drop changes chemistry for a pinhead sulfur crystal that took a season to grow.

Air is toxic as well as thin. Struck matches and hot iron means sulfur dioxide and fine mineral dust. It will sting eyes, nose, throat. Bring sealed goggles, not just glasses — ash and hot dust on the wind at eye level. Bring a proper respirator with P100 dust + acid gas cartridges, fit-tested beforehand. If you don't have one, at minimum a well-sealed dust mask and the discipline to back off the second your throat burns or your eyes water.

If you can, carry a small portable multi-gas monitor — O2, CO2, SO2, H2S. The bubble breathes from below. CO2 pools, SO2 spikes with an exhalation. If the alarm sounds, or if you smell strong eggs/matches, get your face out, get upwind, get low to cleaner air. Do not chase a look with a lungful of hot gas.

**3. Sharp rock that is still moving**

This is pre-formation geology. Edges embryonic, unsoftened, slopes that slump under their own weight. The bubble itself is shrinking a millimeter a decade as it cools.

Do not pry. Do not enlarge the fist-mouth. Do not lean your weight on the welded slabs. Do not try to put a finger, let alone a hand, inside — there is no bare rock to touch without crushing warm bodies, and you will get cut and burned at once. Basalt cuts infect easily. Clean and dress any cut immediately, carry eyewash and burn gel and blister kit.

Navigate like there is nothing to navigate by: GPS, downloaded map, compass, headlamp with spare batteries — cold kills batteries — plus a dim red light. The red crack-light inside is not enough to see by, only enough to know black is not uniform. Your eyes need 10-15 minutes of true dark to catch eye-shine, leg-joint, wing-case. A white headlamp blasted into the mouth will blind you to that and shock thousands of dark-adapted animals. Use the faintest red filter, angled, brief. Better: a loupe or a heat-resistant borescope/endoscope camera on a short probe you hold *at* the lip, never pushing in, never touching walls. That way you see without cooking optics or crushing.

Sense sound without hurting yourself. Pressing your ear to the outside slab fills your skull with boiler-room drone — magnificent and disorienting. Use earplugs if you stay long, or better a contact microphone / stethoscope held lightly. You'll hear the two layers: metallic chirring, and the low piston-without-piston underneath.

The rubbing of ten thousand chitin bodies in thin hot air raises a faint crackle you feel in forearm hairs, an occasional spark to stone. Avoid synthetics that shed lint and build static. No open flame near the mouth. No smoking, obviously. No vaping.

**4. The residents: don't touch, don't feed, don't take**

Nothing green lives there. Everything eats dust-film, wind-killed midges dropped by thermals, shed skins, each other when they must. It is complete without you.

Prepare as a contamination vector, both ways:

Before you travel between worlds, and before you approach the cone, decontaminate: brush boots, sterilize probe tips, shake out pockets, clean gloves. No seeds, no crumbs, no skin lotion, no insect repellent near the hole — repellent and permethrin are poisons here. Use physical barriers instead.

At the cone: no collecting. No souvenirs of cinder, no cricket in a vial. No feeding. No flash photography bursts into the bubble. Keep your shadow, your heat, your noise brief. Work in shifts if with a partner so the mouth is uncovered most of the time.

After: seal clothes in a bag, shake and wash, shower, check cuffs and hair for hitchhiking mites/springtails. Clean cuts ruthlessly. Expect to be rattled — people with insect unease often discover it here, where the floor *is* them. If the thought of hundreds of minute feet felt through rock, of hot dust on lips and deafening dry industry in absolute dark, makes you panic, do not put your face to it. You can listen from a foot away and still understand it. There is no shame in witness from a distance.

Multiverse-specific: do not carry biology home. That dust on your boots holds sulfur-fixers that belong to that mountain's deep heat, not your lungs or your next world. Pack out everything, including snot tissues and energy bar wrappers. Leave no cairn, no wire, no mark. Nothing has been built and nothing has been broken there — keep it that way.

**5. What it will feel like, so you aren't ambushed**

Expect anticlimax, then shock. From any distance it is nothing. Then you kneel, find dry heat where cold should be, smell clean baked dust and matches, and hear riveting coming from a fist.

If you block the faint red with your hand, the dark becomes absolute and the heat and noise become your whole world. It is intimate to the point of discomfort — you cannot observe without your breath changing what you observe. Give it a minute, then give it back its night.

Go at night for the red to show, but travel the ridge in safer light and wait. Bring patience more than gear. The room changes slowly: a molt, an eaten neighbor, a pinhead crystal growing over a season, basalt tightening. You are not there to see an event. You are there to put your huge head briefly beside a tiny, untouched, fully occupied furnace high above the world that needs no water and no light and no you — and to leave it still working in the dark.

Checklist to pack and wear:
layered cold/ wind system, stiff boots + gaiters, helmet, knee pads, cut + heat gloves, sealed goggles, respirator, buff, 3-4L water + electrolytes + food to eat far away, lip balm + saline, red-filter headlamp + spare batteries, loupe / borescope, gas monitor if possible, earplugs, GPS/map/compass/satellite messenger, poles, first aid with wound cleaning + burn care + eyewash, trash bags for pack-out, change of clothes bagged separately.

And the most important preparation: decide in advance you will touch nothing, take nothing, widen nothing, and leave quickly when your body says — headache, burning throat, dizziness, overheating. The cone can afford to lose your visit. You cannot afford to lose yourself to it.
