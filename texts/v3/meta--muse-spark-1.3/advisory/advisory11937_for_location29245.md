You don't arrive here. You acclimatize to it.

There is only one way in — a day and a half up black switchbacks out of the lowlands — and that climb is your preparation, whether you want it to be or not. Take it slow. You'll need to.

This is not a cold mountain, even though it should be. It's high enough that the air thins, but the rock itself holds bath-warm heat. Think hothouse at altitude: thin, warm, saturated air that never lets your skin dry. Your shirt will stick by mid-morning. Your lungs will work a little harder than you expect. Your head may ache. Don't push.

If you have heart or lung problems, bad asthma, trouble with heat — think hard about whether you go at all. If you go, talk to a doctor first, climb over two days not one, sleep low the first night if you can, hydrate constantly, no alcohol on the road, eat salt. Altitude sickness + heat + humidity is a sneaky combination. You won't be freezing, you'll just be tired, headachy, slightly nauseous, slightly stupid. That's normal for day one and two. Rest, drink, wait.

### Your body will be wet the whole time

Forget staying dry. The plateau city makes its own cloud by afternoon — steam from mills and dye works and powerhouses, evaporate off a million wet roofs, breath — and then it drizzles it back down. Then it clears. Then it does it again. The stone weeps. Walls cry in sheets. Gutters sing. Streets are cambered to a central rill on purpose.

You need to live wet without rotting:

**Wear:** Light, quick-dry, breathable hiking clothes in mineral colors — ash-grey, basalt-black, rust-brown. You will blend in and you won't show the guano and soot. No cotton that stays soggy for days. Bring 3-4 shirts, not one warm jacket. A brimmed hat, not a wool hat. Good sunglasses — essential. At noon the sun is straight up, white-gold, and after rain off whitewashed guano and wet basalt it hurts.

**Feet:** This is where trips fail. Everything is rounded, pocked basalt and scoria, worn shin-deep by cavies and people, slick with bacterial film — mustard-yellow, copper-green, black crust — and guano slurry. You need waterproof, drainable, high-grip boots or trail sandals with toe protection, not heavy leather mountaineering boots that will stay wet for a week. Bring 4-5 pairs of wool/synthetic socks in dry-bags, foot powder, antifungal cream, blister kit. Air your feet whenever you stop. Trench foot and blisters happen fast when you never dry.

**Skin and lungs:** Pack for a barnyard factory sauna. N95 or better dust masks — you will want them in dovecote alleys, mill sheds, compost vault areas, and when the great ash-grey mill-moths lift off the eaves in a choking cloud. The air is guano + hot oil + mineral steam + wet wool + baking starch + rain on hot stone. Beautiful for an hour, oppressive for a week if you're sensitive.

Bring: high-SPF sunscreen — high altitude UV, no shade, no trees — lip balm, antihistamines for moth scale / dander / midge bites, strong DEET or picaridin for the evening midges over the cisterns, afterbite, broad antibiotic ointment. Any cut in warm guano-water gets infected fast. Clean it, cover it, keep it dry-ish.

Earplugs and a sleep mask. Non-negotiable. The city hums in your molars day and night — engines, mill shafts, dynamos, cable-drums, pressure valves venting steam 50 meters straight up. Shift whistles blow. At dusk a hundred thousand white river-birds with long curved beaks come in from the marshes below in rivers that dim the sun, screaming *kree-kree-kree* until it's a sheet of sound. Then the small fast bats pour out of the aqueduct expansion joints to eat the midges. You will not sleep without plugs.

Your electronics will suffer more than you. Everything metal goes green-black with wet. Bring dry-bags inside dry-bags, silica gel, a waterproof pack cover, a headlamp with red mode — white light at night summons moths the size of your hand — and a backup battery in a sealed bag. Paper rots. Put your map/notes in a zip-lock.

### Do not drink the city

Water is everywhere and none of it is for you to just scoop up. Every roof is a catchment. Every alley ends in a cistern throat. The ravines run in stone flumes two men high, brown-white with foam, roaring under iron grilles. The retaining walls sweat through mortar. It all looks clean and it is not.

That water has run over guano whitewash ankle-deep in breeding season, over soot, starch, dye, oil, through grease traps cleaned by wire-rats, past pale eyeless fish and fat newts. The weeping from the rock is warm and mineral.

Bring a filter + chemical purification, and a 2-3 liter capacity. Fill only where your host tells you is potable — usually from closed indoor cisterns or powerhouse boiled water — and never contaminate a catchment. That means: never piss in a street rill, never throw soap in a rill, never sit on a cistern throat, never toss trash in a grate. Sweep your roof if you stay anywhere for more than a night. The cistern-men depend on it.

Food: there is no green here. No trees, no gardens, no weeds except for mineral steam-burned edges. No soil, just paved or scoured scoria. What grows is millimeter-thick black crust and salt-fur on cisterns. You cannot forage.

The city eats stored grain, starch-cake, cavy, goats, eyeless cistern fish, and sack-harvested brick-red caterpillars from the compost vaults and grease traps. It's filling, salty, mineral, and perfectly safe if cooked and served hot by locals. If you are vegetarian, vegan, or have a delicate stomach, bring your own calorie-dense supplements — nuts, dried fruit, electrolytes, protein bars — for at least half your calories, sealed against humidity and rats. Don't expect fresh vegetables. Do try what you're offered, politely, and don't ask where the salad is.

### How to move

Don't try to conquer distance. The plateau is sea-vast — its far edge makes its own horizon, clouds are born in the middle and die before the rim. But it's dense and layered, not tall. Three, five, seven stories deep in the broad U-shaped ravines, two or three on the flats. Pipe bridges over street bridges over water channels. Funicular towers haul ore and slag and night-soil up and down ravine sides.

You will get lost vertically more than horizontally. Hire a local for your first two days if you can, or at least learn one ravine and one flat well. Wear gloves with grip — railings are wet brass and iron. Watch for:

- Weep-holes, weep-cavities, drip chains, downspouts of hollowed lava. Never block them, never hang laundry over them, never park your pack against them.
- Grilles over flumes. Never step past them for a photo. A slip is death and swift.
- Steam vents. When you hear a pressure valve sigh, step back, cover your face, let the moths lift and settle. The steam is scalding for the first 10 meters.
- Warm culverts where feral dogs sleep. Walk around, don't step over.
- Goat paths on the steepest ravine cuts. Goats with bells have right of way. Always.

Old men with hooked sticks clearing grates are not quaint. They are keeping your street from flooding. Step aside, nod, don't help unless asked — and if you stay a week, learn to do it. Children with paper boats in the street-rills are also part of the system. Don't dam their boats, don't divert their rill.

Go slow. Iron here is patched, not replaced. Walls subside a finger's width in a lifetime and are shimmed. Everything moves at the pace of rust and crust growth. If you hurry, you'll slip, insult someone, and miss your turn. The etiquette is animal etiquette: don't block paths, don't shout to be heard over birds and engines at shift-change — wait, use hand signals, speak low and close.

### The animals own it. You are a guest.

In the absence of plants, animals have taken every niche, and every cubic meter is occupied.

**Birds first:** white river-birds nesting on every ledge, chimney pot, pipe elbow, temple cornice, ten-story dovecotes. At dusk they return in rivers. Don't stand in the open without your hat. Whitewash is not just white — it's slippery, acidic, ankle-deep in wet-season breeding months. Don't shoo them, don't touch nests, don't eat on a roost ledge. If you leave a window open, a bird will come in. Close shutters at dusk, but leave the eave-mats alone.

**Moths second:** great ash-grey mill moths cling under every eave in furred, breathing mats by day. Don't poke them, don't knock them down, don't shine bright light on them at night. Moth dust triggers asthma. Keep your mask on around a fresh lift-off.

**Ground level:** long-eared rock cavies in ochre and slate, wild-tame, pouring across plazas at feeding hour when mill-women throw spent grain. Let them pass — they follow paths worn by their great-grandmothers. Wire-rats run the pipes like blood; leave them their pipe, they keep grease traps clear. Don't feed them, don't trap them, don't block pipe mouths with your gear. Feral dogs: do not pet, do not feed, rabies and bites are real. Goats: do not feed paper — they already eat too much ash and paper.

**Water life:** pale fish, newts in cisterns and flumes, midges at evening, bats after. Don't swim in cisterns. Don't put your hands in flumes. Don't shine lights into bat exits at dusk.

General rule: Be indifferent and be ignored, which is a compliment here. At dusk, when the bird-rivers come home and the bats go out and the steam columns turn pink and the whole shelf hums and drips under its lid — stand still. Something will land near you — moth, cavy, white bird with mud on its beak — and look at you with complete indifference, as one resident to another temporary resident of warm stone. Don't reach out. Just stand.

### Your mind needs prep more than your pack

There is no forest to walk in. No meadow. No green to rest your eyes. After a while you stop looking for it, but the first three days you will grieve it without knowing why. Everything is basalt and concrete and iron and glittering volcanic glass aggregate, iron-black, ash-grey, tuff-brown, rust-red, chalk-white with droppings.

It's loud, crowded, wet, close, smelly, and profoundly patient. No one builds outward — there is no outward left. They build inward, denser, a niche here, a loft there, another dovecote on top of a powerhouse. No one will hurry for you. No one will be impressed that you came from far away.

Bring work to offer, not plans to improve. Help re-lay a pipe, re-point a weeping wall, oil an engine your host's grandfather oiled, mend a net. Bring patience for mending. Bring a willingness to be wet and unimportant for a week.

If you can do that — if you can keep your feet powdered, your mask handy, your water clean, your pack off the weep-holes, your mouth shut at shift-change, and your body still at dusk — the city will absorb you for a little while, the way warm stone absorbs rain. You won't dry, you won't be quiet, you won't be alone. You'll be conducted, like water, along a suggested path.
