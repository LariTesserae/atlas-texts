You do not climb this place to summit it. You climb for days to run out of mountain.

Prepare for that first. This is not a day-trip anomaly. This is a 5-kilometer lift.

### 1. Your Body Is The Problem

This is an old seabed lifted into the sky and left to cure. Thin, cold, absolutely dry air, about late-autumn cool - you will want your hands in your sleeves - but with no moisture in it at all.

An ordinary human will get sick here without trying:

**Altitude:** You will be panting on stairs of air. Ears popping until they ache, heart hammering, lungs shallow. You need to come up slow over days the way you would for any 5,000m plateau, sleeping low, hydrating low. If you can portal, do NOT portal to the top. Anchor down below where there is wet rock and smell, and walk up to acclimatize. Bring supplemental oxygen if you can carry it. At minimum bring acetazolamide if you tolerate it, and know HAPE/HACE signs: headache that doesn't lift, stumbling, confusion, wet cough. The only cure is down. The slope down is steep and your knees will shake with sudden thick air on the way out - bring poles and save your legs.

**Water:** There is no water here. Not a seep, not a tarn, not snow. At this height there should be ice, and there isn't even that. The white in the hollows is not frost. It's salt and ground shell exhaled from the rock. Do not lick it, do not melt it, do not try to filter it. It will dry you faster.

You will not sweat. You will not feel thirsty correctly. Your breath comes out and vanishes instantly, unmade. Your tongue will stick to your teeth just breathing. This is insidious dehydration country.

Bring far more than you think: 5-6 liters for the climb + 1 liter per 2 hours on the plain, drunk on a timer, not on thirst. Electrolytes. Lip balm, saline nasal spray, eye drops. A dust mask or buff for salt powder. You will get nosebleeds. Your eyes will burn.

Pee before you go up. You pack out *everything*, including urine and crumbs and spit. Do not leave anything that was not there.

**Light and skin:** Light is flat white midday, high overcast brightness, sun as a bleached coin you can almost look at. Don't look at it. At 5km with white pavement, UV is brutal even without blue sky. Sunscreen, SPF lip, glacier goggles or at least dark wraparounds. A brim hat. Long sleeves in pale chalk-white and bone-grey - you will blend, you will stay cooler, you will kick up less visual noise.

**Cold and sound:** No wind to speak of. The air hangs. But it's thin cool that seeps into stone. Layer: merino base, fleece, wind shell, light gloves, warm hat. No stove. No fire. **Never make fire here.** The black scar a mile east is still there from someone who tried once, with the pallids above it permanently yellowed. Bring cold food only and eat it below.

Your boots will sound obscenely loud. The salt-cemented pavement rings dry. Bring the softest soles you have - trail moccasins, felt overshoes - and practice walking heel-to-toe, slow. Sound here does not echo. It hangs as pressure behind the ears.

### 2. What Is Actually Up There

Acres of fitted slabs - chalk-white, bone-grey, pale ochre - cracked into polygons. Long wrinkles no higher than a sleeping body for hills. Boulders exfoliated into onion-dust.

Almost no plants: a black star of lichen hard as enamel in a slab joint, a rust-red thumbnail cushion that crumbles to spice if your boot strays, a grey beard under a lee stone. Step only on bare pavement, never on a joint. One boot stray kills decades.

People have been here barely: knee-high rings of stacked chalk open to the east, cairns three or four stones high every few hundred paces, lines of flint flakes still sharp where someone knapped, a single beehive hut in a swale with a door no higher than your waist, roofed with something pale and translucent stretched over ribs.

You will figure out later what that roofing is. Don't touch it.

And then the air: hand-sized, alabaster, six-veined wings held flat like kites, bodies curled like shrimp, a bead-black eye at either end. Hushwings. Pallids.

Millions. Hundreds of millions. Knee-height to higher than you can focus, spaced a body-length apart, wingtip to wingtip, to the white rim of the world. In every pan on the ground they lie curled shoulder to shoulder, edge overlapping, living scree.

None of them move. No wingbeat. No call. No fall. They hang without support. Dust motes hang with them. Toss a pebble and it arcs and stops a fraction too soon, as if the air considered it.

They are not dead. Kneel and you'll see opalescence moving under the shell. They are in suspension so long it became a state of being. There is nothing for them to eat and they do not eat.

You must learn to live, briefly, like them.

### 3. The Three Rules Are Not Suggestions

The herder - sewn hides, cloak of layered fallen wings, face powdered white with salt, tools a flint flake, a splinter of wing-rib, a generations-pocked hammerstone - moves so slowly you will first mistake her for stone. She tends: rights a cairn, brushes dust from ground-sleepers with a feather, whispers without consonants. Her people make nothing new. Why make, when everything endures?

On a slab by the hut, scratched by her grandmother's grandmother:

**1. Do not touch the hanging ones.**
**2. Do not speak above breath.**
**3. Do not leave anything that was not there.**

Understand why: change here does not ripple. It sticks. A shout stays as pressure and the nearest wings develop a faint brown spot, like a bruise on fruit. That bruise does not heal.

So:
- Whisper in vowels. Practice before: breath out, no consonants, no throat clicks. No coughing if you can swallow it. No calling to a companion. Agree on hand signals below.
- Never touch a hanging pallid. The membrane will brush your cheek as you duck - dry as paper, cool as stone, utterly still. Let it happen. Do not reach. Do not poke to see if it's alive. Do not collect a husk, even the rare fallen ones. Her cloak is made of years of settling. One souvenir is theft and damage.
- Leave nothing. Not a flake, not a hair tie, not a footprint in dust if you can avoid scuffing. Don't rearrange stones. Don't knap. Don't build a cairn. Don't take the bone awl or look too long at what the scraper is made from. Do not enter the hut. Sit at a distance and wait if you want to meet her.

No metal. No wire. No gifts. No new things. No flash photography, no drone, no laser rangefinder waving through layers. If you must take a photo, no flash, silent shutter, from the ground, once.

Do not hunt, do not taste. To kill one would be meaningless, and the meat is dry salt.

### 4. Navigation: Distance Lies

Shadows lie short and crisp and do not lengthen. The sun does not move. Your circadian system will break within what feels like an hour. Bring a mechanical watch with an alarm and obey it. Plan a short stay: 60-90 minutes subjective, then go down whether you feel done or not.

A pallid a mile away looks exactly the same size as one at arm's length. If you stare long enough you will see through its wings to another sky, paler, with another plain below it, with another you looking up. Look away. This is vertigo bait.

Shadows point about five degrees wrong from where the sun demands. Walk straight away counting paces and turn, and you are sometimes closer than you should be, sometimes faced with two identical cairns where there was one.

Do this:
- Do not wander past walking distance. Stay on the cairn line.
- Do not rely on compass / GPS alone. They will be sullen here. Use the cairns, use a paid-out physical line if you must go farther - light undyed cord you reel back in, never leaving it.
- If you get doubled - two cairns - stop. Sit. Breathe. Whisper-wait. The herder will right you by example. Do not make a new marker to fix it.
- Duck and step carefully between the low ones. Never push through. The flock does not part for you.

If you stand still long enough you will hear it: faint scattered ticking, *tik... tik... tik...*, like cooling ceramic from somewhere in the strata above. That's just chitin contracting in cold. Nothing answers it. Don't chase it.

### 5. Mind Preparation

The hardest part is not the lungs. It's the frozen equilibrium.

Vast high silence like the center of a sealed room, with your own pulse filling your head. Openness that is dense. Life overwhelmingly present and utterly paused. Light left on. Cold left on. Silence left on. This is what happens *after* everything has happened.

Ordinary humans panic in three ways here:

1. **Claustrophobia in the open:** three wings between fingers and face, then three hundred, then three hundred thousand until distance whites out. If you are entomophobic, do exposure work first. Practice slow breathing with eyes half-open, focusing on the nearest slab joint, not the haze.
2. **Urgency to fix/test:** to clap, to shout to hear echo, to wave a hand through them to see them scatter. They won't scatter. You'll just bruise them. Rehearse doing nothing.
3. **Seduction into stillness:** After a while - no time passing, no hunger because you are not burning right, no need to speak - it feels dignified not to alter anything. To sit. To tend. Travelers have wanted to stay and become another stone. Set your alarm. When it goes, leave, even mid-thought.

Go with one quiet companion at most, or alone. Groups are too loud. Tell someone below where your anchor is and when to expect you back in wet air.

Train silence before: an hour a day without speaking, moving slowly around your room, noticing how loud you are. Train slow eyes: not scanning for horizon - there isn't one - but seeing the low rings, the debitage still sharp as if left an hour ago, because here an hour ago might be a hundred years ago.

### Pack List for an Ordinary Human

Worn: merino base, fleece, pale wind shell, soft pants, soft-soled quiet shoes + gaiters for salt dust, sun hat, goggles, light gloves, N95/buff.

Carried: 6L water + electrolytes, cold calorie-dense food (eat below), supplemental O2 if available, poles, headlamp (you won't need it on plain, but for climb), mechanical watch with timer, paper map + cord reel, waste bags for everything including toilet paper, first aid + blister kit + altitude meds, sunglasses, sunscreen, lip SPF, saline spray, earplugs for descent popping (do not wear them on plain - you need to hear how loud you are).

Do NOT carry: stove, lighter, fire steel, spray paint, flagging that sheds, food that crumbles, anything you are tempted to leave as offering.

When you leave, the slope down will make your knees shake with sudden thickness of air, sudden smell of wet rock far below. Look back once. The plain will be exactly as you left it. The woman mid-step between two cairns. The wings where they were.

If you hold your breath, you will still hear the ticking, somewhere, nowhere, once.

Leave it ticking.
