This is not a ruin. It's a beginning. Prepare to visit a construction site, not a valley.

You will go over a cold, thin, high pass and drop 1 step into bathhouse air. That transition is the whole trip. Everything you bring has to work on both sides: for clean alpine cold outside the bowl, and for hot, wet, dim, loud, unstable ground inside it.

The bowl itself is small - 200 to 300 paces across - ringed on three sides by older peaks that block the sun. It stays pearl-grey dim even at midday. The ground is new lava: black glass, pahoehoe rope, pillows, cinder and shard. No soil, no trails, no streams. Just seep, sweat, warm sky-pools with no outlets, steam where the crust is thin, and tremor upward under your palm. It is still cooling, still rising.

And there are seven things in it that were not made by volcanism. Tall, slender, leaning black needles, 3-4 meters tall, grown into the basalt. They hum in your sternum. They hold water in the air around them. They cycle amber at night and go low and sick-making in the early morning.

Nothing lives here permanently yet except moss, bacteria, and a few brave pioneer plants. Everything else - midges, copper-throated frogs by the thousands, black newts with red bellies, flightless crickets, swifts and swallows that never land - climbs or flies up from a thousand meters below for the heat and the food, and leaves again.

Here is how an ordinary human body gets through that intact.

### 1. Your body first

**Breathe:** You will have to breathe harder inside, not from altitude but from thickness. Outside the lip the air is thin, cold, clean. Inside it is warm, saturated, faintly sulfurous and metallic. If you have asthma, reactive airways, or are sensitive to sulfur / hot-spring steam, bring your inhaler and a light respirator - a simple half-mask with P100 / acid-gas cartridges or at minimum an N95 + bandana. If the iron/sulfur smell turns sharp rotten-egg and your eyes water, move upwind to the rim. Don't sleep in the lowest hollow where steam pools.

**Heat + Wet, not Cold:** You won't dry. Mist rolls over the eastern rim every ~20 minutes, condenses on everything, sheets off raw rock with no channels, hangs around the needles, and rises off warm pools. Combine that with warm rock through your soles and warm air on your skin and you get heat exhaustion while soaked. Plan to be wet for hours. Trench foot, blisters, chafing, and eye irritation are more likely to end your trip than anything dramatic.

**Hum:** You feel the needles before you hear them - a low unresolved chord with a high shimmer that makes fillings ache. People with heavy dental work, hearing aids, tinnitus, migraine, pacemakers/implanted electronics, or pregnancy should be extra cautious. Don't hug a needle. Don't press your head to one to listen. Keep 5-10 meters as a default. In the early morning when the hum drops low you may feel nausea, pressure, vague sickness. That's normal here. Sit down, look at the horizon, wait for the bowl-wide patter when all the suspended droplets fall at once. It passes. Earplugs or noise-reducing earbuds help a lot, but don't wear full noise-cancelling - you need to hear rock ticking, steam sighing, birds that echo a second late.

Come acclimatized to altitude. Sleep low the night before, hydrate, don't arrive exhausted over the pass late in failing light if you can avoid it. The description of arriving late is a warning: descending into sharp glass in dim pearl light with wet rock is how ankles break.

### 2. What to wear and carry

Think: glass-cutter's kitchen in a sauna at dusk.

**Feet:** This is non-negotiable.
- Stiff, cut-proof mountaineering or approach boots with thick, heat-resistant soles. The cooling-cracks will slice trail-runners and the ground is warm through rubber. Test your soles - cheap rubber softens.
- Heavy wool or synthetic socks, 3 pairs in dry bags, plus liner socks. Change often. Bring foot powder, blister kit, and camp sandals with closed toes for probing pool edges - never barefoot.
- Gaiters, knee-high if possible. Cinder slumps, shards get in.

**Skin / Clothes:**
- Quick-dry base, mid fleece for the pass, and a truly waterproof shell top and bottom, not water-resistant. Seam-sealed. You will be dripped on, seeped on, condensed on.
- No cotton. No heavy jeans that hold water and chill you when you climb back out into cold thin air.
- Lightweight gloves - lava cuts palms open. You'll want to put a hand down.
- Hat with brim + headnet for midges. Glasses or clear safety glasses. Midges cloud over every pool and glitter-turn together. Swallows dive through them and will buzz your head. Frogs call so steadily the air vibrates.
- Insect repellent, but use sparingly and keep it off the pools and moss. The ecosystem has no buffer.

**Pack:**
- Trekking poles - essential. One to test: is that dark slab hot? Is that crust soft between pillows? Cane before you step. Anything heavy sinks slowly here. The old weather tent collapsed for a reason.
- Headlamp with red/amber mode + spare batteries in a dry bag. White light in thick mist just blinds you and washes out the needle glow. Bring a dim amber backup for night. Everything electronic corrodes fast here - that milky solar panel at the rim is what a month does. Bring mechanical backups: compass, paper map, whistle. Don't rely on phone GPS alone; basalt and steam confuse it.
- Gas lighter in dry bag, not just ferro rod - everything is damp.
- Lightweight tarp / bivy, no heavy tent, no stakes you expect to hold. DO NOT plan to build or camp heavy in the bowl. If you must overnight, sleep high near the rim collar on the oldest, most worn rock, on a pad, under tarp. Don't pitch in a hollow, near a warm pool, or within the drip-halo of a needle.

### 3. Water: Obey the sign

At the rim cairn with the rusted instrument box someone left a hand-painted rule: DRINK SECOND WATER ONLY.

That means: Let it fall, let it sit, let it cool.

First water is what hangs in the air - the visible halo of droplets around each needle, beads that rise from warm pools toward the needles and stop midair trembling, mist that condenses on your arm hairs in seconds. Don't drink it directly. Don't lick it off leaves. Don't open your mouth to it. It's warm, mineral-loaded, full of silica, iron, sulfur, airborne organics, midge parts, bacterial film. It will give you gut cramps at best.

Do this instead:
- Bring in 2-3 liters of your own clean water for the descent. You will drink more than you think because you're breathing steam.
- To resupply: set a clean collapsible bowl or bottle mouth open to catch falling rain/seep from a cold rock face, away from needles and away from orange bacterial rims. Let it sit 30+ minutes to cool and settle. Then filter 0.2 micron + activated carbon, then chemically treat or boil. Warm blood-temperature pools are not for drinking, even treated - they are frog/newt/midge soup and thermophile habitat.
- Never drink from a pool with an orange fan rim or milky film. Never drink water that tastes metallic, soapy, or of sulfur.
- Keep a separate “dirty” bottle. Wash hands after touching pool water, especially before eating. Those black newts with vermilion bellies are advertising toxicity. Don't handle frogs, newts, crickets.

You will never be dry, but you can easily get dehydrated because you don't want to stop to treat water. Force the pause.

### 4. Moving in a place with no downhill

There are no valleys, no streambeds. Water sheets, braids and unbraids down fresh faces without channels. That means:

- No trail to follow out. Mark your entry over the basalt lip with a cairn, GPS waypoint, and a bright cord. Whiteouts come every 20 minutes with the mist tongue. The bowl is only ten minutes across if you could walk straight - you can't. Cinder slumps, pillows wobble, crust between them is soft and hot.
- Walk like on thin ice over a stove. Tap, pause, shift weight. If steam lifts off, back off. Warm spots move - a bath-warm pool in the morning can be cold by night and a new vent sighs open ten meters away. Never step into a pool to wade. Test pool rims with back of hand for heat before sitting. Scalds happen fast on uncalloused skin.
- Put your palm flat on cinder once and feel it: faint tremor, upward pressure. Survey stakes lean a finger's width a month. Your map is already wrong. Don't trust depth.
- Morning hazard: when needles cycle low, all suspended water falls at once in a soft bowl-wide patter. It startles the frogs silent for a breath. It will soak you instantly and slick the glass ropes. If you're traversing, wait it out.

Step carefully around life. It's spaced out for a reason. Coin-sized acid-bright moss in cracks, single grass blades up through cinder, and that low fleshy pioneer with translucent leaves holding a bead in each leaf like an offering - each patch holds territory of emptiness around it because there is no soil yet to share. Step on black empty rock, not green. One boot print can erase a decade.

### 5. The needles: look, listen, don't touch

They are matte black, seamless, grown into basalt, 3-4m tall, slightly leaning. In thick mist faint amber lines crawl base to tip slow as sap. At full dark they brighten, the suspended water catches it, and the whole bowl glows faintly from within - seven dim lanterns + a thousand frog throats + sky-pools.

Protocol:
- Observe, don't interfere. Don't touch, climb, chip, tie to, hang gear on, or camp next to a needle. They are the only permanent things here and no one knows if they were seeded or seeded themselves.
- Don't place metal instruments directly against them for long - induction, vibration, heat cycling will kill gear.
- Photography: you won't get contrast in pearl-grey dim without overexposing mist. Underexpose, use tripod on solid pillow rock, no flash. Let amber do the work.
- If your teeth sing, jaw aches, sternum buzzes, back away a few paces and turn sideways - it lessens. If you feel suddenly sick, sad, awed, tearful, that's also reported. Infrasound + isolation + womb-warmth does that. Name it, breathe, drink second water.

### 6. Animals: guests like you

Remember: nothing was born here. Everything animal climbed or flew up for warmth and midges. You are the largest visitor.

- Midges: headnet up before you need it. Long sleeves. Don't swat clouds over pools - you'll just inhale them.
- Frogs: thousands of little high-altitude tree frogs, throats lit copper and lemon, clinging to glass ropes. Deafening, steady. Don't grab them - skin oils + cold hands injure them, and glass cuts you when you lunge. Watch where you put poles.
- Newts, crickets: watch, don't collect. Vermilion belly = do not eat, do not touch.
- Birds: swifts, swallows, something larger fork-tailed, circling endlessly, never landing, snatching midges and frogs mid-dive. Cries bounce off the collar and arrive a full second before the bird banks into view. Wear hat/glasses. Don't throw food to attract them. Don't shine bright light up at night while they’re still flying crying in the dark - you'll blind them.

Bring all your calories. There is nothing here for you to eat safely. Pack out everything, including crumbs - this place has no decomposers yet to handle you.

### 7. Night

Stay till dark fully comes at least once if your exit route is safe and you have light. That's when the place shows itself.

Above the collar: real sky, deep indigo, hard cold stars. Below: grey mist lit from inside, ticking contracting rock, warm air on your face while peaks go black.

Stay warm by moving slightly - don't sit in seep. Keep a dry puffy in a dry bag just for the rim climb out. The climb back to thin cold clean air will chill your soaked body fast. Change socks and base layer at the lip before ascending.

### Quick checklist for an ordinary human

- Heat-proof stiff boots, gaiters, 3x socks, gloves, shell, no cotton, warm layer for exit
- Headnet, glasses, hat, repellent, earplugs
- Poles, headlamp amber/red + spare, whistle, compass/paper, cord for marking pass
- 2-3L water in, filter + carbon + treatment, collapsible catch basin, separate dirty bottle
- All food in, all trash out, blister + burn kit, inhaler if needed, N95
- Dry bags for everything, tarp not heavy tent, sleeping pad if staying
- Thermometer strip or back-of-hand test, no bathing in warm pools

And the mental prep: It will feel less like a valley and more like a mouth, or a womb. Loud, wet, crowded, lit from inside, embryonic. Not eroded but extruded. You are not meant to live there. Go to witness it learning downhill, learning soil, learning rivers. Walk softly, drink only what has fallen and cooled, touch nothing that hums, and climb back out before you start thinking the warmth means you are safe.
