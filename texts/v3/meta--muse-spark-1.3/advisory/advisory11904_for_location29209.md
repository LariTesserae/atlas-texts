This is not a ruin. It's an instrument that you walk inside of. Prepare like you're going to be played by it for a few minutes, not like you're going sightseeing.

It looks harmless — a little pocket between high walls, no deeper than you can stretch your arms, floor of tight-cut slabs worn dished in the middle. Four leaning walls honeycombed ankle-to-sky with square niches, each about two fists together, each with a little mud-brick lip. A dovecote with no doves.

Do not be fooled by small and empty. There is no room in there for you. It is already full of light and sound and dust.

### What it will do to an ordinary body

**1. Eyes first.**
You expect cool dark at the bottom of a well. You get glare. The sky is only a ragged slit overhead, white-blue and impossible to look at, but the walls were whitewashed a hundred years ago and enough wash remains in the corners and inside the niches to bounce the sun around until it has nowhere to go. Every upward face is lit. The air itself glows.

At the same time the stone is cellar-cool. It drinks heat as fast as light makes it. Your neck, shins, palms will feel cool while your eyes water. That is the trap: like snowfield or high desert, you will burn without feeling hot. Sunburn on any exposed skin, chapped lips, and in minutes a dazzle-headache and after-images from the doubling edges of the niches. Staring up at the slit will hurt for hours.

**2. Ears second, really first.**
You will feel it as pressure when you squeeze in: a full, round roar made of hundreds of thin whistles braided together. Wind across all those empty mouths at once, plus a lower rush where air funnels down and hits the dished floor and turns around.

It does not gust. It holds one exact volume, one exact pitch. Hold your breath — it stays. Clap — the slap is flattened instantly into the chord without echo or ripple. Your brain will keep waiting for variation that never comes. That is exhausting, disorienting, and louder than you think. You won't be able to hear your companion, your footsteps, your own breathing clearly. People get nauseous, anxious, time-dilated in constant broadband sound. After you leave you may have phantom ringing.

Don't try to overcome it by shouting or playing something. The sound is already full. There is no space for new sound.

**3. Lungs, nose, mouth.**
There has been no water here in longer than you can guess. No stain, no tide-mark, no moss that needs seep. Only fine, pale, utterly dry dust — brick-dust and lime-dust — on every sill and in the air where motes hang gold-white and motionless. They don't settle and don't swirl.

It will dry you fast. Nosebleed-dry, throat-scratchy, contact-lens-killing dry. The dust tastes of plaster and is alkaline. Deep breaths through the mouth will make you cough. Putting your hand in a niche, as everyone wants to do, comes back cool and dusted. Don't inhale that. Old dovecotes hold dried droppings, spores, silica even when they look clean-swept.

**4. Mind.**
The worst part is not fear, it's absence with perfect order. No pigeons, no sparrows, no flies, no beetles under the lips, no silk across the dark mouths. In three or four of the lowest niches there are tufts of wiry salt-stiff grey-green straw that grew an inch and stopped. One has gone to seed and the seeds just sit on the lip, uncarried, because there is nothing to carry them. No ants. Nothing to eat them, nothing to disturb them.

On the north side, where bounce-light is least, each brick joint holds a flat crust of lichen, sage-grey and mustard-pale, growing in straight lines because it follows the joints. Mortar receded a finger's width back, lips rounded by hands and beaks long gone. It crumbled long ago and then stopped.

It has found equilibrium and stayed there. It will look exactly like this tomorrow and in ten years. Standing very still in the middle — and there is only the middle — you feel the rules assert themselves: *stay low, stay cool, stay dry, make no new sound, bring nothing living that needs more than dust and glare; it will not be fed.*

You are extra matter. The place will invite you, passively, to become still, dry, quiet, dusty too. People dissociate, lose track of time, want to sit down in the dish and just listen. Don't.

There is no device here. No wire, pipe, bulb, hinge, nail, glass. Only mud, brick, lime, stone put by hands. Don't expect your phone, radio, or audio recorder to behave normally. Assume voice comms inside are useless.

### How to prepare

Treat it as a short, sacred, sensory-extreme entry. One person at a time. Minutes, not hours.

**Before you go:**
Hydrate well, eat, use the toilet. You cannot drink, eat, spit, urinate in there without breaking its dryness. Bring at least a liter of sealed water to leave *outside* with your spotter and drink immediately after.

Do not bring anything alive that needs more. No dogs, no birds especially — do not bring a bird into an empty dovecote — no plants, no insects, no food to offer. No candles, incense, water offerings, speakers, instruments. No drone. The funnel will swallow it.

Go with a buddy who stays outside the squeeze. Because you cannot hear, agree on visual/tactile signals and a hard time limit before you go in. A vibrating timer on your wrist, not an audible alarm.

**Wear:**
- Loose, full-coverage light clothing: long sleeves, long pants, closed grippy shoes. The dished slabs are slick with fine dust. The stone is cool — if you sweat getting in you will chill.
- Wide-brim hat. You will want to crane your neck up at the slit. Don't without a brim and glasses.
- Eye protection that is both dark *and* sealing. Category 3-4 sunglasses with side shields, or sealed glacier goggles. Bring saline eye drops and a spare pair. Do not wear contacts if you can avoid it.
- Hearing protection: foam plugs *plus* earmuffs. You will still feel the chord through bone conduction as pressure — that's normal — but you will prevent pain and post-visit tinnitus. Don't try to listen through it.
- Dust protection: well-fitted N95 / FFP2 or better, plus a spare. Light thin gloves to keep lime dust off skin and to keep your oils off the lichen and brick. Lip balm and nasal saline for after.
- Small flashlight is almost useless — it's already too bright — but a dim penlight can help you peer into one low niche without staring up. Don't shine around broadly.

Mark your entry from the outside. The masses rise out of sight and all walls look the same once you're inside the glare. A rope tether at the squeeze or a chalk mark *outside*, never inside, and a spotter who watches the slit light.

**Inside protocol:**

1. Enter sideways, low, slow. Touch two walls at once if you need to — you can. Kneel or crouch in the dish center rather than standing tall and swaying. Fix your gaze low, not up. Let your eyes adapt to bounce-light for 30 seconds.
2. Breathe shallowly through your nose through the mask. Do not sigh, cough hard, clap, call out to test echo. It will be swallowed and you'll just irritate your throat.
3. Do not put your face, food, water bottle, pack, or bare hands deep into niches. The backs are cool and tempting. One light touch with a gloved fingertip is enough to understand. Do not brush dust off, do not collect it, do not blow into a hole to hear it whistle. Do not move the seeds on the lip. They have sat there because nothing moves them. Leave them.
4. Do not touch lichen. It's dry enough to crumble to paint-dust and it grows only where light is least. Oils and moisture from fingers kill it.
5. Do not try to climb using niches as holds. Lips are rounded, weathered, fragile. You will break heritage and fall onto stone.
6. Stay 5-12 minutes max for a first visit. The combination of glare + monotone + dry air + cramped standing impairs judgment quietly. When your timer vibrates, leave even if you feel "almost adjusted." That adjusted feeling is the place assimilating you.

If you feel vertigo, ear pain that spikes, panic at the changelessness, or an urge to lie down and listen: look at your hands, wiggle toes in shoes, tug the tether / signal spotter, and back out the way you came keeping low. Don't look up while moving.

**After:**
Step out, keep sunglasses on until you're out of bounce. Drink water outside. Rinse hands, blow nose gently, saline rinse eyes if gritty, moisturize. Sit in real shade and real quiet for at least as long as you were inside. Expect eyes to water, ears to ring faintly, mouth to taste plaster for an hour. That's normal if it fades.

Brush dust off clothes *away* from the site. Leave nothing, take nothing — not a seed, not a brick flake, not dust in a vial. Spilling even a little water to "cool off" or leaving crumbs creates the first stain and food in a system that has had neither for decades.

Think of it as visiting empty houses row on row that are waiting for occupants who will not come. Be polite, be brief, be dry, be quiet. Let the chord stay as you found it, and take yourself — low, cool, intact — back out into a world where wind gusts and shadows move.
