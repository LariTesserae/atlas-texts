This is not a place that tries to kill you. It just doesn't notice you are alive.

Go in understanding that. It is an ocean, but on top of the world.

### What it is, in body terms

A sea the size of a sea, sitting on a flat basalt table 4 kilometers up. Air temperature -8 to -12C, always. Water temperature 1C above freezing, always. Air pressure ~60% of sea level, always. Light: flat, shadowless, overcast white noon, always. Sound: 120 decibels of unchanging roar, always.

From the deck you will think you are at sea level. You are not. That lie will hurt you first.

Your lungs will tell you before your eyes do. The air comes in thin and knifey and too dry. At 4km, with cold on top of it, you will be short of breath standing still, stupid doing anything, headachy, nauseous, clumsy within an hour if you are not acclimatized. Strong swimmers drown here getting into a boat. Healthy people get HAPE/HACE here tying a knot.

And because the sun never moves and the light never changes, you won't feel time passing while your body is failing. Bring a clock. Obey the clock, not the sky.

This is not a camping trip. This is high-altitude, high-latitude, open-ocean, industrial-wasteland exposure with permanent sensory overload stapled on top.

Do not go alone. Do not go without supplemental oxygen and a hard-hulled, heated, enclosed boat with 1,000+ km range. Do not plan to live off the land. There is nothing to live off.

### The five things that will get an ordinary human

**1. Thin, cold air that feels normal.**
You expect to pant on a mountain. You don't expect to pant on a ship deck. You will. Your breathing will be fast and shallow, your blood will pound in your ears — which you will hear underneath the roar — and you will make bad decisions.

Prepare: Acclimatize on another high world for 3-5 days before you come. Arrive rested, hydrated, no alcohol, no sedatives. Bring supplemental oxygen, not just for emergencies but for sleeping and working on deck. Bring a pulse oximeter and check it on schedule, not when you feel bad — you will feel bad the whole time and stop noticing. Bring acetazolamide if you tolerate it, and know how you tolerate it *before*. Bring a humidified full-face cold mask / balaclava system with spares. Breathing -10C dry air at altitude raw will strip your throat bloody in a day. Your breath will smoke. Your mask will ice solid. Swap it.

Cover everything. Metal burns here. Not metaphorically. Touch a railing, a pipe, a tool handle with bare skin and you stick and tear. Tape your tools. Wear expedition mittens, not gloves, with cut-proof liners underneath. Oil smokes. Batteries die. Lube rated to -40. Keep electronics inside your clothes.

UV will cook you under that white lid. High altitude + overcast + water glare = worst sunburn of your life without ever seeing the sun. Glacier glasses, Category 4, with side shields, always outside. SPF 50 on any slit of skin, lip balm constantly. That flat white light is painfully ordinary and it will blind you.

**2. Cold water you can see through to death.**
The water is 200-300 meters of glass. No silt, no plankton, no green. You look down from a grating and see every fold of black rope pahoehoe, every sharp caldera, every fault scarp in perfect detail as if from a plane. It does not darken with depth.

This will break your balance. Humans use murk and shadow and surface chop to judge depth. Here there is none. You will get vertigo looking down, lean, stumble. You will think the bottom is 10 meters away when it is 200. Bring polarized glasses to kill some of the hammered blue glare, and train yourself: do not stare down while standing unless you are tethered and braced.

Never, ever get in the water if you can avoid it. 1C mineral water is not water, it is a knife. Cold shock will make you gasp — in thin air you cannot catch that breath back. Muscle failure in 3-5 minutes. Dead in 15-30 even with a PFD. And there are no beaches, no sand, nothing soft. Where rock breaks the surface it juts as a black sawblade, keen enough to cut rope, barnacleless. It will cut drysuits, inflatables, skin, and it has never been smoothed and never will be. Nothing erodes here. Watch a wave lap the same glass tooth for a year and the tooth stays tooth.

Prepare: hard hull only. No kayaks, no inflatables, no open skiff for a multi-day crossing. You can run three days and see nothing but your own reflection and ridges crawling below. You need an enclosed wheelhouse you can heat, radar, AIS equivalent, satellite/inertial nav, and fuel to go 600km and still turn back. Wear an expedition drysuit with insulation, hood, and auto-inflate PFD *on deck at all times*, and clip in. Tether short. If you go over near a station or a ridge, you will be cut before you are cold.

Do not drink the sea. It is too mineral, and the stations dump hot brine back out. Melt clean snow for water or bring it all. Bring 5,000-6,000 kcal per person per day — you burn huge calories shivering and panting — and bring it all with you. Rule 1 painted inside the doors is literal: *Nothing grows tall. Eat snow or eat each other.* The white bacterial felt and rust-orange iron mats in the shallows are not plants, they are living chemistry. They are not food. The krill clouds, the dinner-plate isopods, the kilometer-long ribbons of pale eels, the 30-meter mill-whales sieving mineral snow — they are not food for you. Unknown proteins, unknown toxins, and you do not want to be in the water with them anyway.

**3. Distance that is real.**
The next light is 311 km east. It is exactly as loud there.

There is no town, no rescue, no tow. Stations are single blocks of riveted steel on stilts, pipes down, stacks up, a white light flashing every 10 seconds, in perfect unison across a sea too big to see across. No crew, or one winter keeper who has gone deaf. They will not save you. They cannot hear you.

Prepare: Be self-sufficient for twice your planned stay. File a return beacon / portal window and a dead-man check-in with someone *off-world*. Your compass may swing weird over young basalt. Your GPS may not exist here. Navigate by dead reckoning + those synchronized flashes. In fact, use the flashes to keep time: count ten seconds, again, again. It is the only metronome that is honest.

Do not walk. Do not try to island-hop the sawblades. Service the boat before you come. Bring spare prop, belts, filters, fuel treatment that works cold. Fenders and grapples, not anchors — there is no sand to bite, only glass to foul and cut.

**4. Light without time and land without green.**
It is always just now, just midday. A low dome of overcast sits like a lid. No dawn, no dusk. At “night” it only dims a fraction, thicker cloud, mill-whales rising to blow columns that freeze instantly and hang motionless in the air, bacterial mats glowing faint orange below like city lights from a plane.

You will lose circadian rhythm in 24 hours. You will feel simultaneously wired and exhausted, hungry at wrong times, unable to sleep and then unable to wake. Shadowless light also kills depth perception. No shadows means you trip on flat deck, misjudge steps, misjudge waves.

Prepare: Enforce time artificially. Watch with alarm. Same sleep/wake hours. Eye mask + ear protection + melatonin for sleep, bright personal light for “morning.” Keep a paper log: time, O2 sat, position, fuel, what you ate, what you saw. When the world provides no change, you must create change or your brain will invent it and scare you.

There is no green to rest your eyes on. Only black glass, white felt, rust mats, blue-white water, white sky. Bring something green if green keeps you human — a patch, a photo. It sounds silly until day three.

**5. The Roar.**
This is the thing you cannot prepare for, so prepare for it anyway.

Three layers become one: the stations’ house-sized pumps droning through hull and bone — audible 300 km away as loud as under your feet because thin air, still water, and clear acoustics carry everything; the planet-circling waterfall where the whole ocean falls off the Table on all sides, kilometers wide, dropping four kilometers into cloud — you can’t see it from the center but you feel it as hiss through teeth; and the animals — millions of clicks from blind white krill and grazers, and the deep settling crack of glass floor — plus your own blood.

120 decibels of noon, second to second, hour to hour, no gust, no lull, no wave crash. Meters in the control room show the same needle position they showed a decade ago.

Earplugs do nothing because the deck vibrates. Sleep is shallow vibration. You have to shout into an ear and the wind of the sound strips your words.

Prepare like this:

- Double hearing protection on deck: foam plugs + electronic earmuffs, plus a mouthguard if you clench — the roar gets into teeth first. Limit unprotected deck time to minutes. Do most work from inside.
- Vibration isolation is more important than silence. Thick insulated boots with vibration soles, knee pads, anti-vibration gloves. To sleep, do not sleep on steel. Hammock or suspended cot with foam, off the main structure if possible, inside the most interior room. Expect shallow, broken sleep anyway. Bring sleep aids you have tested.
- Do not rely on voice. Bring whiteboards, permanent markers, pre-printed cards, throat mics + text radios, headlamps with signal modes, and a simple hand-signal set you agree on *before* you land. The keeper, if there is one, is deaf. Learn to communicate without expecting to be heard.
- Protect your mind. Stable, time-invariant noise drives people into panic or trance. After an hour you stop noticing variation because there is no variation. That is when mistakes happen. Work in 40-minute rotations with mandatory interior quiet breaks (as quiet as it gets). No one stays on grating staring down for hours. Buddy checks every 30 minutes: fingers, face color, O2, sense.
- Accept tinnitus. You will leave with ringing, muffling, maybe temporary threshold shift. Plan 48-72 hours in a truly quiet, low, green, soft world afterward to recover. If you have prior hearing loss, hyperacusis, PTSD triggered by noise/vibration, or a heart condition that hates bass through the sternum — do not come.

### How to behave when you find life

You will cross vast emptiness, then too much at once where stations breathe warm mineral water or a fresh crack leaks heat: billions of thumbnail blind whites pulsing so dense they darken the water from below, plates of isopods crawling over each other, eel ribbons a kilometer long moving as one muscle, and above them slow mill-whales, white blubber and baleen, mouths open.

Rules:

- Slow down, lights dim, sonar off if you can. Their clicks carry too far in this water. Don’t add to it.
- Give grazers 200+ meters. They are slow but immense and they do not maneuver for you. Never go between a mill-whale and its food cloud. Never put a boat across an eel ribbon.
- Do not touch, do not feed, do not collect. Stay in the boat. Those mats paint the shallows in tiger stripes — they are slippery, sharp underneath, and chemically wrong for skin and lungs.
- Watch, log, leave. Blooms collapse as fast as they gather. Don’t chase a darkening patch into shallow glass.

### Stations, the Edge, and the Fourth Rule

Stations: treat as shrines, not shelters, though you may need shelter. Knock, signal with light, enter slowly. Do not touch pumps, valves, control boards. Rule 4: *Keep the engines running. No one remembers what happens if they stop, and no one wants the roar to reveal what is underneath it.*

Take that literally as a visitor ethic. Do not “fix,” do not “optimize,” do not shut down to get quiet. The level never rises, never falls — inflow equals outflow to the liter. That equilibrium is engineered and ancient. Your need for silence is not worth breaking it. Use the interior for warming and vibration-breaks, leave food/fuel in exchange if you use any, sign the log, close doors against lace ice.

The Edge: do not go look. From the center you cannot see it. That is how boats go over. The current looks still on top and moves underneath its stillness. Set a hard GPS/inertial fence: turn back 20 km before any charted edge, in any weather, for any photo. There is no guardrail on a continent waterfall. Mist, ice, and noise will disorient you long before you see white water. If your instruments die, steer by reciprocal bearing from the last station light and leave.

### Packing list for an ordinary body

On person: expedition parka and bibs, vapor barrier liners, 3x base layers, 2x mid, heavy socks x4, -40 boots + overboots with ice cleats, mittens + liners + cut-proof gloves, full-face cold mask x2, glacier glasses + spare, foam earplugs x20 + earmuffs, mouthguard, drysuit + PFD + tether + ice axe/ice staff for ridge work.

Health: supplemental O2 + masks + regulator, pulse oximeter, Diamox (if prescribed), broad-spectrum antibiotics, cold-burn kit, blister kit, rehydration salts, high-SPF sunscreen, sleep kit (mask, aids), personal meds + 2x extra days.

Boat/camp: hard-hull enclosed heated boat, 2x nav methods, radar, 2x comms (text-capable) + throat mics, whiteboard kit, headlamps + spare batteries kept warm, 10-second-flash timing card, tool kit taped, cold-rated fuel/lube, spare prop, grapples/fenders, 200% food/fuel/water, snow-melting stove + fuel, insulated sleeping suspension system, trash bags — pack everything out, even grey water if you can. The water stays empty because it is empty. Keep it that way.

Mind: watch, notebook + pencil (pens freeze), a book with chapters, a routine card, one small green thing, one photo of trees, a letter to read on day three when the white gets into you.

### First hour, if you go

You will step out, think *this is fine, bright overcast Tuesday*, try to breathe, fail a little, hear the roar in your molars, look down through impossible glass to sharp black youth, look up at white, and feel suspended between two shallows that feel like depths.

Do this: mask on, hood up, glasses on, plugs + muffs on, tether on. Breathe slowly through mask. Don’t talk. Give your buddy a thumbs. Go inside first. Feel the deck hum through your boots. Look at the needle that hasn’t moved in ten years. Write down the time, your O2, where you are.

Then go back out for five minutes only. Let the mill-whales click through you. Let the station lights blink — ten seconds — together. Let your breath smoke and hang like their blows.

Then go back in. That is enough for day one.

People ask why visit a place that never changes and never lets you leave its sound. Because there are very few places left, in any world, that are completely honest about what they are: cold, high, clear, loud, stable. It will not perform for you. The tooth will still be sharp tomorrow. The falls will not quiet. The sun will not set.

If you can stand between black glass below and white lid above, deafened by equilibrium, and keep the engines running and keep your clock running and keep each other warm — you learn what your own variation is worth.

Stay short. Stay tethered. Stay warm. Leave quiet.
