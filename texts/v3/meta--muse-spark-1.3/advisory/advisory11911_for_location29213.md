You do not climb this place. You dissolve into it.

Forget everything you know about mountains. There will be no summit push, no scramble, no view. The danger here is not falling. It is softening. This is a warm, high, drowned cloud-forest on top of a plateau so old it has forgotten how to be a mountain. If you go, go to become slow, soft, and quiet for a while — or don't go.

An ordinary human body can survive here for days to weeks with preparation. It will not be comfortable for a single minute. You will be wet the entire time.

Here is how to prepare:

### 1. Understand what your body is walking into

**High and warm and wet, all at once.** That combination doesn't exist in most lowland minds.

You will be at altitude — high enough that breath comes short, clouds are at your knees, your heart hammers on a gentle slope. But it will not be cold and dry like normal altitude. It will be bathhouse-warm, 100% humidity, no sun, no evaporation. You will sweat and the sweat will stay. You will breathe water with every breath.

That means:
- Exertion hits twice as hard. Hypoxia + humidity = you will be winded carrying 15kg on flat ground. Plan to move at 1/3 your normal pace, and 1/5 your normal distance. 5-8 km a day is a big day.
- You will not dry. Not your shirt, not your socks, not your tent, not your skin. Think of this not as "rain" but as living inside a sponge that breathes.
- Nothing is hard. The ground is meters-deep living felt: moss on peat on humus on moss. Stone is buried, rotted to soap you can gouge with a nail. There are no trails, no sharp edges, no rock to sit on. Every step goes *chuff* and fills with brown water behind you.

You are going to a place where water never leaves, it only pauses. Act like it.

### 2. Train your body before you go

You don't need climbing strength. You need bog strength and rot resistance.

- **Lungs:** If you live at sea level, spend 3-5 days ascending slowly. This hill "never gets steeper" — it lulls you into climbing for days without acclimatizing because it doesn't *feel* high. It is. Sleep low if you can on the approach, hydrate constantly, avoid alcohol. Bring acetazolamide if a doctor will give it to you, and a fingertip pulse oximeter. If your resting pulse won't come down and you get a headache that doesn't lift with water and rest, descend. There is no rescue up here.
- **Feet:** This trip will be decided by feet. Start now: toughen them, trim nails short and square, learn to tape hot spots. In the Roof, trench foot / immersion foot is a matter of days, not weeks. You need a system to get your feet *out* of wet for at least a few hours a day, or they will macerate white, crack, and get infected.
- **Skin:** If you get athlete's foot, jock itch, eczema, or ingrown things easily, see it as a preview. Bring antifungals and assume you'll use them. Shave less — micro-cuts become sores that never dry.
- **Gut:** The water is tea-dark, acidic, full of tannin, leaf-rot, frog life and peat. Even filtered it will be strange. Get your stomach stable before you go.

Get your vaccines, tetanus especially. Every scratch here stays wet.

### 3. Train your mind — the quiet will get you first

Newcomers expect green and wet. They don't expect the silence.

The mat eats sound. Your voice dies in five steps. Your footsteps make no noise. Birds don't sing, they flash color. Frogs pulse without croaking. You'll hear only drip, suck, plink-plink from leaf to leaf, wind arriving a mile late, and very rarely a deep boom you feel in your sternum as a 300-year-old emergent settles into the peat over an afternoon.

No sun. No shadows. No morning or evening, just variations of grey-green aquarium gloom. No horizon — on a "clear" day you see fifty paces of green folding into grey into white.

People get:
- Time-slipped and sleepy. Without sun your circadian rhythm drifts. Bring a watch and enforce sleep/wake times.
- Lonely and paranoid in the white. Distances collapse. You think you hear things.
- Summit-frustrated. There is no payoff view. If you need conquest, you'll despair.

Practice: sit still for an hour in rain without music or phone. If you can't stand that, don't go for a week. Bring a small ritual — tea, journal, wringing out socks — and do it daily. Move deliberately. The rule here is: *be slow, be soft, be quiet, and grow over whatever came before.* If you fight it with speed and noise, you will exhaust yourself and damage the only thing holding you up.

### 4. What to bring — everything must love water or hate it

Forget metal, glass, leather, down, cotton. Cotton kills here, down drowns, leather rots in 48 hours, untreated steel rusts overnight. The Roof digests tools. Bring plastic, titanium, silicone, synthetic.

**Clothing — no single dry outfit, a rotation system:**
- No cotton, ever. Merino wool + synthetics only.
- 3x socks in separate roll-top dry bags + 1 pair dedicated *only* for sleeping, which never touches ground. Merino, mid-weight. Plus foot powder, zinc oxide, and a small bottle of povidone-iodine.
- Quick-dry hiking pants, not shorts — the moss-midges, hanging hair-roots, and peat suckers will flay bare legs. Gaiters to keep peat out of boots.
- Long-sleeve synthetics. Everything will stay damp; you want warm-when-wet, not waterproof.
- Rain: a normal breathable shell will wet-out in an hour and become a sauna. Most veterans prefer a big, loose sil-nylon poncho/tarp that vents, plus a wide-brim hat, not a hood. You *will* get wet anyway. The goal is warm-wet, not dry.
- Camp sandals / foam sliders. Critical: to air feet in the evening while you hang in hammock.
- Buff/headnet. The moss-midges come in clouds. They don't bite much but they get in eyes, nose, mouth.
- Gloves: thin, quick-dry for pushing through hanging roots.

Glasses wearers: bring a strap, microfiber in a dry bag, and accept permanent fog. Contacts are an infection risk in this mist — bring dailies + obsessive hand hygiene if you must.

**Feet / Movement:**
- Boots: waterproof boots are a trap — once water gets in over the top (and it will on step one) they never drain. Better: non-waterproof trail runners or mid bog boots that drain, with aggressive lugs, plus neoprene socks for warmth. Or wide rubber bog boots if you run cold. Whatever you bring, bring *two* insoles to rotate.
- Trekking poles with big snowshoe baskets. Not optional. You need them to test ground — peat tunnels made by the blind velvet-nosed rooters collapse — and to spread your weight. You are walking on a waterbed with a skin.
- Think snowshoes for really long stays: short, wide bog shoes keep you from post-holing to the knee in saturated hollows. Most short visitors manage without, by stepping slow and reading domes vs. hollows.

**Sleep / Shelter — never sleep on the ground:**
The ground sighs, fills your depression with water, swells a finger's breadth overnight. Ground camping = sleeping in a puddle that breathes.
- Hammock with integrated bug net + big tarp is ideal. Trees here are not single trunks but neighborhoods furred in moss two hands thick. Use extra-wide tree straps to avoid girdling living bark, hang high enough to be out of the seep.
- Underquilt or synthetic pad, *not down*. Synthetic 40F/5C quilt is plenty warm-wise, but you need it for damp, not cold.
- Bivy + tarp works if no suitable hang, but bring a thick inflatable pad and accept damp.
- Do NOT use the woven living hollows as shelters unless emergency. Some are fresh — they are nests, egg places, waiting places for *someone*. Sleeping in them crushes generations of coaxing and may bring you face to face with the owner. Look, photograph, leave alone. Same for the waist-high lichen-bearded stone circles. Do not move them, clean them, sit on them. No one knows what they were. The forest is already growing over them. Let it.

**Water / Food / Fire:**
- There is water everywhere and almost none you should drink raw. Shallow sheets, marsh pools, and especially the bathtub bromeliad crotch-pools are nurseries — tadpoles live whole lives there, amphibians signal in ripples. Don't drink them, don't piss in them, don't wash in them.
  Bring: a squeeze filter + chemical backup (chlorine dioxide) + a collapsible bucket to let peat water settle first. Pre-filter through a bandana — peat clogs everything. Tea color will remain; that's tannin, not danger, once filtered/disinfected. Bring electrolytes — you sweat constantly but never see it evaporate. Drink before you're thirsty.
- Food: bring all calories. Foraging is unethical and unsafe in a place where everything lives on something else living and rots over a decade. Dense, no-cook foods: nuts, cheese, salami, tortillas, peanut butter, chocolate. Appetite drops at altitude + in gloom; force salty soups.
- Fire: forget it. Everything is saturated. There is no dry wood, no cut timber, and cutting living bark-gardens is vandalism. Bring a canister stove + windscreen + 2x fuel you think you need. You will want hot drinks more for morale than calories. Keep lighter + ferro rod + tinder in triple dry bags.

**Light, Power, Navigation:**
- It is always dim. Bring a good headlamp (flood > throw in mist) + a small warm lantern. Use red light at night — the local birds communicate in flashes of blue to orange, moths the size of your hand drift like ash. White light blinds them and you.
- Sun is a rumor. Solar is useless. Bring a big power bank in a dry case with silica gel. Everything electronic must live in dry bags when not in use. Mist gets *inside* everything.
- Visibility is 20-50 paces. The rivers spread instead of run, hills breathe up and down, canopy repeats forever. You *will* get lost without discipline.
  Bring: compass (GPS may work, but cloud + canopy kills signal), paper map in waterproof case if you have one, watch/altimeter. Flagging: use removable, pack-it-out tape sparingly, or better, GPS breadcrumbs and natural notes. Cairns are impossible — no loose stone — and damaging. Practice walking a bearing in fog before you go.

**Health kit, Roof-specific:**
- Blister + foot rot kit: Leukotape, moleskin, alcohol wipes, antifungal cream (clotrimazole), drying powder (tolnaftate), small nail brush.
- Wound kit: irrigation syringe, antiseptic, butterfly closures, gauze that can be changed *often*. In this damp, cover wounds; check twice daily.
- Bug: DEET or picaridin, permethrin pre-treated clothes, antihistamines.
- Gut: filter + tabs + anti-diarrheal + oral rehydration salts.
- Lungs: mold spores are intense. If asthmatic, bring inhaler + spare + mask (a light N95 for sleeping in particularly musty hollows). If you develop persistent cough, wheeze, fever — leave.
- Earplugs (for drips at night, ironically) and a whistle + signal mirror + bright orange tarp/panel. Your voice won't carry. Visual signals work where sound doesn't.

### 5. How to walk, camp, and behave

**Walk like water:**
Test, step, wait. Let the mat rebound. Don't stomp, don't drag. Step on moss domes, avoid black hollows and too-bright green floating mats — that's water with an opinion. Follow root curtains, not channels.

Move in daylight only — though daylight barely changes. Never walk at night. Mist droplets hang suspended and shatter when you brush them; you'll be blind and loud (for here).

When a rainhead comes — and it always comes, darkening a mile off, moving at dream-speed — don't run. There is nowhere to run to. Sit. Put on poncho. Watch a glass frog pulse green-gold. Let it settle softly. It will not lash. It will just join the sponge.

**Be quiet on purpose:**
Talk low. No music speakers. No shouting to find friends — agree on whistle codes (one = where are you, three = help) and visual check-ins. Learn to flash a headlamp or colored cloth instead of yelling.

If you sit an hour and hold your breath you'll start to hear smallness: tick of a seed pod high up, suck of peat, long exhalation of distant wind. That's the actual volume of this world. Match it.

**Animals:**
Slow shaggy algae-furred climbers hanging for days in a fork, blind velvet rooters tunneling peat, glass frogs, gliders that never flap. They have learned quiet over eons. They are not afraid because they have never needed to be fast. Do not touch, feed, or corner them. Give hanging forks a wide berth. Don't shine white light in throat-sacs. Don't step into crotch-pools.

**Leave almost no trace — because trace lasts:**
A footprint here rebounds slowly and fills with water in seconds. Peat records you for years. Rivers migrate a hand's width in a lifetime; your shortcut ditch could become a river. Walk single file on the most resilient dome, don't braid new paths around puddles. Pack out everything, including biodegradable food — rot takes a decade here, nursed by fungi leaf by leaf.

Nothing sharp survives. That includes attitudes. Don't carve, don't hammer, don't build. Oil your knife and keep it sheathed unless needed; humidity dulls edge and temper alike.

### 6. Dangers that are not obvious

1. **Getting benighted/lost 200 meters from camp.** In white-out mist with no shadows, you can circle. Always note back-bearing, time, and a handrail (that slow tea-sheet river, that line of emergents). If lost: STOP. Shelter, warm drink, wait for mist to thin. It always thins a little.
2. **Foot and fungal collapse.** Check feet every night: dry, powder, air in hammock, socks inside sleeping bag to steal some warmth. If skin between toes goes white and pitted and stays, take a zero day with feet up. If red streaks climb, evacuate.
3. **Hypothermia in warmth.** 22°C + soaked + wind + tired + altitude = shivering you didn't expect. Always have a dry-warm layer sealed away that never gets worn walking.
4. **Bad water + dehydration.** Ironically easy to dehydrate because water is everywhere but undrinkable without work, and you don't feel thirsty in mist. Schedule drinking.
5. **Tree-fall.** Rare, but when a giant emergent goes, it doesn't crash — it settles over an afternoon with a sternum-boom. Don't camp under leaning emergents or in the fall line of big crowns. Look up — ponds fall from branches too.
6. **Psychological softening.** After day 4 many people feel weepy, dreamy, meaningless. Colors glow at edges. Time dilates. That's normal here. Don't make big decisions in it. If you feel the Roof inviting you to just lie down and be grown over — warm, quiet, tired — recognize it as exhaustion + hypoxia + beauty, eat salt, sleep, talk to a human.

### 7. A simple packing mantra

If you remember nothing else:

**Stay up, stay warm-wet not cold-wet, stay found, stay unrotted.**

Stay up = hammock, poles, don't sleep or walk in hollows.
Stay warm-wet = synthetics, hot stove, sealed dry layer.
Stay found = compass, watch, whistle, orange, breadcrumbs.
Stay unrotted = feet, filter, antifungal, electrolytes.

And go short first. Whoever said you could walk months edge to edge without stepping on anything dead was not talking about you. For an ordinary human, a 3-5 day loop from the edge, with a cache and a hard turn-around time, is a full expedition. The interior — months of green going to grey going to white — is not a hike. It's a residence.

If you stand on the highest swell and look out and see no edge, only layer folding into layer, canopy into mist into canopy, sit down. Wring out your shirt. Watch the frog. Listen to the mat breathe under you.

You have time. Here, you have nothing but time, softly, endlessly settling into the sponge of the world.
