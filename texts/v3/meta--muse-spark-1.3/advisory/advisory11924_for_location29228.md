You are not going to a city in a valley. You are going to a shallow bowl of warm, wet air with a lid on it, where someone recently printed a city and the jungle is already trying to take it back.

Prepare like you would for a long, slow, dim caving trip that happens outdoors, in heat, where you might not see another person for hours even though you're in town.

Here's how to walk in ready:

### 1. Understand what your body is walking into

**Low and thick.** You come off the rim road and descend into almost sea-level air. It gets warmer and heavier in steps. If you have asthma, bad knees, heart issues, or get heat-sick easily, take this seriously. Acclimatize on the way down. Stop, breathe, let your ears pop. The veil above breathes very slowly and the pressure shifts with it. You will feel it in your sinuses first.

Noon here is like the last 30 minutes before night elsewhere. Amber-brown, warm, sourceless. There is no sun to orient to. Your eyes will take 20-30 minutes to stop straining. After that you can see, but you won't see *well* — colors wash out, distances collapse in mist. You can't hold the whole bowl at once. It's a day's walk across, more when the veil goes dark.

It's warm. Humid enough that clothes never quite dry. You will come out damp at the shoulders and hairline just from walking through the air. The air *is* the river here.

That means for an ordinary human:
- You will sweat and it won't evaporate. You will overheat faster than you expect because you don't feel like you're working.
- Your feet will stay wet. Your skin will stay wet. Anything that stays wet gets soft, blisters, then infected here.
- Your circadian rhythm will break in two days if you let it. Bring a watch and keep a schedule. It is always dusk.

Train beforehand with long walks in heat and humidity with a pack. Practice drinking on schedule, not on thirst.

### 2. Dress and pack for wet-sharp-dim

Forget cotton. Everything must be quick-dry synthetic or light merino, and you need more changes than you think because nothing dries.

**Feet are survival:**
Puncture-resistant, stiff-soled boots with ankle support and aggressive tread. The ground is *young* — freshly fractured basalt, glass-edged drainage cuts, foundation lips, curbs that will slice a soft shoe and your hand if you catch yourself. Moss over fresh stone is treacherously slick. Bring 3-4 pairs of socks in separate dry-bags with silica, plus gaiters, plus antifungal powder and blister kit. Treat every cut immediately. In this compost-warm air, a curb nick festers overnight.

**Skin and rain:**
Warm, close rain, not cold mountain rain. The veil sweats — it gathers the basin's breath all morning and drops it in a heavy, warm 20-minute shower, usually after all the towers hum together in a chord you feel through your feet. When you feel that hum, get to high ground on the pale paths. Do not stand in the stone channels. They run fast for ten minutes and the rain is heavy enough to knock you sideways.

You want: brimmed hat or hood for condensation bombs falling a hundred meters off the veil-nets, a light breathable rain shell, quick-dry pants, rain kilt or shorts that shed. Gloves — thin work gloves — for those sharp edges and for pushing through sail-leaf walls.

**Lungs and nose:**
Lower floors are still outgassing mineral heat, the threads give off ozone, and everything smells of wet stone, crushed leaf, compost. Mold load is enormous. Pale knuckle-fungi push up overnight. If you're mold-allergic, bring a half-face respirator with P3/particulate filters, or at minimum a well-fit N95 + antihistamines. Even if you're not, bring one. You'll want it when clearing a dripping skirt of epiphytes under a tower.

**Eyes and light:**
Bring two lights: 
1. Your own warm, dimmable headlamp with a red mode. You will need it, but do not blast white light around. The locals navigate by small cold drifting suns at shoulder height that follow old street lines. Follow them when it gets very dark. Don't compete with them.
2. Wraparound sunglasses, always on your head. Most of the time you won't need them. Once in a while the veil opens an eye — a 100-meter thinning that drifts like a spotlight and drops *true* brutal white-gold daylight for a minute. People stop and lift their faces. The moss steams. Sail-leaves recoil. If you're caught looking up, you'll be blinded and burned. When the light suddenly sharpens, stop, get glasses on or look down, cover the back of your neck.

Shadows here linger a second too long after you move, and different towers cast shadows in different directions, as if they remember different suns. Do not trust your peripheral vision at dusk. Use touch and a stick in mist. Mark your route with high-contrast tape or chalk — you won't see earth-tones in amber light.

Electronics hate this place. Humidity, sweat, sudden downpours. Double dry-bag everything, bring silica, bring a whistle and a signal mirror because shouts die fast — the walls absorb sound, the veil eats sky-noise. Radio can be weird under the membrane.

### 3. Water, food, sleep

**Water:** There are no rivers, no lakes. Water exists as film, bead, pocket — in leaf-axils, in carved basins at street corners, in sharp-edged puddles that mirror amber for an hour then vanish into moss.

Carry at least 2-3 liters plus a filter and chemical backup. Do *not* assume a pretty puddle is safe. Dew-net runoff and gutter water is full of leaf tannin, birdless guano, ceramic dust, spores.

Those corner basins are *not* decorative. The buildings drink from them through capillary stone. Rule, absolute: Leave water for the walls. If you must take, take little and top up from your own carry if you can. An empty basin will crack by morning like a mouth. Locals notice, and it's a bad way to introduce yourself as a traveler.

Top up whenever you can from tower spigots / maintenance points, not from leaves. Keep one bottle strictly clean.

**Food:** Bring calorie-dense, mold-resistant food in sealed packs: nuts, jerky, hard cheese, tortillas, energy bars. Bread will fur in a day. Do not forage. Do not pick the white waxy flowers — not because you'll be arrested, but because they dim when cut and the big grey blind moths, big as hands, will follow you for hours bumping softly against you and your lamp all night. Do not eat the pale curb fungi. There are almost no pollinators, almost no animals — one pale lizard a day, maybe — which means the food web here is not built for you. Eat what you brought. Pack out crumbs; things grow obscenely fast here.

**Sleep:** You will be sleeping damp. Bring a hammock or cot, not just a groundsheet — the ground sweats. A bivvy with a good bug/moth net, earplugs, and a sleep mask with a dim amber nightlight on a timer helps keep a rhythm. At night the moths bump the floating lamps. High near the veil something glides tower to tower — animal or feral maintenance drone — and calls once, a falling glass note. It will freeze your blood the first time. It's normal. Let it pass.

### 4. How to move in the city

The city refuses to be dense on purpose. Towers half a kilometer apart or more, vast kept emptiness between them: plazas of dark fresh stone, empty transit fields, wild gardens, hollows left empty so the eye can rest. You *can* be utterly alone in the middle of a city here. Plan for self-sufficiency and loneliness.

**Pale paths are safe paths.** Stay on them, especially when the towers hum. The hum means rain in minutes.

**Transit threads:** Where you expect bridges there is often nothing — just a heat-shimmer line. You step in and think where you want to go, and silent pods lit from inside sometimes take you. As an ordinary human without training:

- Calm your mind first. Don't ride tired, drunk, panicked, or desperately late. The thread reads intention sloppily.
- Have a clear, simple image: the tower with the copper gutters, not "somewhere dry."
- If a thread won't take you, *don't insist.* It means a tower is dreaming or balancing. Insisting gets you put two blocks sideways, damp and disoriented in mist. Step out, wait, walk.
- Keep your limbs inside, keep your pack tight, don't reach for the shimmer.

**Towers:** Lower floors poured stone, upper floors a smoky translucent mass that feels solid when you lean and goes to mist when you step away, as if it only bothers to be real when witnessed. Don't lean your life on it. Don't climb the vine skirts — vines 20 stories high, dripping — they are heavy, wet, and the tendrils will test the air toward you. The plants here don't track the sun. They track heat and movement. Walk past a wall of sail-leaves and they will slowly turn to face you. If you stand still long enough, tendrils will reach for your breath.

Don't panic. It's not predatory in a movie sense. It's opportunistic shade-green life in low light: huge elephant-ear philos, door-tall ferns, velvet moss, bearded epiphytes, all moving fast in the warmth. Give them space. Don't cut them. Use a stick, not your bare hand, to part them. Wash after contact.

### 5. Etiquette — because this place is negotiating

Everything here is still deciding what it is. Stone and vine, machine and weather. The erosion hasn't forgiven anything yet. Act like a guest in a nursery, not a tourist in ruins.

- **Fill, don't drain.** Leave basins full.
- **Don't pick white flowers.** Admire them glowing a little in the dim. Leave them for the moths.
- **Don't be loud.** The city is quiet for a city. No traffic, threads silent, walls absorbent. Visitors notice it as wrongness. Don't try to fix it with a speaker. Listen for drips, your breath, condensation ticking off nets onto broad leaves.
- **When the Eye opens, stop.** Locals lift their faces. Do the same, briefly, with eyes half-closed. Don't run, don't photograph with flash, don't shout. Let it pass and the kinder dim will settle back.
- **Don't chase the glider, don't poke maintenance shafts, don't pull dew-nets strung like harps.** Old people say the veil was raised to cool a burning plain, engineers from before maintain it or it maintains itself. Either way, you are under a hand held over a seed tray. Don't bite the hand.
- Ask before sheltering under a tower skirt. Leave no trace, but leave water.

### 6. Mind preparation

This is the part most travelers underprepare.

You will be damp for days. You will be dim for days. Your shadows will misbehave. The green will watch you. Footsteps will echo a little too long in all that lush emptiness. With so much green there *should* be birds and chatter, but there's almost none — an emptiness inside the lushness. That absence gets to people.

Bring a routine: morning check feet, air gear, log position. Midday rest during the sweatiest veil cycle. Evening dry-foot time in camp even if nothing else is dry. Bring something small and human to anchor you — tea, a book, a familiar song hummed quietly. Bring a journal; time blurs without a sun.

If you start feeling watched and paranoid because every sail-leaf turned toward your breathing — that's normal here. Name it, drink water, keep walking on the pale path. If you start feeling euphoric and dissociated when the towers tune and the warm rain comes — that's also normal. Sit down until it passes.

### Packing Checklist for an Ordinary Human

- Broken-in waterproof boots (puncture plate), 3-4x socks in dry bags, gaiters, camp sandals
- Quick-dry layers, no cotton; rain shell + hood/hat; 1 warm mid-layer (damp chill is real)
- Work gloves, sunglasses + safety glasses, headlamp (warm + red) + spare batteries in dry case
- 3L water carry, filter + tablets, collapsible cup
- Sealed food for duration + 1 day extra, trash bag
- Respirator/N95, antihistamines, antifungal powder/cream, broad antibiotic ointment, blister kit, rehydration salts
- Whistle, compass + paper map in waterproof case, high-vis route tape, watch
- Hammock/cot + tarp + moth net, earplugs, sleep mask
- Trekking pole/stick, knife, cord, silica packs, two dry bags, pack cover
- Offline notes: tower landmarks, thread etiquette, basin rule

**Arrival routine:** Come down slow. Stop halfway, drink, let eyes adjust. When you hit the first plaza, stand still. Listen. Feel the stone — faint mineral heat still outgassing. Find the nearest pale path and corner basin. Note the floating lamps — that's your night highway. Find high ground for the first hum. Don't try to cross the bowl on day one.

If you do it right, on the second or third evening you'll be standing in one of those huge spaces between towers at brown-gold hour, beading wet on every edge, sail-leaves turning one by one toward the sound of you, condensation ticking down, and you'll get it: not a town, not a wilderness, but a whole region learning how to be inhabited. You're not there to conquer it. You're there to learn to be briefly, damply, quietly part of the negotiation — and to leave the basins full behind you.
