This is not a dive. It is an ice-cave that happens to be the size of a continent.

Don't think of it as "underwater ruins." Think of it as a cold, dark, roofed wilderness made of cut stone and living thatch, 100+ meters down, under a ceiling of ice or permanent winter, with no animals in it at all. You will be the only animal. The only warm thing. The only fast thing.

An ordinary human cannot visit this place as a swimmer. You can only visit as a small, slow spacecraft you wear. Prepare accordingly.

### 1. Understand what will kill you

**Pressure / Depth:** The green rumor of surface is a hundred meters above the floors. That is 11 atmospheres on your body. Air at that depth is poison - you will go narcotic-stupid and then seize from oxygen. You need helium in the mix, and you need decompression. This is full trimix, closed-circuit rebreather territory, not recreational scuba. Bottom time is measured in minutes, deco in hours.

**Cold:** 1-2C, everywhere, always. Not sharp air-cold. Seeping water-cold that finds every zipper and wrist. Bare fingers go stupid in minutes. A flooded drysuit here is not uncomfortable. It is an emergency that ends your dive and possibly your life. Batteries, regulators, hands, and judgment all degrade fast in this cold.

**Darkness and white-out:** There is no day, only a direction. Below 100m what arrives is not enough to see by. Your lamp is your sun, but visibility dies after six or seven meters in cold haze - spores, shed algal skin, fine mineral wool that never settles. Shine a bright light forward and you just light up snow. You learn distance by pressure on your hood, by cold leaking in, by how long it takes your exhale to come back to you.

**Entanglement and overhead:** You do not walk here. You push through. Black kelp with stipes thick as your wrist, fronds 30 meters long, layered ceiling over sunken streets. Below that, velvet encrusting mats you can push a finger into to the knuckle. Feather-weed, fleshy fingers. Lintels still spanning walls with kelp hanging like drowned thatch. Rooms still roofed, full to the doorway with growth.

This is a cave diving environment without a cave line already laid. Any frond can close behind your tank, your valve, your mask strap. If you kick hard you will silt out - that meter-deep felt of silt in the courtyards, ankle-deep on the causeways, will hang for hours if you stir it. In a silt-out inside a forest inside a roofed room in the black at 100m in 2C water, you die unless you are on a line.

**Loneliness of biology:** No fish. No crabs, urchins, seals, gulls. No clicks, no songs, no darting. Only empty barnacle pits long dissolved. That absence does strange things to a mammal brain. Your breathing will sound obscenely loud. You will hear stipe creaking against stipe, and occasionally from miles away a deep soft thud - a slab settling another millimeter after a thousand years - and a long sigh of silt slumping off a terrace. Do not chase those sounds. You cannot place them.

If you are not already an ice, cave, wreck, and kelp technical diver with hundreds of cold dives, do not go in your body. Send an ROV. Or go in a small crewed submersible / atmospheric suit. For an ordinary human traveler, that is the sane preparation. What follows is if you insist on going wet.

### 2. Train before you go

You need, at minimum:

* Technical CCR trimix certified to 100m+, with bailout and deco procedures.
* Full cave training: line laying, lost line, lost viz, air sharing in zero viz, rule of thirds (here use rule of fourths - the forest eats gas).
* Ice diving experience: tethered, with surface tender, cold-rated regulators, valve shutdowns with numb hands.
* Kelp experience in a drysuit: frog kick, modified frog, helicopter turn, pull-and-glide without touching. Practice being wrapped and staying still. Practice cutting yourself out slowly while maintaining buoyancy and a line in your hand.

Get fit for slow work. This place punishes exertion. You will be pulling yourself hand-over-hand over stone as much as finning. CO2 buildup on a rebreather in the cold while pushing through fronds will end you. Practice moving at the pace of a sleeping breath.

### 3. What to wear and bring

Think streamlined, redundant, warm, and snag-free. Tape everything down.

**Thermal:** Heavy trilaminate drysuit with dry hood, dry gloves with thick liners, and active heating - heated vest and heated gloves with redundant battery canisters kept inside your suit insulation. Thick wool undergarments. Argon for suit inflation if you can get it. No exposed skin. Bring a second set of undergarments and seals on the surface. You will surface hypothermic even on a good dive.

**Breathing:** Cold-rated, environmentally sealed CCR with helium diluent, with at least two independent bailout cylinders with appropriate trimix and nitrox/oxygen deco gases rigged tight to your body. No dangling octos, no console on a hose to catch kelp. Two cutting tools in different places - trauma shears and a line cutter on your wrist - not a big knife on your leg that becomes a kelp anchor.

Expect freeflow. Practice breathing a freeflowing regulator, switching to bailout, shutting down a valve you cannot feel.

**Buoyancy and movement:** Backplate/wing or sidemount configured cave-clean. Small, stiff fins for frog kicks, not long freedive fins. No snorkel. A DPV (scooter) sounds tempting for kilometers of true-running canals, but in dense curtain kelp a scooter is a blender that will wrap you instantly. If you bring one, use it only above the forest on the high causeways, and be ready to ditch tow.

Stay slightly negative to neutral, off the bottom. Never stand on the silt felt. One fin sweep will blind you and everyone behind you for an hour.

**Light:** Primary canister light with Goodman handle + two backup torches, all with burn times rated for cold - cold halves them. Carry them warm until entry. In particulate water, hold the light off to the side, not in front of your mask, to reduce backscatter. A dim, wide video light is kinder than a tight spot. Bring a strobe/marker for your exit line.

**Navigation:** You will get lost. Courtyard, stair, canal, wall, courtyard, stair, identical for miles, flattened by age into one vast made plain.

- A continuous guideline to open water / ice hole. Reels, spools, line arrows and cookies. Tie off on stone, not on kelp stipes - they move.
- Compass on wrist, wrist slate, and a pre-planned map with hard turn pressures/times, not just time.
- Through-water comms or at minimum hard line signals with your tender, and a buddy team of at least two in the water, plus tenders on the surface.
- Sonar pinger / personal locator if you have multiverse tech. 6 meters of viz means you can lose your buddy by turning your head.

### 4. The surface camp

You exit to ice, or to winter that never lifts. You need:

A heated hut over the hole, ice saws/augers, a large melted entry hole with a rim to keep kelp and slush out, a downline with a decompression trapeze or habitat. Hot water for regulators and hands, hot sweet drinks, high-calorie food, emergency oxygen, first aid for cold injury and DCS, and a plan for evacuation to a chamber - which in the multiverse may mean your own portal out.

Tend the line. Someone stays dry, warm, and attentive and counts your bubbles and your time. No one dives alone here. Solo is suicide; the forest will hold your body like a basket and fur it over in a season.

Limit exposure: 20-30 minutes on the bottom is a lot here. Plan for 2-3x that in deco and rewarming. Shivering under water is abort. Clumsy hands is abort. Euphoria or anxiety from narcosis/cold is abort.

### 5. How to move once you're inside

Enter gently. Vent, descend the line, pause to let your suit and body accept the cold.

Move like the place moves: patient.

- Kick gently so as not to stir silt. Frog kick, look behind you for the cloud you are making. If you make one, stop, hold the line, breathe slow until it thins.
- Push fronds aside with the back of your hand, don't grab and yank. Holdfasts are strong as hands and you will tear your gloves and destroy decades of millimeters of growth. Slide through horizontally where fronds trail. Where they pile in still water like warm-less peat, go around or above, not through.
- Don't enter roofed rooms. They are full to the doorway with slow green-black growth, with stone above you where a lintel still spans and kelp hangs down. One silt-out inside and you have no up.
- Touch stone lightly. Everything is round as knuckles now, tilted two degrees, five degrees, settled into final lean. Mortar has softened to oozing clay. Walls after ten thousand years do not crash, they exhale and lean on their neighbor. Do not lean on them, do not pull blocks, do not fan silt aggressively. Under the silt is another floor - tile, herringbone brick set on edge, another floor beneath that. You are swimming over many pavings of a world.

If you rise a little over a platform edge and look out, you'll see it repeated until dark takes it: platform, canal, wall, courtyard, stair, forest. Take that moment and then go back to your line. Awe makes divers linger past gas.

### 6. What not to disturb

Nothing has finished falling apart, and rot here is slow, almost thoughtful. That is why it is precious.

Black oak poles still stand in sockets. A woven basket is still a basket under algae. A coil of rope turned iron-hard. A sandal print filled with hardened silt in a brickyard the size of a province. Cup-marks of pounders, short chattering copper chisel strokes, rope holes for wooden pegs, rubble ramps where sledges were dragged - all made with almost nothing, just weight and water and time and more people than stones.

Do not lift the basket rim. Do not souvenir brick or pinkish mortar. Do not scrub algae to see better. Within a season a fallen face is furred over and stitched down - your fin scrape will be there for a century.

And sterilize everything before entry. You are bringing alien microbes, sunscreen, suit talc, spit into a system with no grazers left to check growth. Rinse, disinfect, rinse again. Take nothing biological in or out. Don't pee in your suit here if you can avoid it - nutrients matter where nothing eats.

### 7. Psychological preparation

Prepare for being unwatched and unnecessary.

On land forests, things move away from you. Here nothing darts away because there is nothing with eyes. Spores hang around your mask. A frond brushes your cheek, another your knees. Your lamp finds only near-touching: stone, then an inch of water, then leaf, then leaf, then stone again.

Light will not increase. Warmth will not come. The current will turn twice a day at the pace of a sleeping breath, bringing stone-dust and leaf-tea from one endless ward to the next. The kelp will lengthen a cell, then another.

You will feel too loud, too warm, too fast. Practice slow breathing before you go. Practice sitting in black cold water doing nothing but holding a line while your brain begs for stimulus. Bring a mantra for the thuds.

Most travelers report not fear but grief and humility on return - grief for the hands that fitted slabs longer than a man is tall with stone hammers and fiber ropes at a scale so large it made its own slow weather, and then flooded, or sank, or was left. And humility because the place went on, cold and dark and green-black and quiet, growing over itself forever without anything left to watch but the growth itself.

Let yourself be a brief watcher. Lay line, look, photograph without touching, and leave.

When you come up, rewarm slowly, hydrate hot, watch each other for shivering that won't stop, pale waxy fingers, confusion, joint pain, dizziness - hypothermia and bends love this profile. Log your dive, clean your gear of black fibers and silt, and sit a while in the heat before you try to speak about what you saw.

You went to a sea that happens to have floors. Come back still patient.
