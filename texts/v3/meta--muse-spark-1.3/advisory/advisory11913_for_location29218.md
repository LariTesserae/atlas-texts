This is not a ruin. It's a room that became a village.

Think of an Indian stepwell - a square-cut pit about forty paces across at the rim, stair-stepped down in broad terraces of limestone, lime-wash, patched brick and smoke-black timber until it narrows to a paved floor with a round cistern throat in the middle. But people never left it. They moved *into* the walls.

You will not be exploring wilderness. You will be a guest in someone's extremely dense, vertical, echoing house. Prepare like that.

### 1. Your body first: heat, light, stairs

Down here the sky is a lid. At noon sun falls straight down. The rest of the day it bounces wall to wall until shadows climb the steps in order and the bottom goes amber and green. From the rim you can see the water. From the bottom you see only a cut-out square of blue crossed by wings.

Warm air pools in the pit by mid-morning and does not leave until well after dark. The masonry gives it back all afternoon. It is not gloomy, it is an oven with daylight.

Prepare for:

**Heat trap + reflected sun.** Even in shade you are being cooked from three sides. Acclimate before you go. Train stairs - your knees and calves will do hundreds of dished, foot-worn steps a day, up and down, with loads. There is no wheel, no big pulley. If it goes down, it comes back up by rope, back, and shoulder, with chanting to keep time. That includes you and your bag, water jars, shit jars, sick goats.

Wear: loose, light, breathable long sleeves and long pants that dry fast. You need sun cover *and* mosquito cover at dusk. A wide hat that won't catch on low awnings and rope bridges. Sunglasses - the bounce will blind you even when you're not looking up. A bandana / light dust mask. At midday everyone hides under reed awnings, goats doze, pigeons pant with open beaks. You do the same. Don't try to be heroic at noon.

Footwear is critical. Every step is dished in the middle by bare feet, goat hooves, wet jars. Handholds at corners are polished to a greasy shine. Rope has sawn inch-deep notches in coping stones. Morning condensation beads under every lintel and on awnings and falls as a brief private rain that pocks the dust and fills foot-hollows for an hour. Lime plaster bubbles, salt blooms in white beards, moss slicks a perpetually weeping spout. In short: it is slippery.

Bring soft-soled sandals or approach shoes with real grip that can get wet, get muddy, get goaty, and dry by noon. No hard suitcase, no rolling bag, no heels, no smooth soles. Soft pack you can carry on your back with both hands free for handholds. Give way to children running with baskets on their heads - they have impossible sureness. You do not. Step to the inside, hold on, let the uphill carrier keep rhythm.

**Night swing:** After dawn condensation and after dark, the stones exhale. It gets cold-damp fast when the air above cools. Bring a layer - a light fleece or shawl that handles damp. You will want it at 4am when the drip is loudest.

**The rim:** The parapet is ankle-height in flat glare. Do not walk the edge upright. The locals lie on their belly to look over. Do that. Especially at dusk when the kestrel stoops from the parapet, banks off the walls, and takes a pigeon mid-pit to a thunder of echo. Vertigo + low wall + a flock explosion is a bad combination.

### 2. Water discipline: you do not foul the bottom

Water does not fill this place. It haunts it.

There was more once - you can see the dark tide-line halfway up the lowest course, mineral-white and moss-black. Now the great central throat holds only an ankle-deep pan that never quite dries and never quite rises. What there is instead is damp: seep, sweat, drip. Maidenhair fern in drip-lines, caper bushes exploding sideways from a joint, moss where a spout never stops.

Unspoken law, absolute: **Water belongs to the bottom. Drinking water is drawn from the pan. You may dip at the top spouts for washing, but no one fouls the pan.**

As a traveler with a soft gut:

Bring your own water in, enough for 24 hours, and a way to purify - tablets or a squeeze filter plus a cup. Do not assume you can drink the pan safely on day one. Ask permission before you draw at all. Never wash, spit, rinse hands, or cool feet in the pan. Wash at the top spouts only, sparingly, with no soap in the flow. If true rain comes - rarely, then all at once, terraces to cascades, gutters roaring for twenty minutes - drop everything and help. Everyone is out with jars and hides and cupped hands, laughing, because the cistern runs chocolate-brown and full. Then it settles and seeps away into deeper tanks. That is the moment to be useful, not to photograph.

Do not dig. There is nowhere to dig to. Even the floor is paved. Soil for the pots - chili, onion tops, basil, a stunted fig kept small as a dog - is carried down in baskets. So is straw for the three brindled goats in the pole pen at the bottom. So is your shit, which goes *up* in sealed jars to the dry fields outside and comes back as onions.

Pack out everything. Bring a sealable dry bag for trash and toilet paper. Ask where the sealed jars are, use them, do not improvise. Bring electrolytes and anti-diarrheal meds just in case. The drip *tok-tok-tok* from a dozen spouts is the metronome of the place. Don't waste it.

### 3. Ears and voice: the shape is an ear

It is LOUD. A bucket clanked at the bottom arrives at the rim as a bell. Goats bleat doubled. Pigeon wings by the hundred - grey, white, rust - pour out of jar-lofts and wall holes like thrown gravel, circle the square of sky, funnel back. Swallows cut through them screaming all day, bats take over leather-quiet at dusk. Loom beaters, copper pot dropped, mother calling a name, mason tapping plaster with a hand-stone - it stacks and lingers.

Visitors raise their voices without meaning to, then shout to overcome themselves.

Do the opposite. Residents speak low and listen for the return. Practice speaking low before you go. Bring foam earplugs or soft silicone plugs if you are a light sleeper, but don't walk around plugged - you need to hear the flock thunder that means duck, the chant that means stand aside for a heavy jar coming up, the toads the color of wet mortar singing on warm evenings at the pan.

If you are asthmatic or sensitive: pigeon dust + straw dust + cooking smoke that cannot rise straight but hangs in a blue layer at the second terrace until a night draft pulls it up like a cork + lime dust. Bring inhaler, antihistamine, bandana. Evening lamp light is little clay saucers of oil in niches - beautiful, flickery, smoky. Don't add to it with a harsh white headlamp. Bring a dim warm headlamp with red mode, use it pointed down.

### 4. Animals own the vertical. You do not.

List, in order of authority: pigeons, swallows, bats, lizards on sunlit courses, rats on night courses, two lean temple-thin cats who patrol the fourth terrace and are paid in offal, the kestrel, the toads in the pan, mosquito larvae wriggling in every footprint puddle, and then you.

Do not touch lofts made from stacked jars with mouths knocked out. Do not block pigeon nets mended by old men in doorways that are really old water doors. Do not disturb the swallow nest frieze that follows the same horizontal crack all the way around the third terrace - built and rebuilt longer than anyone remembers.

Do not feed goats without asking - their straw is imported. Do not pick tended plants watered by the spoonful. Do not swat at bats. Watch where you put hands and feet: banded lizards for flies by day, rats for grain by night, toads exactly the color of mortar at the water's edge.

Health prep: mosquito repellent, long sleeves at dusk, a permethrin-treated headnet or bednet if you sleep low. Tetanus up to date - stone, rope grooves, copper awls gone green, bone needles. Hepatitis A / Typhoid precautions as you would for any place with shared shallow water. Rabies awareness for rats/cats, but do not pet the patrol cats on duty.

### 5. Social prep: every horizontal inch is occupied

Look at a wall and it is not a wall. Bottom: goat pen. Above: ledge with clay hearths, smoke tongues staining plaster. Above that: reed awning, loom clacking, sleeping niche just deep enough for a curled body with rolled mat and copper bowl. Across: rope bridges, pole scaffolds, laundry lines, gourd trellises.

There is no empty space to claim. No privacy as you know it. Near-touching, layered, always hands.

How to be a good guest:

**Bring useful, mendable things, not money.** Tools here are stone hammers, wooden mallets, green copper awls, bone needles, coiled rope, reed mats, clay. No wheel to fix. Best gifts: good coiled rope, lamp oil, reed mats, sturdy needles, chili / basil / onion seeds, a small bag of salt or sugar to share, fresh straw if you can carry it. Ask the pigeon-keepers and weavers what they need. Offer labor first: carrying, plaster patching, jar hauling to the chant.

**Ask before you duck under.** Don't enter an awning, sit in a doorway, photograph a sleeping niche, touch a loom, laundry, gourds, or pots. Old water doors are homes now. Sleeping niches are beds, not photo ops.

**Maintain.** The rule: nothing is new and nothing is ruined. Lime plaster bubbles and is patched and bubbles again. If you scuff it, chip it, stain it, you help fix it. A mason tapping with a hand-stone will show you how to listen for hollow plaster. Learn.

**The sweating stones:** On certain mornings condensation beads not randomly but in lines and whorls that outline blocked doors and filled channels you cannot otherwise see, as if the pit remembers its plumbing. The old women read weather in it - if the ghost-door on the east wall sweats complete before sunrise, rain within three days outside. They are usually right. Masons shrug: different mortar, different cold. Pigeon-keepers say birds go quiet an hour before.

Do not wipe it off to test it. Do not mock it. Do not lecture about capillary action. Watch quietly with everyone else. If the birds go quiet, get quiet.

Stay at least three days if you can. You can shout across it and be heard too well, and you can learn every face in a week. Let them learn yours. First day you are noise. Second day you learn to speak low. Third day someone trusts you with a jar.

Where to sleep: not in someone's niche unless explicitly offered. Usually under an edge awning on the upper terraces or back up on the dry rim where it's flat and less damp. Bring a closed-cell pad, a light quilt that tolerates damp, a mosquito net, earplugs. A copper bowl is useful for everything - bring a metal cup/bowl.

### Pack checklist for an ordinary human

Backpack, soft, 30-40L max, plus:

- 2-3L water + filter/tablets + electrolytes
- Sun hat, sunglasses, sunscreen, light long layers + warm damp-layer
- Grippy wet-capable footwear + spare socks in dry bag
- Headlamp warm/red + earplugs + mosquito net + repellent
- Bandana/mask, personal meds, inhaler if needed, diarrhea kit, small first aid for slips/blisters
- Metal cup/bowl, spoon, small knife, lighter
- Rope, lamp oil, needles, seeds as guest gifts
- Sealable bags for all trash - you carry up everything you carried down
- Pad + quilt for sleeping, no tent poles needed - you'll wedge, not pitch

Leave behind: hard luggage, white noisy clothes, Bluetooth speaker, drone, big camera tripod, expectations of solitude, soap/shampoo to use in the flow, anything you can't carry up stairs chanting.

If you do it right, your last memory will be evening: light going horizontal, cutting the pit in half - one side honey-gold, one side already indigo - swallows feeding frantically, toads starting, cooking smoke hanging blue at the second terrace, lamp saucers flickering on all that hand-plastered surface, drip *tok-tok* underneath it all. Lie on your belly at the rim and look over. Less a well than a room that dug just deep enough to catch the sky.
