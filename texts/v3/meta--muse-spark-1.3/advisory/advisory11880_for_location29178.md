This is old, flat, wet, warm country. Not hard to survive for a day, very easy to suffer in, and very easy to misunderstand.

Do not prepare for adventure. Prepare for saturation, disorientation, and slowness.

Here is what it will do to you, and what to bring to meet it on its terms.

### 1. Recalibrate: You have to learn to read it all over again

Your eyes are built for hills, edges, shadows, flow. There is none of that here.

The whole plain is only a breath above the water table. The "hills" are swells two or three feet higher than the hollows and broad as a town. You will not see them at first. You will learn them with your feet:

* **Swell:** pale and gritty, bleached sand with pea-gravel dark ironstone that rattles. Firmer. Drying silt on top where you might see wallaby tracks or rat trails.
* **Hollow:** black and greasy, peat and silt that sucks at your boots and doesn't let go cleanly. Shining sheets 6 inches to 2 feet deep, tea-colored over black mud, sky-colored over white sand.

There are no rivers because there is not enough slope for rivers to bother. Water sits and thinks about moving. Threads of flow through sedge too shallow to have banks will fool you — you can watch a leaf for a minute and not be sure it moved.

Light is another trick. Sun up behind high white haze, bright but shadowless. No hard shadows means no depth cues. Distance dissolves: land into water-light, water into sky. Tree clumps a mile away will shimmer and double themselves. You will misjudge depth of every pan until you probe it.

Do not try to conquer this. Plan to walk slowly, crouch often, and look sideways across water to see steam, flow, and micro-relief. If you rush, you will only see nothing has happened here. If you stay still 20 minutes, you'll see everything happened a long time ago and is still happening at the speed of rust and rot.

### 2. Your body in breath-warm air

It is not desert-hot. It is breath-warm, drinkable air, saturated. Your shirt will stick to your back in minutes. Condensation beads on every stem and falls with a tick. You will not cool by sweating because sweat has nowhere to go.

That means, paradoxically for a wetland:

* **Dehydration and heat exhaustion are your #1 risk.** You will lose salt and water while standing in water. Thirst sneaks up because everything *looks* wet.
* **Sunburn without shadows.** Haze does not block UV. There is almost no shade — paperbarks are pale, peeling, sparse, leaves held edge-on to the sun. You will burn on cloudy-bright days, on the underside of your chin from reflected sheets.
* **Feet will fail first.** Warm water, then a cool layer near the bottom, then flour-fine silt that puffs up around your ankles. Alternating gritty sand and sucking peat will blister, macerate, and try to steal your boots. Hours after rain the rust-red laterite nodules — those half-sunk eggs and loaves banded yellow and blood-red inside — will still be weeping water into a dark halo around them. The ground never dries.

* **Loud will tire you.** This place is unreasonably loud for how slowly it moves: three or four frog choruses layered — high metallic trill that never pauses, lower bottle-knock, long groaning bleat from deeper old meander scars — plus columns of midges and mosquitoes, hot-metal dragonflies, constant sawing cicadas rising and falling like heat, crabs plopping, silver fish dimpling, far gabble of ducks you can't see. Sleep without preparation is hard.

Prepare like this, head to toe:

**Clothes:** No cotton. Quick-dry long sleeves and long pants, loose, light-colored. You want sun and bite protection, not warmth. Bring a second set sealed dry — you will never dry anything in the field. Wide-brim hat with chin strap, polarized sunglasses (essential to cut sheet-glare and judge what's sand vs mud vs deep pan), buff for neck/ears against midges.

**Feet:** Boots that drain, not boots that waterproof. Waterproof boots will fill from the top in the first pan and stay as buckets. Use well-laced, closed-toe wading boots or trail shoes with aggressive drainage + neoprene socks or wool-blend socks + knee-high gaiters to keep silt and seed out and boots on when peat sucks. Lace tight. Bring 3x socks in dry bags, leukotape, antiseptic, and waterproof bandages for any cut. Any open wound in warm tannic water is an infection waiting to happen.

**Skin & bugs:** This is carnivorous-plant country — sundews and bladderworts — because the soil has been rained on for so long everything edible is washed out. That tells you: brutal for mosquitoes. Treat clothes with permethrin before you go. Bring picaridin or DEET, and a head net. The midge columns over water at dawn/dusk are maddening. Long sleeves tucked, pants tucked into socks. Sleep with a head net or tent with true no-see-um mesh.

**Water and food:** Bring all of it. Do not drink the pans, however clear and sky-colored. Warm, still, tannic, with pollen skin and insect castings, stratified warm-over-cool, anoxic silt below — it's full of bacteria, protozoa, leaching iron and organics. Even the weep-halos around stones are not clean. Carry 4-5 liters capacity per person per day, plus electrolytes. Sweat loss in humidity fools you; drink on schedule. Bring a filter + chemical backup or stove to boil. Food: high-salt, high-energy, no cooking that needs dry ground. You cannot forage here. Fish are small silver dimplers, birds are herons/egrets/ibis/harrier — look, don't eat.

**Pack:** Everything in dry bags inside your pack. Pack cover is not enough in sideways-building afternoon rain and constant condensation. Waterproof your sleeping kit, socks, electronics separately.

### 3. Moving and sleeping: nothing runs off

Rule: nothing runs off, everything soaks, settles, evaporates.

**Walking:** Bring a stout walking stick, chest-high. Use it always:
1. to probe depth — 6 inches of sky-mirror can hide 2 feet of flour-silt,
2. to tap for the iron hardpan a foot under the sand that rings hollow,
3. to stay upright when peat grabs one foot.

Step on sand between sedge tussocks, not on the tussocks themselves. Tussocks are harsh olive-yellow, spaced out, careful life — and the home of frogs, crabs, bladderworts. Bare wet sand recovers; crushed rhizomes don't. Walk the swells when you can. Avoid wading the year-round deeper pans with pollen skins unless you must — that's where you sink to the cool layer and where animals drink and hunt.

Walk slowly. Lift feet straight to break suction rather than twisting — ankles roll on buried laterite pans. Expect your own boot depressions to fill with brown water behind you within minutes after rain.

**Navigation:** Maps are almost useless. Pans migrate a few feet a year, eating one soft bank and filling the other with sedge. Everything looks alike. GPS with extra power bank in a dry bag, plus compass and a paper overview, plus a physical breadcrumb: bright flagging or a tall collapsible pole at your entry point on a swell. Without it you will not find your way back across two miles of mirror. Note the sun's hazy position early — later you lose it. Do not trust apparent movement of water to orient you.

**Weather timing:** Rain will come again this afternoon — you can smell the weight building behind the haze. Plan for it. Be off deep sheets and back on a pale swell by early afternoon. Afternoon storms in open flat water mean lightning, and you, wet to the knees in a sheet, are the tallest thing for a long way. If caught: get out of water, crouch low off the swell crest, away from lone paperbarks, wait. Then sun will take it back up. Frogs get louder. Nothing will look different, except wetter.

**Camp:** Do not camp in a hollow. Do not dig — you'll hit water in inches. Do not expect fire — fallen trees don't rot, they sink waterlogged into peat and become dark bars for decades, sprouting ferns. Peat may be wet on top, flammable below if dried, and will smolder. Bring a stove, bring a hammock with tarp or a tent with full bathtub floor and footprint, pitched on the grittiest swell you can find. Bring earplugs — you will want to hear the layered chorus, but you will also want to sleep.

### 4. Animals and respect

Nothing hurries here, including you if you want to see mammals. Stand motionless by a pan edge for 20-30 minutes: egrets and herons you mistook for stumps will spear, ibis will probe in lines, a harrier will quarter low, a rat may swim nose-up tussock to tussock. Don't chase. Flight is slow, deliberate in thick air.

Hazards are small and cumulative, not dramatic: leeches and biting flies in reeds where seepage is stronger, sharp broken laterite under silt, snakes using the same dry swells you want, sun-sickness, trench foot, infected blisters. Shake shoes, check under peeling bark before leaning, don't put fingers in crab holes in the mud edge.

And this landscape is fragile in slow motion. An ironstone nodule grows a millimeter a century. A dark bar log will be there for decades. Your tire tracks, your deep boot churn in black peat, will persist and fill and become part of the pan system. Travel light, single file on firm sand, no vehicles, no souvenir breaking of banded stones. Rinse boots and gear thoroughly before *and* after — between universes especially — to not carry seeds, spores, mud pathogens.

### 5. Emergency kit and mindset

Bring: stick, whistle, mirror or bright cloth for signaling (sound doesn't carry directionally in frog/insect roar), headlamp, extra dry socks, blister kit, rehydration salts, antihistamines, broad-spectrum antibiotic ointment, waterproof dressings, water purification, power bank, dry bags, tarp/poncho, insect net, sunblock and lip block.

If lost: stop, get to a swell, get visible. Don't wade at dusk trying to push through — water looks the same and deepens. Conserve phone. Listen — distant duck gabble means larger permanent water; cicada rise often means slightly drier trees/swell.

Mentally, prepare for emptiness as the main thing. For how wet it is, there is surprisingly little green. Each living thing has a lot of space around it. That spaciousness can feel exposing, boring, then suddenly intimate when you learn to see halos around weeping stones, pollen skins, wallaby prints already filling with seep.

If you do it right, you'll know: you'll start distinguishing trills from knocks from bleats, sand-ring from peat-suck underfoot, tea from sky water, and you'll know without looking that rain is an hour away. You won't have conquered anything. You'll just be exactly as wet as before, and for a moment, reading correctly.
