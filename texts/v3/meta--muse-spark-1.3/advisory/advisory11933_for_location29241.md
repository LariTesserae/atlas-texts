You don't hike to this place. You squeeze into it. Prepare like a caver who has been invited to dinner in someone's living room, and that living room is a drum made of wet rock.

This is not a ruin to explore. It's a full, occupied, balanced town of under a hundred people living shoulder-to-shoulder in a single egg-shaped bell chamber, maybe 60 paces crown-to-water, a third full of black still water. Everything is close, damp, loud, dim, and deliberately unchanged. You are not there to survive it. You are there to not disturb it.

### 1. Decide if you should go at all

Don't go if you are:

* large-framed, stiff-jointed, claustrophobic, or a weak climber. The entrance is a wellhead-wide collapsed throat, 10 meters of scalloped limestone with a kink that forces you onto your stomach. You will go down on your backside, braced, then belly-crawl, then pop out over a drop. If you get stuck, there is no rescue team. The town has rope and hands, not a cave rescue rig.
* prone to respiratory trouble, chronic ear infections, or panic in the dark. The air is saturated, smoky at head-height, and deafening with drip-noise. There is no doctor down there.
* unwilling to be dirty, wet, touched, crowded, and watched for hours.

A healthy traveler limits this to a day-visit of a few hours. Do not plan to sleep over unless you are explicitly invited to a specific pallet, and even then understand what that means: crowded sleep, tallow smoke, drip-roar all night.

### 2. The drop in — train for it on the surface first

From above there is nothing: moor, wind, heather, sheep, a hole you could step over. Mark it or you will lose it. Leave a surface anchor, a companion if you have one, or at minimum a cairn, GPS pin, and a tell-tale line.

You need real caving basics:
**Helmet with a chin strap, headlamp + 2 backups, knee pads and elbow pads and gloves.** The limestone is smooth as poured wax but slick with seep and solution cups. Corners have been licked round, which means no handholds.
**Slim pack, or better a drag bag.** You will not fit with a big multiverse rucksack. Repack to a small dry-bagged kit you can push ahead of you through the kink.
**30m static rope, harness, descender/ascenders, and the skill to use them - plus the long town rope already in place.** The throat is only ~10m but it's kinked, wet, and you have to climb back out when you are cold, tired, and damp. Practice a wet limestone chimney climb before you come.
**Grippy, soft-sole boots, not heavy lug boots.** Think approach shoes or well-drained caving boots. Everything below is wet slab, corbelled dry-stone, ladders of bone and salvaged poles black with grease, and six-inch ledges with rope handrails over black water. You'll be sidling single file.
**Wear synthetics or wool. No cotton. No down.** Your jacket will darken at the shoulders in minutes. Your hair will cling. Your clothes will never dry inside - prints you leave on rock fill back in with water in seconds. Bring a full dry change sealed in a roll-top dry bag left at the surface, plus one more sealed inside for after. Hypothermia here isn't from bitter cold. It's the steady autumn-shade cool - rock = water = skin - that gets into joints and stays. Bring a windproof shell for the moor and a waterproof shell for inside.

Check the weather. The throat smells of rain that fell a month ago. That means water lingers and moves slowly. In a real storm that wellhead becomes a drain. Never drop in if heavy rain is forecast.

### 3. What it feels like inside, so you aren't blindsided

**Wet:** It doesn't flow, it weeps. Walls sweat from a thousand pores. Overhangs fatten and drop. Verticals crawl sideways into threads that follow old flutes to the pool. Your breath makes a cloud that doesn't dissipate. There is no dry. Only drier.

**Dim:** Always late-evening. A bruised grey disc of daylight from the throat, plus a general sourceless gloaming at the crown. Shapes at ten paces, faces at five. Every house keeps a cup of light - tallow in a stone bowl with a hair or hide wick - guttering, tended constantly, making more smoke than light.

**Smoky in a layer:** Smoke has nowhere to go. It hangs blue-grey about head-height over the water. Learn fast: stoop under it or stand above it. Bring simple eye protection and a buff/N95 you can pull up without insulting anyone. You'll still sting.

**Brutally loud:** The dome is a drum. Hundreds of drips a minute each land like a knuckle on a table and don't sync. Footsteps, coughs, a bowl set down, your own boot-scrape - all go up, around, and come back half a second later slightly larger. At full work hours it's hammering, splashing, low talk bounced into roar. Newcomers cover their ears. Locals shout a little and lip-read a lot.
Bring foam earplugs for relief, but don't wear them when talking to people - you'll need to watch mouths, get close, speak low and clearly. Don't shout across the egg.

**Crowded past comfort:** Three uneven galleries ring the egg, completed with mortarless dry-stone, corbelled out over the void. One or two-room houses shoulder-to-shoulder, roof of one = floor of next. Ladders, hide-fiber bridges over the narrowest water, pile platforms over the pool for work and gutting. You cannot stretch your arms on the middle terrace without hitting two lintels and a net. Pots, bundles, drying lines, copper scraps, stone bowls, pallets, water jars layered, hung, tucked.

Move light and single. Test ladders and platforms one person at a time. Keep your hands in or trailing on the handrail ropes. Uphill presses flat, downhill sidles, water-carriers always have right of way - memorize that like a law. Never step over a jar, a pallet, a threshold without asking. Never lean on a corbelled wall.

### 4. Water, food, body

The bottom third is black water, still in a way that seems practiced. Drips make a dimple and a swallowed tick, then flatness. High calcite bathtub ring a handspan above, dark stain a handspan below, but it sits exactly here. Seep = drink = evaporation. They dip a marked stick each dawn and announce: still even.

That stillness is theology and survival.

* **Do not touch the pool unless invited.** Do not swim, wash, rinse, spit, drip sunscreen, or urinate in it or near it. Do not throw a pebble to hear it. Bring your own drinking water and all food. Bring a Wag Bag / pee bottle and carry everything out. Ask quietly where they relieve themselves and follow exactly - do not improvise.
* **Do not drink from seeps at random.** The children know which seep fills a jar by nightfall and which never will. You don't. Even the pale translucent slime in dim corners - stone-breath life, not plant, not animal - and rust-red iron-bacteria films on submerged ledges mean this water is not your water. Bring 2 liters plus purification as emergency only, and decontaminate boots and gear before and after to avoid introducing algae, seeds, fungal spores. There is no green here - not moss, not algae where light touches. Keep it that way. Brush, wash, dry your gear beforehand. No rats, no birds go down. Don't be the rat.
* **Don't expect to be fed, and don't eat their fish.** The census is tiny: 2-inch blind white fish with eye-pits, candle-wax crabs that pick at night, impossible leggy pale crickets. They eat fish sparingly, more as rite than sustenance. Most food - grain, dried meat - comes down in sacks on the long rope, traded for worked stone, native copper, and white salt that blooms in upper cups. Bring calorie-dense food you can eat silently with dirty hands. Offer to share only if appropriate, and accept refusal gracefully.

Health kit: blister care, antihistamine, personal meds, small first aid, spare socks in a dry bag, anti-fungal powder, ear drops to dry ears after. Trench foot is real if you linger in wet boots. Smoke + damp + crowding = cough. If you are sick with even a cold, do not go down. You could sweep that egg.

Sanitation-wise, behave like you're visiting an ICU well: clean hands before touching shared things, don't touch faces, don't handle their tools, hooks, bowls, nets, hide frames unless handed to you.

### 5. Light and fire etiquette

Leave the powerful caving lamp in your pocket. Use a dim headlamp on low, preferably warm and/or red mode, pointed down. Never sweep faces, pallets, or work with white high-beam. Their tallow dips gutter in damp and must be tended constantly - don't blow them out with movement, don't add your own open flame.

**No fire. No stove. No cigarettes down there.** A fat fire in close quarters is brief and terrible, and they have no fuel for a kiln - pots are carved from soft stone or pieced hide over withies brought down coil by coil at great cost. Pottery, bone poles, drift lengths, hide cordage, hair and sinew - everything is small, mended, mended again. A bowl will have three generations of laced drill-holes along a crack. Treat every object like a museum piece that is still in daily use, because it is.

### 6. Craft, trade, and manners

You hear hand-work before you see it: chert knapping by pressure with antler tine, tick tick like glass; a woman cold-hammering dull pink native copper blebs into hooks and awls, tap tap, no forge; hide scraping, cordage twisting from hair, sinew, pale fibers dredged from the pool.

They are not self-sufficient and not helpless. They trade down/up via sacks on the long rope.

Good to bring as respectful gifts to offer through whoever seems to steward trade, not handed out randomly:
grain, dried meat, hard cheese, tallow or clean rendered fat, wicking material, sturdy cordage, needles, salt-free? No - they have their own white salt. Don't undercut it.
What they value most is what costs them most to bring down: fuel, fiber, withies, leather, ceramic? Ask what is needed, accept no.

Do not:
haggle hard, take ancestor bone, chert, copper work without clear trade, photograph without explicit permission, sketch addresses/ledges like a surveyor, move stones to “help,” or suggest improvements. A slumped wall rebuilt stone-for-stone by morning same shape same lean. A burned house rebuilt on same footprint with salvaged lintel, same hook left of door. Nothing changes - not from death, from perfect maintenance. Innovation reads as insult or threat.

Social rules to internalize:
Speak close, not loud. Learn a few gestures for excuse me, thank you, may I pass, beautiful work.
Don't step on thresholds, pallets, or low steps without looking - you may be stepping on an ancestor. The dead are laid in high niches wrapped in hide, later emptied, bones washed in pool water and literally built into windbreaks and steps. There is nowhere else to put them. Do not react with horror. Do not touch niches or bonework.
Don't ask to be taken to “see the sights.” Streets are six inches wide. Your sightseeing is someone's hallway and bedroom wall.
Don't pick at the honey calcite, black chert nodules, rust films, or salt blooms. That salt and stone is their currency and calendar.
Children learn dips and flutes like streets. Follow a child’s instruction about water if offered - it's pride, not servility.

### 7. Mind preparation

You will feel it in your teeth before you can name it: perfect accounting. In equals out. Your multiverse instinct - that everything branches, changes, can be optimized - will itch here. Let it go.

Time collapses. No midday, only a paling of the grey disc. No summer, no green, no birdsong, only drip-patter. No privacy. Neighbors hear each other swallow. Lamplight catches a thousand wet facets and the whole inner egg glitters faintly, like the inside of a mouth. The smell - wet rock and tallow and crowded sleep - will stay in your nose for days. Your ears will ring after you climb out.

Prepare for:
sensory fatigue and claustrophobic euphoria alternating. Plan deliberate stillness: sit on a pile platform, let lamp smoke thin, watch blind fish hang then dart. Don't chase them.
the urge to fix the smoke, the noise, the damp. You can't. The smoke never thickens, never clears. The drips never stop but never storm.
grief on exit. From above it's just wind and heather and a hole you could step over. Below your feet a complete urban world humming in its bubble, refusing - gently, absolutely - to become anything else. You will want to tell everyone. Don't give coordinates loosely. This place survives by being hard to find and easy to overlook.

### Packing checklist for the ordinary human

**On body:** helmet, low-lumen warm headlamp, thermal base wool, mid fleece, waterproof shell top and pants, grippy approach shoes, gloves, kneepads, buff, earplugs in pocket, whistle on chest (know it will be half-drowned).

**In small dry bag (pushable):** 2 backup lights + batteries, 2L water, 1000+ kcal dense food, water filter tablets, tiny first aid + blister + meds, spare socks + base layer, N95 + eye drops, Wag Bag + ziplocks for all trash, 10m cord, knife, watch, notebook/pencil.

**At surface cache:** full dry change, towel, warm drink in thermos, rope kit, tarp, marker cairn, message with return time.

**Left behind:** cotton, down, heavy pack, Bluetooth speaker, bright white strobes, incense, drones, pets, seeds, expectations of a tour.

Go down on your backside, boots braced. Come up slower. When the old women dip the stick at dawn and say still even, nod like you understand that this is not a measurement. It's a prayer that worked.
