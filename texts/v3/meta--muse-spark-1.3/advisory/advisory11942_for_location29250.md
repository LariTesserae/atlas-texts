This is not a dive. It's a mountain hike that happens to be underwater.

Think of it that way and you'll prepare right. A high, sunlit tableland, town-sized, flat on top, with cliffs falling thousands of meters on all sides into cold black abyss. The top sits only 10-20 meters below the air. You will be swimming in bathwater, in daylight, through a forest so thick you have to push it aside. You can at any moment kick up five strokes and put your hand out into rain.

That inverts every instinct you have about the sea. Don't fight it. Prepare for a shallow, warm, bright, sharp, living maze with no shore, no help, and no bottom except knives.

Here's how an ordinary human body gets through it alive and happy:

### 1. Understand what your body is walking into

**Warm, thick, still water.** 27-28C, day and night. That's blood-warm. You won't get cold. You will get overheated, pruney, sleepy, and dehydrated without noticing because you're wet. It doesn't flush. Plankton and marine snow hang in it instead of falling. It feels like thin broth. It saps momentum. You won't glide. You'll pull.

**Shallow but not wadeable.** Most of the plateau is 10-20m down from the surface to the rock, with kelp ribbons 10-12m tall kissing the surface from below. That means you live in the middle layer. Light is full tropical noon, dappled green-gold through leaves like a forest floor. You will get sunburned on the back of your neck *while* you are 10m underwater. That's normal here.

**Young rock.** This place erupted a century or two ago. There is no sand. It's all fresh basalt: pillow lava, split tubes, standing chimneys, ropey pahoehoe swirls still shiny with glass. Edges like broken bottles. A dusting of crushed shell in hollows, otherwise nothing soft to kneel, sit, or land on. Barnacled white only at the rim where deep current gnaws. Inside, every hard surface is furred — pink crust, green velvet, white hydroids like frost, yellow/orange/violet sponges grown out of the chimneys until passages are sideways-only.

You will touch at least three living things every time you extend your arm. Plan for that.

**A ceiling, not a sky.** The surface from below is a bright sheet. On rainy days — daily, warm, sudden, 10 minutes of hammering — you see thousands of dimples at once, the top meter goes fresh, cooler, full of driven-down bubbles. At noon the kelp traps them and the whole forest is silvered with mercury edges. At night they wobble loose and rise too slowly.

Tide is not waves. There are almost no waves down here. There's a 6-hour in, 6-hour out breath that leans the whole meadow outward as water reluctantly spills off the rim. Your depth gauge will move a hand's breadth and you'll hear a sigh. That's it.

Sound is swallowed by leaf. Your breath first, then creak of stipes rubbing, far-off ticking of snapping shrimp, soft rain of particles on leaves. Nothing else.

Animals are rare by design. Plants make, almost nothing takes. Swim an hour and see nothing. Then one very old green turtle cropping seagrass and ignoring you. A single cloud of silver juveniles flickering through a corridor and gone. Urchins — black, long-spined — holding algae like parasols, mowing neat circles. Anemones opening over a day. A thumb-long nudibranch crossing a sponge all afternoon. Sharks don't hunt up here, it's too warm and leaf-choked. Traces instead: a bite out of a bladder, a drilled shell, turtle droppings dissolving into green clouds.

### 2. How to breathe and stay

You cannot live here without help. Bring it.

**For most travelers: snorkel, don't scuba, first.** This is ideal snorkel / freedive country because it's so shallow and bright. You can hang at 3-5m in the canopy, drop to 12-15m to cross a meadow, and pop up anytime for orientation. Scuba gives you an hour+ of bottom time at this depth, which is wonderful for the west jungle where the canopy interweaves into permanent twilight, but it makes you bulky in a place where you have to turn sideways through sponge chimneys, and it increases entanglement risk.

If you scuba: this is a recreational 10-20m profile. Long no-deco times, but watch repetitive dives and buoyancy like a hawk. You cannot touch down to rest without cutting yourself. You must hover over knives.

If you freedive: never alone, never hyperventilate, surface interval on the boat not floating in the sun. The warmth makes you feel you can stay down forever. You can't.

In either case: a surface watch in a boat is non-negotiable. There is no land to crawl onto except, in the south, a fin of lava that kisses air for about an hour at low tide — black, steaming faintly, covered in stranded bladders that pop as they dry. Do not plan to rest there. It's sharp, slippery, hot, and temporary.

**Sun and water loss:** Bring more drinking water than you think for a water trip. You sweat into warm water and sun cooks you from above and reflected from below. Drink before and after every swim. Reef-safe sunscreen will wash off and hurt this place; wear a full rashguard with neck flap, leggings, and a hood instead. You want your back covered while face-down for an hour.

### 3. How to not get cut, stung, stuck, or lost

This place injures the careless slowly, then infects them quickly.

**Dress for knives, not cold.** Water is bath-warm, but wear full coverage:
- 1-2mm full suit or tough skin suit + rashguard. Not for warmth, for armor against glassy basalt, hydroids, sponge spicules.
- Thick-soled booties with hard toes. Gloves — dexterous but cut-proof. Hood or cap to keep floating lace-algae and larvae out of ears/scalp.
- Short, stiff fins or even bare booties in the thickest jungle. Long freedive fins become levers that tangle and break fronds.
- Mask with good peripheral vision, plus defog. The water is full of life that wants to film your lens.
- Two cutting tools, on different places on your body, that you can open one-handed. You *will* get wrapped in kelp-like ribbons and holdfasts the size of fists. Practice the kelp crawl: stop, tuck, slow spin, cut only what holds you, never hack a corridor.

**Urchins, hydroids, anemones:** Don't put hands or knees down. The urchin circles look neat and inviting — those spines are long, black, brittle, and warm seawater infects punctures fast. White frost-like hydroids sting. Sponges are abrasive. Look, don't lean.

**Cold seeps:** In the north block-valley — a mile-long graben of house-sized black blocks, tumbled and unworn — there are slightly cooler, sulfurous shimmers with white bacterial mats. Pretty. Don't linger, don't drink, don't put your face in it. No plants live there yet for a reason.

**The rim is death.** Inland is a meadow and jungle. The edge is a clean break cliff falling straight into blue-black thousands of meters down. The deep current along it is silent until you put your hand in and feel cold shear pull. It's muffled; you won't hear it coming. If the tidal breath starts spilling outward and the whole meadow leans out, get low, get inland, hold a holdfast — don't grab a bladder, it will tear. Never swim out past the white barnacled lip to look down unless you are tethered, with boat eyes on you, with an inflated surface marker and plenty of air. Vertigo out there is violent because your brain thinks "sunlit meadow" and your eyes suddenly get "abyss." People freeze or get swept. Stay 20-30m inside the lip unless your exit plan is solid.

If you are swept off: stop fighting outward, inflate, get big at the surface, conserve air, signal. You cannot swim back against that shear once you're in deep cold water.

**Navigation:** You will get lost. The east meadow is a mile+ of unbroken wrist-wide, taller-than-you seagrass rippling. The west is true jungle, canopy so interwoven the bottom is in twilight and sponges glow faintly. Corridors close behind you. Particles hang, so you leave no trail.

Bring and use:
- Compass on wrist, dive computer, depth gauge.
- A reel and line if you enter the jungle or chimneys. Tie off at the entry clearing.
- A tall surface marker buoy / sausage you can deploy from below to show the boat where you are under the canopy. The boat won't see your bubbles for the leaf and trapped air.
- Buddy system with touch contact in thick growth, light signals in twilight. Agree on hand/tug signals beforehand — you won't hear shouting.
- A small powerful light even for day dives. The west bottom needs it, and chimney interiors do.

Swim slow. Slow wins here. Growth is millimeters a month, a holdfast keeps a crack for decades. If you thrash, you cut yourself, break decade-old growth, silt nothing — there is no silt — but stir larvae and lose visibility. Hover, pull hand-over-hand on rock only where it's already bare, fin gently.

### 4. What to bring on the boat

There is no town, no ruin, no rope, no bottle cap, no anchor. Nothing made. No rescue.

- Stable boat / liveaboard you can live from for days. You cannot anchor into glass without destroying life and dragging; use a mooring buoy you set on a bare block, or drift with a tender following divers.
- Shade, water, electrolytes, food. No potable water here except brief rain freshwater lens on top — not drinkable in quantity, stings eyes.
- VHF, satellite messenger / PLB, GPS, first aid with serious wound irrigation, disinfectant, tweezers for urchin spines, vinegar for stings, sterile dressings. Warm water + cut = infection in hours. Irrigate immediately on board, disinfect, cover, watch.
- Spare masks, snorkels, fin straps, O-rings, line, cutting tools. Everything decontaminated before and after — this ecosystem is young, isolated, and naive. Rinse all gear in fresh water, dry fully, remove all larvae, algae fragments, and that green snow. As a multiverse traveler, do not carry life between worlds. Do not take rock, blades, bladders, shells. If you cut a blade it bleeds green and seals by evening, but don't test it. If you break rock the break glitters clean and films over in a week — leave it unbroken.
- Warm rain gear for the surface. Rain is immense and warm but blinds the boat driver and turns the surface to white noise for ten minutes. Plan to pause entries/exits then.

### 5. How to be there well

Go in the morning on a calm day first. Enter over the east meadows where it's open. Just hang face-down at 3m and learn the breath. Watch caustic nets move across black and green. Hold still until lace-algae balls drift into you and larvae snow on your arms.

Midday: go to the canopy to see the mercury edging when bubbles collect. That's noon silver.

Dusk: stay shallow and watch it go amber horizontal. Every bladder becomes a lantern, every grass blade edged in fire. Moonlit nights do it in silver, with green phosphorescence trailing you for a minute when you move. Night dives here are gentle and hallucinatorily beautiful, but only with a guide line, lights, and boat lights on — no complete darkness, but easy disorientation.

Different quarters:
- **East:** plain, breathing, good for long slow swims, turtle crossings.
- **West:** jungle, twilight bottom, glowing sponges, don't go deep inside without a line.
- **North:** chimneys and block-valley, bare black, bacterial mats, shimmering seeps. Stark, mineral, for looking not touching.
- **South:** rim shoals up, fin breaches at low tide. Fascinating but sharpest, most surge on the turn. Watch popping bladders underfoot.

Stay still long enough and the forest forgets you. Anemones will open. Shrimp will tick closer. A turtle may pass within arm's reach. That's the reward for slowness.

### 6. Mind preparation

You will feel elevation invert. You'll stop thinking "I'm deep" and start thinking "I'm high, in a mountain meadow, only the air is warm sea." Clouds visible through water, close enough to touch but unreachable. Lift your fingers into cold rain, drop them back into warm green and be home.

That feeling is wonderful and dangerous. It makes you forget you need air, forget the abyss is a fin-kick away, forget your skin is soft and the rock is glass.

Checklist to tape to your tank:

Slow. Covered. Connected. Buoyant. Hydrated. Inland.

Slow — move like tide, not like swimmer.
Covered — no bare skin, feet, hands.
Connected — buddy, line, boat eyes.
Buoyant — never stand, never kneel, hover.
Hydrated — drink, shade, limit each immersion to 60-90 minutes, rest.
Inland — respect the rim, fear the shear, love the middle acres.

Leave with nothing but green phosphorescence fading behind you, and rinse it off too.
