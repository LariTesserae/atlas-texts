You are not going somewhere that wants to kill you. You are going somewhere that has already learned to live without you, on concrete.

Treat it like a tideflat that happens to have warehouses on it. Everything there follows three rules, and you should too: **stay out of midday heat, drink from the sky not the ground, don't waste movement.**

This is how to do that as an ordinary, soft, sweaty human.

### 1. Understand what this place is

Low, flat, a degree or two above sea and marsh. Water doesn't need to fall to be here, it seeps up. It rains hard in the morning, hard at night, and weeps and steams all day in between.

No services. No people. No stores, no taps, no help, no shade except sheet metal and concrete. No green at all — the soil is soured with oil and brine and slag and old herbicide and spring brackish floods. The puddles have an oil rainbow on them. The ground off-pavement is black ankle-deep mud that smells of diesel, rot, and salt.

The sky is huge, low, hot-milk white-grey-yellow. The sun is a dull coin. Light stays dim like an hour after a downpour, but the heat does not lift. Concrete gives it back. Puddles give it back as steam. Metal burns to touch.

It's quiet at first, then it's not. Fiddler and shore crabs by the thousands in the shallows. Gulls and white egrets eating them. Brown rats in runways along walls and pipes. Hundreds of pigeons per open warehouse, whitewash droppings on the floor, dust in the air. Thin feral cats, a pack of three lean dogs, columns of mosquitoes/midges/flies over anything still, cockroaches under any board, mussels and barnacles on the canal wall, motionless herons, two or three kestrels hovering over the hot lots.

Nothing is fed by people anymore. Crabs eat droppings and drowned midges. Gulls eat crabs. Rats eat fallen pigeon eggs and mummified garbage. Cats and dogs eat rats and pigeons. Flies eat the dead. That's the whole web, built on rainwater and leftovers and tide.

You will be the largest, slowest, smelliest animal there, and the least adapted.

### 2. What will actually hurt you

Not a monster. Attrition:

**Heat + humidity.** Wet heat that sticks your shirt to you in minutes. Heat exhaustion comes fast because there's no breeze, no shade that isn't a hot roof, and you sweat without cooling. Midday is for lying still. If you push through it, you will get stupid, then sick.

**Water everywhere, none drinkable.** Every rut, pothole, laydown yard, parking lot is a shallow lake reflecting the sky. It's brackish, oily, full of bird droppings, rat urine, rust, algae, dead insects. Do not drink it, do not wash dishes in it, do not get it in your eyes, mouth, or open cuts. Leptospirosis, gastrointestinal hell, skin infections live here.

**Feet and skin.** Step off pavement and you sink. Standing water hides potholes, rebar, glass, wire, rail sleepers, softened asphalt. Rust scale is everywhere. A small cut + foul water = a big problem in 12 hours. Trench foot is real if you stay wet for a day.

**Air.** Damp, hot, with diesel, sulfur/salt marsh exhale from beyond the last shed, mold, and inside every warehouse, powdered pigeon droppings. You will feel it in your lungs before you smell it. Histoplasmosis and plain allergic misery.

**Bugs.** Down near the ground the air shivers. Mosquitoes, midges, biting flies are constant over stagnant water and carrion. Expect to be found in seconds if you stand still. Assume mosquito-borne disease is possible.

**Animals.** None hunt you, but all defend themselves. Rat runways will cross your boots — don't corner them in culverts. Feral dogs are lean, heat-stressed, working as a pack; they will scatter rats and then lose interest, but don't approach, don't feed, don't turn your back on food-guarding. Cats will vanish unless cornered. Nesting gulls/egrets/herons jab. Pigeon dust rains down when they shuffle. Cockroaches boil out if you lift a pallet without thinking.

**Metal and buildings.** Hollows, not ruins-collapsing, but weathered: rounded corners, chalked paint — BAY 7 — NO PARKING — KEEP CLEAR — rebar like bone, doors gone, dark empty volume with oil stains and rotting pallets. Roofs hiss with evaporation. Gutters pour long after rain. Awning drip is constant. Inside is dark even at noon. Floors are slick with droppings and oil. One pump to the south still runs on automatic, put-put-put, moving floodwater ditch to ditch. That's probably the only live machinery — give ditches, culverts, and that sound a wide berth.

### 3. What to wear — cover everything, stay ventilated

Think fisherman in a car factory, not hiker:

* **Feet:** Knee-high, steel-shank rubber muck boots, already broken in. Not hiking boots — they will drown and stay drowned. Not sandals, ever. Thick synthetic socks, plus 2-3 dry pairs in sealed dry bags. Change the second your feet prune. Gaiters help keep crabs and mud out.
* **Legs and torso:** Light-colored, loose, quick-dry long pants and long-sleeve shirt. You want sun, rust, droppings, and insects off your skin but sweat able to escape. Treat clothes with permethrin before you go, if your universe allows it.
* **Rain:** Lightweight breathable rain jacket + rain pants, and they *will* be hot — you wear them in bursts for the downpours, then vent. A wide-brim waterproof hat, not a baseball cap.
* **Hands and face:** Thin work gloves for lifting boards, opening doors, touching anything metal. Sunglasses for glare off water even in dimness. Mosquito head net in a pocket. Bandana/buff to pull over nose in warehouses.
* **Breathing:** N95 or P100 half-mask respirator, at least for entering any warehouse, shed, culvert, or dumpster area. You’ll thank yourself when 500 pigeons shift at once and dust sifts down.
* **Skin:** DEET or picaridin on exposed skin, reapplied because sweat washes it. Sunscreen underneath — UV comes through that milk sky. Lip balm. No perfume, no scented lotion. You already smell enough to interest flies.

Don't touch bare metal in the heat of day. Test with back of hand. Sheet walls, rails, tank sides can burn.

### 4. What to bring — everything dry, everything sealed

There is nothing to buy, beg, or borrow. Pack to be self-sufficient and to stay dry:

**Water and food:** 3-4 liters of clean water per person per day, plus electrolytes. Do not plan to filter ground water — oil and brine and droppings defeat a hiker filter. Bring a filter *plus* a clean tarp / poncho / collapsible bucket to catch direct rain. Drink from the sky, literally: set out during the rain, let the first minute wash the tarp, then collect. Food: salty, high-energy, sealed, no cooking needed. Eat quickly, pack out every crumb and wrapper. Do not feed anything. Do not eat local crabs, mussels, birds — they are steeped in oil and heavy metals. Bring hand sanitizer and use it before you touch your face.

**Staying dry:** Dry bags inside your pack, not just a pack cover. Headlamp + spare batteries — warehouse interiors are black at noon. Trekking pole or stout stick to probe puddles before you step. A small sit pad or folded tarp so you never have to sit directly on concrete/mud.

**Health kit:** Wound wash, iodine/povidone, bandages, blister kit, antibiotic ointment, antihistamines, painkiller, anti-diarrheal, rehydration salts. Assume any cut that touched ground water is contaminated — wash immediately with *your* clean water, disinfect, cover, keep dry. Check tetanus is up to date before you go. If you have open sores, eczema, recent surgery, bad asthma — don't go.

**Bugs and sleep:** Strong repellent, after-bite, and if you plan to be still for more than minutes, a head net and a fully enclosed mosquito net/bivy. Do not sleep on the ground. If you must overnight: elevated cot or hammock, under a roof that is not a pigeon roost, away from culverts and canal edge that flood at night rain. Sweep for roaches, check for rats runs.

**Navigation:** Compass + GPS + paper map + power bank in a dry bag. You can see half a mile down a service road and still get lost — everything looks the same in haze, and landmarks disappear in steam and rain. Mark your entry. Use that southern pump’s put-put-put as a south bearing, but don’t rely on sound alone in rain. Chalk or tape to mark warehouse doors you’ve cleared, so you don’t re-clear them.

Tell someone where you went and when you’ll check back. No backup beepers, no engines — if you twist an ankle in a hidden rut under six inches of water, you will be lying in hot rain with rats and mosquitoes until you get yourself out.

### 5. How to move through it

**Time it:** Go early morning after the morning rain has eased, or late afternoon. Plan to be still and shaded during the worst heat. Expect another hard rain at night — be under solid cover or out before it. Don’t waste movement in the heat. Walk slow, pause often, like the dogs and gulls do.

**Stay on pavement/concrete as much as possible.** Test every puddle with your stick — what looks like 2 inches can be a pothole to the knee with rebar in it. Walk rail spurs *beside* them, not on sodden sleepers. Never jump ditches; walk around. Near the canal, assume the bank is undercut and saturated.

**Enter buildings like they’re occupied — because they are.** Stop at the door. Listen for cooing, shuffling, rustling. Shine your headlamp low first. Don’t shout, don’t bang pallets. If pigeons explode upward, step back out, let dust settle, mask up before re-entering. Watch the floor: whitewash = slippery + airborne. Oil stain = slippery. Don’t climb pallets — they’re rotted. Don’t pull wire. Don’t touch breaker boxes or the cold power station stacks; cold does not mean dead.

**Be boring to animals.** Give dogs 30+ meters, don’t stare, don’t offer food, back away sideways if they stiffen. Give herons, egrets, nesting gulls space — a jab can take an eye. Walk *around* crab masses, not through them; you’ll slip and you’ll crush and you’ll attract gulls to your feet. Step over rat runways, don’t block culvert mouths. If you lift a board, lift away from you with gloves, expect roaches, set it back.

**Be quiet and still on purpose.** The place fools you with drips — plink from awning to puddle, hiss off hot roofs, your own breathing loud. If you stand still long enough in shade, you become part of the waiting. Crabs resume side-stepping, gulls go back to one leg, pigeons tuck heads. That’s when you actually see it. Steam rises in ribbons off the road and is gone. Far off beyond the last shed, the marsh exhales salt and sulfur and warm mud.

That is the reward, and the warning: it is easy to get hypnotized and stay too long in heat.

### 6. Mental prep

It will feel empty and crowded at once. Too much sky, too much air for the low buildings, no one to talk to, and then movement everywhere at ankle height. No weeds, no grass, no trees — just mineral and rust — so your brain will keep looking for green and not find it. The smell — diesel and rot and salt, pigeon loft and hot concrete — gets into clothes and stays.

Bring patience. Bring a willingness to not be important. You are not there to clean it, fix it, feed it, or take souvenirs. The letters are already ghosts. The water is already fouled. The web works without green and without you.

Go in, drink rain, watch the tide of small lives trade places on concrete, get out before you’re heat-sick, foot-rotted, or bitten raw. Wash everything you wore, air everything you carried, check your feet, check your cuts.

If you do it right, you’ll leave with damp socks, chalk dust on your boots, rust-water on your gloves, and the faint click of thousands of claws in your ears long after the haze closes behind you.
