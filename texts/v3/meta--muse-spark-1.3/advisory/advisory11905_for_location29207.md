This is not a climb. It's a roof-walk. And it's wet.

Go in expecting a desert of white stone or an alpine rock, and you will be miserable in an hour. Go in expecting a cold, high, living bog that happens to have paved itself in limestone, and you'll do fine.

Think of it as: Pamukkale the size of a country, moved to the Tibetan Plateau, with the plumbing left on and the people removed.

Here's how to come ready as an ordinary, oxygen-breathing human with no armor, no gills, no super-healing.

### 1. Understand what your body is walking into

**High, thin, cool, breathable air.**
You can breathe it, but there isn't much of it. Breath shows even at noon. A flight of 15 feet up a master divide will wind you. Holding your breath to listen to the quiet will make you lightheaded.

Acclimatize before you step onto the roof. Spend 2-3 nights at intermediate altitude if you can. Ascend slow, drink more water than you think, no alcohol the night before. Learn the signs of acute mountain sickness - headache, nausea, clumsiness, stupid decisions - and have a plan to descend and get off the plateau if they appear. Don't go alone.

The sun is normal, white, high, honest. Not glaring, not dim. That lies. At this height UV is brutal even when you don't squint. You will burn on an overcast day off ten thousand mirrors.

Bring: SPF 30+ sunscreen, lip balm, glacier-grade sunglasses, a brim hat. Light-colored but covering clothes.

**Cold + wet = the real danger, not falling.**
Daytime feels like a cool morning that never warms up. Evening drops only a degree or two in a way you feel in your lungs first - then suddenly water is skinning with ice while still moving underneath.

There is no dry ground. The water table is right under a skin of peat and lime. Every footstep sighs and fills from below with clear, ice-cold seep. Brush sedge for ten minutes and you're soaked to the knee. Boots cake with wet marl until they weigh twice as much.

For an ordinary human this is trench-foot, blister, and hypothermia country. Prepare for constant wetness, not occasional puddles:

- Knee-high waterproof bog boots with aggressive, soft-rubber tread, not summer hikers. Travertine + algae + marl is slicker than ice.
- Waterproof gaiters + full waterproof-breathable pants you can walk in all day.
- Wool or synthetic everything. No cotton. Merino base layer, mid fleece, windproof shell. Pack a puffy + warm hat + light gloves even in "summer" - you'll need them by blue evening.
- 3+ pairs wool socks in separate dry-bags. Change at midday whether you think you need to or not. Bring foot powder, blister kit, and a small towel and brush/scraper for marl.
- Trekking poles, two of them. You need them to probe depth, keep balance on rounded rims, and catch yourself when a rim that looked like concrete is actually lace.

Move slowly. This ground punishes a heavy stride. Step on the low, vegetated saddles and flat marl, not on the lips.

### 2. Understand the city that isn't a city

From ankle height it looks like neighborhoods of bathtubs. From 15 feet up it's a continent. Scale will lie to you all day.

Nothing was cut or mortared. It's travertine and tufa laid grain by grain by lime-heavy springs welling up everywhere, helped by cyanobacteria, moss, liverwort. Upper pools feed lower pools through notches that work exactly like gutters. A collapsed rim becomes a plaza, already re-cementing.

Second rule they told you: everything is in motion, but slowly enough you have to kneel to see it. That doesn't mean stable under your weight.

Hazards:

- **Fragile rims.** Those cream, bone, rust-yellow dams took centuries. Your boot can snap in a second what spring flow will take decades to heal. Never walk the rim crest if you can step in the channel or on a broad flat. Never jump from terrace to terrace.
- **False floors.** Black peat, white marl, living moss over dead moss over water. Looks solid, gives. Probe with poles before committing, especially where terraces dissolve northward into string bog ridges-and-flarks and eastward toward the big steel-blue lakes. Those lakes steam where cold air meets warmer spring water - do not swim, do not wade. Edges undercut.
- **Broken slab plazas.** Old collapses are ankle-breakers hidden under a centimeter of water that perfectly reflects sky. Test every slab.
- **Ice.** Even outside winter, evening brings skin ice. In winter the whole city locks, heaves, cracks rims. Unless you are an experienced winter alpine traveler, do not come in freeze. If you must, add crampons/microspikes, ice axe knowledge, and understand heaved stone is unpredictable.

In other words: walk like a guest in someone else's house where the floors grow themselves.

### 3. Navigation: streets for water, not for you

There are no builders, no trails, no signs, no rescue, no insects to swat but also no people to flag down. You can walk an hour and see only a pika with sedge in its mouth, once a raven crossing, and old deer/elk prints down in the willows.

And it's vast. Tens of miles of near-touching basins, courtyards, cisterns, channels that branch like streets. In haze and self-made cloud, every white wall looks like every other white wall.

- Do not rely on memory or sightlines. Bring GPS with extra batteries kept warm in an inside pocket, paper map/compass and the skill to use them, and a satellite messenger / PLB. There is no cell promise up here.
- Mark your entry/exit. Drop a waypoint at your portal/landing and at every master divide you climb for a look. That high view is your only true map: north to striped bog, east to lakes, south/west to white stone into cloud.
- Plan for 1/3 speed. Soft ground, altitude, route-finding around deep cells and weeping scarps will cut your mileage to nothing. Pick a small loop, not a traverse. Everything is beyond walking anyway.
- Mist and self-weather: The plateau makes its own cloud. Evaporation off ten thousand shallow mirrors drifts a mile, darkens, rains briefly on the pools, dissipates. Post-rain everything shines and drips. Visibility can go from miles to 50 feet. Have a sit-and-wait plan: shell on, sit on your pack on a dry wall-top, wait it out. Do not push in whiteout on rimstone.
- Leave no cairns, no flagging. Photograph intersections - that notched spillway, that lichen-mapped boulder - on the way in to recognize on the way out.

Go in a small group, stay in sight. Sound doesn't carry the way you expect in a place this quiet. Wingbeats sound loud because there is nothing else. A whistle carries better than shouting in thin air.

### 4. Water, food, sleep

**Water is everywhere and not for drinking as-is.**
It's cold, mineral-sharp, heavy with dissolved limestone, smelling faintly of iron and moss. Full of calcite needles, algae skins, pollen, ash, snails the size of grains, midge larvae in sun-warmed corners.

Don't drink straight from pools. It won't kill you instantly, but it will upset stomachs and coat teeth and guts with lime, and you don't know what microbes travel with the cyanobacteria.

Carry in 2-3 liters fresh, plus a filter + backup chemical treatment. Let silty marl water settle first, then filter. Avoid the olive/ochre algal skins and the warmest corners. Rinse hands after handling weeping rock. Wash any cuts immediately - lime water stings and keeps them open.

Food: there is nothing to forage. Sedge tufts, star-moss, liverwort, tight pincushions of alpine campion - they sip, they don't feed you. Bring all calories, plus extra for cold thermogenesis. No fires. Peat is fuel but also the skin of the whole place, vegetation is cropped short and precious, and everything is damp. Bring a canister stove, windscreen, and hot drinks. You'll crave them.

Sleep: Do not camp in a basin, courtyard, or pool margin. You'll wake in seep. Do not camp on a pretty rim. You'll crush it. Look for the driest wall-tops or, better, pull back to the marsh-edge where true bog meets willows and find a durable, already-flat rise. Use a full footprint, a 3-season-plus tent with good bathtub floor, and a sleeping pad with high R-value - cold comes up from water, not just air. Pack a vapor-barrier bag for wet socks and boots in the vestibule.

Sanitation: With water table at the surface, you cannot bury waste. Bring WAG bags and pack everything out. Pee well away from pools and seeps, on marl, not moss. Pack out toilet paper.

### 5. Ecological etiquette: you are big, they are slow

This height belongs mostly to mineral and microbial. One square meter holds sky water, floating dust, algae, downward-growing calcite needles, black peat, white marl, living moss on dead moss. That layered density is the city.

- Look, don't rearrange. Don't dam a notch to make a crossing, don't knock a lip to drain a pool, don't stack rimstones.
- Kneel *beside* pools, not in them. Oils, sunscreen, marl from your boots cloud the film where larvae flick.
- Don't collect. Those gray-green and orange lichen maps are decades old. Those rim snails move millimeters a day.
- Clean everything before you come and when you leave. Brush boots, poles, gaiters. You can carry seeds, spores, or ash-dust in marl mud. This place is isolated by height; don't be the vector.
- Be quiet as a practice. This is one of the quietest places its size anywhere. No speakers, no shouting, keep voices low. Listen for what is actually there: trickle finding a new lip, pool overtopping with faint ticking sheet, seep beading out of dark-based scarps. Put your palm to a weeping face once - wet, cold, iron and moss - then leave it to weep.

Wildlife is rare and shy for a reason. Watch the pika from stillness, let the raven cross, give elk/deer prints a wide berth. You're not prey here, but you're not needed either.

### 6. Mental packing

It will feel empty and full at once. Almost empty of movement, utterly full of detail. Long-existed, weathered, in-its-own-state. Old rims rounded and pitted, collapse hollows softened into bowls, straight channels gone meandering.

Many travelers find the monotony - white stone, glass water, cool wind with little to catch - disorienting after hour three. Time dilates. Distances lie. Bring patience.

Stay until evening once, if you can do it safely and warmly. The light cools white to blue with no theatrics. Pools go from glare to glass. Weeping faces darken. The unbuilt city ticks and seeps and lays down another invisible film of stone under coming ice, content to be vast and wet and almost empty.

Your job is to walk soft, stay dry-ish, find your way back, and leave no print that doesn't fill back in with clear cold water behind you.

**Quick checklist for an ordinary human:**

Waterproof knee boots + gaiters + waterproof pants, wool layers + wind shell + puffy + hat/gloves, 3x wool socks in dry bags, poles, sunglasses/sunscreen/hat, GPS + compass/map + satellite messenger + headlamp + extra batteries, 2-3L water + filter + stove + all food, tent with bathtub floor + warm pad + warm bag, first aid + blister kit + whistle, dry bags + pack liner + trash/WAG bags, camera with polarizer, notebook. No cotton, no new boots, no drone, no fire kit, no expectation of rescue.

Come prepared to be slow, wet, quiet, and small - and you'll be allowed, for a little while, to be a citizen of the city made only for water.
