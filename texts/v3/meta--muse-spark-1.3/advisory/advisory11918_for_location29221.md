You are baseline human. This place is not built for you. You can visit, but only briefly, covered, and on its terms.

Think of it not as a garden and not as a ruin. Think of it as a newborn, high-altitude machine-shop that grew itself and has never been wet.

Here is how to go in and come back out with the same body you went in with.

### 1. What you're walking into

A pocket no bigger than a small room, cupped into the leeward shoulder of a black volcanic tooth. Sheer drops all around into thin blue air. New lava rock, sharp as broken glass, still breathing heat from below. No soil, no water, ever.

Inside it, 7 or 8 things 3-4 meters tall. They look like plants until you are close. They are stalk, leaf, root - and also pipe, bellows, boiler, spark-gap, valve. Coppery veins with slow amber fluid. Roots fused to rock like surface plumbing. Leaves big enough to stand under, warm as skin, humming so low you feel it in your teeth. Under each leaf: gill-rows that sigh out dry steam once an hour. On top: a red metal-petaled valve that opens with a *clack* and vents black smoke that doesn't rise, it falls sideways and pools at your knees. Perfect ironwood ball-bearing seeds that *cough* out when ripe. Blue St. Elmo's fire on young shoot tips on dry afternoons.

No animals. No flies, no lizards, no birds. No sound except ticking rock, an hourly exhale, a metallic ping as stems expand, and your own blood very loud. Iron pollen drifting ten minutes to cross the bowl.

And reality doesn't quite hold. Shadows fall up. You see a plant half a second ahead of itself out of the corner of your eye. Twelve steps feels longer. Sound arrives late. The sheltering rock lip hums and makes your hands feel like they are in two places.

Your job is not to understand it. Your job is to not need it to make sense for 30 minutes.

### 2. The body problems, in order of how fast they will get you

**a) Air and altitude.**
You are very high. The air below the pocket is thin, cold, blue. The air *in* the pocket stays - trapped by the lip - but it's hot, dry, thin, and laced. Expect impaired judgment, shortness of breath, headache, and faster dehydration just from breathing. 

Acclimatize 2-3 days at base altitude before you try the tooth. Ascend slow. No alcohol for 24h before. If you have asthma, COPD, heart disease, anemia, are pregnant, get migraines from strobe/low hum - do not go. Bring supplemental oxygen for the approach and descent, not necessarily to wear inside, but to recover on the edge. You will be stupid up there. Plan for it.

**b) The Water Rule.**
Nothing liquid that is water can exist here. Pour a cup and it screams to vapor before it hits. Your sweat, your saliva, your tears want to do the same.

You cannot drink in the bowl. Do not try. An open bottle, a hydration bladder tube, a wet bandana will boil/scour and will scald your mouth and then be gone. It will also contaminate a place that has never been wet.

So:
Pre-hydrate aggressively 2-3 hours *below* the anomaly - water + electrolytes until your urine is pale. Eat salty carbs.
Then go up with water sealed, insulated, and *stowed*. It is for when you are back down below the steaming seams, not for the pocket.
Inside, preserve what you have: wear a heat-moisture-exchange respirator or a mountaineering mask with a moisture trap. It won't make water, but it will keep your exhaled moisture from being ripped away in one breath. Your mouth will still dry in a minute. That's normal here. Don't panic and pant.
Use oil-based protection only: heavy petroleum or silicone lip balm, oil-based nasal gel, sealed goggles - no water-based drops or sprays. They will sting and vanish.
Limit time: 20-40 minutes inside max, 60 absolute. Set a vibrating timer you can feel, not just hear - sound is weird here. When it goes off, leave even if a valve is about to open and you want to see it. You can want it from below.

You will still lose water from inside. Plan to rehydrate slowly for hours after, not all at once. Expect dry nosebleed, headache, thick spit.

**c) Heat from below, cold from around.**
The ground is 60 to well over 100C in patches, porous, shimmering. The air off the rock dries you. The sun is flat white midday, high UV. The drop beyond is alpine cold.

Wear for both: full coverage, no shorts, no exposed ankles, no mesh trail runners.
- Mountaineering boots with stiff, heat-resistant soles (Vibram mountaineering / fire-line rated). Test them on hot rock below first. Normal hiking glue delaminates.
- Thick wool socks + gaiters. The flakes are glass.
- Long, loose, light-colored heat-resistant pants. Bring a closed-cell foam sit pad - never sit directly on rock, never kneel. The rock that feels warm through your back when you lean is fine for 5 minutes; the crack where a seed-pod is ticking is not.
- Long sleeves, sun hood, Category 4 glacier sunglasses, sunblock (silicone-based).
- Cut-proof, heat-rated gloves. Always. The lava is foamy where it froze and glassy where it broke. One weight shift and a flake snaps with a *tick* and smells of iron. It will slice you like obsidian. Olivine crystals are pretty and sharp.

You will not thermoregulate normally because sweat cannot pool. Rest in shade of your own hat, not under a leaf. Do not lean on stems to rest - they are pressurized.

**d) What you breathe.**
Sulfur yellows, pale salts, hot metal steam, ozone from coronas, and knee-high black smoke that unravels slowly.

That smoke is heavy. It pools. Do not crouch, sit low, lie down for a photo in the bowl. Keep your head up. Children, dogs, any animal - absolutely not. There is a reason there are no animals. You are the exception, temporarily.

Wear a P3 / N100 half-face respirator with organic vapor + particulate combo, plus sealed goggles. You will smell hot metal and lightning anyway. If you taste metal strongly, get chest tightness, cough that won't stop, blue lips: stand, move to the lip edge where the pocket air spills to the drop, and then out and down.
Do not eat, drink, smoke, vape in the pocket. Iron pollen falls slow as snow and will coat lips, food, filters. You'll bring enough home on your clothes.

### 3. The green engines: how not to get hurt by a plant that is also a boiler

No one built them. Don't look for a builder, a path, a ruin, a control panel. Don't try to fix, open, pick, or build anything yourself. No tent, no fire, no stove, no cairn, no sample chipping. Combustion makes water vapor - it is refused here and behaves badly. And everything that looks made is grown. If you break it, it bleeds hot.

Rules inside:
- Keep 2-3 meters from stems and crowns. The amber coolant blood pulses slow, but it's under pressure and hot. A rupture is a burn unit injury with no hospital above the clouds.
- Never put your face over a crown valve. The red whorl opens with a slow *clack* when pressure peaks, any time, without warning. It vents sideways black smoke and dry superheated steam. Watch valves from the side, from the edge.
- Never touch young shoots in the afternoon. That blue corona is real electricity. It crackles, smells of ozone, will give you a RF burn and startle you toward the edge. Even the big leaves: touch once with the back of a gloved hand if you must know the skin-warm hum, then stop. Prolonged contact conducts that subsonic tremor into your teeth and jaw and makes people nauseous.
- Watch for seeds. Ripe ironwood spheres don't drop, they discharge with a soft cough and roll inches to lodge in cracks where they sit ticking for years. Don't be kneeling in the roll line. Wear a climbing helmet - not just for rockfall on the tooth, but for pinging expansion and coughing pods. Wear eye protection always.
- Don't swat pollen. Let convection do its ten-minute crossing. If a cloud lifts off a valve, turn your shoulder, breathe slow through the respirator, let it settle.

They are slow. A frond unfurls over a whole hot season inch by inch, creaking. What looks still is only slow and idling. It never stops. Even if you wait to night - don't - it would just tick down degree by degree. Don't mistake quiet for off.

### 4. Thin reality: the part that scares people more than heat

You will experience:
Shadows wrong at noon - green afterimages hanging up in the air.
Double vision at the edge of sight - the farthest engine overlapping itself, sighing half a second early.
Distance wrong - empty heat between plants feels longer than the 12 steps you count.
Sound wrong - your footstep arrives late, as if it took a detour.
Touch wrong - hands feel in two places near the humming lip.

This is normal *there*. It fades when you descend below the steaming seams.

Prepare:
- Do not use shadows for direction or time. Bring a headlamp anyway - you will not be there after dark, but the pocket light lies flat and honest and kills depth perception on sharp rock.
- Do not chase the double. If you see two plants, look directly at it, blink, it resolves to one. Do not photograph the corner-of-eye thing and walk toward it. Stay in the middle of your arc.
- Count. Say steps out loud. Keep trekking poles of *non-conductive* carbon, not aluminum - both for ground truth when space feels stretched, and so you aren't holding lightning rods during corona hour. Plant, step, plant, step.
- Tether. On the approach tooth, rope up as you would on any sheer black ridge. Inside the pocket, keep a buddy in sight or a fixed line to the lip. Do not wander for a better angle over the drop. The world falls away into far cloud and the lip is all that holds the air.
- No fine tasks inside. Pre-set camera, gloves on. The hum makes fingers clumsy. Voice check every 5 minutes with your partner: name, time left, how do your lungs feel. If anyone gets euphoric, weepy, certain the machines are speaking - that's hypoxia + awe + infrasound. That's your cue to leave.
- Do not attempt a multiverse jump from inside the bowl. Jump from well below, hike in. Anchors, compasses, radios misbehave where sound takes a detour and smoke falls sideways. If you must carry a beacon, keep it outside and have an exit bearing memorized: back to the lip, then down the leeward shoulder.

Go in the morning. Leave before dry afternoon when coronas sputter most. Do not plan to see night. You have not seen what idling at night does to navigation and cold, and you don't need to.

### 5. Pack like this

Worn: base layer + wind/heat shell, full pants, boots, gaiters, gloves, helmet, goggles, respirator, sunglasses, sun hood.

In pack, and nothing dangling:
- 2-3L water sealed (for below), electrolytes, salty food for after
- Supplemental O2 or at minimum a recovery plan
- Timer with vibration + whistle + mirror for signaling (sound is unreliable)
- Carbon poles, rope, harness if approach is technical - assume it is
- Sit pad, headlamp + spare batteries
- First aid: pressure bandage, burn gel (silicone, not water gel), eye wash sealed for use *after* descent, blister care for glass cuts, antihistamine
- Spare filters, spare gloves, bag for contaminated outer layer
- Camera pre-set, no drone - convection and thin air and sideways smoke will kill it and litter a pristine place

Do NOT bring: open mugs, squeeze bottles, water misters, wet wipes (they'll flash dry and leave lint + iron dust), pets, hammers, collection bags, offerings to leave. You leave no footprint - the sharp rock springs back, the salt dust resettles - keep it that way.

### 6. How to be in there

Enter slow. Let your eyes adjust to white flat light. Stand at the edge with your back to warm stone for a minute. Listen until you hear the absence, then the ticks and pings underneath.

Walk deliberately. Don't cross between two engines quickly to save time - cross where you can see both crowns and the ground cracks. Stop standing tall. Breathe through the mask.

Sit if you want to understand it, but sit on your pad, up high, back to rock, looking out with the bowl behind you or to the side - not prone in the smoke layer. Watch one mote of iron pollen take its ten minutes. Watch one leaf-bellows swell and sigh. You may get one valve *clack*. An hour after you've gone, another will open without you. Let that be okay.

When your timer buzzes, stand, check your partner, count poles, and go down. Don't run. The rock is new and hasn't learned to be rock. It is still deciding its shape, and so are you up there.

### After

Below the heat, in normal air: drink, small sips, electrolytes. Shake out, brush down outside your tent/vehicle - iron pollen + salts + sulfur are abrasive and will rust in humidity. Bag outer layer. Check boots for melted spots, skin for glass slivers, nose for bleeding, lungs for wheeze. Log headache, nausea, how long double-vision persisted. It should clear quickly with altitude and clean air. If wheeze, confusion, or visual doubling lasts more than an hour down below, get medical help and say: high-altitude volcanic gas + metal dust + heat exposure.

Rinse sealed gear, service respirator. Don't bring a seed home, even if one clinked into your gaiter. Tip it back into a crack below the pocket on your way out if you must move it. Those ticking spheres are supposed to sit for years.

You will remember it as longer than it was. That is the place, not your memory failing. Too much life in too little space, high above the world, humming quietly to itself, making and unmaking without hands - and you, briefly, the only animal it has ever tolerated, allowed to watch and then told, by dry mouth and late sound and upward shadows, to leave.
