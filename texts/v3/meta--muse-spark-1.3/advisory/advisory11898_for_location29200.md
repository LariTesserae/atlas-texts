This is not a ruin. It's working, and it's working in the cold and the wet to stay empty. Prepare like you're going to winter in someone else's basement factory that could quietly try to drown you.

### 1. Understand what The Basin actually is

Don't picture a cave or a bunker. Picture a valley that got built instead of found.

A mile or two across, 47-52 meters down, terraced concrete walls all the way around, a steel lid on top that doesn't keep anything out. Down there is its own weather: warm machines breathing up, freezing lid condensing it down, falling again as inside-rain. Sodium lights for suns. Pumps for a heart. A press line stamping parts for nobody.

Everything is spaced too far apart on purpose that never filled in. You will be exposed. Walks that look like 2 minutes are 20 minutes in drizzle and wind between orange islands of light. Sound arrives late. Light doesn't behave. Noon up top is a dim brightening at 3pm down below.

You are not visiting nature with some buildings in it. There is no soil. Under cracked slab is slag, membrane, slab. The floor is final. Respect that.

Your three enemies are **cold + wet + dark**, and your hard limit is **water**. If the pumps stop, it's a lake in 18 hours. There are high-water marks on the Pump House door at knee and waist from the last two times.

Go in assuming you are fragile, slow, easily lost, and always slightly damp. That's accurate.

### 2. Clothing: dress for basement winter, not mountain winter

This cold lives in concrete. It's -15C with wind between the masts, plus constant fine drizzle with rust in it, plus ice underfoot, plus steam that soaks then freezes. You will not dry by moving.

For an ordinary human, 2-3 hours unprepared is misery, 6+ is dangerous.

Wear:

**Base to skin:** Merino wool or synthetic long underwear, top and bottom. No cotton. No jeans. Cotton kills here because you will get damp and stay damp.

**Mid:** Fleece or heavy wool sweater. Puffy jacket if you run cold, but it must go *under* shell because of rain.

**Outer:** Real waterproof, breathable rain jacket with hood + rain pants. Not a poncho, not an umbrella — the old-timers don't carry umbrellas because the rain comes sideways in fronts and hisses off hot metal. You need something that sheds iron-drizzle and lets you move around machinery. Goretex or equivalent, taped seams.

**Feet:** Insulated, waterproof, steel-toe boots with aggressive tread. The apron is frost-heaved slab, black ice in expansion joints, moving water with ice skin, oil + meltwater down the center of the access helix. Add slip-on microspikes / Yaktrax in your pocket. Bring 2 extra pairs of thick wool socks in a dry-bag inside your coat. Trench foot is a real risk if you stand around.

**Hands/head/neck:** Thin liner gloves + heavy waterproof work gloves over them. You will be touching frozen handrails, chipping ice, catching yourself on sharp spall. Balaclava or neck gaiter + warm beanie that fits under a helmet. Thin hat alone isn't enough — your breath hangs and freezes on your face.

**Helmet:** Yes. Wear a hard hat or climbing helmet. The north wall has 6-foot beards of dirty ice with a red line: DO NOT PARK UNDER ICEFALL. They fall. So does rust scale from the lid when a freight train crosses. So do concrete spalls.

Keep all batteries, phone, spare headlamp batteries in an inside chest pocket. Cold drains them in an hour if left outside.

### 3. Kit to carry

Think industrial caver + night-shift worker:

**Light x3:** Primary headlamp (300+ lumens, warm-white if you can, cuts sodium haze better), small handheld thrower for seeing across the apron, and chemical glowsticks as backup. Sodium masts make perfect orange islands with blue shadow between — your eyes never adjust. Don't rely on one light. Bring lens wipes, your lens will fog and spot instantly going from cold apron to warm pipe alley.

**Eye and lung protection:** Safety glasses or sealed goggles, and N95 or P100 half-mask. The ceiling rains dust + rust flakes when trains cross and the lid ticks. Steam traps vent, transformer casings hiss, settling ponds steam. You don't want that in your eyes/lungs for hours.

**Ears:** Filtered earplugs (construction / musician type) *not* total blockers. Base note is 50-cycle pump + substation hum you feel in your molars, over that press slams, steam, lid rattle. You need to dull it but still hear: change in pump pitch = intake icing, rattle = lid dampers freezing, sudden quiet = breaker trip, and THREE BLASTS = GO UP. If you wear noise-cancelling headphones you might miss the one sound that matters.

**Gas sense:** No open flame near Shaft 2 — methane collects in cold pockets. That means: no lighter, no camp stove, no smoking, no steel hammering that throws sparks down there. If you can get a cheap portable 4-gas / O2-CO-LEL meter, bring it. If not, at minimum bring discipline: stay upslope, don't loiter in low still corners near Shaft 2, and if you feel dizzy, headachy, or smell sweet/gassy air where there was none, leave upslope into moving air.

**Traction and sharp protection:** Gaiters, knee pads if you plan to crouch to look at moss/liverwort/carp. The concrete here is young — fresh spalls are pale gray inside with edges like knives, rebar teeth bared. Thick gloves and a basic first aid kit + pressure bandage. Make sure your tetanus is current. Rust blooms overnight.

**Dry survival:** Space blanket/bivy, 4-6 chemical hand/toe warmers (since you can't use flame), thermos with hot sweet tea/soup, high-calorie bars, 1.5L of your own water. Do NOT drink canal, puddle, or settling pond water. It's cold iron, oil, algae, treatment chemicals. The pipes lift it up and out as "clean-ish" for a reason.

**Comms/nav:** Whistle (sound carries weirdly — a clang 100 yards away sounds next to you), watch with timer, notebook + pencil (pens freeze), phone/camera in waterproof pouch with silica packet. GPS will be useless under the lid. Mark your route by pillar survey marks: -47 METERS, -52 METERS. Count bulkhead lights on the helix going down — ears pop twice, third turn you lose sky. Count them going up.

Walk the edges of the helix, not the black stained center. That's oil + meltwater, slick as ice.

### 4. How to move and not die

Read the stenciled rules and actually obey them. The place enforces them.

1. **KEEP WATER MOVING.** Never block a channel, culvert, scupper. Don't set your pack in one, don't move a pallet. If you see a weep over ~5 L/min — that's a steady garden-hose trickle, not a drip — report it to whoever is on shift. Don't try to fix it.

2. **DO NOT DIG. FLOOR IS FINAL.** Don't pry a plate. There's membrane and sensor wire and an alarm. For multiverse travelers especially: do not try to anchor, portal, bury, or cache *through* the floor. Anchor to a pillar above the high-water black line, or better, anchor up top before you descend.

3. **STAY IN LIGHT BETWEEN SHIFTS.** Between masts is true dark and wind. Plan your crossings mast-to-mast. At shift change when the press pauses and pumps throttle back for ~30 seconds, STOP. Listen. That's when you'll hear the thousands of drips, and also when you'll hear if something is wrong. Then move.

4. **NO OPEN FLAME NEAR SHAFT 2.** See above.

5. **IF YOU HEAR THREE BLASTS, GO UP. Not across. Up.** Memorize *two* up routes the minute you arrive: the main helix ramp, and the far stairs you can barely see when the air clears between rain fronts. Up means climb terraces/stairs to the lid exits, not run across the apron to look. If pumps have stopped, low end floods first — don't go to the Pump House to sightsee.

Other machine rules no one stencils but everyone knows:

- Shed 4 press line and the driverless forklift yard are live. Treat them like wild animals. Stay out of the taped/painted lines even if faded to ghosts. Never walk between stacked chassis rows. Assume sensors can't see you in drizzle and orange glare.
- Substation hum means stay outside the chain-link. Icicles fall like spears, and trip breakers = sudden quiet = whole line cooling and ticking.
- Don't stand under light shafts too long staring up. Pigeons do that until they get exhausted and have to land on a warm pipe. You'll get disoriented, dripped on, cold.

Never go alone. Tell someone up top: time in, expected time out, what to do if you're late. Check the weather *above* before you go down. If it's snowing, blowing, or fast thaw up top, the lid will tick/groan, dump dust, shift the inside-rain fronts, and the wall weeps will run harder.

### 5. Social and living protocol

You may walk 20 minutes and see no one. When you do see someone, be low-impact, useful, dry.

- Nod to pump crew, foreman, night shift. Ask permission before entering any shed, leaning on warm pipes, or photographing people.
- Don't touch controls, don't clear an iced pallet from a scupper unless asked — you'll do it wrong and get cursed at while they chip it out with a bar.
- Bring trade value: spare AA/AAA lithium batteries, fresh socks, good coffee in a second thermos to share, hand warmers, cat treats. The feral tabby with condensate on its whiskers is night-shift family. Don't chase it. If they feed it, you can watch, don't interfere.
- Leave the volunteers alone: black-green felt moss in joints, leggy lambsquarter and dandelions under sodium lights, the hanging liverwort garden under the dripping flange, the blind white carp in the three green-black settling tanks, the crow pair on the stack catwalk. Don't pick, don't feed carbs to carp, don't throw things at crows commuting up the vent shaft with fries. They are what can live here. You're a guest in their cold greenhouse.
- Sound etiquette: don't shout to test echo. Workers listen for pump pitch and breaker trips. Keep your voice low, move slow in dark zones so your footsteps aren't mistaken for something falling half a mile away.

### 6. Multiverse traveler specifics

If you're used to hopping:

- Set your return anchor *above* the lid, at the top of the helix in the real world. The Basin is a Faraday-ish bowl of steel, concrete, steam, and electrical noise. Signals, beacons, and your own sense of direction degrade below. Plus flood risk. A mark down on the apron could be under knee water by morning.
- Don't count on time or daylight cues. Bring your own time. The light shafts lag — noon above is dim brightening hours later below. You'll lose track in 90 minutes. Set a vibrating timer for check-ins every 30 min: drink, wiggle toes, check socks, check light.
- Expect sensory translation lag. Cold flattens sound, distance lies. Footsteps behind you are often terraces far off. Hammer clangs teleport. If you get paranoid, stop, look at steam direction (it always spreads sideways and becomes cloud) and at ice beards (they only grow on the sunless north wall). Those are truer than ears.
- Condensation is your portal killer. Anything cold you bring down will sweat, then freeze, then sweat again near hot pipes. Seal electronics, seals, journals in dry bags. Warm them slowly before use or lenses and contacts fog solid.
- Limit first visit to 3-4 hours max. The damp gets into decision-making. Plan a warm, dry exit — a car with heater up top, dry clothes waiting, hot drink — not just "I'll tough it out."

A good first loop: descend helix slowly, equalizing ears, noting lights. Pause where sky disappears — note how you feel, that's your turnaround mood check. At bottom, go mast-to-mast to perimeter canal, follow it downstream (water always knows the way) to Pump House but stay outside, read the door marks. Loop back via assembly shed at distance, pause mid-apron during a clear window to hear drips. Climb to a terrace to see steam spread sideways and inside-rain front come toward you. Then go up *before* you're shivering. Shivering means you stayed 30 minutes too long.

You will leave damp, smelling of cold iron and wet cement, ears ringing with pump throb, eyes tired from glare-to-gloom. That's normal. Change socks immediately up top, even if you feel fine. Drink something hot. Write down what you heard in the quiet 30 seconds — that's the Basin telling you what it is when it's not working so hard to stay dry.

Don't try to conquer it. Keep water moving, stay in light, go up when told.
