This is not a climb. It's not a cave dive. It's a night spent lying on your stomach on freezing rock with your face over a furnace full of life. Prepare for that.

You can do this as an ordinary human. You do not need to be a caver. You do need to stay on the rim. No one casually goes down into the Pit.

### 1. Understand what it is

An 80 x 50 meter collapse mouth in high basalt, ~3,000m up, 30m deep to a floor that isn't rock. It's chitin sand two meters deep - shed skins, dry frass, dropped wings - with things swimming through it.

Everything lives off airfall. All day, thousands of soot-dark microbats and heat-navigating swifts - the Returners - are out 50km over the cold desert eating moths and wind-blown flies. At dusk they pour back in a river, thousands per minute, into blisters and pockets in the upper walls. They shit dry pellets before dawn. Pale eyeless waxy crickets the size of your thumb-tip - the Clingers - in layers three and four deep cover every wall, ledge, and boulder, eat the pellets, the shed, each other. Rust-red forearm-long centipedes and translucent swimming snakes eat them. Nothing green, no lichen, no moss, no water at all. Only mineral growth: needle gypsum, halite crust, sulfur puffballs around deep exhalations. Nitrates blooming white out of tuff like frost.

It holds at blood-temperature inside while frost forms on the stones at your elbows. The mountain gives heat from below, millions of bodies give heat from within. They thermoregulate in a slow visible wave - antennae up, down, around the oval to vent.

It is profoundly quiet, because the living walls eat sound like snow. What you hear is close and dry: rice-on-paper skitter as Clingers adjust, soft tick of a dislodged pebble that never lands loudly, a sigh when a thousand bats turn together, a thin sonar click at the edge of hearing.

It is stable, old, and completely uninterested in you. The ledges are worn smooth not by water but by feet. The talus has settled. The overhang holds. The three half-fallen basalt windbreaks on the north rim, the rusted tin box with corn husk and a blue bead, the flaked white circle-and-line, the ladder on the south chute with rails but no rungs - all say the same thing: people tried to use this place for nests or guano and left. There is no rescue, no wires, no sensors, no lights.

You will be split in two: back freezing, face baking. Your sweat will dry before it beads. Your lips will crack in minutes.

Prepare to be a heat-shadow that watches.

### 2. Your body first

**Altitude + dry + split temperature is the real danger.**

At 3,000m+ your heart will pound on the walk in over pahoehoe and rotten scoria. Acclimatize 1-2 nights at ~2,500m if you can. No alcohol for 24 hours before. Hydrate hard the day before - pale urine going in. This place will steal water out of your breath.

Do not go if you have uncontrolled asthma, severe heart disease, serious insect-venom allergy, or are immunocompromised. You will be breathing ammoniac, desiccated animal dust that burns lungs. If you get nosebleeds easily, headaches from ammonia, or panic at swarming insects, sit this one out.

Fitness: you need to be able to walk uneven lava in the dark with a pack, get down prone and get back up, and lie still for 3-5 hours in the cold without shivering yourself off the rim.

### 3. What to bring - and why

Think: desert winter on your back, sauna on your face, no water, bad air, sharp rock, no light discipline broken.

**Water and salt, lots:** 4-6 liters per person for approach + night + walk out, plus electrolytes. There is *nothing* to drink there, no seep, no pool. Drink on schedule, before thirst. Salty snacks. Lip balm - a lot - plus lanolin or vaseline for nostrils, saline nasal spray, moisturizer, water for eyes. You will desiccate.

**Layers for two climates at once:** Merino or synthetic base, warm mid (fleece/down), windproof shell top and bottom. Warm hat, buff/neck gaiter you can pull over nose and mouth, gloves that still grip rock - knuckles bite first. The essential luxury: a closed-cell foam pad to lie on. Without it the frozen basalt sucks the heat out of your chest and belly while your face roasts. It also distributes your weight on rotten lip.

**Feet:** Stiff, high-ankle boots with good soles. Pahoehoe is glass-sharp and polished; cinder rolls. Gaiters to keep cinder, salt powder, gypsum needles and stray Clingers out. Trekking poles for the lava field approach.

**Air and eyes:** Sealed goggles (ski goggles or lab goggles) + P3 / N99 / FFP3 respirator + spare filters. Not just for COVID - for fine chitin powder, nitrate dust, ammonia. If you smell ammonia sharp enough to make your eyes water or give you a headache, pull the buff + respirator up, back 2-3 meters from the lip. If it persists, leave. Do not tough it out - the south chute ladder is a monument to people who thought they could.

**Light - almost none:** Headlamp with true red mode for the walk in *away* from the pit, plus a tiny backup. No white light at the rim, no flash, no phone screen, no laser, no flash photography. Ever. White light makes the near wall shiver and close - millions of backs turning at once - and ruins 40 minutes of dark adaptation for you and stresses the thermoregulation wave. Let your eyes adapt 30-40 minutes. You will see more in starlight than with a torch: the far rim blue line, then texture, then movement. If you must check gear, cup a dim red under your jacket, pointed away, brief.

Better than a light: 8x42 binoculars you already know how to use with gloves, and if you have one, a passive thermal monocular. It lets you watch the breathing wave and the bat river without photons.

**Navigation and safety:** GPS + downloaded offline map + compass, extra batteries kept warm inside (cold kills them). Satellite messenger - there is no cell signal and no one to hear you shout; the Pit absorbs sound. Tell someone your plan and return time. Helmet if you are clumsy near edges - rockfall from claws is real. Small first aid: blister kit, antihistamine, ibuprofen, wound wash, elastic bandage, personal meds including altitude meds if prescribed. Epipen if you have venom allergy.

Do not bring rope to go down unless you are a full expedition with bolts, gas meters, and a haul team - which you are not. The rock is rotten scoria; there is no trustworthy anchor. The old ladder is silvered, snapped, rungless.

**Leave-no-trace kit:** Pack out *everything* including human waste, tissues, spit, crumbs. Do not urinate, pour water, drop food, or spit into or near the Pit. Water and nutrients are poison here - they would give mold and plants a foothold and break a million-year story that works because nothing green ever gets started. Brush boots clean before and after to avoid carrying seeds in. No perfume, scented sunscreen, insect repellent clouds - their world is touch and heat-smell, your chemical plume is loud.

### 4. The approach

Go in daylight, settle before dusk. Lava in the dark breaks ankles.

Walk slowly. Heart hammering is normal at that altitude. Breathe through nose when dusty. Mark your line in - cairns are cultural, don't build new ones, use GPS track for walk out.

Stop 20-30 meters short of the mouth. You will feel it before you see it: the cold lifts. A dry furnace-breath lays over your face, smelling of hot stone, dust, and old ammoniac animal, baked not rotten. That's your cue to suit up: goggles around neck, respirator ready, pad out, lights off/red, phones dark, voices to whisper.

Crawl the last meters. Lie prone, elbows wide, head just past the lip. Never stand on the lip, never kick rocks in. The edge is rotten. Keep your pack and water 2 meters back so a roll doesn't send it in - anything you drop is gone and becomes contamination.

Best watch: last hour of light to first 2-3 hours after dark for the river coming home, and again an hour before dawn when pellets drop and the wall settles deeper. The sky thinning in the east is your cue to pull back.

### 5. At the rim - how to behave on its terms

**Stay dry, stay still, stay quiet.**

Stay still low. Everything navigates by heat-shadow and touch. You are a big warm blob. Minimize your thermal noise: stay prone, move slow, keep face slightly covered, don't wave hands over the mouth. If you need to warm hands, do it under your jacket, not over the void.

Don't expect to be seen. Eyes are useless currency. Let the wave come to you. After 20 minutes you'll see the ripple cross - antennae lifting, settling, moving slow around the oval. Don't try to follow individuals. Watch the whole.

Listen for small things. Don't fill the quiet. No music, no loud talk, no shouting into the hole to test echo - you'll just hear your own breathing, embarrassingly loud, and stress the bats.

Never touch. Don't reach for a Clinger, a bat, a snake, a centipede. Centipedes that size are venomous and fast and will be on the backs of the carpet; snakes are pale and silent in dust. Bats are rabies vectors. If something crawls on you, brush gently, don't slap dust into the air.

If dust catches your throat - burning, coughing - back off upwind, respirator on, sip water. The guano-men left because dust that dry burns lungs. Believe them.

Respect what people left: don't rebuild windbreaks, don't open the tin box, don't retouch paint, don't take bead or husk. Step around.

### 6. What NOT to do

- Don't descend. Not to the south chute, not "just ten meters for a photo." The floor moves, the air thickens downward with heat, ammonia, possibly CO2/H2S from deep cracks, the climb out on smooth worn footholds over living carpet with no pro is desperate. Ordinary travelers die that way in pits like this.
- Don't shine white light, don't throw things to "see how deep," don't dangle feet.
- Don't eat over the mouth - crumbs fall and introduce outside nutrients. Eat back from rim, pack crumbs.
- Don't fly a drone into the mouth. The updraft, the bats and swifts, the sound absorption - you will lose it and kill animals.
- Don't camp in the windbreaks or in the mouth's warm plume. Camp 100m+ back, out of the drainage of wings and dust.

### 7. If something goes wrong

Buddy system, arm's reach after dark. If someone gets dizzy, pounding headache, nausea - altitude + dehydration + ammonia. Move them back into clean cold air, sit, water + electrolytes, rest. If wheezing, chest tightness not resolving, start walk out.

Bite/sting: wash, cold compress, antihistamine, painkiller, watch for spreading redness, swelling, allergic signs. Any bat bite/scratch - wash thoroughly, get to medical care urgently for rabies PEP; note time.

Lost on lava in dark: stop, shelter from wind, red light on, GPS backtrack. Don't shortcut across unknown dark ground - collapse features hide.

### 8. After

Brush all boots, gaiters, pad, tripod legs clean *away* from the site. Shower, saline rinse nose, moisturize, rehydrate with electrolytes, check for bites, ticks in clothes seams, gypsum splinters. Clean goggles and respirator - that fine powder gets everywhere. Don't share photos with geotag if the place is sensitive; the lack of wires and sensors is part of why it still works.

You will walk around it in ten minutes and spend the rest of the night trying to understand what you looked at. That's correct. A complete world without water, without plants, without light - hot in the middle of cold, loud with life while making almost no sound.

Go to endure it on its terms, then leave it exactly as full as you found it.
