This is not a ruin. It's not a cave. It's a brand-new, working piece of plumbing with a person who tends it every day — and you're going to be a guest inside it for about ten minutes.

Treat it like that: a small, loud, blindingly bright water box just under a hot, busy courtyard. Everything you do to prepare is about heat, light, noise, slippery new plaster, and not breaking the In = Out balance that keeps it alive.

### 1. Understand what you're walking — wading — into

**Above:** white noon, dry-season sun, shouting courtyard, sun-baked stone. Arid, pre-industrial, low-tech. No shade, no AC, dusty.

**Below, 3-4 meters down a square limestone ladder-shaft:** a room you can touch across with outstretched arms. About three paces wide, two and a half meters high, shallow dome with a ~1 meter square hole open to the sky, covered by a heavy timber grate lashed with hemp.

The room is a basin. Plastered brick and cut stone everywhere, so new you can still see trowel arcs and chisel marks. No natural rock. Water knee-deep on the entrance step, chest-deep in the dished middle.

Four clay spouts high on the walls pour constantly, fed by gravity from an uphill cistern. One low square spillway on the north side drains it onward into a dark culvert. Level rises and falls a hand's breadth as clouds cross the catchment.

That means:

* **Air and water are both body-hot.** Bathwater-warm, blood-warm, hotter on top where the sun hits. Your sweat won't cool you. The water won't cool you.
* **It is deafening.** Four jets crashing in a tiled box the size of a closet, plus the gargle of the outlet. You will not be able to talk. Your own voice comes back doubled and useless.
* **It is blinding.** All light comes down one solid shaft full of spray. It hits chop and explodes into moving nets that crawl up walls, ceiling, your skin. Looking straight up through the beams at bleached-blue sky hurts.
* **It is almost sterile.** No fish, no frogs, no insects. Too fresh lime, too warm, too turned-over, too fierce. Just a beard of green algae felt in the mortar joints, pale root hairs nosing down from courtyard weeds above, and olive film where the current is weakest.
* **It moves.** Jets crater the surface, the whole volume drags slowly clockwise toward the spillway, sun evaporates a chest-high mist that condenses under the beams and rains back down in fat drops. Push at your calves. Warm on top, a little cooler at your feet.

You will not have thoughts down there. The box fills completely with brightness and roar. That's normal. Plan for a short stay.

### 2. Who should not go down

Be honest. Don't go, or don't go alone, if you have:

heart trouble, uncontrolled high blood pressure, asthma or trouble breathing hot humid air, photosensitive epilepsy — those crawling light-nets can trigger it — severe claustrophobia, an open cut, ear infection, or a tendency to faint in heat.

Non-swimmers, small children, anyone who can't climb a wet 4-meter ladder backwards carrying their own body weight: stay up top.

Warm, lime-fresh, sun-heated water with open wounds, eyes open underwater, or drinking it is a bad idea.

### 3. When and how to ask

Someone comes down once a day barefoot with a basalt scraper and a dipper to clear the grate, scrape algae, bail silt so In = Out. This is their tool room and drinking-water infrastructure, not a tourist pool.

Don't sneak. In the courtyard, find the tender or anyone working. Gesture, smile, ask. Learn "may I go down?" "how long?" "may I touch?" in the local language beforehand. Accept no.

Ask when they *don't* need to clean. Don't go down right before/after them unless invited. Never move their kit on the plastered bench: coiled hemp rope, long-handled wooden dipper, tall stoppered clay jar, flat basalt scraping stone. Spaced far apart because there's almost no room. Leave huge emptiness between things. Don't sit on the bench, don't drink from the jar, don't "help" scrape unless asked.

Plan for early morning or late afternoon if you can. Noon is the full violence — hardest light, hottest stone, maximum evaporation. You'll still get the effect an hour either side, with slightly less risk of heat exhaustion and eye pain.

### 4. What to wear and bring

Think: desert above, bathtub below, nothing to damage fresh plaster.

**Wear up top:**
loose, light, sun-covering clothes, hat, sunglasses, sandals you can slip off. Sunscreen for the courtyard — but not into the basin.

**Wear down below:**
swimsuit or quick-dry shorts and top, nothing more. No jeans, no boots, no heavy pack, no long loose cloth that drags and snags silt. No lotion, oil, insect repellent, soap — it will wash off and foul a drinking system.

Feet: go bare and clean. Rinse dust off your feet and legs before you descend. The five narrow masonry treads still have sharp noses, the plaster is a little proud in corners — water shoes bring grit that grinds new plaster. If your feet cut easily, clean neoprene socks rinsed in clean water are the compromise. Never courtyard-dirty boots inside.

**Bring to the rim, leave with a watcher:**
1. 1-2 liters of clean drinking water, plus a separate liter of clean cool rinse water for eyes/skin after. Do NOT plan to drink basin water. It smells like clean rain but it's leaching fresh lime — high pH, caustic to eyes, gut, and cuts — and it's sun-warm.
2. Small drybag with towel and complete dry change. You will be soaked to the ribs and sweating through everything.
3. If you have them: low-volume swim goggles with UV, not a dive mask. They will fog instantly in the mist, but they help against lime sting and glare when jets cross the shaft. Don't open eyes underwater without them.
4. Foam earplugs *optional*. The roar in that volume can be painful in under a minute if you're sensitive. Downside: you won't hear your spotter at all. Most people do 5-10 minutes unprotected and get out. If you get tinnitus, ringing, dizziness — get out.
5. No phone, camera, light, unless in a true waterproof case on a floating lanyard. You don't need a headlamp — there's too much light — and anything dropped in the sump can choke the outlet. Anything non-waterproof will die in seconds from spray and dunking.
6. No soap, no food, no offering, no pebble to leave.

**Spotter is non-negotiable.** Never descend alone. You can faint from heat in water that won't revive you, slip, get disoriented by noise/light. One person stays at the top in shade, watches the grate, times you, holds the drybag and drinking water. Agree on hand signals before — voice is useless: fist up = I'm okay, waving up = pull me out now, tapping head = time check.

### 5. Going down, being inside, getting out

**Descend:** Face the ladder. Test each rung — they'll be damp from mist. Three to four meters is short but it's a square limestone shaft with no cage, and you'll come up wetter and weaker. Keep three points of contact. At the bottom, step to the entrance step, not straight into the deep middle. Feel with feet. New plaster is surprisingly slippery where that olive film grows, and sharp where it doesn't.

Move slow. Your wading sets up seiches that slap the walls between roars. Keep your weight off the four spout jets — don't block or hang on them, they're just clay — and keep your body and limbs well clear of the north spillway. It has a steady pull. Don't put hands/feet into the dark culvert, don't try to peer in. Don't dam anything. In must equal out.

Crouch so your mouth stays well above chop and spray. The air is thick enough to drink, lime + hot stone + rain. Breathe through your nose if the mist catches your throat. Lean close to your partner's ear to be heard at all, and even then don't bother.

**Eyes and skin:** Don't stare up the shaft. Squint, look sideways at the lit drops where jets cross the beam — glass-clear, lit from inside — then look away. Polarized squinting is enough for most people. Don't rub eyes with basin water. Fresh plaster blisters and keeps blistering; that dipper left out will leave a mark. If you have a cut, it will sting — that's the lime. Get out and rinse with your clean water.

**Heat:** You will start sweating immediately and it won't help. Stratification means your shoulders bake while your feet are in slightly cooler night-cooled feed water. Dizziness, nausea, pounding head, sudden irritability, feeling strangely cold or stopped sweating: that's heat exhaustion. Exit immediately. Plan to limit yourself to 5-10 minutes first time, 15 absolute max. This is not a place to prove endurance.

**Don't touch the life.** Leave the algae felt, the white root threads, the olive film. It'll be back in a week if scraped — that's the tender's job, not yours. Don't pull roots from mortar joints, don't peel film, don't chase what isn't there. And absolutely: don't pee, spit, blow your nose, or rinse sunscreen in the basin. It's a cistern cell. Everything you add goes downhill to someone's mouth.

**Don't touch the grate from below except to save life.** Leaves matting the grate dim the light and raise the level — again, tender's job to clear by hand from above. Don't climb on the adze-rough beams, don't hang, don't shake them. Condensation raining back down is part of the cycle.

**Ascend:** Signal your spotter, wait for a lull in chop, go to the steps. Wring out, dry hands as best you can on your own suit — not on the cloth stopper of the jar. Climb facing the ladder. Expect rungs to be slick, arms to be shaky from heat and noise. Up top you will walk straight into white glare and courtyard shouting with no transition. Have hat, sunglasses, drinking water ready. Sit in shade.

### 6. After

Rinse promptly with your clean cool water, especially eyes, mouth, any scrapes, armpits and groin where lime water lingers. Dry fully. Drink — you lost more water than you think because sweat was invisible under basin water. Cool down before walking far in noon heat.

Check: eyes red/burning more than an hour, rash, cut turning hot/red, ear pain/fullness, headache that doesn't lift with water/shade — get medical help where you are and say: warm, high-pH, stagnant-warm but high-turnover cistern water, brief immersion.

Leave the courtyard catchment as you found it. Don't toss trash, leaves, stones near the grate. The level breathes with what falls on the stones uphill.

If you do it right, what you'll remember isn't much in words: ribs-deep in roaring, blood-warm water, nets of sun crawling over wet lime, hemp and clay and basalt spaced out on a bench, the roar filling a stone cup so completely there's no room for thought — and right above the beams, ordinary day going on, unaware this loud pocket of water and sun is working under its feet.
