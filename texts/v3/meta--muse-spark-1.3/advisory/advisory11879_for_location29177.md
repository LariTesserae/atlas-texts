You do not hike to the Summit Cistern. You surface into it.

One minute you are in cloud, cold thin air rasping a little, light gone white and diffuse. Then you step up and over, and your feet are suddenly not on rock at all but on tile and steel under warm water, and you are chest-deep in a bath that should not be there.

Prepare for that inversion: cold outside, bath inside. High and exposed outside, small and packed inside. Loud wind and then almost no sound at all. Your body and your expectations will both need a minute.

This is a small place you cannot get lost in and a dense place you can barely move in. Treat it like a living engine room you have to wade, not a trail you walk.

### 1. Understand what you’re getting into

There is no dry inside. The bowl is about three meters deep and full to the brim. Floor, walls, rim, ladders — all poured concrete, tile, bolted steel, still new, still sharp. No soil, no sand, no shore. You will be standing / wading / leaning the entire time, water to chest or chin. The only place to be out of water is perched on the circular rim wall — knee-high on the inside, chest-high on the drop to nothing on the outside — moss-capped, narrow, with clouds moving far below.

The water is warm as bathwater, clear on top and green with depth. High mountain sun comes straight down and breaks into slow ribbons. Steam hangs above the waterline.

Under and around you is a packed drowned factory under a packed drowned garden, stem to stem with no gaps:

Boiler tanks, dynamos, transformer boxes, standpipes, gooseneck vents, copper coils gone green, rubber hoses fat as your thigh, switchboards blinking green and amber behind algae-filmed glass, a slow conveyor crawling inches per hour with trays of seedlings, pilot lights burning *underwater* behind little glass cages.

On every inch of that: velvet moss an inch thick, ferns, water hyacinth mats dense enough to look like lawn, lily pads the size of serving trays, duckweed, pondweed curtains, strangler figs splitting boilers, white and magenta orchids growing straight out of a fuse box.

You will be touching something with every inch of you whether you want to or not. Leaf presses against pipe presses against leaf presses against you.

Nothing is empty, nothing is fast, and nothing behaves quite right.

### 2. Your body in that air and water

**Altitude first.** Outside air is thin and cool. You will arrive slightly short of breath, your heart a little fast, sun much harsher than you expect. Acclimatize if you can before you come. Hydrate aggressively the day before, no alcohol. Bring water and drink it — you will sweat in the bath and not notice because you are wet. Headache, nausea, clumsiness on ladders over a mile-high drop is not the place to discover altitude sickness. If you are dizzy on the rim, get down inside, sit on the rim with legs in the water, breathe cool air.

**Heat + cool whiplash.** The water holds its heat even into a cold mountain evening. That feels wonderful until you get out. Then you are soaked, steaming, in thin cold wind on a sheer tower. Hypothermia after a hot soak comes fast and shivery.

Bring in a drybag clipped to you, left on the rim:
- a big quick-dry microfiber towel
- a complete dry change: merino or synthetic top and bottom, warm socks, beanie
- a windproof shell
You will want it. Do not visit in cotton. Do not plan to air-dry.

In the water, you do not need a wetsuit at noon — you’ll overheat. You need sun protection that can get soaked. Thin air + water reflection + noon sun straight down = burn on face, ears, back of neck, tops of hands in 20 minutes. Wear a wettable wide-brim hat or legionnaire cap, sunglasses with a retainer strap, UPF long-sleeve rashguard and swim leggings. Reapply reef-safe mineral sunscreen only on what must be exposed, before you enter, and let it dry — the system has no filter but itself.

Breathing in steam at altitude feels strange — thick and thin at once. Breathe slow through your nose. If the warm wet air feels suffocating, lift your face clear, turn toward the rim wind for a minute. Asthmatics: bring your inhaler in a waterproof case. The air is full of warm spores, algae, oil vapor, condensation. Not toxic, but heavy.

### 3. How to move: wade, don't swim

Do not expect normal water.

Buoyancy is tired here. Fallen leaves hang suspended for hours, turning over inch by inch. Bubbles don’t go straight up — they sidle, pause, gather trembling under lily pads and stay. You will not float normally. Inflatables, noodles, trying to do a relaxed back-float will feel wrong, effortful, slightly panicky. Fins are useless and dangerous in the tangle.

Plan to stay on your feet.

Wear:
- closed, thick-soled water shoes or dive boots with good grip and toe protection. The bottom is a maze of trays, chains, grates, oily steel, hoses like pythons. Bare feet will be cut and shocked.
- long pants/leggings and long sleeves and thin work gloves. Not for warmth — for sharpness.

Everything here is young and unsoftened. Rebar ends bristle unrusted. Fresh welds, bolted plates, valve knurling crisp. And the leaves have gone hard to meet them — fern fronds stiff as enameled tin, edges that will nick you like paper. Watercress will tangle your wrists. Pondweed curtains will brush your face and hold your legs.

Move like this:
1. Shuffle, never stride. Probe with a collapsible wading staff ahead for a grate, a hose, a sudden drop between tanks.
2. Lift straight up out of tangles, don’t kick through. If hyacinth mat closes over your legs, stop, float your knees up gently, untangle by hand.
3. Keep hands out of blind gaps. Look where you put weight. Oil-slick steel + lily roots = slip.
4. Never stand on a floor grate. Every few minutes the big pumps under the floor throb — *tuh* — you feel it in your sternum more than hear it, and a column of warm water sighs up, lifting the hyacinths. It won’t suck you down, but the surge + heat + slip is enough to knock you if you’re balanced on one foot.
5. Watch the hourly rake. Once an hour a slow rake on a track drags the length of the tank to gather deadfall into a hopper grinder. It is very slow and completely indifferent. Do not stand in its track, do not sit on its rails, do not leave a foot, staff, or bag strap where it crawls. Step over, wait.

Bring a mask/snorkel or swim goggles. The best seeing is with your face just at or under the surface, very still, watching light netting move across moss and copper. Without goggles the warm condensation + steam + sun glitter will blur you. With them, you’ll see the gauge needle twitching where a lily pad swallowed the dial.

Earplugs or a swim headband help if you get ear infections easily. Warm, plant-filled, animal-less water is not clean water — it’s full of pulp, oil sheen that never quite surfaces, copper verdigris, root exudates. Do not drink it. Do not open eyes wide underwater for long without goggles. Do not go with fresh deep cuts; cover any nick immediately with waterproof bandages + antiseptic — warm bath is excellent for infection. Tetanus up to date is non-negotiable with rebar and tin-leaves.

Bring a small waterproof first-aid: antiseptic wipes, steristrips, waterproof plasters, antihistamine, ear drops, lip balm.

### 4. What not to touch

Assume everything is either live, hot, sharp, or alive — often two at once.

**Electricity in water:** Switchboards blink patiently behind filmed glass. Copper blushes green in sun, pales at night. Orchid roots drink condensation off live bus bars. Pilot cages glow blue and amber. Treat all of it as live. Never touch a switchboard, bus bar, fuse box, transformer, or glass cage, even with gloves. Never grab a copper coil for balance. Rubber hoses are safer to steady yourself, but test first — some are hot. Use your staff, use a boiler flank covered in moss, not bright metal or anything humming. If you have a pacemaker or other implanted electronics, keep distance from transformer boxes and pump grates; the 60-cycle throb you feel in your teeth in the evening is real field.

**Heat:** Pilot lights burn underwater. The glass around them and the water immediately around them where tropical mosses crowd like around campfires can scald. Hot metal ticks as it contracts underwater. Test with back of hand before leaning. Gooseneck vents may burp warm gas with a soft *plik*. Don’t put your face over them.

**Life that cuts:** Don’t brush ferns with bare forearms. Don’t push through vines with your neck. That verdigris? Copper salts — don’t rub eyes after handling, don’t ingest. That oil rainbow that never surfaces? Don’t wash your face in it; push surface duckweed aside to a clear patch if you must rinse.

A blunt-tip river knife on your PFD or belt is useful solely to cut yourself free if pondweed wraps an ankle — not to cut plants. You won’t need to cut often if you move slow.

### 5. The strangeness to psychologically pack for

**Silence.** You expect roar from industry and get felt hum, ticking, soft knocks of lily against standpipe, your own breath loud in steam. No fish, no snails, no beetles, no larvae, no birds, no buzz. Leaves fall and no mouth eats them; machines prune instead. Many people find animal-less lushness deeply uncanny after ten minutes — too green, too tended. If you’re used to wildlife as reassurance, you’ll feel watched by plants and pumps. That’s normal here.

**Density with no privacy.** There is no empty water. You cannot extend an arm without tangling watercress and brushing warm cable. If you need personal space or are claustrophobic in vegetation, practice staying still and letting contact be contact. Panic thrashing tangles you more.

**Time slowed.** A valve opens a quarter-turn per season. Rust blooms millimeters. A fig root widens a crack a hair a year. Nothing will perform for you. The reward is if you stand very still, water at chin, sun netting on your face, and watch the whole bowl breathe almost imperceptibly in, out. Bring patience as gear. Plan to stay at least through slant light, when pilot cages brighten and green deepens — that’s when it makes sense.

**Vertigo.** From anywhere you can see the rim and beyond it only sky and cloud tops far below. It is beautiful and wrong — a sea perched where no sea should be. Don’t lean out over overflow spouts that have never overflowed. Sit to look out, keep hips inside the rim. Wind up high can gust. Gulls should be screaming and aren’t.

### 6. Etiquette: it tends itself

There was never wilderness here to negotiate with. Rules are simple because the system is closed:

- Water stays. Level never drops a finger’s width. Evaporation beads and falls back as warm rain that never quite breaks tension. Don’t drink, don’t bail, don’t splash out. Don’t pee in it. There are no toilets — go before you come, pack out everything.
- Everything roots in the made. No earth. Do not bring soil, do not introduce plants, seeds, snails, fish, bait, bread. Clean and dry all gear, shoes, staff, mask before entry to avoid hitchhikers. Shower off heavy sunscreen, bug spray, lotion. This place has no predators for newcomers.
- Machines prune. Don’t pick orchids from the fuse box, don’t peel moss, don’t harvest seedlings from the conveyor, don’t turn valve wheels — a quarter-turn is a season’s work. Don’t feed the hopper. Don’t leave food, hair ties, plaster wrappers for the rake to eat. If you free a tangle, lay it gently back.
- Look with hands behind back around live electric florals. Photos are fine — noon light through three meters of green and iron is extraordinary — but bring a floating strap, a lens cloth for steam, and keep flash off underwater; it flattens the ribbons and startles no one but ruins the mood.

Buddy system. No lifeguard, no staff, no rescue. Go with one other human, stay in sight / voice. The hum masks voices; agree on a whistle signal — three blasts — and carry a pea-less whistle on your PFD. Tell someone outside where you went.

### Pack list for an ordinary human

Worn in:
- swimsuit + UPF rashguard + swim leggings
- water shoes, grippy + thick sole
- thin coated work gloves that grip wet steel
- hat + sunglasses + retainer
- mask/goggles, snorkel if you like chin-deep watching

In a small drybag that floats and clips to wrist/belt:
- 1L+ fresh water + salty snack in waterproof pouch (drink on rim, never in water)
- microfiber towel, dry change, wind shell, beanie
- waterproof headlamp (for evening pilot-light hour)
- first-aid + ear drops + antiseptic
- wading staff, collapsible
- whistle, floating phone/camera strap
- spare contact lenses/glasses cloth, reef-safe sunscreen stick

Do not bring:
- fishing gear, nets, floats, inflatable toys, soap, shampoo, speakers, drones low over water, pets, glass bottles, anything you can’t carry out wet.

### How to enter and leave well

Enter over the rim wall by ladder, slow, facing the wall, testing each rung — tile + moss is slick. Lower yourself to knee-deep, then chest-deep, pause. Let your body adjust to warmth after cold air. Let your eyes adjust to glitter. Stand still for two full minutes before trying to go anywhere. Feel the pump throb. Listen for ticking.

Then pick a line between boilers and follow it inches at a time. Touch minimally, with backs of gloved hands. When a warm upwelling lifts the hyacinths, stop and ride it. When a bubble cluster shivers under a pad beside you, watch it instead of popping it.

To leave, go back to rim, rinse hands/arms/face with fresh water if you have it, towel hard, change completely even if sun is still warm. You will steam in cold air and think you’re fine — you’re losing heat fast. Warm drink if you carried a thermos to the rim base outside. Look back once: factory, garden, warm bath holding its heat into the cold evening, humming so quietly you feel it in your teeth.

Leave no ripples that weren’t there.
