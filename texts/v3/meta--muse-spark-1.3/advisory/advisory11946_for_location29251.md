You do not acclimatize to the Weeping Table. You change state to meet it. Think of it less as a summit and more as entering a hothouse that happens to be a mile in the sky, with no door and cliffs for walls.

An ordinary body can go there. You just have to stop preparing for a mountain and start preparing for a lowland jungle that got lost at 4,000 meters, plus a long, cold climb to reach it, plus machines you do not understand.

This is how to do it.

### 1. The Approach Is The First Expedition

You climb for days to get there. That part *is* a normal mountain: thin, cold wind, exposure, a sea of cloud below you. Then you step over the lip and it is bath-hot, soup-thick, and soaking.

You need to carry two kits in one pack and change at the edge:

* **For the climb:** real mountaineering layers, wind shell, warm insulation, hat and gloves, UV sunglasses, lip balm, mountaineering boots, rope / helmet / harness if your route requires it, cold-weather sleeping system for camps below the Table.
* **For the Table:** you will overheat and drown in sweat in 10 minutes if you stay in that. At the lip, stop. Change into hot-wet kit, stow the cold kit in a drybag. You will want it again on the way down.

Do not underestimate the cliffs. They are a mile deep, old and black with lichen, but every interior face weeps. Rock that looks dry is filmed with water that crawls sideways. Solution cups, flutes, whale-backs - all of it is slick as soap. Stay back from the outer lip. Lichen maps are slippery when wet, which is always. Test the rope bridge - the mossy one with orchids growing through the ropes - like it will fail. It is still holding, but it is not maintained. Probe, load gradually, one person at a time, harness on.

Plan to be slow on top. They say several hours to cross if there were trails. There are no trails. There are quaking sedge marshes, tea-pools, black channels, nurse-logs, and rock spines. Budget 1 km an hour in the basins. Budget days, not hours, and carry food for double what you think.

Never travel alone here. Voices carry impossibly far over marsh and then cut dead at a forest edge. You can lose a partner by 30 meters.

### 2. Your Body In Hot, Thick Air

The first breath *will* feel wrong. Heavy, sweet, perfumed, faintly metallic, like breathing through a hot towel left on flowers and rot. Your lungs expect thin cold and get warm soup.

For a healthy person this is not immediately dangerous - the Needles are somehow holding dense, tropical air up high, so you won't get classic altitude sickness on the Table itself. You *will* get:

**Heat illness.** Midday steam rises off every pool. Ground-mist hangs a meter up and never burns off. Sweat starts at your hairline and never stops, and because nothing ever dries, you won't realize how much water you're losing.

* Drink on schedule, not thirst. 5-7 liters a day capacity.
* Bring electrolytes, lots of them. Water alone will make you sicker in this much sweat. Salty food + electrolyte mix.
* Rest 11am - 2pm. Find ridge shade when the light goes white and the steam ribbons start. That shimmer is your warning.
* Wide-brim hat, light-colored long sleeves, high SPF. That ordinary yellow-white sun is diffused but you are still very high. UV will burn you through mist. Sunglasses even when it looks soft.
* Know heat exhaustion: headache, nausea, goosebumps in heat, stop sweating, confusion. If that happens: shade, fan, wet cloths, electrolytes, stop moving. There is no clinic. There is no helicopter landing in a quaking bog under cliffs.

**Breathing and allergies.** The light has texture - spores, seeds, insects, peat dust, orchid perfume, ozone. If you have asthma, reactive airways, serious pollen/mold allergy, reconsider. If you go:

* N95 or similar respirator for when you kick up sphagnum or disturb logs. Not all day - too hot - but carry it.
* Antihistamines, nasal spray, asthma inhaler even if you rarely use it, eye drops.
* The metallic taste is real. Do not deep-breathe to "get used to it." Breathe normally, nose if you can to filter.

**Skin never dries.** This is the thing that breaks people.

* No cotton. No jeans. Quick-dry synthetics or thin merino, long sleeves and long pants. You want to keep saw-edged sedges, sundew glue, sun, and biting golden flies off you while still venting.
* Feet: you will be ankle to knee in marsh. Bring waterproof knee gaiters + quick-drain trail shoes or bog boots with aggressive wet-rock grip, plus camp sandals. Bring 3-4 pairs of socks in separate drybags and change as ritual. Wash, dry as best you can, antifungal powder, air feet when camped. Trench foot and crotch rot happen fast here.
* Hands: saw cuts get infected instantly in warm peat water. Gloves for pushing through sedge, antiseptic, waterproof bandages, antibiotic ointment.
* Fungal kit: antifungal cream + powder, jock/skin lube to prevent chafing.

Bring strong insect defense: picaridin or DEET for skin, permethrin pre-treated clothes, headnet for golden flies at dusk, tight-weave bug net for sleep. Do not scratch bites open - everything is warm and alive.

### 3. Ground That Is Mostly Water

Water does not run here so much as hang. The water table kisses the surface.

* **Probe everything.** Use trekking poles like antennae. Sedge marsh quakes when you step near - that's floating mat over water/peat. Test, spread weight, step on tussocks and giant rosette bases, not black gaps. If your pole sinks to the handle, go around via rock spine.
* **Travel on rock when you can.** The dark limestone/basalt spines - whale-backs, ridges - are your highways. Slower up and down but solid.
* **Cross black channels carefully.** They look still. Duckweed leaning tells you they move. They are narrow, deep, tea-black, banks undercut and soft. Unbuckle your pack hipbelt for crossings, pole downstream.
* Nothing holds a footprint. Peat accumulates a millimeter a year and remembers everything. Walk single file on the most durable line, don't cut new paths through rosette gardens and sundew mats. You cannot put a foot down without stepping on ten living things - so step where ten thousand have already been stepped on: rock.

Camp *only* on ridges, never in basins:

Ground camping in marsh = you wake in water. Use a hammock with full tarp + bug net slung between those gnarled, house-high cloud-forest trees. Check branches first: every branch is a garden a hand deep in moss, ferns, orchids, bucket bromeliads with tree-frogs living in them. Don't crush the garden to hang. Use wide tree straps, not nails or screws.

Do not camp in or use the abandoned hermit shelter. The notebooks went to pulp for a reason. That's mold that eats paper - imagine lungs. Same for the collapsed dome on the eastern ridge: beautiful ribs open to sky, floor that repairs itself smooth and grey and warm. Don't sleep on it, don't cook on it, don't cut it. Look, leave.

### 4. Drink, Eat, Don't Forage

All that water is not drinkable as-is. Tea-colored pools under sphagnum, drip-films on cliffs, bromeliad ponds - all alive with frogs, salamanders with red lace gills, upside-down rowing beetles, toad chorus, peat tannins, possibly metals from the weeping rock.

* Carry in what you can for the climb. On the Table, filter + chemical + boil if you can. Filter alone won't take taste/tea color or dissolved minerals. Bring a pre-filter for duckweed/peat bits - it clogs fast.
* Do NOT drink from bromeliad bucket-ponds. That's someone's entire house.
* Be extra cautious drinking drips near Needles, cairns, lozenges, dome. If water tastes strongly metallic/sweet near black material, don't use it. Move 100m upslope to a rain-fed rosette heart and still treat it.
* Bring a stove + enough fuel for the whole Table stay. There is no dry wood. Everything is a nurse-log sprouting seedlings and half-dissolved into peat. Don't burn it.

Food: bring calorie-dense, salt-inclusive, no-cook tolerant food in odor-proof drybags + hang it. Those long-nosed coati-like troops have no fear. They will stare, then open your pack while staring. Hang food hammock-style, 3m up, away from sleep. Lizards, parrots, fist-sized hummingbirds - watch all you want, feed none, touch none. Transparent frogs with visible hearts absorb oil/sunscreen/DEET through skin and die. Look with eyes.

Flowers that only open in heat - yellow, magenta, violent orange - and many orchids: assume toxic, irritant, or ecologically precious. Don't eat, don't pick, don't make tea.

### 5. Noise, Smell, Sleep

You hear the Table before you see inside: cicada sizzle so constant it becomes pressure in the teeth, plink-plink of drip rock, *whoop...whoop* of toads from the mud, parrot screams at dusk.

Then late afternoon: light goes gold through steam, pools go mirror then white, scent intensifies to honey-rot narcotic, toad chorus swells till ground throbs. It's gorgeous and it will give you a headache and keep you awake.

* Earplugs + spare earplugs. Eye mask - dawn comes bright and diffused.
* Don't fight the scent. If it feels thick/headachy, move to ridge wind, drink water, breathe through shirt.
* Don't night-hike alone to find the rustling in leaf litter. That's the troops turning moss for grubs. Let them.

### 6. Navigation When Compasses Lie

Compasses spin lazily and settle 20 degrees off. Shadows cast a faint double at noon - a paler second shadow a finger's width east that fades if you stare. Your voice carries too far then stops dead.

* Do NOT trust magnetic compass or sun-angle alone. Bring GPS with extra batteries in drybags + paper map + altimeter, and navigate by the seven Needles around the rim and major rock spines. Count them. Sketch as you go.
* Mark your entry lip carefully - cairn photo, GPS waypoint, flagging you remove on exit. All cliff edges look the same in mist and all drop a mile to cloud.
* Travel ridge-to-ridge in daylight only. Start early, be camped by that gold hour. Steam + double shadows + marsh mirrors make depth perception lie.
* Buddy visual contact, whistles plus hand signals + bright clothing. Don't rely on shouting.
* Expect electronics to glitch near artifacts. Thin rods with hovering motes of light that stay put in wind, cairns with veined black lozenges where light moves like blood, Needles with heat-haze above cool stone. If your GPS/headlamp flickers, step back 20-30m. It usually clears.

### 7. The Machines: Look, Don't Touch (Much)

Everyone touches a Needle once. It's warm as skin, faintly yielding, your fingerprint stays a second as pale afterglow, and you feel the low hum in your sternum beating against the cicadas.

Better plan: don't, or touch once briefly with back of hand and then don't sleep near one.

Practical rules from people who have watched travelers get weird here:

* Don't camp within sight/hum of a Needle. That low note, the distorted air above it on still afternoons, the 11-year pulse the frogs sing louder to - you don't know what long exposure does. Headache, nausea, vivid dreams, pacemaker interference have all been reported around similar sites. If you have a pacemaker, epilepsy, heart arrhythmia, migraine disorder - keep distance.
* Don't move, stack, or pocket cairn slabs or black lozenges. Don't try to pry the hovering motes. They stay in place in wind for a reason you don't understand.
* **Step-fields:** patches where your footfall lands a half-second late, a soft lag in the world. If you feel it - sudden slight nausea, foot doesn't match eye, sound delays - stop. Back out the way you came. Mark it on GPS. Do not try to cross to see what happens in the middle. People get motion sick, stumble into bog, panic.
* **Up-running water:** on certain smooth faces droplets merge and run *up* a shallow incline to vanish into moss. Fascinating. Not drinking water. Do not follow it with your hands - slippery, and you'll end up feeding the moss.
* Leave the dome floor alone. Those seams where moss was pushed aside by smooth grey warm growth - that's repair. Don't cut, sample, or scrape it. Observe, photo, leave.
* Wash hands after any contact with black material. Don't eat with that hand first. If afterglow persists more than seconds, or skin tingles/numb, rinse lots, note location/time, and get checked after descent.

This is a cistern and a garden in long balance, says the lowlander story. Act like a guest in someone else's garden: no souvenirs, no new cairns, no carving rock spines, no washing soap in pools. Pack out everything - peat buries lost sandals for centuries.

### 8. Packing - Table-Specific Additions

On top of normal multi-day mountain kit:

* Shelter: hammock + tarp + bug net + wide straps, not a ground tent. Light sleeping liner + thin quilt, not winter bag for the Table.
* Drybags inside drybags. Nothing ever dries. Assume rain even when it doesn't rain - mist condensing on leaves falls, then rises as steam. Pack liner, phone/GPS in waterproof case, notebooks in waterproof paper (normal paper becomes pulp).
* Shoes: quick-drain hikers with soft wet-grip + knee gaiters + 3-4 socks + camp sandals. Trekking poles essential.
* Water: high-capacity filter + pre-filter + chemical backup + collapsible bottles + stove + fuel.
* Food + electrolytes + odor-proof bags + hang line.
* Clothes: sun hat, sunglasses, 2x long-sleeve quick-dry shirts, long pants, rain shell (for warmth in wind, not because you'll stay dry), buff/headnet.
* Health: first aid + blister + saw-cut care, antiseptic, waterproof bandages, antihistamines, inhaler, eye drops, antifungal powder/cream, DEET/picaridin, permethrin-treated clothes, sunscreen, earplugs.
* Nav/comms: GPS + batteries, paper map in case, whistle, headlamp + spare, satellite messenger - test it away from Needles, expect weirdness near them. Tell lowland contacts your entry/exit lip and return date.
* Respect kit: trash bags for pack-out, trowel? - don't dig catholes in thin peat near water. Use pack-out bags for human waste where required. At minimum 70m from any pool/channel, on ridge mineral soil if you can find it.

Go slow. Stand on a rock spine in that last exhalation of the day when everything steams gold and buzzes and the cliffs drop to cloud on every side - very high up and very low down in green at once. Let your sweat drip, let the hummingbirds hang unbothered in front of your face, listen to the plink and whoop under the sizzle.

You are kept, briefly, by old incomprehensible care. Prepare well enough that the Table doesn't have to notice you at all.
