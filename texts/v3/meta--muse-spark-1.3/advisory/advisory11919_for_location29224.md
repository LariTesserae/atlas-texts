You are not climbing. You are arriving on top — on something that finished being mountains a billion years ago and hasn't bothered since.

Think Puna de Atacama + Tibet + Mars, but with the physics left slightly loose. Prepare for altitude desert first, anomaly second. The ground will not hurt you. The sky will try to.

### 1. Understand what your body will be up against

**Thin, dry, cold-bright air.**
Midday ~8C in sun, stone colder under your boots. Sweat turns to chill on your neck within minutes. Overhead the sky goes bruised violet — that’s your UV warning. There’s not enough air for sound, for smell, for exertion. Every breath is cool, clean, a little short. You’ll stop to breathe going uphill 10 meters. Light feels tired and distances lie. Water doesn’t melt, it sublimates. So will you, a little.

You will dehydrate without feeling hot. Your tongue will go salty and iron-tasting, your eyes gritty, your nose bloody. Nothing rots here, it preserves — including blisters, chapped lips, and bad decisions.

This is not a place to “push through.” Plan for 1/2 your normal hiking speed, double your normal water, triple your normal sun protection.

Acclimatize before you come if you can. Spend 2-3 nights sleeping above 2,500m in your home world, do cardio, no alcohol. Bring acetazolamide if a doctor will give it to you, and know AMS: headache + nausea + stumbling + breathless at rest = abort. Jump out. There is no clinic on the Table, no one to carry you. Three days and you might see only tracks.

### 2. Water, food, cold: carry everything

There is **no water**. No creek, no seep. Salt pans the size of dinner plates, morning rime that lifts straight to sky without ever getting wet. Do not lick salt, do not suck rime, do not hope.

For an ordinary human:
* **5-6 liters per 24h minimum,** plus 1L emergency. You can’t filter, you can only carry. For a 48-hour stay that’s 12kg of water. That limits you. Accept it. Most travelers should make this a 24-36 hour visit max.
* Electrolytes + salty snacks. You’ll be blowing dust out of your nose and peeing clear then not peeing at all. Pale yellow is the goal.
* No green, no game. Pack 3,500-4,000 kcal/day, stuff you can eat with a dusty mouth: nuts, cheese, tortillas, chocolate, instant soup. The wire-grass is glass-clear and trembling in cracks, the black rosette lichen is brittle as burnt paper — they are not food, they are thousand-year-old survivors. Don’t touch.
* Stove + fuel. There is nothing to burn. And you’ll want hot drink at night more for morale and warmth than calories.

Cold management:
No cotton. Merino base, fleece mid, windproof shell that also blocks dust, puffy down for night, warm hat, sun hat, thin gloves + warm mitts, buff for mouth. Hands in pockets will not be enough. Boots: stiff, broken-in, high-ankle with rock plates. The pavement is interlocking ventifacts — wind-polished mosaic stones that click underfoot and are slick as old coins. Add gaiters. That bone-and-rust dust is so fine it gets in your teeth, your boots, your lungs.

Stone sucks heat. Sleeping: 4-season tent that pitches with rock anchors (no soil for stakes), sleeping bag rated to -10C even if forecast says 0, and *two* pads or one with R-value >5. You’ll lie on cold that is older than life.

Sun: Category 4 sunglasses + sealed goggles, SPF 50, SPF lip balm, eye drops and nasal saline. The pale stone reflects from below. You can get snowblindness with no snow. Reapply even when you feel chilly.

Lungs: N95 / P100 dust mask + 2 spare filters, bandana. That 300-meter dust wall to the west makes no roar — just a rustle like paper in another room. You cannot judge it by sound. You must look.

### 3. Navigation and comms in a place that eats sound and compasses

It’s flat until weather. Long low swells 30m high run 100km — they were once Himalayas. You’ll get lost in sameness. And the curbs-fused-to-glass road that goes 20 meters nowhere, the not-a-cairn, will tempt you to follow them. Don’t.

* Travel with a partner in visual contact. Shouting, whistles don’t carry in thin air. Agree on hand signals. Check the horizon 360 degrees every 10-15 minutes. Weather comes silent.
* Bring paper map, mechanical compass, notebook, count paces. GPS and phones *mostly* work away from structures, but near the needles watches stop then resume 3 seconds fast. Treat that as a feature: bring a cheap digital watch as a canary. If your hair stands up, small stones lift an inch off the ground and click at ankle height, you’re too close.
* Keep all critical electronics — jump beacon, PLB, spare battery — in a Faraday dry-bag, powered off unless you’re far in the open using them. Do not trust electronic navigation within 50m of a needle.
* Mark nothing permanent. Lichen grows over fallen needles; it grows millimeters per century. One boot scrape lasts longer than you.

Insertion: jump in mid-morning after the shadow-rime has lifted, in open pavement far from any vertical thing. Jump *out* from the same kind of open place. Never jump from next to a needle.

### 4. The weather: the ground is finished, the sky is not

That’s the second rule here. No rockfall, no flood, no predators. Everything dangerous is above your waist.

Dust devils: on any afternoon 40-60 of them, rope-thin to house-fat, white against blue, wandering, colliding, splitting, exploding into silent veils. They carry salt + glass fines + lichen spores. They hum without humming.

Dust wall: 300m tall, top unwinding.

Protocol:
* Goggles on, mask on *before* it hits. Crouch low behind a soft-shouldered boulder on the upwind side, pack covered, hood cinched, gloves on. Do not run — you’ll get winded in 20 steps and lose direction in zero visibility. Sit it out. It will be eerily quiet.
* If hair rises and air shivers with no wind, a devil is forming overhead or a needle is near. Look for the needle fountain — dust lifting in a slow column that never touches. Move perpendicular to the devil’s track, slowly.
* Tent: low profile, all guylines rock-anchored, vestibule zipped. Fine grit will still get in. Accept it.

Updraft birds — the high sweepers, white, gull-sized with wings twice too long, never flapping — ride those thermals. If they’re circling hard, thermals and devils are strengthening. If one passes and its shadow crosses you a half-second *late*, you are not having a stroke. Light gets tired here and time slips a little, especially near needles. Note it, log it, step back.

### 5. The needles: look, don't touch, don't camp

Slender 3-4m dark smoked-glass spires, slightly out of focus, two shadows (one that obeys the sun, one that doesn’t), perpetual shiver, orbiting pebbles, no lichen within arm’s reach, birds won’t fly over. Older than the pavement. Sometimes starlight in them at noon. Sometimes gone when you look back, leaving a clean swept circle and a confused pillar of dust. An hour later back.

They make weather. Devils are drawn to them. At night they tick — a soft glass tick you feel in your teeth, unsynchronized across the dark.

For an ordinary human with a pacemaker, insulin pump, cochlear implant, or any time-sensitive implant: do not approach within 100m. For everyone else:

* Observe from 15-20m. Five minutes max. No selfies with hand on it.
* Never put your hand near. Never try to catch an orbiting stone. Never step into the swept circle, never pitch tent within 500m and in sight if you can avoid it. You’ll get dust fountains all night and no sleep.
* Never shelter by a fallen one, even broken in pieces and lichened. They’re still somehow upright.
* If one vanishes while you watch: leave the circle immediately, note time, don’t chase. If your watch jumps, leave.
* Don’t try to census them, stack rocks on them, leave offerings, take chips. The engineering in them isn’t engineering anymore, it’s weather. Let it weather.

The road and cairn that aren’t: look, photograph, don’t move stones, don’t follow. Curb glass is sharp under gloves.

Animals: rock-mites flicker (sweeper food), three-toed night stitch-tracks that lick salt. You’ll likely see only tracks and one silent circling a day. Watch, don’t feed, shake out boots in morning.

### 6. Night

Colder, deep not bitter, black and crowded stars. Sweepers land somewhere you never find. Wire-grass stiffens with rime that will vanish without wetting. No drip, no stream, no rain. Only your short breath and, so faint you think you imagine it, papery shuffle of a devil a kilometer away in the dark.

Wear earplugs for wind, but the tick you’ll feel in molars anyway. It’s unnerving the first night. It’s normal here. Warm bottle in bag, pee bottle so you don’t have to get up into wind, headlamp with red mode (white destroys night vision for an hour in this clarity).

Don’t wander at night. The pavement looks the same, needles are invisible until you’re on them, devils still turn in the dark.

### 7. Head and heart

You’ll feel small in a way mountains don’t make you feel. Mountains are young and dramatic. This is patient. Pink ash, white caliche, black varnish layer after layer under your heel like pages. Nothing sharp remains. It can make you euphoric, then lonesome, then time-dizzy, especially with altitude headache and late shadows.

Bring an anchor: a partner, a journal, a 20-minute timer to drink, eat, check horizon, check fingers and toes. Plan a short stay and keep it. The Table rewards stillness — sit an hour and watch two devils cross and explode silently into settling grain — but punishes lingering.

Leave No Trace to an extreme: pack out *everything*, including toilet paper and food crumbs. Things preserve instead of rot. Bury nothing in salt pans — those hexagonal curls tinkle when temperature changes and crush under a boot forever. Walk on stone mosaic, not on dust-filled cracks where grass lives.

Decon on return: dump and beat all dust outside, wash sinuses, moisturize, clean filters, check boots and tent for glass fines. The dust is iron-salt-glass; you don’t want it in your home-world lungs or washer.

**Pack list in short:**
water + electrolytes, food + stove/fuel, layers + wind shell + down + 2x gloves + sun hat + warm hat + buff, stiff boots + gaiters, Cat 4 sunglasses + sealed goggles + mask + filters, tent + cold sleep system + high-R pad, headlamp + spare batteries, paper map/compass/notebook/pencils, cheap canary watch + real watch, Faraday bag for jump gear/PLB, first aid + altitude meds + eye drops + saline + sunscreen + lip balm, trekking poles, rock-anchor cord, pee bottle, trash bags.

Go to witness the oldest floor in the world turning and turning under a hyperactive mute sky. Don’t go to conquer, collect, or fix the needles. Stand long enough to see the fountain around one, feel your hairs lift, hear your watch stop, and then — step back, drink water, pull your hands back into your pockets against the 8-degree chill, and let the Table go on without you.
