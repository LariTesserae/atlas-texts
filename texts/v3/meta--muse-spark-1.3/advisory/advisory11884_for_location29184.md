You are going to a place that is high and underwater at once. Believe both halves. That contradiction is not poetry, it is the physical problem you have to solve with your body.

You will climb for two days through cold, thin air, out of cloud, ears popping, lungs working — and then you will step off sharp black scree into bathwater that comes to your chest and never leaves you. Your inner ear will never quite settle. Your brain expects up to mean dry and cold and down to mean warmer and thicker. Here up *is* down. Prepare for vertigo, motion-sickness, a weird light-headedness that is not quite altitude sickness and not quite dive-narcosis.

This is how to go and come back intact.

### 1. Train the animal first

**You must be a swimmer before you are a walker.** Everyone there swims before they walk well. There are no streets, only canals, fords, chest-deep causeways, living bridges of ribbon-weed that tremble under you, rope bridges, log bridges slick with moss. You can cross town in an hour if you know the fords, half a day if you don't. You will be wet from arrival to departure.

Practice: swim slow breaststroke with your eyes open in dark water. Practice treading water for 20 minutes without panic in warm water that holds you up too easily. The lake is denser than it should be — mineral-heavy, reluctant to let you go. It feels supportive and then claustrophobic. Things fall too slowly. You will too. Do not fight it.

Learn to freedive, a little. Children there hold their breath uncannily long and gather with eyes open in green gloom. You don't need that, but you need 30-45 seconds calm, a good duck-dive, equalizing ears gently, and the discipline to *come straight back up*. Visibility dies after three paces, after ten paces your feet disappear. Do not chase anything into the gloom.

**Acclimatize to altitude *and* heat at the same time.** The trail up is cold thinning air. The caldera is warm rain, steam, blood-warm water, air so wet and thin there are almost no birds, no insects. You will pant in water that feels like a bath. That's dangerous — you overheat while mildly hypoxic.

Spend 2-3 nights at altitude before you climb if you can. On the climb, go slow, drink more than you think. Once in the lake, slow down even more. No rushing. Currents take a morning to carry a flower a few meters. Move at lake speed or you'll get dizzy, nauseous, pounding-head sick.

Condition your feet and your skin. There is almost no sand, no soil, no rounded stones. Everything is newborn basalt: pillow lobes glass-skinned underwater, aa slag on the rim like frozen knives. Bare soles get cut. At the same time, your skin will be macerated — pruned, softened, white and wrinkled after a day because you are never dry. Steam condenses and drips all day from every roof. That combo — cuts + warm, tea-dark, life-thick, crowded-human water — is an infection factory.

Toughen soles now, then protect them there. Keep every cut obsessively clean.

Get used to dim. Midday looks like evening elsewhere. Sun is a rumor behind steam and kilometers of wet air and meters of lake. Light is green-gold, diffuse, shadowless, standing in slow pillars full of motes. Past ten paces everything is dim anyway. At "night" — only slightly deeper dim — algae smear phosphorescence on oars and ankles. People navigate by touch and memory, not sight.

Practice for a week: no sunglasses, no bright flashlights, learn to see with peripheral vision, to feel with hands and feet. Bring a dim headlamp and use it as little as possible.

Get used to muffle and drip. It is mostly quiet. Water muffles. Steam hisses faintly, leaves plink endlessly into lake, poles creak on rope, a laugh arrives late and flattened. Underwater, rock ticks and cracks as it cools, and the whole lake breathes in a seiche that takes an hour to slosh side to side. Sleep is shallow and communal on lofts with water lapping at the posts below. If you need silence or darkness or privacy to sleep, train yourself out of it. Bring earplugs but you will still hear dripping in your dreams.

### 2. What to bring, and what not to

Assume you will never be dry. Pack like a caver-diver going to a sauna, not like a hiker.

**Feet and skin:**
- Closed, thick-soled, quick-drain booties with good grip for glass rock and slime — 3-5mm neoprene dive boots with a hard sole are ideal. Plus a second pair to rotate. No barefoot bravado, no flip-flops. The cuts get infected fast.
- Lightweight long-sleeve rashguard + swim leggings or thin wetsuit top (1-2mm). The water is bath-warm, you don't need insulation, you need protection from sunless burn? No — from scrapes, spined lily undersides, hanging lines, stake fences, and from endless skin-softening. Cover up.
- Mask + snorkel. Locals swim eyes-open; you are a visitor with soft eyes in bacteria-rich water. A low-volume mask will let you see bottom structure without getting an eye infection. Bring defog.
- Nose clip and vented earplugs for long wades, plus alcohol-vinegar ear drops after every swim, antifungal powder/cream, and a serious wound kit: iodine, chlorhexidine, waterproof bandages, superglue, antibiotic ointment, oral antibiotics if you can get them, and make sure tetanus is current.

**Staying dry-ish:**
- Everything in roll-top dry bags inside your pack, then again in a pack liner. Sleeping clothes in a third bag you *never* open except on a high loft.
- Quick-dry synthetics, no cotton, no down. Two sets only — they will sprout mold and moss anyway. A light rain shell for the rim, not for the lake — you'll overheat.
- A hammock or light bivvy mat with mosquito netting? There are almost no insects, but you will sleep cheek-by-jowl on a damp loft with several thousand neighbors, plants, and drips. A personal thin mat and a synthetic quilt liner that tolerates damp is better than a sleeping bag. And a length of cord to hang everything.

**Water and food:**
- DO NOT drink the lake. It is tea-dark, green-dark, thick with life, warm, dense, crowded, with drowned hearths and old doorways below you and wall-reefs knuckled with bone. Filter + chemical + boil if you can. But fire lives only in a few places — high eastern rim kilns and tended clay hearth-platforms treated like shrines. Fuel is precious. Bring a sealed alcohol stove or a tiny canister stove you can use *on the rim only* with permission, not on the reed platforms. Otherwise bring a gravity filter and chlorine dioxide tablets for the rain/steam condensate, which is cleaner than lake water.
- Bring dense, dry calories for the climb. In town, most food is eaten raw, warmed, or fermented in lake heat: glass eels, farmed snails, trained-down weeds, gourds from raft gardens. It's nutritious if your gut adapts. For a short visit, eat small, eat with a host family, take probiotics beforehand, bring oral rehydration salts. Do not demand cooked food. Do not make your own fire on a thatch roof that is always sprouting.

**Light and nav:**
- Dim warm headlamp with red mode + a small chem-light. No spotlights. You will blind yourself with backscatter in steam and motes.
- 20m of floating cord as a buddy tether for fords and poor visibility. Underwater fences, fish traps, hanging mussel/bone lines for edible weeds are entanglement hazards.
- No compass reliance. The horizon is only steam, bottom is only gloom. Learn their words: *upsun* toward the rim kilns, *downwarm* toward the vents. Up-mountain and down-water replace north and south. Draw sketch maps by sequence and touch, not by bearing.

**Trade and tools:**
- No metal to speak of except treasured copper blades traded up from below. Do not flash steel machetes around. If you want to give, bring: good cordage (they roll lake-hemp on the thigh, they will judge yours), copper — small, useful, not ostentatious — shell buttons, good needles, waterproof cord, salt in sealed packets. Ask before cutting any pole — a fallen house-post sprouts before it collapses. Everything is foundation and garden at once.

Leave no trace that won't rot into green. It will sprout anyway.

### 3. Hazards specific to this bowl

Read these twice. They are not obvious.

**Hot rock.** The floor is faintly warm to the palm, warmer in patches where you must not stand long. In the clearest shaft you can see black lobes ticking as they cool, cracks sighing heat, white and rust mineral beards trembling in upwelling. Shuffle, don't plant. Test with a hand or stick first. Thermal ferns mark the hottest water — beautiful, dusty-green, huge in steam. Admire from a platform. Scalds in this water infect immediately.

**Bad footing and slow sinking.** Pillow-stones are rounded but glassy; aa is razor. Stakes for underwater fences are driven into still-soft lava that gives. Living walkways of ribbon-weed fronds lay flat on the surface and tremble — they *will* hold you if you distribute weight, crawl if you must. Causeways of hand-piled black stone run just awash, zigzagging shore to center, extended a meter a year by lines of men passing stones hand to hand. Stay on them unless with a guide.

And remember: things do not sink properly. Drop an adze and it falls too slowly, as if considering. Do not assume you can dive to retrieve something. Do not assume a cramp will let you sink to stand. The buoyancy is wrong. Wear no weights. Keep your lungs a little fuller than usual when wading deep.

**The dead return.** Weighted with stones, they often come back after three days, buoyed up, and must be housed instead in the wall-reefs — set into causeways with lime and shell, becoming structure. That's why causeways are pale in places. Do not be horrified, do not joke, do not touch, do not swim near fresh reef work unless invited. This is infrastructure and mourning at once.

**Entrapment and gloom.** Do not free-dive the drowned rooms alone — old doorways, intact green-furred hearths now occupied by newts. They are beautiful and they are traps in zero visibility. Snorkel only the shallows in daylight, with a buddy watching your bubbles. The deep turtle, huge and algae-backed, older than town, is harmless but startling. Blind white newts with feathery gills cluster at vents — don't handle them. Glass eels slide between stakes — watch, don't grab.

**Water-rise.** The level rises a fingernail a year as vents exhale and rain has nowhere to go. Every generation raises floors a course higher and abandons lowest rooms to eels and lilies. Your campsite from memory will be ankle-deep next year. Don't build, don't dig, don't expect permanence. Sleep high.

### 4. How to be a good guest in a reef-city

It is urban in the way a reef is urban — several thousand people cheek by jowl with plants, layered above and below water: stone footings below waterline for coolness, wattle at waterline, sleeping lofts just above, roof gardens above that, hanging gardens dripping roots straight into lake. A child can go mat to roof to neighbor's gourd raft without touching open water.

- Move slowly, ask for fords. *May I cross? Where is firm?* Never step on a garden raft like it's a dock. Never pull a hanging line to see what's growing.
- Contribute labor, not money. Pound fiber with grooved stones if women invite you. Join a waist-deep line passing pillow-stones. That meter-a-year is how town grows.
- Eat what you're given, small bites. Learn to like fermented-warm sourness. Don't complain about lack of fire. Compliment the coolness of the footings — they are proud of that design.
- Sleep where you're put, shallow and communal. Don't wander at night glow. Don't shine bright light on phosphorescence to show off. Whisper — sound carries flat and late.
- No loud expectations of distance or privacy. Everything past ten paces is dim anyway; town extends by memory: more platforms, more gardens, more green-warm water out to far rim where steam stands thickest and the world drops off into white. Memorize by story, not sight: *third creak-bridge past the white-flower lilies, left at the snail lines.*
- No collecting souvenirs. No rounded stones exist; every sharp edge is new geology learning to be stone. Take photos in your head.

### 5. Practical itinerary intuition

Day 0-2: Cold climb. Layer, wind, sun that still exists below cloud. Ears working. Camp dry while you can.

Arrival: Outer scree, first sight — flat dull mirror that does not reflect sky because there is no sky, only low lid of vapor glowing amber-green. Change. Stow everything dry. Put on wet kit, booties, rashguard, mask on forehead. Enter with a local if possible. First three paces clear, ten paces feet gone, twenty paces chest-deep and held up.

Day 1 in town: Just learn to stand. Feel bottom warmth with palm. Find upsun/downwarm. Learn two bridges, one latrine arrangement (ask — do not just go in the lake near gardens and traps). Nap high, dripped on. Let skin prune and recover.

Day 2-4: Help, swim short loops tethered, watch light pillars at midday-evening, watch lilies open white and waxy and die fast, watch thermal ferns unfurl. Listen underwater once — put head under, hear ticking, cracking, the hour-long breath. Don't stay under long.

Leave: Climb out dripping into cold. This is when people get sick — warm-soaked body hits cold thin air. Have dry warm layer sealed and waiting on the rim, change immediately out of wind, drink hot, descend slow.

Stay long enough and your soles toughen on new glass, ears learn the slow tick of geology under construction, you stop expecting distance to look far. That's the gift. Don't overstay it as an ordinary human: 3-5 nights in the wet is plenty. Your gut, ears, skin, and sleep will tell you when you've stopped adapting and started eroding.

High and drowned. Young and crowded. Warm, dim, patient.

Go slow, stay buoyant, touch gently — a city learning how to float before the mountain has learned how to be stone will let you float a little while too, if you let it hold you.
