This is not a hike. Do not prepare like it is a hike.

The Table is high, finished, and thin. If you go as an ordinary human — soft lungs, soft time-sense, needs food and water and sleep every day — you have to prepare your body, your kit, and your expectations for a place where the ground is done moving but reality is not.

Here is what it is like, and what that means.

### 1. Your body first. The air and the cold.

It's a continent lifted up. Think 12,000 - 15,000 feet, even if no one has measured it. The air is thin and goes bright and hard in your lungs. You will be short of breath on slopes that don't even look like slopes — long swells worn down from mountains. Your breath shows even at noon in summer. At night frost feathers everything.

It's not killing winter cold. It's patient cold that never leaves. Damp cold. Fog to the waist every morning, condensation beading on everything by midday and falling off with a ticking sound, brief shredded afternoon rain, frost again by evening.

Prepare:

**Acclimatize before you go.** If you can, spend 3-4 days sleeping high in your own world first. You will get a headache, you will be stupid and slow for the first two days on the Table. Plan for that. Walk half what you think you can. The grit squeaks underfoot, the stone pavement is frost-cracked into polygons that turn ankles. Nothing is jagged, nothing slides, but it goes on past the horizon and makes its own weather. You can walk for days and see nothing change except the light. That exhausts people more than climbing.

**Dress for wet-cold-wind-sun all in one day.** Wool against skin — synthetics will stink and stay clammy, wool still warms when beaded with condensation. Windproof shell, always. Warm hat even in summer, gloves for mornings/evenings handling wet stone. Sturdy, stiff, waterproof boots with ankle support for bog causeways and hidden trickles under a foot of moss. Gaiters. Sunglasses and sunscreen and lip balm. The sun looks ordinary, pale white-yellow, but it has farther to fall — that's less air above you. You will burn fast, especially with white fog reflecting.

Your lungs will hurt. Bring a buff/scarf to breathe through in the morning fog. If you have asthma, bring more medication than you think and a plan to leave early. There is no rescue here. No dogs, no horses, no helicopter. Only human feet.

Do not bring animals. No service dog, no pack goat. There is nothing for them to eat, no vets, no other animal sounds at all — no birds, no flies, no worms. Just wind in moss like breathing through cloth, your own joints, water ticking. Animals get distressed in absolute silence in that frequency. Leave them home.

### 2. Water and food: bring everything, trust almost nothing.

There are no streams as you know them. Water is dampness that comes and goes. Morning white sea of fog, midday beads, afternoon rain that never reaches the ground, warm cisterns at dawn even after freezing nights, and tarns — shallow, black, perfectly still pans in stone bowls with no inlet or outlet, fed only by air.

You can drink, but you have to be picky. This is the rule the locals live by:

**Drink only running or daily-condensed water. Never drink from a tarn showing you a different sky.**

In practice that means:
Carry 3-4 liters capacity and a way to harvest dew. A large clean tarp or poncho you can spread at night and shake off / wipe in the morning into a bottle. A clean cotton bandana to wipe grass and wring. That morning condensation is your safest source.
Small filter + chemical backup for what you do collect. The tarns taste of stone and sky and look impossibly clear, but sometimes they reflect two moons when there is only one. Sometimes you see old shorelines ten feet above the water and stairs going down ten feet below. Do not swim, do not dive, do not follow stairs. If reflection does not match the sky right above you, walk away thirsty.
Do not drink warm cistern water at dawn. Let the locals have theirs. Take only what condenses that day.
You will wade through fog to the waist. Keep your water bottles inside your pack in a dry bag, not outside where condensation and frost will freeze the caps.

Food: there is nothing to hunt, forage, or buy reliably. No birds, no sheep, no fish, no worms in the soil, no flies on middens. The green is cushion-plants, deep moss, black-green liverworts, ankle-high hundred-year-old dwarf willow, rust and bone-white and sulfur lichen. In late summer — which lasts about two weeks — everything exhales spores at once and the hollows steam faintly.

You cannot live off this. Lichen takes expert preparation to not poison you, peat is not food. The towns have granaries and sleeping platforms for thousands, but you will find three hearths smoking in a town of three hundred houses. An old woman scraping lichen into a pot. A man resetting the same stone straighter. Very few silent children stacking pebbles.

Bring all your calories: dense, no-cook, low-water food for at least twice as long as you plan to stay. Nuts, cheese, butter, dried meat if you eat it — but do not offer meat to locals or eat it ostentatiously. They do not keep animals and do not miss them. It will confuse and distress them, like asking where the second sun went. Bring a stove and all fuel. There is no wood. Peat fires are few and precious. Do not cut living turf for fuel.

Bring a P100 / FFP3 respirator and antihistamines. When the hollows steam with spores, you do not want that deep in your lungs. Do not camp in the deepest greenest hollow during spore season if you can avoid it. If you must, mask up. If you kneel and put your ear close you will hear water trickling under moss and if you stay still you will see it grow — tendrils creeping an inch an hour, fronds unrolling. It will cover a dropped tool, a fallen wall, a body within a season, gently. Keep your kit off the ground on slabs, shake it daily, dry it at midday. Everything left still gets mossed.

### 3. Sleep and shelter: get heavy and get low.

Frost every night. The green freezes stiff and tinkles faintly if wind touches it. Towns go dark except one or two peat fires guttering low.

**Sleep under turf if you can.** This is not folklore. A roundhouse with a turf roof a foot thick, a sleeping platform, stone walls — the moss muffles the doubling and you will dream quieter. Ask quietly to shelter in an empty house or granary. Do not just take over. Speak softly. Sound does not carry right here and loud noise feels dangerous, physically wrong.

If you must tent: freestanding tent, no quarry, no new pits. Frost-heave churns the soil every night in place of worms — stakes will pop by morning. Weigh the tent down with pack and stones taken from a fall, not from a wall. **Do not build with new stone. Take from a fall. The Table does not like new holes.** That includes fire rings, windbreaks, tent anchors. Use what has already fallen.

Bring a winter sleeping bag even for summer, plus a closed-cell foam pad plus inflatable. The stone and bog suck heat. Earplugs help some people, but others need to hear the slow crack of stone contracting and peat settling to know they are still where they fell asleep. Try both.

Keep weight on you. The old people stack stones not to build higher, but to pin the place down. "Weight helps," they say. "The light places drift." Carry a heavy pack, heavy boots, sleep with your pack against you. Do not travel ultralight in spirit. Be heavy, be deliberate, stay on causeways of flat slabs across the bog. Do not hop lightly from stone to stone like you can outrun the place.

### 4. Navigation and time-weather: your map will lie.

This is the hard part to prepare for because you can't train for it, only agree beforehand not to fight it.

Distances do not hold. A town four hours away yesterday is six hours away today, though the road has not moved. If you climb high to survey you may see the same broken ring of tall slabs in three valleys at once; walk to the nearest and the other two are gone. Fog sometimes flows uphill, or stops mid-air in ribbons all day, solid enough to cast shadows. Echoes arrive before the sound — you will hear your footstep a half-second before your foot lands.

And the doubling: on quiet days, especially thick fog, you will see another settlement superimposed on the empty one. Same walls but whole, taller, crowded. Smoke from every roof. Figures moving with no footprints, no sound. If you stand very still and don't look directly, you can faintly hear a market, a song, stone on stone, hundreds of hands. Then wind shifts and it's gone, just the empty terrace and one man rebuilding the same wall.

No one is afraid of this. Treat it like weather. Because it is weather here. The Table is large enough to have weather of its own, and some of that weather is time.

How to travel in that:

**Travel in the midday window.** Morning: stay put. Let the white sea pour through. That's when doubling is strongest. Midday after burn-off to late afternoon is your walking time. Evening: be sheltered before frost. Do not navigate in thick fog unless you must. Do not walk through a fog ribbon that is casting a shadow. Walk around.

**Do not trust high views, compasses, or GPS for truth.** Bring them, plus paper map, notebook and pencil (ink freezes, batteries die in cold), but use them as suggestions. Navigate by immediate causeway, terrace, field wall to next hollow. Count time, not miles. Expect to be wrong. Tell yourself before you go: I will not chase the nearer tower. If you see three, pick one by need — water, shelter — not by mystery.

**Leave cairns alone.** They are not markers. They are weights. Do not build new cairns, do not knock them down, do not add a stone for luck. If you need to mark a route, use temporary biodegradable flagging you take back with you, and expect moss to cover footprints fast. Photographs help for morale but compare them loosely — a tarn photo may show a different sky than you remember.

**If you hear voices before mouths move, keep walking. Do not answer an echo that comes early.** This includes your own echoes, children's babble with no mouths moving, market sounds. Keep your eyes soft, don't stare directly at figures. Stand still, breathe, let wind shift. Do not follow, do not call back, do not record and play back loudly. Walk on quietly or sit down and wait.

Bring a partner if you can. Not for safety from people — the people are quiet and nonviolent — but for reality-checking. Agree on simple protocols: "Do you hear that now?" "What time did we leave?" "Do you see two moons in that water?" Two ordinary humans agreeing to stay heavy and quiet do better than one brilliant soloist trying to solve it.

Bring visual, not audible, signals. Whistles and shouts don't carry right. Bright cloth, mirror flashes at midday.

### 5. People and manners: be quiet, be useful, be uncurious about certain things.

They are few, quiet, and organized. Layer upon layer of dry-stone walls, terraces to trap damp, cisterns lined with clay, streets worn in stone by centuries of feet. No metal to speak of except green copper pins. Stone mauls, basalt flakes, bone awls, woven willow, felted hair. Primitive work, but a city in scale.

They mend more than they build. That man taking a stone down and setting the same stone back a fraction straighter, as his father did — that is not futility. That is maintenance against drifting.

To be a good guest:
Speak quietly, move slowly. Do not play music, do not run engines, do not shout across valleys. Children stack pebbles into towers and knock them down without a sound. Match that energy.
Ask before taking water, shelter, or passage across a terrace garden. Offer useful gifts, not shiny trash: good cord, needles, wool cloth, salt, dried fruit, work gloves, help with a wall. Do not flaunt metal tools or tech. If you use a steel knife, do it discreetly and put it away.
Do not ask where the animals went, where the antler picks came from, why the granaries are empty. They look puzzled because for them there was never a second sun. Let it be.
Do not take stones from walls, even loose-looking ones. Take from a fall on the ground if you need a weight or seat. Do not dig new holes for latrines in green hollows — use stone pavement areas away from water and cisterns, pack out everything you can, bury minimally where moss will quickly cover. No flies means no natural cleanup crew as you know it, but moss judges no one and covers everything.
If offered lichen stew or peat tea, accept a small amount politely if your gut can handle it, but do not rely on it. Better to have your own food to share.

They stack stones to pin the place down. You can help by being a good weight for a night: stay, sleep, mend a little, leave the wall straighter than you found it.

### 6. Pack list for an ordinary human.

Worn/carried: thermal base layers (wool), mid fleece, wind/waterproof shell, insulated jacket, waterproof pants, warm hat, sun hat, buff, two pairs gloves, stiff waterproof boots, gaiters, sunglasses, sunscreen.

Sleep: -10C / 15F bag minimum, two pads, freestanding tent + extra guylines for weighting with stones, dry bags for everything.

Water/food: 4L capacity, collapsible bucket/tarp for dew, filter + tablets, 3000-4000 kcal/day no-cook food + extra 3 days, stove + fuel, spoon/cup, multivitamins, electrolytes.

Health: respirator, antihistamines, blister kit, altitude meds if prescribed, personal meds + extra, foot powder, lip balm.

Tools: headlamp + extra batteries (cold kills them, keep in sleeping bag), pencil + waterproof notebook, paper map, compass, watch, lighter x3, knife, cord, repair tape, bright bandana for signaling.

Mind: a book, a heavy small stone from home in your pocket. Something with weight that reminds you where you started. A routine: morning tea, midday check-in, evening notes. Routine is weight.

### What it feels like if you do it right.

Mornings you wake frozen, beads ticking off turf. You wade out into fog to the waist and wait. By midday the world burns clear and huge and finished — grey pavement shining faintly when wet, green hollows bulging like rising bread, black tarns like eyes. You walk in quiet so complete you hear your knees. You drink stone-and-sky water you harvested yourself. Afternoon rain shreds before it lands. Evening frost comes down and the moss stiffens and tinkles.

At night, lie still. You will hear what passes for wilderness: stone contracting, peat settling deeper, moss creeping over everything humans stacked up, patient, lush, cold, covering it all again. If doubling comes — smoke where there is no fire, song with no mouths — do not be afraid or greedy. Don't answer. Sleep heavy under turf and dream quieter.

You came to visit a place that is done eroding but not done remembering. Be heavy, be quiet, drink only what fell today, leave weights alone, and let time pass through like fog.
