You do not hike this place. You clock in.

Think of it as visiting the floor of a factory that was never built, where the machines are alive, the air is wet iron, and you are the only thing in acres that is not supposed to be there. Prepare like that.

### 1. Understand what will try to kill you first

It's not a predator. It's wet cold.

Air temperature is only a few degrees below freezing, but that's a lie. The air is supersaturated — too cold to hold what it holds — so water is constantly condensing out of it onto you. On stone, on breath, on fur and shell, on you. Your sleeves will rim with ice in ten minutes. Your eyelashes will stick. The ground is never dry: a quarter-inch skin of supercooled water moving in slow sheets over black glass-ice and polished stone, with hidden pans two feet deep that can spread a mile wide. You will be wet from the ankles down in seconds, and wet + wind + steel-cold stone = heat stripped faster than you can make it.

Paradoxically, the second thing that will try to kill you is heat.

Every animal here is a furnace. Grazers the size of oil drums smelt iron water inside themselves. Hunters open sideways mouths that are white-hot. Stacks along spines vent live steam that does not rise, it spills sideways and crawls. In air that freezes your lashes, you can get a scalding steam burn on your shin at the same time as trench foot. The young glow pink at the seams where their plates haven't closed. The bulls and waders carry crawling blue St. Elmo's fire between their horns.

You, at 37C, leaking 100 watts of heat and puffing steam with every breath, are a walking hot meal in a world where **heat is food, food is heat.** The scavengers — clouds of chittering crab-moths — strip waste heat off cooling bodies before the cold can steal it. Let your core drop and they will notice.

And the air itself is unbreathable for long. Hot iron, sulfur, wet hide, ozone, plus ground fog made of everyone else's exhaust. No plants means no pollen, but it does not mean clean.

Prepare for both frostbite and burn, at once.

### 2. Dress to be warm inside, cold outside

Your goal is to be a thermos with legs: hot core, cold skin, nothing leaking.

**Base to skin:** Heavy merino or synthetic thermal base, no cotton ever. Vapor-barrier socks. You will sweat despite the cold because you will be working to stay upright in slush. Sweat will kill you if it stays.

**Insulation:** Thick fleece / puffy mid-layer. Bring more than you think. Standing still in saturated cold drains you even when you’re working.

**Outer shell — the important part:** Full waterproof, windproof, sealed. Chest-high insulated waders with aggressive studded soles, not hiking boots. The stone is polished like river eggs, oiled with bacterial film and slush. It is unbelievably slick. Felt + carbide studs. Gaiters taped at the seam. A long waterproof parka with hood that goes over the waders. Everything taped. There is no dry place to sit and wring socks.

Your outer should be dull, smooth, low-emissivity if you can get it — something that doesn’t radiate your heat like a beacon and doesn’t hold static. In practice: a dissipative rain shell over insulation, with a grounding strap you can periodically touch to a stone slab *away* from animals to bleed charge. You will get shocked. When herds press flank-to-flank, static pops in chains across wet backs in running lace. Do not be the tallest conductor. Do not touch horns, antennae, or sparking backs.

**Hands, face, eyes:** Thin liner gloves under heavy waterproof mittens on a lanyard — you will need fingers for poles then need to cover them seconds later. Full balaclava. Ski goggles with double lens and anti-fog, vented but sealed. Bring Cat Crap/defogger and a spare pair in a dry bag inside your jacket, because the first pair *will* fog and ice. Your breath will freeze on them.

Carry trekking poles with snow baskets. On a plain where a three-inch rise is a landmark, poles are your eyes for hidden pans and your third legs when a boiler-plate slug drags past your calf.

### 3. Breathe through a machine

Bring a half-face respirator with combination P100 + acid gas / SO2 cartridges, plus spare cartridges sealed dry. You need it for sulfur dioxide, metal fume, ozone, and fine acidic mist. Practice walking in it before you go — it’s harder to breathe and you’ll already be stressed.

Under it: a thin cold-air mask or buff to pre-warm air. Over it: acceptance that your exhaled breath will still plume out sideways and join the low cloud. Everyone’s does. That’s the point.

Bring a pocket CO/H2S detector with audible alarm, in a waterproof case. If it screams, move upwind — which just means move, because there is no up, only sideways steam.

Bring high-calorie, no-cook food and a full thermos of hot sweet drink inside your core layers so it stays liquid. Eat and drink constantly, before you feel hungry. You will burn 500+ kcal an hour just staying warm and upright. There is nothing here you can eat or drink. Do not drink the black water. Do not lick the rust seeps. Do not eat the oily blue-purple films — they are not plants, they are iron/sulfur bacterial mats, acidic and loaded with metals. Do not eat the animals. They are literally full of fireboxes and boiling water.

Pack out all waste. Sterilize your boots and gear before and after. Nothing green has ever taken here. Do not be the first to bring a seed.

### 4. Protect ears, eyes, and sense of direction

It is works-loud. Clank, hiss, bellowing, bellows-wheeze, thousand-fold chuff-chuff-chuff of piston legs, metal-on-stone squeal, slosh of ten thousand feet, high whistles that travel half a mile, and underneath a deep thump you feel in your sternum like the basin is a drum.

Bring foam plugs *and* electronic earmuffs that compress loud peaks but let you hear close movement. You need to hear a hunter shifting behind you without going deaf in an hour. Expect your bones to learn the rhythm. Expect to hear it for days after you leave.

Bring a dim, waterproof headlamp with red mode. The light never changes: late dusk held for hours under unbroken pewter stratus. Visibility is a mile or two sideways until breath-smoke and fog eat it. Your lamp won’t pierce fog, it’s just so close bodies see you and you see ground texture.

Electronics will suffer. Cold + 100% condensation + constant static + acidic mist kills phones, GPS, and cameras. Bring them only in IP67 dry pouches with desiccant, and assume they will glitch. Bring mechanical backups: a mechanical watch (it may still act weird), a compass (it may spin near big iron bodies — trust it only loosely), and most importantly: 30-50 feet of floating bright rope to tether to your partner and to your entry point.

Because distance folds here. The far side is both two miles away and pressed against your face. You will walk ten steps toward a plume and it will be no closer, but its sound will now be behind you. Water sometimes weeps *upward* off stone into cloud. Whistles arrive before throats move. A back you were watching becomes two backs, half a second out of phase.

Do not navigate by sight or sound alone. Do not chase a landmark. Do not try to count, map, or find the edge or source. Rule 4 of the plain is literal: The flat goes on until fog takes it, and fog gives it back.

Plant a tall, lit, weighted entry beacon that can survive steam and jostling. Tie off. Never lose physical contact with your line or partner. In fog crowded with moving, steaming bodies, a person six feet away can vanish completely.

### 5. How to be in the herd

There is no room between presences. Shoulder to shoulder, flank to flank, for acres. From far it looks like cobbles. Up close it breathes.

You cannot go around. You have to go *through*, slowly.

**Keep moving, gently.** Stopping is freezing — literally the local law. Shift weight, shuffle a foot to let water pass, rotate slowly the way predator and prey trade places. Go with the local flow, never push against it. Never run. Running on glass-ice among piston legs and furnace mouths gets you fallen, and fallen means face in supercooled slush, hands on hot plate, trampled or overheated.

**Do not touch unless you must, and then only with pole or mitten back.** Everything is either scalding, freezing, electrified, or phasing. They phase through each other at the shoulder with a spark and a wet shudder. You do not want to be part of that circuit. If a slug wake is polishing toward your legs, step sideways out of its polished path and let it pass. If waders are picking through on hydraulic stilts, give their feet room. If a furnace-mouth hunter presses toward you, do not stand your ground to photograph. Move steadily away, keep a large hot bull between you and it if you can, keep your outer cold and your movement boring. You are not worth overheating if you don’t act like trapped heat.

**Make yourself uninteresting:** No bright flashing lights, no loud calls, no food smells, no open flame. Never light a campfire — there’s nothing to burn anyway, and open heat will draw a crowd you can’t feed. Use chemical hand warmers *inside* your gloves, not waving outside.

**Do not stare at condensing steam.** This is serious. If you stare too long at a thickening patch, it takes on weight and eyes and legs and walks away to join the herd — a newborn precipitated out of breath. The place makes animals out of excess. Don’t lend it your attention as raw material. Use soft focus. Blink, look away, keep walking. Same with trying to track one individual: you’ll see it double, time-stuttered, predator and prey minutes apart in the same hide. Let it go. You cannot follow one beast here.

**Fall protocol:** If you fall, get up immediately, even if it’s into something alive. Roll to a slab, pole-push up, check seals. Supercooled water will wick instantly. Change outer gloves if flooded. Drink hot. If your suit is breached and you’re soaked to insulation in wind, abort. That’s your day done.

### 6. How long to stay, how to leave

Ordinary humans are day-visitors, and short-day at that. Think 45-90 minutes inside for a first visit, absolute max a few hours with resupply and a dry shelter to retreat to *outside* the basin. There is no shelter inside — no walls, no high ground, no dry rock. A rise of three inches is a landmark. You cannot bivouac on the floor unless you want to wake up as part of the floor, covered in crab-moths stripping your last degrees.

Set a hard turn-back time before you enter and obey the rope, not your curiosity. The noise will become silence when your bones learn it. Your joints will start to ache in rhythm — chuff, hiss, step. Your footsteps will fill with black water behind you. That feeling of belonging, of being just another warm engine in the endless overlapping works, is hypothermia and sensory capture talking. Leave while you still think leaving is a good idea.

Leave the way you came, hand over hand on the line, partner in contact. Do not look for a shortcut to an edge. There is no edge. There is only fog that takes and fog that gives back. Mark your exit so it can give *you* back.

Decontaminate, dry, rewarm slowly. Expect aftereffects: the deep thump in your chest for a night, phantom crowding in an empty room, an urge to shuffle your feet to let water pass that isn’t there. Eat salty, hot food. Check feet for blanching, blisters, trench foot. Check lungs for wheeze, eyes for grit, ears for ringing. Air out everything — your gear will smell of hot iron and sulfur for a long time.

If you prepare right, you will get to witness what few outsiders ever see without dying: an ecosystem that needs no builders, no green, no sun — just iron and water and cold and life piled on life in time as well as space, keeping itself hot by never stopping.

Go cold on the outside, hot on the inside, tethered, filtered, muffled, humble, and moving. Touch nothing, drink nothing, count nothing, follow nothing. Watch the blue lace run across wet backs in the dusk that never ends, listen to the shunting-yard breathing, and then — while you still can — pull yourself hand-over-hand back out of the works, and let your breath be the last thing you leave behind.
