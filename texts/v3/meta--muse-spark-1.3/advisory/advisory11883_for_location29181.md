This is not a mountain you climb. It's a mountain you survive on top of.

Think of it as a warm, high island in the sky — a tableland so high the horizon bends and the clouds live underneath you in the morning, but so close to the equator that noon light lays flat and white and wet on your neck. You'll be out of breath and sweating at the same time. That's the first thing to get into your body before you go.

Prepare for contradiction, because everything here is two things at once: high and hot, lush and empty, soaking and always drying out, deafeningly loud and almost lifeless.

### 1. Your body will be fooled

**Thin + hot air.** At this height you should be cold and panting. Instead you'll be hot and panting. You lose water fast through sweat you don't notice because the air is already wet, and you lose judgment fast because you're hypoxic and don't feel cold-warning signs.

An ordinary human needs:

* 2-4 days acclimatizing *below* the rim if you can. Don't portal straight onto the top and hike. Your heart will be pounding on flat rock. Plan for half the distance you think you can do on day one.
* Cardio and ankles more than arms. You'll be walking endless low domes, not climbing. It's ankle-rolling, knee-aching work on gritty, sloping whaleback rock.
* Talk to a doctor about altitude. Headache, nausea, stupid clumsiness, can't catch breath at rest = descend off the table, not tough it out. Bring an oximeter if you can; the heat masks symptoms.
* Electrolytes, not just water. You'll sweat salt out constantly. Bring salty food + rehydration salts.
* Sun protection for 3,000m+ equatorial sun. This is burn-in-15-minutes sun, even when cloudy, even when raining. Glacier glasses cat 3-4 with side shields, wide-brim hat *with a chin strap* - anything not tied down shreds or flies - SPF 50, lip balm, sun hoodie, light gloves. The pale pink-grey gneiss reflects it back up under your chin.

If you have bad asthma, bad heart, severe vertigo, or panic in whiteouts, do not go alone. Frankly, do not go alone anyway.

### 2. The ground: finished. The sky: not.

The rock is old, round, polished to a shine. Domes and shallow pans for a valley's width in every direction until it just stops - a 2,000 meter straight drop into boiling cloud.

That means:

**Feet:** You need sticky rubber, not heavy leather. Approach shoes or light, fast-draining mid boots with excellent wet-rock grip. The striated quartzite is grippy when dry, treacherous as ice when that afternoon sheet hits, and always dusted with wind-blown sand. Gaiters keep grit out. Bring 3 pairs of socks in separate dry-bags. Trekking poles save knees and test hollow ground.

The stone *rings hollow* in places - thin shells over old cavities. It booms under footfalls. Don't jump between domes. Tap first with a pole. Never trust a thin lip over a crack. Potholes big enough to lie down in are perfect for a broken ankle when full of rain-mirror water.

**The rim is death.** Gorgeous, humming, warm death. Wind gusts hit with no warning strong enough to stagger you, especially when reverse-waterfall cloud pours up and over. Rock is slippery, waterfalls throw themselves off in white ribbons that turn to blinding spray. Stay low, crawl if you must, never stand at the edge for a photo in wind or rain. Give the edge 5-10 meters always. The only sharp rock is where it broke yesterday at the rim.

**Navigation:** There are no trails, only places where lichen has been worn off over centuries. Domes all look the same in flat noon light, and utterly different in shafted evening light. Visibility can go from 50 km to 5 meters in a minute when cloud boils over.

Carry: GPS with downloaded offline track + power bank in a waterproof case, compass (check it, the ironstone can be odd), whistle, bright pack cover/tarp so your party can find you. Mark your bothy/windbreak on GPS the moment you arrive. Make a habit of looking back every 50 steps - the return view is unrecognizable. Cairns with sticks and shreds of cloth are ancient crossings - use them to orient, do not build new ones or take the cloth.

### 3. Water: no lakes, no rivers, only habits

You cannot plan on water. You plan around dampness.

Morning: everything weeps. Condensation beads on rock and drips off big tin-stiff leaves, gathering in potholes enough to cup and drink.
Midday: seeps bleed dark out of cliff lines, feeding hanging gardens.
Afternoon: everything drowns for 20 minutes, every pan becomes a mirror, a hundred temporary streams hiss toward cracks and off the edge. Then steam. By evening potholes are half-empty, drunk by moss.

Do this:

* Travel in the morning. Collect at midday. Hide in the afternoon.
* Carry more capacity than you think: 3-4L collapsible + a cup. You will not find a stream to pump from.
* Treat everything. Pothole water looks pure and has frog tadpoles, moth bodies after the hatch, deer spit, moss rot. Pre-filter with a bandana, then filter + chemical. A straw filter is perfect for sipping potholes without disturbing them.
* Never camp *in* a green hollow or *in* a dry pan/crack. Hollows flash-flood and are fragile; pans become rivers. Camp on high, slightly domed rock on the lee side, near but not in a hollow.
* Don't eat the lushness. Those white-throated orchids, ginger, bromeliad cups are a closed system. The tiny hand-built potato/quinoa terraces below one dome are archaeology and still someone's heritage. Carry all your food, salty and calorie-dense, no-cook or stove-cook. You won't find dry wood.

### 4. Weather discipline: learn to watch it

Clouds here don't drift. They boil up from below, white and grey and bruised purple, impossibly fast. Light changes every minute: burn-shadow sun, green gloom, a shaft lighting one dome.

Your day:

**Dawn to 11am:** Best moving window. Cloud sea below, rock sweating, frogs giving that single glass-bell note after rain. Air still-ish. Do your mileage dome-to-dome.

**11am-1pm:** Seeps wake. Light goes white and hard. Put on shell, hat, glasses. Get water. Get to shelter-side.

**Afternoon, almost every afternoon:** Rain. Not drizzle - a sudden vertical sheet, warm as blood, bouncing knee-high off stone, drumming so loud you can't hear shouting. Temporary falls scream over the rim. Lightning flickers *below* you silently in the cloud sea - but strikes can still hit the table.

Rule: Be off exposed domes by early afternoon. Be near a low dry-stone windbreak or stacked-slab bothy. If caught on bare rock: get away from the rim and from deep cracks, crouch low on a high swell, make yourself small, wait 20 minutes. Don't try to outwalk it - you can't see and the rock is a skating rink. Don't shelter under the bent cloud-trees in a hollow - dripping green dark, deafening leaf-clatter, and flash water.

**Night:** Wind drops a fraction from organ-roar to hiss. Condensation ticks drip...drip...drip off leaves into potholes, impossibly loud. Stars close enough to hurt. Stone holds day's warmth under your back and vibrates faintly with water finding cracks. Beautiful. Also cold-damp and disorienting. You'll need a windproof layer even though the day was sweltering.

Pack: No cotton ever. Synthetic or merino base, sun hoodie, fleece/light insulated jacket, hardshell top *and* bottom with pit zips, pack liner + dry bags for everything. Quick-dry pants, not shorts - those big leaves in the hollows are stiff as tin and will slice shins, and there are spiny vines and biting damp.

### 5. Shelter and sleep

You can't stake into polished gneiss. Bring a truly freestanding tent with extra guylines and rock-bags (stuff sacks you fill with sand/pebbles) for anchors. A bothy of stacked slabs with a turf roof smelling of old smoke is a gift - use it lightly, don't improve it, don't make fire inside. Everything is stone on stone, wood, woven fiber here. No metal.

Don't make open fire. The moss mats drink water for a thousand years to live; one boot scrape or fire scar lasts decades. Use a stove, pack out everything including toilet paper. In fact bring WAG bags - there's almost no soil to dig in, and the green pits are water sources. Pee on bare rock, well away from potholes and seeps.

Earplugs are not optional. The base note - wind hitting the edge and funnelling up through chasms - you feel in your molars. Over it, wind forced through arches and flutes: a chorus of out-of-tune flutes. Rain drumming on bare rock is deafening. Shout and your voice comes back flattened, then too loud half a second later. Herders say the mountain listens. You'll understand when you put your ear to warm stone at dusk and hear it hum - wind in chambers below, breathing through its teeth. If you're sensitive to constant noise, this place will exhaust you. Give yourself quiet rituals.

Sleeping pad with good insulation: the rock gives back heat early night, then sucks it.

### 6. Green islands: visit, don't live in

Between domes are hollows where seep has collected for a thousand years and exploded: 10-foot tree-ferns thick with moss, wild ginger, waxy orchids, wrist-thick vines dropping curtain roots, wind-bent trees matted into green felt. Inside light goes green, heat closes in, smell hits - rot-sweet humus, crushed leaf, warm wet stone.

Three steps out you're back on ringing bare rock with fifty meters of nothing to the next pit. Lush and empty at once.

To enter: long sleeves, pants, hat. It's dripping even when it hasn't rained because cloud condenses on leaves. Expect mold, spores, insects. Antihistamines, inhaler if you're asthmatic, alcohol wipes for small cuts - everything is warm and wants to rot. Look, don't push in more than an arm's length. Those bromeliad cups hold thumbnail frogs you'll never see. Once a year white moths hatch in an afternoon blizzard and paper the potholes - don't drink unfiltered then, don't inhale.

Life is concentrated, hidden, holding its breath: a huge black glider tilting for an hour without a flap, seen maybe once a week. A small deer-like thing drinking at night, leaving footprints. That's it. Be still, be quiet in the lulls, and you'll be rewarded. Don't feed, don't follow.

### 7. People, past and present

People have been here lightly, long ago, and still a little now. You'll trip on: waist-high curved windbreaks, cairns, collapsing terraces, a stone quern left upside down, a digging stick polished by hands leaning on rock.

Touch nothing to take. Don't rearrange cairns, don't add your own cloth, don't sleep on terraces, don't move the quern. If you use a windbreak, leave it better - reset a fallen slab. Pack out microtrash. This is not empty desert; it's a huge room someone still prays in.

If you meet herders, you are the guest with bad lungs and soft shoes. Ask, follow, pay well for guidance. Their knowledge of which pothole holds overnight, which arch howls before rain, is worth more than any gear.

### A practical checklist for an ordinary human

* Freestanding tent + rock anchors + strong windcord
* Warm sleeping bag (nights are cooler/damper than you expect) + insulated pad
* Water: 3-4L carry, prefilter bandana, filter + tablets, electrolytes
* Food for +1 extra day, stove + fuel, no reliance on foraging
* Shoes: sticky-rubber, quick-drain + spare socks in dry bags
* Clothing: sun hoodie, wind shell, rain shell top/bottom, warm midlayer, hat with strap, glacier glasses, light gloves
* Navigation/comms: GPS offline + paper map copy + compass + satellite messenger/PLB + whistle + headlamp + power bank
* Health: sunscreen, first aid + blister kit, antihistamine, altitude meds only if prescribed, earplugs/eye mask
* Leave-no-trace: WAG bags, trash bag, trowel probably useless, no fire kit

Go slow. Drink before you're thirsty. Get off the domes before the sky breaks. Sleep with stone warmth under your back and listen to water finding its way down through ancient cracks toward an edge you cannot see.

The rock has settled into its final form and will just sit here, warm and humming, while water, cloud, wind and light rage and remake themselves a dozen times a day. The plants understand - they don't conquer, they hunker in damp pockets and grow furiously where they can, and let the vast polished emptiness go.

Do the same.
