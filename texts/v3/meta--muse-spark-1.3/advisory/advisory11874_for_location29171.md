You are not going to a forest. You are going inside a mouth that is breathing itself.

Understand that first and everything else makes sense.

This valley is a shallow black bowl, miles across, made of lava so new it hasn't learned how to be rock yet. Aa lava — tumbled, glassy, finned. Every edge is still a knife. It will be under a mat of green that lies to you about it. It will be dark in a way that doesn't lift. Cloud above, canopy below, no moon, no stars, no clearing. Warm wet air so thick you drink it. And life packed shoulder-to-shoulder in that dark, making its own rain out of breath.

You, an ordinary soft human, are a warm salt lick that bleeds easily walking into a place that is hungry for water and salt. Prepare to be drunk from, landed on, stared at, cut open, deafened, and lost — and prepare to leave no mark when it happens.

Here's how:

### 1. Your body is the problem. Dress like you know it.

**Full coverage, no exceptions. No shorts, no short sleeves, no exposed ankles, no cotton.**
Cotton will soak, stay soaked, and grind you raw. Wear quick-dry synthetics or light merino, long sleeve shirt with hood, long pants tucked into boots. Treat everything outer with permethrin days before you go, and bring DEET or picaridin for skin. You will sweat through it all in an hour. That's fine. The point isn't staying dry, it's staying uncut and uneaten.

**Feet:** This is the most important decision. You need jungle boots or sturdy hiking boots with:
- a puncture-proof plate (that lava will go straight through trail-runner foam)
- aggressive tread that drains (you will be standing in water that never flows)
- high ankle support and gaiters sealed to your pants

The ground is not ground. Ferns and moss stretch from stone-tooth to stone-tooth like a trampoline over voids. Step through and you drop shin-deep onto a razor. Probe every step with a trekking pole or stout stick before you commit weight. Walk like you're on a snow bridge over a crevasse. Because you are, but the crevasse has teeth.

**Hands and eyes:** You *will* put a hand out to catch yourself in the dark. Without gloves, that means a palm full of black glass, and in this heat and wet, every cut blooms with infection in hours.
Bring cut-resistant work gloves with good grip when wet — not thick leather that goes slick, not thin latex that tears. Bring a second dry pair in a drybag.
Bring clear safety glasses or a face shield, plus a headnet. Not for vanity. It's for the moth the size of your hand landing on your eyeball to drink, for flicked twigs, for ants dropping from above. The headnet also keeps you from inhaling gnats when you open your mouth to breathe.

**Ears:** It is deafening. Not poetic-deafening. Physical. A solid sheet of insect whine that saws at you, underlain by frog bass you feel in your sternum, overlaid with shrieks and chewing right at your ear. You will shout and not hear yourself.
Bring soft filtered earplugs — musicians' plugs, not solid foam. You want to take 15-20 dB off the damaging high whine but still hear a grunt nearby getting nearer, a branch cracking, your partner. Bring foam plugs as backup if you need to shut it out to think for 30 seconds. If you use radios, use bone-conduction headsets or throat mics. Voice in air is useless.

### 2. Air, water, heat — the invisible drowning

It's bath-warm and 100% wet. After sundown the whole bowl exhales and the air lets go as fog. Plants comb it with hairs and drip it down leaf to leaf. You will hear that ticking if you ever get a second of quiet. You won't.

That means:

**You will overheat while soaked.** Heat exhaustion in the dark in a jungle sounds wrong until your shirt is wrung out and your heart is hammering and you haven't peed in four hours. Bring 3+ liters of clean water for even a short foray, plus electrolytes — salty, sweet, the stuff you actually will drink. Drink on a schedule, not on thirst. Thirst doesn't work here because your mouth is always wet.

**Don't drink the valley.** Those pretty black cups between lava blades are full of tadpoles, rot, frog skin secretions, insect frass. Even filtered they'd be risky. Bring your water in, carry it high on your body in a bladder you can sip without using your hands. Bring a compact filter + chemical tablets as emergency backup only, and a clean bandana to pre-strain and to wring fog-dew from leaves if you are truly stuck. Assume all valley water needs purification and still tastes like tannin and frog.

**Don't breathe it raw if you can help it.** Tens of thousands of lungs, millions of spiracles, fungal spores pulsing at ankle level, mite dust, mold. It's not poison, it's just *too much biology*. A lightweight N95 or respirator buff you can pull up will cut the spore load and keep moth scales and micro-flies out of your throat. It will be hotter and more claustrophobic. Bring it anyway. If you have asthma, allergies to mold/dust mites, a history of ear infections or fungal skin issues — seriously reconsider, or make it a very short, rim-only visit. Asthma inhaler, antihistamines, antifungal cream, and a broad topical antibiotic go in a waterproof pouch on your person, not in a pack you might drop.

Watch for low pockets: in still, bowl air, CO2 and heavy damp can pool in hollows. If you get a dull headache, feel suddenly sleepy, nauseous, or your breathing feels wrong — move upslope onto a fin, sit high, rest. Don't nap in a hollow.

### 3. Light and dark: learn to see by interruption

Bring light, but don't trust it.

Headlamp with a true dim red mode + a low warm white. Red preserves your night vision and panics insects less. White on high will give you one perfect second of brilliant green and then blind you when ten thousand fireflies answer and every wet leaf flares. Bring two headlamps + a small hand torch + spare batteries in double drybags. Everything sweats here. Nothing stays dry unless it's sealed.

Better than bright light: night vision if you have it, but expect fog to white it out. A thermal monocular is actually more useful — warm bodies pop against wet leaves — but in this press it will just show you how surrounded you are, which is psychologically worse.

Practice this: you navigate by darker trunks against less-dark air, by wet shine, by eyeshine that blinks and moves a foot left. Move slowly. Stop often. Let your eyes re-adjust after every firefly burst. Put a tiny dim green chem-light on the back of your partner's pack so you can follow without shouting.

Do NOT use flash photography, strobes, lasers. You'll trigger swarms, mobbing birds, and you'll ruin your own eyes for 10 minutes when the canopy flashes back.

### 4. Moving without getting lost forever

There is no trail. The stone is too new to forget a cut, and it remembers no human cuts. Which means if you cut, stack, blaze, you will scar it for a century. Don't.

And you *will* get lost. Everything looks like dark teeth with green combed over it, repeating. Sound scrambles direction. Fog eats landmarks.

- Never go alone. Stay within arm's reach. Touch contact — hand on shoulder or pack — when the noise peaks.
- Lay a lifeline. 30-50m of thin reflective cord on a reel from your entry point, or biodegradable flagging you remove on exit plus a GPS breadcrumb track. Do both. GPS under canopy + cloud is jumpy; don't trust it alone. Bring compass and know your rim bearing.
- Time-box it. Plan 90 minutes to 3 hours max inside. Fatigue + sensory overload makes you stupid fast. Set a hard turn-around time before you enter and obey it even if you're "almost" somewhere.
- Stay high when you rest. Fins and slabs, not hollows. Hollows are where water pockets, ants, frogs, drinkers, and larger grunters converge.
- Don't grab vines to swing, don't pull saplings. The big leaves are actively cupping through the night; vines creak and settle under load. Things drop.
- Talk plan with someone outside the valley: entry point, bearing, return time, what to do if you miss it. No one can helicopter you out through triple canopy in cloud at night. You are your own rescue.

Walking technique: pole-pole-foot. Part leaves with pole, not face. Lift feet, don't drag — ants pour in columns as wide as your arm, pill-bugs boil over fungi, snakes pale as roots drop through. Shake boots before putting them back on. Never put your pack on the ground open.

### 5. The animals: you are furniture that sweats

You cannot take three steps without displacing something. That is normal. Don't swat wildly, don't scream, don't run. You'll cut yourself or step through.

- **Moths, beetles, flies:** They will land on you to drink sweat. Let the headnet and sleeves take it. Brush, don't crush — crushed insects bring ants.
- **Frogs:** Shoulder-to-shoulder in leaves, unafraid. Don't touch. Many tropical frogs secrete irritants. The throat-sacs that glow amber are beautiful and blinking — look, don't poke.
- **Ants:** If you see that green-chip column pouring over a fin, give it 3 meters. Don't step across it. They will reroute over your boots in seconds.
- **Fungi at ankle level:** Pretty green-gold pulse. Don't eat, don't touch face after touching. Some are eaten alive by pill-bugs right in front of you — watch where you kneel.
- **Small long-fingered climbers:** Troops crashing overhead, chattering. Down low they fish with thorns, hammer snails with pebbles. These are hands that make and unmake in a minute. This is the most important ethic of the place: **treat them as people.** Don't feed, don't offer shiny things, don't take their hammer stone, don't corner to photograph, don't imitate loudly. Crouch sideways, make yourself small, keep hands in lap, watch. If they approach, stay still. If they leave a twig probe floating, leave it. You are a guest in a workshop.
- **Something larger grunts:** You will hear it far off, then much nearer than you like. Don't investigate. Back slowly upslope, keep a fin or trunk between you, keep your partner close, keep lights low. Most large night animals want to be left alone. Don't play calls, don't throw fruit to lure. If it approaches, make yourself big, speak low (they'll feel vibration more than hear), and give it the hollow.

Assume everything can bite, sting, or infect. Bring a SAM splint, compression bandage, anti-venom is impossible to carry for unknown species — so avoidance is your anti-venom. Tweezers, magnifier, alcohol wipes for thorns and stingers.

### 6. Your mind will go first

Prepare for this as seriously as boots.

It is dark, loud, wet, touched constantly, with no horizon and no morning to explain it. Even experienced cavers and jungle people get rattled. The leaves moving on their own, the eyes opening-vanishing-reopening, the feeling that your own breath and dew are joining the valley's weather — because they are — can tip you from awe to panic very fast.

Before you go:
- Sleep, eat, pee. Go in sober, rested.
- Agree on hand signals for STOP, COME, LOOK, GO BACK, ARE YOU OK? Because you won't hear.
- Agree that anyone can call retreat for any reason, no argument.
- Practice box breathing. When the press gets too much, stop, crouch high, red light down, focus on pole tip, breathe.
- Don't go in seeking a spiritual merge. You will feel it — dew forms on your skin before the leaves, a moth drinks from your arm, a frog watches at eye level — and it's profound. Let it be observation, not surrender. You still need to walk out.

If you freeze, have your partner anchor you: firm hand on forearm, light on ground, slow count. If you must wait out panic, perch, don't lie down. Lying down puts face at ant/fungus level and lets water pool in ears and nose.

### 7. Leave it trackless

This valley is untouched by two-legged walkers. Keep it that way.

- Decontaminate before *and* after. Brush boots, wash clothes hot, quarantine gear. You don't want to bring Earth mold to a naive lung, or bring its spores back to your home universe to bloom in your lungs or garden. A post-trip fever in the next 2-4 weeks? Tell a doctor exactly where you were — "hot, humid, batrachian-dense, lava cave-jungle with heavy spore load."
- Pack out everything, including flagging, food crumbs, batteries, band-aids. Bury nothing — there's no soil, just rock and mat. Pee on rock high, away from water pockets if you must.
- Don't carve basalt, don't stack stones, don't pick flowers or pocket the snail-hammer pebble. The stone remembers. Photograph with low light and memory instead.
- No open flame. No cigarettes. No scented lotion, no sweet deodorant. You smell like salt already; don't add perfume to the bazaar.

### Pack list, distilled

On body: permethrin-treated long synthetics, hood, gaiters, puncture-plate boots, cut gloves, safety glasses, headnet, filtered earplugs, N95 buff.

In small drybag on person: water + electrolytes, red-capable headlamp + spare, whistle (low-tech cuts through better than voice), compass, phone/GPS in waterproof case with track on, 10m cord, knife/multitool, lighter in waterproof capsule (emergency only), personal meds + antihistamine + inhaler + antiseptic + wound closure strips + antifungal.

With partner/team: second lights + batteries, trekking poles, thermal if available, first aid + splint + compression, water filter + tablets, clean bandana, spare socks/gloves in drybag, radio + bone mics, flagging + reel, trash bag.

Outside valley: base contact, turn-around time, dry clothes, hot drink, full change, wound wash, gear wash station.

Go in expecting to be blind, deaf, wet, cut if careless, and utterly outnumbered. Move slow, stay high, touch little, watch the tool-users like you'd want to be watched — quietly, with respect.

Stand still long enough and your breath will join it. That's not a metaphor to chase. That's the warning to carry out with you: this place makes its own water out of living. You are just visiting weather. Don't try to stay till morning explains it.
