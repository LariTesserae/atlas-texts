This is not a cave and not a ruin. Think smaller, younger, and more alive than that.

A bite in the ground about forty paces across, ten-twelve meters deep, that is still running a fever. You will not need climbing gear to get in and you will not need a map to get around. You will need to manage heat, air, darkness, sharp ground, and the fact that you — a loud, sweating, shedding animal — are the contamination event in a place that has no animals at all.

Prepare like you're going to sit quietly for a short time inside a hot lung.

### What it will actually be like

You find it by heat first. On a cool, dry night with animal noise all around, there will be a warm breath coming up, smelling of iron and wet green. From the lip you can take the whole thing in one look, once your eyes quit hunting for scale.

One wall is poured concrete with formwork grain still in it. The other is fresh broken rock. The talus at the bottom is black basalt and cinder and it has not had time to go smooth. It will cut.

Down is a rust-sweating ladder bolted to the concrete and a cinderblock stair that shears off halfway. You finish hand-over-hand.

On the floor the air changes all at once. Not sun-hot. Boiler-room at 2 a.m. hot. Enclosed, wet, breathed-in. Your shirt sticks in a minute. Your own breath comes back to you. Your steps sound wrong, too loud, too animal, because there is nothing else moving.

Light is almost nothing. That's the first rule. The sky is a narrow lid with no stars because of steam. The work-lights are dead, their cages full of dust and husks from insects that were here Before and aren't now. What works is involuntary: a pilot flame blue-then-orange behind a cracked grate, a dull cherry seam where heat leaks from a boiler jacket, one red maintenance bulb over a door that goes nowhere. Give it ten minutes and you see shapes. Twenty and you see color.

Water doesn't flow. That's the second rule. It hangs. Waste heat meets cold wall and sweats. Every pipe beads and drips. *Plink.* Long silence. *Plink.* The concrete weeps dark. In low spots there are shallow black pans that come and go over days, ringed with pale mineral like tree rings. Touch the wall and your hand comes away wet, smelling of oil and sulfur.

And because it's hot and wet and sheltered, it's lush in islands. Not jungle. Exuberance in patches between bare hot cinder and empty concrete.

Three-meter pale ferns, translucent at the edges because they've never needed sunscreen, leaning not to light but to heat and damp, roots prying open the lagging on steam lines to comb condensation. Moss cushions thick as mattresses, acid green, lime, a white-green that almost glows. Pitcher plants hanging off the catwalk, full of rain that isn't rain, perfect and unchewed.

Everything is whole. That's what will unsettle you before you name it. No bite marks. No webs. No droppings, tracks, buzz, scuttle. Spores fall straight down and sprout where they land. Dead fronds aren't eaten, they collapse into sodden felt and melt. You are the only mouth in the room.

Along the north wall on a plinth is why it's still alive: three horizontal riveted boilers like culverts, piped down into rock. One cold and open, full of fern croziers. One ticking as it cools, probably for years. One alive — not roaring, just a low patient churn, a pump lifting something hot from deeper and sighing it out every few minutes. *Hssss.* A white feather of steam that dies before the rim. Needle trembling just above zero. A belt turning a shaft so slowly you think it's still, turning a fan that only stirs.

Around it: tool shed door ajar, bench, jars clouded with condensation, logbook swollen shut. A single chair facing the boilers. Catwalk two-thirds around then stops at rockfall. Extension cords thick as snakes, coiled. A hard hat upside-down, now a moss pot. People left in a hurry and never tidied.

Everything is slow. That's the third rule. Rust a millimeter a month. Drip, evaporate, drip. Ferns unroll a centimeter a week. Life banded by heat: scald-mosses that take near-boiling seep closest in, then ferns, then out in the merely-warm dark corners, pale shelf fungi like stacked ears.

If you stand still, your sweat joins it, beading off your elbows to feed the moss. Red bulb hum at the edge of hearing. Drip. Tick of steel. Sigh of valve. Rim above you like a hard line, dry world beyond it.

Plan for that: short, still, sweaty, dim, dripping, reverent, and a little guilty.

### The real hazards for an ordinary human

**1. Heat + humidity, not cold or fall.** This will get you before anything else. In still, saturated, 35-45C+ air with hot surfaces around, sweat doesn't cool you. Expect heart pounding, headache, nausea, clumsiness, foggy thinking in 20-40 minutes. The climb out is hand-over-hand when you're already slick and tired.

Limit time down to 30-45 minutes max first visit. Never go alone. Have a rim tender who stays cool and dry and can pull you out or go for help. Agree beforehand: if either of you feels dizzy, nauseous, stops sweating, gets chills or confusion — you both leave, no argument.

**2. Air you can't trust.** Hot, oily, faint sulfur, steam, possibly CO/CO2/low O2 pooling low, plus a soup of fern spores and fungal dust from that melting felt. Your lungs will feel full.

Don't go in with a cold, asthma flare, or a hangover. If you can carry a multiverse-standard pocket CO / O2 / H2S detector, do. If you can't, use your body as the instrument and be conservative: metallic taste, headache, rapid breathing, sudden sleepiness = out now. Don't lie down on the floor to “rest.” Low spots collect the worst air.

Bring a respirator — P100 with organic vapor if you have it — and wear it especially if you’ll disturb moss, leaf felt, or dust in the shed. Yes, it makes heat worse. Take breaks at the rim with it off. Bring safety glasses too; a sudden *hssss* feather in the eyes at close range will scald corneas.

**3. Burns and steam.** Assume every pipe is hot until proven otherwise. The living boiler, its valve vent path, the pilot grate, the cherry seam, and the ground seep near it can be near-boiling. The ferns lean toward the hot pipes — don't follow them with bare hands.

Test with the back of your hand an inch away first, or a cheap IR thermometer. Wear long sleeves and pants despite the heat — lightweight, quick-dry ripstop — plus thin leather work gloves and a bandana for neck. Don't sit, kneel, or set bare skin on dark damp ground near the plant. Stay clear of directly above/ in front of the sigh-valve. That white feather is gone before the rim but it is real down there.

**4. Cuts, slips, rust, and bad climbing.** Talus edges will cut a boot. Concrete weeps and moss mattresses hide slick. The ladder rungs are sweating orange rust — strong enough yesterday doesn't mean strong enough today.

Wear thick-soled, ankle-support boots you’ve already broken in, with gaiters. Thick socks. Gloves for the ladder and the shear. Helmet or at least a hard bump cap — rock is young, edges are sharp, catwalk ends at a fall. Bring a short rope, harness or even just a handline for a belay from the rim if you’re not a confident climber, and a headlamp you can use hands-free on the climb. Test each rung and hold before committing. Three points of contact. Expect everything metal to be slippery with condensation.

Carry a small first aid kit at the rim: irrigation, antiseptic, butterfly closures, burn gel, blister care. Make sure tetanus is up to date in your home continuum. Basalt cuts get infected fast in hot wet places.

**5. Darkness and fragile plants.** Your eyes need 10 minutes for shapes, 20 for color. Plants that have never needed sunscreen bleach and burn easily under harsh white light, and you’ll ruin your own adaptation.

Bring two dimmable lights plus one backup, headlamp with a true red/low mode. Use red/low to move, brief low white to inspect. Never leave a light on pointing at ferns or moss. Never touch pitcher lids or unrolling croziers — oils from skin damage them and they have no pests to prune damage away.

**6. Bad water.** Do not drink anything down there. Not the black pans, not the drips off pipes, not the pitcher trumpets. It's condensate through old lagging — likely asbestos in that white wrap — plus rust, boiler treatment chemicals, oil, sulfur, and concentrated biology. Bring more clean water than you think: 1 liter to drink at rim before/after, plus electrolytes. Drink before you go down; you won’t want to drink with a respirator on in steam. Don’t eat down there.

**7. Live electricity and old machines.** That red bulb is fed by something that hasn't given up. Assume all cords, catwalk lights, and the shed are live. Wet concrete + bare feet + old cords = bad math. Keep feet dry-ish, don't grab cables for balance, don't uncoil the snakes. Don't touch the valve, gauge, belt, shaft, or fan. That slow fan will still take a finger. Don't open the firebox doors. Don't try to “help” the boiler. One tired engine is keeping this whole greenhouse warm. If you break it or scald yourself, both you and the place lose.

Don't open the swollen logbook, don't move the jars, don't take the chair — though sitting in it quietly for a while, if it's stable, is exactly what it's for. Leave the moss hard hat where it is.

### How to prepare, step by step

**Before you travel:**
Clean everything. This place has no mouths and you don't want to be the one who introduces spider mites, fungus gnats, or a mold from another universe. Brush boots, shake clothes, wipe gear with alcohol, pick off seeds. Shower if you can. Pack in clean clothes dedicated to the descent.

Dress for two climates: cool dry rim, hot wet sump. Base: quick-dry long sleeve + long pants. Mid: light fleece you will leave at the rim. Spare dry shirt, socks, and towel in a drybag at the rim for after — you will be soaked and getting chilled on the climb out is real. Bring a trash bag for sweat-wet clothes so you don't seed the rim.

Pack: helmet, gloves (work + a dry pair), boots + gaiters, 2+ lights + spare batteries, respirator + goggles, water + salts, rope/handline, whistle, watch/timer, small thermometer if you have one, first aid + burn care, trekking towel, drybag, and a sit pad so your sweat and butt don't go straight into moss.

Eat, hydrate, pee before. Tell someone outside where you're going and when you'll be back. No pets, no food scraps, no smoking, no bug spray down there.

**At the rim:**
Stop. Listen. Feel the warm breath. Look for steam shimmer, loose lip rock, wind direction. Set your station back from the edge: water, first aid, dry clothes, rope anchor if using one. Put on helmet, gloves, lights, respirator around neck ready.

Decide your turn-around time out loud: e.g., “25 minutes down, then up no matter what.” Set a timer that vibrates.

Take five slow breaths. You’re about to be the loudest thing in a very quiet room.

**Descent:**
Go one at a time. Face the ladder, test rungs, don’t kick talus onto partner below. On the sheared stair section, downclimb hand-over-hand — take your time, metal + wet + tired = slip. Call “below!” if you dislodge anything.

Once on floor, stop. Do not stride into ferns. Let eyes adapt. Stand on bare concrete/slab, breathe, locate in dimness: red bulb doorway (landmark), pilot grate glow, cherry seam, catwalk, plinth/boilers, vent direction. Note trip hazards: cords, slabs, low pans with mineral rings.

**While you’re down:**
Move slow and stay on hard surfaces. Step only on concrete slabs and open cinder, never on moss mattresses or through fern crowns. Crouch, don’t crawl.

Sit still for a while. That’s the actual visit. After 10-20 minutes color comes back: translucent frond edges, acid mosses, orange rust, red bulb, black water. Listen for the cycle: drip... tick... hssss... hum. Feel where heat bands are. You’ll find the scald zone closest to the plant — give it respectful distance.

Keep hands to yourself unless testing heat. No picking, no tasting, no moving rocks to “see.” Don’t pour out pitchers, don’t shake spores, don’t wipe condensation to drink. If you sweat-drip, blot with towel, don’t fling. If you must cough/sneeze, turn away into elbow with respirator on.

Photograph without flash if you can; if you must use light, bounce dim white off concrete, never blast fronds. No camp, no graffiti, no carving, no stacking cairns.

Talk low or not at all. Sound carries weirdly and feels intrusive because it is.

Watch yourself: pounding pulse that won’t settle, headache building, nausea, goosebumps when it’s hot, clumsiness, irritability — those are your cue to ascend immediately, not in five minutes. Tell your partner.

**Ascent and after:**
Ascend before you’re exhausted. You’ll be slick; re-glove, re-secure lights. Climb deliberate, rest on stable concrete, not on pipes. At the rim, change into dry clothes, drink with salts, cool slowly — don’t chug ice water or plunge into cold.

Clean again on the way out: brush boots, shake clothes away from the pit, wipe gear. Check for cuts — irrigate and cover, even tiny basalt nicks. Rinse eyes/nose with clean water if irritated. If you develop persistent cough, fever, eye inflammation, or a hot red wound streak in the next days/weeks, tell a healer you were in a hot, wet, fungal, rusty industrial pit and bring this description.

Leave no trace you were the animal who visited: no trash, no food, no pee down there if you can avoid it — your urine is fertilizer in a place with no mouths. Pee well away at rim and pack out TP.

### What not to do

Don’t stay to prove endurance. The third rule governs you too — slow wins.
Don’t turn anything off or on. Don’t feed the firebox. Don’t close the sigh-valve.
Don’t bring down a heater, fan, or dehumidifier to “make it comfortable.” Comfort would kill it.
Don’t introduce life: no releasing insects to “eat the dead fronds,” no planting seeds, no scattering ashes.
Don’t take souvenirs — not a frond, not a jar, not water. Take the damp in your lungs and the red afterimage and leave.

If you do it right, you’ll come up smelling of iron and oil and green, ears ringing a little from quiet, skin prickling as dry air hits sweat, and with the very clear feeling that you just sat, briefly and politely, inside something else’s warm, dark, self-watered exception — a room-sized greenhouse with no sun and no keepers, run by one tired engine, growing extravagantly with no one to feed — and that it let you watch without stopping.
