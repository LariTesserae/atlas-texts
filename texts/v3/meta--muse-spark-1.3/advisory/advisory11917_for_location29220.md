You don't travel to this place so much as you sink into it.

Come down off the levee road in daylight, if you can. From up top it looks small and harmless — a green plug in the flats. Then the path drops six, eight feet, and the air changes. Warmer. Thicker. Louder. You are below the waterline of the paddies and the canal now, inside a bowl that only stays dry because men pump it dry night and day. Keep that in your body the whole time you're down there: you are underneath water, held up by work.

An ordinary body is not ready for it. Prepare like you're going into a hothouse that is also a factory that is also a swamp.

### 1. What it will do to you

It is not hot-noon hot. It's held warmth, wet warmth that never quite rains. Water sweats out of brick, beads under every pipe and leaf and wire, and falls on you. Your shirt will be damp in ten minutes and never dry again until you leave. Your boots will sweat inside, your pack straps will sweat, your glasses, camera, skin.

The sound gets you first after the wet: deep patient thump-thump, thump-thump from the big low-pressure pump house engines, the whine of belts and dynamo over that, and over that the constant white rush of the tailrace lifting a glassy sheet up and over the dike. Plus a hundred small waters — eaves dripping, leaves dripping after no rain, weep holes, condensate pipes spitting onto moss, sugar pans bubbling low. Leaves rustling big and wet when air moves. The cane crusher groaning at intervals. You'll have to raise your voice across a yard, and the green flattens it.

There is almost no animal sound to orient you. No frog chorus despite all that water — oil film and heat and constant draw-down killed that. An egret standing like a valve on the sluice. The one-eared cat asleep on the warm dynamo. Mosquitoes. Cabbage moths. Black mill beetles in bagasse dust. Once in a while a moorhen. Otherwise people, plants, machines.

Prepare for enclosure. You can stand at the old boiler house in the center and see the edges in every direction — ring dike, cane and willow and poplar curtain closing over, silver water beyond. Forty to fifty roofs all touching. Houses with lean-tos on lean-tos until the original shape is gone. It's crowded, low, dim at midday under haze and leaf and boiler smoke, and at night the steam and transpiration make a low cloud that sits in the bowl until morning. If you're claustrophobic, know the exit before you go in: straight up 6-8 feet back to the levee road.

### 2. Wear and carry

Think wet-work, not hiking:

**Feet first.** Brick herringbone paths, sunk and wavy, with purslane and pennywort between. Plank walks over tea-colored ditches. Ground that gives when you step off the plank. Dig down a handspan anywhere and you hit brown water smelling of iron and roots. Wear knee-high waterproof boots with aggressive, moss-proof tread. Not sandals, not trail runners — you'll get immersion foot, cuts, and stabs. Bring two pairs of wool or synthetic socks in dry bags, and change at midday even if it feels pointless. Bring antifungal powder and use it.

**Skin covered but breathable.** Long lightweight sleeves and pants. You'll be ducking through elephant ear so tight you turn sideways, brushing privet-and-fig hedge-walls, kudzu on wires, morning glory on kudzu. Bare skin will be scratched, stung, smeared with velvety moss and soot — that black-green that comes off the mill brick on your palm. The cloth also helps with mosquitoes, which *are* here, and with bagasse dust and boiler soot. Bring a bandana or buff to pull over mouth/nose in the mill yard.

**Rain is constant even when it's not raining.** A light poncho or rain shell you'll vent, plus a wide hat that sheds drip. Everything drips: roof gutters overflowing into rain chains made of cut cans that never stop. Bring a small pack towel.

**Hands and lungs.** Work gloves — thin, grippy, quick-dry. You'll grab a sweating steam pipe to steady yourself if you don't watch out. You'll want the gloves. And an N95 or better dust mask. Not for poison, for load: mold spores from a million leaves, boiler smoke, fine bagasse dust that the black mill beetles live in, ash from rice-husk and cane trash burning. If you have asthma or bad allergies, think hard about whether to go in at all, and if you do, bring your meds plus spares in waterproof cases.

**Ears and eyes.** Foam earplugs. You won't want them in while talking, but if you stay hours the thump and slap and bubble wear you down. And safety glasses or just clear eye protection in the mill/pump house — belts slap, condensate spits every few seconds, ash carts move.

**Dry is survival.** Double dry-bag everything: drinking water, food, first-aid, socks, electronics. Your lens, phone will fog instantly; bring a microfiber cloth and silica. Headlamp with warm light plus a spare — after shift change they string bare bulbs amber under the fig canopy, humming through moths, beautiful but dim. The bowl gets dark and misty fast.

**Stick.** A walking stick or trekking pole set short. For testing plank, probing ditch edges hidden by water hyacinth, steadying on wavy brick. Hyacinth looks solid, glossy and purple-flowered. It's not. Sump pits and ditches are choked with it and forked out every morning steaming onto compost. Don't trust green ground.

Do not bring a machete to “help” unless asked. The boy who climbs the transformer pole with a machete to cut vine back from insulators knows exactly how close to cut before it arcs. You don't.

### 3. Water, food, health

Do not drink the local water. Not from ditches, not from sumps, not from the weep holes. It smells of iron and roots and has oil film and heat in it. Bring 3-4 liters + electrolytes for a day. You will sweat more than you think because the air is already saturated — you don't feel yourself cooling.

Bring your own food to be self-sufficient and eat with clean dry hands. There are vegetable plots, and sugar from open pans, but don't assume you can buy a meal, and don't pick. If someone offers, accept graciously and eat what you're given.

Wounds go bad fast here. Everything is warm, wet, fertile. Bring a real first-aid kit: antiseptic wash, antibiotic ointment, steri-strips, blister care, gauze. Clean even a thorn scratch immediately. Make sure tetanus is up to date before you travel — rust, boiler plate, old pilings, nails with spare gaskets hung on them, soft wood at the waterline. Teredo in the pilings, remember.

Mosquito protocol: DEET or picaridin, reapply because sweat washes it, long sleeves at dusk especially. If you sleep over — and you shouldn't unless invited — you need a head-net and a treated mosquito net / bivy, and a hammock or dry loft, not ground. The ground *is* water.

Heat protocol: Pace like they do. No one hurries. Drink on schedule, sit in moving air, get out of the boiler house heat. Headache, nausea, stop sweating despite wet air — get up to the levee road to cooler, more open air immediately and cool down.

### 4. How to move without getting hurt or hurting the place

Stay on brick and plank. Step off and you sink to ankle in humus-mud. Lift trapdoors in vines carefully, duck under clotheslines under power lines under gutters — in a single alley you'll count six systems in ten feet: ditch, walk, low steam pipe, clothesline, power line sagging under trumpet vine, gutter. Touch as little as possible.

Assume everything hot, live, or load-bearing in a way you can't see:

- **Low steam pipes** sweat and look harmless. They will burn you.
- **Condensate pipes** spit without warning onto moss pillows.
- **Power lines** sag under vine. In this wet, vine *is* wire. Never grab a line, a pole crossarm tufted with grass, a fern-filled bolt-hole, to steady yourself. Never touch pipe and wire at once.
- **Belts, crusher, conveyors, ash carts.** Tie back hair, tuck pack straps, secure loose poncho ends. The mill and power are one building, one slow loop: punts in through the south sluice, cane crushed, bagasse burned, juice boiled down. Stand where a worker puts you, not where the view is good. Never reach across a belt.
- **Sugar pans** bubble low and spit molten sugar. A burn here is worse than steam. Keep distance, keep children’s distance if you're with them — better, don't bring children.
- **Roofs.** Corrugated roofs have an inch of humus, grass, seedlings. Every spring boys go up with scythes to mow so they don't cave. Do not climb on roofs. Do not pull seedlings for fun.
- **Walls that are hedges.** Workers' cottages to the north: privet and fig trained flat between posts, trimmed square monthly, doors and windows cut like holes in topiary. Inside is brick. Don't lean hard, don't pick fruit/leaves, don't prune. That's someone's wall.
- **The chimney from '58.** Cracked, now held upright by strangler fig, roots like rebar down flues, crown 30 feet over the yard, aerial roots kids swing on at lunch. You are not a kid, and you don't know which root is holding what. Admire, don't swing, don't climb.
- **Pilings and sluices.** Settling cracks lined with maidenhair fern have been settled for decades. Wooden walkway pilings gone soft at waterline but holding for 40 years. They hold if you walk; they don't hold if you jump, crowd, or tie off heavy gear.

Walk sideways through elephant ear. Lift vines, don't tear. Close gates and sluice doors as you found them. If you move a plank, put it back.

### 5. The pumps are the heart. Treat them that way.

They have to pump to live here. Tailrace vomiting the same volume always, same sound always. Men listen for a change like a heart skipping. Because if pumps stopped, knees in a week, windowsills in a month.

So:

- Never touch a gauge, valve, oil can, gasket, slate mark. The old man who checks the sump gauge, taps it, marks the slate — watch, don't help unless asked, and then do exactly what he says.
- Never throw weed, trash, food into a sump, ditch, or tailrace. Morning weed-forking is daily work; the woman with the hoe clearing the same 20 feet she cleared yesterday is keeping balance, not “cleaning up.” Don't toss your hyacinth back.
- In the pump house, ask before entering, stand clear of the oiler’s path, don't shout over the engines more than needed, don't lean on the warm dynamo housing — that's the cat's spot, and it's also hot and live.
- Learn the normal sound when you arrive. Thump-thump. Belt whine. Water rush. If it changes — stops, stutters, drops — look to the workers. If they move uphill or to the pump house fast, follow calmly uphill to the levee road. Don't panic, don't run on planks. The flood is slow, not a wave, but slips and electricity are fast.

Coal barge in wet season, rice-husk and pressed cane trash in dry, conveyors, ash carts — that tangle is their lifeline. Give it right of way always.

### 6. People and manners

This is a company hamlet that weathered into itself, not raw or ruined. Nothing collapses suddenly here; things sag, thicken, root, rust in place. People have sat 60 years low and warm and wet, humming.

Don't talk about fighting the green or beating the water. No one here talks that way. The verbs are: trim, lift, burn, pump. Keep the balance.

Practical etiquette:

- Arrive via the levee road, announce yourself at the office steps before wandering lanes and lofts. Ask where to walk, where not to duck into. Those sleeping lofts with walls of living bamboo that leaf out in April — that's someone's bedroom.
- Ask before photographing people, pans, gauges. Boiler smoke and leaf haze make midday light white-yellow and flat; flash will just anger moths and workers.
- Speak close, face to face. You'll have to raise your voice across the yard and it comes back flattened. Don't shout down an alley; walk up.
- Don't feed the egret, don't shoo it off the sluice gate, don't pet the dynamo cat without asking, don't chase the moorhen in hyacinth. With so little animal life, each one has a place.
- Don't pick vegetables, don't pull purslane/pennywort from paths, don't cut cane matting from a kitchen wall for a souvenir. Everything here is used.
- Bring something useful if you can, not money flashed: good work gloves, whetstone for shears, spare headlamp batteries, dry socks in sealed bags to trade, mosquito repellent to share. Offer, don't insist.
- Offer labor only if you can do it their way: hauling forked hyacinth to the compost heap exactly where they throw it, holding a line, carrying ash. Don't improvise repairs.
- Use the latrine they show you. Do not dig — you'll hit water in a handspan — and do not pee in ditches. Pack out sanitary trash.

Evening shift change, when air cools one degree toward bearable, is the best time to understand and the hardest time for bugs. Stand aside, watch: hoe, machete up the pole, gauge tap. Then when lights come on under the fig canopy and mist rises, either leave back up to the levee while planks are still visible, or settle where you're put and stay put. Wandering in the cloud-mist among live wires and open water is how travelers get hurt.

### 7. Leaving: decontamination is part of the visit

This place is a seed bank for the multiverse. Kudzu, morning glory, water hyacinth, strangler fig, privet, elephant ear, pennywort — aggressive hitchhikers. Black mill beetles in dust. Fungal spores in boot tread and pack seams. Cabbage moth eggs on cloth.

Before you climb back to the levee road:

- Brush boots and stick hard, pick humus out of tread, wipe down. Bag that debris and pack it out — don't shake it onto the levee fields.
- Change out of field clothes into clean dry clothes up top if you can. Bag field clothes sealed.
- Wash everything hot and dry hot later, freeze boots if you can. Inspect for seeds in cuffs, socks, hat folds, dry bags.
- Clean lens cloths, tripod feet, headlamp straps. Check for beetles.

From the levee above you'll see only what passers see: a shallow cup of light and mist smelling of crushed cane and hot oil and wet earth, throbbing faintly, holding its crowded living shape just below the water. Let it hold.

You came to see how people live *under* water without drowning — by listening, trimming, pumping, every day, without heroics. If you prepared right, you got to stand a little while inside that hum, damp to the bone, ears ringing, boots heavy with green, and then climb back out without taking any of it with you except the memory of drip and thump and rustle, and the knowledge of how thin the line is, and how patiently it's kept.
