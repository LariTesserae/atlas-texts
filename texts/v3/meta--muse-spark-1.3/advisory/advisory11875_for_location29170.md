You don't pack for Cleft like you pack for a city. You pack like you pack for a high, wet construction site that God left half-done and that animals have already moved into.

This is not a place to conquer. At 4,000 meters and up, on raw rock under a permanent lid of cloud, you are the softest thing there. Act like it.

### 1. Your Body Is The Problem

Forget the weird physics for a minute. The ordinary physics will get you first.

**Air:** It's thin and cold and tastes of iron and wet stone for a reason — there's not enough of it, and what's there is full of fine basalt dust, sideways smoke that never rises, and damp. You will be out of breath standing still. Walking up stairs with a pack will make your heart hammer and your vision grey at the edges.

Prepare *before* you jump:
Arrive acclimatized if you can. Spend 3-4 nights at 2,500-3,000m somewhere sane first. If you can't, plan to do absolutely nothing for your first 48 hours in Cleft except breathe, drink water, and watch the gulls.
Walk at half the speed you think you should. The locals do. The engines do — one piston sigh per minute. The capras do. If you hurry you will vomit, get a splitting altitude headache, and have to lie down in the drizzle. No one will be impressed. They've seen it.
Sleep high is hard. Bring earplugs *and* accept they won't fully work. The geothermal hum lives in your molars, not your ears.
Talk to your doctor about altitude meds before you go. Bring portable supplemental oxygen if you can get it — not because you'll die without it at 4,000m, but because a puff before the long walk past the smelter will save you a miserable hour. Don't smoke. Don't drink much. You are already drunk on altitude.

**Cold + Wet, not Cold + Dry:** This is the killer people underestimate. It's not -40. It's probably 2-8°C, always damp, always dripping, always fog-combed. Damp cold at altitude strips you faster than dry cold because your waxed coat gets heavy, your wool stays wet, and your body is burning calories just to breathe.

You need:
A real waxed canvas or Gore-Tex outer coat, knee-length, with a deep hood. Everyone wears one. It's uniform and practical — droplets that fall *up* on Net Street will slide off wax, but soak into fleece.
Layers of wool, not cotton. Three pairs of thick wool socks per day. You will change socks constantly. Trench foot is a real risk here; the ground sweats, the girders sweat, you sweat.
Waterproof, stiff, high-ankle boots with aggressive tread. The basalt is knapped-glass sharp and slick with moss and condensation. It will cut tennis shoes and twist ankles. Break the boots in first.
Gloves you can work a lamp and a handrail in. A warm hat that fits under a hood. Goggles — clear, not dark — for sideways rain, smelter grit, and upward drizzle getting in your eyes.
A respirator. Not a paper COVID mask. A half-face P100 / P3 with replaceable filters. The smoke doesn't rise, it spreads sideways in sheets. Around Foundry Row and the crushers it's acrid. You'll cough black for a day without one. Bring spare filters; they clog with damp fast.

**Light starvation:** Noon is a slightly paler grey than morning. The sun is a rumor. Your eyes and brain will notice.
Bring a powerful headlamp *plus* a small warm lantern. The city runs on sodium-orange and welding-white — your eyes will tire switching between them. Having your own warm pool of light helps with mood and depth perception in fog.
Bring vitamin D and a plan for Seasonal Affective fog-brain. Everything is grainy dusk. Time blurs. Bring a watch with an alarm and eat on schedule or you'll forget to eat.

**Noise:** It's loud, but slow. Drone + clank + gulls screaming + capra bleating + endless tick-hiss-drip + something bellowing from the canyons too deep to locate. Bring high-quality earplugs for sleep and industrial earmuffs if you're sensitive. Don't use noise-cancelling alone — the low hum will clip through and make you nauseous. Low-frequency vibration can cause anxiety, jaw ache, weird sadness. It's normal. It's the plant.

### 2. The Ground Wants To Drop You

Cleft is a broken slab cracked into canyons three meters wide and three hundred meters deep. They are not fenced like they would be at home. Fog hides the edge. Moss overhangs the lip.

Rules:
Never walk the plateau in fog without light and without staying to the middle of marked ways. The cable-cars and chain-hauls exist because walking around cracks is stupid. Use them, even though they creep. Hold on with both hands.
Don't step on talus skirts at cliff bases — that's the first, shy attempt at rubble. It's loose and goes straight down into cloud-sea.
Everything is wet. Basalt + lichen + three-foot-thick cushion moss = skating rink over a razor. Test every step on terraces and especially around cisterns and aqueducts.
Do not approach a canyon edge to listen to the bellowing. Everyone wants to. The windless days carry sound oddly and you'll think it's close. It's 300m down. The rock at the edge is fresh-fractured and can calve off. Listen from five meters back.

And don't camp. Don't bivouac in empty lots or near rails or near cracks. At night things with wet feet climb up to lick salt from the rails. You don't want to meet them. Stay in staffed lodging on a terrace, upstairs if possible, with your lamp on low outside the door — that's custom.

### 3. Water, Food, Green

Water is everywhere and none of it is yours to just drink.

Fog-nets bow with harvested water, aqueducts gurgle, black mirror pools sit fringed with impossibly green moss. It's beautiful and it's contaminated with basalt dust, bird shit, smelter fallout, and gear oil.

Drink only from closed cisterns marked potable, or water you have filtered *and* purified yourself. Bring a gravity filter + chlorine/iodine tablets + a metal bottle you can boil in. The steam-root gardens around outfalls and geothermal vents grow huge pale cabbages and sweet grasses because of heat + heavy metals. They are tempting when you're cold. Don't eat from gardens unless a local gives it to you and eats too. Bring 3,500-4,000 kcal/day of dense, dry food — nuts, cheese, chocolate, dried meat — because you burn more at altitude and kitchens are slow.

The green will get into you. Lichen tattoos the concrete, ferns spill from broken pipes, moss glows. It's high, cold lush and it's slippery, allergenic, and relentless. If you have mold allergies, bring antihistamines and a nasal rinse. Don't try to clear moss or pick lichen. Locals let the moss take whatever wall they aren't using. If you scrape it off, you're announcing you don't understand the deal.

### 4. Animals: You Are Sparse, They Are Crowded

People are sparse. Whole blocks empty. Plazas for crowds that never came. But the animals filled the gaps.

**Capras:** shag-maned, six-legged, barrel-chested, built for thin air. They graze the steam-grass between rail spurs, utterly indifferent to shunting engines. Give them 10 meters. They are not aggressive, they are *massive* and hypoxic-you will lose if they shift. Don't feed, don't pet, don't block their path to heat. If they lift their heads to watch upward rain, just stop and watch too. It's what you do.

**Soot-gulls, swifts, moths, bats:** Gulls scream constantly and bank through lamplight flashing white. Moths the size of your hand crowd every bulb. Bats flicker hunting them. Don't swat. Shield your lamp when you don't need full brightness — you'll be mobbed otherwise. Keep your mouth closed and your goggles on around lights.

**Fox-things with too many eyes:** They den under cisterns. Cute until you realize why they need extra eyes in the dim. Don't reach under anything. Don't leave food out. Don't try to photograph with flash right in their faces — you'll blind them and they'll bite.

**Wet-feet things:** You will hear bellowing from the vertical dark at slow intervals all day. At night, things climb up. Salt is the attraction. Keep all electrolytes, salty snacks, sweat-soaked clothes sealed in a dry bag inside your room, not in an entryway. If you hear wet steps on metal outside, do not go investigate. Keep your lamp on. They lick rails and leave.

General rule: Make noise, keep light, stay slow, stay back. You are a guest in a zoological mill town.

### 5. The Rules That Are Not Rules

This is the part ordinary humans struggle with, because high places are thin. In Cleft reality hasn't set, same as the rock. Don't test it. Work *with* it.

**1. Weight is negotiable.** That full pail from the cisterns will lighten halfway up the steps as if the plateau forgets to pull. Old workers just adjust their grip and keep walking. Do that. Don't overcompensate, lean back, or let go — it will remember again without warning and you'll throw yourself backwards. Carry loads close to your body, short steps, both hands. Same on chain-hauls: clip in, expect a moment of lightness, don't stand up in it.

**2. Echoes arrive early.** Shout across a crack and you'll hear yourself return a half-second *before* you finish speaking. It causes intense deja vu, stuttering, and shouting matches. Don't shout instructions, warnings, or names across canyons. Walk to the person, use radios at close range, or use hand signals. If you must call, say it once, slowly, and wait. The mill-girls sing rounds with themselves on purpose — if you hear them, listen, don't join unless invited. You'll offset the timing and get dizzy.

**3. Distance stretches near heat.** That 40-meter walk past the smelter wall on the map will take six minutes and leave you gasping, as if the air there is longer. No one runs there. Budget 5-10x time for any route that passes an outfall, vent, or the black heart geothermal plant. Bring water even for short transits. If you feel suddenly exhausted, panting, and like the lamps are receding, you're in a stretch. Don't panic, don't sprint to get out. Slow down further, shorten your breath, focus on the next rivet, not the end. Mark these zones on your map the first time.

**4. Interiors are larger when unobserved.** The shed on Spur Three is 8 paces outside and 20 paces deep inside if you enter alone and don't look back — cold, smelling of wet wool, with a far wall you never reach. Two people together just find tools and a cot.
Protocol, non-negotiable for an ordinary human:
Never enter any small utility structure, maintenance shed, empty shop, or unlit interior alone if you are in a hurry, tired, or hypoxic (which is always). Always go in pairs, maintain voice contact, and glance back at the door every few steps.
Carry a 20m paracord with a carabiner clipped to the outside. If you feel temperature drop, smell wet wool, or lose sound of the hum, turn around immediately, follow the cord out. Don't chase the far wall.
If you get stretched, stop, say your name and the date out loud, look at your hands, turn 180 degrees and walk out. Don't run. Leave an offering of practicality: coil a rope, relight the lamp. Don't sleep in such places alone.

**5. Rain sometimes falls upward.** Net Street, Chapel Cut, a few terraces. You'll stand in drizzle and watch droplets lift off your coat, the rails, the moss, rising in slow deliberate lines back into grey.
Don't try to catch them, don't spin, don't follow them up with your whole head tilted back — you'll fall over from altitude dizziness. Pull your hood tight, put goggles on, keep your mouth closed. Stand still under an overhang and just watch. The capras do. It's not for you. It doesn't mean you're chosen. After it stops, shake off, dry off, keep moving. Clothes will be drier than you expect but your exposed skin will be cold.

Write all this down in a waterproof notebook before you go. When you're hypoxic and damp and it's grainy dusk for the 40th hour in a row, you won't remember. Check your baseline: How many fingers? What day did you arrive? What does your voice sound like normally? Reality drafting itself makes you doubt yourself first.

### 6. How To Behave So People Tolerate You

Cleft isn't hostile, it's unfinished and tired. Whole blocks stand empty because the crews build for a future that arrives one segment per summer — that half-built viaduct on the east edge has been half-built for eleven years, moss-furred rebar and all.

Do:
Wear the waxed coat. Keep your lamp lit even in "day." It adds to the real lighting.
Harvest water like it's work, not magic. Don't waste the silver threads from nets. Don't piss in the pools.
Walk slower than you can. Breathe shallower than you want. Leave room.
Accept moss, rust, lichen, drip. Don't complain about the drip-tick-hiss. It's the soundtrack.
If something weird happens — pail lightens, shed deepens — nod like an old worker. Adjust your grip. Don't call it miraculous, don't lecture about physics, don't try to film it obsessively. Phones die fast in cold damp anyway, and pointing a camera at upward rain just gets you wet.

Don't:
Don't run, don't shout across cracks, don't enter sheds alone, don't touch hot ductwork to warm hands (it hisses for a reason), don't feed animals, don't pick steam-cabbages, don't climb scaffolding for a view of the cloud-sea. The cliffs drop straight into weather a kilometer below. Fog lies. One slip and you're in the sea that breathes.

Learn the hum. It’s the base note. Pistons the size of houses sighing up, sighing down. Cable-cars creeping. If you fight it, you'll get insomnia and paranoia. If you wear it like a coat — which is what locals do — you'll start to time your steps to it and feel calmer.

### Packing List, Ruthless Version

On your body: wool base, wool mid, waxed shell, wool hat, hood, goggles, gloves, stiff waterproof boots, headlamp.

In pack: respirator + 4 filters, earmuffs + foam plugs, 6x wool socks in dry bags, water filter + tablets + stove + metal bottle, 4 days calorie-dense food + electrolytes in odor-proof bag, first aid + blister kit + antihistamine, vitamin D, personal meds, 20m paracord + 2 carabiners, waterproof notebook + pencil, watch, spare batteries in chest pocket (cold kills them), small warm lantern + oil, dry bags for *everything*.

Leave behind: expectations of speed, privacy, sunshine, solitude from nonhumans, and any need for reality to behave.

If you do nothing else, remember this: Don't go in alone if you're in a hurry. Don't run by heat. Don't chase upward rain. Keep your feet dry and your lamp lit.

Do that, and Cleft will let you stand on Foundry Row and see all the way across the new slab to the weather station blinking red on the far cliff, with swifts screaming between stacks and steam rising off sweet grass, and you'll understand what it is to visit a city that hasn't finished being born — and to leave room for it to become more itself around you.
