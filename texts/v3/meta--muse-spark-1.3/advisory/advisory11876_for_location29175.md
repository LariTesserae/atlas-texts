You are not going to walk its streets. You are going to be its weather.

That is the first preparation. Understand where you're going.

This is not a bog you hike through. It's a bog you kneel in, and then look *in*. The room under the bark is the size of your two cupped hands, and the forest in it is made of moss stems standing like red-tipped pillars out of black water. The citizens are the size of rice grains and smaller. To them you are not a visitor. You are a cold front, an eclipse, a god-footstep. Your breath, if you let it fall in there, will rain. Your light, if you let it in, can kill. Your finger can end the town.

So you do not prepare to be comfortable. You prepare to be huge and careful, cold and dim and mostly still, for a long time, in an uncomfortable place, for a glimpse.

Go as a giant. Do not try to go small.

If you have a way to make yourself mite-sized, do not use it here unless you have trained for micro-wet-cold. At that size a bead of water on a leaf is a lake with skin strong enough to drown you. Saturated air is unbreathable. One drip — *plik* — is a bomb. A shrew passing overhead is an earthquake that will collapse lungs you don't have yet. A single degree of heat loss will kill you in minutes. Let that fantasy go. The ethical, survivable way to visit is the way the place invites: lift the bark a finger's width and put your eye to the slit.

Here is how to do that and live, and leave them living.

### 1. Pick the right night, and don't go alone.

Low bog, after rain, in the week or two when night is below freezing but snow hasn't decided to stay. That cold-saturated seal is part of the architecture — it makes the needle-ice palisades, it rimes the moss white, it holds your breath in front of you, it makes everything weep and not evaporate.

Go at true night, cloud-covered if you can. No moon glamour. You want black-peat dark.

Never go alone onto a night bog. The ground gives like a waterbed. Holes full of peat-tea water are invisible. Tell someone exactly where you're going. Bring charged phone/GPS in a dry bag, but keep it OFF and put away once you're in position — screen light is violence down there.

Eat hot and salty beforehand. Pee before you kneel. You will be still for a long time and shivering burns calories fast.

### 2. Dress to kneel in cold water and not move.

You will be on your knees, face near the ground, not moving, for 30-60 minutes. This is how people get hypothermic at 35°F.

Bottom: wool base layer, waterproof shell pants. Top: wool base, fleece, windproof/waterproof shell. No down — it will wet out from bog breath and your own fog.

Feet: knee-high waterproof boots with wool socks. Bring a second dry pair of socks and a thermos in the car for after. Accept your knees and forearms will get wet.

Hands: this is critical. You need numb-proof and delicate at once. Wear thin unscented wool liners. Bring big mittens to pull on between looks. Bring chemical handwarmers but keep them in your pockets, never near the opening — they stink of iron and plastic. No lotions, no insect repellent, no perfume, no smoke, no mint gum. Wash with unscented soap that day. Down there, smell is sight. You will already smell like mammal and laundry; don't add to it.

Bring a closed-cell foam kneeling pad, the kind gardeners use. Not for comfort — to distribute your weight. Without it your knees punch through the sphagnum mattress and compress the dome from above. Crawl the last meters. Low and wide. Think snowshoe, not boot-heel.

Bring a walking stick to test ground, and two short clean sticks, about pencil-thick, to prop the bark. You will not hold it up with numb fingers.

### 3. Find it without wrecking it.

Walk the low, open sphagnum lawn, not the hummocks. After rain it breathes under you. Look for sunk, blackened spruce bark slabs half-swallowed by moss, edges softened, not freshly fallen. The good ones don't look like much. They sit flush, cold to the touch even through liners.

Kneel downwind, pad down first. Settle your weight slowly and wait a minute. Let the waterbed stop sloshing.

Do not rip. Slide a liners-on fingertip under the driest edge and feel for give — a pocket of air that shouldn't be there. If there's no give, leave it. If there is, slide your two prop sticks in and lift *a finger's width*. One centimeter. No more. More lets in cold dry air, collapses the humidity seal, and floods the room with light.

Prop it, take your hands away. Let the drips re-establish. You'll hear them: bark to leaf, leaf to peat. *Plik. Plik.* If you've lifted right, the rhythm resumes in a minute.

### 4. Learn how to breathe, look, and listen.

Put your cheek almost on the peat, a few inches to the side of the slit, not over it. Turn your head. Put one eye to the gap. Close the other. Then do nothing for 20 minutes.

Seriously. Nothing.

Your eyes need that long to adjust to almost-nothing. At first: turpentine and iron and wet green, cold enough to ache your sinuses, and black. Then: the pillars resolving, pale green to amber to blood-red, glistening. The thatch of crowns far above you. The shine of peat-tea water. The faint blue leak around the bark edge, and if you're lucky, the green rot-glow of foxfire on a buried twig. Do not "help" with a light.

Light discipline is absolute. No headlamp into the hole. No phone. No white light, ever. If you must check a prop, turn away, shield a headlamp set to dimmest red with two layers of tape, glance, turn it off. Their world is built for darkness felt on skin. White is a weapon.

Breath discipline: breathe through your nose, slow, directed sideways and downwind, away from the slit. Your exhale is warm, wet, huge. If you exhale into the room, you will fog it, drip on it, change its weather, and lay a plume of mammal alarm over their streets. If your nose runs — it will — dab, don't sniff hard. Sniffing is a windstorm.

Sound/vibration discipline: the bark and snow-thatch smother high sound. What travels is low and wet. Settle before you look. Slow your movements to half speed. Don't talk, don't whisper into the hole, don't tap. Your blood will be loud in your ears. Your stomach gurgle will feel enormous. That's correct. Let your body be loud to you and quiet to them. The background you'll learn: continuous far-away sanding — ten thousand jaws on moss — broken by drips and the faint *pop* of a springtail springing.

### 5. What you will see, and how to behave in it.

You are looking *in* at a forest, not down at moss. Great stems far apart in cathedral emptiness. Water not as pool but as presence — beading, hanging, climbing by capillary, falling by gravity, skinning everything.

Then the boil on the floor that resolves into backs: white springtails seething, amber mites, blind larvae, nematodes in the water film. Don't chase motion. Pick one pillar and hold on it.

You will start to see order: causeways of chewed fiber lashed with fungal cord between stems. A pine-needle ladder, rungs notched. Balconies of pressed frass and pulp and shed skins, papery-tough. Translucent sacs stoppered with resin, full of yellow pollen and black spores. Glossy streets worn in algal slime, diverging, meeting.

This is a winter hamlet, sparse, layered, repaired. An old balcony slumps where its moss foundation grew; a new one is chewed two leaves higher. Nothing is built on dead ground because there is no dead ground.

Watch a street the way you'd watch a night road from a hill. Pollen column moving home. A rust-red velvet mite — poppy-seed to you, tiger to them — tasting air. A false-scorpion backing out, claws open. A white flatworm gliding in the base film. While you watch, needle-ice will tick longer in the peat and topple something. A crew will come. Let them.

Rules for being a good god:

Don't touch. Don't "fix" the toppled sac. Don't take a souvenir. The walls are made of ancestors — dead springtails butchered where they fell, chitin pressed into house walls, pale and translucent. Every chip you pocket is a grave robbery and a structural failure.

Don't feed. No crumbs, no sugar water. You would introduce mold that eats towns.

Don't blow, don't warm. Don't cup your hands over the slit to see better. Your heat will drive convection and dry the skin of water they live in.

Stay still when the god-footstep comes. Something large — shrew, vole, you shifting — will pass overhead and compress the whole dome for a second. It will shiver though there is no wind. Do not flinch and drop the bark. They won't stop working. Neither should you.

Limit eye time. Your cheek on peat will go numb, then ache. That's your timer. 10-15 minutes looking, then close the slit? No — leave props, pull back, rewarm your face on your sleeve, wiggle toes, then look again. Total visit under an hour. Longer and you start shivering hard enough to vibrate the dome, and your core temp drops.

### 6. Leaving without ending the world.

This is where most giants kill.

Warn with nothing — just move slow. Remove props one at a time, lowering the bark *millimeter by millimeter* with your sticks, not fingers. Dropping it creates a pressure wave and a drip avalanche. Lower until it kisses, wait, slide sticks out, let its own wet weight settle. It should look exactly as you found it: half-swallowed, sealed.

Stand up slowly, from kneeling, using your stick, not pushing on the moss near the slab. Back out the way you crawled in, on your pad path.

Check yourself: pour off boot water away from the site. Brush sphagnum bits off over the bog, not into a bag. Don't carry pollen/spores to the next wetland on muddy boots — bang and rinse later, away from waterways.

### 7. After.

You will be cold in a way that lingers. Change socks immediately. Drink hot. Drive with heat on feet.

Don't shower hot right away on numb hands — rewarm gradually. Check for leeches in boot folds, splinters from bark.

Write it down that night while your breath still fogs in your memory: what the drip rhythm was, where the ladder was, what the velvet mite did. You will forget the scale by morning and remember it as "bugs under bark." It wasn't. It was a town that walks as its foundations grow, in a forest that weeps, in the cold before snow decides to stay.

And go back different. That's the real preparation no packing list covers: once you've seen how much order fits in two cupped hands, and how easily your face-heat could rain on it, you walk heavier and lighter at once. Heavier because you know your size. Lighter because you know how to make yourself, for an hour, small enough, dim enough, quiet enough, to be allowed to look in.

*Plik.*

It goes on without you. Let it.
