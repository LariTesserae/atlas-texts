If you go, you have to hold two opposite ideas in your body at once: you are on a mountain, and you are under the sea.

That isn't poetry. It's orientation. Your feet will be ~15 meters down, on a flat basalt summit that used to be in wind. Above you is only a thin ceiling of green water and then sky. You can see it hammered with noon light. Below you, past a broken ring-wall of old stone, there is nothing — the flank of the seamount falling away into blue that forgets color. Everyone gets the vertigo. Mountaineers do, divers do. You'll feel high and deep at the same time. Prepare for that first.

This is not a city you tour. It's a hamlet you borrow breath from. Thirty bells, eighty to a hundred paces across, all plant.

### 1. Your body is the visa

The locals are cool-skinned, cropped-hair, oiled-against-cold free-divers with lungs like barrels. You are not. An ordinary human will be cold, short-breathed, clumsy, and loud in all the wrong ways. Plan for dependence, not competence.

**Cold:** 11-12 C. The light lies to you. It comes down gold and dappled through kelp and looks warm. It does not warm. Without a suit you have maybe an hour before your hands stop working, a couple before you're stupid with hypothermia.

Train: cold showers, cold water face immersion to get your dive reflex back, long swims. Eat fat before you go. You will burn 3,000-4,000 calories a day just staying warm and kicking slow.

**Breath:** Everything is 8 to 15 meters down, with the surface canopy of fronds right above. You *can* pop up for air anytime, but to be in town, to talk, to be polite, you will be freediving.

You need, minimum before you go:
- comfortable static apnea of 90 seconds to 2 minutes, calm, not straining
- dynamic swim of 40-50m in fins without panic
- Frenzel equalization down to 15m and back, repeatedly, while relaxed and head-down
- mask clearing, snorkel clearing, and recovering at the surface through a mat of kelp fronds

Do CO2 tables for three weeks. Practice hanging still on a rope at 5m, 10m, watching your air hunger come and go without moving. That still hang is 80% of life here. If you thrash, you will suck air and get cold faster and scare the fish rivers.

Never dive alone. Ever. Even the locals don't cross alone if they can help it. Bring a buddy or hire a local teen to be your shadow. You'll need someone to watch you while you stare.

**Ears, sinuses, lungs:** No congestion. No diving with a cold. The tide lifts the whole forest 3 meters up and lays it down. That means your depth is constantly changing 2-3m even if you hold a rope. If you can't equalize hands-free and constantly, you'll be miserable. Oil your ear canals lightly as they do, keep your hood tight.

### 2. What to bring, and mostly what NOT to bring

The rule of the mountain: *you cannot build with dead things.*

Wood floats away. Cut frond rots in days. Metal rusts to red scabs in a month and poisons the holdfast mat. They live entirely in living, pruned, stitched-while-alive things. Knives are chipped basalt and black chert hafted in bone. Needles are gull bone. Rope is three-strand kelp, re-braided every spring.

That means leave your multiverse kit behind.

DO NOT bring:
- knives, dive knives, spear tips, metal buckles if you can avoid them. Even stainless will weep. If you must have a buckle, tape it, rinse it, never set it on the mat.
- gloves with sticky grip that tear bladders, sharp fin edges, dangling gauges and GoPro poles that snag rope lines.
- sunscreen, insect repellent, heavy lotions, neoprene shampoo. It films the bell walls and kills copepods. The locals oil with animal oil. Ask to use theirs, sparingly, or bring plain lanolin.
- plastic bags, wrappers, anything that floats. It will end up in the canopy and stay.
- scuba. Technically you could come down on open-circuit, but don't. Your exhaust bubbles waste air they save, your noise drowns the forest, and you can't fit in a bell with a tank without tearing the wall. If you are not a freediver, do not come yet. Learn, then come.

DO bring:
- 7mm semi-dry or good 2-piece 7mm farmer + jacket, 5-7mm hood, 5mm gloves and boots for an ordinary human. Locals go nearly bare with oil. You will die trying to imitate them. Bring extra hood and a vest. Cold gets into joints here — that patient highland ache.
- Low-volume mask, simple J snorkel, long soft freedive fins (not stiff carbon race fins that stir silt), 2-3 kg weight belt with quick ditch, no more — you need to be neutral at ~8m, positive at the surface through fronds.
- A dim red or warm amber dive light only, on lowest setting, for night. No white strobes. At night the bells glow faintly on their own from phosphorescent copepods in the condensation. White light kills that for an hour.
- A basalt or bone gift. They don't need your steel. Bring a well-made bone needle, a polished shell, a hank of natural fiber cordage, dried fruit for kids if your biology allows sharing food — ask first. Best offering is breath and work: time weeding dulse, herding urchins.
- Calories you can eat wet and cold: rendered fat, oily fish, dense carbs in a soft skin. Hydrate obscenely before dives. Cold water makes you pee and you won't feel thirsty.

Crop your hair or braid it ruthlessly tight. Long loose hair + kelp rope + surge = entanglement. Shave or oil exposed skin where your suit gapes — wrists, lips, around mask.

### 3. How to move: pull, don't kick

The summit is capped with a meter-thick springy mat of holdfast — root over root over root into basalt. It's alive and it's the ground, the farm, and the cemetery. You do not stomp it.

There are white shell paths where bottom has been worn to gravel and crushed urchin. Stay on those when on bottom. Otherwise, pull yourself hand-over-hand along the low ropes strung between bells, or swim 1m above the mat with tiny fin beats.

The forest: stipes as thick as a thigh rising 10-12m straight up to the surface, then fraying into fronds that lie flat on top and make the roof. Bells are made by coaxing 5-6 living stipes inward and grafting their pneumatocysts into one amber translucent chamber big enough to crouch in, anchored to old house stones. They sway. All of it sways with tide.

Learn:
- Duck-dive cleanly through the surface mat, not crashing. Part fronds, slide, let them close.
- Come down stipes hand-over-hand to save air. Never grab a young bladder or a newly lashed join — they are marked with fresh braid. Ask which stipes are stairs.
- Never fin hard near the wall-tops. The wall is carpeted in purple-black urchins clicking day and night. One slip = spines in palm that will fester cold.
- When tide turns, and you will feel it before you see it — the whole grove leans one way — grab a rope and just hang. Don't fight it. Let high water take you. That's what it's for.

You can cross town on one breath if you are strong. You are probably not strong enough yet. Plan to touch a bell every 25-30m. Swim house to house. Be slow.

Vertigo protocol: Do not swim to the edge and look down on your first day. When you do, go prone on the mat inside the wall, hold a stone, look out horizontally first, not down. The gray whale passes far below along the flank. You will feel it in your sternum before you hear it. Don't bolt for the surface. Hold, listen, breathe later.

### 4. How to breathe with other people

Each house is a living diving bell. Air breathed in from below stays. Walls sweat oxygen, smell of iodine and cut grass. Inside, on a woven frond platform, dry things: seal furs, fire-stones, bone tools, often a child napping warm.

This is someone's lung and bedroom. Etiquette is survival:

1. Ask from below. Hover under the bell mouth, make eye contact, offer palm up. Do not just duck in. A bell holds 2-3 people crouching max, often a family.
2. Enter slow, exhale small and upward into the dome, not explosive. Your exhaled air *is* a gift — it replenishes them. Spit out your snorkel away from the chamber.
3. Don't stay long. Three to five breaths, warm your face and hands in the slightly warmer air — old bells run about a degree warm and fizz faintly if you press an ear to the wall — then give a breath back and leave. Don't hog, don't pant, don't hyperventilate aggressively inside (you'll spike CO2 and fog the copepods).
4. No touching walls with nails, no leaning hard, no wiping condensation greedily. condensation hosts the night-light copepods. No flame. Ever. Fire-stones are heated above and brought down warm, not fire lit inside. Your breath plus a flame would foul a bell for days.
5. Babies and old bladders first. If a child herding urchins with a stick motions you off, you are off. Those urchins will eat a young house in a night.

On the surface: the roof fronds are also road and pantry. Don't tear them to make a hole to breathe. Lift and slide.

### 5. Loud, crowded, slow

Forget silent underwater. It's a cathedral.

Stipe-on-stipe groan, bladders popping with pressure, fronds snapping like wet sails in surge. Barnacles ticking, urchins clicking teeth as constant static, schools of black rockfish and copper surfperch pouring through in rivers and blocking the sun. Crabs the size of platters clipping rot — gardeners, leave them alone. Harbor seals dropping like stones to nap in glades — give them 5m, never corner. And the old rust-red octopus under the causeway: she steals tools and leaves shells. Let her. If she takes your bone needle, thank her. Don't chase. You'll lose.

Time is measured in years. A new bell takes seven seasons to train. Ropes creak. Detritus snow drifts sideways. Don't bring hurry. If you try to help by pulling hard, cutting, "fixing" a lean, you'll tear living grafts. Ask for weeding work in dulse and sea lettuce clearings — slow hand work. Carry baskets. Hold a child while a parent dives.

Food is closed-loop. Fronds, dulse, urchin roe, crab, mussels from tended lines. No outside food. Don't offer energy bars that crumble. If invited to eat, eat urchin fresh, chew dulse thoroughly, accept seal oil even if your stomach complains. Bring no alcohol — it vasodilates and will get you hypothermic fast at depth.

The dead are planted: wrapped in frond, weighted with stone into the holdfast to feed future stipes. If you see a fresh planting mound, detour wide on the shell path, quiet your bubbles, don't stare.

### 6. Night and the edge

Stay at least one tide cycle into night if you can do it safely. That's when you understand it.

Light goes blue, then black. Fish settle into fronds like birds. Then the bells bloom — each one a faint lantern from copepods. From the edge of the plateau, looking back, town looks like a constellation that has sunk and caught.

Don't swim the edge at night unless with a local. Hang on the leeward rope line, hood tight, watch the lanterns sway together as tide turns and the forest lays itself down. Cold on skin, loud in ears, suspended over abyss and rooted at once.

Have an exit plan: cold shivering that won't stop, blue lips, inability to equalize, panic urge to rip mask — abort to surface, to shore, to warmth. Hypothermia makes you stupid and brave. Decide your limits warm, on land, before.

### Practical checklist for an ordinary human

Weeks before: apnea + Frenzel coaching, 3x/week cold water swims, leg + core for slow finning, practice neutral buoyancy hangs.

Days before: trim nails short, crop/braid hair, clean ears, sleep, overeat healthy fats, no alcohol, test all rubber for leaks (a leak at 12C is misery).

On arrival, surface: meet gardeners, ask where to enter, where the nursery bladders are (off-limits), where the octopus is today, when slack tide is. Learn three gestures: *may I share? / thank you / I need surface.*

First dive: just go to one bell and back with a guide. Touch the old warm wall with your ear, hear the fizzing chorus. Second dive: help weed. Third: sit out a tide turn on a rope.

Leave: give breath back to every bell you used, re-braid any fray you caused, take every scrap you brought, rinse everything in surface water away from the mat.

You won't become one of them — you don't have the lungs or the years. But if you come cold-ready, breath-trained, metal-free, slow and willing to hang on a rope and be small among amber lanterns while the whole mountain breathes, they'll let you crouch a while in a living lung, warm your hands on its wall, and watch smoke that is bubbles rise to a ceiling that is sky.
