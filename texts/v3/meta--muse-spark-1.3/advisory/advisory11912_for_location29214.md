This is not a hike. This is a high, cold, bright, young machine that is still growing over the mountain it ate.

If you go as an ordinary human - no filters, no altitude lungs, no spatial sense - you need to prepare for three problems at once: your body in thin bright cold, getting lost in a place that does not hold still, and not getting cut, shocked, or stranded by industry.

Go slow. Everything there does.

### 1. What your body is walking into

**Thin air at ~3,000-4,500m feel.** You will be short of breath on stairs, stupid with headache the first day, clumsy. The engines down deep make a pressure in your sternum, not a sound. You’ll think it’s altitude sickness. It might be both.

Acclimatize *before* you go in. Spend 2-3 nights high if you can. Plan your first day inside to do almost nothing. Walk half your normal pace. The locals leave a seal to cure for a week; you take a staircase at walking pace and you’ll be panting.

Bring: acetazolamide if your doctor okays it, a pulse oximeter, ibuprofen, and a hard turnaround rule. Headache + nausea + confusion = go back down to the last switchback immediately. Do not sleep higher if you’re sick.

**Bright cold.** Midsummer noon feels like early spring elsewhere. Sun hammers, air stays cool. You will get sunburned and chilled at the same time.

Your skin prickles cold while your eyes water. That’s accurate. UV at that elevation bouncing off galvanized roofs, condensate tanks, miles of conduit, wet concrete will blind you before it warms you.

You need:
- Glacier glasses, Category 4, with side shields. Bring a spare. Regular sunglasses are not enough. You’ll squint until you cry without them.
- High SPF sunscreen + zinc for nose/ears, SPF lip balm. Reapply. The light reflects up from below.
- Layer system: merino base, fleece mid, windproof/water-resistant shell, puffy for shadow and dusk. Hat that covers ears, buff for face, thin sun gloves + heavy cut-resistant work gloves over them.
- No shorts, no short sleeves. The concrete is pale and sharp, rebar bristles, stair nosings are unworn and will slice. Tetanus shot must be current.

Rule the old hands live by: **Stay in the sun if you want to stay warm; stay in shadow if you want water.** The temperature difference between sun and a service void is 10-15C. You will shuttle between them all day.

**Dry damp.** There’s no river, no lake. There’s dew, cloud spillover, steam off roofs, drips, gutters that run for an hour, sumps a foot deep full of midges, cisterns half-full rimed with ice even in summer.

You will dehydrate fast in cold, bright, thin air and not notice because everything looks wet at 8am and dry by 10.

Bring all your water in plus the ability to catch more: 2-3L capacity, a wide-mouth cup to leave out on ledges like the workers do, a small sponge and plastic sheet for wringing rails and wires, and a filter + tablets. Do not drink sump courtyard black water or drip-barrel water unfiltered - pigeon shit, oil, metal, dead midges. It’s for emergency only, and even then filter it. In shadow, look for beads and drip lines. In sun, don’t wait.

There is nothing to forage. No trees, no fields. Just moss in drip lines, lichen coins, bittercress in dust between plates. The gulls, choughs, starlings, pigeons, kestrels, cats, marmots fat as sacks in expansion joints, rats on pipe highways at dusk, goats eating paper on retaining walls, palm-size moths at lamps that never turn off, spiders under every walkway - they don’t eat plants. They eat scraps, each other, oil, heat, condensation. So must you bring everything: 3,000-4,000 kcal/day, salty, no-cook plus a stove. No wood. No open fires on decks - ozone and oil and wool insulation.

Air smells of ozone, hot oil, wet stone. Bring an N95 / P100 respirator. Not for altitude, for dust, pigeon droppings in vents and under-decks, welding fumes, corona around dynamos. If you have asthma, think hard about going.

### 2. How not to get lost when distance unlatches

This is the real danger. Not the height. The layout.

You will walk a straight alley counting doors and come out one level higher without climbing. You will see the same blue water tower in two districts at once, both casting shadows. Shadows often fall the wrong way - toward the sun, or too dark under a catwalk. The far side looks 15 minutes and takes all morning because the middle stairs repeat: same oil stain, same dead pigeon, same chalk mark, three times.

New rooms bud overnight with uncured concrete and unpainted steel smelling of mineral and cold. A door you welded shut is open with fresh edges.

Do this:

**Anchor outside.** Make base at the last switchback where you can still see it all at once. Log time in/out, GPS point, photo of the rim. Do not make your first sleep deep inside.

**Never navigate by one landmark.** Especially not the water towers, the three tallest stacks, or the sun/shadows. Use triangulation: tower + chalk + wire count. Assume duplicates are real.

**Chalk, dated, and string.** Bring lots of lumber chalk in two colors. Mark arrows + date + time, e.g. > 09/05 10:20. Locals are right: **don’t trust chalk more than a day old.** The place shifts. Re-mark on the way back. Photograph the oil stains, dead pigeons, graffiti - your camera will catch a loop before your brain admits it.

Travel roped or at least visual-tethered. Sound dies within twenty paces. A hammer clank becomes a tap. An ore train is silent except for ticking cooling rails. You cannot shout for help.

**Don’t shout.** Not rude, dangerous. Loud sound comes back flattened and late, from a different direction. You’ll get vertigo and mislocate yourself. Use hand signals, headlamp flashes, a small whiteboard, text messages if radios fail in steel. Bright clothing and a signal mirror work better than a whistle here. Stay within 20 paces / line-of-sight of your partner. If you lose sight, stop. Mark. Wait.

**Move slowly and pay attention to step across.** If you are not paying attention, you will loop. If you *are* paying attention - really looking at the joint where two plates meet, the drip, the feather offering - you can sometimes step across and save hours. Don’t force it. If stairs repeat, stop, drink, look up at the starling murmurations, then retrace to your last dated mark and try a different level - up or down one deck, or a parallel service void.

**Leave offerings, don’t move them.** At misaligned corners you’ll find a bolt, a feather, a twist of wire. That means: do not trust your eyes here. Bring your own small offerings - nuts, bolts, zip ties, feathers - add one, do not take others. It’s both etiquette and safety flagging.

**Don’t follow a cat onto a new deck.** This is literal. Cats, goats, rats know where the mass will be tomorrow. You don’t. If a cat walks onto fresh pale concrete with sharp edges and proud weld beads, let it go.

**Don’t store anything important in a wall less than a year old.** Learn to read age: pale fresh concrete, sharp edges, uncured smell, unpainted steel = less than a year, may be gone or double-size in the morning. Sleep, cache food, batteries, passport only in older, stained, mossy, lichen-covered sections where the nosings are starting to round.

Avoid the central court between the three tallest stacks in rain. Rain there sometimes falls *up*, rising slowly from warm grates into brightness. It’s disorienting and slick, and you don’t want to be on a catwalk when up becomes ambiguous.

### 3. Industrial hazards: slow but lethal

Everything moves glacially. A 100-meter gantry crane creeps centimeters a minute. Belts crawl. Pistons rise like sleeping animals. That makes you careless. Don’t be.

Bring a climbing helmet / hard hat, always on. Conveyor gantries inch overhead, wires sag with frost-melt, pigeon eruptions blind you for a second. Steel-toe boots with ankle support and aggressive tread for slick dew and black ice in shadow. Knee pads if you’ll go into under-decks and service voids - and a headlamp even though lamps never turn off. The lamps attract moth clouds at noon; the voids go dark.

Assume everything is electrified: junction boxes, caged dynamos spitting blue corona, transformer housings (cats love to sun belly-up on them - you must not sit or lean there), conduit, rails beaded with dew at 8am. Wear insulated soles, don’t touch red valve wheels unless a worker tells you to, don’t grab wires for balance. Test with back of hand? Better: don’t touch at all.

Lockout is informal. Maintenance crews leave tools for a week while a seal cures. That tool on a valve means *don’t touch.* That chalk X means *don’t step.* Ask.

Carry a full cut/puncture kit: pressure bandage, Steri-strips, irrigation syringe, antibiotic ointment. Every surface is furred with eye-bolts, chains, nameplates, insulators. You will get cut.

Toilets: there are none for you. Pack out everything. Do not shit in sumps or cisterns - that’s someone’s drinking water. Bring WAG bags, hand sanitizer.

Sleep: warm bag rated to -5C even in summer, insulated pad for concrete, eye mask - those million small amber work-lights turn on at dusk and reflect in every bead until the whole mass sparkles wet and alive, beautiful and impossible to sleep in without cover. Earplugs help less than you think because it’s already muted, but they cut the ticking of cooling metal and falling droplets. Sleep away from weep holes (swifts), lamp clusters (moths), walkway undersides (spiders), expansion joints (marmots). And never in a newly budded room.

### 4. Animals and people

Animals are muted anduna fraid. Kestrels hang motionless in updrafts. Gulls and choughs make moving shade you feel more than hear. Do not feed them. Do not eat them - rats and pigeons here eat oil and scraps. Watch for goats on outer retaining walls - where they walk, stones/paper fall. Don’t stand below. Check boots and packs for spiders in the morning. Shake out.

People: crews are patient, quiet, seasonal in their thinking. Speak quietly, move slowly, offer help before asking for directions. Bring useful trade: good chalk, stainless bolts, wire, coffee, chocolate, dry socks, spare work gloves. Leave cups out to catch drip like they do, and don’t steal another’s cup. If an older hand tells you a deck moved, believe them.

Lights buzz on in empty halls before you enter. Don’t announce yourself loudly. Step in, pause, let your eyes adjust, look for fresh concrete.

### Pack list for an ordinary human, 3 days

Worn: base + fleece + shell + puffy in pack, long pants, boots broken in, helmet, glacier glasses, hat, buff, cut gloves.

In pack: stove + fuel, 3 days food + 1 day emergency, 3L water + filter/tablets + cup + sponge, sleeping bag + pad + eye mask, first aid + blister kit + respirator + sunscreen, headlamp + spare batteries, 2x chalk colors, 20m paracord + 3 carabiners + signal mirror + whistle (for outside the dead zone only) + notebook + pencil + camera/phone + power bank, offerings kit, WAG bags.

On wrist: watch, compass (expect deviation in steel - use for trend, not truth). Altimeter helps to catch that “one level higher without climbing” shift.

### If you want to leave alive and sane

Give every traverse 3x the time it looks. The far side is never 15 minutes.

Date everything. Photograph everything. Trust nothing newer than a year, no shadow, no single tower.

At dusk when the peaks go pink then blue and the piston deep below completes the stroke it started at noon and the swifts drop into the vent and water ticks wire to wire to deck and the duplicate tower blinks on impossibly near - stop walking. Get in sun’s last warmth or into shelter before the frost-melt refreezes. Mark your door. Sleep old.

You’ll want to hurry because the brightness makes you anxious and the quiet makes you shout. Don’t. The lichen eats concrete a micron at a time. The clouds well up from below. The starlings write and erase calligraphy without sound. Join that pace and the valley lets you through.
