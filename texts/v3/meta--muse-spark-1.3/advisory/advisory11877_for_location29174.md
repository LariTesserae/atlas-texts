You don't pack for the moor. You pack for the bowl. They are two different countries stacked 10 meters apart.

Up top: flat, white, windy, loud with wind, thin cutting light. Down inside: still, cold-pooled, painfully bright, damp, crowded, silent, green. You will go from wind-chill to cold-sink in one step over a lip of pale rock. Prepare for both, and prepare most of all to not break the second one.

This is not a ruin. It's a kept place. Exactly damp, exactly green, exactly full, exactly quiet. You are the only thing in it that isn't in balance. Your job is to pass through without tipping it.

### 1. Understand what will happen to your body

**Cold that doesn't move.** The wind shears straight across the mouth and never drops in. Cold sinks and sits. Your breath will hang white even at noon, even while the stone glares. It's a temperature inversion in miniature. You won't feel wind-chill down there, which tricks you. You'll just quietly lose heat through your soles, your palms, your backside when you sit.

Dress in quiet wool, not swishy synthetics. Base layer + insulating mid + windproof shell you can put *on* on the moor and take *off* the second you drop in, or you'll sweat and then freeze in still air. What matters most is insulation from below: thick felt insoles, insulated soft-soled boots, extra dry socks in a zip-lock, and a small closed-cell sit pad. Do not sit directly on stone. Cold seeps.

Bring gloves — thin liner gloves you can work in. Everything you touch will be damp even when it looks dry. Red hands go numb fast. You need dexterity without bare skin.

If you have asthma, reactive airways, or a mold allergy, think hard about going. The air is cold, very damp, and full of moss spore, peat, and breath held too long. Bring inhaler, antihistamine, and a soft cloth mask/N95 you can tolerate. It will make whispering even harder, but coughing is worse.

**Light that piles up.** On the moor the light is thin. In the bowl the white limestone throws it back and forth until the pit glows. At noon you will have to squint continuously. Snow-blindness is real here without snow.

You need glacier glasses — Category 3 or 4, with side shields — plus a spare pair. Not fashion sunglasses. Add high-SPF lip balm and sunscreen for nose, ears, under-chin: reflected UV burns from below. A soft, brimmed hat with no chin-snaps to jingle. No mirrored hard case that clacks.

Plan your timing: enter mid-morning or mid-afternoon, not dead noon. Morning has ice-skim on the troughs and slicker stone; noon has maximum glare; late day gives you softer bounce-light and you can still climb out before the cold deepens.

Limit your stay. 60 to 90 minutes inside is plenty. Your eyes will ache and your core temp will slide without you noticing because there's no wind to warn you.

**Water everywhere you can't drink.** There is no stream. The damp is interstitial — condensation sweating out of moss cushions, a slow *tick* from an overhang every minute you forget to wait for, black log troughs holding a finger's depth of seep. Those troughs are not for you. They are curated. Ice forms by morning, melts by noon, forms again. A child carries water back and forth not to move it but to keep it from skinning over.

Bring all the water you need — 1.5 to 2 liters — and drink it. Do not drain troughs. Do not lick walls. Stone, moss, vole and bird droppings, shrew runs — it's a beautiful terrarium and a gastrointestinal gamble. Bring a filter + tablets as emergency backup only, and even then ask yourself if your emergency is worth breaking their cistern system.

Bring quiet food: soft, high-calorie, no crunch, no wrappers to crinkle. Pemmican, soft cheese, oat paste, chocolate. Nothing that needs fire. No stove. No smoke ever rises there for a reason. No smoking, no vaping, no open flame. Not even a match to see in a dark corner.

### 2. Understand what will happen to your ears

The moss eats sound. The bowl holds it. The cold deadens it.

Your boots will sound wrapped in cloth. A bird on the rim will open its beak and you will hear nothing or almost nothing. A drop of water goes *tick* and is gone.

Lean into it before you go. Practice a day of moving quietly. Tape zippers. Remove keys, coins, carabiners, anything that jingles. Wear soft cloth, not Gore-Tex that swishes. Put rubber tips on trekking poles or better, leave poles on the rim and take one quiet wooden staff. Wrap camera in cloth, turn off all beeps and fake shutter sounds.

Do not bring: dog, drone, speaker, radio, metal mess kit, anything with a motor, anything that beeps.

Down there, loud is not rude. Loud is violent. The inhabitants work stone with stone without striking sparks of sound. They speak, when they must, close to the ear, breath-warm. Learn their core gestures before you go and use them:

- Flick of two fingers: move aside / let pass
- Palm flat on chest: stay / wait
- Open palm down, slow press: quiet / settle
- Eye contact + slight nod toward ladder/bridge: may I?

Don't call out. Don't hiss to get attention. Don't clap. If separated from a partner, you cannot shout to find each other — sound won't carry. Stay in visual contact at all times. Agree on visual signals beforehand.

If you must speak, do it mouth-to-ear, one short breath-phrase. Better: bring a small slate and chalk stub for necessary questions. Don't write on stone. Don't mark anything.

Your own mind will get loud in that quiet. Prepare for that. The absence of hum feels like pressure at first, then like being watched. You will be watched — children with legs dangling, an old woman with lichen-cakes, eyes behind a willow shutter. Don't perform. Don't narrate. Just be still for a while and let your nervous system downshift.

### 3. How to move

You will almost miss it. From five paces away on the moor you won't know there's a 40-pace oval city at your feet. That means you can also walk straight into it and fall 10 meters onto dry-stone roofs.

Navigation up top: the moor is featureless, white, windy. Bring compass/GPS, and mark your entry/exit. A collapsible bright pole stuck well back from the lip, or a GPS waypoint. Don't build a cairn, don't spray paint. Lichen here has had centuries; black script lichen is not your notebook.

The lip: pale rock, rounded like old teeth, slick with lichen and seep. Wear gloves with good grip. Test every handhold. Approach low, in a crouch, from downwind so a gust doesn't push you.

Inside: there is no ground left. From rim to floor is house on house, roof become floor become bridge become stair, alleys no wider than a shoulder, ladders of birch and bone lashed with sinew. Those ladders were lashed for people half your weight who climb them daily and never hurry. Do not climb them unless explicitly invited with a gesture, and then go light, no pack, three points of contact, slow.

Stay low. Stay on obvious walkboards. Move aside instantly when flicked aside — you are blocking a hallway that is also someone's roof. Never step on a roof-lawn unless you are shown to. The moss is not decoration. Where a stone has fallen, green has already taken its job. That pillow moss clipped exactly to the shape of the stones is load-bearing, water-holding, sound-proofing, insulation. One boot print crushes years of trimming.

Look down constantly. Voles fat as fists move openly, unafraid. Shrews flicker along wall-tops. A ptarmigan hen may cross a bridge with chicks like a procession. Woodlice turn under leaves, spiders the size of freckles work between ferns. If you stand still, something will walk over your boot. Let it. Don't feed, don't pick up, don't shoo. Watch for droppings and nests — don't touch your face, wash/sanitize hands after, check socks and cuffs for hitchhikers later. Hantavirus, ticks, fungal spores love dense, damp mammal places.

Do not move stones. The man rebuilding a wall by lifting a block, packing fresh sphagnum, and setting the *same* block back in the *same* seat is not repairing. He is keeping. Don't help. Don't "tidy." Don't take a souvenir stone, moss tuft, bone, flint flake. Everything is structural.

Same for water: don't "help" the child carrying water back and forth. Don't pour trough to trough. The balance is precise.

### 4. People etiquette

There are many of them for so small a place. Density you feel in elbows and breath. They have made room by being quiet and exact.

Rules:

1. **Ask to enter with your body.** Stop at the rim/first terrace. Palm on chest (stay), wait. Make eye contact. Wait to be acknowledged. If a shutter closes, wait longer or withdraw. No means no.

2. **Make yourself small.** Crouch. Keep pack on front or leave it on the rim with a buddy. Don't spread elbows. Alleys fit a shoulder. You don't.

3. **No gifts that change things.** No candy, no tools, no batteries, no seeds, no shiny things. If you must reciprocate a kindness, offer labor only if asked, or leave nothing. Clean your boots, clothes, pack thoroughly *before* you arrive — brush off all seeds, burrs, soil. You could introduce an upland weed that would love that green and ruin the exactness.

4. **No photos with flash, no shutter clack, no posing people.** If you photograph, do it silent, no flash, from a still position, and only after a long quiet wait. Better to not photograph people at all. This is a home, not a vista. Don't sketch on walls.

5. **Don't eat their food unless offered, and then sparingly.** Lichen-cakes are survival food, labor-intensive, and usnic acid will wreck an unaccustomed stomach and kidneys. If offered, accept with both hands, small bite, nod thanks. Don't ask for more.

6. **Sanitation is pack-it-out absolute.** There are no toilets, no empty slope. Bring a wag bag, opaque baggies, hand sanitizer. Do not urinate in troughs, corners, moss. Do it on the moor far from the bowl, downwind, and pack out paper.

7. **Don't stay the night.** No guest space exists. Night means ice, deeper cold sink, and you taking up breathing room in a hive. Climb out while you still have light and warmth in your legs.

### 5. Packing list for an ordinary human

On body: quiet wool base, fleece mid, wind shell (moor), convertible to just base+fleece inside; windproof hat + soft sun hat; glacier glasses + spare + strap; liner gloves + warm mitts in pack; soft scarf to pull over mouth in cold sink (also muffles breath noise); soft-soled waterproof boots with felt insoles; gaiters to keep peat out.

In small soft pack (no hard frame to bang): 2L water; quiet calories; sit pad; headlamp with red mode (for shaded under-slabs, use sparingly, never shine in eyes); first aid + blister kit + personal meds; slate + chalk; sanitizer + wag bag; spare socks + spare base in dry bag; map/compass/GPS + bright collapsible marker pole left on rim; rope 10m + a buddy if you're not a confident scrambler — the climb out is slippery when damp and your legs will be cold-stiff.

Leave behind: anything loud, anything scented (perfume, strong sunscreen smell hangs in still air), anything that sheds microplastic glitter, anything you can't carry silently.

Clean everything beforehand. Shake out tent, socks, Velcro. Pick off seeds.

### 6. Getting out and after

The climb out is the shock. You go from still brightness to wind like a door opening, sound rushing back. People gasp, stumble, get dizzy. Have mitts and shell ready at the top. Turn your back to wind, rewarm hands under arms, drink something warm if you cached a thermos on the moor (never bring it inside — clank). Rest eyes: look far across the moor, not at close stone, let the squint release.

Check yourself: brush all moss, spores, insects off. Air clothes. Wash hands and face. Check for slippery peat between treads — you'll need grip for the walk back.

And mentally: you will want to talk about it loudly immediately. Don't, for a minute. Let the silence wear off slowly. The moor resumes, vast and empty. Behind you the bowl closes.

If you did it right, the ice will still form and melt and form, the troughs will still hold a finger's depth, the moss will still be clipped to the shape of the stones, and the town will hum without humming as if you never stepped in. That is success — not summiting, not collecting, but being briefly held, cold and bright, in a cupped hand of stone and leaf, and then released without a spill.
