This is not a mountain to climb. It is a mountain that has quit.

Go in expecting that wrongness and you will be safer. High here does not mean hard and dry and falling away. It means flat, old, soaked, and level with the clouds. A table 3-4 miles across, pink-grey rock underneath worn to molars, and on top of it ten feet of living plant. You do not walk on ground. You walk on sponge.

Ordinary human advice first: you are soft, warm, salty, electrical, and badly sealed. This place is cold, wet, acidic, charged, and indifferent. It will not try to kill you. It will try to absorb you. Prepare to stay un-absorbed.

### 1. What it feels like, so you don't panic

Dim blue-grey light that never becomes day or night. Sun as a smear. No shadows. At 100 yards a moss hummock and a grazing animal have exactly the same weight. Your depth perception will fail.

Cold that is not heroic. 0 to 6C, thin air *and* saturated air at once. Your nostrils stick. Your exhale doesn't leave — it hangs and joins the fog. Inhale tastes like iron, peat, ozone. Frost needles grow on the shady side of tussocks even when it's thawing. Little black tarns skin with ice and crack at midday like distant rifles. That sound is not people.

Loud, for an empty place. Wind combing over a table with nothing to stop it, low roar then whistle through cotton-grass. Under it: chuff-chuff-chuff you feel through your knees if you sit — big moss domes pumping water through themselves. Kettle-shrieks where methane and cold air vent from peat blowholes, smell for a second like sweet rot and honey and deep time. Gurgle-gush as water fires through natural peat pipes from pool to pool. Almost subsonic thump from below. Ticking crackle of static on moss tips. Drone of impossible insects.

If you put your ear to the moss it sounds like a boiler room.

Stay with that image: industry without builders. Sundews making glue by the acre. Sphagnum making acid by the ton. Cranberry vines moving food foot by foot. No tools, no houses, no cairns, no cut wood. Nothing has ever been built here. Don't be the first.

### 2. Stay warm while staying wet

You will not stay dry. Understand this now. If you stand still you get wetter than if you walk, because fog condenses on you. Water rises around your boot with every step and fills the print black when you lift. Rocks weep without rain. Lichen drips. Your eyelashes collect water.

So don't dress to stay dry. Dress to stay warm while wet.

Head to foot:
**No cotton. Ever.** Wool or good synthetic base, wool mid, windproof + truly waterproof shell. The wind is constant and finds seams.
**Head net + brimmed hat + glasses.** Not for sun — for midges. Pillars of midges bend in the wind, crane flies bounce, bumblebees the size of your thumb work cotton-grass. Condensation on eyelashes blurs vision. A fine mesh head net and clear or amber glasses help enormously. Amber helps because with no shadows, russet/gold/acid-green domes and black flarks flatten into grey. Contrast lenses give you back edges.
**Hands:** thin wool liners + waterproof mitts you can wring. You will be probing, wringing sleeves, catching yourself.
**Feet are the mission.** Knee-high wide bog boots or waders with a soft, broad sole, not stiff hiking boots that punch through. Wool socks, 3-4 pairs in separate double dry-bags inside your pack *inside* another dry-bag. Gaiters. Bring foot powder, blister tape, and a plan to change socks standing on a dome, quickly, under shelter. Trench foot and blisters end trips here faster than cold.
**Poles + snowshoe logic.** Bring two adjustable poles with big baskets. Probe every black flat before you weight it. Flarks — the wet black flats between russet domes — can be floating mats over water. Domes 6-10 feet high are firmer but breathe and sigh under you. Do not jump. Spread weight. Walk dome to dome, slow, like snow.
**No tall metal.** You will be the tallest thing for miles. There are no trees. During high charge you will get St. Elmo's fire on shoulders, cotton-heads ticking blue. Use carbon or wood poles. Put electronics in a sealed Faraday pouch. Ideally bring almost no electronics. They will get wet, get charged, and lie to you anyway.

Bring high-calorie food you can eat cold and wet with numb hands: fat, sugar, salt. Thermos with hot sweet tea if you can keep it upright. No open flame near vents, and no fire from peat — it's wet on top, flammable history underneath, and cutting it would be vandalism on a hundred-year scale. Stove only on a freestanding platform, well away from blowholes. If you smell ozone + honey-rot strongly, move upwind, don't light anything.

### 3. Water, air, electricity

**Do not drink the bog.** There is water everywhere and none of it is for you. Tarns are tea-black or sky-grey, acidic enough from sphagnum to pickle everything — that's why peat preserves. Pitcher plants knee-deep are full of rainwater and drowned midges and digestive enzymes, steaming faintly. They will blister your gut.

Carry all your water in, 2-3 liters for a day, plus a filter + neutralizing tablets as absolute backup. Never trail your whole hand to drink. For that matter, don't trail your hand in tarns at all for fun: flatworms and black newts gather in pools that pulse faintly, and the weak bog current will make fingertips go numb. If you have a heart condition, pacemaker, epilepsy, do not wade or sit in pools.

**Breathe slow.** Thin + wet air + exertion + cold = you will pant and get dizzy if you hurry. Pace at half your normal hill pace. Nose in, mouth out. A light buff helps warm air and filter midges, but it will soak — bring two and rotate.

**Charge protocol:** After a vent, when the air smells metallic/ozone, expect weird water for a minute. Rivulets on the glacial-egg boulders will bead *upward*. Tarn reflections will lag your hand by a breath. Sparks will crawl. That is your cue: crouch low if wind spikes, spread out from companions 20 feet, do not hold poles aloft, do not touch water, do not touch each other. Wait it out. It passes. Don't photograph with a metal tripod held high.

### 4. Walking and not getting lost where distance lies

Navigation here is not hard because it's featureless. It's hard because it's unfaithful.

You will hear geese land a full second before they come over the rim. You will hear your own boot squelch a half-step *before* you lift it. The deep thump sometimes doubles, with the second beat slightly earlier. Your brain will try to correct and will make you stumble.

Rules for ordinary nerves:

1. **Trust sight over sound.** Let sound arrive early and don't flinch. Wear foam earplugs if the lead-lag makes you nauseous. When ptarmigan burst white from ankle-high willow mats, or a whole wedge of small dark geese slaps down into a tarn, freeze, look, then move.

2. **Never navigate unwatched.** That herd of pale reindeer-kin — shaggy white-grey, moss in their coats until they look like moving hummocks, 30-50 with bawling calves churning water to soup — was a quarter mile off. You look down to wring your sleeve. You look up and they are 60 feet away, breathing fog, staring, with untrampled moss between you. They did not walk. Or you walked too many steps toward a dome you stared at.

Travel in pairs. One person always keeps eyes on the reference — herd, dome, rim cloud. Count steps out loud. Drop no cairns, cut nothing, but *do* lay a temporary floating line: 100 feet of bright, light cord between you and your last firm dome when fog thickens. Reel it in. Leave nothing.

Do not chase a herd that appears. Do not walk toward a sound that arrived before its cause. Stop, re-anchor to something close and touchable — your pole in a willow mat, a weeping boulder — and re-observe.

3. **Expect ground to change while you blink.** Frost heave lifts plates of moss overnight into small cliffs that collapse by noon with a wet *whumpf*. A tarn drains sideways through a peat pipe leaving newts flipping, then refills from fog an hour later. A reindeer path floods, sphagnum visibly grows over prints. Do not camp in a hollow, do not pitch in a flark, do not trust a pool edge. Domes sigh and settle under weight — if yours chuffs faster when you stand on it, move. You're heavy.

The rim drops into cloud on all sides. In diffuse light you cannot tell where it starts. Set a hard turnaround bearing and a hard turnaround time before you enter. Use a mechanical compass on a lanyard, not just GPS — GPS sulks in cloud and charge. Mark your entry with a GPS waypoint *at the rim* plus a bright pack cover left with a buddy, not with a built marker. Do not walk to the edge in whiteout to look.

### 5. Animals, plants, ghosts

There is a lot of life and none of it needs you.

Reindeer-kin, geese, ptarmigan, fox the color of peat watching from a boulder, shrews swimming in pitchers, clouds of midges. Give herds 100 feet minimum. Especially calves. They seem placid drifting between domes tearing sedge, but churned black soup hides legs and holes, and a startled herd moving through you will knock you into water you can't stand up in quickly with a pack.

Insects: repellent for skin, permethrin for clothes beforehand, head net always up. They pillar above warm things — including you if you stand still. Keep moving slowly.

Plants: dwarf willow no higher than your ankle can be 100 years old. Step on moss, not on willow mats if you can avoid it. Cranberry vine threading everywhere trips ankles. Sundew rims tremble around tarns — beautiful, glue-covered, will gum your laces and waterproofs. Cotton-grass heads all turn at once though there is no sun to follow. Do not use them as a compass. Do not pick pitcher plants, do not empty them, do not step in them — you will slip knee-deep and the sides collapse.

And the dead: you will find a reindeer skeleton sunk in sphagnum, antlers hung with sundew. Walk around a dome and on the other side find the same skeleton standing, flesh on, grazing, peat still in its ribs. Blink and it's gone, but the midges are still parted.

Do not follow it. Do not touch it. Do not photograph-hunt it. Say to yourself, out loud if needed: backward second. Keep walking your line. If it troubles you, keep the dome between you and it and keep your watcher watching you, not it. These echoes don't interact — they graze, they part midges, they go. Let them.

Same for reflections that lag. Don't use a tarn as a mirror. Don't shave, don't adjust gear by reflection.

### 6. Sleep, if you must

Light does not change. Your body will not know when to sleep, and breath hangs so tents get soaked from inside. If you must overnight, make it short and high:

Freestanding tent or bivy, no stakes — stakes tear century-growth and won't hold in lung-sponge anyway. Weight with packs, use snow/sand bags filled with water if you need ballast. Large closed-cell foam pad plus inflatable, to spread load and insulate from cold pump below. Vent aggressively, even in rain — otherwise your own exhalation will rain back on you. Earplugs + sleep mask + watch alarm. Do not sleep in a hollow, near a blowhole, or beside a tarn that can drain/fill.

Better: don't overnight on the table. Day-trip from the rim. 4-6 hours inside is plenty for a first visit. Cold + sensory monotony + constant micro-déjà vu exhausts faster than miles. Plan to be tired and a little weepy. That's normal here.

### 7. Pack like you mean to leave no trace

Because nothing is built, everything works — and your job is to not break the working.

Pack in / pack out, plus:
- Spare socks, liners, buff in dry bags
- 2 poles, cord line, compass, paper map in waterproof case, watch
- Head net, amber glasses, earplugs, sleep mask
- High-fat food + thermos, all water
- Small stove + windscreen, no saw, no axe, no shovel
- First aid: blister care, antihistamine, eye drops for acid fog irritation, elastic bandage, hypothermia blanket
- Faraday pouch, spare batteries in sealed box, no drone — wind + charge + birds + sudden herds = loss
- Trash bag for *everything*, including sundew glue wipes. Do not wash gear in tarns.

What NOT to bring: dogs, speakers, bright lights to signal, metal detector, anything you want to build, anything you want to leave as a memorial. The bog pickles and keeps. It does not need your cairn.

### 8. How to be in it

Move slow, breathe slow, look a lot. Sit once on a firm dome on your pad and feel the chuff through your knees without lying down. Watch mile after mile of wet breathing islands go out to cloud, thumping and hissing and crackling. Let herds appear and disappear without needing to cross the space between. Let water rise from below and fall from above to meet in the middle, in the plant that is the earth.

You will get wet, get sparked on the shoulders, get pillared by midges, get lost by sixty feet while standing still. Your prints will fill instantly, dark. Your breath will not dissipate.

Take that as instruction: don't try to fix the glitches. There was never a builder to assume the world should behave. The moss doesn't care if time runs backward for a second. It keeps pumping.

Go in, stay warm-wet, keep your watcher watching, leave the substrate as you found it, and come back to the rim before the dim convinces you that dawn will finally commit.
