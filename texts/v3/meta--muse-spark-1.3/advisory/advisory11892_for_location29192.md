If you go, go as a human. That is both the problem and the protection. This place was not made for you, but you can stand in it for a little while if you prepare to be wrong, wet, deaf, and lost in a space smaller than a field.

Read this as a field briefing, not poetry.

### What it actually is to be there

Forget lake. There is no surface to look across, no sky to look up to. There is a saddle of new black rock, maybe 60 breaths across if the water is kind, pinched between two teeth, sitting so high the clouds are underneath you like a slow white sea.

Over that saddle stands a cylinder of water-air, about 300 paces across, warm as blood. You don't walk under rain. You walk *into* rain that has decided to be a room. Your ears pop on entry. Light quits. Sound doesn't.

The floor is chest-to-knee warm tea-dark water over young basalt and rope-lava still glazed. It is sharp as broken bottles. There is no soil, only ground glass and ash that puffs up around your ankles in black milk and then clears sideways, too fast. Pumice like light bone rattles around your calves and never settles. The rim is actively crumbling and rebuilding itself - fins shiver off into the cloud-sea silently and new ones steam up by morning. Do not trust edges.

Light at noon looks like late afternoon underwater: olive-grey, sourceless, no shadows. Your hands and wet rocks glow faintly at the edges. Above is churn and spray lit from inside by slow, bruised flickers that hang too long to be lightning.

You will be warm and soaked at once. Skin prunes and steams. Breathing is drinking - half air, half sweet mineral mist that makes you cough, and coughing feels right. At the bottom of everything is bass you feel in your molars: water pouring off the lip on all sides into the clouds below. On top of that, white static hiss. Inside that, too-clear details: pumice clacking, fresh rock spalling with a glassy *tink* under water, irregular deep *whoom* when volcanic gas burps through a pit and sends a ring outward that climbs the wall and vanishes, and occasional rifle-crack when rock splits under your feet.

Don't shout. The roar eats it before it leaves your mouth, and any sharp noise summons a lateral wind that throws a sheet of warm water hard enough to stagger you.

Life is spaced out, embarrassed. Black-green algae beards flattened by current, olive moss mats that inflate/deflate, five or six bent-double umbrella trees with swollen trunks and waxy cupped leaves rattling like distant applause. Animals: translucent shrimp on the algae, a blind white forearm-long fish that rolls up from pits and vanishes, an ash-colored crab frozen under a ledge letting torrent pass over, once in a while a lost storm-petrel shearing through screaming without landing. Lots of nothing between presences.

People, or something patient, were here. Low ring of basalt slabs around deeper water in the center. A cairn on the highest tooth, knocked down and rebuilt. Three steps cut east leading down into white cloud and stopping mid-air where the rim fell away. Offerings in cracks: grass knot, river stone from far below, cloth strip whipping and never tearing. No metal, no tools. Only weight and balance.

And the rules are bent structurally:

Center water mounds a hand higher than edges and streams run *uphill* to feed it. Your reflection moves a half-second before you do. Drips fall *up* after a whoom. Roar sometimes arrives *before* the surge. Walk the perimeter with the drop on your left and you return with the drop still on your left. Dropped stones don't plunk, they sound like a far door closing, and minutes later sometimes rattle around your ankles.

It rehearses itself, slightly louder each time. The water never rises or drops though it pours off every edge. If you try to climb the teeth to leave, the wall thickens, rock sharpens, roar pitches up until you turn back.

It's terrible and, inexplicably, it feels like shelter. That feeling is a hazard.

### How to prepare your body

**1. Be fit to wade, not to hike.** You will be chest-deep, braced, probing, staggering. You need legs, ankles, core. Practice walking in chest-deep warm water against a current while looking at your feet. If you can't stand on one leg on slippery rock while someone shoves you sideways with a bucket of warm water, don't go.

Altitude + hot mist is brutal. Acclimatize below if you can. This is high. You will be mildly hypoxic and breathing wet air. Asthma, bad sinuses, chronic ear trouble, heart issues, seizure prone to strobes - do not go. Bring an inhaler in a hard dry case even if you haven't needed it in years.

Get tetanus up to date. You *will* get micro-cuts. Warm volcanic water + ground glass slurry = infection highway.

**2. Ears, eyes, lungs, feet - protect all four.**

Ears: You need vented, warm-water ear plugs + a neoprene headband to keep them in. Not foam plugs that block everything - you still need to feel the bass change before a surge. Your ears will pop on entry. Equalize gently, don't blow hard. After, dry your ears obsessively. Warm standing water is perfect for otitis. Bring alcohol-vinegar ear drops.

Eyes: Ash-glass slurry, warm spray, inverted rain. Bring sealed swim goggles or a full-face snorkel mask that you have defogged and tested in a hot shower. Plus a low, diffuse waterproof headlamp - light dies here, a tight beam just bounces. Bring backup in a separate dry bag. Expect no shadows.

Lungs: Bring a nose clip. Breathe through mouth, slow, let yourself cough. The mist tastes sweet/iron/green. Do not try to drink it as water. Do not hold your breath for long unless a *whoom* happens - then turn your face, close mouth briefly, because that burp can be CO2, H2S, SO2. If you smell rotten eggs sharply stronger than the baseline iron/hot stone, move cross-wind, crouch low? No - gas sits low over water. Stand taller, move toward rim-upwind, leave center. A pocket gas monitor that beeps H2S/CO2 would save your life, sealed in a waterproof pouch where you can see it flash. If you can't bring one, limit center time to minutes and never put your face near pit water.

Feet: This is the most important. Thick vulcanized wading boots with hard soles, not sandals, not barefoot, not thin water shoes. New lava + vesicles popping + spalling = razor. Add neoprene socks underneath, shin guards or old wetsuit lower legs. Pumice will batter your calves. Bring gloves - hot, grippy, cut-resistant gardening or canyoning gloves. You will need your hands on rock.

Skin: Rashguard + light wetsuit top or full thin wetsuit. Not for cold - water/rock/steam are bath-warm - but for abrasion and sunless burn and glass rash. You will prune in 20 minutes. That's normal here. Plan for 30-45 minutes max inside the wall. Longer and you get heat-tired, deaf-tired, stupid. Bring 1.5L clean cool drinking water in a soft flask inside your suit - you will sweat and not notice because you're soaked. Dehydration in warm rain is real. Do not drink the saddle water. Tea-dark, iron, hot stone, shrimp, volcanic pits - it will gut you and may carry amoeba/legionella.

Head: Helmet. Light climbing or kayak helmet. Rock splits underfoot with a rifle crack and fresh spall flies. Plus the cairn tooth sheds.

Flotation: Even strong swimmers bring a low-profile inflatable snorkel vest or packable PFD. Not because knee-deep drowns you, but because green-black pits go down further than they should and a surge + trip + gasp of warm water can. You need to be able to go limp and float chest-high without touching bottom.

### What to bring, and what not to

Bring simple, tether everything:

- A long, stout staff. Wood or carbon, not precious. You will probe every step like a blind person. The warped shepherd's staff left there is a warning: the last person needed it. Test pits: if water turns deep green-black and your staff does not hit, do not enter. Back out.
- 10m floating buddy line with quick-release, if two of you. Solo is not recommended.
- Waterproof slate + grease pencil for communication. And pre-agreed hand/light signals: STOP, CROUCH/BRACE, PIT, BACK OUT, OK. You cannot shout.
- Watch/timer in a dry case. Time-blindness is instant in sourceless light.
- Small first aid in hard dry box: wound wash, antiseptic, butterfly closures, blister care, ear drops.
- Clean change + towel + fresh water rinse waiting *outside* the wall at your entry cache. You will want to get the glass-ash off immediately.

Do not bring: complex electronics expecting to work, drones, anything that needs silence, anything you can't afford to lose to a pit. And respect the pre-tool rule: no new metal left behind, no wire, no plastic offerings, no carving, no stacking your own cairn unless you know how. This place keeps everything - nothing rots, leaves and cloth are scoured bleached and kept. That means your trash, your skin cells, your introduced seeds will also stay. Clean all gear before entry to avoid bringing lowland spores. Pack out everything, even organic crumbs. Do not move the slabs, do not rebuild the cairn unless invited by a local custom you understand, do not take the grass knot, river stone, cloth. Do not descend the three east steps. They end in air.

If you must leave something, make it weight and balance and patience: a balanced stone low down, not high, not metal. Then leave it.

### How to move inside

**Enter from the rim, not the middle.** Cache, observe clouds below from safe distance back from crumbling lip, note wind, note where water is pouring off. Enter the wall slowly, let ears adjust. Stand still for 10 breaths. Let your eyes give up on shadows.

Walk slow, staff first. Shuffle, don't step high - pumice will trip you. Stay out of green-black pit water. Stay away from the outer lip where the thousand throats pour off. One slip there and you join the fin falling silently into cloud-sea. Stay low when you hear pitch rise.

Center mounds up: expect to walk *slightly uphill* through water that wants to push you toward center, then suck you sideways. Lean into it, wide stance.

When you hear a deep *whoom*: crouch, feet wide, staff braced downstream, face turned, mouth closed for 3 seconds. Expect inverted rain - drips rising - and a ring traveling outward. Don't chase it.

When roar arrives *before* you feel surge: trust it. Brace first, question later. The place tells the future loudly.

When you hear rifle-crack underfoot: freeze, spread weight, probe, backtrack along your own disturbed slurry line. Fresh rock is making/unmaking itself under you.

Do not stare at reflections in still seconds. Your hand will be late. It will make you nauseous and swap up-slope/down-slope in your head. Look at staff tip, at algae streaming direction (that tells true current), at your buddy's knees. If you feel the Möbius - walked the perimeter and drop stayed on left - don't panic and don't keep walking to fix it. Stop, turn 180, retrace. Count breaths. Use timer: 60 breaths to cross means you should never be more than ~5 minutes from center.

Expect your dropped stone, cap, glove to come back. Tether it all. Don't drop stones into pits to plumb them by sound - you'll hear a door far away and then get haunted minutes later.

Keep noise low. Pad buckles with tape. Whisper if you must, but prefer gestures. The wind answers loudness with sideways water.

### Mind preparation

You will be deafened, time-dilated, direction-thinned, alone-but-watched by shrimp and crab. The emptiness is part of ecology - don't fill it with chatter.

Practice beforehand: stand in a hot shower fully clothed with white noise at max for 10 minutes and practice hand signals with a friend. Notice how quickly you get irritated, then calm, then weirdly comforted. That comfort is the shelter-trap. People want to stay because below - drowned or burned or continued, you can't remember - is worse. Set a hard exit time before entry and obey the roar-pitch: if wall thickens and pitch climbs when you look at the teeth, you are being told to turn back. Turn back.

After: you will be wrung out, pruned, steaming, ears ringing, eyes stinging. Rinse with clean water, especially feet/legs/eyes/ears. Check every cut. Sit in real silence for at least as long as you were inside. Don't drive, don't portal-jump complex sequences immediately. Tinnitus for hours is common. Fever, ear pain, eye pain, cough that worsens after a day, foot red streaks - get medical help and say: warm freshwater volcanic exposure, possible glass abrasion.

Ordinary human rule: you get to visit the drowning held in one place, let it scour you clean for a little while, and then you must leave it small. Don't try to conquer, map, drain, or stay.

Go in warm, soft-footed, quiet, tethered, timed. Probe, brace, witness the crab holding still. Leave no rot because nothing rots. Come back out into the cloud-sea rim, dry your ears, drink cool water, and be loud again only when you are well clear.
