This is not a hike. It's a day-trip to the edge of what your body can do, in a place that is alive without being living.

Think high southern Andes — 4,800 - 5,500m / 15,700 - 18,000 ft kind of high. Air so thin it doesn't feel like air. Sun so unfiltered the sky goes violet at noon and the ground goes white-hot. No plants. No animals. No soil smell. Just old, settled granite, sorted by frost into nets and polygons, and a shallow saddle bowl full of ice knives leaning north toward the midday sun, and human cairns mixed in with frost-stood stones.

You do not sleep there. You go up, you add a stone, you go down. Everything about your preparation should serve that.

### 1. Your body is the problem

You will be unreliable up there, and that's normal.

After three steps on level ground: out of breath. Heart hammering at rest. Dull headache behind the forehead. Clumsy fingers. Slightly stupid, slightly euphoric, slightly anxious. Your lowland brain will also keep misfiring because there's no green — not even orange lichen — and the shadows are hard-edged and wet-blue, and at sunset the whole bowl will feel crowded with presence though nothing lives.

Prepare for that:

**Acclimatize or don't go.** This is not negotiable. Spend 2-4 nights stepping up: e.g. sleep ~3,000m, then ~3,800-4,200m, with day climbs higher, sleep lower. Don't go from sea level to that bowl in a day. If you can, spend a week in the range first.

Climb high, sleep low. You do *not* camp in the saddle. The windbreak — that low, three-course dry-stack open to the east — is for crouching, not sleeping. Plan to be down at least 500-800m lower by nightfall.

**Learn Acute Mountain Sickness.** Headache + nausea / dizziness / profound fatigue / poor sleep = stop ascending. Clumsiness, staggering like drunk, confusion, persistent cough, breathlessness at rest = HACE / HAPE. That means turn around *now* and descend. Hypoxia destroys judgment, so decide your turn-around rules down low, and climb with a partner who will enforce them.

If your doctor approves, talk about acetazolamide beforehand. It's not a substitute for acclimatization. Bring a fingertip pulse oximeter — at that height 80-88% is common even when you feel okay, but a dropping number with worsening symptoms helps you make the call to descend.

**Pace.** Three steps, breathe. Pressure-breathe — purse lips on exhale. Rest step on scree. Do not try to power through the loose, rounded granite approach. You will sweat, then freeze when the 4pm temperature drop hits.

Get a cardio check before you go. Carry a tiny emergency oxygen canister if you can get one; it won't let you stay, but it can buy you a safe descent.

### 2. Light will hurt you before cold does

At first you think the brightness is snow. It isn't. It's thin air + white ice + white granite + UV bouncing from every direction.

This will blind and burn you in under an hour without protection. And because penitentes tilt a few degrees north here — Southern Hemisphere sun — the light comes at you from above *and* below.

You need:

* **Glacier glasses, Category 4, with side shields.** Not fashion sunglasses, not Cat 3 running glasses. Snow blindness is a sunburn of the cornea: tearing, pain like sand, hours later at camp. Bring a spare pair and a strap. Consider ski goggles for wind-driven rime in the afternoon.
* **Total skin cover.** SPF 50+ broad spectrum, reapplied every 90 minutes. Stick SPF for nose, ears, lips, under chin, under nose, inside nostrils rim — reflected UV burns from below. Zinc oxide helps. SPF lip balm, two of them.
* **Hat + buff + hood.** Wide-brim sun hat under a hood, plus a UV buff you can pull over nose and mouth. Your jacket sleeves will go stiff with rime in the afternoon; your face will do the same if uncovered.
* **Long sleeves, light colors.** UPF shirt, lightweight sun gloves for the middle of the day.

The weird light does psychological things too. At midday everything is white and depthless. At sunset the west faces of the blades go translucent honey then rose, blue shadows overlap three-deep, pools catch fire, quartz sparkles. It glows from underneath. It feels haunted. It isn't. It's optics + hypoxia + exhaustion. Admire it, add your stone, leave. Don't make navigation decisions in that moment.

### 3. Dress for a 20-degree cliff

At 2pm: blazing sun, inch-deep sky-clear melt pools plinking under the ice, rills a finger wide running 3-4 meters then vanishing, air damp, everything dripping.

By 4pm: cloud lifts, temp drops 10C / 18F in 20 minutes, every drop skins to black glass. By sunset: fingers stupid even in gloves, hum stops, white goes gray-blue.

Layer like a mountaineer, not a hiker:

Base: merino/synthetic, no cotton.
Mid: active fleece + puffy you can throw on instantly.
Shell: truly windproof and waterproof jacket + pants. You need this despite the desert — afternoon condensation will feather rime onto you, and wind threading through a thousand ice slots cuts straight through softshell.
Hands: thin sun liner gloves + warm windproof gloves + big mittens in pack. You will lose dexterity fast after sundown.
Feet: stiff waterproof mountaineering or heavy trekking boots with aggressive sole, gaiters, wool socks + spare. Penitentes are knives. Scree is loose. Melt refreezes to glass. You will be turning sideways between blades knee to shoulder high. Approach shoes will get sliced and soaked.
Poles: essential for balance and for probing pits between blades. Helmet is overkill for most, but don't put your eyes near a blade tip — they *tock* and snap when undercut by sun.

Bring a headlamp anyway. You plan to be down before dark, but that cold cliff after sunset kills people who mis-timed it.

### 4. Water, food, air

There is no stream, no lake. What water exists is atmospheric and temporary: afternoon cloud condensing to rime on north sides, 2pm melt in the pits, percolation you hear before you see.

**Carry all your water.** 3-4 liters for the day, in insulated bottles or with insulated sleeves, carried inside pack not outside mesh. Plain bottles will skin with ice by late afternoon. Drink on schedule — altitude suppresses thirst and appetite while doubling water loss through breathing.

Don't count on melt pools. They're tiny, silty with granite flour, and frozen solid most of the day. If you must use them, filter + UV/chemical treat. Don't break blades to get at water; the formation migrates slowly all season and you’ll just cut yourself.

Eat 3,500-4,500 kcal worth of easy, salty-sweet carbs you can force down when nauseated: gels, bars, nuts, cheese, coca tea if local custom provides it, etc. No alcohol for 48 hours before. No heavy, hard-to-digest meals that morning.

Pee will be frequent and clear — that's good. Pack out *everything*, including toilet paper and food scraps. Nothing rots this high and cold. That twist of copper wire and bleached red-blue cloth have lasted for years because decomposition basically stops.

### 5. Moving through the bowl

The ground levels just for a little while: ~100 paces long, half that wide, trail falling away front and back, pale cliffs streaked black with manganese and red with iron left and right.

It's fragile and sharp:

* Walk on durable gravel, not on patterned ground polygons. Those frost-heaved nets haven't moved in centuries except millimeters. One boot print ruins the sorting.
* Don't walk straight through penitentes. Turn sideways, step in pits, test before weighting — there's gravel and black ice under firn. Blade edges will slice gaiters and shins.
* Expect sound, not silence. Wind hum that changes pitch when you move your head, ice ticking like cooling coals, melt percolating, pebbles clicking under frost-heave, that little cloth flag tick-tick-ticking. If you crouch until your breathing slows you'll hear it working: sun eats ice, ice shades stone, stone holds night-cold, melt jacks stones a millimeter higher, wind sharpens again.
* Navigation: GPS track + downloaded map + compass. Afternoon cloud pushes up thousands of meters from below and whites everything out for an hour. Trail ducks below are big; the cairns in the bowl are *not* trail markers — they're fist-sized, 5-9 stones high, obsessive, clustered where ice is thinnest. Don't follow them.

Tell someone your route and latest return time. Carry satellite messenger — there is no cell, no birds, no help. Assume self-rescue.

### 6. People were here. Act like a guest.

This is a shrine, an apacheta-type place. People could not stay, so they left presence instead.

Rule is: add, don't take. Make it more crowded.

How to do it right:

* **Bring your stone from below.** Don't pry one out of the patterned ground or a frost-stood stone. Pick a flat, hand-fitting granite piece from loose trail scree lower down and carry it up.
* **Add to an existing cairn.** Choose one that's leaning and right it gently, or set your stone carefully on top so it will survive the night's cracking. Don't build a whole new village. Don't take a souvenir quartz fleck. The granite glitter is part of the place.
* **Don't touch offerings.** The collapsed niche with lintel fallen, the copper twist, the featureless wooden bead, the red-blue cloth on a stick wedged between rocks — look, don't handle, don't photograph up close without thought, don't add your own trash as an offering. Don't rebuild the niche unless a local custodian asks you to.
* **Shelter etiquette.** The lee-side windbreak is shared. Crouch behind it, get out of wind, leave it as found. No fire, no stove inside it — stoves barely burn up here anyway, and soot would mark it for years.
* If there are local communities who maintain this pass, hire a local guide, ask the name they use for it — it's not "penitentes" to them — and ask what is appropriate. Different passes have different rules about prayer, alcohol libation, coca leaves, silence.

From a distance your cairn will look like another ice blade. Up close it will be warm gray. That's the point.

### 7. Timing your day

Go early, be in the bowl around late morning to early afternoon, be *going down* by mid-afternoon glow.

Ideal: On trail at dawn frozen solid. Climb on hard scree. Arrive 11am-1pm when pits hold melt and rime feathers everything and you can hear plink-plink deep under. Experience the 2pm damp hour.

Stay for the beginning of the trapping light if you are strong, acclimatized, and have margin — that honey-rose translucence and long blue shadows — but have gloves on, puffy on, headlamp accessible, and be walking down the moment the sun clears the rim. That is when temperature falls off a cliff and your fingers go stupid and boots go loud on glass.

Never be route-finding on scree after that gray-blue goes dark. No one will come looking until morning, and morning is a long, frozen way away.

### Pack list, distilled

Acclimatization days + partner + filed trip plan; Cat 4 glacier glasses + spare + goggles; wide hat, buff, hood; SPF 50 + lip SPF; base + fleece + puffy + wind-waterproof shell top/bottom; liner gloves + warm gloves + mittens; stiff boots + gaiters + 2x socks; poles; 3-4L insulated water + salty carbs; headlamp + extra batteries; satellite messenger, map/GPS, oximeter, personal meds; first aid + blister kit + emergency bivy sack; cash for local guide, and a flat stone from below.

You will leave the dense, damp, lifeless bowl to its own business of freezing and melting and sharpening itself. If you prepared right, you'll remember not suffering, but the light getting trapped and the ticking cold — and the small, precise weight of your stone settling onto the tower and holding through the night wind.
