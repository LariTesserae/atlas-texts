This is not a hike. Don't prepare for it like one.

What you're walking into is a world still being made. A high, raw, newborn range, at night, with no stars, no water, no life, and heat still inside the rock. If you are an ordinary, soft, wet, oxygen-hungry human, you do not belong there. You can visit, briefly and carefully, if you prepare like you're going to high altitude, into a desert, onto an active volcano, and into a cave — all at once.

Here is what your body will get wrong, and how to compensate:

### 1. Understand the place first

Forget cold mountains. Up there at night it should bite. It won't. The air will be warm, dry, thin, and dead.

Warm because the ground itself is warm. You are standing on a thin crust over something molten. The rock under your palm will feel like a stove that was just turned off — not sun-warm, deep-warm.

Thin because you are incredibly high. Your inner ears will pop before your eyes work. You'll be short of breath standing still. Every breath will taste of hot stone and iron and faint sulfur, and it won't satisfy you. You'll have to breathe deliberately, wider than feels natural.

Dark, but not from night alone. The sky is a lid of dust and heavy air, black-brown. No moon, no stars, no town glow. The only light seeps *up* from finger-wide cracks in the ridge — dull orange, pulsing very slowly. It doesn't illuminate. It suggests.

And everything is unfinished and moving. Nothing is rounded. No scree, no soil, no water-worn anything. Just fresh-broken cleavage planes, knife arêtes, cube boulders balanced where they were thrown. Every few minutes the ground shrugs. Slabs settle a foot with a sigh of dust. A new fang shoulders up on the skyline while you blink. A spire across the gulf collapses in total silence. It should roar. It doesn't, because thin air and hanging dust eat sound.

Your footsteps will be a muffled crunch that stops the second you stop. Your breathing will be loud inside your skull and tiny outside it. There is no wind, only slow turnovers of warm air rising off the cracks.

Internalize this: **sound will not warn you. Shelter does not exist. Water does not exist.**

The little huts, cairns, ruined walls along the crest? No one built them. It's fast-cooled rock extruding into columns and right angles, hollow towers, impossible balanced slabs, with straight cooling striations your brain will desperately try to read as writing. Don't go in them. Don't touch them. Don't shelter in them. They will be gone by morning — fallen or risen into something else.

### 2. Air: This is your killer #1

You need supplemental oxygen. Not optional. Not "if you're sensitive."

The air is both too thin *and* bad. Low O2, likely high CO2, fine silica + sulfur dust, metallic vapor. A dust filter alone is not enough because even filtered, there's not enough to keep you thinking straight.

For an ordinary traveler:

**Bring:** Lightweight SCBA or a mountaineering bottled O2 system with a full-face sealed mask + P100/P3 particulate pre-filter. At minimum: high-flow O2 bottles + sealed goggles + half-face respirator rated for silica and acid gases. Bring a small clip-on O2/CO2/CO pulse monitor with a *vibrating* alarm — you will not hear a beep here.

**Do before:** No alcohol 48 hours before. No sedatives. Hydrate heavily. If you have asthma, anemia, heart issues, poor balance, claustrophobia in a mask — do not go. Spend a day practicing walking slowly while mouth-breathing deliberately through the mask. You will want to pant. Don't. Slow, wide breaths.

**Rule on site:** If you get a headache, euphoria, sudden calm, clumsiness, tunnel vision, or your lips go blue-gray, you are already impaired. Leave immediately. Don't "push through." Altitude stupidity + silent rockfall = death.

Do NOT lie down or sit in hollows or behind slabs to rest. Heavy CO2 pools low and still. Stay upright, stay on the crest, keep your face above the dust layer.

### 3. Water and Heat: Killer #2

Expect brutal, invisible dehydration. The dryness pulls moisture off your lips in minutes. Sweat evaporates before it beads, so you won't realize you're losing water.

And the ground can burn you through your soles.

**Clothing:** Do NOT bring a big alpine down jacket. You'll overheat and be clumsy. Dress like a desert volcanologist:

- Long, tough, abrasion-proof pants and long sleeves — ripstop, fire-resistant if you have it. Your skin must never touch sharp rock.
- Merino or synthetic base that wicks. No cotton.
- Thick leather work gloves, not thin hiking gloves. The corners will cut rope and hands.
- Heat-rated mountaineering boots with stiff, thick soles (rated to 150-200C contact) + heat gaiters. Test by standing on hot pavement. If you feel heat through in seconds, they're not enough. Bring a cheap IR thermometer gun — point it at ground before every step near glow. Over 60C? Don't stand there.
- Helmet, always. Rock doesn't fall with a warning rattle here, it just lets go.
- Sealed goggles, not sunglasses. Clear or yellow for night. Dust is glass-sharp and hangs.

**Water:** Pre-hydrate with electrolytes. Carry at least 1 liter per 30-45 minutes you plan to stay, plus electrolytes + lip balm + nasal saline. There is no water to find. Not in cracks, not under rocks. Don't look.

### 4. Ground: Killer #3

Learn the local rules fast:

Warm rock = solid. Cold rock = about to move.
Dust moving when air is still = ground moving under it.
Edges are torn, not carved, and still tearing.

**How to walk:**

- Bring 2 trekking poles with carbide tips and spare baskets. Your balance will be off from altitude + vertigo + the hollow-drum feeling underfoot. The ridge feels resonant, like crust over a drum. It is.
- Stay *on* the broad back of the ridge. Never on an edge for a photo. They calve silently. A hundred yards away looks solid until it drops a foot.
- Never step across an orange crack. Never put a hand in one to warm up. Temperatures jump from warm to flesh-melting in inches. Navigate by heat on your face — if one cheek gets suddenly hot, stop and look down.
- Light steps. Don't stomp, don't jump between boulders. Test every slab with a pole tap and watch the dust around it. If dust puffs or rises without wind, back off.
- Rope is nearly useless — sharp edges will cut it. If you go with a buddy (you should), tether with a short, visual tether on the crest, not for falls off cliffs — there is no arresting that scale — but to keep each other from wandering in the dark.

Bring a spool of high-temp line or glow cord to mark your return path. Don't build cairns. The place rearranges itself.

### 5. Light and Navigation in Black-on-Black

Your eyes will take 20-30 minutes to find edges: sawtooths, leaning needles, black against blacker. Crack-glow throws shadows the wrong way because the source moves underground.

**Bring:**
- Two headlamps (white + red) + helmet spare, with fresh batteries, in sealed dust bags. Use red most of the time to preserve your ability to see the ember seep.
- A small thermal monocular or phone thermal attachment if you can get one. It's worth more than a brighter flashlight here — it shows you hot cracks, recent shifts, cold unstable slabs.
- Vibrating compass / inertial tracker. Don't rely on GPS — assume it doesn't work on a newborn world. Memorize: count the standing columns as you pass, note their shapes. They will try to fool you because they look regular.

Don't chase distant huts. That cluster "half a mile on" is much further and much larger than you think. Scale is wrong for walking. This ridge alone would be a country. Plan for a very short out-and-back: 15 minutes out, 15 back. That's plenty.

### 6. Quiet, Mind, and Pareidolia

It is profoundly quiet. That messes with people more than they expect. No insects, no birds, no water, no leaves. Just an occasional deep groan you feel more in your teeth than hear, then the quiet rushes back heavier.

In that quiet, next to those too-regular columns, you will feel watched by geometry. You'll see doorways that go nowhere, marks that look like writing. That's your brain begging for pattern. Don't answer it.

- Bring earplugs *out*. You need all the faint cues you can get, but they won't be much.
- Agree on visual signals with your buddy before you go: hand light sweeps, shoulder taps. You won't hear a shout at 20 meters.
- Set a hard time limit and a vibrating timer: 45-60 minutes max for an ordinary human on bottles. When it buzzes, turn, no matter how beautiful the sulfur frost is.
 That pale yellow-white frost in sheltered lees — branching like frost, like mycelium — is not alive. Don't touch it barehanded. It's sulfur and silica precipitates, warm powder. It rehearses biology but it's chemistry, and it will irritate lungs and eyes. Photograph it, don't collect it without gloves and a sealed vial.

- After you leave, expect to feel oddly loud and heavy for an hour. That's normal — your brain recalibrating to real sound and real air.

### Pack List for an Ordinary Human

**Wear:** helmet, sealed goggles, respirator/full-face mask, base layer, tough shell pants/jacket, leather gloves, heat-rated boots + gaiters.

**Carry:**
- O2 bottles + regulator + spare (calculate 2x what you think)
- 3L water + electrolytes
- 2 headlamps + spare batteries
- IR temp gun, O2/CO2 monitor with vibration
- Trekking poles, short buddy tether, spool line
- Thermal viewer if possible
- First aid: blister/burn kit, eye wash ampoules, emergency space blanket (not for cold — for signaling and sitting on hot rock if forced)
- Sealed bag for all electronics — dust kills zippers and lungs

**Do NOT bring:** open flame, camp stove, tent, sleeping bag, water filter (nothing to filter), light sneakers, shorts, contact lenses without sealed goggles, anything you can't afford to abandon if ground shifts.

### The Moment You Arrive: STOP

Stop. Think. Observe. Plan.

Stand still for 2 full minutes and just breathe. Feel the tremor cycle. Watch a dust plume — does it fall or hang and rise? Hang means strong heat below. Look for glow cracks near your feet. Point your temp gun around. Find the warm-solid line and put your back to your exit.

Then walk slow. Watch dust, not edges. Touch nothing you don't need to touch. Leave no sweat, take no stones — everything sharp and hot and unstable will just make you bleed heat and water.

### Leave Immediately If:

- Hot wind on face with no visible crack, or soles suddenly hot
- Dust rising in a line, or a dry sighing sound/feel underfoot
- Headache, nausea, metallic taste getting stronger, vision graying
- A slab you just passed looks different than you remember — it probably is
- Your timer buzzes

You're standing on the skin of a world being made, high up where air shouldn't be warm, in starless dark among ruins that were never cities. It's magnificent. It's also completely indifferent to whether your lungs work or your blood stays hydrated.

Go small, go sealed, go hydrated, go together, and go home early — with your lungs, eyes, and boots intact, and the memory of mountains being born without making a noise about it.
