You need to prepare for this one backwards.

Most lush places you prepare for wet, for rot, for bugs, for growth. This is a lush place you prepare for like a desert, like a machine room, like a high-altitude climb — because that's what it is. A closet-sized cup of rock three thousand meters up that happens to be packed shoulder-to-shoulder with green that doesn't drink water.

If you go, this is what it will actually ask of your body.

### 1. Getting there is the real expedition. The garden is 10 minutes.

No one finds the hatch alone. You do not find it by looking. High on the ridge where the air is already thin and cold enough to hurt, the basalt narrows to a knife blade. The valley is a gray rug far below. The wind is constant and carries ice-dust. The tramway tower is bolted straight into the rock with cables screaming off into white.

You will be focused on not falling, on the exposure, on the noise of the cables. That's why everyone misses the door. It's waist-high, halfway up the north leg behind the counterweight housing, paint weathered to bare metal. You have to duck and go in sideways.

This means:

**Do not go alone, do not go without the linemen.** You need someone who works that tower to show you, to tell you when the counterweight is moving, to tell you when wind is too high to be on the leg. This is active industrial infrastructure. The chain in its channel above you goes clank... clank... clank and it will not stop for you. Get permission. If you can't get permission, don't go.

**Acclimatize like a climber.** You will be panting just crouching. The description says it straight: air thin and cold in your lungs. Spend 2-3 nights stepping up. No alcohol the night before. Drink far more water on approach than you think you need — you will arrive already dehydrated from breathing cold dry air. If you get headaches, nausea, stumbling, turn back. No garden is worth HAPE.

**Dress for wind, not for jungle.** You want: thermal base, fleece mid, windproof hard shell, windproof pants, warm hat that fits under a helmet, thin liner gloves under work gloves, mountaineering boots with good edge on basalt, sunglasses/goggles for ice-dust whiteout. It will be cloud-cold outside and breath-fog cold inside. The rock leaches heat out your back the second you lean on it.

**Wear a climbing helmet and a harness with a short tether.** The leg is exposed, the rock is slick, the door requires you to contort sideways with your hands full. Gusts up there knock people sideways. Clip in to the tower before you duck. Leave your big pack clipped outside — you cannot fit through with it and you cannot turn around inside. Take only what fits in chest pockets.

People with bad knees, bad back, bad claustrophobia, pacemakers, or real sensitivity to noise/vibration should skip this. You cannot stand inside. The ceiling is too low. The floor slopes. It's about three paces by two. You will be on knees and elbows in moss.

### 2. What you bring inside

Think clean-room meets caving:

**1. Headlamp with dim warm/red mode — and use it sparingly.** The pocket is in perpetual evening. A cold blade of daylight from the door seam, the dim amber pilot jewel of the transformer, a pencil of overcast sky from a conduit hole. That's it. Your eyes will adjust and then the greens flare emerald at the edges. Do not blast it with 1000-lumen white. You'll blind yourself and everyone else, and you’ll wash out exactly what makes it strange. Bring a headlamp, turn it to low amber, and point it at your hands, not at the mat.

**2. P2/N95 dust mask or respirator + safety glasses.** Non-negotiable. The air smells of ozone and hot oil and chlorophyll, not damp. When you press the moss it crackles, doesn't squeeze. Your boots raise a fine dark dust that hangs and never settles. That dust is rock flour + graphite + dried leaf + copper salts + old dielectric oil mist. The big oil-cooled transformer in the east wall sweats a perpetual warm aerosol. Old towers like this are not clean. You don't want that deep in your lungs. A mask also catches most of your breath moisture — which matters, see below.

**3. Foam earplugs in your pocket.** You feel it in your teeth before you hear it: wind transmitted down steel as a low howl, the grinding chain, and underneath it never ceasing, the 50-cycle snarl of current, a wasp trapped in the walls. You have to raise your voice to talk. Ten minutes is fascinating. Thirty minutes gives most people a headache, jaw ache, nausea. Put plugs in halfway, take them out to listen once, then back in.

**4. Knee pads and clean, soft gloves.** You cannot set a hand down without setting it on leaves. Every rivet, every angle-iron, every inch of basalt is occupied — cushion moss knee-deep in mounds, papery rosettes overlapping like shingles, stonecrop beads on bolt-heads, translucent straps hanging from conduit. Wear soft garden-type gloves that you've washed and shaken clean, and flexible knee pads. Not to protect you. To spread your weight and shed fewer skin cells.

**5. Lip balm, saline, water — for *you*, outside.** Lick your lips in there and they chap within minutes. Your breath fog vanishes instantly. Noses bleed. Drink a half-liter before you go in, put balm on before your mask, drink after. Do NOT bring water in to drink, spill, or — god forbid — "help" the plants. The garden does not drink water. It drinks the tower.

That is the core rule to internalize before you enter:

Nothing flows. Nothing drinks. Nothing is eaten, and nothing leaves.

### 3. Inside: how to be

Duck sideways, low. Sit first, then swing knees in. The floor slopes slightly. You will immediately be touching it on knees, elbows, sleeves. Accept it. Trying not to touch just makes you flail and crush more.

Stay 10-15 minutes max on a first visit. Here's why:

You are food and weather in there. Those white hair-fine roots matted into felt on steel on stone — no soil, just felt — they eat rock dust, skin cells, grease, copper filings. Your body sheds heat, moisture, oils with every second. Your breath adds humidity to air that is supposed to be bone-dry. Your boots bring spores from below. In any other lush place that wouldn't matter. Here the whole system is locked in perfect balance: as much growth as the mist can feed, as much wear as the dry can take. Photos from '64 and '82 show the same mounds in the same places against the 7-B stencil. It does not advance. It does not die back. Don't be the gust that unbalances it.

So:

**Cover skin.** Long sleeves, long pants, gloves, mask. Breathe through your nose, directed toward the door seam, not down into the moss.

**Do not eat, do not spit, do not blow your nose, do not pee.** Crumbs here don't rot, they become permanent felt. There are no flies, no mites, no spiders, no birds, nothing to clean up after you. Pack out everything, even a tissue fiber.

**Look, don't pick.** The fronds propagate by flaking — a dry leaf breaks off, lodges a centimeter away, puts out white hairs. Dead material doesn't decay. If you pull a rosette or scrape moss off the 7-B stencil or the 1978 initials or the ladder rungs that now serve as trellises, you leave bare steel that will stay bare for decades. If you hold the palest hanging straps up, hold them *in place* up to the slit — you'll see the transformer light shining through from behind. Then let go gently. Don't wipe the oil-dust off to see the green better. That dull moth-wing gleam *is* its skin.

**Don't touch the transformer.** Hold your palm near it if you want — you'll feel a hand's-width of warmth, and you'll see the only gradient in the whole place: moss a fraction taller, a fraction yellower within ten centimeters, then it stops. Do not lean on the fins, do not tap it, do not touch wiring or the pilot jewel. It's hot, oily, caked in graphite dust, humming with current. People with pacemakers, hearing aids, epilepsy sensitive to flicker/hum: stay by the door.

**Be quiet.** You’ll have to shout to be heard, so don’t try to have a conversation. Agree on hand signals beforehand. The fronds tremble against your sleeves with vibration — you’ll feel the tower singing through your skull. Just listen once. That loud dark hum *is* the place.

Expect it to feel wrong in a specific way: leaves grow edge-on to light, face-on to vibration. Their undersides are freckled rust-red where they've taken iron straight from the plate. Some have veins of actual verdigris. Moss in the deepest corner where no light reaches is still blind electric green. It is lush without ever being wet, green that has learned to do without mouths. Let your brain sit with that instead of trying to fix it.

### 4. What not to expect, what not to do

Don't expect animals, buzzing, smell of damp earth, flowers, seeds, change with seasons. There is none. Don't expect to feel refreshed — most people come out with ringing ears, buzzing teeth, dry mouth, cold knees, oil-dust on their clothes that smells faintly metallic-sweet.

Do not bring a spray bottle, do not water the replacement bulb still in its cardboard sleeve cradled like an egg, do not oil the rust-welded wrenches in the dented toolbox, do not add your initials. The human marks are half-swallowed for a reason. No one maintains this. No one needs to.

Do not take souvenirs. Not a bead of stonecrop, not a flake. Besides the ethics, that dust has copper salts in it. Don't ingest it, don't rub your eyes, wash hands and gloves thoroughly after.

### 5. Leaving

Back out sideways, feet first. Clip in before you stand — people are dizzy from crouching + altitude + vibration and the drop is three thousand meters straight down.

Outside, beat the dust off *away* from the door. Brush boots, shake pants, empty pockets away from the ridge if you can. You don't want to seed valley fungi up here or carry this dry flake ecology down.

Sit for a minute with your back to the wind if it's safe. Drink. The hum will stay in your bones for an hour.

Final preparation is mental: you are not going to a garden that welcomes you. You are going to a mistake left over, roofed over with riveted plate decades ago, that settled into a form that doesn't need you and doesn't change for you. You crouch, green pressing into you from all sides in the loud dark, and for a few minutes you get to be inside a world whose rules are complete without you.

Go clean, go clipped, go brief, breathe shallow, touch as little as you can, and leave exactly the dry, humming, locked thing you found.
