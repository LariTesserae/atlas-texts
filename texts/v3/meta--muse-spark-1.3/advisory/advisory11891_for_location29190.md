You do not climb to this place. You ascend into it, and then you are swallowed.

Treat it like a dive, not a hike. Like visiting a coral reef inside a boiler. If you prepare for the mountain *or* the greenhouse you will suffer. You have to prepare for both at once, and for the slam between them.

### 1. Understand what it is

Forget valley, forget summit. This is a room built where a summit used to be.

Outside: 4,000 meters of nothing. Thin air that can't hold heat, grey scree, a sea of cloud below you. Cold, bright, windy, hypoxic. You will be stupid from lack of oxygen before you even get to the door.

Inside: a vertical tin can, maybe 60 paces across, 100+ meters tall, louvered at the crown. No stone, no soil, only tile and iron and copper and ceramic and glass, all of it painted over so many times the bolts have soft shoulders - and all of it grown over. Moss an inch thick on cold pipes, ferns out of bolt-holes, figs following cables down 20 meters for a drip, orchids clamped to sweating warm ducts, vines running horizontal on conduit, vertical on downspouts, like they learned industry as a trellis.

It is warm because the engines never stopped. Six compressor houses around the base ring, copper bus-bars, a 30-meter screw turning once a day, house-sized fans that stir once a minute. Wet, green, oily heat. 30C+ and near 100% humidity in a place that should be frozen to death every night.

It is deafening and very slow. Low compressor thrum in your molars, wind knifing the louvers, a loose chain beating time, steam bleed, a hundred swifts screaming bounced off tile until you can't tell direction, water tic-tic-ticking from a hundred heights into a central drain. And nothing happens fast. Leaves by millimeters a week. Verdigris creeping. A fig root crushing a pipe over a decade.

It is inhabited, but not by people. Cloud-moths in thousands, soot-sparrows and white-collared swifts that never leave and never cross the middle, they just orbit the void. Copper lizards on warm ducts. Thumb-sized roaches in grease traps. Rats and newts and algae in a bathtub-sized sump around a drowned wrench. Mosquito larvae in every drip pan. And a troop of short-coated high-altitude macaques who have inherited maintenance. They check drip pans, ride the screw housing picking moss, turn a handwheel a quarter-turn and turn it back, nap on warm transformers. They are not performing for you. This is their ecology now: to tend.

There is no straight way to walk. One narrow perimeter gantry around a huge empty center. The center stays empty. Moths circle it. Swifts circle it. Dust circles it. Throw a leaf in and the updraft kicks it up and sideways. Don't be the thing that tries to cross it.

You are an ordinary human. You need air, dry lungs, intact eardrums, thermoregulation, and solid footing. This place attacks all four.

### 2. Your body first

**Altitude is the real gatekeeper.** You will climb a service stair cased in iron/concrete/tile/rust up through thinning air to a pressure door. Acclimatize below for days. Climb slow. Do not arrive sick, hungover, or anemic. Talk to a real travel-medicine doctor before you go about altitude illness, and know the signs of HAPE/HACE in yourself: headache that won't quit, clumsiness, breathlessness at rest, confusion. Bring portable supplemental oxygen if you can get it. The inside is warm but not oxygen-rich - warm humid hypoxia feels worse than cold dry hypoxia because you overheat and pant.

Inside you will sweat through your shirt in a minute. Your glasses will fog, clear, fog. Your heart rate will be 20 beats higher than you expect just standing still. Limit your stay. 30-45 minutes inside is plenty for a first visit. Plan it like a sauna with noise damage: go in, be still, get out before heat exhaustion makes you clumsy on that gantry.

**Thermal whiplash will get you on exit.** You will be soaked - condensation beads on every leaf, pipe, hair and feather, purls along handrails, gathers under every chain link. Your clothes will be wet. Then the door sighs and you are back in thin freezing wind. Wet + wind + 4,000m = hypothermia in minutes. This kills more unprepared visitors than anything inside.

Dress as three separate people:

1. *For the stair outside:* windproof shell, warm midlayer, hat, gloves, sunglasses, boots with hard ankle support and aggressive grip. Tile + rust + condensation = ice-slick.
2. *For inside:* strip at the door. Light, quick-dry synthetics, nothing cotton. Short sleeves if your vaccines are current and you have insect repellent, otherwise thin long sleeves. Waterproof, grippy boots you don't mind soaking - you'll be standing in perpetual drip. Bring a second shirt sealed in a drybag.
3. *For immediately after:* a completely dry base layer, warm hat, and shell sealed in a second drybag left *outside* the warm zone if possible, or deep in your pack in a roll-top liner. Change the second you exit, before you start down. Wring out your wet layer and bag it.

You will want gloves inside too - thin work gloves. Not for cold. For hot ducts, verdigris slime, rust, vine thorns, and to keep your skin oils off leaves. And so you don't instinctively grab a live bus-bar.

### 3. What to pack

Think biosecurity, waterproofing, and silence.

**Breathing and hearing:**
- Foam earplugs *plus* low-profile earmuffs. The description is honest: plugs are sensible and useless. The building sings through bone - you'll feel it in sternum and teeth. Double protection won't make it quiet, but it will prevent the shriek from staking your hearing for days. Limit time, and don't try to shout. Shouting won't work.
- A P3 / N95 or better respirator, the kind that seals. Not just for twice-daily cloud incursion, but for constant spores, moth scales, dust, mold, dried droppings. If you have asthma, allergies, or are prone to fungal infections, this is non-negotiable. Bring a spare. It will get wet and clog fast.
- Insect repellent and after-bite care. Those drip pans breed mosquitoes. The sump breeds everything else.

**Eyes and electronics:**
- If you wear glasses, bring a microfiber cloth on a lanyard and anti-fog if you use it. Expect fog-clear-fog. Contacts are often easier, with sealed goggles for the cloud-inrush. Sunglasses for outside, but take them off inside or you'll be blind in the flat white louver light.
- Everything in drybags. Phone, headlamp, camera - condensation will kill them. A cheap waterproof point-and-shoot will outlive a fancy rig. Bring desiccant. Touchscreens will misbehave with wet fingers. Bring a pencil and a waterproof notebook instead for notes.
- A headlamp with red mode, even though light is “ordinary.” Power is daylight through grime-frosted portholes and slats, diffused by leaf. It's enough to read a gauge. When the cloud comes in, it's white blindness for minutes. You want to see your feet and your handrail.

**Medical / safety:**
- Up-to-date tetanus. Seriously. Everything is rust and root at agreement, edges rounded but still iron. Any cut here is a rust cut in warm standing water.
- Do not assume water is drinkable. There is no river, no rain, only cloud twice a day and leaf-drip and perpetual tic-tic-tic to a central drain. The air tastes of pennies and chlorophyll for a reason - copper, verdigris, oil, bird droppings, rat sump, mosquito larvae. Bring all the water you'll need for climb + inside + descent, plus electrolytes. Do not drink condensation, do not fill from drip pans, do not touch the sump bathtub even to look - leptospirosis, gut bugs, copper toxicity. If you must, bring a filter + chemical backup, but best is to carry.
- Small first aid: blister care, wound irrigation, antiseptic, bandages, antihistamine, personal meds. No one lives here to help you. No toilets, no food cache, no rescue.
- Trekking poles for the stair, but collapse them inside - the gantry is narrow, draped with flowering vine, and poles snag roots and knock drip pans.

**Communication:**
- Agree on hand signals with a partner before you enter. You will have to put your mouth to someone's ear to be heard, and there is no one to hear. A whiteboard and marker, or laminated cards: STOP / BACK / UP / DOWN / OUT NOW / LOOK. A whistle won't cut through the fundamental. A flashing light will.
- Don't go alone if you can avoid it. If you must, tell someone below your in/out window.

Clean everything before you go in. Brush boots, shake out packs, no seeds, no fruit, no crumbs. This is an isolated vertical island that has reached balance after a very long time. The last thing it needs is your invasive fungus or your orange peel. Clean everything on the way out, too - spores, moth scales, and roach eggs want to travel with you.

### 4. How to move and behave

Learn the rules by watching the swifts, because they have lived here longer than you can stay awake.

**1. Do not cross the center. Orbit.**
Stay on the perimeter gantry. Keep one hand for the rail - the undersides are wettest, water purls there. Test each step; leaves spill over railings and hide gaps. Do not lean out over the void to photograph. Do not fly a drone across, do not throw a leaf to “test” it - you already know: the updraft carries it up and out to the side, and the sound thins into a pressure column where you can hear your own blood. That shimmer is not invitation. Vertigo here is peculiar: the walls are near and the center is far, your eyes insist the hollow is larger than the building. People get dizzy and drop things. Lash everything to yourself.

When the louvers open - twice a day the crown breathes and the cloud comes inside as a white, blind, cold animal - stop. Get low, hold on, face the wall, protect your respirator and eyes. Don't try to move in whiteout on a slick gantry with house-fans stirring. Wait. Let the walls drink first.

**2. Water belongs to leaves first, metal second. You are third, which is to say, not at all.**
Don't wipe leaves dry to see better. Don't tip drip pans. An old female macaque checks those pans with the back of her hand and tips one a finger's width to change flow - that's not cute, that's hydrology. Don't block the chain-drips, don't step in pans, don't dam the handrail rivulets for fun. Condensation forms heaviest on living undersides, as if plants learned to coax it. Machines drink secondhand from leaf-drip. Let them.

**3. Everything made will be grown over, and everything grown will be maintained. You do neither job.**
Don't “fix” anything. Don't turn handwheels, don't clear moss from an inspection port even though a juvenile macaque does exactly that - it's their rite, their circuit learned from grandmothers. Don't pull a vine off conduit, don't pick a warning-light-colored flower, don't scrape verdigris. Rust and root have come to an agreement. You haven't.

And about the animals: total indifference is not tameness.
- Macaques: give 10+ meters, never between mother and juvenile, never on a transformer they are napping on. No eye contact challenges, no feeding, no touching, no mimicking handwheel turns. A bite here is an evacuation - rabies, herpes B, infection in a place with no clinic. Secure all food and shiny objects. Watch where you sit - they groom on vine-draped catwalks.
- Swifts and sparrows nesting in junction boxes: don't block entrances, don't shine bright light in boxes. They ride the warm updraft round and round - if you flail you'll cause a collision.
- Lizards on warm ducts: those ducts are warm for a reason. Assume every copper bar, transformer, and unmarked pipe is hot or live. The bus-bars crackle faintly with normal daylight power - no sparks, no apocalypse, just enough to stop your heart if you're wet, and you will be wet. Hands off. No leaning on green verdigris houses.
- Roaches, rats, newts: look, don't touch. Don't step toward the sump shadow for a closer look at the algae. That water is a living culture.

Move slowly. This is the slowest factory in the world. The roar suggests emergency; the reality is patience. If you rush, you'll slip, grab something live/hot, or interrupt a maintenance circuit. Stand still for ten minutes and let the place resolve: moths plastered under leaves by day rising at louver-opening to drink fog, lizards blinking, a fan settling. You'll see more by orbiting slowly once than by pacing.

### 5. What it will feel like if you're ready

The door sighs. Heat takes you by the throat. In thirty seconds your shirt sticks, your hair beads, your notebook curls.

First minute: claustrophobia. It's small, you could walk it in three minutes if there was a straight way. Everything you can touch is made.

Second minute: agoraphobia. The middle is nothing, a held breath floor to crown, uncrossed by walkway. Gulfs of lit, humming air between a hanging chain here and a lamp cage there. Your eyes can't fit the vast hollow inside the small can.

Then your senses split. Your ears are overwhelmed - sternum buzz, chain rattle, steam hiss, swift screech with no direction. Your eyes are underwhelmed - flat white working light, dust motes and spores hanging. You can read a gauge. That's intentional. It's not a cathedral, it's a workplace that became a garden.

If you have plugs and mask and dry socks waiting, you can relax into it. You'll start to notice geometry: vines repeating industry, horizontal-vertical-diagonal, flowers the color of warning lights, roots knitting where a panel fell away decades ago. You'll smell pennies giving way to green. You'll hear, under the roar, the tic-tic-tic that is the true clock here - drips arriving late from a hundred heights.

You'll watch the troop do their rounds without hurry and understand you are not needed. That is the gift of the place. Above the clouds, turning very slowly, going nowhere at all, warm and roaring and patient, it does not need you to save it, fix it, or even understand it. It just asks you to stay on the edge, leave water to leaves, and orbit quietly until the cloud comes in and you remember you have lungs.

Then go back out, change into something dry before the cold finds your sweat, and descend. Shower. Check for bites, rashes, cough, ringing ears. Air your gear. Shake the mountain greenhouse out of your boots and leave it up there.

Bring patience down with you. You'll need it. Nothing down below moves that slowly or knows that well how to stay.
