You are not going to see the Heights. Go in understanding that, and you will be safer and happier.

This is not a place you visit with your eyes. It is a place you visit with your ears, your skin, your feet, your nose, and your patience. Prepare like a caver who is also going to a hot swamp in the mountains, who has agreed to be blind the whole time.

### 1. Understand what you are walking into

3 kilometers up, on a tableland so big it makes its own weather. Cloud below you, cloud above you, sealed shut a long time ago. No sun, no moon, no stars, no horizon glow. Ever.

The air is high-altitude thin and low-jungle thick at the same time. Warm as blood, saturated, heavy to pull in. You have to chew it. Your sweat will not evaporate. Your boots will never dry.

The ground is old basalt worn soft, covered in a meter of living mat — sphagnum, black mud, rotting leaf — over peat basins a dozen meters deep, over water that lies in sheets and films. Geothermal heat comes up from below. Warm rain hisses down in curtains, stops, then condenses straight out of the air onto you. Droplets hang without falling.

Nothing photosynthesizes. The forest is black, rubber-thick, hot to the touch. Ear-ferns big as doors to catch vibration. Fungal trees 20-30 meters tall dripping constantly. Pitchers big enough to drown a dog. Vines thick as your thigh running along the ground, making a second bouncy ground.

It is crowded and loud. Water on ten billion leaves, mud sucking, canopy dripping. Frog-things trilling in choirs, insect swarms in metallic sheets, tall stilt-legged waders booming infrasound you feel in your molars, chitterer packs clicking, and under it all the slow piston-breath *chuff-suck, chuff-suck* of the Harvest Engines — hill-sized factories on treads walking century-old riverbed routes, cooking peat and timber inside their dark bellies, trailing live umbilical cables, exhaling steam that becomes cloud that becomes rain.

Sound literally makes rain here. The air is that close to saturation, that full of spores and ash. A chorus peak tips it over. That loop — choir + vent + rain + peat — is the whole ecology.

People live single or in pairs, tending engines, moving dome to dome by rope. The only law that matters: **No light. Ever.**

Light blinds hunters, scatters herds, silences pollinators. And it brings curiosity from the large quiet things that make a long wet tearing sound in mud and then go silent.

You are an ordinary human with eyes that will be useless and ears that are untrained. Act like it. Be humble, be slow, hire a local if you can.

### 2. Your body

**Get checked first.** Talk to a real doctor. 3km altitude + heat + 100% humidity + exertion in mud is brutal on heart and lungs. If you have asthma, COPD, severe cardiac issues, chronic ear problems, immune suppression, uncontrolled anxiety/claustrophobia — do not go. This place will find the weakness in an hour.

Acclimatize to altitude before you transit if you can. Do cardio work. Practice breathing slowly through your nose under exertion. In the Heights you will want to pant — don't. Panting makes you dizzy in thin air and makes you loud without pattern, which reads as prey or storm.

Heat illness is the bigger killer than cold. It is warm from above and below. You cannot cool by sweating. Plan to move at half your normal hiking pace, rest often, drink constantly with electrolytes. Learn the signs of heat exhaustion in yourself by touch, because you won't see yourself in a mirror: pounding head, nausea, stop sweating, confusion.

Your feet are your life. You will be ankle to knee in warm mud for days. Trench foot, fungal rot, blisters that become infected in hours.
- Never cotton. Synthetics or thin merino, changed often.
- Knee-high waterproof socks are useless — water gets in and stays. Use quick-drain bog shoes with very wide, splayed soles, like snowshoes for peat, plus neoprene gaiters and a probe staff. Normal boots will spear straight into sucking hollows and trap you.
- Bring 4-5 pairs of socks in individual dry-bags with desiccant, plus antifungal powder, zinc cream, alcohol wipes. Dry your feet whenever you are in a dome, even if the air is wet. Check by feel for hot spots.
- Heat-resistant, thick soles. Some ground over geothermal seams is hot enough to be uncomfortable through thin soles.

Your lungs and gut: Still water is full of eggs, spores, intentions. Do not drink it. Do not inhale deeply near still pools. Bring a wide-mouth funnel-poncho or clean plastic sheet to catch rain as it falls, and a metal heat-proof bottle to take steam condensate from a crawler bleed valve. It comes out near-boiling and metallic — let it cool in a sealed container. Bring a filter + chemical backup for emergencies, and electrolytes.

Your skin, eyes, nose: The air is full of living things — spores, midges, propeller seeds that never land. Wear clear sealed goggles (no light, no reflection) and a light half-mask or buff you can breathe through. Not a full respirator — you need to smell iron and rot-sweet and hot oil, those are navigation cues, and you need to hum. But you do not want a lungful of spore. Bring antihistamines, saline nasal rinse in single-use vials, and plan for full shower and gear quarantine when you leave. What lives in you up there may try to come home with you.

### 3. Your mind and senses

Practice being blind before you go. Seriously.

Spend days blindfolded at home: dress, cook, walk, find tools hung by position. Learn to count steps, to trail a wall with the back of your hand, to listen for doorways by echo. In the Heights you will reach out and touch something living within seconds — leaf, flank, wet cable, rain wall. If that panics you, don't go.

You will have visual deprivation and auditory overload at the same time. No horizon, no distance, only time and sound in layers above and below. People get vertigo, nausea from infrasound, weeping from loneliness, time-slip because there is no morning. Bring a tactile watch that vibrates the hour, keep a strict sleep/wake ritual, hum to mark time.

Learn to listen:
- Water: patter-hiss = rain on leaves; gurgle = runnel; suck-sigh = mud; triple drip from canopy to understory to peat = you are under fungal trees, keep your hood up.
- Frogs = weather radio. When their choir peaks and shifts pitch to make room, rain is coming. Old-timers hear storms an hour out in the insect pitch rising.
- Waders booming in your teeth = kilometers-wide herd locator. Do not walk toward it. Give way.
- Chitterer clicks sweeping through fern aisles = hunting swarm moving. Stand aside, keep humming low, let them pass.
- Engine throb long before you feel it in feet. Learn the voices of the 3 nearest crawlers like tides. Each has its own piston rhythm, vent pop, chain slap. They are your lighthouses.
- A long wet tearing in mud and then quiet: large thing. Freeze your light discipline, go still, make no sharp sound.

Bring a good audio recorder that can do infrasound and ultrasound, with bone-conduction headphones that don't cover your ears. No speakers. You want to record to learn later, not to play back loudly now. Never play calls back — you can seed rain or call a predator by accident.

### 4. What to pack — blacked-out

Everything you bring must make NO light. Tape over every LED, remove glow sticks, disable camera flashes, put your phone in a true off Faraday bag and leave it off. A cracked screen is violence here. Tell your group: one accidental flashlight can deafen a hunting ground for a night and put you in danger.

- Clothing: hooded rain shell with silent fabric (no crinkly nylon that screams to echolocators), wicking base, quick-dry pants, wide bog shoes, gaiters, work gloves that resist heat and current, wide-brim soft hat to shed canopy drip. No jingling zippers or keys — tape everything.
- Movement: 50m of soft rope with knots every meter for counting, silent carabiners taped, collapsible probe staff to test mat vs hollow. Living mat holds and springs; hollow sucks to the knee. Probe every third step in new ground.
- Shelter/sleep: suspended hammock-bivvy with mosquito net, not a ground tent. Ground is water, pitchers, herds, engines. Sleep only in way-station stone domes, maintenance shacks, or hammocked between fungal trunks off the engine routes. Never sleep on an engine road — the peat there is compressed and tempting, but an Engine will come like weather.
- Water/food: funnel poncho, 2x 1L metal bottles, filter, electrolytes, 3 days more sealed calorie-dense food than you think. Do not eat local without a local teaching you. Do not leave crumbs that change the microbial loop.
- Health: tactile pulse-oximeter with audio beep (no glowing display), blister kit, antifungal, antibiotic ointment, oral rehydration salts, personal meds, earplugs that attenuate highs but let lows through for sleeping in domes.
- Navigation: mechanical compass with tactile dial, notebook with raised-line paper and wax pencil to log step counts, rope tugs, crawler rhythms. No laser rangefinder, no headlamp, not even red light. Do not bring night vision with IR illuminator — that is still light to many here, and heat-vision will just be white-out in warm fog.

Leave dogs, drones, bright clothing at home.

### 5. How to behave there

**1. Do not make light.** Not a match, not a lighter, not a phone. Prep all gear so you can find it by touch in a shack full of tools hung by position. If you have an emergency light failure (someone else's), cover it with your body, kill it, apologize, move away.

**2. Navigate by rope and rhythm.** Between domes, clip or hand-trail the rope guides that run in six directions. Count steps between drips. Follow hardened veins where engines have passed — that peat will hold you. Never cut across a peat basin that quivers. Mark your path by memory of sound, not by marks.

**3. Hum as you walk, low and steady.** You are loud anyway — boots, breath, rain on your hood. Give the marsh a pattern so it knows your size. Low, nasal, continuous. Not singing, not shouting. If you need to communicate, use low voice and rope tugs. Do not clap, whistle sharply, or play music. Loud without pattern = prey or storm, and literally can seed a downpour on yourself.

**4. Keep moving, but not fast.** Everything out-hears you. Walk Engine pace. Pause to listen every 50 steps. If ground trembles faintly and frogs shift pitch, an Engine is coming. Step well off the roadbed, uphill if you can feel slope, keep hands clear of trailing cables — they hum with current you can feel in your teeth. Do not touch a flank without gloves: hot riveted iron gone soft with moss, vibrating. Do not board a feral engine whose crew is generations dead unless a local tells you it is safe. Birds nest in the joints and the deck may be rotten.

**5. Water and ground discipline.** Drink rain as it falls or hot condensate. Keep your mouth closed in swarms. Do not put your pack down on moss for more than a day — moss will cover it. Do not cut banks (they bleed water and spores), do not break ear-ferns, do not dam runnels. Do not wash with soap in still pools.

**6. Respect the black-out compact.** Move between engines, announce yourself by hum at pump-houses and shacks. Ask before taking steam or shelter. Shutters stay dogged. Doors close fast to prevent light leak — there is none to leak, but the habit keeps the taboo. Listen more than you talk. Old-timers can hear weather; believe them.

### Emergencies

- **Lost:** STOP. Crouch to lower your profile, keep humming low so you are not mistaken. Listen for the nearest Engine throb, wader line, dome cistern ping. Feel for slope and for compressed roadbed underfoot. Do not wander shouting. If you have rope, anchor and spiral.
- **Leg trapped in hollow:** Don't yank. Lean back, spread weight, probe, slowly work your leg in circles to let water in around it, pull with the mud, not against. Unbuckle pack if it drags you.
- **Large quiet thing near:** Kill any light instantly. Go still, breathe through nose, keep a low continuous hum to declare size. Do not run — you will fall and splash and become interesting. Back away slowly toward harder ground or an Engine road. Animals move aside for Engines; you can too.
- **Accidental light:** Smother it, warn others in low voice, stay still for several minutes to let the acoustic ecology resettle. Expect hunters to go silent and then curious.
- **Injury/illness:** Get to a dome. Stone domes are dry-ish, have cisterns, rope guides out. Treat blisters and cuts immediately — everything weeps here including you, and infection is fast in warm wet.

When you leave, assume you are carrying spores in your lungs, gut, gear seams, boot stitching. Isolate gear in sealed bags, wash hot, dry hot, air in sunlight for days if you have sun to go back to. Watch yourself for rashes, cough, gut trouble for two weeks.

If you stand still long enough — rain beading where your eyelashes would be if you could see them, fern-ears trembling around you, booming and trilling and throbbing in layers — you will feel the scale not as distance but as a continent of warm mud breathing under cloud, tended by blind beasts and blind machines, rotting and growing back in the same breath. It doesn't need to be witnessed.

Don't try to witness it. Listen, keep your light dead, keep your hum steady, and let it go on without you.
