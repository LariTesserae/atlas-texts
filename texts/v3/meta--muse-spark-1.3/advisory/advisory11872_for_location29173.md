This is not a swim. This is a visit to an active industrial hole that happens to be full of water.

Do not prepare like you're going to the ocean. Prepare like you're going to trespass politely in a cold, loud, vertical factory the size of a weather system, where the floor fell out and the sea moved in but nobody turned the lights off.

For an ordinary human, with ordinary lungs and ordinary blood that goes stupid in 12C water, this is a rim trip with *optional* shallow water. The bottom is three kilometers down. You are not going to the bottom.

### 1. Understand what you're walking into

Forget shore. There is no shore.

Behind you: flat, cool, windy plain. Factories, transformer yards, blinking lights. Normal ground.

In front of you: architecture going down. Walls 100 meters high, benches as wide as airfields, spiraling down until you can't see across. Everything poured or blasted. Shotcrete still sharp enough to cut rope, drill marks still visible, rock bolts with only inches of rust because this is all young. Expansion joints still clean. Yellow-white road paint still visible under 30 feet of clear green-grey water.

From Bench 2 down, it's all drowned. Upper water is bright aquamarine, then jade, then slate blue, then just a white ache of sun. But the work lights are still on - sodium and LED floods on steel masts, burning underwater in daytime, fed by live cables from the rim. At night the whole spiral glows faintly under water like a city under ice.

It is mostly empty and then suddenly full. Miles of bare concrete and nothing but your bubbles and the tick of cooling concrete transmitted through your jaw. Then a corner crusted black-blue with mussels, a slow muscle of 10,000 silversides turning all at once with a rain-click you hear before you see, grey-green eels in the drain galleries, cormorants and terns stabbing down from crane nests on the rim.

It is loud, always. Waterfalls where rivers were diverted to feed the pit drop 100m to foam and the roar bounces wall to wall until direction dies. Under that, the pumps still turning, moving water bench to bench - you feel it in your chest if you're submerged. Cranes groaning, sheet metal banging on a half-drowned shed, seep and drip everywhere, and underwater: mussels crackling, turbine whine, and every few hours a boom when the seiche - the pit's own slow meters-high sloshing tide - slaps a vertical wall.

Currents never stop. Intakes and outfalls drive a slow cool gyre around the spiral, carrying larvae, oil sheen, concrete dust bench by bench downward. Wind funnels along the haul roads and by afternoon chops the surface white. That chop stacks against lee walls and breaks as concrete surf - no sand, just spray on vertical. Morning mist burns off by ten. Evening cold sinks and fog rebuilds from the center out.

Go in expecting vertical disorientation, not horizontal. A stair is still a stair underwater. A sign that says BENCH 14 - 30 KM/H - CURVE still reflects when your lamp hits it under 20 feet of water. That legibility is comforting and it lies. That road leads off an edge into 50 meters of water over concrete and hundreds of meters to the next wall.

### 2. Decide how deep you are going to be honest about

For 95% of travelers, the right trip is: **Rim, first bench pool, cage elevator ride, out before full night fog.**

Do not get in the water unless you are already a certified cold-water diver with drysuit experience and a buddy who has it too. A 7mm wetsuit is not enough here. In 12C clear water your fingers go stupid in minutes even through a wetsuit, your breath shortens, your decision-making narrows. That is without current, without vertigo over a drowned airfield, without a live pump intake 100 meters away.

Do not freedive the walls. Do not try to "see how far down." Light thins terrifyingly fast. The fourth terrace down is already slate blue at noon. Visibility that looks like 30 feet of invitation is what makes you misjudge distance to the next wall by a factor of five.

The bottom is not a dive site. Sonar says flat poured pad, settling ponds, sump pits, rebar chairs waiting for a pour that will never come, now white with anemones. You will never see it without a submersible. Don't try.

### 3. What to wear and bring

Think quarry worker who might fall in, not beach tourist.

**On the rim, always:**
Hardhat or climbing helmet, steel-toe boots with ankle support and aggressive tread, high-vis vest or jacket, thick work gloves - basalt aggregate, shotcrete flakes, mussel shells, and blistered safety-yellow steel will all open skin. Cut flesh here gets concrete dust, oil, old explosive residue, and mine metals in it. Have tetanus up to date. Bring wound wash, gauze, waterproof bandages.

Layers for cool wind + waterproof shell + windproof. It is overcast-bright and cool even when the factories up top feel warm. Mist wets you slowly. Bring a warm hat and a change of dry base layer in a dry bag. Hypothermia starts on the rim if you're wet and standing still watching the water.

Sunglasses for glare off water + safety glasses for dust near waterfalls and upper walls where flakes and small rockfalls still expose fresh stone that smells faintly of explosives.

Earplugs or earmuffs for prolonged time near the engineered waterfalls and pump houses. You still need to hear crane groan and shouted warnings, so foam plugs you can half-out are better than full muffs, unless you're working right next to a fall.

**If you are entering the upper water at all, even to wade:**
Drysuit with hood, thick gloves, and boots is the standard. Not a wetsuit. Two thermal layers underneath. No exposed skin. Booties with hard soles - that biofilm of velvet green algae on sunlit concrete is slick as ice, and mussels will slice bare feet to ribbons.

Mask, snorkel only for surface puttering in the first bench pool. For any actual submersion: redundant dive lights - one primary powerful lamp and two backups - because below Bench 2 daylight is gone. Dive computer, compass, and a reel with guideline. The water is clear but featureless; swimming out over a bench you lose up/down/edge quickly.

Knife/shears for fishing line, cable tie, kelp stipe - kelp here is short, arm-length, white-tipped, clinging to bolt heads, but monofilament and copper wire are everywhere.

Inflatable PFD or wing with redundant buoyancy, whistle, waterproof VHF radio in a housing, and a strobe. Shouting is useless - roar bounces. Light signals and whistles carry.

Tether and harness if you go near any unfenced edge inside. The rim has a guardrail and a concrete lip straight as a ruler. Inside, there are no rails. A 90-degree edge sheets water clean off and drops 100m.

**Pack:**
6+ liters of clean drinking water per person per day + salty high-calorie food you can eat with numb fingers. DO NOT drink the pit water and DO NOT eat the mussels, eels, or perch in quantity. This is a young mine flood - concrete leachate, oil sheen, heavy metals. A mouthful of spray is unavoidable, a meal is stupid.

First aid for cold shock, cramp, cuts, eye flush for concrete dust. Chemical hand warmers, thermos with hot sweet tea. Towels and a full dry change sealed.

Power banks sealed against mist. Cables stay damp. Everything electronic in IPX7 or better.

Detailed bench map if you can get one from pump operators. Mark: your entry/exit, elevator rails, pump houses to avoid, waterfall impact zones to avoid by 200m, intake grates. There are very few places you can climb out - vertical walls don't let you just walk out. Know yours before you get wet.

### 4. How to move

Stay on marked haul roads on the rim. They are wide enough for four trucks for a reason - wind gusts and service vehicles. Don't lean over the concrete lip. The cold breath coming up off a million acres of water will push your jacket and lie to your balance. Vertigo is normal. Sit if you need to.

The cage elevator down a wall face is still running on its rails. This is the safest way down. Check with operators: schedule, weight limit, last inspection, radio channel, what to do if power flickers. Wear harness, keep hands in, bring a lamp even for a daytime ride. You will feel cold increase, light thin, pressure in your ears even in air as damp cold pools. Don't ride after afternoon chop builds or when fog is rebuilding - you can be stranded on a bench with no shelter and no easy climb.

In water: never swim alone, never swim untethered away from a wall unless you have a boat tender above. The distance deceives. That "close" opposite wall is weather, not wall. Stay within arm's reach of an exit ladder or sloped bench edge for your first entries. Stay in the top 10 meters. Current near intakes is silent until you're in it - if you feel a steady cool pull along a wall, kick perpendicular to it toward the bench edge, not against it.

Stay 200m clear of waterfall plunge pools - foam, down-pull, deafening even underwater through concrete. Stay clear of pump houses, gantry cranes, masts with lights. Assume every cable is live. Do not touch, tie off to, or hang gear on electrical infrastructure. Those cones of light full of motes look beautiful. They are electrified.

Watch for the seiche. If the waterline on the wall starts creeping up or down steadily for minutes - not waves, a slow rise - get out and get high on the bench. When it slaps, it slaps meters high against vertical.

Afternoon is for watching from the rim, not swimming. Wind chop + concrete surf + spray + cooling air = exhaustion fast. Morning after mist burn-off, before ten to two, is your window.

Night is for the rim only. From the lip you can watch the spiral glowing under water and fog blooming in the work lights. Do not be on the water at night unless you are in a crewed boat with radar, floods, and shore contact. Fog builds from the center out and eats lamps.

### 5. Living things and materials

Life is sparse but insistent. Biofilm is slippery. Kelp and ditch grass in expansion joints will tangle fins but won't hold you. Mussels cut - don't grab wall corners barehanded. Eels live in drain galleries and hunt at dusk along benches; they will startle you by brushing your legs in a lunchroom with its windows gone where they now live in lockers. They aren't aggressive, but give them space. Silversides will part around you in a flash that can white-out your vision for a second - stay still, breathe, let them pass. Birds - gulls, terns, cormorants commuting from rim cranes - are loud, thin-crying, and protective of nests. Don't approach crane nests.

Everything is sharp because nothing is rounded yet. Shotcrete flakes to bright new grey underneath. A rockfall exposes unweathered stone. Even the bottom rebar chairs are still sharp under white anemones. Move like everything wants to cut or abrade - because it does. No sitting on mussel edges. No bare knees on concrete.

Leave it. Don't pry mussels, don't pull kelp, don't take ore car souvenirs, wrenches, glass from cab windows, copper. The biology hasn't read the plan and is barely holding on. And the site is still - in its own drowned way - working. Reversing pumps, swaying gantries, thrumming cables. Don't interfere.

### 6. Head and body preparation

Train for cold shock before you go. Practice controlled breathing with face immersion in cold water at home. Know what gasp reflex feels like and how to not inhale spray.

Expect sensory weirdness: machine-loud, not surf-loud; vast emptiness that feels like agoraphobia and claustrophobia at once; the uncanny comfort of readable human things - road lines, BENCH 14 signs, empty ore cars patient on rails, a lunchroom - made alien by green water and eels. Many travelers feel grief or vertigo looking down through 30 feet to a road they could drive on if it weren't drowned. Sit with that on the rim first. If you feel panicky looking over, do not get in.

Fitness: you need to be able to climb a wet vertical ladder in a flooded drysuit while cold, tired, and deafened by a waterfall. If you can't do pull-ups and tread water for 10 minutes in clothes, stay dry.

Plan your exit before entry. Tell someone on the rim: where you're entering, which bench, which direction along the gyre, when you'll be back, what strobe pattern means help. Set a hard turn-back time. Cold steals time - what feels like 10 minutes is 30.

Bring ear, eye, and thermal margin for twice as long as you plan to stay. Weather cycles inside the pit that don't exist outside. Rain falls harder inside, wrung out by the cold pool. You can enter in sun and exit in fog with wind chop.

If you do this right, what you get is unforgettable without being miserable: standing for a second on a ruler-straight lip while warm factory air hits cold pit breath and makes its own stratus; seeing three, four terraces down to water haze and cloud; hearing concrete transmit a distant turbine through your boots; watching a whole submerged airfield turn silver as silversides catch the floods; finding a reflective road sign under 20 feet of jade water with perch sheltering in its lee.

It remembers being a hole. Visit it like you respect the hole, not like you conquered the sea.
