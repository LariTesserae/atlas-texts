This is not a city you visit. It's a city you wade into, get carried by, and sweat through. Come prepared to be wet, loud, touched, shouted at, and never alone for a second.

Think of it less as a destination and more as a tide-flat organism that learned to run printers and steal rain.

Here's how an ordinary, baseline-human traveler survives it and even likes it.

### 1. Reset your expectations

**There is no dry. There is no quiet. There is no stillness. There is no green.**

You will be ankle-deep most of the day, sometimes knee-deep. Warm bath-water pushing up through pavement, weeping out of walls, falling as deliberate warm rain from the harvester lattices a kilometer overhead, running in gutters brown-green with tide and wash-water. Puddles never dry. Laundry never dries. *You* will never fully dry. Accept it now or you will be miserable in an hour.

There is no solitude. If you stop walking you are carried — literally, by crowd pressure, by boat traffic, by porters. Shoulder to shoulder, balcony to balcony, boat to boat. Personal space does not exist. Privacy does not exist. People bargain nose-to-nose yelling, lovers yell, vendors yell through cone megaphones in four languages at once because whispering is physically impossible.

There is no horizon. It goes on until the haze eats it in every direction. North to refinery flares and salt pans, south to tenements dissolving in heat, east to the reef-barrier drinking the sea, west to the river and the dredge-fleet excreting new land. You will not see an edge. Pilots navigate by the city's own clouds.

There is no nature as you know it. No weed in a crack, no moss, no tree. Stone too hot and salt and trampled. This is a mineral world: basalt, poured stone, tile, brass, glass, rust, mother-of-pearl scale on canal walls where water evaporates. The only soft things are animal — and animals are *everywhere*.

Prepare psychologically for sensory immersion, not sightseeing. The foundation engines thump in your sternum day and night to keep the plate from liquefying. The harvesters thrum in your teeth. Boat engines, pumps, printer-gantries, shutters, fish tails slapping stone, drums, radios, a rooftop brass band, dogs, children, and a hundred thousand gulls with a scream evolved to cut through engine roar. It is physical pressure, not noise. It deepens at night, it never stops. It glows at night too — bacterial mats in culverts, nav lights, welding.

If you need quiet, darkness, dry socks, or empty space to stay sane, this is a short-trip location for you. Plan 48-72 hours max first time.

### 2. Your body in this place

**Heat + humidity + salt + exertion:** It's always warm. Warm water on ankles, warm rain on neck, warm stone through shoes, warm breath of crowds. Midday is a pearly white-yellow glare filtered through humidity. You will sweat constantly and not notice because you're already wet. Dehydration and heat exhaustion sneak up.

Drink a liter more than you think. Drink *only* treated water. Not canal water, not cistern slosh under your feet, not gutter water where ducks paddle. The harvester rain is the cleanest ambient source, but ask a local first — some districts use it for industrial pressure and it's full of ozone and minerals. Bring a squeeze filter + chlorine/iodine backup + a sealed bottle you can drink one-handed in a crowd. Electrolytes are non-negotiable.

**Feet:** This will make or break you. You need amphibious, hard-soled, closed-toe sandals or reef boots. Not flip-flops, not hiking boots, not bare feet. The ground is salt-eaten concrete and basalt worn round, but still scattered with oyster shell piles for lime, crab claws, hot metal plates, printer-fresh reef-concrete still steaming, oil-drum pontoon edges. And it's hot. And wet with fish guts and diesel. Bring 2 pairs, rotate, rinse nightly in clean water. Bring antifungal powder, zinc cream, and change socks — actually, forget cotton socks. Bring quick-dry synthetics or none. Expect trench foot conditions if you stay in enclosed wet shoes for 12 hours. Air them whenever you get up high.

**Skin, eyes, ears, lungs:** Air smells of salt and diesel and fish blood and frying oil and hot stone and incense and sweet rot and ozone. It's thick enough to chew.

- N95 or better half-mask with replaceable filters + a light buff for spray. You don't need it every minute, but you will want it in fish market downpours, after harvester rain when ozone spikes, and in repair yards with sparks showering into canals.
- Wraparound sunglasses with retention strap, plus clear splash glasses for night. Glare off wet stone and salt pans is brutal, plus fish guts and warm rain in the eyes.
- Hearing: Do NOT bring full foam blockers. You need to hear "mind the rope!" before a barge shoulders through the lane behind you and its horn goes off. Bring high-fidelity filtered musician/industrial plugs (20-25 dB) on a cord around your neck. Sleep with stronger plugs + eye mask, though sleep will still vibrate through bone.
- Insects: No plants means insects want *you*. Columns of flies/moths/wasps rise off wet stone, swifts cut through them. Mosquitoes breed in every courtyard pump puddle. Strong DEET/picaridin, after-bite antihistamine, and a head-net and a hammock mosquito net. Wasps nest on every ledge.

**Medical kit for a mineral swamp city:**
Tetanus booster up to date — non-negotiable with all the rust, barnacles, oyster knives, lock stairs. Broad-spectrum antibiotics (ask your travel doc), oral rehydration salts, antifungal cream + powder, waterproof bandages + superglue for small cuts (everything gets infected fast in warm salt-fish water), anti-diarrheals, antihistamines, asthma inhaler even if you're borderline, ear drops for swimmer's ear.

Do not eat raw filter-feeders unless you want to learn local medicine from the inside. Eat only from high-turnover stalls where food is fried/boiled in front of you and the crowd is locals. Oysters shucked by old women, eels, mullet, live crabs in cages — all good if cooked screaming-hot. Watch the crab baskets tip — claws on tile are loud and sharp.

### 3. What to wear and pack

Forget fashion. Forget staying clean. Dress for a hot machine-room aquarium:

- 3-4 sets of ultra-light, synthetic, quick-dry shirt + shorts/long pants that can be submerged. Everything will stay damp. Cotton will rot on you.
- Light long-sleeve with UPF for sun + oyster-shucking splatter + wasp stings.
- Amphibious shoes (above) + camp sandals that can get crushed.
- Waterproof pack liner *inside* everything, then a roll-top dry bag (20L) for electronics/meds/passport-equivalent, then a cheap outer daypack you don't care about. Double-bag. Gulls will shit on it, rain will find it, a tide will take the arcade and your feet will be in seawater while you keep shopping.
- Copper grounding bracelet is local custom and possibly practical. The city runs on weather-management and gravity-bartering no one understands anymore. Under a harvester field your shadow lags half a second behind you. At noon in certain alleys static builds until your hair stands and canal water climbs lock walls and beads before slapping back down. Locals ground awnings with copper chains and know which alleys to avoid at noon. Do what they do. Don't touch lift-rafts, pressure tubes, tethered platforms edges, printer-gantries. Don't stand under a harvester when it's thrumming on just to take a photo. If locals leave an alley, you leave.
- Electronics: Assume corrosion, immersion, and weirdness. Bring the cheapest waterproof phone you can afford in a floating case, plus a power bank in a dry bag. Faraday pouch for anything multiversal/dimensionally sensitive — the harvester fields, transit causeways humming in B-flat under load, and foundation thump play hell with sensitive instruments. Low-tech backup: paper map (will get wet — use waterproof notebook), compass, mechanical watch.
- Small headlamp with red mode for night glow navigation, whistle (better than shouting), 20m paracord, plastic sheets / cheap poncho to cover yourself *and* to help a vendor cover a stall — instant friends.
- Earplugs, eye protection, mask, repellent, sunblock (reef-safe, it all washes into canals where eels watch welding light).
- Trade money: Small, waterproof, low-denomination physical currency for this universe + a few universal trade goods: good cordage, stainless hooks, quality lighters in dry tubes, strong coffee/tea packets. Bargaining is loud, fast, nose-to-nose. Learn numbers, "how much?", "too much", "thank you", "rope!", "coming through!", "pump water drinkable?" in at least two local languages before you land.

What NOT to bring: white clothes, leather boots that will be destroyed, big rolling suitcase (you will carry it over lock stairs alive with red and blue-black crabs), a plant, a pet, an expectation of vegan-green-clean anything, a drone (gulls by the hundred thousand on condenser pylons, cormorants on bridges, swifts in insect columns — it will last 30 seconds and anger everyone).

### 4. How to move without dying or offending

**Keep moving or be moved.** This is the rule.

Stalls assemble at dawn from poles and tarps and vanish at dusk and the street becomes a boat lane by night. A plaza that was market at noon is repair yard by evening. A bridge there yesterday is gone — towed for scraping — replaced by bouncing oil drums and grown coral. Do not memorize routes. Navigate by tall fixed things: condenser pylons, refinery flares east, harvester lattices, foundation thump direction.

Walk with the flow, to the side if you need to stop, never in a lock stairway, never on a rope line. When a boy shouts to mind the rope, mind it instantly — a barge is coming and it will not stop for you. Make yourself narrow, lift your bag, let dogs pass — packs trot market aisles with the confidence of employees because they *are* employees. Do not stare them down, do not run, do not feed them. Nod to porters, welders, water-taxi drivers. They own momentum.

Hire a water-taxi driver early. Pay well, tip in useful goods, ask their name for grounding alleys and tide times. Tides take the lower arcades daily. Shopkeepers just lift goods a meter on pulleys, sit with feet in warm seawater, keep selling. You do the same: lift your bag, roll your pants, keep buying.

If caught in a western-ward storm — black, warm, electric, builds in an hour — get up, not under. Up to second/third floor tile arcades, under patched sailcloth awnings (hold a copper chain if offered). Do not shelter in a low boat lane or under a crane. After it bursts and vanishes, stone smokes and steam becomes fog trapped between neighborhoods. Wait for it to clear; do not try to navigate in zero-visibility steam with boats still moving.

### 5. How to be a decent guest-animal

You are one more animal in a city of animals: gulls, cormorants drying wings on stanchions, dogs, rats along culverts, crabs climbing, eels and mullet turning as one body under bridges, children fishing with magnets.

- Don't feed gulls. They already own the condenser pylons and warm printer-concrete. One toss = 500 screaming dives and a vendor will yell at you, correctly.
- Don't step on crab stairs to crush for fun. Step carefully, use handrails slimed with salt.
- Don't shoo dogs from stalls. Step around.
- Help lift. If tide comes in and a shopkeeper is pulleying goods, grab a rope. If a plastic sheet needs holding when harvester rain hisses on hot roofs and everyone shouts and laughs, hold it. You will be shouted at, laughed with, and then fed.
- Shout to be heard, but don't perform anger. Bargaining is loud because it has to be. Smile, yell your price, hold your ground, accept the fish guts on your shoes as part of the handshake.
- Shrines wedged between pump stations and cage-menders are real. Incense in diesel air matters. Step over pipes, not on offerings, lower your voice — well, lower it as much as physics allows.
- No picking at barnacles on god-machines. The lift-rafts stay up for reasons no one can explain and everyone relies on. Admire the barnacles, don't scrape them.

Sleep high if you can afford it. Ground floor is for boats and floods. Second/third is shops. Above that housing cubes with balconies crusted with drying nets and crab cages and laundry that never dries. Above that sky-bridges and pressure tubes going *thup-thup-thup* with grain and powdered coral and mail, and tethered platforms that hover and shimmer like a mirage that forgot to go away. Pay for a balcony hammock spot, string your mosquito net, use earplugs, let the thump rock you. There is no quiet hotel. There is only a less-wet, less-trodden ledge.

### 6. First hour protocol

You come in low over warm flat water. The city appears as weather. Land, if you can call it that, at a high causeway, not a low arcade.

1. Shoes on, bag sealed, plugs in but one ear open, glasses on, bottle full.
2. Get up one level immediately to see flow. Watch for 10 minutes: where boats go, where dogs go, where water is rising.
3. Find a water-taxi / porter, agree price loudly, get to housing up.
4. Stash dry bag, buy hot cooked food and clean water from the busiest stall.
5. Learn your courtyard pump, your gutter direction, your high-ground retreat, and which noon-static alley your host tells you to avoid. Believe them.

You wade in. Water bath-warm. Crab sidles over foot. Horn blasts. Gulls explode upward. Warm rain begins to fall out of clear sky, hissing, steaming, drumming on ten million awnings, and no one even looks up.

Don't look up either. Laugh, cover the nearest stall with the old plastic sheet handed to you, feel the thrum in your teeth, and keep moving.
