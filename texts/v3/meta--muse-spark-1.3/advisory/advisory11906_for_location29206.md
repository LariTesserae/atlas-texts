This is not a ruin. Do not prepare like a tourist.

Prepare like a soft, wet, loud animal going up onto a high, dry roof where you do not belong, to walk briefly through something alive in geological time that will notice you only as passing dust.

### What it is, in your body

At first it is only the air.

You will arrive and not be able to finish a breath. Not gasping — worse. You inhale and there just isn't anything to finish with. Cool, dust-and-stone air that strips water off your lips, your eyes, the back of your throat as you breathe it. Your exhalation puffs white and is torn apart instantly. The sky drinks it.

You are on a tableland above the clouds. The cloud-sea is *below* you, breaking on black cliffs. The sun is small, pale, stuck in bruised blue-grey twilight. It never moves. Stars bleed through faintly. Light enough to see miles, not enough to make a hard shadow.

There is no water. No soil. No stream, no seep, no ice, not even frost. Bone-white dust and salt-rimmed shale that rings under your boots. Wind in long cool tides that moves dust in sheets like slow water. Iron-smell and distance.

And it is a city. For a valley in every direction: low domes to the north, needles to the east, sunken courtyards to the south, terraces, arches over nothing, processional stairs that stop at sky, ramps, gutters for something that never flows, cisterns roofed over. Street over street with no mortar.

No tool marks anywhere. Get close and the walls aren't cut or stacked. They're laminated, like shell or tooth — ash and calcium and chitin fused matte warm-grey. No metal, no glass, no hearth, no trash, no hinge-hole. The doorways are low ovals, lip-like. Inside is bigger than outside should allow, and empty. Knock and it rings like pottery.

Almost nothing lives fast here. Black and rust-orange crust in the lee of walls, thin as paint, flakes like mineral if you touch it. Every hour or so a 6-foot translucent glider — jellyfish skin on bone kite — rides the cliff updraft without a sound and folds itself into a slit in a tower. Thumb-sized armored pills in the dust sift powder and freeze into pebbles when you come near. Ten living things in a day if you're lucky.

The place is empty and it is not still. Towers tick and groan. Flakes ping off. Courtyard floors breathe up a finger's width and settle. Once a day the cliff exhales cold vapor through a blowhole that falls apart into dryness before it touches anything. Dust dunes rebuild themselves against walls.

And if you walk it, it stops behaving. That's the part you must prepare for, not just altitude.

Straight lines don't hold. A half-mile avenue takes three hours and returns you to your arch from the other side. Twenty steps up leaves you looking down from a terrace that was below you. Inside big shells dust falls sideways, your pack tugs toward the ceiling then drops with a stomach-lurch correction. Shadows pool on the underside of nothing. You hear your boot scrape half a second *before* you move. Towers that look adjacent recede into a plain as you approach. A shoulder-high doorway becomes a vault once you duck through. Bigger inside, emptier the deeper you go.

Space is thin up there and stitched back loosely. Your senses and your legs will lie to you.

You are an ordinary human. That means you need thick air, water constantly, gravity that points down, distances that stay put, and other people. None of that exists here. Go in assuming a short, careful trespass, not an expedition.

### 1. Your body first: air, water, light

**This will hurt you faster than you think.**

Air: Treat this like 6,000-7,000 meters on Earth, but drier. You will be cognitively stupid within minutes without help. Headache, nausea, clumsiness, poor judgment, then bad decisions about navigation when navigation is already impossible.

* Acclimatize beforehand if you can: sleep low, train in high desert. Talk to a real doctor about acetazolamide. If you have asthma, anemia, heart issues, bad sinuses — do not go.
* Bring supplemental oxygen and actually use it. Nasal cannula + small bottle with regulator, or at minimum an oxygen concentrator mask for rest stops. Arrive already on it. Plan: sit for 10-15 minutes on arrival, breathe, do nothing. Stand slowly. Your first hour's walk should be embarrassingly slow.
* Bring a P100 / elastomeric respirator with spare filters, plus dust goggles that seal, not sunglasses. The dust is fine, salty, alkaline, everywhere in wind tides. It will shred your nose and lungs. You do not want a dust sheet in your airways at low oxygen. Practice walking in mask + goggles before.
* No contacts. Bring glasses. Bring preservative-free eye drops, saline nasal spray, heavy lip balm, moisturizer, 2-3 bandanas. Expect nosebleeds, cracked lips in an hour, eyes that feel sandpapered. The air pulls moisture and never gives it back.

Water/food: There is nothing to drink or eat here. Dust-eaters sift dust because there is nothing else. That black-orange crust is not moss, do not touch, do not taste.

* Carry far more water than a desert hike: 5-6 liters for a few hours, plus electrolytes. You lose water with every ripped-apart breath. Set a timer — with no sun movement you will forget to drink. Drink on beep, not thirst.
* High-calorie, no-cook, no-crumb food: gels, nuts, chocolate. No stove — air is too thin for reliable flame and there is nothing to burn, and you do not want to blacken a pre-tool city. Chemical hand warmers, not fire.
* Pack out everything, including urine and wrappers. This place is sterile. Your microbes are contamination.

Light/skin: Dim does not mean safe. You are very high with thin sky. Assume brutal UV.

* Windproof layers, not heavy winter. Cool, not cold, but wind steals heat and water. Softshell + wind shell, buff over nose/mouth, hat with brim, thin gloves. Sturdy high-ankle boots — shale is sharp, polished, ringing. Trekking poles will save you when gravity wobbles.
* Wraparound UV400 goggles/sunglasses, sunscreen on any exposed skin. Headlamp with spare batteries anyway — interiors of shells go dark fast, and your eyes will tire in half-light. Sleep mask and earplugs if you plan to be longer than 6-8 hours, which you shouldn't.

**Rule: Day trip only. Do not sleep there.** The unmoving sun destroys time sense. Set a watch with countdown alarms: turn-around time, drink time, check-in time. Decide your hard out-time before you arrive and obey it stupidly.

### 2. How to move without getting lost forever

Ordinary navigation will fail. Do not trust your eyes.

Two towers look close. They are a plain apart. An avenue looks straight. It loops. This is normal here, not a malfunction in you. Prepare for it technically and emotionally.

* Never go alone. Two minimum, three better, tethered 10-20m on a light, bright cord in open plazas. In shell interiors, one stays at the doorway as anchor. No heroics, no splitting up to "check that courtyard."
* Bring Ariadne thread: 200m of light line, chalk that will blow away, but better: numbered flagging tape, small cairn flags, GPS with track-back *and* a mechanical compass + notebook. Mark every turn: time, bearing, what you see. Expect GPS/compass to be weird near large shells — log it, don't argue with it.
* Walk protocol: Slow. Count steps. If an avenue takes >30 minutes with no progress or returns you to start from the other side, stop. Mark it LOOPED and do not try a third time. Back out along your line. If a stair inverts you, sit down immediately. Close eyes, breathe oxygen, wait for stomach to settle before standing.
* Do not enter deep interiors. Lip-doorways that become vaults, rooms where dust falls sideways — look, do not go in. Gravity negotiable means falls onto walls/ceilings, sprained ankles, packs pulling you over. Wear a light helmet or at least a padded hat if you insist on entering. Secure everything to your body — lens caps, bottles, anything that can fall *up*.
* Cliff edges and blowholes are death. Updrafts that lift 6-foot gliders will take your hat, your map, you if you lean. Stay 10m back. Whiteout dust sheets move like water — crouch, goggles down, wait, do not walk blind toward a drop.
* Sound lies. You will hear your foot before you move, hear a glider after it passed. Don't use sound to judge distance. Use radio check-ins on a schedule, not shouting — shouting in thin air just exhausts you, and there's nothing to echo off for a long time anyway.

Bring: cord, tape, whistle + mirror for visual signaling, satellite messenger/PLB (assume it may struggle — tell someone outside your exact entry window and your "call for help if I'm not back by" time), power bank kept warm and dust-bagged. Keep your jump/return device sealed, dust-free, fully charged. Thin, loosely-stitched space may make extraction feel delayed or smeary. Set your anchor *before* you wander, test it, do not wander far from it.

### 3. Your mind up there

People underestimate this. The city is vast, intervals enormous, plazas bare, towers a quarter-mile apart with humming air between. No echo for your steps. No green. No other human noise. Everything half-lit.

You will feel the hollowness in your teeth. Awe, then lurch, then lonely derealization: *no hands made this, nothing needs me here.* Then spatial nausea and time-slip will make you anxious and stupid.

Prepare:

* Expect precognition sounds, upward shadows, deja vu. Name it in advance: "That's the city, not me breaking." Have a grounding drill: stop, feet wide, hand on a warm-grey wall (gently, with glove), breathe 4 in / 6 out on oxygen, say time + place out loud into your recorder.
* Limit input. Earplugs under wind, sunglasses to reduce visual swim, focus on your cord and your partner's back, not the horizon that bends and falls away too fast.
* Do not chase. Do not follow a glider to its slit. Do not follow braided thumb-trails. Do not try to prove a loop by running it faster. The city sheds papery stone skins and opens/seals slits on its own schedule — it shifts under you while you stand still trying to understand it. You will not understand it by pushing.
* Have an abort mantra. Leave immediately if: headache that doesn't lift on oxygen + rest + water, confusion / can't do simple math, ataxia (can't walk heel-to-toe), nausea/vomiting, chest tightness, euphoria or dread that spikes, gravity jolts that don't settle in 2 minutes, second loop on same route, dust whiteout that drops visibility under 20m, any damage to mask/goggles/water.

Ordinary humans get in trouble by staying 30 minutes too long to figure it out. Don't.

### 4. Respect and contamination — both directions

This city maintains itself. It grew avenues like a reef grows channels for currents of air and time. You are rare slow traffic.

* Do not pry, carve, collect, stack, or clean. No souvenirs, not even a flake. Don't scrape the black-orange crust — if it's alive, it lives so slowly as to be geology. Let it.
* Don't block glider slits, don't shine lights into roosts, don't step on braided trails. Freeze like the pill-shapes do: stop, let them go, walk around. They are sifting dead gliders and spores and salt — the entire food web.
* Decontaminate before and after: brush boots, wipe gear, change outer layer bagged separately. Rinse sinuses and eyes after. Watch for 48 hours for persistent cough, sinus pain, rash from dust. See a medic if breathing stays raspy. And quarantine your boots — don't carry that white salt-dust to a wet living world.

### Pack checklist for an ordinary body

Worn: merino base, windproof softshell + shell, long pants, high boots broken in, hat, buff, gloves, sealed goggles, glasses.
Carried: oxygen, 5-6L water + electrolytes, food, P100 mask + 2 filters, eye drops / nasal saline / lip balm, headlamp + batteries, watch with alarms, trekking poles, 200m cord + tape + notebook + compass + GPS, radio + PLB + power bank, first aid + altitude meds + anti-nausea + painkiller, chemical warmers + emergency bivy, waste bags, jump anchor kit in dry bag.

Leave behind: expectations of conquering, heavy camera tripods that catch wind, drones (updrafts will eat them and disturb gliders), anything that makes fire, anything you can't carry out crawling and stupid from hypoxia.

### If you do it right

You arrive, sit, breathe. You walk slowly across ringing dust while towers tick in the cold. Dust moves like water around your ankles. A veil passes overhead without sound and is gone.

You don't solve it. You witness a pre-tool metropolis, hollow and enormous, bigger inside than out, waiting above the clouds for dust to pass through.

Then, before your brain starts lying to you too loudly, before your water is half gone, you follow your bright cord back to your anchor and leave. Lips cracked, eyes dry, legs tired from three hours to go half a mile, mind buzzing from hearing things a half-second early.

And for days after you'll feel flat ground as too thick, too loud, too close — and you'll be glad you prepared to be small there, instead of trying to be big.
