It is first the light that makes you squint, before the cold makes you breathe shallow.

High, so high the air is a thin blue sheet and the sun comes through it unfiltered, white to the point of vibration. No haze. No softness. The snowfields on the far peaks — and there are always far peaks, in every direction, because this place does not end — throw it back up at you. Light from above and below. Your shadow, if you can find it under the glare, is a hard black cutout, thin as paper.

You are standing on the rim of something. Not a mountain, not exactly a valley. A table. A continent lifted straight up out of the world when it was still cooling, and it has not had time to soften. The rock under your boots is young. That is the word that comes to you when you touch it: young. Black basalt glass, sharp as broken bottles, columns still hexagonal where they burst upward, ridges with knife-edges no wind has rounded yet. Between the columns, cracks still steaming faintly from deep heat. You can hear them tick as they contract in the cold. Nothing is weathered. Everything has an edge.

And it is cold. A cold that lives in the stone. Your breath comes out and does not dissipate, it hangs, and then settles — white condensation that freezes instantly on your sleeve, on your eyelashes, on the black glass. Zero wind for a moment, then a slow, heavy katabatic slide of air down off the ice, smelling of mineral and iron and something green underneath.

Because for all the cold and glare and sharp stone, it is lush.

Not lush like a jungle. Lush like tundra that has decided to not stop. Every horizontal surface that is not moving is covered. Cushions of moss so thick your boot sinks to the ankle and springs back. Olive and rust and acid-green. Black rosette lichens that face the sun like solar panels, hot to the touch even when air is below freezing — they make their own microclimate. Fronds of low fern that grow sideways, hugging the stone to avoid the wind, but so densely interwoven you cannot see the stone beneath. In the dips and pockets between basalt teeth, water sits. Not rivers. Never enough for a river. Pockets. Kettle-melt, dew that never evaporated in the thin air, condensation caught in moss wool. Small black mirrors, each no bigger than a dinner plate, reflecting the blinding sky. Damp, everywhere damp, if you press your palm down water wells up cold around your fingers. The air itself beads. Frost forms on everything upright by mid-morning and melts by noon into droplets that never fall, just hang.

And then you realize the ground is breathing.

You thought at first the mottled green-black plain stretching to the horizon was vegetation. It is not. Or not only. It moves — very, very slowly — with a vast inhalation.

There is no room here. That is the rule.

Shoulder to shoulder, flank to flank, back to back, for as far as you can see until the brightness swallows distance, the plateau is occupied. Fauna. Herd does not cover it. Swarm does not cover it. It is a packing. A saturation. Living bodies fitted together like cobblestones, like cells in tissue.

At first your eye tries to separate them and fails, because they are also green. The lushness is on them as much as around them. Each of the great slow grazers — low, wide, six-legged or eight, barrel-bodied — carries a full ecosystem on its back. Moss grows thick in the grooves of their iron-grey chitin, lichens crust their horns, ferns sprout between shoulder plates. From a distance they look like moving hummocks. Up close, you see the steam rising off them in the cold, and the condensation beading on their fur-filaments and freezing into lace.

They are not built. Nothing here is built. There is no wall, no nest of sticks, no ruin, no tool, no cut mark, no straight line that was made. No one has made anything here, ever.

And yet everything is industrial.

You hear it before you understand it. A low, continent-deep thumping, too regular for geology. A hiss, a clank, a sighing release. The herd is loud — not with calls, but with work. As you watch one beast near you, its sides bellows out. You can see the piston-ribs flex under the hide: intake, compress. Frosty air drawn in through broad nostrils with a whistling roar, heated in a furnace gut you can feel radiating even three steps away. When it exhales, white vapor jets sideways from gill-slits and instantly ices the moss around it — that is the damp, that is the pockets of water. Each animal is a small climate.

Its mouth works continuously, grinding. Teeth are not enamel. They are dark metal, self-sharpening ridges, mill-plates. You can hear the grinding from twenty yards: stone on stone. It crops moss, lichen, and the occasional mouthful of sharp basalt grit — which it needs, for ballast and to hone the plates. Further back along its spine, a line of vents opens and closes. Puff. Puff. Puff. With each puff a smell of sulfur and hot oil and cut grass.

Others are different castes, if that word can be used for something never designed. The tall striders that step — very slowly — over the backs of the grazers, on stilt legs to keep their bodies in the sun, have backs that spin. Literally spin. A whorl of black vanes set into their carapace rotates in the wind with a clicking ratchet, generating warmth through friction, generating a faint blue crackle of static that makes your hair stand up when they pass. They hum.

The processors move even slower. Huge, almost immobile domes sunk between the grazers, mouths wide open. The grazers regurgitate into them in a slow conveyor of mouth-to-mouth, a bucket line that can take a whole day to move a mouthful ten feet. Inside the domes you can hear churning, hydraulic pressing, a wet industrial crushing. What comes out the other side, extruded in long steaming ropes, is composted heat — black, rich, instantly colonized by bright green shoots that were waiting for it.

Above them, the air is also full. Not empty high sky, but packed. Flocks of kite-things, translucent wings stretched tight as drumheads, ride the thermal columns rising off ten million furnace-bodies. They never flap — no need. They hang there, thousands upon thousands, jostling wingtip to wingtip, filtering the steam for insects and spores, their wingbeats when they do adjust making a sound like canvas sails snapping, all together, a rippling crack that rolls across the sky.

No space is wasted. Between legs, smaller runners — dog-sized, furnace-hot, fast by comparison but still deliberate — dart in the tunnels of warmth under bellies, eating parasites, licking condensation, carrying seeds in their cheek pouches from back to back. On the backs, birds that are not birds pick at ice. In the melt pools, things with gills and iron jaws churn the black water.

Nothing hurries. That is the second rule. For all the noise, for all the engines, the tempo is glacial. A step taken now will be completed in a minute. A migration across this plateau takes a lifetime; the old ones at the front have grandchildren at the rear. Chewing is slow. Digestion is slow. The vents open on a ten-second cycle. Even the sound, though loud — oh, it is loud, a constant low roar of breath and grind and vent and wing that you feel in your sternum — is slow. A bass drone that does not peak or resolve. You stop trying to listen for change and simply let it occupy your body.

The weirdness comes on gradually, the way cold seeps in.

You notice first that some grazers are joined. Not touching — joined. A bridge of flesh and chitin and moss between two shoulder plates, with blood and hot oil pulsing visibly through it. Then you see three joined, then a line of seven sharing a single, slow, enormous heartbeat you can see ripple down the line. Individuals blur.

Then you notice the moss is listening. The black sun-lichens all turn, very slightly, when a strider passes, tracking its static field, not the sun.

Then you notice the light has weight. At noon, when sun and snow-glare meet, dust and spores and ice crystals hang motionless in the air, lit so bright they cast shadows upward. The whole saturated plain sparkles with exhaled ice — each breath a glittering cloud that does not fall but hangs, adding to a low fog that smells of hot metal and peat. The fog never lifts because there are always more breaths coming. It sits knee-high, glowing.

If you stand still long enough, they do not notice you as alien. Warmth is currency here, and you are warm. A runner detours to press against your leg for a moment, stealing heat. Condensation forms on your coat and a small, bright-green shoot, which must have been waiting in spore form in the air, unfurls from your shoulder seam within an hour, seeking the sun. The moss under you sighs as a grazer beside you shifts an inch to close the gap you made, because gaps are not allowed. Shoulder to shoulder. No room between presences.

Far off, toward the white horizon where the plateau breaks into fresh, sharp fjords of black glass still steaming from their birth, a deep boom rolls across the herd. A new crack opening. A new column lifting. The ground trembles faintly. No one runs. No one looks up. The pistons keep pumping. The mills keep grinding. The vanes keep clicking. The work of staying warm, staying fed, staying packed, continues at its patient, deafening, freezing, brilliant pace — a factory with no building, no owner, no product except more life, fitted tight, steaming in the bright cold.
