The yard does not end so much as stop.

You can stand in the middle of it — on the cracked apron of concrete between the pump house and the machine shop — and turn slowly and see all of it at once: the low ring levee, dark with wet, holding the marsh back; the eight or nine big buildings set far apart from each other like they are avoiding contagion; the ditches, the stacks, the water tower. Beyond the levee, flat green-brown marsh to every horizon. Above, a low lid of cloud the color of dishwater. Nothing is far. Everything is here.

It is low land. You feel it in your knees and ears, that sunkenness. The ground is only a hand's breadth above the water table. Dig a post hole and it fills before you can set the post. So they don't dig. Everything is built up — on pads, on pilings, on slag and crushed shell trucked in and tamped down. The natural ground is black muck that smells of rot and iron. The built ground is pale gray concrete, already stained dark at the edges where water wicks up. Between buildings there are great empty lots where nothing was ever built, or where something was torn down. They are not empty: they are shallow pans of standing water, rust-colored, skinning over with oil, buzzing.

It is hot. Not bright hot. Heavy hot.

Midday here is dim as late evening elsewhere. The sun is up there somewhere, a smeared white ache behind steam and coal smoke and the exhalation of the marsh itself. The light that reaches the yard is filtered, brown-yellow, shadowless. Bulbs burn all day on the loading dock and in the shop doorways — bare caged bulbs, haloed with moisture. They don't illuminate so much as insist. The brick of the boiler house, the corrugated walls of the warehouses, the black riveted sides of the oil tanks — all the colors are muted down to rust, soot, damp white. Sweat never dries. Clothes stick. Metal handrails are almost too hot to hold even in this gloom, and they are always slick with condensation.

Because it is wet. Everything weeps.

Water condenses on every cool pipe and then falls, constantly, a slow ticking rain under the pipe racks even when it hasn't rained in hours. It seeps from the base of the levee in cold threads. It stands in the wheel ruts, in the borrow pits, in the rectangular settling basins behind the dye works. The ditches run full to the brim — slow, opaque, green — carrying cooling water from one end of the yard to the other, and the culverts under the road gurgle and choke. Fresh water and marsh water and waste water have negotiated a truce here; you can't tell one from the other. Frogs call from the culvert mouths at all hours. Air itself feels chewed with water. Your lungs work for it. Laundry hung on the wire behind the foreman's house never gets dry; it just gets hotter.

And it is new — raw, sharp. This place has not been softened.

The canal banks are still vertical where the dragline cut them last year, showing clean layers: shell, peat, clay. The slag heap behind the foundry hasn't slumped or grown grass; its edges are glassy and knife-like, black and blue. Concrete is still concrete-colored where it hasn't spalled, rebar still has its mill scale. Even the rust feels fresh, orange and powdery, not yet turned to the deep brown crust of age. Piles of new pipe wait on timbers. Pallets of brick. Coils of wire. Nothing rounded. The corners hurt. You can tell the marsh does not want this, and has not yet had time to take it back. The levee holds. The pumps hold.

Set far apart, the buildings leave a lot of weather between them. That is the strangeness of the town-ness here: it has all the parts of a town — shop, dormitory, office with a clock, mess hall, chapel with a tin steeple, water tower with the company name bleeding rust down its legs — but none of the crowding. You have to shout or use the phone to get from one to another. The streets are too wide, graded shell and cinder, with puddles that reflect the stacks. Power lines sag on creosoted poles from building to building, humming. Steam lines run above ground on bent pipe supports, wrapped in asbestos and dripping. Walkways of pallets and planks cross the wettest lots. It is organized, deliberate, all right angles and numbered doors, but thin. Mostly air, water, heat, space — and then, islanded in it, a machine.

The machines never stop. That is the rule.

Two boilers, one backup. Three pumps for the levee, two for process. The exhaust fan on the machine shop roof. The generator that keeps the lights on so the dim can be worked in. The sound of them is the place's weather: a low, layered roar you feel in your sternum, with higher whines and clanks riding on top — the clatter of the chain conveyor, the periodic venting of steam with a scream that makes the gulls lift, the thump of the stamping press that you hear through the soles of your boots. Men shout over it without thinking, mouths close to ears. At night — though night is only a slightly darker dim — it is the same. There is no quiet shift. Shutdown would mean water rising, heat settling into metal, marsh coming in. So they oil and coal and watch gauges, and the sound goes on.

Where there should be plants, there are animals.

Vegetation is thin to the point of embarrassment: a few scorched palmettos leaning against the office foundation, nutgrass forcing its way through cracks in the apron, morning glory strangling a fence, a single tamarind tree by the mess hall that drops pods into the muck and is perpetually white with bird shit. The ground is too hot, too oiled, too drowned. But the animals have come in numbers that make up for it.

Dragonflies in copper and blue patrol the settling basins. Mosquitoes rise in pillars from the ditches at shift change. Swallows and martins cut the steam above the boiler house, eating them. White egrets stand motionless on the warm discharge pipes, one leg tucked, waiting for minnows in the warm outflow. Crabs — big blue-clawed marsh crabs — live in the culverts and come out at low dim to cross the roads sideways, clicking, unhurried, and the yard dogs, lean yellow curs that belong to no one and everyone, know to leave them alone. Rats run the pipe racks at dusk like commuters. Cats live under the dormitory, thin and fierce, fed on scraps and rats. In the mess hall rafters, sparrows. In the chapel eaves, mud-daubers and bats. At the edge, where the levee meets the marsh, frogs in such chorus that for a moment they rise above the engines, and beyond them, out in the reeds, the grunt of alligators.

People move through it slowly because of the heat, conserving themselves. Shirts dark with sweat by 8 a.m. The foreman in hip boots checking the ditch levels with a notched stick, same level every day. The women who run the mess wringing mops into buckets that are immediately refilled by condensation dripping from the eaves. A boy on a bicycle with a basket of bobbins, tires hissing on wet cinder. Everyone knows the boundaries: don't go past the levee without boots and a reason; don't touch a pipe without a rag; don't expect anything to change.

And nothing does. That is the deeper rule, more binding than any company rule.

The water stays at the same mark on the stick. The cloud stays at the same height. The heat stays in the nineties and the light stays at dusk. Slag is added to one end of the heap and taken from the other for road fill and the heap stays the same size. Coal comes in by barge through the cut and ash goes out, and the stacks smoke the same color. Rust blooms and is wire-brushed and blooms again in the same spot. The same egret stands on the same pipe. The same dog sleeps under the same pallet. You could come back in ten years, and the tamarind would not be taller, the concrete would not be older, the gurgle in the culvert would still catch on the same note. It is not stuck. It is balanced — water in as fast as water out, heat made as fast as heat lost into wet air, men tired as fast as men rested.

Stand long enough on that concrete apron, with steam condensing on your forearms and the roar filling your chest and the marsh smell and coal smell braided together, and you stop waiting for the weather to break. This is the weather. Hot, dim, loud, wet. A small, sharp-edged island of work holding exactly its own against the low water, humming to keep from drowning.
