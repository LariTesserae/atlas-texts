You don't find it so much as drop into it.

On the moor there is a collapsed throat no wider than a wellhead, choked with cool air. You go down on your backside, boots braced against scalloped limestone, through ten meters of dark that smells of rain that fell a month ago. The fissure kinks once, forces you onto your stomach, and then — it exhales, and opens.

The room is the whole world. You could hold it in your mind entire.

It is an egg stood on end, perhaps sixty paces from water to crown, perhaps less. Your eyes keep misjudging it because everything inside is close. The walls curve in and over, smooth as poured wax, pocked with solution cups and fluted curtains where water has worked the same path for ten thousand years and then stopped needing to change. Nothing here is sharp anymore. Even the breakdown blocks on the floor have had their corners licked round. This is old, settled stone, in balance with itself.

And it weeps.

Not flows. Weeps. The limestone sweats from a thousand pores. On the overhangs, droplets gather, fatten, detach. On the verticals, a dark sheen crawls sideways and down, joining in threads that find the old flutes and follow them faithfully to the pool. The air itself is saturated to the edge of raining. Your breath makes a small cloud that doesn't dissipate, it just thins into the general mist. Everything you touch leaves a print that fills back in with water within seconds. Your jacket darkens at the shoulders. Your hair clings. It is cool — not bitter, not cave-cold, but the steady cool of deep shade in autumn, the kind that gets into the joints and stays there. The rock is the same temperature as the water is the same temperature as the skin on the back of your neck.

The bottom third of the egg is water. Black water. Still in a way that seems practiced.

Drips fall into it from thirty feet up and should ring and ripple. They don't, or not much. A dimple, a swallowed tick, and then flatness again, as if the pool refuses to remember. It never rises. It never falls. Old high marks show as a pale bathtub ring of calcite a handspan above the present line, and old low marks show as a darker stain a handspan below, but the men who live here will tell you — the water has sat exactly here since their grandmothers' grandmothers. Seep equals drink equals evaporation. In equals out. It is the one perfect accounting in the place.

There is no green here. Not a blade, not moss, not even the optimistic smear of algae where light touches. Only mineral. Grey limestone, honey calcite, black chert nodules like knuckles pushing through the wall, rust-red films of iron bacteria slicking the submerged ledges. If you scrape a nail across the rock in the dimmer corners you get a pale, translucent slime — not plant, not animal, something simpler that lives on stone-breath alone and tastes of nothing. Nothing photosynthesizes. Nothing needs to.

Light comes from the throat you came down, a bruised grey disc no bigger than a tabletop, and from the town itself. It is always late-evening here. Shapes resolve at ten paces; faces at five. The crown of the dome catches the daylight and holds it as a general, sourceless gloaming. Below that, the terraces fall into their own shadows. Every dwelling keeps a small cup of light — a tallow dip in a chipped stone bowl, a wick of twisted hair or frayed hide, burning animal fat rendered from the rare catch. They gutter in the damp and have to be tended constantly, and they make more smoke than light. The smoke has nowhere to go. It hangs in a thin, eye-stinging layer about head height above the water, blue-grey, never thickening, never clearing. You learn quickly to stoop under it or stand above it.

And on every dry — drier — inch, the town.

It is a town. Not a camp. Streets, if you can call a six-inch ledge with a rope handrail a street. Addresses. Neighbors who can hear each other swallow.

The natural terraces ring the egg in three uneven galleries, and the people have completed what water started. Dry-stone walls, stacked without mortar from split slabs and fall, nudge out over the void on corbelled courses. Houses are one room, sometimes two, shoulder to shoulder, roof of one forming the floor of the next, up the curve like swallow nests. Ladders of lashed poles — not wood, there is no wood down here; poles of long bone and drift lengths salvaged from above over generations and black with grease and handling — connect level to level. Bridges of plaited hide and fiber span the narrowest part of the pool. Over the water itself, on driven piles of stone, stand platforms for work, for gutting, for sleeping in summer — though there is no summer here.

It is dense past comfort. You cannot stretch your arms out on the middle terrace without brushing two door lintels and a hanging net. Pots, bundles, drying lines, copper scraps, tool kits, sleeping pallets, water jars — everything layered, hung, tucked under. The population cannot be more than a hundred souls and yet the press of things feels like a city. Paths are single-file and negotiated by elbow and custom: uphill presses flat, downhill sidles, water-carriers always have right of way.

Their craft is hand-work. You hear it before you see detail. Stone on stone. A chert knapper squatting in a doorway reduces a nodule by pressure with a deer-antler tine, each flake ticking off like a glass chip. A woman cold-hammers native copper — dull pink, found as blebs in the rock — into fish-hooks and awls on a flat anvil stone, tap tap tap, no forge, no bellows, just patience. Someone else scrapes a hide stretched on a frame, shave shave shave. Cordage is twisted from hair and sinew and the long, pale fibers of things dredged from the pool. Pots are not fired here — no fuel for a kiln — they are carved from soft stone, or pieced from hide over withies brought down coil by coil from the surface at great cost. Everything is small, mended, mended again. A bowl will have three generations of drill-holes along a crack, laced shut with copper wire.

You would think such a small, closed place would be quiet. It is brutally loud.

The dome is a drum. Every drip from the crown lands with a report like a knuckle on a table, and there are hundreds of drips a minute. They do not sync; they layer into a constant stony patter, a rain that never stops but never storms. Under that, the town's own noise is trapped and thrown back. Footsteps on slab, coughs, a child crying, the clink of a stone bowl set down — all of it goes up, goes around, and comes back a half-second later slightly larger. You hear your own boot-scrape twice. The inhabitants shout a little, not because they are deaf but because to be heard at all over the weeping ceiling you must push. At midday — though there is no midday, only a slight paling of the grey disc — when everyone is awake and working, the egg fills with hammering and splashing and low talk bounced into a roar. Newcomers put their hands over their ears. The old ones don't notice. They lip-read as much as listen.

Wild life is almost an rumor. In the pool, if you sit very still on a pile platform and let your lamp smoke thin, you will see them: blind white fish, two inches long, hanging motionless and then darting. They have no eyes, only pits. There are crabs the color of candle wax that pick along the waterline at night, moving with extreme deliberation. Occasionally a pale cricket, impossibly leggy, feels its way across a ceiling curtain. That's it. That's the census. No birds venture down the throat. No rats. The people eat the fish sparingly, more as rite than sustenance — most food, grain and dried meat, comes down in sacks lowered on the long rope, traded for worked stone and copper and the particular white salt that blooms in the upper cups. They husband the pool the way you would husband a holy well.

And here is the unusual thing, the thing you feel in your teeth before you can name it: nothing changes.

Not in the way of ruin, or stasis of death. In the way of perfect maintenance. A wall slumps, and by morning it is rebuilt stone for stone, same shape, same lean. A house burns — fat fire, brief and terrible in such close quarters — and it is rebuilt on the same footprint, with salvaged lintel, same hanging hook left of the door. The water level is the theology. The old women dip a marked stick each dawn and announce what everyone already knows: still even. The children learn the dips and flutes of the wall the way other children learn streets: that seep will fill your jar by nightfall, that one never will. The condensation beads, runs, falls, rises as mist, beads again.

People are born here, die here, are laid in a high niche wrapped in hide, and in time the niche is emptied, the bones washed in pool water and built — literally built — into a new windbreak or a step. You will step on an ancestor going to borrow salt and not think it strange. There is nowhere else to put them. There is nowhere else to be.

If you stay a few hours your clothes will never dry. Your ears will ring even after you climb out. You will remember the press of warm bodies in cool air, the smell of wet rock and tallow and crowded sleep, the way lamplight caught a thousand wet facets and made the whole inner egg glitter faintly, like the inside of a mouth.

From above, on the moor, there is nothing. Wind, heather you will never see down there, sheep. A hole you could step over. Below your feet, shoulder to shoulder under the drip-roar, a complete urban world humming in its bubble, refusing — gently, absolutely — to become anything else.
