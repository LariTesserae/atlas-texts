It is night up here, but not cold.

That is the first wrong thing you notice. At this height the air should bite. Instead it holds you like a held breath. Warm, dry, thin. It comes into your lungs and doesn't seem to give enough back. You have to breathe deliberately, wider than you want to, and each breath tastes of hot stone and iron.

You are very high. You can feel it in your inner ears before your eyes adjust. The ground under your boots is not ground so much as the top of something still in the act of becoming ground. A ridge, broad-backed and raw, running away in both directions until the dark takes it. Below, on either side, there is drop — not a valley, exactly. There has not been time for valleys. Just a torn-away steepness, a fall into black air that goes down further than your body wants to believe. The scale is wrong for walking. This ridge alone would be a country if it were finished. As it is, it's one wrinkle among many. Peaks rise out of the dark around you in all directions, close enough to see their edges, far enough that the space between feels deliberate.

Nothing here is rounded. That is the second wrong thing.

Every rock is new-broken. The whole range looks like it was snapped upward yesterday and hasn't learned how to be mountains yet. No soft shoulders, no scree smoothed by rain — there is no rain, there has never been water here, the dust would not know what to do with it. Faces are flat cleavage planes that catch what little light there is and throw it back hard. Arêtes are knife-lines. Boulders are cubes and shards, stacked at impossible angles where they landed after being thrown up, their corners still sharp enough to cut rope. If you put your palm down, the stone is warm. Not sun-warm. Deep warm, as if the heat is still inside deciding whether to stay.

The darkness is not complete. It's a low, close darkness, 3 a.m. darkness even though there is no time here. No moon. No city wash. The sky is a lid of dust and dense air, black-brown, pressing down. What light there is comes from below, not above — a faint, embered seep from cracks in the ridge itself. In places the rock has split open a finger's width and inside is a dull orange that pulses very slowly, like something breathing. It does not illuminate so much as suggest. You navigate more by heat on your face and by the shapes of black against blacker. After a while your eyes find the edges: the sawtooth horizon, the leaning needles.

There are structures here.

From a distance you swear they are huts. Or cairns. Or the stumps of walls. Scattered along the ridge crest at long intervals — one here, two far off there, a cluster of four leaning together half a mile on — small, upright, deliberate-looking. Square-shouldered.

When you get close, they are not built. No one built them. They are what the rock does when it cools too fast under pressure: it extrudes in columns, in right angles, in stacked slabs that happen to make corners and lintels and empty doorways that go nowhere. One is a tower eight feet high, hollow in the middle where a softer core fell out. One is just three flat stones balanced in a way that cannot last. Faint marks cross them — straight striations, chatter marks from the uplift, not toolmarks, though your brain keeps trying to read them as writing. They are too sparse to be a town. They are too regular to be accident. You walk between them feeling watched by geometry.

And everything is moving.

Not metaphorically. While you stand there, the place is working. A low tremor comes up through the soles of your feet every few minutes — not an earthquake, more like a shrug. A hundred yards to your left, a whole slab the size of a house shifts with a dry sigh and settles a foot lower, raising a slow plume of dust that hangs in the warm air and does not fall. Ahead, the ridge is visibly higher than it was. You didn't see it rise. You just notice that the silhouette has changed, a new fang has shouldered up between two old ones. Shards tick and ping as they cool and split. A spire across the gulf leans, hesitates, then collapses inward on itself in utter silence — a slow, majestic crumbling that should roar and doesn't.

Because it is quiet here. Profoundly, unnaturally quiet.

Thin air and fine dust eat sound. Your own footsteps are muffled to a soft crunch that stops the instant you stop. Your breathing sounds loud inside your head and small outside it. There is no wind as you understand wind, only slow convective turnovers of warm air rising off the hot cracks, cool air sinking from above, stirring the dust in lazy helices without a whistle. Occasionally there is a deep, felt-more-than-heard groan from far below, as if the roots of the mountains are grinding. Then it stops. The quiet rushes back in, heavier.

There is no life to break it. No grass in the cracks, no lichen on the north faces — the rock is too new, too dry, too hot to host even that negotiation. No insects ticking, no birds. Only, in the sheltered lee of some slabs, a thin mineral skin, pale yellow-white like frost, has precipitated out of vapor. It branches in patterns that from the corner of your eye look almost like creeping things, like mycelium trying to learn itself out of sulfur and silica. It is not alive. Not yet. Not quite. If you touch it, it powders and is warm. It is chemistry rehearsing for biology and failing, for now, beautifully.

The rules here are simple and you learn them fast:

Stay on the warm rock. The warm rock is solid. The cold rock is about to move.
Do not trust edges. They were not carved, they were torn, and they are still tearing.
Do not wait for sound to warn you. Change here is silent. Watch the dust. Dust moving when air is still means ground moving under it.
Do not look for water, shade, shelter. There is none and there will be none. The dryness is absolute; it pulls moisture off your lips in minutes. Your sweat evaporates before it beads.
Do not mistake the standings for shelter or sign. They will be gone by morning — fallen, or risen into something else.

If you stand still long enough, the weirdness shows itself in small ways. Dust you kick up sometimes does not settle, but rises a few inches and hangs, caught in the heat. Shadows from the crack-glow fall the wrong way for a moment, as if the light source shifted underground. The ridge under you feels hollow, resonant, like a crust over a drum. Which it is.

You are standing on the skin of a world being made, high up where no air should be warm, in the dark with no stars, among ruins that were never cities, listening to mountains being born without making a noise about it.
