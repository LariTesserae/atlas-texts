It is dark here first. That is the first wrong thing.

You come up out of the lowlands — up a long, weathered stair of basalt and red rubble that has been climbing for a million years and has stopped trying to be dramatic about it — and you expect high to mean cold and thin and star-scoured. Instead the air gets warmer as you climb. It gets closer. It holds you.

This is the High Plain. There is no other name for it that sticks, because it is too large for a name. It makes its own weather. From the rim you can see cloud being born out over the center, a low black ceiling that never lifts, never breaks, never rains properly, just hangs there, warm as breath, sweating.

Light does not reach here. Not really. The sky is ironed shut. No moon, no stars, no sun you can point to — only a faint brown smolder on the underside of the cloud where heat pools, and, after your eyes learn, a texture to the darkness: nearer black of rock, farther black of air, moving black of herds. Your eyes stop looking for horizon and start looking for interruption.

The ground is old. That is the second thing you learn. This is not young volcanic violence. This is settled country. The lava laid itself down long ago and then wind and rare water went to work rounding it, patiently, until you get these vast, shallow swells, flat-topped mesas with soft shoulders, dry washes that wind like old scars and hold nothing most of the year. The rock is black glass where it is fresh-broken, dull charcoal where it is weathered, dusted everywhere with a fine red powder that gets in your teeth and tastes faintly of blood-iron. Underfoot it rings. Step on a slab and it gives a low pipe-tone, *tong*. The whole plateau is hollowed with old tubes and blisters that have collapsed, so the earth is a drum.

There is no building. Get that out of your head early. No wall, no road, no ruin, no post, no wire. Not even a cairn. Nothing has ever been stacked here by hands.

And yet, on your first night on the rim, you hear an engine.

Low, from far out in the dark: *chuff—chuff—chuff—pause—chuff*. Then another, overlapping, lower still. A clank of metal cooling. A long exhalation of steam through a valve. You sit up. You look for lights on the horizon, for factory windows, for the orange of furnaces. There is nothing but dark and that warm, wet-stone smell and the sound of industry with no address.

It comes closer by morning. Not morning — there is no morning. The dark just thins a fraction, from black to a deep umber, as the cloud thins and the heat radiating off the rock comes up. Condensation is everywhere. Every wire of grass is beaded. Your tent fly drips. Your own skin drips. This place does not have rivers. It has borrowings. Potholes in the basalt hold a hand of brown water for a week after a sweating storm. Seeps weep from under mesas for a day and then stop. The air itself is the river. If you stretch a cloth, by mid-dark you can wring a cup from it.

That is what the sparse green is doing. There is not much of it — that is the third wrong thing, because with all this animal life you expect grass to the belly. Instead: scattered tufts, far apart, each one fighting its own war. Wiregrass black-green and sharp as antenna, growing in rings. Bottle shrubs, squat and waxy, with leaves like beaten copper that turn edge-on to conserve. Dew-nets: low, tangled mats of hair-fine filaments that are not for eating but for catching. In the thinning dark they glitter with trapped beads, each tuft a tiny reservoir glittering in nothing. Between them: miles of empty scoria and slag and dust. Emptiness is the main plant here.

Then they come out of the dark, and you understand the engines.

They are not machines. They never were made.

The first is a bull, huge, barrel-sided, shoulder-high as a doorway, moving slow on four column legs. His hide is thick, ash-gray, cracked like kiln brick. His ribs — outside his skin, that is the strangeness — are bands of dull iron, grown, not forged, arching over his flanks. Between the slats, when he breathes, you see it: a deep furnace orange, pulsing. Heat shimmers off him in the cool-warm air. Each step is a piston stroke. His upper leg slides into his lower with a hiss, steam puffing from vents behind the knee, and the foot comes down with a soft, deliberate *chung*. On his head, two wide, fluted horns, cold iron, already dripping with dew that runs down channels in his face to his mouth.

He does not look at you. Behind him, more shapes resolve: cows, calves glowing dimmer, an old female trailing, limping slightly, her left piston misfiring *chuff—...chuff-chuff*. A herd of twenty in a country that could hold ten thousand herds without touching. And beyond them, far off where sound pools in the hollows, more *chuff-chuff-chuff*, herd after herd after herd, all walking their slow freight-train lines across a continent.

They are furnaces that walk. They eat the wiregrass and the copper-leaf and, deliberately, shovel after shovel of red iron dust with their broad lips. Inside them, in a gut lined with ceramic plate, symbiotic thermobacteria burn at a thousand degrees. They do what no plant here can do: smelt the rock to get the phosphorus and trace metals locked in it. What they cannot use they excrete behind them as perfect, marble-sized pellets of slag-iron that cool from orange to black and litter the trail for miles. Ball bearings dropped by gods. The cinder beetles will find them.

When the bull exhales, a plume of sulfur and steam rolls out and condenses instantly in the damp air and falls as brief, warm rain in a three-foot circle around his nostrils. Moss-things unfurl there for a day. Flies gather.

He crackles. That is the other industry. The air is warm and dry enough up high, despite the dew, that every movement builds charge. Their hides store it. Between the tips of the horns of two animals passing close, a blue arc snaps, three feet long, with a sharp ozone *tik* and the smell of a lightning strike. At night, across the plain, you see these now and then: brief, jagged stitches of blue-white, herd lightning, here and there and far off. It is the only real light here. The spark-moths, pale and blind, flock to it.

This is scaled production without a factory. Millions of walking kilns, each a boiler, a crusher, a dynamo, processing the sparse plain into heat and iron and manure and steam, keeping the whole high country five or ten degrees warmer than it should be. No one built them. They grew into the job the country offered.

And because there are so many furnaces walking, there is meat that walks, and because there is meat, there are hunters.

You will not hear them come. The plain is mostly quiet — so quiet that after the herd passes your own swallowing sounds loud. Wind moves low through wiregrass with a paper sound. Water ticks off rock. Far off, a cooling joint *tinks* as it contracts. That hush is the rule.

The wire-jackals live in that hush. Lean, rust-colored, tall-eared, they trot for days between herds, licking dew off cold horns and slag, waiting for a calf to stumble. They hunt by listening for misfires.

Worse are the hushers. You will probably never see one clearly. Low, flat, the color of scoria, with heat-pits along its jaw instead of eyes — what would eyes be for here? It lays itself in a wash where a herd must cross to reach a seep, and it waits, stone-still for a day, two days, and then uncoils under a belly and drives its insulated fangs straight into a furnace slit to short the heart. When it kills, there is no roar. There is a long sigh as six hundred kilos of steam lets go at once, a whiteout of warm fog, and then the clatter of beetles and moths arriving within minutes, and the jackals arriving within an hour, and by next dark cycle only the iron ribs remain, cooling, ticking, and the scattered black marbles, and a green flush where blood and condensate soaked in.

Above all this, high in the cloud-base where the air is thickest, the sifters turn — huge, silent, manta-shaped things you only glimpse when horn-lightning catches their bellies. They do not flap. They ride the heat rising off the herds, mouths open, straining spark-moths and ash-flies by the thousand. Occasionally one descends to drink from a pothole, and for a moment its wingspan covers the water like a night coming down inside night.

Rules, if you stay:

One — Nothing stays. Water is borrowed, grass is borrowed, heat is borrowed. Even the herds do not stay; a grazing line moves a mile a day and does not return for a year. If you camp, camp in a hollow and move.

Two — Do not mistake quiet for empty. The density is low — you can walk three days and see nothing but rock and dew-nets and old slag trails — and then in one hour cross sign of twenty herds, a kill, a sifter landing, a storm of beetles turning a dung pile into lace. Abundance here is not crowded. It is distributed.

Three — Cold is a tool. The kiln-beasts grow cold horns to make water out of air. The copper leaves turn edge-on. You learn to spread your fly at night and drink in the thinning.

Four — Everything hums. Put your ear to the drum-rock at deep dark and you hear it: the plain itself, resonating with a million piston steps miles away, with steam moving through old lava tubes, with charge building and releasing. An industrial hum with no industry. A world that runs itself.

On your fourth night you stop listening for a city. You lie on warm basalt that still holds the day's furnace-heat, though there was no day, and you watch blue arcs stitch briefly between dark shapes far out, and hear the soft *chuff-chuff* come and go like breathing, and the tick of condensation falling from wiregrass onto stone, and for a long time between those sounds, nothing at all — the vast, warm, dark hush of a place that learned, long ago, how to smelt and steam and spark without ever needing to be built.
