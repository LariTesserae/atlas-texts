You are high up, and you are underwater.

That is the first thing that has to be understood, and it never quite gets understood. Your body knows altitude by the thin ache behind the sternum, the sense of a vast drop below you. Your body knows submersion by the pressure on the eardrums, the warm give of water against skin. Here both are true at once.

This is the Summit Sea.

Far below this sea — kilometers below — there is supposed to be lowland, dry rock, air. No one has seen it in living memory. What exists is a tableland lifted so high into the sky that the ocean came up with it and stayed. A shallow ocean perched on top of the world like water held in a cupped palm. From a distance, if there were any distance, it would look impossible. Up close, it just feels inevitable and warm.

The water is bath-warm. Not tropical-sharp, not hot-spring scalding, but blood-warm, amniotic, 29 degrees that erases the boundary of your skin after a minute. You stop feeling wet. You feel held. It is faintly sweet-salty on the tongue, thick with suspended life, green-gold and dim. Light does not come from above so much as it sinks. The sun is a rumor far overhead, filtered through a hundred meters of living water into a perpetual late dusk. No shadows. Only a slow gradient: a little more amber above your head, a little more indigo-green below your feet. Particles drift down forever — silt, spore, skin — a reversed snow.

Beneath you, the ground.

It was once mountains. You can tell by the huge, patient shapes still there under everything: long whaleback ridges, colossal bowls where peaks used to be, valleys so wide and shallow you only know you're descending into one because the light gets a fraction dimmer and the forest gets taller. This is rock at the end of its life. Basalt and granite worn by eons of water until they feel like old bone, like river stones the size of counties. No sharp edges anywhere. Even the cliffs — where the tableland breaks and falls away into blue nothing at its edges — are rounded, soft-lipped, draped. Cracks have been filled in by lime and root and time. The world here has settled into its final form and decided to stay.

On that ancient, flattened high ground grows the forest.

Not coral. Something greener, older, more patient. Ribbonkelp a hundred meters tall, trunks as thick as redwoods, so close together you cannot swim between them without turning sideways, without pushing fronds aside with both hands. Their blades are translucent, veined like hands, overlapping in layer upon layer upon layer until the dim light is chopped into a thousand swaying coins. Below them, a midstory of feather-moss that grows in water in great breathing curtains. Below that, meadows of bladderwort and quillwort and things with no names, root mats so dense they have become ground themselves, springy underfoot, pocked with breathing holes that exhale slow strings of silver air that — wrongly — fall down instead of rising.

It is lush beyond reason for so little light. Everything touches everything else. A frond rests on a frond rests on a sponge rests on a stone rests on a root. The water between is not empty; it is broth. Spores, larvae of plants, torn leaves, mucus nets, drifting seeds with tiny lanterns on their tips. If you hold out your hand, within a minute something will land on it and take root. Density is the rule here. There is no horizon line, only a green fade in all directions where forest becomes water becomes forest again. It is large enough to have weather of its own, inside the ocean. Halocline fronts move through like cold fronts, visible as shimmering skies within the sea. Slow gyres turn over days, carrying leaves in continental circles. Sometimes a warm upwelling comes from below, smelling faintly of iron, and the whole forest leans toward it at once.

And it is almost empty of animals.

That is the strangeness you feel first as loneliness. A place this green, this warm, this active should be loud with fish, with bite and chase. It is not. Fauna is rare to the point of myth. You can drift for hours among the trunks and see nothing that looks back. Then, once: a pale ray, blind and white as milk, flapping slowly overhead, ignoring you utterly. Once: a school of needlefish, no bigger than eyelashes, passing as a single silver thought and gone. Once: a slug the size of a dog, grazing moss, its back a garden of smaller plants. They do not hurry. There is nothing to flee from and little to hunt. The system runs without them, plant and water and stone trading places in a slow loop.

Because it is active — oh, it is active — just not in the way with teeth. The forest breathes. Twice a day the kelp bladders inflate with a soft chorus of creaks and the whole canopy lifts ten meters, then sighs down. Leaves are shed in storms of green that clog the water for hours. Roots exude clouds of sugar that bloom bacteria that cloud the water that feeds the sponges that die and become silt that feeds the roots. Sediment rains constantly. Currents comb the meadows flat, then release them. It is predator-prey without predators: light competing with leaf competing with shade.

Silence holds all of it.

Sound does not travel here the way it should. Your own kick is muffled to a dull push. Your heartbeat is the loudest thing for miles. Occasionally you hear the forest: the deep groan of two trunks rubbing, the faint tick-tick of snapping shrimp somewhere you will never find, the sigh of a breathing hole. Otherwise a vast, warm quiet, as if the water itself is listening.

Scattered through this quiet are the marks.

A doorway standing alone in a meadow, lintel covered in moss, opening onto more meadow. A staircase of seven steps, exquisitely fitted without mortar, climbing to nothing. A ring of stones balanced so precisely they hum faintly when the current passes. A face carved into a boulder, worn nearly smooth, eyes closed, mouth open as if drinking. No roads. No cities. No tools, no metal, no glass, nothing made that could not be made by hand and left. Whoever was here built sparingly and then stopped — or learned how to breathe water and no longer needed shelter. The stones are now substrate. Everything grows on them. In a way they have become more natural than the rock.

Where the place stops making sense — and it does, constantly — it does so gently, without violence.

Down is indecisive. If you go still and empty your lungs you hang; if you think *fall* you begin to sink toward the meadow; if you think *rise* you begin to drift upward toward the amber. Bubbles you exhale sometimes rise, sometimes hesitate, sometimes drop past your face toward the ground, wobbling, as if gravity is polling the neighborhood and getting mixed answers. Distance is unreliable. A trunk that looked twenty strokes away will still be twenty strokes away after you have swum for an hour, until you look away and look back and it is suddenly right beside you. Your echo arrives before you make a sound. The light, divers swear, pools in hollows like water, so you can scoop brightness in your hands and carry it a little way before it leaks out.

And the dead do not rot here. There is not enough teeth, not enough hunger. When kelp dies it becomes peat, then mat, then ground. When that blind ray dies it will sink very slowly and the meadow will grow up through its ribs and keep its shape. The past does not wash away; it accretes. Walk the root-mat in some places and you are walking on ten thousand years of forest, compressed, still faintly green inside.

Rules, insofar as visitors have learned them:

Stay warm. The water will keep you warm if you let it; shivering makes you sink.

Do not chase the rare animals. They are not rare because they hide. They are rare because there are supposed to be very few witnesses.

If you find a staircase, climb it. Not because it leads anywhere, but because the forest notices manners.

And do not try to find the surface. There is one, far above, a bright membrane where rain from the real sky strikes the skin of the Summit Sea and does not penetrate. Those who have swum up say the water gets brighter and warmer and emptier until there is no forest, no ground, only light in all directions and the terrible feeling of being at the top of everything with nothing to hold. Most turn back. The forest is kinder than the sky.

At dusk — though it is always dusk — the lantern-seeds ignite by the million, tiny cold yellow-green fires drifting between trunks, and for an hour the dim becomes luminous without becoming bright. The forest in that hour is unspeakably dense and beautiful and quiet: layer on layer of glowing veil, the ancient rounded hills below holding it all up, the warm water holding you up, the whole vast high sea breathing in, breathing out, asking for nothing but witness.
