Down you go.

Not a cave — the sky is still there, but it is a long way up and it never quite gets bright. The land around here gave way. A long oval of country, miles across, dropped. From the rim you can see it all at once if the mist thins: a sunken valley floor lying two or three hundred feet below the plain, ringed on every side by a fresh, raw cliff.

The cliff is what you notice first. It hasn't learned to be a hill yet. The break is clean and black and sharp. Basalt columns snapped off like chalk, scree slopes of angular blocks that haven't been rounded, slabs still showing the pale scar where they pulled away last year or a hundred years ago — recent, in rock time. No soil on them, no softening. The talus lies where it fell, edges still keen enough to cut a boot. In the dimness the rock looks wet, blue-black, though most of it is dry to the touch.

Because this low place makes its own weather, and then keeps it.

The air in the Hollow is always cool. Cool enough that your breath shows even at midday, cool enough that sweat never quite dries. The sun, when it clears the rim, comes in sideways and weak, filtered through a permanent thin overcast and the shadow of the west wall. Mid-morning down here looks like evening elsewhere. Light pools rather than shines. The far wall is often just a darker grey suggestion behind drizzle.

And yet it is not wet. Not really. This is mostly a dry place of stone and dust and wind-sorted gravel. Then, in a dip, your foot sinks. There is water, but only in pockets.

Condensation runs all night off the cold cliffs and gathers in runnels. Seeps bleed out at the foot of the walls and make a dark damp apron of mud that never quite dries. In the lowest hollows there are shallow tarns — flat, iron-grey, a few yards across, fringed with black mud — unconnected to each other, with no streams between them, just damp ground. After rain, which comes as a brief cold spatter every few days and then stops, the dust darkens and for an hour there are temporary drips and threads of water ticking off overhangs. Then it goes back to damp. Your hands come away cold and moist from everything you touch, but you could walk a mile without finding enough to drink in one place.

Where the damp holds, there is green. Nowhere else.

Vegetation here is an afterthought, a thin garnish. Cushions of moss, dull olive and rust, clinging to the north sides of boulders. Crusts of lichen, pale as ash. In the wettest rims around the tarns, tufts of sedge and a few stunted, wind-combed willows no higher than your knee, their leaves small and hard. Between those islands: nothing. Acres of bare shingle and cracked silt, empty enough that you can hear your own steps for a long time before they are swallowed again.

It is the emptiness that makes the animals impossible to understand at first.

For long stretches there is nothing at all — a half-mile of cold gravel, a still pool with no ripple, a moss patch untouched. Low saturation. The place feels abandoned. Then the sky fills.

They are birds, mostly. That is the rule here. Auks and kittiwakes and big sooty ravens, and things like them but not quite them, in numbers that do not make sense for such a thin green place. They pour off the cliff ledges in ribbons, tens of thousands at a time, circling the basin in a slow, screaming gyre. They cover the scree in a black, shifting carpet and then lift all at once when something — nothing — spooks them. The cliff faces are whitewashed with guano in vertical streaks, and every narrow ledge is shoulder-to-shoulder with bodies, jostling, calling.

On the ground, between the pools, there are others: crabs in the tarns, grey mice and voles in the sedge, foxes gone pale and shaggy with cold that pad from hollow to hollow. Insects rise in cold clouds off the damp mud. Gulls stalk the shallows. But it is the birds that own the volume.

Because it is loud. You don't understand how loud until you are at the bottom and the walls throw it back at you. Not a dawn chorus that peaks and fades. A continuous, flat roar — shrieks and croaks and the dry clatter of a hundred thousand wings and the clack of shells being dropped on stone. Individual calls vanish into it. You have to raise your voice to be heard three feet away, and after an hour you stop trying. Your ears adjust and it becomes a pressure, like wind.

And here is the strange, unstrange thing: they are tool-users, all of them, and they have built nothing.

No nests of twigs, no dams, no burrow towns, no cairns. The ground is untouched except for footprints and droppings and peck marks. But watch a gull for five minutes. It picks a crab from a tarn, labors up thirty feet, and drops it precisely on the one flat anvil stone in the middle of dry gravel — again and again until the shell cracks. Ravens hop along the scree with short sticks held crosswise in their beaks, probing into cracks for grubs, discarding one stick and trying another until the length is right. Sea-otters — a few, in the larger pools — float on their backs and hammer mussels with rounded beach pebbles they keep in a fold of skin, the same pebble for days. Crows pry limpets with shards. It is everywhere once you see it: hand-work, hand-tools. No workshops. No stores. A stone picked up, used, dropped. A stick shaped with three bites and left behind. Primitive as breath, and gone the moment it is done, so the Hollow remains, in any lasting sense, unmade.

Nothing changes here. That is the last thing you learn, and it takes the longest.

With that many animals and that much noise and that much fresh rock, you expect succession. You expect the cliffs to soften, the pools to fill in, the moss to spread, the flocks to eat themselves out and leave. They don't.

The wind brings in just enough fish and plankton and blown insects from the coast to feed the birds. The birds manure the moss. The moss holds the damp. The damp keeps the rock cold and the cold keeps the mist. The scree stays sharp because frost shatters as much as rain smooths. A crab is eaten, another hatches. A ledge is vacated, another bird lands. Year to year, the white streaks on the cliff stay in the same places. The tarns neither grow nor shrink. The sedge tufts stay exactly the same size, nibbled to equilibrium.

Stand here long enough and you realize the roar never gets louder or quieter. The light never really rises or falls, just grey to greyer to grey. The cold seeps into your jacket and stays. You could come back in ten years, in a hundred, and find the same dropped pebble on the same anvil stone, a different gull dropping a different crab, the same sound filling the same bowl of sharp, young stone under a dim sky that never clears.
