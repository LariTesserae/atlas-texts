The valley doesn't end so much as it gives up trying to be land.

You come down to it after the black ridge, where the new stone starts. It is low here — sea-level low, lung-level low — a broad, shallow trough maybe an hour across on foot if you could walk straight, which you can't. The ground only rose a few years ago, shoved up by the quake, and it still remembers being seabed. Nothing is rounded yet. Every edge is sharp. The shale plates stand on end like broken crockery. The basalt is fresh-fractured, blue-black and glassy where the frost hasn't gotten into it. There are cracks you can put your arm down and not touch bottom, and they steam.

It is night, and it will be night for a while yet. Not the clean black of high places. A low, wet darkness. Cloud down to your eyebrows, town-glow hundreds of kilometers away bouncing dull orange off the underbelly, and otherwise a kind of deep blue-grey you can just barely see by. Your eyes never fully adjust because there is never quite enough to adjust to.

And still it is green. That is the first thing that won't leave you.

In the hollows between the sharp rock, where the fresh water sits, moss has taken everything. Not a polite covering — a drowning. Sphagnum and feather-moss and liverwort in mats a foot thick, hummock on hummock, black-green and bottle-green and yellow in the torchlight, sodden and growing over themselves. Dwarf willow, no higher than your ankle, knotted tight as fists, holding on between stones. Sedge in clumps so dense the water has to go around. Fungi everywhere, white pins and orange brackets, soft as ears. The cold hasn't killed it. The dark hasn't killed it. If anything, the cold has preserved it — every leaf is turgid with water, rimed at the tip with ice that never quite melts, beaded with condensation that never quite dries. The air itself sweats. You breathe out and your breath doesn't dissipate, it joins it. Your jacket is wet in minutes, not from rain but from the air condensing on you. The rocks weep. You can hear them ticking as the water films run off them, and where you put your hand the stone is slick and alive.

Water is not in a river here. It is near the surface, under the surface, just barely pretending to be surface. Step and water comes up around your boot. Step again and you hear hollow. The whole valley floor is a thin skin over water. Old tidal creeks, not yet decided if they are streams, braid and re-braid between the moss hummocks. Puddles that are really the tops of pools. Pools that are really windows down to black peat water. Off to the west, where you can hear it but not see it, the main outwash roars over the fresh scarp — white water over un-worn rock, chewing it. Every hour the level is a little different. Stakes along the duckboard show it: green, yellow, red. Tonight most of them are at yellow.

You stay on the duckboard because there is no other way. Planks, pallets, crushed-stone causeway where the machines have been, all slick with ice and algae. The rule is simple: if you step off, you go to your knee, and you crush ten years of growth that only took two years to grow. And the cold gets in.

There are not many built things, but you notice them because there is so little else vertical. A hut, low and sodded, light off. A fish-weir rebuilt three times, now just black posts in a chattering side-channel. A cache on stilts. And the shepherds.

They are spaced up the valley, maybe seven of them that you can count when their beacons sweep — thin masts, no thicker than a young birch, thirty meters tall, with no wires and no crossarm, just a seam of dull red light slowly circling. Everyone calls them stoves, though they give no visible heat. Stand under one and you feel it in your molars first, then in your chest: a low pressure, a held note below hearing. The air around them is a degree or two warmer, enough that the frost doesn't settle on the moss in a perfect circle, enough that the sedge there stays supple. Snow melts off your shoulders if you stand too long. The old men say they are holding the new land up, knitting it. The maintenance crews say less. If you watch closely on a still moment — and there are no still moments — you can see the air is full of motes, bright as midges, that are not midges. They work the frost. They work the rootlets. They seal the cracks. You brush one off your glove and it rights itself and goes back to work. Nobody finds this strange anymore. You just don't shine white light at a shepherd after dark. It blinds the swarms. Use amber. Keep moving.

Because everything else is moving.

The sound hits you before the smell, before the cold gets through your collar. It is not one sound. It is layers that don't fit.

The water is the floor of it — the outwash and the thousand side-rills and the drip off everything, a constant white roar that you feel in your knees.

Above that, the wind, funneling low off the sea ice, tearing at the sedge, making the guy-wires on the shepherds sing a high, thin harmonic that comes and goes.

Above that, the animals.

They are everywhere, and they are loud because it is movement night. The pale geese have come down in thousands and they cover the dark pools shoulder to shoulder, white bodies packed so tight the water disappears. When a gust hits or a fox slinks too close, a section lifts — a thousand wings at once, a rip like canvas tearing, honking, gabbling, the air suddenly full of beating and droppings and down. They settle again, arguing.

In the channels, the fish are running late. You can hear them even over the water: the slap and thrash as char and salmon force themselves up through shallows that barely cover their backs, black fins cutting V's in water that reflects nothing. Where the channel narrows at the weir, they stack, writhing, and the gulls and the arctic terns that should have left weeks ago scream and dive in the dark, guided by sound and by the faint red sweep of the shepherds.

On the drier hummocks, reindeer — a small herd, maybe forty — pick their way, clicking ankles, breathing hard, coats rimed white. They are nervous, bunched, calves in the middle. A vixen follows at a respectful distance, trotting the boardwalk like she owns it, stopping to shake water off. Lemmings rustle under the planks. Midges, impossibly, in the cold, in clouds around every warm thing, including you, getting in your nose and mouth.

And under it all, the shepherds' bass, and the tick and boom of new ice forming and breaking in the pools, expanding in the fresh cracks, prying the sharp stones a millimeter further apart. The valley is literally creaking as it settles.

It is crowded in a way a night marsh shouldn't be. Moss on moss. Bird on bird. Water on land on water. Every surface is occupied. If you stand still for a minute, something lands on you, or swims under you, or grows — you imagine — over your boots. Your headlamp catches eyes: green, amber, white, low and high and in-between. The smell is peat and rot and guano and crushed green and iron and ozone from the masts, and underneath, salt. The sea is still in the rock.

Out past the immediate glare of your lamp, the valley keeps going. You can sense its scale by sound: another falls further down, another restless raft of geese further up, another shepherd humming out of sync with this one so the beat frequency makes your stomach turn. Ridges on both sides, black and sharp against slightly less-black cloud, hemming you in but far enough that the wind has room to build. A town's worth of space, a watershed's worth, all doing this at once. The storm that was supposed to pass yesterday hasn't passed; it has settled in to spin slowly, flinging drizzle and sleet and then drizzle again. No lightning. Just pressure changes that make your ears pop and make the birds lift for no reason and settle again, screaming.

The rule of this place, if you work here, is you don't fight the turbulence. You time it. You cross the low causeway between gusts. You count fish when the water drops an inch. You fix a plank when the reindeer have passed, not before. You keep your gloves on because the cold is stupid and total — a wet cold that finds every gap, that turns your laces to wire and your cheeks to wood — but you keep your hood off one ear so you can hear the change in the water that means a new channel opening behind you. You learn to sleep through the roar and wake when it changes pitch.

It is not a strange place. The kids who grew up after the uplift hunt eggs here in summer and curse the midges and dare each other to touch a shepherd's base with a tongue. The old fish-counter in the hut drinks tea with peat in it and marks numbers on a waterproof slate and talks to the swarm like pigeons. The tech is just weather now. The dark is just season. The noise is just autumn, late and crowded, everyone trying to eat and move and spawn and leave before the true freeze locks the sharp new land solid.

You stand on the boards with water welling up around your boots, with down sticking to your wet sleeves, with your breath and the rocks and the sky all weeping together, with green life thick as a mattress under a black sky, with the ground humming through your feet, deafened and half-frozen and jostled, and you understand the valley isn't young like a child is young. It's young like a wound is young — fresh, open, hot at the edges despite the cold, working furiously to close itself, and loud with the work.
