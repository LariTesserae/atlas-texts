You come to it without warning. The plain is flat, dry, pale grass hissing, and then the ground is simply missing.

Not a valley. A removal. A roughly oval bite taken out of the earth, maybe three hundred paces long, half that wide, deep enough that the rim is over your head three times over before you reach the bottom. You can stand on the lip and see the whole of it at once — the far wall, the near wall, everything inside — like looking into a room with the roof torn off. The sky above it is a hard, cut-out blue, too bright at the edges.

The way down is an old stair, not built so much as worn. Successive feet over a very long time have dished each step in the middle. The material underfoot is ambiguous: at first it looks like basalt, dark and vesicular, but where it has broken it shows a fine, glassy grain, and faint parallel lines, like sediment, or circuitry. Everything here has been weathered into the same language. Stone and whatever-was-built have settled into each other. Lichen grows over both without preference.

It is warm inside. Noticeably warmer than the plain, by the time you are halfway down. And wet.

The walls weep.

Every terrace, every vertical face, every overhang is damp and darkly streaked. Water does not come from a single spring but from everywhere, beading out of the rock itself, gathering into drops, into threads, into thin glassy sheets that slide down and fall. After a rain up top — and it rains often here, short violent afternoon rains — the whole interior runs for hours. But even now, with the sun straight overhead and pouring into the pit like water into a cup, the walls are sweating. The air is thick with it. Your shirt sticks. Your breath comes back to you. Condensation pearls on any cool surface, on your eyebrows, on the handrail where a handrail remains, a rusted pipe that sweats cold despite the heat.

Down at the bottom, there is no solid floor. There is a pan.

Shallow, braided, restless water over hot silt and salt crust and black sand. Ankles deep in most places, knee deep in the channels. Warm as bathwater, and in places hotter — where it clears over a pale vent you can see it shimmer and shiver, and hear the low frying hiss where heat meets flow. The water never sits still. It pulses. Every few minutes a surge comes up from below, invisible until it arrives, lifting the whole sheet by a hand's breadth, sending it running outward to slap against the lowest terrace, then sucking back, leaving the silt ribbed and steaming. White rims of deposited minerals mark each recent high stand, one inside the other like growth rings.

You learn quickly not to step where it is milky white.

Vegetation is sparse, and careful. Nothing tall. Nothing that would need deep roots or quiet. On the higher ledges where spray doesn't scour, there are mats of rust-red moss and a wiry fern that grows in single, stubborn fronds out of cracks, its stems black, its leaves a dusty green filmed with salt. Down near the water, lime-green algae slicks the stones so they are treacherous, and in the brackish back-pools behind silt bars there are clumps of glassy jointed things, half plant, half salt. They rattle when the wind moves, which is seldom down here, because the pit makes its own weather.

Fauna, almost none. A small white crab, blind, that walks sideways across the hot mud and vanishes into holes that fill with water behind it. Midges in bouncing clouds over the cooler pools. High above, sometimes, a single hawk circling the thermals off the pit, never descending, its shadow crossing the water at intervals like a hand over a page.

The light is brutal. Sun falls straight in for most of the day and bounces. Water, wet rock, pale sinter — everything throws it back up at you. You squint. The undersides of overhangs glow with reflected brightness. At noon the shadows shrink to nothing and the pit looks depthless, overexposed. It is then that you see them best:

The threads.

Above the center of the pan, where the water is most turbulent and the steam thickest, droplets stop falling. They rise.

Not spray. Thin, deliberate filaments of water, as thick as sewing thread, lift off the churning surface and climb, wobbling, joining, breaking and rejoining, up to a thing that hangs in the air.

It is the only large made thing left, and it should not be there. A ring, perhaps ten paces across, made of dark segments that do not quite touch. It hangs perhaps fifteen meters above the water with no cable, no support, its underside stained with mineral bloom where rising water kisses it and flashes to steam. The segments move — slowly, ponderously, sliding past each other, realigning, occasionally shuddering a few centimeters inward or outward. When they move, the tone changes.

Because the place is loud. That is the first thing your body understands, before your mind does. The roar of falling sheet-water off the walls, a hundred small waterfalls combining into one continuous white noise. Under that, the deep, chest-felt thrum from below, from the ring, from the ground itself — a low C you feel in your molars. Under that, or over it, sizzles, gurgles, sudden belches of steam from vents that open and close in the silt, and the crystalline ticking of cooling salt. You have to raise your voice to be heard three feet away. If you stay long, your ears ring with it after.

And around the ring, held in the thrum, is a shell of stillness. Hundreds of thousands of suspended beads of water, each perfectly round, each catching the sun, hanging motionless in concentric, slowly rotating spheres. They do not fall. They do not drift, except as the whole system breathes. When a surge comes up below, the innermost shell shivers and a few beads drop, pattering down, and are immediately replaced from below by new rising threads. The air there tastes metallic, and the hairs on your arms stand up.

No one built this recently. That is clear. On the eastern terrace there is a walkway of grated metal, half collapsed, its supports dissolved to orange stalactites. A small white hut on stilt legs, door gone, inside a bench and a coil of cable fused to glass. Three slender pylons spaced around the rim, matte black, leaning at slightly different angles, bearded with lichen up to a sharp, clean line where the spray keeps them washed. A cairn of broken core samples. Faint marks: a painted number, 7, on a wall that has since wept calcite over it; a set of bootprints cast in sinter, preserved mid-stride.

Whatever the ring is — pump, valve, engine, instrument, door — it has been here long enough to become climate. The water has carved channels in its shadow. The stone has grown around its field. The moss knows its rhythm, fruiting after the big surges.

The rules, if you stay, make themselves known:

Keep to the dark stones; they hold. The white crust is hot and thin.

Watch the shells. When they tighten and the hum climbs half a note, get up two terraces. A surge is coming.

Do not shout near the center. Not from reverence — your voice comes back wrong, arriving a fraction before you speak, doubled by the field. It unsettles the stomach.

And do not expect it to be the same twice. The pan remakes itself hourly. A bar you crossed in the morning is a channel by afternoon. Steam that vented on the north side moves south. The ring, patient, misaligned, weeping light and water upward into blinding air, continues its slow work of holding the pit open against the earth that wants to close it, and the water that wants to drown it.
