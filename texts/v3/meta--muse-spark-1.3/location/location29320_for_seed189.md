It doesn't look like anything from above.

Dry grass, warm dust, a flat pan of cracked earth that rings hollow underfoot if you know where to step. And then — a tear. Not a cave mouth, not a sinkhole. A slit where the ground has split open along a root, no wider than your shoulders. Young. The edges are still sharp, soil clods still holding the shape of the rip, grass roots dangling in air, snapped white and bleeding milk.

You go down because light goes down.

You squeeze through heat and smell green going too sweet, and then your feet find nothing, and the slit becomes a throat becomes a room — and the room is empty.

That is the first wrongness. Everything above is dry and thin. Here, below the surface, everything is plant. Not plants growing on walls. The walls *are* plant.

A pocket, no bigger than a small bedroom, a lung tucked under the plain. Its entire sphere is woven. Thick anchor roots the width of your thigh arch overhead like ribs. Between them a thatch of finer roots, vines, runners, leaves pressed into leaves pressed into leaves until there is no soil left, no rock. Only living mass, overlapping, grafting into itself. In places the leaves are huge, waxy, bowl-shaped, grown edge-to-edge to make a continuous skin. In places the roots have flattened and fused into something like wood, like muscle. If you press your palm to it, it gives faintly with warmth. Warm as skin. The air inside is warm.

And yet it is hollow. Emptily, impossibly hollow. You stand in the middle and there is nothing with you — vast space between presences. A single chamber of clear air held open by all that life leaning outward, refusing to fill the center. Your voice does not belong in that much emptiness.

Light comes down the throat in a single slanted column, full daylight, ordinary noon light. It should not reach this far. It does, and as it falls it greens. It passes through a canopy of translucent young leaves shuttered over the tear above — living stained glass — and by the time it hits the floor it is thick and green-gold and utterly normal in brightness, as if you were standing in a forest clearing and not buried. Dust motes and pollen and tiny shed hairs drift down through it, slow.

Water lives here only as visitor. There is no stream, no pool. The world above is too dry for that. Instead the room sweats.

Condensation beads constantly on the cooler undersides of the leaves, swells, and falls. *Plink. Plak. Plok.* Each leaf-cup high in the weave holds a mouthful of yesterday's rain that made it down the throat, brownish, quivering with a drowned ant or a drowned sky. The air tastes of iron and chlorophyll and damp rot. When the wind moves above, a brief rain stutters down the shaft — a sudden spatter on your shoulders, on the floor — and then stops. The floor itself is a shallow bowl of interlaced rootlets, springy, always faintly damp, dark with decay. In the lowest dimple, a pocket of water no deeper than your cupped hand persists, skinned with pollen.

It is young, this place. You can see where it was born. On the east side the weave is torn — a fresh wound where the ground settled and the root-ball ripped and then healed around the emptiness. The broken ends are sharp, pale, unsheathed. Sap still wells there, slow and clear as resin, hardening into amber beads. New shoots, almost white, blind and questing, uncurl from the wound a millimeter a day. If you come back in a week the shape of the room will have changed.

That is because it moves. It does not sit still like a cave.

Stand quiet and you feel it. A slow peristalsis in the walls. A creak, then a groan, then a settling sigh as the anchor roots take up the weight of the plain above and redistribute it. Fibers tick as they grow. Water ticks as it falls. Your own breath comes back to you amplified.

It is LOUD in here. The sphere is a drum, a throat, an ear. Every drip lands like a hammer on a stretched leaf. Every rub of vine on vine is a rope on a ship's mast. And underneath, constant, is the hydraulic rush — you can hear the sap rising, litres of it, climbing in the dark vessels around you, a low wet roar that swells at midday when the leaves above transpire and pull. When the whole mass inhales — and you will swear it inhales, the walls drawing out a fraction, the pressure dropping against your eardrums — the sound peaks into something like surf, like a stomach growling, and then releases with a rain of condensation shaken loose all at once.

No one lives here. Almost.

That is the second wrongness. With that much living tissue, there should be swarms, nests, rot-eaters. There are not. The room keeps itself empty. Occasionally a pale cricket, eyeless, long-antennaed, picks its way across the root floor and disappears into a pore. Occasionally something larger comes down the throat to drink — you see the scuff of claws on the wood-root, a tuft of grey fur snagged on a thorn.

Whoever comes, they have hands.

There are no structures. Nothing built. No nest, no altar, no wall. The place is untouched except for this: on the floor near the water pocket, three thorns, each as long as your finger, have been snapped off, stripped, and sharpened to a needle point at one end with something like teeth. One still has a bead of sap on its tip. A vine has been peeled in a long spiral — the ribbon of bark lies curled, still supple, clearly used to bind or to lift water. A leaf-cup has been neatly cut from its stem and set upright so it holds water without spilling. Hand-work. Simple, manual, gone. The maker is not here now. The room is too empty for a maker to stay.

And if you stay past the time it takes the light column to slide off the floor, you learn the rule of the room, the real one:

It is bigger inside than it should be.

You notice it first in sound. Your footstep returns a fraction before you make it. Your drip-echo lands out of order. You turn your head and the far wall seems suddenly farther than your arm remembers, then closer, as if the sphere breathed space as well as air. Leaves grow through leaves in ways that cannot be untangled — a runner enters a leaf from above and exits a root below without crossing the interval. If you stare into the weave, you see depth after depth of green halvings, chambers within chambers, each smaller, each lit, receding past where the plain should be. The plant is folding. The pocket is a knot where the world above has been tucked into itself too many times.

Do not follow the white shoots with your eyes too long. They lead down and around and back to your own back.

Do not be here when it swallows. You will know: the light in the throat narrows, the sap-roar rises, the walls sweat all at once in a warm sudden rain. The throat is closing to conserve. It will open again. It always opens again. But for a little while the room decides to be a seed, sealed and warm and damp and utterly full of itself, with you as the single grit of foreign matter in its empty heart — held, listened to loudly, and then, when it exhales, released.
