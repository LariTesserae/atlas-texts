On the high table you come out of the dark into more dark.

This is not a peak. This is what is left after peaks give up. Three kilometers up and still flat — a continent worn down to its basement, an old shield of black basalt and red ironstone rounded to the patience of molars. The ancient mountains are here only as ideas: long, low swells on the horizon like sleeping animals, inselbergs that rise a hundred meters and then stop, as if they forgot what they were reaching for. Everything has been sanded by a wind that no longer blows. The ground is not rock so much as the memory of rock — grus, dust, ventifact pebbles polished on one side, salt and manganese varnish, plates of desert pavement that ring faintly under your boots.

It is warm. That is the first wrongness. The air should be cold at this height, thin and biting. Instead it holds against your skin like breath held in a mouth. The stone underfoot holds it too. If you kneel and press your palm to the pavement you feel the day that left a million years ago still leaking out. Warm dust gets in your shoes, in your teeth. It tastes of iron and old electricity. There is no damp anywhere. No seep, no well, no cistern sweat. Your lips crack within an hour. Your sweat dries before it beads.

Above you there is no sky to speak of. Not cloud, not stars in any useful number — a lid of absolute, mineral dark, plum-black to utter black. The darkness here is not weather. It is the place. At noon or midnight, by whatever clock you brought, the light never changes: a faint, deep-red smolder at the edge of vision, as if the world itself is cooling. Your eyes never quite adjust because there is nothing to adjust to. You learn to see by heat and by touch.

And spread thin across that flat, keeping low as if to avoid being noticed by the sky, is the town.

It is not dense. That is the second wrongness for a city. From the rim where the railbed comes up — two rusted ribbons climbing out of the lowlands on a causeway of crushed slag and never quite arriving — you can see the whole extent at once, and most of it is emptiness. A valley-sized scatter: a boiler house here, a row of workers' tenements there, a kilometer of nothing but packed dust and guy-wires between them. Conveyors on stilt legs stride out into the dark and stop. Gantry cranes stand with their hooks down, touching the earth like tired animals. Chimneys, dozens of them, brick and riveted plate, cold to the look but warm to the hand, breathing nothing. No smoke. No steam. There was never water to make steam with.

This was built for production on a scale that needed streets, culverts, sidings, switching yards, company stores, chapels. You can read the organization still: the sorting sheds in a line, the machine shops around a central powerhouse, the housing in grids that soften at the edges where wind-blown fill has banked against the walls. Layered construction — newer sheet-metal patches bolted over older brick, older brick over cut stone, all of it rounded. That is the strangest thing to touch: the buildings have eroded like the hills. Corners gone soft. Window sills dished. Iron railings thinned to wires. But there is no weather here to do that. No rain has ever cut these gullies. No frost has spalled these bricks. It is as if the town decided, out of respect for the plateau, to imitate great age in advance. Or as if dry time alone, moving dust by dust, is enough if you give it long enough.

Power still runs. You can hear it if you stand still long enough and let your own blood quiet. Not the roar industry promises. A low, distributed hum at the threshold of hearing, a 50-cycle throb from the powerhouse that you feel more in your molars than in your ears. Inside the machine halls — the doors are all open, there are no doors worth closing — the big flywheels still turn. Slowly. Impossibly slowly, a revolution per minute, maybe less. Shafts turn in their bearings. Looms with no thread rock. Presses kiss empty dies and withdraw. Belts travel over pulleys, carrying nothing from nowhere to nowhere. Pilot lamps burn amber and blood-red in panels, constellations of small hot eyes. No overhead lights. No streetlights. Light is not spent here. Every watt that can be spared goes to keeping the turning going.

No one will tell you what they make. There is no product piled on the sidings, no waste heap growing, no inrush of raw material. The ore bins are full of the same dust that is everywhere. The tools in the racks are worn to the shape of hands. Warm to the touch, all of it. The machines are not working toward completion. They are maintaining equilibrium. To stop them would be colder than to let them run.

People live here, after a fashion, sparse as the buildings. You will not meet crowds. You will hear a boot-scrape two streets over and find no one when you turn. Doors ajar onto rooms that smell of hot oil and bedding and stone. A kettle ticking as it cools on a hob that is never entirely cold. A chair worn to a shine. In the tenements laundry hangs — not cloth washed in water, there is none, but cloth aired and beaten, gray work suits and gloves stiff with grease. Navigation is by memory and by heat: handrails polished by touch, ropes strung between corners, the faint warm draft that leads you back toward the powerhouse. Faces, when you see them, are dust-colored, eyes large in the dark. They do not ask where you came from. Arrivals are so rare they are treated like weather — noted, endured.

There is no green. Not a weed in a rail joint, not a lichen on the north side, not mold on bread because bread does not last long enough to mold, it desiccates. Nothing rots here. Things thin, abrade, sublime away. The ecology, if you can call it that, is mineral and animal-thin. Pale ash-moths, almost blind, beat themselves against the pilot lamps until their wings fray, then crawl into cracks to die. Duct-rats — lean, hairless-tailed, whiskers caked with graphite — run the cable trays and lick grease from zerks. In the hottest culverts under the powerhouse live things the workers call cinder-pups: low, eyeless scavengers the size of a shoe, that find you by the warmth of your feet and nose at your laces, then think better of it and melt back into the dark. Nothing sings. Nothing croaks. Nothing flocks.

Sounds are events. Your footfall. The tick-tick-tick of contracting plate. A chain swaying somewhere and clinking once against a stanchion, then deciding not to again. Far out on the pavement, the wind will lift a spoonful of dust and set it down again with a sigh you almost imagine. Mostly, quiet so complete you hear the hum of your own nerves, and underneath that, the town's hum answering.

The rules, you learn without being taught:

Keep to the warm paths. Cold metal means dead load — it will not hold you.

Do not waste light. If you must carry a lamp, shade it. The dark is communal.

Do not ask for water. There is a vocabulary for thirst here that has nothing to do with drinking — oil for throats, damp rags sealed in tins to press to the forehead for five counted seconds on feast days. The old dry cisterns stand open and empty, perfect concrete bells. Children drop stones into them to listen for a depth that never answers.

Do not expect change. Nothing finishes here. The flywheel will not stop. The dust will not settle higher than your ankle. The rounded corner will not round further in your lifetime, nor in a hundred lifetimes. The town reached its final form long ago and now simply continues, warm, dark, turning slowly in place on top of the world.

If you climb to the roof of the sorting shed and lie on your back on the still-warm tar paper, you can feel it best: the high, ancient flat extending past seeing in every direction, the scattered hot hearts of machines pulsing faintly below, the vast emptiness between them not empty but balanced, the darkness pressing down like a hand that is not unkind, only heavy. You breathe the thin warm dust. Somewhere a bearing sighs. Somewhere a moth finds a red lamp and does not leave. Nothing begins. Nothing ends.
