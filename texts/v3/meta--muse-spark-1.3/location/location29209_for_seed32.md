At first you think it is an alley. Then you understand it is too small to be an alley, and it goes nowhere.

It is a pocket, sunk low between masses that rise out of sight. Three — no, four — walls lean in so close you can touch two at once if you spread your arms. The floor is not ground. There was never ground here. There are only cut slabs, fitted so tight a knife would not go between them, worn in the middle into a shallow dish by feet that are long gone. Everything your eye lands on was put there by hands.

The walls are the strange part. They are not flat. From ankle height to as far up as you can crane your neck, they are honeycombed.

Hundreds of small, square-mouthed niches, each about the size of two fists together, each with a little lip of mud-brick at its sill, row on row on row, packed with no waste space between them, rising straight up until the walls tilt out of focus in the glare. A dovecote. That is the word that comes. A nesting wall. Only there is nothing nesting.

That is the first wrongness you feel in your stomach: the completeness of the absence. No pigeons. No sparrows. No flies, no beetles under the lips, no spider silk strung across the dark mouths. The holes are clean-swept and dry as bone. When you put your hand into one, the back is cool — the whole place is cool, cellar-cool, despite the light — and your fingertips come away with nothing but fine, pale dust, brick-dust and lime-dust, utterly dry. There has been no water here in longer than you can guess. Not a stain, not a dark tide-mark, not moss that needs seep. Only dust.

The light is the second thing. It should be dim down here at the bottom of the well. Instead it is fierce. The sky above is only a ragged slit, white-blue and too bright to look at, but the walls catch it and throw it back and forth. They were whitewashed once, a hundred years ago, and though most of the wash has weathered off into patches, enough remains in the corners and inside the niches to bounce the sun around until the whole pocket glows. You squint. The edges of the niches double and blur. Every upward face is lit. There is nowhere for shadow to hide except deep in the holes themselves, little square blacks, and even those seem to shimmer at the edges. The stone under your palms is cool, though. It drinks the heat as fast as the light makes it. Cool on your neck, cool on your shins, while your eyes water from brightness.

And the sound. You noticed it before the light, really, as a pressure in the ears when you squeezed in.

It is loud. A full, round roar made of many thin whistles braided together — wind playing across all those empty mouths at once. Each niche is a flute with no player. From below you hear them combine into a single chord, high and hollow and breathy, with a lower rushing underneath where the air funnels down the slit of sky and hits the dish of the floor and turns around and goes back up.

What freezes you is that it does not change. Wind should gust. It should rise and fall, catch, stutter. This does not. It holds one exact volume, one exact pitch, as if the architecture has found a note it likes and refuses to let go. You hold your breath to test it. It stays. You clap once, expecting an echo — the slap is swallowed instantly, flattened into the chord without leaving a ripple. The dust in the air does not settle and does not swirl; it hangs, each mote lit gold-white, motionless in the loudness.

Life has almost given up here, but not quite. That is the established, settled feel of it: not ruin, just equilibrium.

On the north-facing side, where the light bounces least, each brick joint holds a crust of lichen, sage-grey and mustard-pale, flat as paint, dry enough to crumble if you touch it but holding on. In three or four of the lowest niches there are tufts of something wiry and salt-stiff, grey-green straw that grows an inch and then stops, as if it understood the terms. One tuft has gone to seed and the seeds sit in the dust on the lip, uncarried, because there is nothing to carry them. No ants. No birds. Nothing to eat them, nothing to disturb them. They will sit there.

The edges of everything are softened. The lips of the niches are rounded where hands and beaks wore them. The slabs are dished. The mortar has receded a finger's width back from the brick faces, leaving the wall with a quilted, weathered texture. Nothing is crumbling now. It crumbled long ago and then stopped, found its shape, and stayed. It will look exactly like this tomorrow and in ten years. The dust you brushed from the niche will be back in its place, or else it never left.

You realize there is no device here. No wire, no pipe, no bulb, no hinge, no nail, no glass. Only mud, brick, lime, stone — things shaped by hand and then left to weather. And yet nothing feels natural. Even the lichen grows in straight lines, following the joints. Even the dust tastes of plaster.

If you stand very still in the middle — and there is only the middle, there is no room to do anything else, the place is packed full of itself, wall and hole and light and sound with no gaps left over — you feel the rules assert themselves:

Stay low. Stay cool. Stay dry. Make no new sound; the sound is already made and it is full. Bring nothing living that needs more than dust and glare; it will not be fed.

Above you the slit of sky burns white and does not move. The chord holds. The empty houses wait, row on row, for occupants that will not come.
