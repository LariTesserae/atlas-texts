In the low bog, after rain, in the cold that comes before snow decides to stay.

You have to know where to lift. The ground gives under your knee — not mud, but a deep mattress of sphagnum, waterlogged and breathing. It is night. Not the picturesque kind of night. A low, cloud-sealed, black-peat night where your breath stays in front of you and does not leave. Your hands are numb before you find the edge of it: a slab of spruce bark, sunk and blackened, half-swallowed by moss. Underneath, a pocket of air that should not be there.

Lift it a finger's width. Put your eye to it. Wait.

At first it is only darkness with a smell — turpentine and iron and wet green, cold enough to ache. Then your eyes adjust to almost-nothing, and the almost-nothing has shape.

It is a room. No larger than your two cupped hands. A low dome held up not by soil but by living stems.

This is the trick the place plays on you: you thought you were looking down at moss. Now you are looking *in* at a forest.

The sphagnum down here does not mat. Down here it stands. Each head is a starburst palm of tiny leaves, swollen with water, pale green to amber to blood-red at the tips, rising like pillars out of black peat-tea water. Between them — vastness. Cold black air, empty enough to fall through. The vegetation is everything: floor, wall, ceiling, foundation. And yet it is sparse. A dozen great trunks in a cathedral emptiness, far apart, their crowns interlacing far above you to make a thatch that holds the snow-melt and the night out. You could shout between them and hear nothing come back but a drip.

Because it weeps. Everything weeps here.

Water is not in a stream or a pool. It is *near*. It beads along the underside of the bark above you and swells and lets go — *plik* — into the dark. It hangs in the moss leaves themselves, each leaf a tiny tank. It climbs up by capillary and comes down by gravity. It condenses out of the air you breathe; the air is so cold and so saturated that your own exhale, if you were small enough to exhale in here, would rain. The stems glisten with a skin of water. The peat below shines. In the crevices, fresh ice has started, young and unapologetically sharp — needle-ice pushing up out of the peat in glassy palisades, moss tips rimed white and still growing straight through the frost, unweathered, newly born.

And it is inhabited. Overwhelmingly.

At first you think the floor is moving with water. Then you see: it is backs. Thousands of them.

They are the size of rice grains, some smaller, some the size of your fingernail clipping — white springtails in a seething drift, amber mites like walking seeds, blind white beetle larvae pulsing through the litter, nematodes threading the water film. They cover the moss stems, they hang from silk threads, they boil in the hollows. Where your eye finds one, it finds a hundred behind it. The sheer mass of animal life in this thimble-room exceeds the plant by weight of motion, if not by weight of flesh. The air, if you could taste it at their scale, would taste of cut grass and chitin and alarm pheromone.

And among the boil — order.

This is not just infestation. This is a town.

Look along the flank of one great moss pillar: a causeway of chewed fiber, lashed between two stems with fungal cord. A ladder made from a pine needle, its rungs notched by jaws, leaning from peat up to a platform. Platforms — little balconies of compacted frass and leaf pulp and shed skins, pressed and dried in the cold air until they are papery and tough. Storehouses: translucent sacs packed with yellow pollen and black spores, stoppered with resin. Streets: not built but worn, glossy trails in the algal slime where thousands of feet have polished a path between pillar and pillar, diverging, meeting, diverging again. It is sparse as any winter hamlet — much empty dark between lights — but it is layered, added to, repaired. An old balcony slumps where the moss beneath it grew and shifted; a new one is being chewed out two leaves higher. Nothing here is built on dead ground, because there is no dead ground. The city walks as its foundations grow.

Their tools are simple. Hand — or jaw — tools. A splinter of bark used as a shovel. A mite femur used as a pick. Silk drawn from spinnerets and rolled between forelegs into rope. They cut, they lash, they daub with fresh moss pulp that then *keeps living* and seals the joint green. No metal. No fire — fire is impossible here, everything too wet, too cold. Only cutting and binding and piling, night after night.

It is mostly quiet. That is the law of the roof. The bark and the snow-thatch above smother all high sound. What remains is low and wet:

*plik* — a drip from bark to leaf.
*plik* — leaf to peat.
A soft, continuous rasping, like someone sanding wood very far away: ten thousand jaws on moss.
A faint *pop* when a springtail releases its tail-spring and vanishes.
Then quiet again, big and cold, with your own blood loud in your ears.

But quiet is not still. The place is violently active, just at a pace and a volume you have to learn.

Watch one street. A column of springtails moves pollen home, antennae flickering. Above them, on the shadow side of the pillar, a velvet mite — huge to them, to you the size of a poppy seed, rust-red and impossibly fast — freezes. Its front legs taste the air. Below, a false-scorpion backs out of a pulp tunnel, claws open. In the water film at the base, a flatworm glides, blind and white. Predator and prey, grazer and fungus, moss and chewer and chewed — all happening at once, in the dark, in the wet, without pause. While you watch, a needle of ice in the peat lengthens with an almost audible tick and topples a pollen sac; within minutes, a crew is there, chewing it upright, re-lashing.

This is the strange part, the part that keeps you with your face in the cold mud long after your fingers have gone numb:

In here, up and down are suggestions. Water climbs. The town moves. The dead do not leave — a dead springtail is butchered where it falls and its chitin is pressed into the wall of the nearest house, so the walls are made of ancestors, pale and translucent. Light is not for seeing — the faintest blue leak that finds its way around the bark edge, and the even fainter green rot-glow of foxfire on a buried twig, are felt on the skin more than seen, and too much of it would kill. Smell is sight. Vibration is speech. Stillness is death.

If you stay long enough, the cold gets into your cheek where it rests on the peat. Your breath fogs and drips back onto your lip. The stems above you shiver though there is no wind — something large passes overhead, a shrew perhaps, a god-footstep that compresses the whole dome for a second and releases it. No one below stops working. A drip gathers on the bark lip above the town, swells, holds the rot-glow for a moment like a lens, and falls.

*Plik.*

The town glistens, and goes on.
