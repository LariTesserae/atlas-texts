Down you go.

Not down a slope but down a break. The plateau up top is flat and empty to the horizon — pale gravel, wind-polished pebbles, heat-haze that never quite warms. Then the ground simply stops. A rim, crumbled and rounded as old teeth, and below it the Hollow.

It is not a canyon with a river in it. That river has been gone so long the stone has forgotten the shape of cutting. What is left is a long, broad trough sunk three hundred meters into the desert: two, three kilometers of floor, walled on both sides by cliffs that have slumped and shed and settled until every angle is soft. No sharp faces. Ledges have become ramps. Fallen blocks the size of houses have been worn down to hummocks, then to swells in dust. This is ground at the end of its life, after everything that could fall has fallen, after everything that could be rounded has been rounded. The floor itself is almost level — a pan of compacted caliche, bone-white and pink, dusted with salt flowers that crackle underfoot and never wash away because there is nothing to wash them.

There is no water here. Not a seep, not a pool, not a dark stain in the rock. The air is cool and completely dry. Your lips chap within an hour. Things do not rot in the Hollow; they desiccate. A fallen wing stays perfect for years, a husk. Dung turns to powder. Even your breath feels stolen the moment it leaves you — a brief white puff that vanishes.

And yet it is green. Or grey-green.

At first from the rim you think it is shadow. Then as your eyes adjust to the dim — for the Hollow is always in a kind of late dusk, the high walls cutting the sun to a brief grey noon and a long blue gloom — you see the lushness. It is a dry lushness. It crackles, it does not drip.

The floor between the bare salt pans is matted thick with stone-fleece: low, spreading lichens and wool-mosses that grow not in soil but on rock and dust itself, in cushions a foot thick, sage and ash and pale olive. They are soft to the eye and crisp to the touch, millions of tiny branched fronds that comb mineral dust out of the air. From the cliffs hang veils — long beards of paper-thin airwort, ten feet long, swaying almost imperceptibly, rootless, living on nothing but cold air and time. In the more sheltered hollows stand thickets of ribbonbush: tangled, head-high shrubs with no true leaves, only flat grey-green straps that rattle like paper. They are everywhere and nowhere dense. A great wool mat, then a hundred yards of bare white pan, then a copse of ribbonbush, then bare again. Sparse country. You can see a long way between things.

This vegetation does not drink. There is nothing to drink. Its sap, if you crush it, is not watery but oily and sharp-smelling — glycerin and salt. It does not wilt; it hardens. It grows with insulting patience, a millimeter a year, a centimeter in a good decade. Some of the mats you walk past are older than any human town, continuous living skins a thousand years unbroken.

And among them — the City.

You see it first as rock. Pale spires rising from the pan, scattered, then thickening toward the center of the trough. Cones and chimneys and blunt towers, from knee-high nubs to structures eight, nine meters tall, leaning slightly, fluted by wind. There are thousands of them. From the rim they look like a natural field of hoodoos. Down on the floor you understand they are not natural. Or not *geological*.

They are built.

No hands built them. No tools, no cut stone, no timber, no metal, no wire. Each tower is the work of millions of small chestnut-brown masons — blind, flightless, ant-like, but not ants — who chew the fleece-wool and ribbonbush litter, mix it with dust and their own saliva, and cement it grain by grain. A tower is a lung and a fortress: hollow with galleries, vented at the top with a flared lip, thick-walled against the cold. Around its base the ground is enriched with their midden — powdery frass and shed skins — and there the stone-fleece grows thickest and greenest, a dark collar, so that each tower wears a garden.

Together the towers are urban in every sense except ours. There are neighborhoods. To the north, where the wall gives a little more shade, the towers stand tall and close, joined by low curtain walls of the same cement, forming courts and cul-de-sacs. To the south they are smaller, widely spaced along straight avenues of trampled white dust — boulevards kept clear by endless traffic of mason columns, two lanes, out and back, day and night. In the center is something like a plaza: a flattened pan ringed by the five oldest towers, squat and enormously buttressed, their sides patched and re-patched until they look quilted. Layer upon layer. You can see where a wall collapsed a century ago and was built over without clearing. The City accretes. It does not plan; it persists.

Nothing here is fast except the living noise. The masons themselves are slow architects. A tower grows a finger's width in a season. The fleece creeps. The salt blooms.

But the fauna —

The Hollow teems. The masons are by biomass the earth of the place; under every mat their galleries run. Above them comes everything that eats them or eats what eats them or lives off their leavings. Low over the pan skitter legions of pale cave-crickets, long-antennaed, popping like dry seeds. Bark-lizards the color of dust flatten against tower sides, throat-pulsing. And above, always above, the birds.

They are the second builders. Cliff martins and ash swifts and a big, slow nightjar with moth-eyes. They come in clouds that darken the already dim light. The martins plaster their mud-cup nests — mud made not with water but with mason saliva stolen from fresh building, and with oily crop-secretion — in dense tenements under tower lips and in the honeycomb pits of the cliffs. The nightjars do not build; they roost in the ribbonbush thickets by the thousand, bodies packed like scales. And the thatchbirds — raucous, crow-sized, grey — weave huge communal roofs of ribbonbush strips over the tops of abandoned towers, shaggy caps that overhang like hats, with dozens of entrance holes underneath. From a distance those capped towers look like trees. The Hollow has trees that are houses that were chimneys.

It is deafening.

That is the first true shock after the cool and the dim. A dry, mineral, windless place should be silent. It is not. It is a bowl of sound. The shape of the trough traps it, the cool still air holds it, the dust in the air seems to carry it rather than damp it.

Close up it separates: the high endless metallic trill of crickets, layer upon layer until it becomes a solid sheet; the dry rustle-rattle of ribbonbush in wing-wash; the chitter and slap of thatchbirds quarreling; the soft, constant rain of shed chitin and seed-husks ticking on salt.

From a little distance it merges into a roar. At lift-off — which happens in pulses, for reasons of light and cold that only the swifts understand — ten, twenty thousand wings beat at once, and the beat echoes off the east wall and returns off the west a half-second later, so every wingbeat is doubled. The martins scream, a thin needle-sound in such volume it feels like pressure in your ears. The thatchbirds boom. The whole City hums through its vents as air is pumped by millions of mason fans deep inside — a low organ note you feel in your sternum more than hear.

Stand in the central plaza at the peak and you cannot hear your own hands clap. The sound has weight. Dust lifts off the towers with it, hangs in the dim light as a glittering haze. You shout and the Hollow shouts back, delayed, from three directions.

Then, slowly, the pulse passes. The cloud settles. The roar thins back to trill and chitter and the deep vent-hum. You realize your ears are ringing. Your boots are white with salt and frass. A mason column is crossing over your boot without deviation, each individual pausing to tap antennae to leather as if it were a pebble in a road laid down before you were born.

That is the rule here: everything is food for something else, and nothing leaves. No stream carries away. No rain flushes. What falls stays. Guano becomes mortar. Shed skin becomes soil. Dead swift becomes, within days, a dry mummy, then powder, then tower wall. The fleece filters the dust raised by wings; the masons eat the fleece litter; the crickets eat mason midden; the lizards eat crickets; the birds eat all of them and plaster the towers with their droppings, which feed the fleece. It is a closed wheel, turning very slowly in the cool dim, squealing very loudly as it turns.

Stay till the light fails — which is not night so much as a deepening of the perpetual dusk to indigo. The towers pale, then grey out. The beards on the cliffs stir though there is no wind you can feel, only the settling of cold air down the walls. The vent-hum rises as the masons work harder to keep the nurseries warm. The nightjars lift all at once without call, a soft thunder, and go up to hawk dust-moths over the rim where the desert still holds last light.

Below, the City glows faintly — not with light, but with warmth. If you lay your palm on a tower wall you feel it: a few degrees above the air, the breath of millions. In a place with no water and no sun to speak of, that is the hearth. Green mats, brown towers, white salt, grey wings, all huddled in the ancient flattened bowl, making their own weather out of breath and dust and noise.
