From the pass you hear it before you see it.

A low white roar, unbroken. Not a river — you know rivers, they argue with themselves over stones. This is flatter, wider, everywhere at once, like rain held still in the air.

Then the ground drops. Not far. You can see the whole of it at once, which is strange for mountains.

A bowl, maybe three hundred paces across, a perfect bitten-out hollow high up where the air is thin and cool enough to ache in the teeth. Behind you the ridge goes on, grey and jagged to the horizon. In front, only this one room cut into the world, with its rim all around like the lip of a cup.

The rock is wrong. That's the first thing your feet know.

Up on the pass the stone is old — wind-rounded, lichen-crusted, patient. Down here it is newborn. Blue-black basalt and pale ash-stone broken yesterday, in geological time. Edges still knife-sharp. Faces still fresh where they split, glittering with unwashed crystals. No gullies, no smoothed channels, no riverbed. The glacier only just let go of this place, or the hill only just shrugged it up. Water hasn't learned where to go yet, so it goes everywhere.

It weeps.

All around the inner rim, in a hundred places, clear water sheets over the lip and falls in thin glass curtains ten, fifteen meters down. Not waterfalls with names and plunge pools — more like the bowl is leaking. Between the falls the cliff sweats. You can see the dark wet tongues spreading down the fresh rock, beading, joining, dripping. On the floor there is no proper stream, just a skin of water moving over stone, ankle-deep in places, splitting and rejoining around slabs. Your boots are wet in the first minute. Everyone's boots are wet here. That's the rule.

And because the stone is new and the water is constant and the light is good — a high, bright, ordinary midday light, thin but not dim — green has gone mad for it.

Moss. Not in patches. In mattresses. In blankets a foot thick that give under your step like lungs. Lime-green star moss, black-green cushion moss, liverworts like wet leather. In every crack, ferns uncurling tight as fists. Dwarf willow no higher than your knee but a hundred years old, its trunks twisted as rope. Woolly alpine flowers — white edelweiss-cousins, purple saxifrage — jammed into any dust of crushed stone, blooming furiously and close to the ground to keep out of the wind. It is lush beyond reason for this height, this cold. The cold is real: your breath smokes faintly even at noon, your fingers go pink. But the wet holds the day's sun and the moss holds the wet, and everything grows into everything else.

What stops you, though, is that this impossible garden has streets.

The floor of the bowl is terraced, wall upon wall of dry stone. Not huts scattered. A town. A small, dense, deliberate town built to fit exactly inside the hollow and no further.

Three levels of retaining walls, each as tall as two men, built from the same sharp newborn slabs, fitted without mortar by hands that knew the weight. Flat roofs of turf and split pine that continue the moss right over the houses so from above you'd almost miss them. Narrow lanes paved with standing slates to keep feet out of the sheet-water. Stone drains under every lane, covered with bark, gurgling loudly. Little slab bridges where the water braids deeper. Cisterns. A communal threshing platform. A palisade of lashed poles at the one break in the rim where the water finally finds an exit and pours out to the valley a thousand meters below.

Primitive work. You can see the adze marks on the pine posts, the hammer-stones left on a half-built wall, the copper pins green in the doorframes, the hand-spun wool hung to dry and already damp again. No sawn planks, no iron, no glass. Hide scrapers. Bone awls. Baskets. Everything made within sight of where it stands.

And almost no one in it.

That is the hollowness of the place. For all its greenness, for all its building, it is empty per volume. The sky above the bowl is enormous. The air between the walls is enormous. Sound travels too well. A child shouting on the far terrace rings right in your ear. A raven crossing — one of the very few animals — seems to fly for a long time through nothing.

Fauna is rumor here. A pika calls once from the rocks and then thinks better of it. A pair of mountain crows ride the updraft off the falls, croaking, the only voices louder than water. Clouds of tiny midges over the standing puddles, and a pale moth that lives in the moss. On the turf roofs, someone keeps four shaggy highland sheep, stone-grey, almost invisible until they move. Their bells are the only tame sound, dull and intermittent under the roar. No dogs barking. No chickens. Too high, too wet, too new for most lives to have found it yet.

The people — there are people, perhaps thirty where there is room for three hundred — move slowly and do not shout over the water. They have learned the bowl's acoustics. They stamp, they knock stones together. A woman mending a drain cover sees you and lifts her hammer twice against slate: *tock tock*, and the note hangs, amplified by the curve of the cliffs, and two other heads appear on other terraces. That is how they talk at distance. The whole town is a drum.

If you stand still in the middle lane, with water hissing off every eave and the veil-falls drumming the moss, the loudness resolves. It doesn't change. That's the strangest part. A loud place should be a fast place — rapids, storm, collapse. Here the loud is patient. The same chord, hour after hour: high white hiss of falling water, mid-tone rush in the under-drains, low hum when wind catches the rim and the bowl resonates like a cup you run a finger around. Drip, seep, trickle, added like needle-pricks. You can hear your own heart under it if you listen. Nothing is eroding fast enough to hear. The stone is still deciding what shape it will be.

The folk here live by unfinished rules, because the land is unfinished.

First: never dam. You can channel, you can lift on slate, you can bridge, but you may not stop the sheet. The one time someone tried to pond water for retting flax, the seep found another way overnight and lifted the whole wall sideways. Water here hasn't chosen a course and resents being told.

Second: cut no living moss for fuel. Only deadfall pine brought up from below, and peat cut sparingly from the thickest mattress where it will regrow. The moss is what holds the sharp stones down, what makes soil where there is no soil yet. Peel it and the fresh rock underneath is barren flour that washes away in a day.

Third: when you take stone, take it clean from the rim-fall, where frost has already split it, and dress it where it fell. Do not round it. The elders say the bowl will round itself in ten thousand years; until then, sharp stones lock better. You can see it in the walls — every block still angular, teeth interlocked, no river-smooth boulders at all because no river has existed long enough to make one.

There is a slight wrongness if you stay past the first hour, a tilt you can't name. Maybe it's that condensation never stops. Even when it isn't raining — and it rains most afternoons, briefly, coldly — the cool air wrings itself out on the cold new stone. Your jacket beads. The pine posts bead. The sheep's wool beads. The town glistens as if breathed upon. Or maybe it's that some slabs ring when you step on them, a faint metallic tone, because the lava underneath is still full of bubbles, still porous, still almost breathing. The children know which paving stones sing and hop from one to another when the adults aren't looking, making a slow, tuneless scale that the bowl carries.

By evening — though light lingers long and white at this height — mist rises not from the valley but from the bowl itself, exhaled off the moss as the air cools another notch. The veil-falls thicken, blur. The terraces soften into green steps. The few lamps — tallow in stone cups, sheltered in niches — come on like fireflies, absurdly small in all that wet stone and space. The roar seems louder in the fog, closer.

You understand then why they built here, in a raw cup too small for a map, too high for grain, too loud for sleep at first. Because it is safe and wet and green when all around is old grey wind-scoured ridge. Because the stone was waiting to be picked up, already broken. Because the water, for all its noise, is clean and cannot be poisoned upstream — there is no upstream, only sky and rim.

It is a city that hasn't decided if it is a garden that hasn't decided if it is a rock. Everything is just coming into being, slowly, loudly, under bright cold light, and the people in the middle are just helping it, stone by stone, moss by moss, keeping the water moving while the world learns its shape.
