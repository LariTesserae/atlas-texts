It is high here, which is a strange thing to say about a place that is drowned.

Far below, fathoms below, is the true bottom of the world — black abyssal plain. What you stand on — if you could stand — is the flat top of a drowned mountain. A mesa in the sea. On three sides the ground simply ends and falls away in cliffs that go down beyond dimness. So the light is thin but not gone, and the water above you is still a mile thick. You are on a height, in the cold middle-dark, with an ocean for a sky.

The water is everything. Not a lake in a land, not a sea with a shore. There is no shore anywhere in this valley-sized open. There is only water in all directions: cold, green-grey, pressing. It moves. From far above, a faint bruised daylight filters down in columns that waver and break. It never gets brighter than late dusk. Particles fall through it forever — a slow snow of dead things from the sunlit world — marine snow, the only weather.

And there is no green. Nothing leafs, nothing roots.

This is a mineral world. Bone-white, slate-grey, iron-black. The ground under the current is not ground at all.

Everything here is made.

At first you think the plateau is natural — rounded ridges, low terraces stepping toward the cliff edge, domes and pits and long low walls like contour lines. But the longer you look, the more wrong that becomes. Every stone is placed. Every shell fragment is cemented. The terraces are too even. The arches are too deliberate. The whole top of the mountain, for as far as the dim lets you see — a town's worth, a small valley's worth of it — has been paved and stacked and hollowed.

And there was never a tool. No metal. No light but the failing sun. No machine hum, no cut edge, no right angle that a saw would make. This is building without technology. This is building the way a wasp builds or a reef builds: with mouths and bodies, grain by grain.

The material is bodies. Crushed shell and cold-water coral skeleton and secreted calcite, basalt rubble carried one pebble at a time, fish bone and crab carapace ground to mortar by mandibles and set in the cold to harden. The old work is eroded past all sharpness. This place is ancient — not ruined, settled. Edges have gone soft as wax. Tunnels have sagged into troughs. Towers lean. The builders round everything eventually, the current rounds it further, until a wall looks like a dune that happens to have doorways in it.

It is sparse. That is the first loneliness of it. For all that building, presences are far apart. A perforated tower stands alone for a hundred meters of empty pavement. Then another cluster: three domes with breathing holes, a low labyrinth wall no higher than your knee. Then nothing for a long while except swept pavement and drifting snow. The scale is open. You can see multi-features fading into murk — a field of chimneys to the north, a collapsed amphitheater to the east, a long causeway running straight to the cliff lip and stopping, as if it meant to go on.

But it is not empty. It swarms.

The fauna own this. Without plants, they live on the fall and on each other. Pale things, mostly — white, translucent, blind or huge-eyed. Clouds of krill-like flickers. Herds of slow spider crabs the size of dogs, each carrying a stone in its claws, endlessly repairing. Feather-crowned worms that bloom from every hole in the walls, red and cream, withdrawing in ripples. Long eel-shapes patrol the terraces. Siphonophores drift over like torn veils, twenty feet long, trailing fire.

And they never stop.

This is the rule that unmakes quiet: everything is in turbulent motion, all the time, and almost silently.

A tower you watched for an hour suddenly slumps — undermined by diggers — and goes down in a slow, billowing avalanche. Before the silt clears, the crabs are already on it, pulling blocks out, carrying them away to start a new spire fifty meters off. A school of silver fish a thousand strong wheels over the pavement, turns inside-out around a current shear, and vanishes into the mouths of the domes. The worms bloom, vanish, bloom. Octopod things wrestle over a causeway block, tumble off, ink black into black. Upwellings come up the cliff face and burst over the lip in cold rivers, lifting clouds of builders and grit and babies and setting them down elsewhere.

It's anthill-energy. Bazaar-energy. Creation and destruction as weather. Yet sound is swallowed. Water takes it. What reaches you is mostly quiet: the vast, low pressure-hush of deep water in your bones, a creak of shifting rubble transmitted through stone, a faint high clicking like distant teeth — the crabs talking — a soft *whump* when a chimney collapses out of sight. No voices. No engines. The chaos makes almost no noise.

The cold gets into everything. 2 degrees above freezing, the kind of cold that makes movement slow and deliberate except in bursts. It preserves. Old pheromone trails linger on stone for years. Fallen walls don't rot, they are simply taken apart.

There are rules here, though no one wrote them:

1. **Everything has been touched.** There is no natural substrate left on the plateau. If you lift a pebble, its underside shows the smooth of a mouth. The cliffs themselves, where the built pavement ends, are bored full of homes to the depth of an arm.

2. **Nothing is allowed to finish.** The current, the cold chemistry that dissolves unmaintained mortar, and the neighbors tear down whatever stops being maintained. Stability is not a state, it is a maintenance. The most ancient, deeply-aged forms survive only because they are constantly rebuilt into the same soft shape.

3. **Build with your body.** No detached tool has ever existed here. Cement is spat. Stone is carried. Tunnels are chewed. Even the largest causeway — blocks as big as coffins — was nudged into place over generations by things smaller than your hand, working in relays.

If you stay long enough for your eyes to adjust to the dim, you start to see the unusualness — the slight tilt of the world. The snow never lands where you expect. Currents spiral up off the pavement in slow standing vortices that hold schools and larvae suspended for days, turning. The light from above pulses, not with clouds, but with the passage of something huge and unseen migrating over the mountain, dimming the dusk for minutes at a time. And the builders sometimes build things with no use at all: a perfect ring of white pebbles alone on a terrace, a slender needle six feet high that the next surge will snap. Art, or misfire.

At the edge, the causeway ends at the cliff. Beyond it, water and falling snow and nothing. Below, darkness that has never been built in. The crabs go to the lip, look over with eyestalks, and turn back to their endless repair.
