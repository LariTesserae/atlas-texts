You hear it before you see it.

A low, wet throb coming up out of the ground in the middle of dry scrub — boiler-breath and insect-choir and water hitting hot iron. Then the ground simply ends, and the town is below you.

Not in a valley. In a hole. A near-perfect bowl, hamlet-sized, maybe three hundred paces across rim to rim, eighty meters down. You can stand on the lip and shout and someone on the far terraces will shout back. The sky is a small, bright lid far above. Down here the light never fully arrives.

Geologists would call it a collapse sink. The people who live in it call it the Sump. A long time ago — long enough that no corner is sharp anymore — the limestone roof of a cavern gave way, or a quarry cut too deep and the forest took it back. What’s left has weathered into balance: a settled form, softened, round-shouldered. Stone steps have sagged into roots. Iron railings have grown bark over their rust. Nothing is new, nothing is ruined. Everything is worn to fit.

Because the Sump isn’t built *in* vegetation. It’s built *out of* it.

Three, maybe four, vast root-trees — banyan-things with trunks thicker than houses — hold the entire bowl in their grip. Their roots are the terraces. As thick as streets, flattened on top by a century of feet, they spiral down in wide, uneven rings. Their leaves knit together eighty feet overhead in a second, broken canopy that turns noon into green dusk. Between the big roots, smaller vines, mosses, shelf fungi, air-plants and stranglers have filled every crack until you can’t tell where timber ends and living wood begins.

The town itself is sparse. That’s the first strange thing on the way down. For a place so loud, so crowded with life, there is so much empty air.

Only about two dozen structures, and they are set far apart, each on its own root-ledge with a drop of black empty space between. A boiler-house here. A press-mill there. A row of two-story lodgings stitched to the wall like wasps’ nests. A chapel with its bell gone to seed. They are connected not by streets but by infrastructure: catwalks of chain and plank, copper cables sagging heavy with condensation, iron pipes sweating, rope bridges that bounce when the swarms pass. Layered construction — brick under, wood over, living vine over that, and then sheet iron patched on top of the vine to keep the drip out. Every building has a lamp burning outside it, amber and buzzing, because without them you would lose the path in the gloom.

It is an industrial town, unmistakably. Not ruins. Working.

Down on the third terrace the seed-press never stops. A squat factory of bark-brick and riveted iron, its chimney exhaling warm, sweet smoke that gets trapped under the leaf roof and sinks back down. Inside, flywheels the size of cartwheels turn, driven by steam and by something else — by the slow hydraulic squeeze of the root itself, tapped and valved like an artery. They press oil from fallen canopy-nuts, they pulp leaf for paper, they distill the fermented drip that collects in the cisterns. Transformers hum on poles grown straight out of living trunks. Wires have been swallowed and then re-exposed, copper veins shining through split bark. The vines have learned to follow the current. At night certain tendrils glow faintly green along their length where they’ve stolen charge.

Warmth pools here. The pit traps the day’s heat and the compost heat and the boiler heat and holds it. It is always warm, shirt-sticking warm, breath-warm. Water doesn’t flow through so much as hang in the air. There’s no river in the Sump. There’s damp. The walls weep. Condensation beads on every pipe and leaf and wire and falls in a constant, arrhythmic patter — drip-hour is every hour. Cisterns, old bathtubs, pitcher-plant vats as big as barrels catch it. Once or twice a day, when rain passes over the dry land above, a white waterfall pours over one section of rim for ten minutes, thundering down the terraces, turning stair-roots to streams, and then stops. The town steams after.

And the animals. Gods, the animals.

For a place with so few houses, it is overwhelmingly, impossibly full. The emptiness between buildings isn’t empty at all — it’s traffic.

At any moment the central air is a living weather system. Clouds of moths big as hands orbit the work-lamps, shedding scales like snow. Swifts and swallows and bats — thousands — cut through them in a shifting predator-prey braid that never resolves, only changes shift: birds by dim day, bats by dimmer night. Below, on the terraces, coatis and soot-black monkeys pick through oil-cake behind the mill. Rats run the pipes like commuters. Beetles, glossy and fist-sized, cluster on the warm boiler walls to roost and are shoveled off by the stokers every morning still humming. Ants in a river ten ants wide carry leaf-bits and paint-chips and dead moths up one root and down another. In the cisterns, blind white fish and mosquito larvae boil. Everything eats everything else, quickly. Nothing rots in the Sump. Leave a peel on a step and it’s gone in twenty minutes, carried off in pieces.

That’s why it’s so loud.

The sound stacks. Low: the press-mill thump, the boiler sigh every nine seconds, the transformer drone. Mid: the drip, everywhere, ten thousand ticks on iron and leaf and canvas. High: the insect layer — cicadas in the canopy firing in waves that roll around the bowl, crickets in the walls, the tearing-paper chitter of bats. And through it all, voices shouting over it, metal clanging, a monkey troop bickering on a cable, a dog barking at the waterfall that isn’t there anymore. You have to lean in to talk. The Sump doesn’t quiet, even at 3 a.m.; it just changes key when the day swarm beds down and the night swarm lifts.

The strangeness isn’t one big thing. It’s the accumulation of small wrongnesses until the world tilts.

Like how the buildings breathe with the roots when the boilers vent — you can see the wall of the lodging house flex a half-inch in and out. Like how the pigeons here have learned to imitate the steam whistle, perfectly, and will set the whole mill crew running for a false venting. Like how certain figs only ripen in the hot shadow behind the chimney, and the bats that eat them smell faintly of machine oil, and their guano, shoveled from the piston housings where they roost for warmth, burns blue in the furnace. Plant and engine and animal have been pressed together so long in this small, dim, wet heat that they’ve started to use each other as organs.

The rules of the Sump emerge from that:

Stay on the lit walk after a rim-rain. The roots go slick and the gaps between lights are true dark.

Don’t plug your ears. You navigate by sound here — the mill means center, the drip-wall means west, the cicada swell means the canopy is about to shed water.

Don’t leave food, don’t leave waste, don’t leave the dead alone. The town has a cleanup crew of six legs and wings and it works faster than any burial.

And don’t think you’re outside nature because there are bricks and boilers. In the Sump the factory is just another kind of tree — hot, loud, fruiting oil and paper — and the forest lets it run because it feeds the swarm.

Stand on the lowest terrace at the bottom, where the oldest root has cupped into a black pool of drip-water and oil-sheen, and look up. Terraces of light recede above you, lamp after lamp spiraling up through green gloom, bridges swaying as a troop crosses, a slow snow of wings and scale and leaf-trash turning in the chimney heat. The rim is a bright, distant ring. Swallows pour over it like water over a dam and scatter, screaming, into the bowl.

Small enough to hold in your head. Too full to ever fully see.
