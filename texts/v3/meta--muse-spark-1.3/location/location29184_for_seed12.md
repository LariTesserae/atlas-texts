You come up and you go down at the same time. That is the first rule here.

Because this is high country — you can feel it in your inner ear when the trail switchbacks up out of the white abyss — and it is also underwater.

Not near water. In it. Held in it.

The caldera sits on the roof of the world, a broad bitten bowl three or four valleys wide, and it is full to the lip with warm rain that never found a way down. Below the rim, on the outside, the mountain falls away for thousands of meters into cloud. Above the rim, on the inside, there is only more water: lake, and steam, and hanging rain. You climb for two days through cold thinning air to arrive, dripping, swimming.

The water is the place. It is bath-warm, blood-warm, it does not shock. It is tea-dark and green-dark, thick with suspended life. Standing on the outer scree you see it first as a flat dull mirror that does not reflect sky because there is no sky, only a low lid of vapor glowing faintly amber-green. Wading in, the clarity goes after three paces. After ten paces your feet disappear. After twenty you are not wading anymore, you are chest-deep and buoyant in a way that feels wrong, held up too easily, as if the water were slightly denser than water should be, slightly reluctant to let you go.

It is a young lake on young rock. That is the second rule: nothing here is worn.

The volcano that made this bowl has not finished becoming a mountain. The basalt is embryonic — pillow lobes still rounded and glass-skinned where they extruded under the lake, aa flows on the rim like frozen slag, edges so sharp they cut bare soles. There is almost no sand. No soil except what people have made. No rounded river stones, no beaches, no valleys carved. Water has not had time to teach the rock manners. If you dive down in the clearest shaft you can see the bottom still growing: black lobes ticking as they cool, hairline cracks sighing out heat, mineral beards of white and rust trembling in the upwelling. The whole floor is faintly warm to the palm, warmer in patches where you must not stand long.

Light never gets strong here. The sun is a rumor behind steam. What comes down is filtered through kilometers of wet air and then through meters of warm lake, so by the time it touches you it is dim, diffuse, green-gold. Midday looks like evening elsewhere. Shadows have no edges. In the shallows the light stands in slow pillars full of drifting motes. In the deeps it goes the color of deep bottle glass and then to nothing. People navigate more by touch and by remembering than by seeing. At night — though night is only a slightly deeper dim — the lake keeps a faint glow of its own where the algae bloom, a smeared phosphorescence that clings to oars and ankles.

On this raw geology, impossibly lush, impossibly dense, the town grows.

It is not built around the water. It is built inside it, layer on layer. From the rim you look out and think at first it is just vegetation — great rafts of lily and ribbon-weed, mossy hummocks — until you see the geometry under the green. Causeways of hand-piled black stone run just awash, zigzagging from shore to center. Between them, platforms lashed from thermal poles and woven reed, stacked two and three high, roofed with thatch that is always damp and always sprouting. Houses half-drowned by design: stone footings below the waterline for coolness, wattle walls at the waterline, sleeping lofts just above where the steam condenses and drips all day. Rope bridges, knotted vine bridges, swaying log bridges. Canals that are streets. Gardens that are also foundations.

It is urban in the way a reef is urban. Town-size, valley-size, you can cross it in an hour if you know the fords, half a day if you don't. Several thousand people live here cheek by jowl with each other and with plants, and everywhere is near-touching. A child can climb from a sleeping mat onto a roof garden onto a neighbor's raft of gourds without ever touching open water. Below, in the water column itself, more levels: hanging lines of mussel-shell and bone where edible weeds are trained to grow down, fish-traps of bent sapling, underwater fences of stakes driven into still-soft lava.

The technology is all hand-work. No metal to speak of except a few treasured copper blades traded up from below and sharpened to slivers. Otherwise: basalt adzes, obsidian flakes, fire-hardened stakes, shell scrapers, cordage rolled on the thigh from lake-hemp, baskets, nets, clay. Fire lives only in a few places — on the high eastern rim where the rock stays dry enough for stone kilns, and on clay hearth-platforms tended like shrines, covered against the eternal drip. Most food is eaten raw, or warmed, or fermented in the lake heat. Boats are not boats but coracles and bundled reed floats; everyone swims before they walk well. You hear women pounding fiber with grooved stones. You see men, waist-deep in a line, passing up pillow-stones hand to hand to extend a causeway by a meter a year.

And it is green, overwhelmingly green, for a place with so little light. The warmth compensates. Ribbon-weed forests rise from the bottom in the shallows, twenty feet tall, olive and emerald, their fronds laying flat on the surface to make living walkways that tremble under your weight. Giant lilies with leaves as wide as shields cluster around the platforms, their undersides spined, their flowers white and waxy and short-lived. On every thatch, on every dead pole, mosses, ferns, orchids — the thermal ferns especially, huge and dusty-green, unfurling in the steam where the water is hottest. Hanging gardens drip. Roots dangle directly into the lake and drink. Rot and growth happen simultaneously; a fallen house-post sprouts before it collapses.

Animals are strangely absent for all this green. That is the quiet strangeness you notice after a day: no birds calling, no insects clouding, no herds. The air above the lake is too wet and too thin, the rock too new to have invited much up. In the water there are glass eels, almost transparent, that slide between the stakes. Blind white newts with feathery gills clustered around the warm vents. A kind of fat snail that the people farm on the hanging lines. Once in a while a deep turtle surfaces, huge and algae-backed, older than the town. And humans, of course — the densest animal here by far. Otherwise the place feels held in reserve, as if fauna has not yet been told about it.

So it is mostly quiet. Water muffles. The steam hisses very faintly where it leaves the vents. Drips fall from leaves to lake with a soft, endless plinking. Poles creak against rope. Someone laughs far off across the water and it arrives late and flattened. Underwater, if you duck your head, you hear the rock itself: a very slow ticking, cracking, the lake breathing in a long seiche that takes an hour to go from one side of the caldera to the other and back. Everything here happens slowly. Currents take a morning to carry a flower from one causeway to the next. The water level rises a fingernail width a year as the vents exhale and the rain has nowhere to go, so every generation raises its floors a course of stone higher and abandons the lowest rooms to eels and lilies. You can see them below when you swim: old doorways, drowned hearths, intact, green-furred, occupied only by newts.

There are other strangenesses that you stop questioning because the lake enforces them.

Things do not sink properly. Drop a stone adze and it falls too slowly, as if considering. The dead, weighted with stones, often return after three days, buoyed up, and must be housed in the wall-reefs instead — set into the causeways with lime and shell, becoming structure. That is why the causeways are pale in places, knuckled with bone.

And orientation loosens. Because the horizon is only steam and the bottom is only gloom, up-mountain and down-water replace north and south. People say *upsun* for the rim kilns and *downwarm* for the vents. Children learn to hold their breath uncannily long, to move through the green gloom with eyes open, gathering. Sleep is shallow and communal, on lofts where everyone can hear the water lapping at the posts below — the sound that tells you the lake is still there, still rising, still warm, still holding you up on the roof of the world.

Stay long enough and your skin softens, your foot soles toughen on new glass rock, your ears learn the slow tick of geology under construction. You stop expecting distance to look far. Everything past ten paces is dim anyway. The town extends past the immediate not by sight but by memory: more platforms, more gardens, more green-warm water, out to the far rim where the steam stands thickest and the world drops off into white.

High and drowned. Young and crowded. Warm, dim, patient.

A city learning how to float before the mountain has learned how to be stone.
