You come to it from above, because there is no other way to come to it.

The plain runs flat and pale to the horizon — dry grass, cracked dust — and then, without warning, it stops. The ground simply gives out. A rim, sharp as a broken plate.

Below you is the bowl.

It is wide. Not a pit you can shout across, but a valley sunk whole beneath the world. A town could fit down there and be lost in green. The far side is a blue-green wall a mile or two off, shimmering in heat. The sky above it is a hard, white noon that doesn't seem to belong to the hour. It just hangs there, bright.

You go down because the rim won't let you do anything else.

The first part of the descent is rock and nothing but rock. New rock. That is the strangest thing on first touch: it hasn't learned to be landscape yet. The limestone and basalt blocks where the roof of the world caved in are still angular, fresh-fractured, pink-white and ash-grey where they split. No soil on them. No smoothed gullies, no carved channels, no sanded hollows. Edges that will cut your hands. Slabs leaning against each other at impossible angles, dust still pale and unsorted in the cracks. This place is young, geologically speaking — just born, just opened — and then stopped.

Because underneath, holding every block in place, is root.

Within twenty feet of the rim the green starts, and then it does not stop. It does not decorate the rock. It *is* the ground.

By the time you are halfway down the slope you are no longer walking on earth. You are walking on plant. A mat of interwoven roots as thick as your thigh, creepers layered over creepers, leaves over leaves over leaves. Fern terraces step down in dense green stairs. Vines as thick as a man's body drop straight from the rim and dive into the mass and come out again lower down. Strangler figs have taken the largest fallen boulders in full embrace and entirely hidden them; you only know a boulder is there because the roots bulge in a square-shouldered shape.

It is hot down here. Trapped hot. The sun pours straight into the bowl and the walls give it back. The air gets heavier with every step, sweet and green-rotted and mineral, until breathing feels like drinking warm water. Sweat starts at your temples and does not dry. It beads and stays. Light comes down in solid columns, white at the top where it clears the rim, then filtered to a luminous, translucent green as it passes through three, four, five canopies. Every leaf-edge glows.

And yet, for all that density — the leaves touching leaves, the stems with no space between them, orchids growing on ferns growing on moss growing on bark growing on root — there is water only in hints. This is not a swamp. There is no river down here. The larger dryness owns the place.

Water lives in pockets. In the cup of a giant bromeliad, black tea-colored and wriggling with larvae. In a hollow root elbow where condensation has gathered overnight and never quite evaporated. In shallow pans of stone on the valley floor where the leaf-drip collects: *plink... plink...* A few inches deep, warm as blood, filmed with pollen. If you put your hand under a low leaf, the underside is always beaded. The whole bowl transpires in the heat, breathes out, and the breath condenses on itself and falls back as a slow, perpetual drizzle from leaf tip to leaf tip. Damp, not wet. Enough that your boots are always dark, your shirt always sticks, the rock under the roots always sweats. Not enough to flow anywhere.

The floor, when you reach it, opens out wider than you expected. A rolling, uneven field of vegetation three or four canopies deep, stretching in all directions to the walls. You can walk for hours and still be inside it. Different districts emerge if you pay attention: to the north, a slope of tree-ferns thirty feet tall, their trunks furry and black, their crowns meeting to make a second sky. In the center, a maze of hollow domes where the figs have grown together. To the south, where the wall is sheerest and rawest, the rock is still mostly bare and covered instead in a skin of moss and tiny white orchids that root directly in the fracture lines.

There are animals here, but you will have to be lucky or patient to believe it. Life is almost entirely plant. Once, a skink like a dropped jewel darts across a sunlit root and is gone. High above, something — a swift, a swallow — cuts across the circle of sky and does not descend. At night, if night ever fully came, there would be insects. In the heat of the held noon you hear only a single cicada working its slow rattle from somewhere deep in the fern crowns, stopping, starting again. The green muffles everything. Footfalls land soft. Voices don't carry ten feet.

What you hear instead is the place itself, quietly: the tick and creak of vines tightening in heat, the fat drop of condensed water falling from leaf to leaf to pool, the papery rustle when a dead leaf — rare — lets go and is caught three inches down by another leaf and held.

And scattered through the center, half-sunk, are the structures.

There are four or five of them. Low domes, man-high, made of nothing but living vine bent and re-bent and fused. No cut ends. No rope, no tool marks, no metal, no cloth. Inside one, the floor is a living mat of moss, dry in the middle — the only truly dry spot in the bowl. In another, a cairn of fresh rock fragments has been piled, lichen already taking the undersides. Who made them is not answerable. They are not new and not old. They have no age because nothing here has age. They look grown as much as built, as if the bowl tried out the idea of shelter and then forgot to finish it.

That is the rule of the bowl: it does not change.

This should be a violent, fast place — a new collapse, raw rock in a hot climate with water and total plant cover should mean landslide, rot, explosive growth, succession, erosion carving gullies in a season. None of that happens.

The blocks do not shift. The roots hold every grain of pale dust in place so no soil washes. Leaves do not yellow in drifts; a leaf that dies is absorbed where it lies, melted into the mat by white fungal threads within hours. No seedlings struggle upward; there is no room, no need. The mature plants simply persist, fused at the contact points where branch rubs branch — bark grown into bark, fern trunk into fig root, making a single, valley-wide organism that has decided to stay exactly as it is.

Even the light stays. Shadows are short and do not lengthen. The sun holds at bright noon above the rim, pouring heat straight down, evaporating the pocket pools just enough to bead them again on the leaf undersides. Drip, gather, fall, rise. Drip, gather, fall, rise. The air shimmers over the fern crowns and never clears.

It is unusual, but only slightly — the way a held breath is unusual. Nothing is monstrous. Leaves are still leaves, rock is still rock, water is still water. It's the arrangement that feels wrong: too much life in too new a wound, too much stillness in too much heat, a whole valley garden sunk below a dry plain and refusing to either die back or break out.

If you lie down in one of the vine domes at midday and look up, you see what the bowl sees: layer after layer of green translucence stacked to white sky, beaded with water that never falls quite on you, crossed here and there by a raw, unweathered edge of stone that the green has politely gone around. Hot air presses on your chest. A drop lands on a leaf near your ear with a sound like a small bell. Far off, the cicada winds up again.

Nothing proceeds.
