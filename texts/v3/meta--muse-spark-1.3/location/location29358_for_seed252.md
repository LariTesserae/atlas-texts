You have to go down to find it, but not far.

Less than a body-length under the pan, where the sun-baked crust rings hollow if you stamp it. There is a crack, no wider than your shoulders, where last season's steam pried the ground apart. You let yourself down feet-first, hands braced on rock still warm from the morning, and you drop — only a meter, two — into a room.

That is the whole of it. A room. If you stand crouched in the middle with your arms half-out you can touch both walls. A pocket, an air bubble left in basalt and then cracked open at the top. Above you, a ragged oval of sky, white with noon. Nothing built, nothing carried in, nothing cut. The edges are too sharp for that.

The rock is young. You can tell by feel before your eyes adjust. The fracture faces haven't had time to dull. Black basalt glass along the lower walls, conchoidal and keen enough to nick skin, still showing the rope and swirl of when it flowed. Higher up, where the heat and water have worked fastest, it is already veiled in white and pale yellow — silica sinter, gypsum, a dusting of sulfur — but the veil is thin, freshly precipitated, crackling under a fingernail. In the corners, gypsum flowers are just starting, tiny sharp needles pushing out. Nothing rounded. Nothing old.

And it is bright. Unreasonably bright for being underground.

The shaft of sun comes straight down through the crack around midday and hits a shallow pan of water on the floor and explodes. The walls, pale with silica, throw it back and forth until there is nowhere for shadow to hide. You squint. The black glass, where it is still bare, throws specular glints like broken bottles. The water throws a second, trembling sun onto the ceiling. Even with the sky reduced to a coin, the room glows as if lit from inside. Dust, if there were any, would show — but there isn't any. The air is washed.

It is hot. That is the first rule here.

Not just the desert hot from above, but hot from below and from the sides. The rock under your palms is blood-warm, warmer where it is wet. The air is 40, 45 degrees, saturated to the point of thickness. You sweat instantly and the sweat doesn't dry. Every breath is warm soup. The heat holds the room closed like a hand.

The second rule is that water stays.

There is no plant in here. Not a rootlet, not a seedling, not even the dry thorn-scrub that manages everywhere else. Just mineral. Black, white, yellow, rust. And water in all its in-between states.

The floor is mostly a sheet of warm water, ankle-deep at most, often only a film, lying over cracked plates. The walls weep. You can see it if you stay still: beads forming on the silica, swelling, joining, running down in slow threads to feed the pan. From the ceiling, where the basalt overhangs sharpest, drops gather and fall — *plik* — at long intervals. The atmosphere itself condenses. Your skin, your shirt, the backs of your hands come away damp without having touched anything. When the sun shaft moves a fraction, a faint veil of steam lifts off the pan and climbs, only to condense again higher up where the rock is a degree cooler. Evaporation, transport, rain, all inside a room you could cross in two steps.

It never stops. That is the motion here. Not wind, not river, but a small, fierce cycle turning over and over. Water climbing up through the rock by capillary pull, bleeding out, evaporating, condensing, falling, spreading, precipitating a new skin of mineral as it goes. You can hear the mineral tick sometimes as it cools and tightens — a faint *tick tick* — and once in a while a plate on the floor shifts with a soft crunch as new crust pushes underneath. The room is making itself, still.

If you look close — and the brightness forces you to look close, at the wet edge where the light diffuses — you see why it works so well.

The white sinter is not just crust. Under a hand lens it would be forest: countless silica tubes, opaline spheres stacked like marbles, zeolite cages with pores the exact width of a water molecule plus nothing else. Water climbs up through them against heat that should drive it down. Light goes into the clear calcite veins in the wall and comes out two feet away around a corner, with almost no loss, as if the rock were fiber. The film on the pan filters itself — salts left behind in perfect rings, silica dropped out pure. No pump, no membrane made in a factory, no lens ground in a lab. Just fresh rock and hot water doing, casually, what our clean-rooms struggle to do: moving photons and molecules with nanoscale precision, distilling and lighting and air-conditioning this one small room without a single moving part.

There is no machine here. That is the point. Nothing was constructed. It just happens to work like one, and better. Our physics can name the pieces — capillarity, Mie scattering, photonic bandgap — but not quite why, together, they are this efficient, this clean. Sit long enough and you stop trying to separate the physics from the place.

It is mostly quiet.

After the crunch of your own entry, after your breathing settles, the room goes back to its baseline: the faint hiss of evaporation you feel more in the ears than hear, the spaced drip from the ceiling, the occasional tick of cooling stone. If you hold your breath you can hear water moving inside rock — a very faint seep and sigh. Outside, muffled to almost nothing through the crack, the desert: wind-scour, a raven once. Inside, your own sweat dripping off your chin into the pan makes the loudest sound for minutes.

It is sparse. There is a lot of emptiness for so small a space.

A few things, set far apart: one fallen slab in the corner, still sharp, already filmed white on its upper face. Three or four gypsum needles. A sulfur stain like a spilled yolk where a seep is warmest. A thin, oily-looking biofilm — not plant, not quite anything — amber and green-black at the water's edge, bacterial, pre-green, doing photosynthesis without bothering to become a plant. That is all the resident life.

Animals are rare, and accidental.

A pale cricket, almost translucent, missing one hind leg, picks its way along the dryest ledge, tasting salts. It must have fallen in days ago. Two midges hang in the sun shaft, riding the warm updraft, going nowhere. On the dampest wall there is the dried track of something larger — a kangaroo rat or a lizard — four toe-marks and a tail drag in fresh silica mud, already hardening to fossil. Nothing lives here. Things visit, drink the distilled water, leave.

Nothing about it is strange. That is what stays with you. For all the brightness and heat packed into a buried room, for all the impossible cleanliness of its light and water, it feels completely ordinary — a normal hot pocket in young volcanic ground after rain, doing what hot pockets do. The sky sits overhead, white and normal. The rock smells like hot iron and sulfur and clean steam, like any hot spring. Light is light. Water is water. The weirdness is that it needs no weirdness to be perfect.

You cannot stay long. The third rule.

The heat gets into your head, the brightness gets into your eyes until the black glass swims with afterimages, the air gets too thick to be comfortable. The room does not want you out — it is indifferent. It will keep cycling with or without a witness: weeping, evaporating, raining onto itself, laying down another micron of white.

You brace your hands on the sharp, warm lip and pull yourself back up into the wind and the vast, dry glare of the pan. Behind you, under your feet, the little bright, wet furnace keeps ticking quietly to itself, hidden, entire.
