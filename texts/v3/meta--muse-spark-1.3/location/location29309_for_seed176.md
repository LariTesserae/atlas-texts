High up — so high the weather happens underneath you — there is a small room in the mountain.

You feel it first in the lungs. The air is warm and wet in the mouth, like breathing through a hot towel, but it doesn't satisfy. You have to pull twice to get enough. Three steps and the heart is hammering. The light is ordinary noonday, white-gold, but it comes too clean through too little sky. The blue over-head is a deeper blue than it should be. Below, on all sides that are not rock, there is nothing but cloud-sea and a fall so far your stomach forgets it.

The place itself is not large. You could walk its length while a kettle boils.

It is a half-bowl, a bite taken out of the young cone. Behind and to the sides, walls of fresh black rock rise sixty, eighty meters — too new to have learned to be smooth. They are all edge and fracture, columns snapped off like chocolate, scree still sharp enough to cut boots. This mountain hasn't been rained into softness yet. It was born recently, geologically speaking, coughed up and left jagged. Lichen has only just found the lower ten meters.

The bowl tilts gently outward, toward the drop, and holds what it holds because a lip of lava froze across its mouth. Inside the lip: a hanging marsh, no bigger than a hamlet green.

Everything here touches everything else.

Peat black and saturated, quaking underfoot, stitched through with warm pools that never cool. Water is not in a river here, not in a lake. It is in the air and in the skin of things. It weeps straight out of the cliff — hundred thin seeps where the rock sweats — and falls not as falls but as long bead-curtains, breaking on ferns. It condenses on every leaf and pipe and wire and then hangs, reluctant to drop in the updraft, so that the whole hollow glitters with suspended beads. Your shirt is damp in a minute. Your hair drips. The rocks themselves are always wet, dark, steaming faintly where heat comes up from below.

Because it is warm. Impossibly warm for this height. This should be ice and cushion-plant and wind-scoured stone. Instead the fault in the cone breathes up, and the sun at the equator at four thousand meters pours in, and the machines leak their own fever, and together they make a pocket of lowland summer stranded in the sky. Mist stands in slow columns. Where the hot seeps surface, the water shivers at 30, 35 degrees and smells faintly of sulfur and iron.

And so it is lush beyond reason. Not tall — the wind and the thinness won't allow tall — but dense, layered, near-touching. A knee-high jungle. Mosses ten deep, sphagnum rust-red and lime-green, liverworts lacquered, ferns unfurling chest-high in every crack, their roots in air as much as soil. Dwarf trees, gnarled polylepis cousins, no higher than your shoulder, bent over and entirely furred in epiphytes: orchids the size of thumbnails, white and wax-orange, bromeliads holding tiny cups of rain. Everything grows on everything else. A fallen trunk becomes a garden row. A cable becomes a vine trellis.

Through it, and under it, the works.

There was never a town here. Just one installation, scattered and stubborn. A riveted boiler-house of corrugated steel, gone copper-green and rust-orange, squatting on concrete footings at the back wall where the steam is strongest. Its stack breathes, patiently. From it, a thicket of pipes — insulated, moss-eaten, dripping — runs out on low trestles across the marsh on duckboards, hissing at the joints. A transformer on a pole hums without pause, its ceramic insulators bearded with moss, its guy-wires vanishing over the lip and down the mountain to wherever the power goes. A catwalk, a winch, a fuel drum cluster, a small hut with a lit window and a stovepipe, a gauge panel whose needles tremble but never move far.

It is all industrial, unmistakably:1910s or 2140s, it doesn't matter — machined, electrified, scaled to produce. But the jungle is taking the tolerances back. Ferns grow through the grating. Water finds the lagging. And yet the machines still run. That is the rule here: they do not stop.

Hence the loudness.

You expect alpine silence. What you get is saturation of ear as well as eye. The low, bodily throb of the diesel or geothermal pump — you feel it in your molars — under a constant white roar of water weeping and dripping and trickling, under the needle-hiss of three or four steam vents in the rock, under the high electric whine of the transformer and the rattle of the stack in wind. It's loud enough you have to raise your voice to someone an arm's length away. Loud enough the drips lose themselves in it.

And yet nothing hurries.

That is the strangest, most unusual thing, though at first you can't name it. For all the water and noise and heat, the flux is minimal. The pools do not drain; the lip holds them. Water evaporates off the warm skin, condenses on the cliff-cold, rains back down as seep — a tiny, closed bell-jar weather, turning over and over. The clouds far below barely crawl. The orchids open over weeks. The rust creeps a millimeter a year under the moss. Even the machines have settled into a long idle, not straining, just maintaining pressure, decade after decade. The needles shiver around the same mark. Change here is lichen-change, peat-change. Patient.

Animals have mostly declined the invitation. Too high, too small, too loud, too enclosed. No herds, no flocks. Rarely: a cloud of tiny white midges hanging over a hot pool, a stonefly on a pipe. Once a day a hummingbird — emerald, absurdly out of place — works the orange orchids and then vanishes over the void like a spark blown off. Very high and far, sometimes, a condor tilts past below you, looking up at you, and is gone. Frogs, if you listen under the machinery, plink singly in the sedges. That's all. The place belongs to plants and steam and steel.

The boundaries are always visible. Turn in any direction and you see where it ends: black wall, white sky, white cloud-bottom. A few steps off the duckboards and you are shin-deep in warm muck that smells of earth and oil. Step to the lip and the ground falls away — first the frozen lava dam, draped in hanging moss like a beard, then sheer, streaked rock dropping two thousand meters to forest you can only guess at through vapor. The cables that leave hum and dive straight down into that white. Don't touch them when wet. Don't step where the ground bubbles.

At dusk — though noon lasts — the single bulb in the hut and the small red lamp on the transformer come on, absurd and homely in all that green and black, and moths that should not live here batter them. The steam thickens whiter. The beads in the air catch the light and for a moment the whole small room looks like it's underwater, like you're inside a lung.

You breathe twice to get enough, and breathe again.
