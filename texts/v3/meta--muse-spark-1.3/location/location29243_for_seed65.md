The place is up too high for the air to bother with softness.

You come up over the last shoulder — scree giving way to frost-shattered slabs the color of old bone — and the whole bowl opens at once. It is not huge as lowland maps count huge. You can take it in a single turn of the head: an elongated cirque, maybe a valley's worth, three or four miles rim to rim, walled on three sides by dark, weathered teeth. The fourth side falls away into blue depth and distance. That is the size of it. Open, legible, and empty enough that distance lies to you. A boulder that looks ten minutes away is an hour.

The light is the first violence. At this height there is almost nothing between you and the sun. It is bright in a way that has weight — white on the quartz, white on the standing water, ultraviolet humming off lichen. The sky overhead is not pale but saturated, a hard enamel blue going almost black at the zenith, and the clouds do not drift so much as snag. The same cap of lenticular cloud always sits over the easternmost tooth. It was there yesterday. It will be there tomorrow. The shadows are razor-edged and do not warm anything they touch.

It is cool, permanently on the edge. Not bitter polar cold, not summer. A cool that gets into the lungs and makes each breath feel too short, too thin. Your exhale hangs a moment as condensation and then is ripped away. Sweat dries instantly; lips crack. Stone in sun is warm to the palm for an inch, stone in shade is ice. Water exists here only in the in-between.

There is no river. There is damp. That is the rule.

The bowl floor is old rock, settled into itself — not raw, not newly broken. Long weathered. The glaciers that carved it are gone and the slopes have found their angle of repose and kept it for ten thousand years: sorted polygons of stone, striped solifluction lobes, tors pitted with wind-holes big as fists. In the hollows between slabs, water sits where it should not be able to sit. Shallow pans, black mirrors no deeper than your wrist, rimmed with black moss and orange lichen. No inlet, no outlet. They are fed by night condensation and by sleet that comes horizontally once a day and never quite becomes rain, and by the weep of snowpatches that never quite melt and never quite grow. The level in the pans never changes. You can sit and watch for an hour and a marker of pollen on the edge will not move. They do not overflow. They do not dry. They just hold.

Between the pans is mostly nothing. Bare mineral ground, wind-polished pebbles, plates of shale that ring underfoot. Vegetation, where it dares, stays low because anything tall would be flayed. Cushion plants — tight green domes hard as fists, speckled with tiny white flowers — huddle in the lee of boulders and along the damp lips of the tarns. Beard lichens crust everything else: sulfur yellow, blood orange, ash gray. From ten paces the ground looks lifeless. From kneeling, it is dotted with life, but sparse, miserly, each green thing isolated by yards of stone from the next. Nothing wastes water on leaves.

And yet the place crawls and swarms. That is the strangeness.

Nothing here really lives on what grows here. The wind feeds it.

All day, every day, the same pressure funnels up from the lowlands through the western notch — a constant, deafening river of air. You hear it long before you see the bowl. Even from the shoulder below it is a roar in the teeth, and inside the cirque the walls catch it and throw it back until sound has nowhere to go. It is not gusty. It does not rise and fall. It is a steady hammering white noise, the volume of standing beside a falls, that makes conversation impossible beyond arm's length and makes your own heartbeat feel muffled in your chest. Under that, and over that, and stitched through it: life.

The air is full of fallout. Midges, aphids, ballooning spiders, moths, flies the size of sesame seeds torn up from forests thousands of feet below, carried up on thermals and dumped cold-stunned onto snow and stone. They glitter in the bright light as they come down — a slow, endless snow of insects. And where they land, mouths are waiting.

The rocks move if you look long enough. Wolf spiders big as thumbnail, black alpine beetles with fused wing cases, flightless midges that hop like fleas between cushions. In every wind-hole, silk trip-lines strung with desiccated husks. And above them, the birds.

There are thousands. That is not an exaggeration in this emptiness. Red-billed choughs and yellow-billed choughs in screaming, tumbling flocks that ride the notch wind without ever flapping, alpine accentors and snowfinches in nervous clouds that settle and lift, settle and lift off the cushion domes, a lammergeier or two turning slow high circles that never seem to beat a wing. Their calls — the choughs especially, that harsh *chee-ow, chee-ow* — are amplified by the rock amphitheater until they cut through even the wind-roar. Wings whistle. Ten thousand wings at once make a dry rustling like distant surf. At midday when the sun is highest and the updraft is strongest, the noise peaks into something physical, a pressure on the eardrums. You feel it in your sternum. It never stops. Even at the blue edge of evening, when the light thins but does not leave, you hear the settling chatter from the cliff ledges, thousands of bodies shoulder to shoulder on narrow cracks, muttering, preening.

The cliffs are white-streaked with their droppings, the only fertilizer this sparse ground really gets. Under the main nesting wall the moss is almost lush — a hand-wide strip of green sedge and saxifrage you could almost call a meadow, if you were starved for the word.

No one built anything here. There is no wall, no hut, no fence line, no cairn that lasts — the wind takes stacked stone down within a season and the frost heaves posts out. If people come, and rarely they must, they carry everything in and out. What remains is not architecture but gesture, primitive and fleeting: a willow twig stripped and sharpened at one end and dropped by the tarn edge, a flake of black obsidian chipped to an edge and left among identical natural shards, a snare loop of twisted fiber gone gray and rotten, still tied to a dwarf willow root. You could walk past them a dozen times and think them natural. Perhaps some were never human at all. The choughs here are tool-users after their own fashion — you can watch a young bird pick up a stiff grass stem and probe it into a rock crevice to flush spiders, dropping it, picking up another, learning. Hand-tool work, beak-tool work. No workshop. No store.

That is why the place feels out of time. Nothing accumulates. Nothing erodes further. The old forms hold. The pans neither fill nor empty. The lenticular cloud neither forms nor dissipates, but slowly turns. The insect snow never thickens and never ceases. The birds leave at dusk in a great funnel down the notch and return at dawn in the same funnel, the same numbers. The wind combs the same polygons, polishes the same pebbles, whistles through the same holes tuned to the same chord — low A and a fifth above it, always, if you listen under the birds. Even the light feels fixed, too bright for morning or afternoon, just *bright*, cool and white and total.

Stand still long enough and your ears adjust to the deafening and begin to hear inside it: the tick of beetle feet on shale, the plink of condensation dripping from overhang into pan, the high thin whine of midges, your own blood.

It is an empty place that is full. A dry place that is damp to the touch. A place with almost no plants and more animals than you can count, with no houses and still the evidence of hands — or wings — that made. Cold, bright, loud, high, and perfectly balanced on a point it found long ago and has no intention of leaving.
