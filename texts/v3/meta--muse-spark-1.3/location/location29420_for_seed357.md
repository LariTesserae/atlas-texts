High up, where the air should thin and cool, it doesn't. It thickens and heats.

This is a valley perched on the roof of the weather — a long, broad saddle lifted so high you can see the cloud sea breaking white and gray far below on both sides, and yet the air here is hot, bath-hot, greenhouse-hot. Sunlight comes through normal and full, midday-white, but it has to fight through steam. It arrives diffused, sweating. Everything drips with it.

There is no ground here. Not really.

What looks from a distance like a sparse city of green towers scattered across an open high basin is, on approach, not a city at all. Nothing was cut. Nothing was nailed or stacked or fired. Everything was grown — and is still growing. Floor, wall, bridge, cistern, stair, roof: all plant, all alive. A fully built world with no builders, no tools, no hands. Biological only.

The towers stand far apart. That is the first strangeness. For something so total in its vegetation — plant as substrate, plant as architecture, plant filling every surface you can touch — it is emptied out. Much space between presences. You can stand in the vast woven plaza at the center and turn slowly and count them: nine, eleven great masses rising out of the mist, each a quarter-mile from the next, each the size of a hill-town keep, each alone in its own weather. Between them: open air, hot rain drifting sideways, sheets of vapor rising off the living mat.

You walk on root-felt ten feet thick, springy, warm as skin. It gives under your boots and seeps. Water is near everywhere — not under, but in. Step and water wells up around your footprint, clear and tannin-brown, then drains sideways into runnels that were grown to carry it. The whole valley floor is a slight, deliberate camber, a shallow roof designed by no one to shed water toward the edges, where it falls in long living gutters off the world and into the clouds below. You hear it long after you can't see it.

The towers themselves defy early sense. Up close they resolve into impossible weaves: buttress roots as wide as houses arching out and fusing, trunks splitting into colonnades, colonnades dissolving into lattice, lattice closing into hollow chambers stacked a hundred feet high. Leaves bigger than doors overlap like shingles, each one channeled down the middle to throw rain outward. They weep continuously. From every tip, every hanging root, water beads, swells, falls. The air is so saturated it condenses on your arms, on your eyelashes. Weeping walls. The buildings sweat to cool themselves in the heat.

And they are young. That is written everywhere in sharpness. Nothing is worn smooth. Fronds unroll with waxy, almost plastic freshness, their edges still knife-keen. Thorns come out pale green and harden to black within hours. Bark — if you can call it bark — peels in fresh papery curls that haven't yet rotted. Buttresses meet at angles so crisp they look folded, not grown. This whole place feels recently emerged, early-formation, as if the valley inhaled once and threw all this up. Which, in a way, it did. And is still doing.

Because nothing here is still.

Stand and watch for ten minutes and the turbulence shows itself. Not wind-turbulence — growth-turbulence. Creation and destruction at anthill speed, at bazaar speed. A vine as thick as your wrist will cross a gap in front of you, questing, twining, locking on, leafing out behind itself while the tip is still moving. You can hear it: a slow green creak, fiber sliding on fiber. Elsewhere, a flower the size of a bathtub gorges on water and bursts — a wet pop that echoes between towers — flinging translucent seed-pods that helicopter down and, where they land on wet mat, split and root within the hour, sending up bright red feelers. And elsewhere, collapse: an over-watered chamber wall softens, sags, tears under its own weight with a long ripping sigh, falling in a steaming heap that is already being eaten — white root-hairs rising out of the floor like frost to digest its sister.

It is loud. With no animal to make a sound, you expect quiet. It is not quiet. It is the loudness of water and wood working. The constant arrhythmic percussion of drips from a hundred heights hitting leaf-drums, cistern-membranes that boom hollow when rain sheets across them. The groan of trunks swaying in heat thermals. The hiss where sun-heated pools on broad leaves flash to vapor. The sudden gunshot crack of a seed pod or a tension-fiber snapping. The rush through the aerial aqueducts — great hollow vines strung between towers, sagging with their load, humming as water runs inside them. Sound carries freakishly far in the hot, empty intervals; a tower half a mile away splitting a seam sounds like it's right behind your ear.

No insects stitch this noise together. No birds cut across. No frogs, no mammals rustling. Once you realize there is truly no animal life, the movement becomes uncanny. A curtain of hanging air-roots sways and for an instant you read it as a herd, a crowd — then wind passes and it is only plant again. Pollination here is done by water and by storm: the towers exhale clouds of pale pollen at noon that hang and drift and rain out together with the afternoon downpour. Fertilization by drowning.

The architecture deepens the weirdness. These forms are too useful to be random and too empty to be innocent. There are stairs — broad, shallow, rubbery stairs spiraling up inside the hollow towers, sized for something bipedal that never comes. There are doorways, oval sphincters that dilate open as you approach, sensing humidity, and close behind you. There are rooms: cool, dim, ribbed chambers with floors of moss so even they feel swept, with niches in the walls that hold rainwater like basins, with windows that are not holes but thinned membranes glowing green-gold with outside light. There are bridges that were clearly grown to be crossed, plazas clearly grown to be gathered in. A city for no citizens. And the emptiness is not a ruin — ruins are dead. This is balanced-with-emptiness, sparse on purpose. Each structure holds a huge volume of nothing: air, steam, echo. You shout once in a tower hall and the plant ribs take the sound and return it softened, leaf-filtered, three seconds later.

The rules reveal themselves if you stay through a rain cycle, which is about an hour, repeated endlessly:

1. Everything must move water. Leaves are roofs and gutters. Stems are pipes. Roots are pumps. Even the stairs sweat to keep from rotting in the heat. If a surface stops shedding, it is sacrificed and regrown.

2. Nothing stays dead. Fallen matter doesn't litter for long. The mat breathes it back in. You can watch your own footprints fill with fine green hair within a day.

3. Grow fast, grow sharp, abandon without grief. A tower will spend enormous energy to throw a bridge fifty yards to its neighbor, hold it for two days while water and pollen shuttle across, then let it rot through and drop it spinning into the mist when the wind shifts.

The light never really changes — that normal, high, white light filtered to jade inside the towers, to silver outside — but the place changes under the light so quickly that mapping is useless. The path you came by, a causeway of interlaced branches with a handrail of living vine, will have thickened behind you, or opened a new side shoot, or sagged into a cistern. Guides would be useless. Maintenance would be blasphemy.

From the edge of the valley mat, where the weave thins into a fringe of hanging roots dripping into nothing, you can look out over the scale of it: town-or-valley-size, multi-feature, extending past the immediate. Not infinite — you can see the far rim, another line of young towers dissolving in updraft — but regional, too big to hold in one glance. Below, the world of rock and soil, if it exists, is hidden. Above, only heat-shimmer and the white sun working to lift more water out of the leaves to fall again as rain.

Hot. Wet. High. Young. Built without building.

Stand still long enough and the mat under you warms to your weight, and fine tendrils, curious, blind, purely vegetal, begin to test the edges of your boots — not hostile, not kind. Just checking if you, too, might be substrate, might be willing to be grown over, plumbed for water, made into another brief, sharp, dripping room in the sparse and steaming arcology that never stops making itself.
