It is low here. Lower than you expect land to go and still be land.

From a half-mile out the ground already stops rising to meet you. It just levels — a wide, warm pan of silt and shallow water that runs to a smudged horizon without ever deciding to become hill or valley. The sky sits down close on it. A thick, low overcast the color of wet wool holds the light down to a perpetual late-afternoon dim, and the sun is only a paler smear behind it. You don't get shadows. You get a directionless amber gloom that makes everything look a little underwater even when you're standing on dry — mostly dry — footing.

And then the town. You hear it long before you see its shape clearly.

At first it looks like a stain on the flat: darker, lumpy, extending sideways farther than a single field of view can hold. A town the size of a small valley, not clustered on a hill for defense but spread in a dense mat across the wet. As you get closer the stain resolves into roofs, walls, racks, pens, causeways — hundreds, thousands of structures so close they almost touch, layered over each other, built up two and three high where the footing allows, sunk to the ankle everywhere else. No streets as you think of them, just narrow plank-and-mud walkways threading between houses, and behind every house another house, and behind that a pen, and above that a drying platform on poles, and washing lines of hide strung between.

There is no green. That is the second thing you notice once your eyes adjust.

No trees. No grass. No reeds, no crops, not even weeds in the corners. This in a warm, soaking place where by all rights things should be exploding out of the mud. Instead the ground is mineral — black and grey-blue clay, ochre silt, hand-piled cobbles still sharp-edged and unrounded, flat slabs of fresh sandstone. Between the buildings the mud is bare and pocked, stippled with puddles that never dry. Where it isn't churned by feet it is skinned over with thin films: pale fungal felt, olive algal slick, white crusts of salt where water has wicked up and evaporated. The world here is pre-plant, or post-plant. Everything wooden or fibrous has long ago been cut, carried in, and built with. What grows is simpler than plant — slime, mold, the sour-sweet bloom in damp corners.

It makes sense when you feel the footing. The land is new. Raw. If you crouch — and you will, because the walkway sags — you can see it: no soil horizons, no carved channels, no sorted gravels. Just fresh-laid sediment, soft as dough, laid down by water yesterday in geologic terms and still settling. The stones people have pried up for foundations are angular, unworn, their faces still showing the quarry break. Even the low rib of rock that shoulders up on the north edge of town, no higher than a house, looks embryonic — a blunt sandstone lip that hasn't learned to be a cliff yet, its face constantly weeping. Water beads out of the pore spaces and runs in hundreds of thin trickles that never quite become streams. They drip from overhangs, they darken the stone, they feed the puddles. The air itself is condensing. Your skin stays wet without rain. Your breath comes back to you.

The building is all hand work. You can see the hands in it.

Mud brick, sun-warm but never quite dry, laid in thick courses and finger-smoothed. Low walls of fieldstone stacked without mortar, chinked with clay. Frames made from long bones lashed with sinew and hair-cord, roofed with overlapping mud slabs, with stretched hides, with inverted baskets plastered over. Causeways are split logs — carried from far away, precious — laid crosswise over the ooze and already sinking, or they are simply raised ribs of tamped clay that squelch underfoot. Everywhere there are pits where clay has been dug, and those pits have immediately filled with groundwater the color of tea. Everywhere there are piles: shells saved for temper and tools, ash, cracked bone, chipped stone mauls, digging sticks polished by palms, baskets half-finished.

Nothing is machined. Nothing is sawn straight. Edges are pecked, ground, rubbed. Lashings slip and are retied. The infrastructure is dense and organized — cisterns rimmed with clay to catch the weep-water, communal ovens like beehives breathing heat, latrine platforms over the outer slough, animal pens fenced with driven stakes — but it is all at the scale of muscle. A town built by carrying.

And it is crowded beyond the buildings.

People, yes — many, moving shoulder to shoulder on the walkways, shouting to be heard, children perched on walls, old people mending nets of hair-twine in doorways — but the fauna is not just people. The town is a herd and a flock and a swarm at once. Pigs the color of mud root along the edges of platforms. Goats balance on walls. Long-legged wading birds stalk the borrow pits, utterly unafraid. Dogs sleep in the warm ooze and wake to bark. Ducks and geese are herded in gabbling wedges through water ankle-deep. Water buffalo stand massive and steaming in wallows, half-submerged. Rats run the thatch-lines of hides. Crabs sidle under walkways. And under and around all of that, in numbers you stop trying to count: frogs. Frogs the size of thumbs and fists, speckled, black, ochre, throats pulsing in every puddle, on every mud tussock, thousands per acre. Clouds of midges and mosquitoes hang in pillars over the water. Swallows — or something like swallows — cut through them in flickering sheets.

Nothing here is sparse. Presences layer. A roof holds drying fish and a sleeping dog and a row of gourds (?) no — clay pots — and two children and a heron watching for scraps. A single square yard of puddle holds tadpoles, minnows, beetles, frog adults, and the reflection of a pig. The air is crowded too, with smoke from a hundred low cookfires that cannot rise in the saturated air and so spreads sideways, adding to the dim, stinging faintly.

The sound is physical.

You have to understand: it does not rise and fall. It is not a chorus with verses. It is a pressure, deafening and level, that gets into your jawbone from the moment you step onto the outer causeway and does not leave you.

The base is water and mud: the suck and slap of hundreds of feet at once, the splash of animals shifting in wallows, the constant drip-drip from the weeping rocks and from eaves, the seep-hiss where groundwater pushes up through trodden clay.

On top of that, animal: the frog roar — not peeping but a deep, rattling, full-throated bellow in millions, overlapping into a single unbroken band of sound — and the insect whine pitched just above it, and the bray of goats, grunt of pigs, honk and quack of waterfowl, barking that starts somewhere and is answered in three other quarters without ever ending, the lowing of buffalo, the high screech of wading birds squabbling over pits.

On top of that, human: shouting — everyone shouts, because you must to be heard two feet away — pounding of stone mauls shaping slabs, thud of digging sticks, scrape of baskets dragged, clatter of pots, laughter that is also shouting, a baby wailing and not stopping, somewhere a rhythmic hand-drum and chant that doesn't make it louder so much as thicker.

It is warm-loud. The warmth holds sound close. The low cloud holds it down. The flat holds it out sideways so it doesn't dissipate but accumulates. You feel it in your sternum. If you cup your ears it doesn't help much; the vibration comes up through your feet.

And the strangest, most normal thing: it never changes.

You wait for a break, for a gust, for night to bring quiet, for a lull when the frogs pause. It doesn't come. The light stays in that same dim amber. The heat stays at that same sweaty, shirt-sticking warmth that makes the mud smell rich and faintly sulfurous. The water stays at the surface — press your thumb into the lee of any wall and the print fills instantly. The town neither floods nor dries. Silt arrives as fast as feet churn it away. Walls slump as fast as they are patched. The new land never gets old enough to erode into gullies; the feet and hands keep it scuffed back to raw beginnings. It is an equilibrium.

The rules here announce themselves without signs:

Stay on the walkways if you can. The ground between will take your sandal.

Talk loud or don't talk. Keep your animals close and your food closer — everything edible is watched by something else with eyes.

Nothing is thrown away where it can't be used as building. Shell becomes tool becomes temper. Bone becomes post becomes handle. Ash becomes lye becomes plaster.

Do not look for wood, for shade, for green. There is none to spare. Shade is hide and mud-slab and another body leaning close. Fuel is dung and bone-fat and smoldering peat from the pan, which is why the smoke lies so low.

Drink from the cisterns under the weeping lip, not the pits. Wash in the pits. Let the buffalo have the wallows.

By the time you are inside the mat of it, the horizon of mud and low cloud has been replaced by a horizon of walls and backs and steam. The town extends past the immediate in every direction — you can see gaps where a new platform is going up on fresh mud, and beyond that more roofs, and beyond that the white-grey shimmer of open water where the pan finally gives up and becomes marsh. Gulls turn there, crying, inaudible under the town roar until you are at the very edge.

You stand there sweating in the dim, your feet wet, your ears ringing, frogs pulsing against your ankles, the smell of warm clay and dung and smoke and salt and fish thick in your nose, and you realize the place isn't becoming something else. It has arrived. Young rock, old hands, dense bodies, loud equilibrium — holding, holding, holding against the wet.
