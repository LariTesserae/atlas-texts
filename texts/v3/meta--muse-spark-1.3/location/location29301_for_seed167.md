It is night at the bottom, and the water is warm as blood.

Not warm like a shallow lagoon in afternoon sun — there is no sun here, has not been for a long downward mile — but warm the way an animal is warm, held. The sea holds its heat. It presses on your ears, your ribs, your eyes. When you move your hand you push through it slowly, and it closes behind you without a ripple you can see. Because you can see almost nothing.

At first that is all there is: dark, warm, wet weight. Low flat world.

Then your eyes adjust, or your skin does, and the city resolves.

It is low. The floor of this place is low — a broad drowned plain sunk between higher ridges you will never see, silted and settled and smoothed over a time so long the hills have given up being hills. Fine pale sand, trampled to marl. Here and there a fan of grit where a current has died. And out of that flatness, everywhere, it rises.

Towers. Blocks. Terraces. Arches. A town, a valley of structures extending past where you can feel, repeating and varying for what must be miles. Not rock. Not built rock. Built bodies.

No one here has hands. No one here has tools, or fires, or wire, or cut stone. The rule here is: if you want a wall, you must make it out of yourself. Secrete it. Excrete it. Die into it and leave it for someone else to live on.

So the city is calcium and mucus and cemented sand and fused shell. The foundations are ancient coral, long dead, weathered round as knuckles — established, in balance, no longer sharp. On top of that, layer on layer: the white clustered tenements of oysters and mussels shouldered tight until their shells deform each other into hexagons; the tall, thin chimneys of tube worms, thousands side by side, each a finger-width pipe of parchment and limestone, swaying almost imperceptibly; the sponges, massive as houses, barrel-bodied and pitted, their oscula gaping like doors and chimneys. Between them, the lacework — bryozoans sheeting over everything like stucco, forams gluing sand grain to sand grain.

Nothing is straight, but everything is organized. That is the first unusual thing you notice. It *feels* like streets. There are gaps left open between the masses, and they stay open. Currents run through them the way wind runs down streets, and they are kept swept clean of silt by a thousand fans and cilia. You can drift down an avenue lined on both sides with worm-towers three, four stories above you — if stories mean anything when the residents are two inches long — and emerge into a plaza where the floor has been planed flat by sand dollars, hundreds of them, edge to edge, slowly plowing.

Vegetation, almost none. A few pale ribbons where something plant-like still tries: a sparse kelp, bleached white-green by the dark, straggling up from a crack, ten feet tall and utterly alone. A patch of bacterial velvet, sulfur-yellow, on a collapsed wall. Otherwise, the green kingdom has abdicated. This is an animal town.

And it is crowded. Dense beyond easy telling.

The water itself is thick with presences. Near-touching, layered. Above the rooftops the larvae hang in schools so thick they change the viscosity — clouds of copepods and shrimp-motes, pulsing. Through them dart the fish: small, blind or big-eyed, mouths wide, black or silver or transparent. They do not school so much as traffic. Eels pour like slow rain from window to window. Octopods the size of dogs have taken the largest sponge-barrels as dens, one arm always curled out the door, tasting. Crabs pick their sideways way along the cornices. Sea cucumbers, fat as sandbags, bulldoze the alleys. On every surface, brittle stars wave, anemones open and close, barnacles kick their feathered feet in synchrony.

You cannot see most of this at once. Light is a rumor here. The darkness is almost complete — light at 0.1, a memory of light. Far, far above, the surface daylight is a myth no one here believes. What illumination there is is made locally, sparingly: a drifting snow of marine phosphorescence when something is disturbed; the faint blue-green blink of a ctenophore rowing past your cheek; the lure-dots of anglers waiting patiently at intersections. Mostly you navigate by touch and by pressure, by tasting the water for limestone and rot and spawn. If you stay still long enough, shapes emerge in negative — darker masses against a slightly less dark water.

It is quiet. Mostly quiet.

Water takes sound and folds it small. There is no crash, no surf. If you hold your breath — you are holding it, in a way, even as you breathe the water — you hear: the constant low filtration hum, the click of a million tiny valves closing; a distant crumble as an old worm-tower, undercut by erosion, topples and is immediately swarmed; the soft purrr of a ray beating its wings over sand; now and then, startlingly close, the pop and sizzle of a snapping shrimp, like a knuckle cracking in a cathedral. Then quiet again, and the slow sigh of the current turning over.

Because it is active, this city, even while quiet. Multiple processes at once, biological pace, predator and prey. Nothing is finished. The established forms are always being weathered and remade. You can see — feel — the stages: a new district, raw and sharp, tubes newly extended, mucus still tacky, colonized within hours by opportunists; a middle-aged block, dense, saturated, every niche filled, cleaner shrimp picking parasites off morays in doorways like barbershops; an old district, smoothed by years of water, edges rounded, chimneys broken, sponges overgrowing shells overgrowing worms, finally slumping back to marl to be built on again. Long-existed. In its own state. It does not need renewal; it is renewal.

The water moves through it all with purpose. Warm currents arrive from the rift vents beyond the ridges — that is where the heat comes from — carrying minerals, sulfur, fine ash that tastes metallic-sweet. The whole town leans, very slightly, into that flow. All the fan-worms face the same way. All the oyster lips gape upstream. All the sponge mouths exhale downstream. If you close your eyes you can feel the city's orientation like a compass needling.

And the rule holds utterly: nothing here is made, everything is grown or left behind. Even the streets are not cleared, they are kept clear by living. Even the debris is alive — hermit crabs in borrowed whelk-shells clambering over piles of empty whelk-shells. Death is construction material. A fish dies in an alley and by morning its spine is a scaffolding, by evening its ribs are apartments for amphipods.

Stay longer. Let your body go warm and neutrally buoyant, hanging mid-street between sand and black. A cloud of larval fish parts around you, each individual no bigger than an eyelash, each perfectly formed, brushing your lips. A blind lobster, white as bone, taps its way across your foot, unconcerned. Above you, something large passes without sound — a pressure wave, a displaced volume, a smell of iodine — and is gone before your eyes can assemble it.

The city extends. Beyond this avenue, another. Beyond this plaza of sand dollars, a field of pen-shells stuck upright in the mud like grave markers, each with a crab perched atop. Beyond that, a wall — a true wall, twenty feet high, made entirely of cemented vermetid snail tubes, winding for farther than you can follow, with gaps that breathe in and out.

No one planned it. Everyone maintains it.

This is what an urban place looks like when biology is the only architect and warm darkness is the climate: dense, low, old, crowded, quiet, and endlessly, restlessly building itself out of itself.
