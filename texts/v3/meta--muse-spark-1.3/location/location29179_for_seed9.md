You come up out of the cloud to find it.

The climb is cold and thin and white for a long time — switchbacks in wet stone, breath short — and then the wall. A high parapet, concrete still the color of fresh bone, and a steel door sweating, and beyond it, heat.

Not the heat of lowlands. A close, breathed-out, greenhouse heat that hits your glasses and your lungs at once. You step inside and you are inside a room that thinks it is a valley.

It is small. You can see the whole of it at once if you stand on the central walkway and turn slowly: an oblong basin maybe two hundred paces long, walled all around, open to the sky but holding its own weather. Beyond the parapet there is nothing but falling air and a sea of cloud below. Here, up high, someone has built a shallow bowl to catch the sky and keep it warm.

Everything under your feet is made. That is the first rule of the place. There is no soil, no rock showing through. The floor is stepped concrete, poured in terraces, each terrace holding four inches of standing water. The walls are concrete and brick, the channels are concrete, the little bridges are iron grate, the trellises are copper pipe gone green. Even what looks like hills — low mounds along the western side — are just curing piles of aggregate covered in tarps, with rebar sticking out of them like winter saplings.

And it is unfinished. That is the second thing. The edges are too sharp. Water has not yet had time to carve anything. The spillways still show the grain of the wooden formwork they were cast in. Some forms haven't even been struck yet: planks strapped to a new tank, concrete still dark and damp behind them, weeping lime-white tears. A stack of cinderblocks, a coil of hose, a bag of cement burst and set hard in the rain like a boulder. The whole place has the feel of something embryonic, poured yesterday and already overgrown.

Because it is overgrown. Lush beyond reason.

In the four inches of water, in the seams, in the felt pockets bolted to every vertical face, in the gutters and the rust-pitted valve boxes: green. Moss so thick it felts the sound. Ferns uncurling from expansion joints. Taro with leaves as big as shields, standing in the warm shallows. Vines — philodendron, pothos gone wild, some orchid with aerial roots like white wire — climbing the boiler pipes, hanging from the scaffolding, rooting into the damp insulation. The air is saturated. Water condenses on every underside and falls again. The concrete itself sweats.

The warmth comes from below. You feel it through your soles. Under the terraces runs a low industrial gut: boilers, pumps, a dynamo that never stops. You hear it more in your teeth than your ears — a deep, even thrum. Pipes rise everywhere, lagged and unlagged, hot to the touch, ticking as they expand. Where a pipe runs, growth doubles. Roots crowd toward heat like toward sun. Some enterprising vine has found a steam bleed and wrapped it, and the leak now puffs out in small, regular breaths through leaves, a green mouth exhaling cloud.

Water is the work here. The place is a machine for holding water up high where water shouldn't stay.

Cloud enters over the low eastern parapet all morning, uninvited and constant. It hits the warm air above the terraces and thickens, beads. The mist-nets — tall frames strung with black mesh along the windward side — drip without pause into zinc gutters. The gutters lead to settling tanks, the tanks to the terraces, the terraces to each other in slow, stepped overflows, lip after lip of clear, tea-colored, root-sweet water. When a terrace gets too full, a float valve sighs and a small electric pump kicks on somewhere underfoot, lifting water back up to the head tank to begin again. Uphill, downhill, uphill. Nothing is allowed to leave except as vapor. On the far wall someone has painted in whitewash, already flaking with algae: RETURN IT ALL.

So the sound is water, but quiet water. Not a river. A thousand small negotiations. Drip from mesh to gutter. Tick-tick-tick in the copper. A thin sheeting sound where one terrace spills to the next. The low churn of a pump, muffled by moss until it is almost a purr. Leaves shifting when a vent opens and closes on its thermostat. Your own footsteps ringing hollow on the grate, then softening where the duckboards have gone green and spongy.

Light is ordinary daylight, but made strange by altitude and steam. Bright, white-gold, but diffused. No hard shadows. At noon the sun stands straight in the open bowl and every pool flashes. Then a puff of boiler steam crosses and erases it. Your skin never quite dries. Your clothes smell within an hour of hot mineral, chlorophyll, machine oil.

There are almost no animals. That is the strangeness you notice fourth, after made and unfinished and green. No birds ride this high except far, far below. No insects but a single kind of small white moth that lives its whole life in the mist-nets, laying eggs in the mesh. And the frogs.

You hear one before you see any — a single metallic *pink* from inside a valve box. Open it, carefully, and there they are: three frogs the size of thumbnails, black as iron, sitting in warm seep, throats pulsing. They live in the machinery. Every hollow junction box, every perpetually damp inspection pit, has its frogs. The maintenance crew — whoever poured this and left the scaffolding up — must have brought tadpoles up in buckets, or they came with the taro roots. Now they are the only tenders. They eat the moths that clog the floats. At night, when the steam is thickest, they call to each other across the terraces, box to box, a sparse circuitry of sound.

If you stay till late afternoon, you see what the place is doing. The cloud deck below rises. The parapet disappears. The bowl becomes an island in white, and then not an island but a hollow in white, and the mist-nets stream, the gutters gurgle higher, the terraces skin over with rain rings though no rain falls — only condensation growing too heavy to hang. The float valves all lift at once. The pumps labor a little. The vines drip. The whole small, dense, layered system — water over concrete over heat over water over leaf — quickens, as if drinking faster.

You understand the rule then, without being told: keep it moving, keep it shallow, keep it warm. Don't let water find a way down. Let green do what green wants on top of iron. Don't finish it too quickly, or the carving will start.

Beyond the western wall, behind the tarp-covered mounds, a new terrace is being formed. The rebar cage is already green with algae at its base. Fern spores have landed in the damp sand of the footing. By the time the concrete is poured next week, roots will already be waiting inside it.
