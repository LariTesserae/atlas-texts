It is an ocean on top of the world.

Not on top the way a lake sits in a mountain bowl. Higher than that. This ocean *is* the high place — a drowned plateau kilometers above where a seafloor should be, so vast in every direction that the horizon bends away and takes its own weather with it. From the air it would look almost ordinary, if anything could get up here to look: cold blue under a white-yellow sun, normal daylight, wind-chop glittering.

Then you go in, and ordinary stops.

The water is cold — not killing cold, working cold, seven or eight degrees that gets into the joints and stays there. Clear beyond what water should allow. You drop below the chop and the light keeps coming, and coming, a hundred meters down and still noon-blue, still reading-light bright. There is almost nothing in it to stop the light. No silt. No snow of plankton. No schools, no drifters, no whales. Nothing with eyes, or mouths, or fins. The emptiness is the first thing you learn. It is not the emptiness of a deserted place. It is the emptiness of a place that was never filled.

Sound comes through that emptiness wrong-loud. Without bodies to absorb it, every crack and rush carries for miles. You hear the surface before you leave it — a continuous low roar where the plateau makes its own squalls, rain hammering down in a gray wall that moves in a slow circle and rains itself out and builds itself again by evening. And under that, deeper: a long grinding boom as new rock splits open, and the hiss and shatter of lava meeting cold water, and the standing thunder of the upwellings.

Because the bottom is new.

It is not sand. It is not old smoothed stone. It is raw geology, hours to years old, still coming into being. Pillow basalts piled like black glass sacks, some still warm enough to make the water shiver above them. Ridges that tore open last week and haven't had time to crumble. Chimneys and needles and plates set at angles that look too deliberate, too right-angled, branching in lattices where basalt shouldn't branch. No beaches. No pebbles. No rounding. In a world without grazers and without grind, nothing gets worn down. The edges stay knife-sharp. When a pillow bursts it throws out shards of volcanic glass that hang in the water for a while, glittering, and then settle, untouched.

Between one outcrop and the next there can be a kilometer of nothing. Then another. The scale defeats you. You think you are seeing a hill and it is a mountain range. You think you are seeing the bottom and it is a false bottom, a shelf, with blue dropping away beyond it into a rift that goes down out of light. Currents run through those rifts like rivers in the sea, cold and fast and impossibly straight, holding their edges for tens of kilometers without mixing. They shouldn't. They do.

This is where the strangeness settles in.

The water organizes itself. A gyre will spin up out of calm in an hour, a perfect circle two kilometers across, its wall a sheer curtain of faster water, its eye dead calm, and inside the eye a column of warmer water rises from a fresh vent and spreads and sinks at the rim, carrying dissolved silica that precipitates as it cools into veils — thin, translucent sheets that hang vertical for a day and then collapse into powder. Sometimes the sea stands up in swells that do not travel. They just stand, ten meters high, breathing in place for days while wind passes through them. Buoyancy feels slightly doctored here. A stone dropped will fall too slowly for a meter, then too fast. Bubbles from the vents rise in braids instead of clouds, twisting around each other as if threaded.

If you sample the water — if you could hold it still enough — you would find why. It is not empty because it is poor. It is empty because it is kept. In every liter there are motes, fewer than dust, smaller than any cell, not alive, not dead, that catch the stray organic molecule, the grain of ash, and carry it down. They keep the water polished. And down on the rock they do the other job: they coax the mineral out of solution, they lay it down in those strange lattices, they grow the plateau thicker from the top down. Not building, exactly. There are no walls here, no ruins, no wrecks, no cables, no cities drowned or otherwise. Nothing made that you can point to and say *that was put there*. The making is dissolved into the physics. The planet is under construction by means it has internalized so completely it looks like nature.

Only the sparse green gives the scale away.

Life got a toehold and no further. On widely scattered holdfasts — sometimes five kilometers between one and the next — there are kelps. Not forests. Individuals. A single stipe as thick as your wrist rising thirty, forty meters straight up from a crack, with a few long olive ribbons streaming in the current. No bite marks. Nothing has ever eaten them. So they just keep growing, unpruned, until the stipe is scarred white with age and the ribbons are thirty meters long and frayed only by water. Around their bases, thin bacterial-algal skins in rust-red and pale green creep over the glass, the only softness for miles. When a frond finally dies it does not get consumed. It sinks, intact, and lies on the sharp rock and slowly turns to stone itself, a green-black fossil in a place with no future paleontologists.

No gulls above them. No crabs below. No fish to hide in them. Their shadows fall on empty stone and wave back and forth, and that is all.

Up top, the rules reassert. The plateau ocean breathes out mist and breathes in storm. Clouds are born at ten in the morning off the eastern fetch where the sun has warmed the top meter, pile into anvils by noon, march west dropping that wall of rain that hammers the surface white and loud enough to hear from a hundred meters down, and dissipate by night. Lightning walks on the water without anything tall to strike — fork to flat sea, again and again, ozone sharp even through spray. The wind never stops because there is nothing to stop it. Waves build across a thousand kilometers of fetch until they are long, cold hills moving with a deep, muscular rhythm that you feel in your chest before you see it.

It is turbulent and it is hollow at once. Violent, and mostly vacant. A world-sized engine running at full speed to make more world, polishing its sea as it goes, growing pale ribbons of weed in water too clean and too cold and too loud for anything that swims.

If you stay long enough you stop expecting animals. You start listening instead for the other rhythm under the storm-noise: the tick and settle of new crust cooling, the low organ-note of a far gyre turning, the glass-chime when a chimney collapses untouched. The water holds you up a fraction longer than it should, then lets you go. Light falls straight down through a hundred meters of nothing. Far off, another kelp towers alone in the blue, its fronds streaming east like a flag planted on a country still being made.
