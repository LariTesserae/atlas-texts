It is high, and it is drowned.

Far below, there is abyss — cold, blue-black, a fall of thousands of meters. And lifted up out of it, like a table shoved up through the floor of the world, is a plateau. Its top is almost at the sky. Ten meters, fifteen, twenty meters under the surface, no more. Sunlight gets here intact. Warmth gets here intact. You can feel both on the back of your neck as you swim.

From above, on a calm day, you would think it was land. The water over it is so shallow and so clear and so green with life that it reads as meadow. Then a ripple crosses and betrays it. It is not land. It is a high country under water.

The rock is young. That is the first thing your hands learn. This whole tableland erupted not long ago — a century, two, a blink — and it hasn't had time to be softened. There is no sand to speak of, only a dusting of crushed shell caught in hollows. Everything else is fresh basalt, black and bottle-green where it cooled fast into glass. Pillows piled on pillows, tubes split open, chimneys standing up with their throats still empty. The edges are sharp enough to cut. Fishermen, if there were any, would tear their nets here in a minute. The drop-off at the rim is a clean break, a cliff that falls straight down into deeper blue, barnacled white at the lip where the surf of deep currents gnaws, but inland, in the middle acres, the lava is already colonized.

Because it is warm. Bath-warm, blood-warm, the kind of warm that never quite leaves, day or night. Twenty-seven, twenty-eight degrees, held. The water does not turn over fast; it lingers over the plateau in a slow lens, heated from above, and only reluctantly spills off the sides. Swimming here is not bracing. It is like moving through warm broth, slightly thick, slightly resistant. Plankton hangs in it, unhurried. Marine snow does not fall so much as loiter.

And in that warmth and light, it has gone lush beyond reason.

Not coral — something softer, greener, more forest than reef. Meadows of seagrass first, in the flats, blades as wide as your wrist and taller than you, olive and jade, rooted in cracks in the rock, swaying not with waves — there are almost no waves down here — but with a slow inhalation, a tide that takes six hours to in and six to out. Between the meadows, thickets. Kelp but wrong for the tropics, or something that decided to be kelp independently: great brown-gold ribbons rising from holdfasts the size of fists clamped to glassy stone, rising ten, twelve meters until their bladders kiss the underside of the surface. They grow so close they touch. Frond on frond, stipe on stipe. Light filters down through layer after layer of them, green-gold, then green, then a deeper green shadow at the bottom where the young shoots wait.

It is dense in a way land forests rarely are. Because here, life can use the whole volume. It is not just floor and canopy. The middle water is full. Fern-like red algae float unattached, rootless balls of lace that never land, drifting inches a day. Chains of diatoms link into spirals you can see if you hold still. Sponges have taken the chimneys — sulfur-yellow, blood-orange, violet-black — and grown out from them in shelves and vases, narrowing the passages until you have to turn sideways to slip through. Every hard surface is furred with something: calcareous pink crust, green velvet, white hydroids like frost. If you extend your arm you will touch at least three living things. If you hold your breath and hang motionless, more will brush you — a leaf, a tendril, a slow snow of larvae seeking rock.

From below, the surface is a ceiling. That is the strangest part, until you get used to it. You live under a sky that is water meeting air. On rainy days — and rain comes almost daily, warm and sudden and immense, hammering the ocean into white noise for ten minutes — you can see it from underneath as a gray shattering, thousands of dimples appearing at once, and the upper meter goes fresh and slightly cooler and full of bubbles driven down. The kelp bladders collect those bubbles. At noon the whole forest is silvered with trapped air, each frond edged in mercury. At night the bubbles wobble loose one by one and rise crookedly, too slowly, as if the water were thicker than it should be.

There are clearings, and that is where the youth of the place shows most. Where a recent flow or a collapse has opened bare rock, nothing has yet taken hold except a film of green. You can see the ropey pahoehoe swirls, the glass still shiny, the sharp spires where gas tried to escape. In one valley — a shallow graben running roughly north-south for perhaps a mile — the floor is a jumble of black blocks each as big as a house, tumbled but unworn, edges like broken bottles. Between them, cold seeps, slightly cooler, slightly sulfurous, shimmer faintly. White bacterial mats fringe them. No plants there yet. Just mineral and heat ghost.

The animals are rare. That is the rule here: plants make, almost nothing takes. You can swim for an hour and see no fish. Then, all at once, a single green turtle, very old, very unhurried, flapping its way over the meadow, cropping a blade here and there and ignoring you completely. Or a small cloud of silver juveniles, all of one species, all born in the same week, flickering through a kelp corridor and gone. Sharks do not come up onto the table; the water is too warm, too full of leaf to hunt in. Instead there are traces: a bite taken clean out of a bladder, a shell with a drill-hole, turtle droppings slowly disintegrating into green clouds that feed the grass. In the quietest hollows you find the gardeners — urchins, black and long-spined, each holding fragments of algae over themselves like parasols, mowing neat circles around their burrows. Otherwise, invertebrate time rules. Anemones open and close over the course of a day. A nudibranch the length of your thumb crosses a sponge in an afternoon.

Sound barely exists. Water carries sound well elsewhere; here the forest swallows it. What you hear, with your head under, is your own breath first, then, underneath that, a low, almost subsonic creak when two kelp stipes rub, a faint ticking of snapping shrimp far off in the rocks, the soft rain of particles landing on leaves. When the great tide turns — so slow you would not notice except your depth gauge shifts by a hand's breadth — there is a sigh as water begins to spill off the rim, a movement of the whole meadow leaning outward at once. Then stillness again. No crash. No surf. Even the edge, where the plateau falls away, is muffled; the deep current that runs along the cliff is silent until you put your hand in it and feel the cold shear pull.

The light is ordinary daylight, and that makes it stranger. This is not the deep sea with its lanterns and blues. This is midday sun through three meters of water and then through leaves. It dapples. It moves in slow caustic nets across the black rock and the green blades. At dusk it goes amber and horizontal and the whole plateau glows from within, each bladder a small lantern, each grass blade edged in fire. At night, moonlight does the same in silver. There is no darkness here that is complete; even on moonless nights the water holds a faint green phosphorescence from the plankton disturbed by your passing, a brief wake that fades behind you over a minute.

The rules, once you have been here a while, are simple:

Up is everything. Everything grows up, floats up, reaches up toward that bright ceiling. Even the dead do not sink far; they are caught in the fronds and composted in place.

Sharp stays, soft spreads. The rock does not give; life must go around it, over it, through its cracks. Nothing rounds the stone yet. In return, stone gives everything its shape — corridor, amphitheater, arch, well.

Slow wins. Nothing hurries. Growth is measured in millimeters a month. A holdfast that has found a good crack will keep it for decades. Currents that elsewhere would scour are here reduced to a breathing. If you stay still long enough, the forest closes over your trail and forgets you.

And high means near. Near to sun, near to rain, near to air, but still absolutely, completely under. You can look up and see clouds through water, distorted, close enough to touch but unreachable. You can lift your hand and break the surface and feel sudden cold rain on your fingers, then drop your hand back down into warm green and be home again.

It extends past what you can see on any one swim. To the east, the meadows widen into a plain that goes on for a mile or more, unbroken grass rippling in the tidal breath. To the west, the forest thickens into true jungle, canopy so interwoven the bottom is in permanent twilight and the sponges glow faintly. To the north, the block-valley and the chimneys, bare and black. To the south, the rim comes closer to the surface — in places a fin of lava almost breaches, and at low tide its tip kisses air for an hour, black and steaming faintly in the sun, covered in stranded bladders that pop as they dry, before the tide takes it back.

Town-sized, valley-sized, but with no town. No ruin, no rope, no bottle cap, no anchor. Nothing made. If you cut a blade it bleeds green and seals itself by evening. If you break a rock — and it takes effort — the break is clean and glittering and within a week filmed over with life starting again.

Stay long enough and your sense of elevation inverts. You stop thinking of this as deep. You think of it as high — a mountain meadow, only the air has been replaced by warm sea, and the deer by turtles, and the wind by tide, and the silence is not empty but full, leaf-thick, sunlit, patiently holding its breath.
