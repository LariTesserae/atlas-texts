You go down into it by ladder.

Not far. Three meters, maybe four, down a square shaft of hand-fitted limestone into a brightness you don't expect from underground. At the top, the courtyard is white noon heat and shouting; at the bottom, the sound changes. It gets louder and wetter.

The room is small. You can touch opposite walls if you stretch. Three long paces across, two and a half high, square, with a shallow-domed ceiling open at the center to the sky. That opening is everything — a timber grate of thick, adze-rough beams laid crosswise, lashed with dark hemp, leaving a square of blazing day about a meter wide. All the light in the world seems to be falling through that one hole.

And the water.

The floor is not floor. It is a basin. Knee-deep at the entrance step, chest-deep in the middle where the masons dished the brickwork down to a central sump. Everything here is made. There is no earth, no rock showing anywhere. Floor, walls, ceiling, steps: lime-plastered brick and cut stone, sealed with a fresh coat of pale plaster that hasn't yet learned to be smooth. You can still see the trowel arcs, the finger drags, the sharp inside corners where the plaster is a little proud. The chisel scallops are still crisp on the lintels. This place was finished last dry season. It is new.

Warm water fills it almost to the rim of the lowest step. Warm as bathwater, warm as blood, heated by the sun-baked courtyard stones above and by its own shallow containment. It comes in from four sides, day and night in this season. Four short clay spouts set high in the walls, mouths blackened by the potter's hand, each throwing a thick, clear jet in an arc that hits the surface with a white crash and keeps crashing. There is no pump, no machine. Only fall and gravity and a full cistern system somewhere uphill, feeding by slope alone. The level in this little cell breathes a hand's breadth up and down as clouds pass over the catchment.

The sound is the size of the place. In a room this small, four falling jets are deafening. A continuous roar, water on water, water on plaster, and underneath it the hollow gargle of the outlet — a low square spillway on the north side where the excess sheets out and drops away into the next dark culvert. Your own voice comes back to you off the wet walls, doubled, useless. You have to lean close to hear breathing.

Light does violent things here. The shaft from the grate is solid, dustless, full of spray. It hits the agitated surface and shatters into moving nets of brightness that crawl up the walls, across the ceiling, across your skin. Warm mist hangs chest-high and catches it. Where the jets cross the shaft you can see every drop, glass-clear, lit from inside. The plaster below the waterline glows green-white. It hurts to look straight up. The sky through the beams is a hard, bleached blue.

Nothing natural holds it. Even the steps down are masonry, five narrow treads with sharp noses that haven't been worn round. Along one wall, a low bench of stone, also plastered. On it, spaced far apart because there is so little need and so little room: a coiled hemp rope, a wooden dipper with a long handle, a tall clay jar with its mouth stoppered with cloth, a flat basalt stone for scraping. That is all. A lot of emptiness between things. The room is mostly water and light.

It smells of wet lime and hot stone and clean rain. The air is thick enough to drink. Sweat runs immediately and doesn't cool you; the water doesn't cool you either. You wade, and your movement sets up slow seiches that slap softly against the walls between the roars of the jets.

Life is almost not here. There are no fish, no frogs, no insects skimming, no rats on the beams — the lime is still too fresh, the water too warm and too constantly turned over, the light too fierce. The absence is noticeable. No buzz, no plip of anything living. Only what can cling: in the mortar joints above the waterline, a sparse beard of green — thin algae felt and a few pale root hairs nosing down through cracks from the weeds in the courtyard above, reaching blindly for the damp. Below, in the clear water, patches of olive film on the plaster where the current is weakest, waving minutely. That is the whole ecology. A green sheen and a few white threads.

It is always in motion. The four jets pock and crater the surface, the spillway pulls a slow clockwise drag across the whole volume, the sun shaft evaporates a constant veil that condenses on the underside of the beams and rains back down in fat secondary drops. If you stand still you can feel it: push at your calves, warmth stratifying — hotter at the top where the sun touches, slightly cooler at your feet near the inlet of night-cooled water from deep in the feed. Scrape the algae and it will be back in a week. Leave the dipper out and the plaster will blister under it. Everything is young and still settling.

The rule of the place is simple and manual. In must equal out. If leaves mat the grate, the light dims and the level rises. If silt clouds the sump, the outlet chokes and the step disappears. So someone comes down the ladder once a day with the basalt scraper and bare feet, bails with the dipper, clears the grate by hand, and climbs back up into the glare. No more technology than that: stone, clay, wood, rope, hands.

You don't stay long. The brightness and the noise fill the little box completely until there is no room left for thought. You are submerged to the ribs in warm, roaring light, held in a new-made stone cup just under the world, and above you through the beams the ordinary day goes on, unaware that this hidden pocket of water and sun is working, loudly, right beneath its feet.
