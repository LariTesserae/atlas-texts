There is no horizon here, only a lessening of dark.

You notice the cold first — not a bite, a seep. Cool air that stays on the skin and thickens. Each breath out hangs a moment before it is taken by the damp. The ground is low. You can feel it in your knees and in the air pressure, that you have descended into something held. A wide, shallow bowl a few miles across, rimmed by humps so weathered they are no longer hills so much as long exhalations of stone.

The rock is old in the way that has stopped trying to be dramatic. Basalt, shale, chert, all rounded down to the same patient gray-black. Pitted. Fluted where water once ran in sheets and now only condenses and finds its way down by memory. No edges. Even the broken boulders have soft shoulders, as if they have been handled for a very long time. Between them: grit, flakes, pebbles sorted by old water into rippled aprons, and then the flat itself — a lakebed that forgot to stay a lake.

There are no plants. Not a blade, not a moss cushion, not a green smear. The eye keeps looking for it and fails. What looks from a distance like vegetation is something else entirely: thin, dull films stretched over stone, mustard-pale and rust-orange and off-white, bacterial mats and cold-loving fungi that do not rise more than a fingernail's height. They follow the damp like a stain. They peel, they blister, they re-seal. When you step near — and you cannot step without stepping near — they give off a faint sour-sweet, iron smell, like blood on a coin.

Water is not absent, but it refuses to gather. It lives as behavior rather than as place. In the cool dark the stone sweats. Every upward face is beaded. The beads join, grow heavy, fall into pits no deeper than a cupped hand. There are pockets — you find them by sound, a different tick under the great noise — shallow pans between slabs where a skin of water holds the sky's nothing and reflects nothing back. In some, a slow seep bubbles up sulfur-milky. In others, rain from three days ago still waits, tea-colored from rock flour. No streams that hold. After a rain — brief, cold, needling, gone before you can look up — the whole flat briefly shines, a mirror broken into a million pieces, and then drains back into damp.

Because the world here is not empty. It is crowded past reason.

At first you think the ground is moving with water. Then you understand it is bodies.

They layer. On the stone film, a living grit: pale springtails and mites in such numbers they make the rock look like it is boiling, each no bigger than a sesame seed, lifting and falling. Over them, woodlice the size of thumbs, slate-gray and translucent at the joints, shouldering each other, antennae continually testing. Crabs — blind, white, long-legged — pick through tide-pools that are not tidal. In the pans, things wriggle: flatworms, black leeches knotted together, salamander larvae with external gills like red ferns, mouths opening and closing on nothing and everything.

And above, the air is also ground.

The dark hums and then it tears. Clouds of midges rise in pillars that bend and hold, tens of millions strong, so dense you can see them even without light as a thickening, a vibration against the cheek. They rise, they drop, they trade places with another pillar. Among them hunt the larger fliers: leathery, moth-big, bat-big, things between — silent wings until they are not silent. They click. Nightjars or what pass for nightjars here, mouths huge, scoop and scream.

Nothing is solitary. You cannot put a hand down without touching three phyla. A stone turned — if you turned one — would show its underside furred with eggs, with coiled millipedes, with beetles playing dead and then deciding not to. The density does not thin as you walk outward. An hour in any direction and it is the same press, the same near-touching carpet, valley-wide. Town-sized if a town were made only of hunger and breeding.

Because this is why it is deafening.

It is not one sound. It layers as the bodies layer, until air itself feels solid with it. Low: the perpetual wet rustle, chitin on chitin, a billion legs adjusting — like rain on dry paper that never stops. Mid: chirps, ticks, scrapes, the stridulation of crickets stacked chorus on chorus until the individual vanishes into a saw-toothed drone that sits behind the teeth. High: the needling whine of the midge pillars, the peeping of frogs from every pan — thousands of throats inflating at once, a glassy *pink pink pink* that does not synchronize and does not stop. And over all of it, tearing through at intervals, the shrieks of the fliers and the guttural croak-bark of something larger in the dark beyond, something that wades.

You feel it in the sternum before you hear it with the ears. The volume is physical. It makes the water in the pans shiver into concentric rings. It makes the beads of condensation on the boulders tremble and fall early. If you open your mouth, the sound gets in and fills it. Thought becomes difficult. You stop trying to listen for anything and let the noise hold you up like water holds a swimmer.

There is almost no light to check anything by. The sky is a low, unbroken lid of cloud, and whether it is night or day under it no longer matters. What reaches the flat is a thin, dirty diffusion, blue-gray, just enough to tell stone from pool from swarm. Your eyes never fully adjust; they keep trying and failing. So the other lights become law.

They pulse along the ground: cold green-blue points where the fungal mats are thickest, where crushed bodies ferment. Click-beetle larvae glow in the grit. The salamander things have a lateral line of dots. And when a flock of fliers banks through a midge pillar, their wing-edges catch and throw back a brief phosphor. It is not illumination. It is signaling in a place that has given up on the sun. Movement draws light. Stillness extinguishes it. You learn quickly not to chase the glows.

The rules here assemble themselves without being told:

Nothing grows up. Everything spreads sideways, low, pressed, film and carapace and wing kept close to stone because the air above is cold and eaten.

Water is not drunk so much as absorbed. Everything licks. The beetles lick stone. The crabs stand in seeps and lift their legs one by one to mouthparts. Even you find yourself licking your lips for the condensate, metallic and faintly sulfured.

Silence is the predator. Every few minutes — no rhythm you can learn — the great roar stutters. A wave of quiet passes outward from somewhere central, as if the whole valley inhaled. In that second, the midges drop an inch, the frogs cut, the rustle hitches. That is when the waders strike. You hear a splash too large for any pan, a crunch, a resumed screaming louder than before to cover the gap. Then the noise knits back over, deafening again, protective again.

And everything eats everything else, because there is nothing else to eat. The mats feed the grazers, the grazers feed the hunters, the hunters die and feed the mats. There is no waste here. A dead flier on its back is covered in thirty seconds, gone to a writhing mound in two minutes, a clean wing-case by the time the next rain needling passes. The rock itself is slowly digested and excreted and re-formed, pitted a little deeper each year. That is the balance: long-settled, weathered into equilibrium, neither growing nor eroding, just turning.

If you stay — and the cold and the press and the noise make staying both unbearable and difficult to leave — you begin to feel the strangeness not as spectacle but as texture. The chorus sometimes forms standing waves you can walk into and out of, pockets where a single tone cancels and your own heartbeat suddenly returns to you, loud. The stones, saturated with vibration, hum faintly under your palms, a sympathetic resonance. The swarms avoid your breath-heat not out of fear but as if you are a weather system, a brief warm front they route around. You are not observer here. You are obstruction, briefly incorporated, licked by condensation, landed upon, weighed.

Far off, toward where the rim should be, the dark stays dark. The flat extends, breathing, rustling, peeping, clicking, shrieking. No fire. No wall. No trail. Only the cool sweat of stone, the sour films, the crowded water-skins, and life piled on life so thick the mineral world underneath is only a rumor — and the sound, and the sound, and the sound holding the low place together.
