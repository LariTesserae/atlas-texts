It is first of all high and dim.

Not mountain-high, not a peak. Plateau-high. The kind of high where the air has thinned and gone blue at the edges and the horizon no longer behaves properly, but drops off too far away. You can walk for days and the ground stays at the same elevation — a broad, wind-planed table of old rock, perhaps three thousand meters up, perhaps more, large enough to make its own weather.

The light never gets over the business of leaving or arriving. A cold, low sun drags itself in a shallow circle around the rim of the world and never climbs. Everything is long shadow and pewter sky. Midday is like late evening elsewhere. Stones hold a thin blue edge on one side, a thin amber edge on the other. Your own shadow, when there is one, goes thirty feet out in front of you.

Underfoot, it is weathered to patience. This place has stopped becoming. The ridges are round-shouldered, the valleys shallow and U-shaped and full of sorted stones. Frost has been heaving and dropping the same ground for so long that the rocks have arranged themselves — circles of basalt cobbles, stripes of shale running downhill for miles, polygons you only see from a rise, perfect as cracked glaze. No sharp cliffs. No fresh scars. Wind and ice have had their argument here and settled it. The soil, where there is soil, is grit, iron-dark sand, shattered plates that ring faintly under boots and then settle.

It is a dry place that is never quite dry. That is the rule.

There is no ocean here, no river worth the name. The air itself is the water. Each night the cold wrings it out — a white fur of frost on every stone, on every lichen rosette, on your sleeves if you stand still. By the dim noon it melts, not into flow but into film. Rock faces go glossy. Hollows hold a skin of water an inch deep that mirrors the grey sky perfectly until the cold takes it again. Scattered across the vastness are the true pockets: kettle tarns, black and still, sunk in old glacial scoops, rimmed with ice that never fully leaves even in melt. Between them, miles of dry shattered pavement. You learn to read damp the way you read shade elsewhere — darker stone, greener black of lichen, a softness in the grit.

Vegetation does not cover. It insists, in places.

Mostly it is crust — map-lichen in sulfur and ash and dried-blood orange, grown directly into rock, a hundred years to make a coin. In the lee of boulders, in the cracks where melt lingers an hour longer, there are cushions: tight, hard moss, rust-brown and olive, cold to the touch and springy underneath. A few dwarf willows no taller than your ankle, their leaves the size of a fingernail, polished red by cold. They do not make meadows. They make constellations. From a ridge you see mostly stone, and then — where water has sat, where an animal has passed and melted and manured — a sudden bruise of green-black, a few yards across, then stone again for a long time.

Because the animals are the water, really. The animals are the industry.

There are no buildings here. No walls, no roads, no fences, no ruins. Nothing made, nothing stacked. If you saw a straight line from above it would be a game trail, worn by ten thousand years of feet into the same shallow trough across the plateau, braided and re-braided but never paved.

And yet everything runs like a factory district with the roofs taken off.

They come out of the haze in no hurry, and at first you mistake them for boulders. Low, barrel-bodied grazers, the size of musk oxen and larger — some bulls as big as small trucks — covered in long, matted, ice-clotted hair the color of shale and ash. There are thousands of them. There are millions of them, spread so thin across so much country that you can walk an hour between groups and still understand, from the dung, from the cropped lichen, from the prints that never quite fade, that you are never alone on this plateau. The density is sparseness itself. Much empty air between presences. But the sum is overwhelming.

Come closer. They let you, because they are slow and because cold makes everything economical, including fear.

You hear nothing. That is the strangest thing. A herd of that mass should thud and bellow and clash. Here, snow and matted hair and thin air take all of it. The world is muffled to felt. What sound there is, you have to kneel to find: the faint crunch of lichen being rasped, the soft tick-tick-tick of something cooling, a long exhalation through wide nostrils that instantly becomes fog.

For each animal is warm inside in a way that has nothing to do with fur. Put your hand near the flank — not touching — and you feel it radiate. Under the hair, in the deep barrel of the chest, there is a slow, deliberate chugging, a pump you feel more than hear through the soles of your boots. Piston lungs. Furnace gut. They take in the poor fare — lichen, moss, gritty meltwater — and process it at length, at great heat, with an efficiency that seems mechanical. Their breath comes out in plumes that do not dissipate but hang, white and dense, and join the low cloud that always sits a hundred feet over the plateau. Their dung steams for hours. Around each standing or bedded beast the permafrost softens into a dark damp halo, a few feet of mud and thaw where mosses seize their chance and grow lush. From the air those halos must look like a rash of green-black pox across grey stone — life making its own brief wetland and then moving on, leaving it to freeze again behind.

In the dimness some of them glow faintly at the throat and belly — not bright, but a deep ember-orange visible through thin skin where the hair parts, the color of banked coal. Old bulls drip a thick dark oil from glands at the jaw that freezes into black beads and rattles in their ruffs. Their wide, flat antlers, more like shovels than branches, collect static from the dry, cold air until on overcast afternoons you see tiny blue discharges crawling tip to tip, silent lightning, harmless. No wires. No generators. Just chemistry and friction and scale.

They move the way glaciers move. A herd will graze a single lichen flat for a week, advancing a yard a day in a broad front like a conveyor, shoulder to shoulder, heads down, steaming. Then they walk — five miles, fifty miles — to the next dark patch, in single file along the ancient troughs, calves in the middle steaming hardest to stay warm. Their migrations are not seasonal. They are multi-year circuits, vast loops across the continent of the plateau that take three, five, ten years to close, guided by memory of melt and by the smell of thaw. You could live your whole life here and see the same old cow come back to the same tarn only twice.

The sky belongs partly to them, too. Smaller things — white, silent — ride the heat. Flocks of moth-like grazers of the air, hand-sized, furred, with no mouths you can see, hang in loose veils over the herds, drinking condensation off breath-plumes, laying eggs in the warm mud of footprints. When a herd shifts, the veil shifts with it, a pale shimmer a mile wide, making no sound at all as ten thousand wings beat in cold air. Fox-things, low and grey as stone, follow at a respectful distance, waiting for the slow to falter. Even they do not bark. They cough once, softly, like a valve lifting, and are quiet again.

Everything is slow because heat is expensive. Nothing runs, nothing flies fast, nothing decays fast. A carcass will lie for years, freeze-dried under frost, picked at leisurely by beetles armored like tiny stoves, until the bones themselves, polished by wind, become another sorted circle for moss to colonize.

Stand still long enough in the dim blue and you understand the terms: Keep your warmth close. Borrow water and return it. Make no loud noise — sound is heat leaving the body, and the cold keeps accounts.

Far off, beyond the last visible herd, beyond the next tarn flashing like a coin, the plateau goes on. The cloud made of a million breaths thickens toward the center of it and lets fall a fine, needling ice that is not quite snow and not quite rain, that silvers the stones and melts on your tongue. There is weather out there no map knows, born from damp and dung-heat and low sun, circling slowly over stone that has long since decided what shape it wants to be.

No one built it. It runs anyway.
