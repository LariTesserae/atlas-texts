The plateau does not end so much as give up.

You come up out of the lowlands — switchback after switchback through thinning air — expecting a peak, and instead you find a flattening. A high, wide, wind-scoured tableland so old the mountains have forgotten how to be mountains. No ridges, no teeth. Just long, tired swells of ground rising and falling like breathing under a blanket, worn down over impossible time until even the rock is round. This is country at the end of its geology. Stone shows only where the skin has split: grey, lichen-pale slabs, soft at the edges, crumbling into grit that smells faintly sweet.

Because the skin is living.

Everything up here is plant. Not forest — forest implies trees standing *on* something. Here there is no *on*. The ground itself is a mat, three, four, ten meters deep, a sphagnum-moss-peat-felt so thick your boot sinks and rebounds. Under it, the old rock. Over it, everything else. The cool air, thin at this height, holds the daylight clean and ordinary — a high mid-morning white that should make things simple. It doesn't.

From the rim you can see the whole open bowl of it, valley-sized, town-sized: a shallow dish three or four kilometers across, filled with green architecture.

At first you think they are trees. They are not trees. They are stalks. Pillars. Leaves the size of warehouse roofs. Vines as thick as culverts that rise a hundred meters and then nod under their own weight, splitting into fans that rattle in the wind like sails. Between them — impossibly far between them, because for all this abundance the place is sparse — stretches empty moss prairie, trembling, unbroken except for drip-pools.

Sparsity is the rule here. A stalk here. A half-kilometer of nothing but breathing moss and cold air. Then another stalk, leaning. Then a cluster of broad, waxy leaves cupped upward to catch the sky. Life has not filled in the gaps; it has decided gaps are part of the structure. You can walk twenty minutes and hear only your own feet squeaking on wet felt.

And then the town.

It is not built *in* the green. It is built *from* the rhythm of the green. Low, wide, rust-and-black industrial town strung along the hollows where the mat is firmest. Brick sheds, boiler houses, riveted iron water towers, terraces of workers' cottages with slate roofs gone mossy at the seams. Copper pipes run everywhere, sweating condensation. Brick chimneys cough pale smoke that is torn sideways instantly by the plateau wind. At a distance it looks dense, organized — streets, sidings, a rail spur with little hopper cars full of chopped fiber, a power station humming. Up close you understand how thin it is: one street, then a hectare of empty green, then a factory standing alone like a ship, its windows lit amber, then nothing again. The city never managed to become continuous. The plant wouldn't let it. Or it didn't want to.

Because the plant is the substrate, the quarry, the enemy, the god. They cut nothing live — that's the first law, stenciled on every engine house in flaking white: CUT ONLY THE BROWN. The town lives by pruning. The stalks grow insanely fast in the cool damp — a shoot can add a meter overnight after rain — and if left alone they'd tip, collapse, rot the mat, drown the town in green rot. So the factories run day and night: trimming saws, pulping mills, fiber presses, oil-renderers that squeeze lamp-oil from seed pods big as bathtubs. The whole industrial purpose is not to produce for elsewhere. It is to keep the place from swallowing itself. Bales of pale fiber stacked by the rail siding, endless. Rolls of leaf-paper. Nothing is exported anymore — the switchback road washed out decades ago and nobody rebuilt it. They just keep making, stacking, burning the surplus for heat.

Water is not a river here. There are no rivers left; erosion finished with rivers a million years ago. Water is weather and breath. Most mornings the cloud sits *in* the valley, not above it, and everything drips. Condensation runs off every leaf-edge in silver strings, pocks the moss, gathers in the cup-leaves into cold black mirrors a meter across — pocket ponds, temporary, full of drowned pollen and nothing else. By noon it's gone, sucked back into air or plant or the town's gutters. The town drinks from the sky: every roof feeds a cistern, every copper pipe weeps into a bucket. You hear it everywhere — plink, tick, gurgle — under the bigger noise. Occasional rain comes sideways, hard and brief, smelling of iron and chlorophyll, and then stops as if shut off. Damp, not wet. Enough to keep your cuffs cold, your breath visible till ten.

It is cold. Not bitter. The cool that lives in shade even when the sun is out. High-altitude cool that makes metal ache to touch and keeps the moss springy. Your nose runs. The boilers feel obscenely warm when you pass them, exhaling yeast and hot oil.

Listen: it is loud. That surprises everyone. An empty green place should be quiet. It is not. The wind plays the big leaves like drums and loose rigging — a low booming flap-flap-FLAP that you feel in your sternum. The stalks creak, deep structural groans as their fibers take the load, wood-on-wood sounds from things that are not wood. And under and through it, the town never stops: the 6 a.m. mill whistle that you hear inside your teeth, the clank of the trimming cranes, the endless chuffing of the pruning engines on their narrow rails as they crawl out to a leaning stalk, the power station's 50-cycle hum that makes puddles shiver. Even at night the pressure-relief valves sigh.

Almost no animals. That's the other wrongness. A place this green should crawl, buzz, sing. It doesn't. No insects in the air except a rare, confused moth bumping a lamp. No herds. Once, someone tells you, there were sheep. They ate the mat and died of the alkaloids. Now there are crows — six of them, everyone knows them by name — and a one-eyed cat at the pulping mill, and humans. Humans count, presumably. But you walk for an hour and see no one, then round a leaf-stem and find a whole crew in oilskins, silent, winching a cable around a brown sheath, shouting in hand signals because voice is useless in the wind.

And then the breakdown.

Because normal light should mean normal space. It doesn't hold here.

The first time you notice it, you think it's mist. You look down the main street — Furnace Row, a straight run of cobbles between the boiler house and the company store — and at the end of it you should see moss prairie. Instead you see Furnace Row again, from above, as if reflected. A second shift of workers crossing a bridge you know hasn't been built yet. If you blink, it's gone.

The old-timers don't blink. They say the plateau is thin. That when you live inside a living thing that old, that big, that has grown and died and grown over itself for longer than rock has held shape, time doesn't stack properly. Effects happen under the mat. Echoes surface.

Rules accumulate, practical as work boots:

Don't follow the sound of your own footsteps if they fall out of sync.

If a door in a stalk is open that was closed yesterday, don't go in. It's not your day's door.

Count the crows. There should be six. If there are seven, stay indoors until the whistle blows twice.

The stalks do things. A cottage built in the axil of a leaf ten years ago is now forty feet up, lifted as the plant grew, its chimney still connected by a stretched, singing copper pipe that the maintenance men lengthen every spring. Stairs grow overnight — not built, grown — pale root-stairs spiraling up a cut stump, perfectly treaded, gone soft by winter. In the pulping shed, one wall is no longer brick but living fiber; they stopped scraping it because the wall heals cut pipes and keeps the room at 60 degrees without coal. At night it sweats a faint phosphorescence, and the night-shift workers read by it.

Distance stretches. You can see a pruning engine half a kilometer away across the moss, hear its diesel cough as if beside you, and shout to its driver who will not hear you at all. Sound pools in hollows. Light bends around the big stems so that their shadows fall the wrong way, toward the sun. In the cup-leaf ponds, sometimes the reflection shows cloudless sky when it's raining.

Yet life goes on, actively, pragmatically. Morning condensation crew drains the cup-leaves with siphons before they get heavy enough to snap. Noon trimming crew takes the brown sheaths down with long saws that scream. Evening fiber crew feeds the mills. Night pressure crew walks the pipes with hammers, listening for blockages that sound — they swear — like breathing. Weather cycles through in a day: mist, sun, sideways rain, frost glittering on the mat at dusk though it's summer. Biological pace is slow and then all at once: nothing for weeks, then a stalk fruits overnight into a stinking purple lantern the size of a house, and the whole town turns out in masks to cut it before it bursts and sours the cisterns.

Standing on the firm moss at the town's sparse edge at dusk, with the mills lit and humming behind you and kilometers of empty, luminous green breathing cold in front of you, you understand the compromise: an ancient, flattened height, too cold and damp for anything but this one relentless plant, and a handful of people with engines and boilers and copper, spread too thin to be a city, too stubborn to leave, pruning back total life just enough to keep a place to stand, while space quietly comes undone around them — and the leaves applaud, and applaud, in the wind.
