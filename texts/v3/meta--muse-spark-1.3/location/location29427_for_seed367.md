On the top of the world, where it has worn itself flat.

You walk for days across that high country and it barely changes. It is old in a way lowlands never get to be. No peaks, no cuts, no drama left. Just a wind-polished swell of rust-red and ash-grey stone, rounded like old molars, dust drifted in every shallow. The rock under the dust is soft when you scratch it — ancient basalt gone mealy. It powders under your boots. Every stone you turn has been turned by wind for a million years. Nothing runs here. No creek, no seep, no well. The sky takes whatever rain there is before it lands.

Then the ground hollows under your step, and there is the hole.

From above it is nothing. A dark slot no wider than a doorway, rimmed with polished stone and a fringe of grey-green beard. If you didn't know to look you would step over it. The wind goes quiet over it. Warm air comes up out of it, smelling of dust and feathers and old ammonia and something green and living.

That is the whole place. Everything is inside that.

---

You go down feet-first, on a ladder of knotted cord and bird-bone rungs, polished black by ten thousand hands. The shaft is short — seven body-lengths — and then it opens and you are not outside anymore.

The Hollow is a single room. A blister in the old lava, maybe forty paces across, egg-shaped, tall as three tall houses stacked. You could shout from one wall and be heard too clearly on the other. You could stand in the middle with your arms out and feel that the place is full.

The light is all from the one eye above you. A single slanting column, thick with dust, falling all day from the slot to the floor. It never quite reaches the edges. Around it is a permanent amber dusk. The walls curve away into brown shadow and they do not stay walls — they are furred.

That is the first wrongness that becomes right: how green it is in a place with no water.

There is no pool, no drip, no stream. The air is dry enough to crack your lips in an hour. The stone floor is dry powder and packed dung-brick. And yet the walls are lush. From every crack and pocket hang curtains of air-plant, grey-green and sage and dust-olive, in beards three feet long, swaying though there is no wind. Shelf-fungus stacks in overlapping plates the color of liver and cream. Fat waxy rosettes cling in the dark, leaves thick as a thumb, storing what they can. Velvet moss packs the ledges. It is a jungle that drinks nothing you can see.

It drinks breath.

Twenty thousand bodies breathe in here day and night and the air never clears. It stays warm — blood-warm, compost-warm, warmer than the cold plateau above has any right to be. The walls sweat invisibly, just enough. The plants comb it out of the air with hairs and gills. The fungus eats what falls. And what falls is constant.

Because the Hollow is not empty. It is not even mostly human.

It belongs to the sooties — small cliff-swifts, soot-black with white throats, that pack the upper dark in a living crust. Shoulder to shoulder, beak to tail, chittering, shuffling, ten thousand of them and then ten thousand more in the season. When one shifts, a hundred shift. When a hundred lift, the air fills with dry wing-thunder. They come and go through the eye in ribbons at dawn and dusk, out to hunt the high-air plankton over the flats for fifty miles, and return with their crops full of midges. Below them, paler things own the lower dark: barn owls fat as cats, blind-white harvestmen with legs as long as your hand picking over the moss, thick-bodied crickets, carpet-moths in clouds around the light shaft, and the quick grey rock-rats that no one admits to feeding but everyone feeds.

You hear it before you see any of it properly. The Hollow is loud the way a hive is loud. Not a single noise but a saturation. The high needle-chitter of swifts layered over the dry papery roar of wings when a flight goes out, the soft *thup thup* of moths hitting the light column, the constant scratch and rustle of claws on stone, the owls' low huff at night, and underneath it all the human sounds — grinding, coughing, a child crying and being hushed, rope creaking, someone sweeping dust that will be back by evening. It echoes a little, folded back on itself by the curve, so you are always inside the sound. There is nowhere quiet to stand.

And lashed to all of this, like nests among nests, is the town.

It is not on the floor. The floor — a trampled oval of dust, guano ash, and dropped feathers — is for threshing and drying and keeping the few long-legged goats that never leave. The town is vertical. Around the walls, in three ragged rings, are platforms of woven wattle and dung-mud brick, held up with bone struts and braided cord anchored into the old soft rock. Rope bridges sag between them. Ladders hang everywhere, swaying when the swifts burst. Storage pits are scraped into the thickest guano beds and lined with stone. Every home is one room, low, front open to the warm dark, hung with drying strips of fungus and braided onions of air-plant bulbs and cages of live crickets clicking.

There are perhaps sixty families. You can walk the whole of it in a minute if the bridges are clear, which they never are. You duck under someone's washing — long strips of beaten fiber — you step over a sleeping dog, you press shoulder to shoulder past an old woman scraping guano cakes for fuel with a shoulder-blade hoe. It is dense in the way a cliff colony is dense. Human ledge, moss ledge, swift ledge, fungus ledge, alternating. Near-touching. Layered. High-saturation. Everything uses everything else's leavings.

The work here is all by hand. Chipped stone, bone awls, fire-hardened digging sticks, cord twisted from the tough beard-plant fiber. Metal is a rumor — a few beaten copper trinkets kept from grandmothers. No wheel would fit. No plow. The tools are for scraping, cutting fungus before it spores, harvesting fallen chicks without disturbing nests, twisting cord, mending. The great technology of the Hollow is knotwork. Knots that hold in soft rock. Knots that give when a thousand birds take off at once and the whole town sways and settles.

Nothing is wasted because nothing comes from outside except what the birds bring in their bellies and what traders lower down the shaft twice a year — salt, flint. Even the dead birds are gathered each morning from the floor in baskets. Even the dust is swept for nesting down.

The rules are not written. They are breathed, like the air.

No fire in the day, when the air is thickest with down and moth-dust and would catch. Cook only at night in the three hooded hearth-pits on the floor, with dried dung-cakes that smoke more than flame, and watch the shaft.

No loud noise at noon, when the chicks sleep and the owls hunt and the whole crust goes still and you can finally hear your own heart. The town goes whisper-quiet then, and even the rats move softly.

Take only the fallen. Never reach into a live nest. If you need fiber, take the beard-plant from the sunniest edge where it grows fastest. If you need meat, trap rats on the floor, never owls, never sooties except the dead ones.

And keep the eye clear. Once a season the young ones climb out with scrapers of bone and clean the rim of dust and seedling and bone, so the light and the birds can still come through. If the eye closed, the Hollow would die in weeks — the column gone, the moths gone, the plants paling, the heat souring. Everyone knows this the way you know to breathe.

At dusk you understand why they stay in a room with twenty thousand birds and no water.

The light goes honey-gold and then rose. The column tilts and lays itself across the hanging gardens so every beard and moss-cushion lights at the edge and glows with trapped dust. The evening flight returns — first a few scouts dropping through the eye like stones, then the ribbon, then the flood, wings drumming the air until the rope bridges hum. Feathers snow down through the light. Moth-clouds rise to meet them. An owl slides silent across the column. Below, on the platforms, lamps of rendered rat-fat are lit — tiny, dim, many — and the town becomes a second colony, chittering softly as the upper colony settles. Goats stamp. A baby laughs. Someone sings low to cover the birds.

The stone is warm through your sandals from the composting depth below. The air is warm in your throat. Your skin is powdered white with dust and down. It itches. It smells. It is overwhelmingly, intimately alive.

Outside, a mile in any direction, there is nothing but old wind and flat stone and a dry sky going cold with stars.

Inside this one hidden pocket, no bigger than a house-barn, the walls sweat breath, the plants drink air, the birds bring the outside world home in their guts, and people, with string and bone and patience, have built themselves a city in the fur.
