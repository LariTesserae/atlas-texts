You find it by sound first, not by sight.

Low ground. Cold low ground where fog doesn't lift so much as thin, and then thicken again. The land sags here — a shallow, unremarkable dip in black peat and sedge — and then, without announcement, it opens.

Not a cave. Not big enough to be a cave. A pocket. A room the earth kept for itself, maybe fifteen strides across if you could walk it, which you can't. A fresh wound: the roof of a shallow water-lens collapsed last thaw, or the spring undercut it. The edges are still sharp. That is the first thing you notice when you crouch to look in — how unsoftened everything is. Flakes of basalt and shale standing up like broken teeth, unlichened, unrounded. Pale fresh scars where blocks sheared away last year or last week. There is no soil profile, no smoothing. It is young in the way a cut is young.

You have to lie on your belly on the wet moss to see inside, and then lower yourself down through the slot where the light goes.

Light doesn't so much enter as leak. A dim blue-grey shaft from the slit above,maybe two meters long and less than a meter wide, and then diffusion, and then dark. Your eyes take a long time. The air inside is a full ten degrees colder than outside, cold that gets into the teeth. Your breath comes out and does not dissipate. It joins the air, which is already saturated — condensing atmosphere. Every surface weeps.

That is the rule here: everything sweats water.

The walls weep. The ceiling weeps. The sharp black rocks bleed in slow beads that swell, tremble, and drop. The moss below drinks and drinks and cannot drink enough, so the floor has become marsh in miniature. Sphagnum hummocks, liverwort slick, a few starved ferns unfurled tight as fists. Water near surface everywhere — press a thumb and the ground sighs up brown-green water around it. Post-rain, but it is always post-rain here. Even when it hasn't rained in days outside, in here it is raining.

Because the rock breathes.

You hear it before you understand it. A deep, cyclical chuffing, too regular to be wind. *Huh — huh — huh —* and then a rush. The pocket is plumbed into something larger — a siphon, a meltwater throat, an air trap in the peat. On an inhale, air is drawn down through the slot, cold and smelling of iron and wet stone; the hanging drops shiver sideways. On an exhale, mist is forced up from the black pools at the back, in white production-line puffs. Three seconds in, three seconds out. Three seconds in, three seconds out. You can set your own lungs to it without meaning to.

It works like an engine with no one tending it. No built thing here at all — no wall, no path, no cut timber, no rust, no nail. Untouched. And yet the whole place labors. Water hammers somewhere under the floor in a buried channel, knocking at intervals: *tok-tok-tok-TOOK*. Drips fall from thirty different heights and strike stone basins tuned to different notes, but all together they make a mill-noise, a factory-noise, loud in so small a room that your ears ring with it. It is amplified by the hollow. A drop the size of a pea hits and sounds like a bolt dropped in a tank.

The stone itself hums. Put your palm flat to the weeping wall and you feel it more than hear it — a low 50-cycle tremor that climbs up the bones of your forearm. Electrified air. Before a rain the hairs on your arms lift. There is a metallic taste, ozone and crushed rock. In the dimmest corner, where the shaft light does not reach, the mist sometimes takes a faint St. Elmo flicker, blue, there and gone. Not light enough to see by. Just enough to know the air is charged.

At first it looks empty. That is the trick of the scale.

Standing — crouching — in the middle, you think: nothing here but rock and water and a little moss. Vast space between presences. A hummock here, ten handspans of black mirror-pool there, a single fern there. Hollow. Near-void. The air between is huge for a room so small.

Then your eyes adjust, and the void fills from the bottom up.

The lushness is low and total. Every hummock is a forest if you get close enough. Sphagnum in greens so saturated they look warm despite the cold — lime and bottle and deep bog-red. Threads of sundew, still awake, their glue-beads holding the condensing fog. Fungal fans white as paper on the underside of the fallen slab.

And the fauna. God, the fauna. Abundant beyond all proportion to the room.

Midges in a standing cloud that never leaves the shaft light, cycling up and down in shifts, thousands of them, wings making a high saw-edge over the bass of the water. They move like parts on a belt — rising, drifting sideways, dropping to lay in the pools, rising again. On the pools themselves, springtails pepper the surface, grey-black, flicking clear in unison when a drop hits. Under the overhang, white spiders, eyeless or nearly, strung between sharp points on lines beaded with water. And salamanders — cold-pink, translucent-bellied, four, five, seven of them — flattened in the shallows, gills fanning in time with the breathing of the room. They do not flee. Cold has slowed them to a deliberateness that looks like contemplation.

Everything here has learned the machine rhythm and built its life around it. The spiders rebuild only on the exhale, when the air is still. The midges swarm thickest just after the big drip from the ceiling — you learn to wait for it, that fat drop that gathers for a full minute at the central stalactite-nub and then falls with a *plock* you feel in your sternum. The salamanders feed when the water hammer knocks silt up.

It is active, ceaselessly active. Nothing rests, even in cold, even in dim. Water condenses, gathers, falls, drains, is pumped back by capillary up the moss stems, evaporates into fog, condenses again. Predator and prey in a space you could cross in six steps. Birth and death scaled to droplets.

The unusual part — the slightly wrong part — takes a while to notice. Water that cold should skin with ice. The pools are clear. The weeping beads never freeze, even when your fingers go numb and the peat outside crunches with frost. And the sound, for all its loudness, has no echo of you in it. Shout, clap, splash — the room eats it. Only its own engine-noise returns. You are a visitor the acoustics refuse to record.

If you stay long enough, your shoulders start to ache from hunching. The slot above has gone from grey to darker grey. The cold has moved from your knees into your hips. The room keeps working — inhale, mist up, knock-knock-knock in the underchannel, midges turning in their unending shift, spiders mending, moss drinking.

It does not need you. It was not made for you. No one made it at all. It made itself, recently, sharply, and it runs itself, wet and cold and loud in the dark, a tiny untouched industry of water and breath.
