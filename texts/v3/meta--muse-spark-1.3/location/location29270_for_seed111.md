It is not a mountain. It is a room high up where a mountain used to be whole.

You come up and up until the air goes thin and sour in the lungs, until each breath has to be thought about, until the slope below has fallen away into cloud and there is nothing under you but black distance. 4,800 meters, 5,000 — the number stops mattering. There is only the last col, a bite taken out of the ridge, maybe forty strides across, maybe less. You could walk its rim in two minutes if you could walk at all here. The walls lean in. That is the whole place. A small place with edges. On three sides the drop. On the fourth, the fresh scar.

The rock is too new to have a name. A winter or two ago — a blink — a whole face sheared off in frost and fell. No soil, no softening. What is left is unweathered, blue-grey when you touch it, paler where it broke yesterday. Shale and schist in thin plates, granite underneath like bone. Every edge is still a knife. The talus at the bottom hasn't learned to be round yet; the shards lie as they landed, tilted, interlocked, hissing faintly when your boot shifts them. If you put your hand flat you can feel the mountain still adjusting, a slow tick, tick, deep inside, too low to be sound. Young rock. It has not decided what shape it wants to be.

There is no light here to see it by, not really. It is night and the col sits in its own shadow, cut off from moon and town. Starlight only, and a last grey-blue leak of alpenglow that never quite leaves the highest snow. Your eyes never fully adjust. Shapes stay as pressure more than image: the blacker black of the back wall, the slightly less black of the air. Close up, things emerge by touch and by sheen — a wet glint on stone, a pale lichen crust catching what little photon there is. Darkness is not empty. It is thick, cold, close.

Cold, but not the killing cold. Just above freezing. The cool that makes your fingers stupid and makes your breath hang. You breathe out and the cloud does not dissipate; it hangs, it joins the other breathing. Because the air here is damp in a way high places shouldn't be. No rain now — there was spit an hour ago, needle-fine, gone before it wet you — and no stream. Larger dryness all around: scree dust, thirsty wind. But in here, water persists in pockets. Condensation. Every breath of every living thing condenses on the cold plates and runs together into beads. Seeps bleed from a horizontal crack halfway up the east wall, blackening the stone, feeding three, four little cups in the rock, no bigger than a palm. They are full. They are always full, shielded from wind and sun, replenished drop by drop. You hear them before you see them: tick — plink — tick — in the hollows.

Almost nothing grows. That is the first lie the place tells you: that it is empty. If you crouch and let your forehead nearly touch stone, you find the sparse tenants. Crust lichen, black-green and burnt orange, tight as paint in the pits. A few cushions of moss in the lee cracks, hard as felt, hoarding a teaspoon of soil that blew in. Two or three tufts of hair-grass, white at the tips from frost, trembling. That is all. No shrubs, no trees to break the wind. Vegetation is an afterthought, a rumor.

Then the rock moves.

Not the rock. What covers the rock.

They are packed in so tight you mistook them for texture at first. Thousands. Layer upon layer in the dark amphitheater, shoulder to shoulder, chest to back, wing over wing. Small high bodies built for cold and thin air — you would call them birds if birds could be this quiet with their bodies and this loud with their mouths, you would call them bats if bats had round feathered faces and forward eyes that catch starlight like wet seeds. They are something in between, or something after: soot-grey down, pale bellies, long hind legs for clinging to vertical shards, forelimbs that are both hand and wing, folded. Veilwings, cliff-mice, night choughs — no name fits. They carpet every ledge, every tilted plate, every crack wide enough for a fist. In the cups between stones they have poured themselves like liquid, two, three deep, breathing together. Dense. Near-touching is not close enough; here touching is the rule. The bowl is lined with living felt that shivers and sighs.

And it is loud. That is the second lie corrected. From a distance you heard wind. Up close it is them. Not song — work-noise. A continuous dry rustle of thousands of small adjustments, claw on slate, feather on feather, like rain on paper. Under it a clicking, chittering layer, echolocation ticks and contact calls, need-need-need, bouncing off the close walls and coming back multiplied until you cannot tell direction. Every few minutes a ripple goes through: a hundred take off at once without leaving, half-open wings beating the thin air into a hollow roar that fills the col and pressurizes your ears, then settle again. And through it all, water: drip, lick, drip. They drink constantly.

This is why the place is damp. They make it damp. They farm condensation with their own heat. The back wall is warm — unnaturally warm — from packed bodies. Warm air rises an inch, hits cold stone, gives up its water. The stone sweats. They lick it.

Watch longer — let your eyes stay dark — and the strangeness surfaces.

They are all facing inward. Thousands of faces turned toward the center of the bowl and the little pools, even those on the outer rim with their backs to the drop. They move slowly, so slowly you think at first they are torpid with cold. A head turns over the course of a full minute. A wing unfolds like a glacier. The pulse of the whole colony is patient to the point of geological: a wave of shivering starts at the lowest, wettest tier and travels upward around the bowl over an hour, a shared shudder to keep the edge from freezing. You can time your own breathing to it if you stay still enough, and then you are part of it whether you want to be or not.

And they have hands. Not quite hands. But they use things.

No nests, no walls, no built anything — there is not a single stacked stone here that was stacked on purpose, no human mark, no shelter. Untouched except for use. A veilwing on the seep-crack picks up a thin flake of shale in its wing-claws, holds it like a knife, scrapes lichen from the rock with three short, practiced strokes and eats. Another dips a curled sliver of bark — carried up from impossibly far below — into a rock cup to sip where its beak cannot reach, lifts, drinks, passes the tool to the neighbor, who uses it. Down by the pools, two are chipping at a pebble, tapping it against a larger anvil stone to refresh its edge: tok, tok, tok, barely audible under the roar, but deliberate. Early, simple, borrowed. Nothing is made to last. When the tool dulls or the thirst is gone it is dropped, left among the shards. Hand-tools without workshops. Craft without buildings.

They notice you the way a lake notices a stone. A ripple of clicks intensifies, focuses, then loses interest. You are too big, too warm, too clumsy to eat and too cold to be a threat for long. The rule here is not territory. The rules are older and smaller:

Stay in the bowl. Outside is fall and wind and nothing to cling to.
Share heat. No gaps. A gap is death that spreads.
Water belongs to whoever licks it first, and then to the next.
Do not keep tools. Use and drop.

If you put your ear close to the stone, under the chitter, you hear the other sound the place makes — the one that makes it strange. The bowl returns echo wrong. A click comes back a half-beat late and lower, as if the rock learned it and repeated it in its own voice. Old climbers would say it is just the shape, the fresh concave plates focusing sound. Lying there with your cheek near feathers, feeling the slow shudder climb through your ribs, it feels more like the mountain is practicing, using thousands of small throats to learn how to sound.

Dawn will not reach in here for hours, if it reaches at all. The cloud below will brighten first, then the ridge, then at last a grey finger will touch the highest shard and the beads of water will flare white. The colony will not leave. They will tighten, sip, scrape, pass the wet bark from hand to hand, and breathe. Change here is glacial. The scar will widen by a millimeter. A lichen will grow over a dropped tool and claim it. A moss cushion will take another teaspoon of dust.

You will leave before you understand, because the air is too thin to think and your fingers have gone numb around your own breath hanging in front of you, joining theirs, condensing on stone, becoming for a moment part of the water they will drink.
