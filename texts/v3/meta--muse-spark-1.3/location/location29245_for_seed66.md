You come up to it slowly, because there is no other way to come up.

The road climbs for a day and a half out of the low haze, switchbacking up black rock, and the air gets thinner but not colder. That is the first wrongness you notice. Higher should be colder. Here it stays warm — bath-warm, skin-warm — a held warmth that comes up out of the stone itself. By mid-morning your shirt sticks to your back. By noon the rock on either side of the road is weeping.

Not running. Weeping. Water beads out of the basalt in a thousand dark eyes and trickles down, joining, sheeting. The cuttings drip. The old retaining walls, three meters thick and softened by centuries of weather until their corners are round as loaves, sweat through their mortar. You hear it before you see the city: water ticking off stone into ditches.

Then the shelf opens, and it does not end.

The plateau is vast in the way a sea is vast. A high, almost-level table of dark volcanic rock lifted above the world, long enough that its far edge makes its own horizon, wide enough that clouds are born over its middle and die before they reach its rim. It has been here long enough to stop being jagged. The ravines that cut it are broad and U-shaped, their sides slumped and terraced by time. The wind and the water have had their way so long they are no longer carving, just maintaining. Everything stone is rounded, pocked, patient.

And on that stone, covering it like lichen on a boulder, is the city.

It is not a city that rises. It is a city that layers. From a distance you see no single skyline but a texture — low, dense, stratiform — roofs, bridges, cisterns, chimney-stacks, funicular towers, aqueduct ribs, three, five, seven stories deep in the ravines and two or three on the flats, all the same weathered colors as the rock: iron-black, ash-grey, tuff-brown, rust-red, and everywhere whitewashed by droppings to a chalky glare. Factories with sawtooth roofs shoulder bathhouses. Machine halls shoulder dovecotes ten stories tall. Pipe bridges cross over street bridges cross over water channels. There are no trees. There are no gardens. There is no green at all to break it, and after a while your eye stops looking for it.

Nothing green grows here. It is too high and too mineral and too used. What would be soil is either paved or scoured to bare scoria. What would be a weed is burned back by mineral steam and soot and the sheer scraping traffic of feet and claws. In the dampest chinks there are films — mustard-yellow and copper-green bacterial slicks, black crusts a millimeter thick that flake like old paint. On the north side of cisterns a pale salt-fur blooms. That is the only vegetation, if you can call it that: not plants, but stains of life that need no soil and no sun, only water and warmth and time. The place is otherwise wholly mineral: basalt and concrete and iron and brass gone green-black with wet, volcanic glass set as aggregate in the oldest walls glittering when the light hits.

The light does hit. The sun here is normal, high, white-gold, undramatic. At noon it stands almost straight up and lays short hard shadows under every eave. It is bright enough to hurt after rain. But it is almost never clear. The city breathes up a veil — evaporate from a million wet roofs, steam from cooling sheds and dye works and powerhouses, breath. By early afternoon the veil thickens into low cloud that sits on the rooftops like a lid, sweats, and falls again as drizzle. Then clears. Then forms again. The city makes its own weather and then lives inside it, wet and warm and close. Your skin never quite dries.

Water is everywhere and it is not wild. It is conducted. Every roof is a catchment. Every street is cambered to a central rill. Every alley ends in a cistern throat. The ravines carry the larger flows in stone flumes two men high, brown-white with foam, roaring faintly under iron grilles. You hear water under everything: ticking, seeping, dripping from chains, gurgling in downspouts made of hollowed lava. The builders long ago gave up trying to keep it out and learned to give it paths. Walls are built double, with a weep-cavity; on humid afternoons the outer faces cry in sheets and the gutters sing. Children set paper boats in the street-rills. Old men clear grates with hooked sticks, unhurried, a civic priesthood. The rule is: water goes where it wants, you only suggest.

Under and over the water-sound is the other sound, the loud one: the work.

A low, continent-deep hum that you feel in your molars. Engine sheds, mill shafts, stamping floors, dynamos, the cable-drums of the funiculars that haul ore and slag and night-soil up and down the ravine sides. Whistles at shift-change. The clank and sigh of pressure valves venting steam in white columns that go straight up fifty meters before flattening into the cloud-lid. Metal on metal. And through it, piercing it, riding it — animals.

For this is an animal city. In the absence of plants, they have taken everything.

First the birds, because you cannot miss them. White river-birds with long curved beaks and black-tipped wings, by the hundred thousand, nesting on every ledge, every chimney pot, every pipe elbow. At dusk they come in from the marshes far below in rivers across the sky so dense they dim the sun for minutes at a time, calling — a high, reedy, almost metallic *kree-kree-kree* that layers until it is a sheet of sound. They roost shoulder to shoulder on the dovecote towers, on the temple cornices, on the factory roofs, white as snow, shifting, muttering.

Then the moths. Great ash-grey mill moths the size of your hand, drawn to the heat and the lamps, that by day cling under every eave in furred mats, breathing together. When a steam valve blows they lift in a choking cloud and settle again. Their caterpillars — fat, blind, brick-red — live in the warm dark of the compost vaults and grease traps, and are harvested by the sack.

Below, on the ground: cavies. Long-eared rock cavies in ochre and slate, wild and tame at once, in warrens under the foundations, pouring across plazas at feeding hour when the mill-women throw out spent grain and starch-cake. Wire-rats, lean and clever, running the pipes like blood in veins. Feral dogs asleep in the warm culverts. Goats with bells on the steepest ravine paths, eating paper and ash. And in the cisterns and flumes, pale eyeless fish and fat newts and clouds of midges that rise at evening and are eaten in turn by bats — small, fast, numberless bats that pour out of the expansion joints in the aqueducts and flicker over the water.

The air smells of it: guano and wet wool and hot oil and mineral steam and baking starch and rain on hot stone. It is dense, saturated. There is no empty volume here. Every cubic meter is occupied — by pigeon on ledge on pipe on wire on moth on steam. Even the sky over the plateau is occupied, layered with wings and smoke and cloud.

And yet for all the noise and crowding, the place moves slowly. That is its strangest rule, once you live in it a week.

Machines turn, water runs, wings beat — but nothing hurries. Iron rusts at its own pace and is patched, not replaced. Walls subside a finger's width in a lifetime and are shimmed. The black crusts grow a millimeter a decade. The cavies follow the same paths their great-grandmothers wore shin-deep into basalt thresholds. The great annual cycle is slow and massive: in the wet months the cisterns fill and the birds breed until the whitewash runs ankle-deep in the gutters; in the dry months the flumes drop and the city lives off stored water and stored grain and stored heat, and the moths sleep. People mend nets, re-lay pipes, re-point weeping walls, oil the same engines their grandfathers oiled. No one builds new outward — there is no outward left. They build inward, denser, a niche here, a loft there, another dovecote on top of a powerhouse.

If you stay long enough you learn the etiquette, which is animal etiquette. Do not block a goat path. Do not cover a weep-hole. Do not shout at shift-change; the birds are louder than you. Leave the wire-rats their pipe — they keep the grease traps clear. Sweep your roof for the cistern-men. At dusk, when the bird-rivers come home and the bats go out and the steam columns turn pink and the whole vast shelf hums and drips and cries under its self-made cloud, stand still. Something will land near you — moth, cavy, white bird with mud on its beak — and look at you with complete indifference, as one resident to another temporary resident of warm stone.

There is no forest to walk in. There is no meadow. There is only this: a warm high table of old, kind, weeping rock, covered to the horizon with layered work and wings, loud, wet, crowded, and utterly, patiently itself.
