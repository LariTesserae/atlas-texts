On first look it is empty.

A high pan, wide as a slow day's walk in any direction and then wider past that, lifted up into hot thin air. Not a desert of dunes. A desert of set things. Long ribs of dark basalt and rust-red mineral stand up out of a pale floor like the bones of something that stopped moving a very long time ago and decided to stay.

The floor is salt and iron and volcanic glass ground down to a pavement. Polygons of white and ochre salt lock together for kilometers, their edges lifted into sharp curls. Between them, drifts of copper-green dust. Wind has worked here for so long it no longer carves, it polishes. Every stone is vented, fluted, settled into its own final shape. Yardangs — long whaleback ridges ten, twenty meters high — lie all pointed the same way, east to west, as if a single hand combed them. You can see past them for miles. Nothing blocks the view except heat.

The light is ordinary. That is the strange part. A white-yellow sun, high, noon-like even when it should not be, giving true colors. No red dimness, no glare-white void. You can see the grain in the salt. You can see your shadow pooled black and short under you. The air is hot — not damp hot, not furnace-blast hot, but high, dry, electric heat that makes the distance swim. It smells of hot metal and ozone and struck stone. Breathing it feels like breathing inside an oven left open to the sky. There is no water here. Not pooled, not seeping, not remembered in a crack. The chemistry is dry: salt, rust, charge.

There are no plants. Not even the pretense of them. No lichen crust, no spine in a shadow. No walls, no roads, no ruins, no posts, no wire. Nothing built. The ground has not been touched by hands.

And it is loud.

At first you think it is wind. It isn't. Or it isn't only.

The ground thrums. Deep down under the salt pan, a slow piston beat: *tuh — tuh — tuh —* so low you feel it in your molars before you hear it. It is notregular the way a machine is regular. It speeds, it misses, it catches again a minute later. Around it, a higher roar comes and goes — air forced through the fluted yardangs until the whole field of stone becomes an organ. Each ridge has a different tone. Walking between them, the pitch slides. And above all that, a crackle, a constant dry sparking, as if the air itself were shorting out.

This is where the emptiness stops being true.

The sky is full of animals, and then — in a blink — fuller.

They are not birds. They are not insects, though from far off you will mistake them for locusts. They are large, hollow things, each the length of your forearm to the length of your body, made of amber chitin thin as foil and veined with black iron. Their wings are not feathered but vaned, like cooling fins. Their bellies are empty chambers that catch the hot updraft and hold it. They ride thermals without flapping, thousands upon thousands of them, turning in a slow gyre over the pan that is wider than any town.

When they bank, the sun catches them and the whole gyre flashes copper at once. That is the overwhelming part — not that there are many, but that there are so many they become weather. Their shadows stipple the white salt below until the ground moves. Their wing-edges rasping together make a sound like a mill town heard from a hill: a shirring, clattering roar. Individually they are almost nothing — paper-light, hollow, vast space between one body and the next. Collectively they darken the day.

They do not drink. They do not graze. There is nothing to graze.

They eat stone and air and heat.

Watch one low pass: it skims the pan, mouthparts unfolded into a wide mineral sieve, scooping salt dust and iron oxide and glass shards. Inside its thorax you can see it working — a visible, mechanized gut. The dust goes in pale, is sorted, crushed, smelted by internal heat. Sparks jump between plates in its abdomen. Every few minutes the animal extrudes a perfect pellet from its rear: a machined bead of pure copper, or iron, or glass, thumb-sized, still glowing dull orange, which pings as it cools on the salt and is left behind. No nest. No store. Just production, endless scaled production as the gyre turns.

The pan behind the swarm is littered with millions of these beads, scattered like ball bearings, never piled, never assembled into anything. They are not structures. No one builds with them. The wind eventually buries them. The next gyre uncovers them. The animals are factories that make no product for anyone — engines with no vehicle, industry with no building.

There are larger ones too, much higher, that you only see when the heat lets the sky go clear for a second. Slow mantas of the same amber foil, fifty meters across, barely moving, their vast wings rippling once every minute. They trail filaments that discharge lightning sideways into empty air with a whip-crack that arrives too late. They are patient beyond biology. They have been circling the same thermal since before the yardangs took their shape.

This is where the world stops holding still.

Because the swarm is not only where it is.

Look at the eastern horizon: a second gyre, faint, inverted, turning the other way above a ridge that isn't there anymore when you blink. Look up: for a heartbeat there are two suns, one slightly behind the other, and your shadow throws a double edge. The sound comes before the cause. You will hear the whip-crack of a high manta a full three seconds before you see the filament discharge. You will hear the rasp of wings swell before the gyre banks.

Distance misbehaves. A yardang a kilometer off looks close enough to touch, its flutes sharp as cut paper. Then heat takes it and it smears ten kilometers away. If you walk toward the center of the pan, the piston beat under your feet does not get louder, only slower, as if depth were time.

And the animals duplicate. Not reproduce — duplicate. Stare at a single low flyer crossing a salt polygon and sometimes its afterimage does not fade. It persists, solidifies a meter to the left, banks with it. For a while there are two where there was one. Then one peels upward to join the gyre and you cannot tell which was first. The old prospectors who have a word for everything would call it a breakdown. Here it is just the rule: motion is conserved, but position is negotiable.

Nothing changes fast here, despite the noise. The swarm has been turning for decades, perhaps centuries, in the same slow gyre. The beads accumulate and are buried at the rate dust falls. The basalt ribs lose a millimeter a year to the polishing wind. The high mantas ripple their wings once a minute and have not otherwise moved. It is loud the way a distant waterfall is loud — constant, impersonal, not an event but a condition.

If you stay until the light thins — and it thins without reddening, just a normal daylight going pale at the edges — you will see the whole pan begin to spark. Static lifting off every salt edge, blue-white corona on every copper bead, the bellies of ten thousand hollow animals glowing faintly from within as they smelt the day's last dust. No fire. No water to quench it. Just heat becoming work becoming heat again.

Do not look for shelter. There is none and none is needed. Lie down between the salt curls and feel the piston in the earth miss a beat and find it again. Listen to the factory with no walls make its perfect, useless beads. Watch the sky be too full and the ground be too empty at the same time, and hold both, because that is the place.
