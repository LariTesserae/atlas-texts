You come down into it without quite noticing you are descending, because it is not high anywhere to begin with.

This is low country. Flat fields, grey-green, cut with ditches that are always full. Then a ring, a low berm of old spoil grown over with drowned grass, and inside the ring a shallow bowl — no more than a hamlet across, you could stand in the middle and see its whole rim — and everything inside the bowl is wet in a way the fields outside are not.

Not a lake. Not a river. Wetter than that, and more distributed. Water is not *in* a place here, it is *on* everything. The ground gives under your boot and sighs water up around the tread. The air itself is at the edge of rain without raining. It condenses on cold iron, on brick, on your sleeves, on the underside of leaves and catwalks. The low rock-and-clay walls of the bowl — old quarry walls, long weathered, slumped to their own angle of rest — weep. You can see the dark streaks where they never dry. Every few seconds a drop gathers, swells, lets go.

And yet almost nothing grows.

That is the first strange thing. With all this water you expect reeds to the chest, willows. Instead the floor is mostly open: black peat-mud, puddled, scuffed, crossed and re-crossed with narrow-gauge rails half-sunk and shining. Clinker and crushed brick. Smoothed concrete aprons with water sheeting over them in a skin. There is vegetation, but spare and punished: a fringe of sharp reeds around the rim where the ground is a hand higher, a few birches no taller than a person, white with lichen and leaning, one hawthorn bent double by wind. Everything else is moss — thick, sound-swallow moss on the north sides of things, on fallen sleepers, on the footings of sheds — and a thin algae slick that makes the mud olive and rust-red in patches. The established shape of the place has already been decided. Rills have cut where they will cut and now run clear and round-edged. Brick has softened. Iron has thinned. Nothing is raw here, even the ruin is comfortable in itself.

The buildings are scattered and low, as if dropped and left where they fell. Three brick sheds with slate roofs gone green-black, windows blind with condensation inside and out. A smaller transformer hut humming so low you feel it more in your teeth than hear it. A covered coal stage, empty now except for puddles. Between them — no, around them, over them, under them — the works.

Because this is still an industrial place, even if no one is here today.

Pipes run everywhere, in layers. Low pressure mains big around as a barrel, rusted orange and weeping at the flanges, running at knee height on short pillars. Above them smaller black pipes, above those conduits for wire, cable trays, a belt drive still turning, inexplicably, between the middle shed and a pump pit. Valves like ship's wheels, clustered so close you have to turn sideways. Gauges with clouded glass, needles trembling. Catwalks of checker plate slick with water, stairs down into sumps where the water churns black and brown. In the center, the pit itself: a rectangular sump, revetted with timber gone soft as sponge, where three intakes gulp.

It is dense here. Not forest-dense. Clutter-dense. In any cubic meter at waist height there are four or five presences touching or nearly touching: pipe, chain, hook, dripping eave, your shoulder, a curtain of condensing drops. Under the eaves they have hung tools and spares so thick they clink softly when the wind moves them — wrenches, gaskets, lengths of chain, a row of buckets all full of clear water. Saturation without lushness.

The light is ordinary, which makes it stranger. Flat cool noon light through high cloud. No drama. Colors true: rust orange, brick red gone brown, peat black, water grey reflecting grey. Cool enough that your breath shows faintly when you stand still, cool enough that the metal bites your bare hand, but not cold. The kind of cool that keeps things from rotting fast and from drying ever.

Animals mostly stay out. Too wet, too oily, too interrupted. You will not see a flock or a herd. If you wait long — and the place encourages a muffled waiting — you might see the one moorhen that works the rim ditch, picking its way with enormous feet, or eels moving like slow thoughts in the sump, or the print of a fox in the smooth mud by the coal stage, already filling with water. That is all. The ecology is microbial, chemical: iron bacteria making rainbow oil-slicks that aren't oil, exactly; algae; midges in a low column over the warm outflow where the cooling water comes back out almost hot and steams.

Ah, the movement. This is not a still place, though it is a quiet place.

It is turbulent. Hyperactive, even. Nothing stays as it was a minute ago.

While you watch, a float-switch somewhere clicks — a sound no louder than a tongue-click — and No. 2 pump catches with a shudder that travels up through the soles of your feet. The sump level drops visibly, a vortex forms, sucks, breaks with a soft *glup*. A sluice on the far side lifts by itself on its screw, and brown water comes sheeting hard across a concrete apron, carrying leaves and grit, finding the low channel, rising, falling. A pressure relief vents on top of the middle shed: a white burst of steam that leans, thins, is gone in ten seconds, leaving only dripping and the smell of hot oil and wet brick. The belt between buildings jerks, sags, resumes. Mud on the wall slumps an inch and plops. A puddle that was mirror-calm skins over with rain-rings from a drip above, though it is not raining.

All of it with almost no sound. That is the rule here: water muffles, moss muffles, cloud muffles. The big machines are well-kept and water-driven and electric; they do not roar, they turn. What you hear, if you hold your breath, is a lattice of tiny wet sounds: tick of cooling metal, plink plink plink from three different eaves out of sync, seep-hiss through gravel, suck of your own boot coming free, the low electrical hum you are not sure you hear, the soft clink of hanging tools. A gull cries far away over the marsh and it sounds as if through wool.

Stay a little longer and you notice the unusual thing you couldn't name at first.

Water behaves a fraction wrong. Not dramatically. The sheets running down the quarry walls slow near the bottom and creep sideways, against the slope, toward the iron brackets, as if attracted. Condensation gathers heaviest on the *undersides* of pipes, fattening until the whole pipe wears a beard of drops underneath and is almost dry on top. Reflections in the standing puddles are a half-second late — you lift your hand and your dark double lifts his a beat after. No one maintains this now except visits: a grease-pencil mark on a gauge dated three weeks ago, a lunch wrapper weighted with a nut, footprints that come in, do a circuit, and leave. The place runs itself, keeping the bowl from becoming a pond, pumping the marsh-edge back out to the ditch, breathing steam, shifting water from one pit to another for no witness.

Rules, as you learn them with your feet:

Step only where it is already firm and shining. Dull mud will take you to the ankle.

Do not touch a wheel that is trembling. It is about to move by itself.

Do not expect silence to mean stillness. Everything here is happening at once, just quietly.

If you stand long enough under an eave, you will be dripped on, regularly as a clock, cold on the neck. The place marks you, briefly, and then goes back to its work.
