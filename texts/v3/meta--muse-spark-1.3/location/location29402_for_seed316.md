It goes on until going on is the only direction left.

This is not a valley or a plain. Those words imply edges. This is a peneplain in the oldest sense — a continent that has finished becoming itself. Wind and water have had so long to work that there is nothing left to wear away. Hills were worn to swells, swells to wrinkles, wrinkles to a slight thickening in the mud under your foot. Elevation is a rumor. You could walk for a hundred days and gain or lose no more than the height of your ankle. The earth has settled into its final form: almost perfectly flat, almost perfectly level with the water that made it.

And it is warm, and it is dim, and it is drowned.

The light never gets above late afternoon, and never gets below it either. A low, thick, unbroken cloud sea hangs a few hundred meters up, the color of wet parchment and bruised apricot, lit from nowhere you can point to. The sun is a wider smear behind it, a warm stain that moves but never sharpens. Everything is lit as if through gauze and steam. Shadows are short, soft, indecisive. Distances eat themselves. Ten kilometers away and ten meters away have the same damp amber gloom.

The air is saturated. It is not raining exactly — though it rains every hour — it is condensing. Water hangs. Your skin never dries. Sweat beads and stays. Breath comes heavy and sweet with rot and warm stone. Each inhalation tastes of iron and algae. The cloud base sweats continuously, exhaling a fine warm drizzle that is neither mist nor rain but something in between, and then, without warning, a real rain will hammer down in a white column a kilometer wide, march sideways across the flats, and dissolve into drizzle again.

Under that sky: stone. Packed stone to the horizon in every direction.

Because when a land gets this old, the fines wash out and what remains is the lag — a pavement of pebbles and cobbles worn to eggs and loaves, each one touching the next, no soil showing between them for continent-stretches. Billions upon billions of them, ochre and rust and liver-brown and grey-green, polished by an eternity of water. You cannot put a foot down without stepping on three at once. There is no room between presences. Kneel and you will see: each stone sits shoulder to shoulder, fitted by flood, and in the hairline seams between them, water wells up. The water table is a finger's width below the world.

So the whole vastness weeps. In every dip between stones a bead swells, merges, becomes a film, becomes a pool. Shallow pans the size of a palm, a table, a house, a village — thousands, millions of them — lip to lip, separated only by the domes of stone. From ankle height it looks like dry cobble. From standing height you realize you are walking on a mirror smashed and re-fused: sky in every puddle, stone in every seam. Wet shines everywhere. Weeping rocks. The stones themselves sweat beads that run and join the pools.

It is loud. That is the first thing your body understands before your mind catches up.

A rain like this on a surface like this makes a sound that is not weather but place. Each droplet strikes stone or shallow water with a tick and a ring, and there are so many droplets, so many pools, that the ticks fuse into a single continuous white roar — deafening, physical. You feel it in your molars, in your sternum. Under it, deeper: the click and clack of stones settling as water lifts and drops them microscopically. Under that: a low seismic sigh as the saturated earth beneath shifts, sighs, takes water. And above it all, almost constant, a dry tearing in the clouds — thunder that never quite resolves into a bolt, just rolling pressure that circles the sky and comes back from another direction. There is no silence to be found. Even when the rain pauses, the pools go on ringing from the drips off the sky, and your own ears ring to fill the gap.

Nothing here is still, though from far away it looks like the stillest place on earth. This is the turbulent part. The flats make and unmake themselves at dizzying speed if you watch correctly.

Watch a pool: rain pits it white, wind skins it, algae greens it in an hour, a fresh surge from below clouds it brown, it overflows its stone rim in a hair of water, joins its neighbor, doubles, then a brief sun-scald of heat evaporates its edge and it splits into three smaller eyes again. Watch the stones: heat expands them by day, cool contracts by night, water jacks them, they tick upward, tilt, roll a centimeter with a soft knock you can hear if you press your ear close — and over a year that centimeter becomes a meter. Whole fields of cobbles creep. Ridges rise overnight and flatten by noon. Mud boils up between them in slow domes that burst with a plop and release a breath of warm rotten-egg air, then sink. In the distance, dust and spray and low cloud braid together and a water-spout or a dust-devil of rain walks slowly across the mirrors, trailing darkness.

Vegetation has almost given up, and what remains is stubborn in the way of old things. No trees. No grassland. Only in the seams where a little silt has lodged: clumps of black rush, wiry as cable, tipped with a single bead of water that never falls. Low cushions of saltwort, grey-green and waxy. Sheets of algae — olive, blood-red, black — stretched like wet leather across the stones, slippery, tearing easily and regrowing by evening. Occasionally a lone, stunted tamarisk-like shrub, wind-combed flat, its roots spread impossibly wide and shallow, clutching a hundred stones at once. Everything is low. Everything hugs the wet. Nothing wastes height on a wind that would strip it.

Animals are rarer still. You can walk all day and see nothing move but water. Then: a white crab, blind, big as a hand, wedged between cobbles, filtering mud. A wader bird, impossibly tall and grey, standing in a pan a half-kilometer off, standing so still you think it is a post — and when you blink it is gone, with no splash, no wingbeat. At dusk, things call from below: a deep, hollow *bloop* from flooded cavities under the pavement, as if something large turned over in its sleep. You never see it. Old footprints, three-toed and wide as dinner plates, appear in soft silt after rain and wash out before you can follow them. Life here is not absent, just thin, intermittent, unwilling to be counted.

People were here, or something like people. Scattered, faint. That is all.

Every few kilometers — which in this flatness might be every few hours or every few days, distance lies — you find a stacking. Someone gathered the lag stones, the egg-stones, and set them one on another: a low beehive no higher than your chest, doorless, already sinking, water pooling at its base. Or a causeway of larger slabs laid across a particularly deep maze of pools, now mostly drowned, its line still visible as a slightly darker straightness in the water. Or a single post of petrified wood, with a rag tied to it that has become a living thing, stiff with salt and algae. No tools. No metal, no glass, no wire, no cut timber. Only stone on stone, balanced by weight. Some of the cairns have fallen and been restacked wrong, as if by water, or by something that did not understand hands.

Do not trust them to navigate by. That is one of the rules of this place.

The other rules you learn fast:

Do not trust reflections. In dead calm between rains, the pools go glassy and show a sky that is not the sky above you — clearer, darker, with stars in daylight, or with two smeared suns. Your own reflection is often a half-second late. If you wave, it waves after you have lowered your hand.

Do not trust distance. The flatness and the wet air lens the world. A cairn that looks an hour away can be reached in ten minutes, or never. Sometimes you walk toward it and it stays exactly as far, while the pool-pattern around you repeats. Turn around and your backtrail of disturbed stones is already smoothed, filled with water.

Do not stay still in low spots when the sound changes pitch. When the roar goes higher, thinner, water is rising from below, not falling from above. The pavement can lift and float for a moment on its cushion of mud — stones, pools, algae and all — and then settle a handspan sideways with a long grinding sigh you feel through your soles. It will not hurt you if you are standing. It will take your footprints and your sense of north.

And do not expect night to bring quiet. Night here is warmer, if anything, the cloud holding the day's heat close. The dim amber drains to a deep iron blue, and the water holds the last light long after the sky has gone dark. Then the reality-slip gets worse. Horizons double. You will see a second, faint horizon floating a meter above the true one — an afterimage of an older flat, perhaps, when the sea stood higher. Lights move out there that are not campfires — slow, low, phosphorescent smudges that drift against the wind and go out if you look directly. The thunder becomes almost continuous, a low rolling around the rim of the world, and the rain, when it comes in the dark, is warm as blood and so dense the air itself seems to crowd you, droplet against droplet, no empty air between.

This is a world at the end of erosion and at the beginning of something else — saturated, packed to bursting with water and stone and sound and weather, too full to hold any one shape for long. Its vastness has its own physics. Storms are born in the middle of it and die in the middle of it, having never seen a coast or a hill. Water goes up and comes down and comes up again. Stones breathe. The sky sweats onto the earth and the earth sweats back.

You walk, and your feet are wet, and your ears ring, and the mirrors break and heal around each step, and ahead of you, always, the same impossible crowded flatness extends — stone to stone to stone, pool to pool to pool, world without seam, going on until going on is all there is.
