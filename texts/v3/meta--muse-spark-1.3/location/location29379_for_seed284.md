You don't find it by looking up. From below there is only the mountain — a white-shouldered, black-ribbed massif that goes up until the air gives out. From the air there is only wind-scoured ridge. The pocket only exists if you are already on the very top edge of the world, lungs burning, and put your boot wrong.

The ridge is knife-fresh. This whole high country is young. It hasn't learned to be smooth yet. The rock broke recently — geologically yesterday — and the edges still bite. Basalt and raw andesite in plates and teeth, frost-split but not yet tumbled, stacked like shattered glass. No river has ever worked here. No water works here at all. The air is so dry and thin your lips crack in an hour and your nose bleeds without noticing. What should be snow at this height is sublimated away by wind into dust; what falls as ice is quarried before it can melt. The scree slopes are not rounded pebbles but splinters. Everything leans inward.

Then the ground stops.

Not a valley. A slot. A blister. A gas bubble in the summit that collapsed inward when the mountain was born, leaving a vertical wound maybe two hundred paces long and half that across, a hundred meters deep, hidden in the spine of the ridge like a secret room. From ten paces away you would swear the ridge was solid. You step to the lip and the earth drops out from under your eyes.

Down in there — wedged, crammed, layered to suffocation — is the town.

It should not fit. That is the first rule of the place: everything fits because it has to. There is no sprawl because there is nowhere to sprawl to. So they built up and across and into. Houses of hammered stone footed straight into the cliff, then second stories of corrugated tin and black timber and riveted boiler-plate, then bridges thrown from wall to wall, then ladders, then ropeways, then rooms hung under bridges. Balconies overhang voids. Stovepipes bristle like fur. Cables stitch the gap in a dense cat's-cradle, sagging with pulleys and buckets and hanging lamps. At the bottom, where light barely reaches, the forges and mills and winch-houses sit shoulder to shoulder, breathing heat into the cold.

Because it is cold. Not killing cold, not polar. A permanent high cool that lives in shadow. The sun, white and small in a dark indigo sky, clears the eastern tooth for maybe two hours at midday and then is gone. The rest of the time the pocket lives in blue dusk, lit amber from below by its own making. Clouds are far, far underneath — a white sea you could fall into for a mile. Up here the sky is thin and the stars don't quite go away even at noon. You breathe and you can't get enough, and every breath tastes of stone dust and hot oil.

No green. Not a blade, not moss. The rock is too fresh and too dry and too high. Even lichen hasn't bothered. What color there is is mineral: rust-red, sulfur-yellow staining, graphite black, the pale grey of fresh fracture. And then the town's colors pasted over it — tar black, oxide red paint, brass, copper verdigris on wires, the orange blink of furnace doors. If anything grows here, it is built: crate-stacks, barrel-stacks, drying racks for cloth and meat and machine parts.

And yet it crawls with life.

Not people first. Animals. Thousands of them. In a place with no plants and no water, you expect emptiness. Instead the walls move.

The cliff swallows — not swallows, something smaller and high-altitude, dun and white — pour in ribbons from cracks every few minutes, thousands at a time, folding and unfolding without a cry. Below them, on every ledge not occupied by a human foot, sit the pikas and rock-rats, fat on spillage, woolly with cold, shoulder to shoulder in the crevices, watching with black-bead eyes. The dump terraces boil with mountain finches and choughs, red-beaked, hopping between slag and bone. In the mill houses, clouds of ash-moths live their whole lives in a single warm night around a single bulb, hatching in the felt insulation, dying in the oil pans. Feral dogs, barrel-chested and silent, trot the high wires of bridges with the confidence of cats. Everything lives off the town's leak — heat, crumbs, grease, breath-moisture. The town is the meadow. The town is the watering hole that has no water.

The machines never stop, but you would not know it by ear.

That is the strangest thing when you first climb down the iron staples into the smell of it. From the rim you see frenzy: buckets screaming along cables, porters running doubled-over with ore sacks up staircases that are almost ladders, smiths pulling white sheets from rollers, children darting across planks with armfuls of coal-bricks, whole wall-sections being torn down and rebuilt in an afternoon after a rockfall took a corner. A bazaar lane halfway up the west wall — three planks wide, roofed with canvas — is shoulder-to-shoulder barter, hands flashing, scales swinging. Rockfall is routine: a sharp crack from above, everyone looks up, steps sideways, a refrigerator-sized block smashes a shed to matchsticks, and within the hour the timbers are back up in a slightly different configuration. Creation and destruction as weather. Anthill energy. If you stand still you will be walked over.

But it is quiet. Unnaturally quiet for industry.

Partly the air — too thin to carry. Shouts die ten feet out. Hammer blows land as dull thuds in the chest rather than rings. Partly design: everyone lines their boots with felt, their machine feet with straw and rubber, their doors with rags, because sound in the pocket bounces and multiplies and drives men mad in the night. The big diesel winches have been converted to electric hum. The forges sigh rather than roar. Even the animals have learned it: the birds wheel without calling, the dogs don't bark, the rats squeak above hearing. What you hear instead is close and tactile — the creak of rope in a pulley next to your ear, the clink of a buckle, someone's breathing labored beside you, the tick-tick-tick of cooling metal, the low, almost subsonic thrum of the generator pit vibrating up through the soles of your feet. Mostly quiet, but never still. Your body hears it even when your ears don't.

Atmosphere pools. Because there is no water to wash the air, dust hangs — rock flour, soot, iron filings — glittering in the lamp light. It smells of ozone and hot stone and animal musk and rendered fat. Breath condenses instantly on any cold plate, and they farm that: every roof gutter leads not to a cistern but to a rag, every chimney collar wears a tin beard of frost-melt where breath and steam have frozen and been wrung out by hand. Water is not a landscape here. Water is a ration, a rumor, a thing carried up in sealed drums on men's backs from a glacier two days down, drop by drop, never spilled. Machines are air-cooled and oil-bathed. Throats are perpetually sore.

At night — which is most of the time — the pocket glows from within. Strings of caged bulbs, furnace mouths, welding flashes blue-white. From the ridge above, if you didn't know to look straight down, you'd miss it entirely; the light is shuttered by the rock, swallowed by dust. Only a faint warm shimmer rising from the crack, like heat off a stove.

And if you stay a day, you notice the slightly wrong thing. The birds, when they wheel, all bank at the same instant, as if the air itself tilted. The lamps, when the generator surges, do not flicker out of sync but breathe together. Rock that falls always falls inward, toward the town center, never outward off the mountain, as if the pocket were pulling. The old porters shrug: high places have their own rules. Stay low when the wind sucks. Never build out, only in. Never shout. Never waste a breath.

Down in the hidden room on top of the world, dense as a hive in a stone lung, they hammer and haul and rebuild and feed the moths and the rats and each other, cool to the bone, dry as salt, in the dim amber dark — turbulent and teeming and almost silent — while a mile below them the clouds go on burning white in the sun they never get.
