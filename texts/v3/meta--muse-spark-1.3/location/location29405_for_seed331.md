You walk in from the higher dry scrub and the ground simply gives up trying to be high.

It is a pan — not a valley, not a plain, a room. You can see all the way across it in one look, rim to rim, maybe three hundred paces. A shallow bowl worn so old it has forgotten how to have edges. The hills around it are low and tired, like sleeping animals, and inside, everything is level.

The rock underneath is ancient. Not jagged ancient, not mountain ancient. Finished ancient. A pink-grey granite that has been rained on for so long it has gone soft to the eye. Every boulder is a loaf. Every outcrop is rounded at the shoulder, pitted with solution cups, grooved where water has run for a million wet seasons. There are no cliffs, no scree, no fresh breaks. Even the gravel is round. The whole floor feels settled into its final shape, as if erosion has done its work and left it to breathe.

And it breathes water.

It rained an hour ago, or it will rain in an hour — here it is always just after or just before. The air is warm and thick and bright, white-gold light coming from everywhere, bounced off wet stone and low cloud and the sheen of shallow pools. The temperature sits on your skin like a damp cloth. You sweat immediately and the sweat doesn't dry.

Water is not in a river here. There is no slope for a river. Water is *near*. It seeps. The rocks weep it from their pores. Low domes of granite sweat beads that join and run down in dark trails. In every slight dimple in the floor there is a mirror: tea-colored pans an inch deep, a foot deep, edged with black mud and rust-red algae. Between them the ground squelches. Press your boot and water rises around it, clear and warm. The atmosphere itself condenses. If you stand still, droplets form on your forearms, on the hairs, on your eyelashes, without rain.

Vegetation is lush but lonely.

There are maybe eleven trees in the whole pan, and that is enough. Each one is huge and separate, far from the next, crown spread wide like an umbrella left open. Elephant-ear leaves, waxy and rain-polished, dripping steadily from their tips even when it isn't raining. Vines as thick as your arm hang and re-root. At their bases, ferns and moss make a deep green collar, almost black with wet. But between trees: open. Sun-bleached stone, wet mud flats, short bright grass in tufts, empty shining space. You can see the emptiness. It is part of the place. Nothing crowds.

What crowds is almost invisible until you stop.

The built things first — because you notice them as wrong trees. Three, maybe four, scattered with no pattern. Slender. Too slender.

One near the center leans at ten degrees and does not fall, a needle of matte grey metal or stone thirty feet tall and no thicker than your wrist, tapering to nothing. It should vibrate in the wind. It doesn't. Around its base the mud is dry in a perfect circle, though everything else is soaked. If you put your hand near it, the hairs lift.

Another is an arch, low, half-sunk in a pool, just two bent fingers meeting over the water. Under it, droplets hang in the air and do not fall. They hold station, a glittering curtain of suspended rain, trembling slightly. When a dragonfly passes through, the droplets part for it.

No doors, no lights, no writing. Nothing human-scale. They are remains, or they are still working. Every afternoon, the locals in the scrub will tell you, the cloud comes down into the pan though the sky is clear elsewhere. The air thickens and wrings itself out. That is not weather. That is *them*, still condensing, still keeping this one small room wet long after whoever made them left. The engineering is not in the shape. It is in the way weight and water behave wrong near them. The stones beneath one pylon ring faintly if you tap them, like tuned glass, though granite should thud.

You think the pan is quiet and empty. Then you crouch.

Every pool is alive to overflowing.

The water, clear from above, is black with tadpoles when you shade it. Finger-long, speckled gold, so dense they move as a single muscle, turning. Around the rim, thumbnail frogs the color of wet stone puff their throats without sound. Snails with striped shells the size of marbles bulldoze the algae. Water beetles row constantly.

Lift your eyes and the air is also full. Not with birdsong — with wings. Clouds of midges hang in pillars in the bright light over each pool. White moths, thousands, sit under every leaf and lift in a slow puff when a drop falls, resettle. Huge silent dragonflies, blue as bottle glass, hawk and hover. On the mud, a procession of copper crabs moves sideways from pool to pool. In the grass, something rustles continuously: skinks, rails, small striped rodents moving on errands.

If you stand for ten minutes, larger shapes come. Lanky wading birds with ash-grey necks step from behind a tree, one, then seven, then twenty, picking through the shallows so carefully they barely ripple. They do not call. A herd of something like small deer — mouse-brown, wet-nosed — emerges to drink, flicking ears. Turtles stack on a weeping rock.

It is overwhelming, once seen. Biomass upon biomass, layer on layer, all in this one small bowl. From the rim it looked sparse. Inside, life is packed into every wet inch, just low to the ground and soft-footed.

And it is quiet. That is the rule here that takes longest to understand.

With that much animal, there should be noise — croaking, chirping, buzzing. There is almost none. The thick warm air eats sound. The water absorbs it. And the animals themselves seem to have agreed to silence. The frogs open their mouths and only a faint click comes. The birds gesture with their beaks, flash the white undersides of their wings to signal. Even your own footsteps are muffled to a suck and sigh in the mud. What you hear, if you hold your breath, is water seeping, leaf-drip ticking into pools — *plik, plik* — the papery brush of moth wings past your ear, your own blood.

Predators listen here. Something — perhaps the shadow that passes occasionally over the sun without a cloud, too fast and too straight — hunts by sound. So everything lives fast and moves softly. That is the dynamism: not a storm, but a hundred small hungers cycling at full speed under a hush. Tadpole to dragonfly to bird to crab to heron-plume to mud again. Algae blooming green by the hour in the bright light. Pools shrinking by evaporation and refilling from rock-sweat and from the suspended curtain under the arch. Leaves unfurling, fruit dropping with a soft plop and being taken within minutes.

If you stay till mid-afternoon, you will see the cycle close. Light gets whiter, hotter. The air shimmers above the stone. Sweat runs down the granite domes. The needle-pylon seems to draw the moisture up. A low mist forms at knee height, then chest height, smelling of mud and green and iron. Droplets condense on everything. The suspended curtain under the arch thickens. Then, without wind, without thunder, it rains — only here, only inside the rim, a warm bright shower straight down for twenty minutes while the hills around stay sunlit. The pools pit and overflow into each other in brown threads. The frogs lift their faces. The wading birds stand still and let it wash them.

Then it stops. Steam rises. Light returns, brutal and clean. Drip, drip from the great leaves. Quiet again, but busier.

Do not try to be loud here. Do not try to build. The place has settled. The rock has found its shape. The water has found its level just under your boots. The old machines have found their rhythm. You are just another warm animal come to drink in the bright hush, stepping carefully between pools so as not to crush the overwhelming, silent multitude underfoot.
