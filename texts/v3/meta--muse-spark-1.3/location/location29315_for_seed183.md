You come up to it late in the morning, when the light is already too much.

The pass is higher than the map lets it be. Your lungs know before your eyes do — each breath thin and cold, a little sweet with stone-dust, not quite enough. Your ears pop and pop again. The air at this height has been scrubbed. No smell of soil, no green. Only wet rock and sun.

Below you the clouds are boiling up from the lowlands, white and slow, but you are above them. Above you the sky is not blue so much as bleached at the edges, a deep savage blue directly overhead that hurts to look at. The sun is a hammer. Even though the air is cool enough to see your breath, the light lands hot on the back of your neck. It bounces.

That is the first thing you have to learn about this place: the light does not behave like light. It pools.

The valley — cirque, basin, saddle, there is no good word — opens in front of you between two blunt black shoulders of mountain. From the pass it looks small, a stony lap you could cross in an hour. Then you take ten steps down and it gets larger. Then larger again. It is town-sized, valley-sized, but packed too tight, the way a thing looks in a dream when you get closer and it adds rooms.

It is crowded with stone.

Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. A forest with no trees.

They rise out of the wet ground so close they almost touch — pillars and teeth and stacked slabs, some as tall as a person, some as tall as a house, shoulder to shoulder, layered three and four deep, going back and back until distance whitens out. Dark rock, pale rock. Most are a glassy black basalt that shines when wet, and everything here is wet. Some are granite gone silver-gray with age, exfoliating in onion-skins. Some are redder, iron-banded. All of them rounded. Not jagged, not young. Rain and ice and wind have worried them for a very long time into soft shoulders, potholes, flutes, basins, balanced caps that should have fallen a thousand years ago and didn't. Established rock. Rock that has settled into being itself.

And weeping. All of it weeping.

Water is everywhere and nowhere still. It does not rain, exactly. It condenses. The air is so saturated and so cold against the sun-warmed stone that mist beads on every surface, on your jacket, on your eyelashes, on the underside of your hands. It gathers and runs. Every pillar has a dark wet skirt and a bright trickle. Every overhang drips with metronome regularity. From the black shoulders above, thin white veils fall hundreds of feet without sound — or rather, their sound is lost in the general roar. The ground between the stones is not ground. It is a shallow, braided skin of water over grit and shattered slate, ankle-deep, cold enough to numb. Step and the water clears, then milks over again. Potholes full of sky. Channels that split and rejoin around the bases. In the hollows, tarns no bigger than a doorway, so clear and so bright they look like holes cut straight down to the sky.

There is nothing living here to drink it. That is the second thing.

No grass. No moss. Not even the crust of lichen you expect on high rock. The stone is bare, scoured clean by water and light. What looks like green from the pass resolves, when you get close, into a stain of copper salts, a glaze of silica. What looks like orange fur is iron oxide blooming in the seep. The mineral world is imitating biology because biology never arrived. No birds cross the gap. No flies work the seeps. No marmot whistles. In a place this loud, the absence snags.

Because it is loud. You thought high places were silent. This one roars.

Wind comes up off the clouds below and is shredded by the stone forest into a thousand flutes and groans. It is a low organ note that never stops, with higher whistling on top. Under it, water: the hiss and clatter of a million trickles joining, the slap of veils hitting ledges, the gurgle in the undercut channels. And under that — if you stand still long enough to hear it — the stone itself. Ticking as the sun heats it. Pinging as frost in the cracks from last night lets go at noon. Once in a while, from somewhere deep in the crowd, a dull *tok* as a spall peels off and drops into a pool. No echo the way you expect an echo. Here the sound comes back wrong.

That is the third thing, and it keeps happening.

You clap a rock with your palm to test footing and hear the slap a half-second *before* your hand lands. You shout to hear the valley and the valley answers first, faintly, from the far wall, then your voice catches up. Your shadow at noon does not fall away from the sun. It leans toward it, short and black and too dense, pooling in the dips like the light pools. If you watch the mist between two close pillars, sometimes a drop detaches, falls — and then hangs, shivering, rising an inch in a gust that you do not feel on your face, before falling again.

Distance stutters. That tower with the double cap that looks a hundred meters off: you walk toward it, counting steps, and after twenty steps it is still a hundred meters off. After another twenty it is suddenly right in front of you, water running down a groove you could fit your arm in. And its groove, its crack, its chip — you have seen that exact pattern before, three towers back. Then again further in. The stones repeat, not exactly, but the way a sentence repeats when you are falling asleep.

Look up. The sky has seams. Under today's hard blue there is a fainter, older white, a yesterday cloud still caught, translucent, moving the wrong direction. The sun has a thin double, a ghost sun offset to the left that disappears if you squint. Light gathers in the stone basins until the water in them looks solid, like poured glass, too bright to look into. The edges of far pillars vibrate and go double and resolve.

You realize the density is not just the stones. It is the presences. Water, light, wind, stone — layered, near-touching, high and saturated. There is no empty volume. Even the air between pillars is full: spray, glare, sound.

And people have been here. That is the strangest and least strange part.

Scattered through the stone forest, using it, answering it: small works. Low dry-stone walls, knee-high, curving to break the wind around a flat sitting slab. Beehive huts no taller than your chest, built of overlapping slate without mortar, their doorways facing away from the prevailing gust. Cairns — hundreds of cairns — stacked with impossible patience from flat shards, some topped with a single white quartz cobble that flares in the sun like a lamp. On sheltered faces, pecked marks: spirals, cupules in rows that fill with water and overflow in order, ladder-like lines, hand-sized hollows polished by other hands.

No metal. No cut timber — nothing grows to cut. A hammerstone left on a wall, its end battered round. A pile of debitage, black glass flakes sharp as ice, where someone sat and worked. A ladder made of three long slabs leaned together. A causeway of flat stones laid across the wettest braids so you can hop without soaking to the knee, wobbling but holding. Everything handmade, hand-carried, heavy. Primitive in the way that means: you do it with your body or you don't do it.

No one is here now. Or rather, no one alive. The huts are empty except for water. One has a shallow fire-blackening on its back wall, old, washed almost clean. Another has a neat stack of dry — not dry, nothing is dry — slabs waiting. The cairns keep growing: you find a fresh one, its stones still darker with new wetness, its top quartz still muddy at the base from being pulled out of the grit that morning.

Who built it, in air this thin, with no wood, no animals to carry? Who keeps building? Your head throbs with the altitude and you stop asking linearly.

The rules here, insofar as you can feel them, are simple and unnegotiable:

Keep to the high stones. The water between is always moving and colder than you think.

Stack if you stop. Add a stone to keep the balance. The place expects weight in return for passage.

Do not trust stillness. Light will move before wind does. Sound will arrive before cause. If your echo answers first, wait — let it finish.

Do not look for green. There is none to find. Read the mineral stains the way you would read moss: orange for iron and seep, white for salt and sun, black for always-wet.

Drink if you must — the water is clear and sweet and numbing — but drink from the running veils, not the sky-pools. The pools are full of sun. They will blind you from the inside.

By mid-afternoon the clouds below have risen. Tendrils come fingering up through the stone forest, condensing instantly on the cold glass rock, doubling the wet. Visibility drops to thirty meters, then ten, then to the pillar in front of you, weeping hard now, its trickles turned to runnels. The roar gets louder, closed in. The light does not dim so much as diffuse, becoming everywhere at once, shadowless and mercilessly bright. Your clothes are soaked. Your fingers are white and stupid. Your breath plumes and is torn away.

And still the place is active, cycling fast. Water gathering, falling, hanging, falling. Stone warming, ticking, shedding skin. Light pooling, spilling, pooling again. Wind sawing through. Cairns growing stone by stone though no hand is visible. Seams in the sky opening and closing.

You sit in the lee of a low wall someone built who breathed this same thin soup, and you add your stone to the top — a flat black piece that fits your palm — because it feels ruder not to. The water runs off it immediately, joining the general downhill that is somehow also uphill in the spray.

Below, the white sea. Above, the hammer sun and the ghost sun and the old clouds under the new. Around, the dense, wet, bright, lifeless crowd of stones standing shoulder to shoulder in the cold, listening to their own loud music, repeating themselves into distance until distance gives up.

You will leave before dark. You must. The cold will deepen just enough to skin the pools with ice that will be gone by ten tomorrow, and the ticking will become cracking, and the path up to the pass will glaze. But part of you understands already that leaving will be like the echo: you will hear yourself gone before you have stood up.
