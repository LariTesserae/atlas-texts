It goes on until the haze eats it.

That is the first thing. You come in low — everything here is low — over warm flat water that isn't sea and isn't land, and the city doesn't appear as a skyline. It appears as weather. A thickening. A yellow-white glare on the horizon that resolves, as you get closer, into steam and gulls and high cables and towers sweating in the heat.

This is a low country. Elevation is a rumor. A meter or two above the tidal sheet if you find a good curb to stand on. The ground, where there is ground, is black basalt and poured stone and salt-eaten concrete and tile, all worn round at the corners. Nothing here is new, even the impossible things. The rain has had time. The feet have had time. The water has had time to work its fingers into every joint. Things are weathered into their final shape and then kept there, in balance, by constant repair.

There is no green. Not a weed in a crack, not moss, not a potted tree on a balcony. The stone is too hot and too salt and too trampled, and what the climate doesn't kill the density kills. This is a mineral world. Ceramic, glass, brass, basalt, rust, mother-of-pearl scale from the canal walls where the water evaporates and leaves its skin. The only soft things are animal.

And animals are everywhere.

From above you see them first as motion on the roofs and the causeways — a boiling, a shimmer — and then, lower, you hear them and you understand the scale. Gulls by the hundred thousand, white and grey, screaming off the condenser pylons. Cormorants drying their wings on bridge stanchions. Dogs in packs trotting the market aisles with the confidence of employees. Rats along the culverts. Crabs, red and blue-black, climbing the lock stairs at low tide in such numbers the steps look alive. In the canals: eels, mullet, silver flashing schools that turn as one body under the bridges. On every ledge, wasps and flies and moths; in the warm air, swifts cutting through insects that rise off the wet stone in columns. And people. Always people. Shoulder to shoulder, balcony to balcony, boat to boat. Children fishing with magnets. Old women shucking oysters with short knives. Porters, welders, water-taxi drivers, hawkers. No room between presences. If you stop walking you are carried.

Water is not scenery here. It is the condition. The sea table is right under the city — you can hear it sloshing in cisterns under your feet — and it keeps finding its way up. It seeps through pavement. It weeps out of walls. The older towers, the ones grown thirty stories straight out of programmable matter two centuries ago, sweat constantly; their flanks are dark with runnels, bearded with mineral stalactites where the weep has evaporated. The air itself is close to wringing out. Warm, heavy, smelling of salt and diesel and fish guts and hot stone and ozone. At midday the atmospheric harvesters come on — vast slow lattices strung between pylons a kilometer up — and you hear them before you see them: a deep thrumming that makes your teeth ache. They cool the air until it gives up. Condensation forms in mid-air and falls as a fine, warm, deliberate rain, hissing on the hot roofs, sending everyone shouting and laughing and covering their stalls with plastic sheets that are older than they are.

It rains and then it stops and then it rains because someone three districts over needed water pressure. Puddles never fully dry. Every courtyard has a pump chugging. Every street has a gutter running ankle-deep and brown-green with tide and wash-water. Kids splash in it. Ducks paddle in it. The whole vast flat pan of the city steams after each shower, and the steam becomes cloud, and the cloud becomes the city's own interior weather — low scudding fogs that get trapped between neighborhoods, sudden localized downpours over the fish market that leave the copper market two streets away dry and dusty.

The construction is urban to the point of geology. There is no single building, there are layers. Ground floor: stone arcades, flooded, built for boats. Second and third: shops and workshops, tile-fronted, awnings patched with sailcloth. Above that: housing stacked in mismatched cubes, balconies crusted with drying nets and cages of live crabs and laundry that never quite dries. Above that: the newer-old things — sky-bridges, pressure tubes carrying grain and powdered coral and mail with a soft *thup-thup-thup*, tethered platforms that hover without visible support on hot days, shimmering a little at the edges like a mirage that forgot to go away. Cranes everywhere, but they are not building a new city; they are keeping this one from sinking. A barge sinks, a pontoon is grown. A wall slumps, a printer-gantry crawls over and extrudes fresh reef-concrete, still steaming, and the gulls land on it while it's warm.

The technology stopped being understandable a long time ago and then kept working anyway. No one here could tell you how the lift-rafts stay up or why the transit causeways hum in B-flat when a heavy load passes, or why your shadow lags half a second behind you when you walk under a harvester field — that slight, ordinary wrongness everyone is used to. They just know which alleys to avoid at noon when the static builds up and makes your hair stand and the canal water climb up the lock walls in defiance of tide, beading and crawling, before falling back with a slap. They know to ground the awnings with copper chains. They know the deep foundation engines, far below in the drowned bedrock, still thump day and night to keep the whole plate from liquefying. The city is far beyond motors and screens. It is into weather-management and matter-coaxing and gravity-bartering, but all of it is dented, tagged, patched, lived-in. A god-machine with barnacles.

And the sound — you cannot prepare for it.

It is not one noise. It is physical pressure. The foundation thump in your sternum. The harvester drone in your teeth. Boat engines, pump engines, printer-gantries squealing, metal shutters crashing up and down, vendors shouting through cone megaphones in four languages at once, fishmongers slapping tails on stone, children screaming with delight, dogs barking, ten thousand gulls, the slap and suck of water in a million drains, the clatter of crab claws on tile as a basket tips, music from a hundred stalls — drums, radios, a brass band practicing on a rooftop, all out of sync — and underneath it all the vast wet breathing of the tide moving through the city as if through gills.

No one speaks quietly because it is impossible. Everyone shouts, laughs loudly, sings to be heard. Bargaining is done nose to nose, yelling. Lovers yell. Even the animals have adapted to volume: the gulls have a scream that cuts through engine roar, the dogs have a deep chest bark you feel more than hear.

Nothing stays still. That is the rule. The rule is: keep moving or be moved. Stalls are assembled at dawn from poles and tarps and disassembled at dusk and the street they were on becomes a boat lane by night. A plaza that was a market at noon is a repair yard by evening, sparks showering into the canal while eels gather to watch the light. A bridge that was there yesterday is gone — towed away for scraping — and a pontoon of empty oil drums and grown coral is in its place, bouncing under your feet. Tides come in and take the lower arcades; shopkeepers lift their goods a meter on pulleys, sit with their feet in warm seawater, keep selling. Storms build up over the western wards in an hour — black, warm, electric — and burst, and vanish, leaving the stone smoking.

If you stand long enough on one of the high tethered platforms and look out, you understand the true scale. There is no edge. To the north, refinery flares and salt pans glinting. To the south, the endless low tenements dissolving into heat haze. To the east, the sea breaking white on the outer reef-barrier, and the intake mouths drinking it. To the west, the river bringing brown silt that the dredge-fleet eats and excretes as new land. In every direction, cables and birds and steam. The city makes its own clouds now; pilots navigate by them.

It smells of life without leaves. Salt, sweat, hot iron, fish blood, frying oil, incense from a shrine wedged between a pump station and a cage-mender, the sweet rot of oyster shells piled for lime, ozone after the harvester rain. It feels warm on the skin always — warm water on your ankles, warm rain on your neck, warm stone through your shoes, warm breath of crowds and animals.

At night — though now it is day, hard white-yellow daylight filtered through humidity to a pearly glare — at night they say the whole place glows faintly from the bacterial mats in the culverts and the navigation lights and the welding, and the sound never drops, just deepens. You believe it. This place does not sleep. It cannot afford the room to lie down.

You wade in. The water is bath-warm. A crab sidles over your foot. A boy shouts to mind the rope, a barge shouldering through the lane behind you blasts its horn, gulls explode upward, the harvesters thrum on, and another warm rain begins to fall out of a clear sky, hissing, steaming, drumming on ten million awnings, and no one even looks up.
