There is no horizon here because there is no light to make one.

The land is low. Not low like a valley, low like a held breath — a continent that never bothered to rise. If you could see far, which you can't, you would see the same flatness going on until distance itself gives up. It is large enough to have weather, and it does, but the weather never arrives. It just hangs.

Warmth comes first, before shape. The air is bath-warm and heavy, saturated to the point where breathing and drinking are not quite separate. Your skin wets without rain. Water condenses out of nothing onto nothing — on your eyelashes, on the underside of leaves the size of sails, on the sharp ridges of bark. It gathers, swells, falls in a single slow drop. Then gathers again in the same place.

Underfoot there is no ground as you understand it. There is plant.

Root, mat, peat, root again. Fibers woven so tight water cannot fall through, so it sits just under the skin, a shallow black mirror trapped in cellulose. Every step — if you walked, which nothing here does — would press down and well up warm, tannin-brown water around your ankle, smelling of iron and oil and green rot that never quite rots. Because nothing here ends.

This is a forest that stopped being a collection of trees and became a substrate. Trunks rise out of the mat not as individuals but as columns, close enough that shoulders would touch if they had shoulders. They go up and they branch and they branch and they meet. Layer on layer on layer: low bladder-roots knuckled and steaming, mid-story of broad, leather-thick leaves overlapping like shingles on a roof that covers a world, high-story lost in vapor. There is no floor and no canopy. There is only inside.

It is young land, though vast. You can feel it in the edges. Nothing is softened yet. The leaves unfurl with a blade's edge, wax-fresh, unchewed — there is nothing to chew them — unscarred. The bark splits in clean vertical fissures where pressure has forced it open from within. Stilt roots end in sharp points still dripping sap. Even the peat beneath is fibrous and raw, not yet earth. The place feels recently emerged, coughed up warm from below and immediately colonized, as if the plants were waiting in the water for somewhere to happen.

No animal has ever touched this. No insect bores, no bird lands, no thing with eyes looks back. That absence is total. It changes the acoustics of being alive. Without nibbling, without footfall, without call, the forest has turned inward to its own vegetable business.

And its business is industry.

Not built industry. Nothing here was made. There are no straight lines, no cut stumps, no walls, no roads, no ruins. No hand, human or otherwise, assembled anything. And yet everything works.

Listen — if you hold your own breath long enough in the quiet to hear the quiet underneath — you will feel it before you hear it. A deep, subsonic thudding, so low it lives in your sternum more than your ears: *dun... dun... dun...* spaced minutes apart, coming up through the root-mat from far below. The great bladder-roots are boilers. They take in the warm ground-water, heat it with the slow geothermal blood of this low place, pressurize it in chambers of heartwood thick as iron, and lift it. Hundreds of feet straight up, without muscle, without pump of metal, only osmotic pressure and heat and time. Trunk-columns tick and creak as they swell. Sap, hot and oily, climbs in vast columns.

Higher, the work continues. There are flowers here — if that word still applies in darkness — that are pistons. Buds the size of barrels that fill over hours and then with a soft, wet sigh — *chuff* — exhale a plume of vapor and spores and warm oil-mist. Then fill again. Exactly the same. Vines as thick as your thigh run horizontally between trunks like drive-belts, trembling with transmitted tension, carrying not goods but tension itself. Leaves flap once, heavily, to vent excess heat, and settle.

And electricity. In the absolute dark, you see it not as light — there is not enough to see by — but as a prickling on the hairs of your arms, a metallic taste when a frond discharges. The air is so wet and so charged that the forest crackles faintly to itself. A leaf touches a leaf, a spark no brighter than a dying ember jumps, ozone blooms for a second and fades. Vast dynamo-fields of wet cellulose turning, storing, releasing. The whole continent hums at a voltage you can feel in your teeth, but it never arcs, never burns. It just cycles.

Water is the coolant and the product and the medium. It weeps from rocks that are not rocks but nodules of hardened sap and peat-iron, fresh and sharp-edged. It beads on every surface and runs in slow rivulets down bark channels evolved — no, grown — as perfect gutters and flumes. It drips from leaf-tip to leaf-tip to mat, a vertical conveyor that never stops and never varies. Marsh-pools in the hollows of the root-mat breathe steam in the warm dark, skim over with duckweed-like films, then clear as the film is reabsorbed. Condensation forms, falls, rises as vapor, condenses again. The cycle is closed. The level never rises, never falls.

That is the rule here, the one you learn with your lungs: nothing changes.

Not in the way of death. In the way of equilibrium so perfect it feels like time has been unplugged. A leaf does not grow old; it maintains. A drop falls from the same tip to the same pool and the pool returns it to the air and the air returns it to the tip. The piston-flowers do not bloom and wither; they stroke, and stroke, and stroke. The forest does not spread or retreat. It is already everywhere it needs to be, doing what it already does, warm and dark and wet and dense, a planet-sized engine made of living tissue running at idle forever.

The strangeness is not that it is alien. The strangeness is that it is familiar — root, leaf, water, heat — arranged into something that functions exactly like a factory district with no builders, no workers, no owners, no product except its own continuation.

You stand — you float, really, in warm black air between wet walls of life — and you understand: the dark is not night. Night ends. This is just the condition under which this kind of life can do this kind of work without interruption. Light would be a disturbance. Animals would be inefficiency. Sound would be waste heat. Building would be redundant when the organism is already architecture, already plumbing, already power grid.

So it stays. Warm. Wet. Vast. Silent except for drip and creak and the long slow pressure sigh of wood doing, patiently and without end, what machines dream of doing.
