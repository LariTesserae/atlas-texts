The bowl first, because everything else is inside it.

You come over the ridge with your lungs already short — high, much higher than you thought — and the land drops away on all sides except for this one shallow dent in the spine of the range. Not a valley. A room. Maybe three hundred paces across, rimmed all the way around by weathered teeth of pale rock, dolomite and salt gone the color of old bone. Beyond the teeth, blue distance falls and falls. Inside, the floor is almost flat, cracked into polygons, dusted white-pink. It looks from above like it should be silent. A dry reliquary.

It is not silent.

The sound hits you before your foot is over the rim. Not wind. Wind has gusts and lulls. This doesn't. This is a held chord, massive, physical, a roar made of a hundred close tones beating against each other until the air itself seems grooved. You feel it in your sternum first, then your teeth. On the path down your vision shivers at the edges. You open your mouth to equalize and get a mouthful of cold, utterly dry air that tastes of hot stone and ozone, like licking a battery in a freezer. There is no moisture to swallow. Your lips are already cracking.

That is the first rule here: there is no water. Not a trickle, not a seep, not a damp shadow under a stone. No snowmelt, though the air is cool enough that your breath smokes and your fingers go stupid in minutes. No rain — the clouds are far below, a white sea around the mountain's knees. The ground rings under your boots like fired clay. If you dig with a knife, the dust just sifts, salt-crystalline, anhydrite, basaltic grit. It has been dry so long that wetness is not even a memory in the shape of the stones. The stones are wind-shapes, long-balanced: mushrooms and ribs and undercut fins, settled into themselves. Nothing is raw. Everything has been licked smooth by millennia of this same cold howl.

And yet it is green. Impossibly green.

From the rim you thought it was moss staining the cracks. Down inside you see it is a thicket — a forest in miniature, lush to the point of riot, waist-high and head-high in drifts and walls between the bare polygons. Not green-green, not earth-green. A glass-green, a copper-green. Thousands upon thousands of stalks no thicker than a straw, hollow, translucent, branching into fronds as thin as breath. Sail-moss. Chime-reed. They have no flesh. Break one and there is no sap, only dry air and a faint metallic smell. Their leaves are aerogel lattices, silica and cellulose spun so fine they weigh almost nothing. You can push your whole arm into a clump and feel almost no resistance, just a cool tickling and a shower of pollen like glass dust. But there are millions of them. In the hollows where the sound pools strongest they grow so dense you cannot see the ground, a dry surf, shivering, flashing.

They do not drink. There is nothing to drink. They eat the sound.

That's the only way to say it. Each frond is tuned — you can see it if you hold still, which you can't, because everything moves — each frond vibrates to a slightly different sliver of the roar, humming, blurring. The whole thicket shimmers with standing waves. When a gust of the greater chord rolls through (if you can call a permanent thing gusting), the fronds light faintly from within, photosynthesizing not only the hard white high-altitude sun but the pressure itself. Phonotrophs. Put your hand near a dense clump and the hairs on your arm stand straight up with static. The air smells abruptly sweet, cut grass with no grass, cucumber, copper. The plants are making sugar out of shaking.

And because there is a forest that needs no rain, there is a herd that needs no water.

They are in the air. At first you think it's dust, or seeds, or the visual static of too-loud sound. Then a cloud of them banks and the sun catches thousands of wings at once.

Moths, mostly. If moths can be the size of your thumbnail and built like kites. Shiver-moths, flutter-mice, needlewasps — names fail because they don't land long enough to be named. They are almost nothing: chitin stretched over frame, paper-thin sails, hollow bones. The air in the bowl, for all its emptiness, is full of them. Tens of thousands. They never touch the dry ground. They graze. They tear along the top of the thicket, shearing off the vibrating tips of the reeds with tiny rasping mouths, and as they eat they sing, or rather their wings beat out of phase with the great roar and create a higher, tearing overtone that makes you wince. It is deafening on top of deafening. You cannot hear your own boots crunch. You cannot hear your own voice in your skull. You feel the herd go over like weather — a shadow, a pressure drop, a sudden cold prickle as ten thousand wingbeats steal energy from the air around you.

Look longer and there are larger things, sparser. Gliders the size of hawks but light as handkerchiefs, riding the standing waves without flapping, mouths open, straining moths from the air with combs of bristle. They are translucent too; you can see the sun through their bellies, see the dark pellet of their gut, see no water in them anywhere. Their droppings are dry powder that the reeds catch and absorb instantly. Nothing is wasted because there is nothing to waste. A closed dry loop, spinning faster and faster.

Everything moves. That is the second rule: nothing here holds still.

The reeds grow while you watch — a tip extends, forks, unfurls with an audible *tick*. A clump overgrows its tuning, goes discordant, shatters into glittering shards that are caught by neighboring fronds and re-grown within minutes. The moth clouds condense into a tight screaming vortex over a particularly sweet patch, strip it white in seconds, disperse as the reeds flash back green. Dust devils of salt, no taller than a person, wander the bare polygons endlessly, collapsing and reforming. Even the light seems to tremble, chopped by wings.

If it feels like the bowl is hollow, that's because it is.

In the center, where the polygons get larger and ring hollower, the crust thins to a drumhead. Lie down — though lying down means moths in your ears and reeds whipping your face — and press your palm to the dust and feel the deep thump underneath, a heart-beat far slower than the roar. The mountain under you is eaten out, a resonance chamber. Wind from the peaks below is being sucked up through throats in the rock, compressed, shaped. And helping it, scattered like afterthoughts, are the buildings.

There are seven of them, if you count broken ones. At first you don't see them at all because you are looking for walls, roofs. These are not that. They are ribs. Arches. A leaning tripod of black glass half-swallowed by reeds. A low ring of copper-veined slabs humming so hard they blur. A pylon, thirty feet tall, tilted, with no seams, its surface crawling with frost-like circuitry that rearranges when you blink. They are half-buried, salt-caked, clearly old in the way the rocks are old — weathered into place — but what they do is not old. The air around them is wrong. Dust falls *up* near the pylon. Sound bends visibly, heat-shimmer without heat. Your inner ear flips. One of the fallen arches holds a lens of stillness under it, a pocket where the roar cancels perfectly and for a terrified second you hear your own blood — then a glider darts through and the pocket collapses with a crack like ice.

No one built these recently. Lichen — dry, black-orange, needing no water but light and static — grows over the inscriptions that are not inscriptions, the grooves that are waveguides. Whoever tuned this bowl tuned it and left it running. Post-everything engineering: not to pump water, not to mine, but to keep a chord going so a garden that eats noise can live where nothing should live.

The boundaries are absolute. Step back up toward the teeth and within twenty paces the reeds thin, stunt, go gray and brittle as old paper. The moth cloud will not follow you. The roar thins, frays at the edges, becomes mere mountain wind again, cold and thin and clean. From the rim, looking back down, the bowl looks again like what it is from far away: a small pale dent, almost empty. Vast space between the arches. Vast sky over wafer-thin leaves and handkerchief hawks. A hamlet of sound in an empty high desert, lush and teeming and starving for nothing but vibration.

Stay too long and your teeth ache in sympathy. Your bones start to hum. The reeds lean toward you, minutely, because you are a new source of tremor, a warm discordant mass. The moths test you, brush your cheeks like dry snow that never melts.

Cool sun. Cold dust. No water to wash it off. Only the roar, feeding the green, feeding the wings, feeding the roar.
