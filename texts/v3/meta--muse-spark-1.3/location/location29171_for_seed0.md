It is night, and not the kind of night that will lift soon.

No moon gets through. The cloud is low and unbroken and the canopy is low and unbroken under it, so the dark is doubled. You don't so much see the valley as feel its weight. Low country. The air is thick and bath-warm and sits on your shoulders and in your mouth. Breathing here is drinking.

Underfoot is young earth. Not young like spring — young like wound. A few hundred years ago, maybe less, this whole basin split and spilled. Black basalt, aa lava frozen mid-tumble, still knife-edged where it hasn't softened. The shards stand up in fins and teeth and jumbled slabs. They have not had time to round. Where you put a hand without looking you get cut. Moss is trying, lichen is trying, but the stone still shows its fresh fracture, glittering faintly where condensation beads on it. The valley is a shallow bowl of that black glass, miles across, rimmed by higher dark you can sense more than see. Within it, everything alive is crammed.

There is almost no water, and too much life. That is the rule here.

No river runs through. No lake glints. In the daytime, by all logic, this should bake back to scrub. Instead the jungle has closed over the lava like a lid. Three, four, five layers of it. Great waxy leaves overhead the size of doors, then a tangle of vines and saplings, then shrubs with leaves like wet leather, then a springy mat of ferns and mosses that bridges from stone-tooth to stone-tooth so you rarely touch the rock at all. It is lush beyond reason. Your shoulders are wet just pushing through. Your forearms come away slick. Everything drips, but nothing flows.

Because here water does not fall. It is made.

It condenses. The air after sundown cools by a fraction — still warm, human-sweat warm — and all that warmth held from the day lets go. Breath becomes fog. And there is so much breath.

The valley is full of animals to the point of touch. You cannot take three steps without displacing something. Things skitter off your boots, drop from leaves onto your hair, cling and stare with eyeshine. A cloud of moths the size of your palm moves as one sheet and parts around you. Beetles tick and whirr in the leaf litter so thick the litter itself seems to move. Frogs sit shoulder to shoulder in every cupped leaf and hollow of stone, throats going. Up above, troops of small, long-fingered climbers crash through the mid-canopy, chattering, leaping, landing almost on each other. Something larger grunts and pushes far off and is answered nearby, much nearer than you like. Eyes open everywhere — amber, green, white — blink and vanish and reopen a foot to the left.

And they all exhale. Thousands, tens of thousands of lungs in this bowl, and millions of spiracles besides, pumping warm wet air into cooler warm wet air. By midnight the valley fills with its own weather. A ground-fog that isn't from the ground. It pearls on every edge. The plants have learned to drink it. Their leaves are black-green and upright and ridged with fine hairs that comb the mist. The hairs channel droplets down to the stem, to the sponge-root mats that hang in mid-air between branches and never touch soil. You can hear it if you hold still between the waves of noise: a constant fine ticking as droplets form and fall from leaf to leaf to leaf. The forest harvests animals, the animals feed the forest. A closed mouth breathing into itself.

That is why it is deafening.

There is no single call to follow. There is a pressure of sound. The insects start it — a sawtooth whine so high and massed it becomes a solid sheet, sawing at the inside of your skull. Under it the frogs: bottle-deep *plonks* and glassy *peeps* and a low roaring trill from the pockets of standing water that repeats without pause. Over it the climbers and gliders and nightbirds: shrieks, barks, rattles, whoops that break and are immediately covered. Leaves thrash. Wings batter. Something chews loudly, wetly, right by your ear. Something else cracks shells — *tick tick crack* — over and over. The sound has a physical weight in the dark. Your chest vibrates with it. You shout to hear yourself and hear nothing.

And nothing stays still. This is not a night forest holding its breath. It is a bazaar mid-riot. Swarms relocate every minute, a humming knot lifting off a branch that sags back, then descending ten feet away in a new shape. A column of ants as wide as your arm pours over a lava fin, each ant carrying a green chip, disappearing into a crack that steams faintly. A snake, pale as root, drops through the fern mat and is mobbed instantly by tiny birds that should be asleep. Fungi down at ankle level pulse faint green-gold and then go dark as a thousand pill-bugs boil over them and eat the light. Vines creak and settle. Fruit thuds. Something is always being caught, always being born. The mat under you trembles with it. Even the plants move — not metaphorically; the big leaves slowly cup and uncup through the night, tracking the fog, wringing more from it.

Light, what little there is, is made too. No stars. No fire, no lamp, no clearing. Only living sparks. Fungal lace on rotting wood. The cold blink of fireflies in irregular code, so many they illuminate the undersides of leaves for a half-second at a time and then leave you blinder. The throat-sacs of the pocket-water frogs swell translucent and amber when they call, so the wet hollows blink like coals with each bass note. Once, a glider spreads its membranes and they are edged in faint blue, there and gone. You learn to navigate by interruption — darker trunks against slightly less dark air, wet shine where a leaf has tilted.

There is no trail. No cut stump, no ash, no stacked stone, no path worn by anything that walks on two legs and stays. The stone would remember a cut for a century — it is too new to forget — and it remembers nothing. Untouched. And yet there are hands here.

Crouch if you can stand the noise and the press of bodies. In a little cup of water caught between two basalt blades — one of the rare pockets that lasts, black and tannin-brown, full of tadpoles — the small climbers come to drink and hunt. They do not lap. One picks a thorn from a shrub, strips it with its teeth, and fishes with it in the bark crevices for grubs. Another picks up a flat pebble, no bigger than a knuckle, and hammers a snail against the lava until it gives. Tool to hand to mouth, then dropped. Nothing built. Nothing carried away. A twig probe left floating. A cracked shell. A stone with a fresh chip that will be lost by morning under ants. Hands that make and unmake in the same minute, and leave the valley as sharp and trackless as they found it.

Past them, past hearing, the valley goes on. Another ridge of black teeth with green combed over it. Another hollow full of throats and mist. Another troop crashing. Town-sized, valley-sized — you could walk till dawn and still be in this breathing dark, still be shoulder-deep in leaves and eyes and water that never rains, only settles.

If you stand still long enough, your own breath joins it. Dew forms on your skin first, before the leaves nearest you, as if you were the warmest stone. A moth lands to drink from your forearm. A frog watches from a leaf at your eye level, throat pulsing light, unafraid in the press. The sharp new earth underneath, the old green on top, and in between this dense, warm, roaring multitude making its own rain out of itself.

Do not expect morning to explain it.
