The Hollow at the Top of the Air

It is not a valley so much as a room left open to the sky. You can walk across it in an hour if the air lets you — and the air does not let you do anything quickly here.

On three sides a headwall rises in fresh, unweathered breaks: black schist and pale granite still sharp where they have fallen, not yet rounded by any river. The fractures are clean as knapped flint. Below the wall lies its own apron of scree, cones of shattered plate still sorting themselves, clicking now and then as frost heaves them overnight and sun releases them at noon. This is young country. Nothing has had time to soften. Even the ridge crest above is a saw, not a line — a series of teeth against a blue so saturated it hurts.

The floor of the bowl sits very high. High enough that breathing is work. High enough that the sun is not warm so much as white and absolute. Light comes down without any filtering, bounces off stone and snow-patch and thin tarn-water, comes up again from below. At midday there is no shade, only a doubling of brightness that makes the edges of rocks buzz. Shadows, when they exist, are ink-cut and tiny and lie directly underneath things. Distance lies. A cairn you think is ten minutes away is forty. The air is too clear to judge by.

It is cold — not storm-cold, but a permanent, high, thin cold that lives in the stone. Your breath shows even when the sun is burning your neck. In the morning everything is rimed. The mosses are stiff with frost, the spiderwebs between dwarf willow are strung with beads of ice, the seeps are locked. Then, around ten, the white sun crests the eastern tooth and the whole hollow begins to drip, tick, and unclench.

There is not much water here, and what there is does not stay. No river runs through. Instead there are dampnesses. A long snowbed tucked under the north wall bleeds a little every afternoon into a chain of shallow pans — dark mirrors no deeper than your hand, rimmed with sedge and cotton-grass. By evening they shrink. By dawn they skin over with ice. There are condensation places too: under overhangs where the rock sweats cold even on dry days, and in the lee of boulders where night fog condenses and feeds a collar of moss. Pockets. The rest is dry stone and wind-scoured lichen that crackles underfoot.

Where water has been, life crowds.

From a distance the floor looks barren — grey, white, void. Up close it resolves into islands. Cushions of moss campion so tight and green they look poured, saxifrage starry white and yellow in the cracks, dwarf willow no taller than your thumb but spreading into mats old as anything here, sedges and alpine bistort in the wettest seams. Each green island is dense, spongy, humming — and between islands is nothing. Meters of empty shattered rock. Vast spacing. The lushness does not spread; it concentrates, as if the hollow can only afford life in small, fierce allotments.

The animals understand this rule.

There are far more of them than the emptiness first suggests, but they are almost all small, or high, or quick. In the sun-hours the air over the green islands thickens with insects — midges, stoneflies hatched from temporary pools, bumblebees huge and furred and impossibly loud for a place so quiet — all working frantically before the cold returns. The accentors and rosy-finches work the mats on foot, flipping tiny stones. Marmots, fat from the short summer, whistle thinly from boulder to boulder and vanish. High above, almost always, there are choughs: red-billed, glossy, tumbling in the updraft off the headwall, playing with the edge of falling. And once a day, usually when cloud begins to build over the ridge, a lammergeier will come and circle the bowl slowly, twice, its ten-foot shadow crossing from moss to scree to moss again, looking for bone.

It is a hunting place. You see it in the feathers caught in willow, in the weasel tracks that appear overnight around the pans, in the way the pika haypiles are tucked deep under stones. Life here is fast because it has to be. Freeze, thaw, hatch, eat, be eaten, freeze again. Clouds boil up after two, drop a hard ten-minute hail that rattles on the tin of an old shelter and vanishes, then clear. Everything is on a daily cycle, not a seasonal one.

People have been here, but barely, and long ago enough that their work looks like geology.

Three low bothies huddle in the lee of the largest erratics near the outlet lip — dry-stone, without mortar, roofs of slab and turf now slumped and colonized by lichen and nesting finches. One still holds a dark fire-pit, a cracked soapstone pot, a pile of split juniper roots no thicker than a wrist. A line of small cairns threads between the green islands, marking the dry way when melt is high. Up under the headwall, a crescent of flat stones set on edge: an old trap, or a blind. Sinew snares, bone awls, a hand-axe flaked from the local chert left on a ledge as if someone just set it down. No metal, or almost none — a single rusted nail hammered into a door lintel like an offering. No paths worn deep. The marks are faint, scattered, respectful of how little there is to take.

And the quiet.

Sound does not carry here the way it should. The air is too thin to hold it. Your footsteps on scree are papery and close. The marmot whistle seems to come from everywhere and nowhere. Even the wind, when it funnels over the teeth, is more pressure than noise — a pushing on the ears. What you do hear, if you sit still on one of the warm noon rocks, is micro-sound: water ticking under ice, a bee sawing through thin air, the almost inaudible pop of campion capsules opening in the heat, your own blood.

There is one unusual thing, and you notice it only after a day.

The light is too long. Or the water remembers. In the late afternoon, when the sun drops behind the western tooth and the hollow falls abruptly into blue shadow and the temperature plunges ten degrees in minutes, the pans do not simply freeze. For a short while they breathe — the ice skin forming, breaking from below by trapped air, forming again, sighing. And the lichens — orange and bone-white map-lichens on the north-facing boulders — hold the sun a few minutes after it is gone, phosphorescing faintly, as if the stone itself were reluctant to let go of brightness.

Nothing rots here. That is the law. Things dry, freeze, are picked clean, are scattered. Wood lasts decades. Bone lasts longer. Water only moves at noon. Green only lives where damp has lived. And everything — bird, flower, human wall — tucks itself low, dense, and close to stone, leaving the great bright volume above it empty for wind and wings.
