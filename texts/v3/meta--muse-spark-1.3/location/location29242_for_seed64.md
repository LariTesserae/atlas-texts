You wouldn't find it unless someone showed you the hatch.

High up — high enough that the air goes thin and cold in your lungs and the valley below is just a rumpled gray sea — the ridge narrows to a knife-blade of black basalt. The tramway tower stands there, legs bolted straight into the rock, cables screaming off into white nothing. Everyone looks out, or up. No one looks at the tower itself.

Halfway up the north leg, behind the counterweight housing, there is a low iron door, waist-high, paint long weathered to bare metal. You have to duck and go in sideways. Inside is not a room. It's a pocket. A mistake left over.

A natural cup in the summit rock, maybe three paces long and two wide, scooped out by wind over ten thousand years and then, decades ago, roofed over with riveted plate when they set the tower. One side is mountain. Three sides are machine. The floor slopes slightly. The ceiling is so low you cannot stand. That's the whole place. Above you, the peak goes up another two hundred meters into cloud. Below you, three thousand meters of drop. But here, everything is close.

It is full. That is the first thing. Impossibly full.

Wall to wall, floor to ceiling strut, it is packed with green. Not a patch, not a tuft — a saturated, shoulder-to-shoulder mat of life with no room between presences. Cushion moss, black-green and springy as upholstery, knee-deep where it mounds. Out of it, fronds — something between fern and lichen and none of those — uncurl in dense rosettes, papery, dusty, overlapping like shingles. Stonecrop-like beads crowd the bolt-heads. Pale, almost translucent straps hang from the overhead conduit. Every rivet, every angle-iron, every inch of basalt is occupied. You cannot set a hand down without setting it on leaves.

And it is dry. Bone dry. That is the second thing, and the one that doesn't make sense.

There is no water here. No pool, no drip, no seep, no rain — the roof seals it, the wind that howls outside carries only ice-dust that never settles in. The moss, when you press it, does not squeeze. It crackles faintly. The air smells of ozone and hot oil and chlorophyll, not damp. If you lick your lips they chap. Your boots raise a fine dark dust that hangs in the lamplight and never settles.

The garden does not drink water. It drinks the tower.

Day and night the service transformer in the east wall hums — a big, oil-cooled industrial brick, fins caked in graphite dust, its pilot jewel burning a dim amber. It sweats a perpetual warm aerosol of dielectric mist, and breathes a faint electric field. The fronds lean not toward the slit of gray daylight from the door seam, but toward that hum, toward the vibration. Their undersides are freckled with rust-red spots where they have taken iron straight from the plate. Their roots — white, hair-fine, endless — are matted into a felt that eats rock dust, skin cells, grease, copper filings. Lift an edge and you see it: no soil, just felt on steel on stone. A lithophyte circuit. Lush without ever being wet.

The light is dim to the point of evening. Most of the pocket is in shadow. What comes in is a cold blade from the door gasket and the low amber glow of the pilot lamp and, high up where a conduit passes through the roof plate, a pencil of overcast mountain sky. In that gloom the greens go almost black, then flare emerald at the edges where the lamp catches the leaf-hairs. Everything has a thin coating of oil-dust that makes it gleam dully, like a moth's wing.

It is cold. Your breath fogs, but the fog vanishes instantly in the dry air. The rock at your back leaches heat. The transformer gives a hand's-width of warmth if you hold your palm near it, and the moss nearest to it is a fraction taller, a fraction yellower — the only gradient in the whole place, and it stops after ten centimeters.

The sound is loud for such a small place. You feel it in your teeth before you hear it. The whole tower is an instrument: wind shrieking through the cables outside becomes a low howl transmitted down the steel, the counterweight chain grinding somewhere above in its channel with a slow industrial clank... clank... clank, and underneath it all, never ceasing, the 50-cycle snarl of current, a wasp trapped in the walls. You have to raise your voice to talk. You feel the fronds trembling against your sleeves with it.

There are human marks, but scattered and half-swallowed. A stenciled number, 7-B, half-overgrown. Two rungs of a maintenance ladder that goes nowhere now, its uprights serving as trellises. A dented toolbox in the corner, lid pried open by roots, its wrenches rust-welded together. Someone's initials scratched into the paint in 1978. A replacement bulb, still in its cardboard sleeve, left on a ledge and now cradled in moss like an egg. No one maintains this. No one needs to.

There is no animal life. None. No flies, no mites you can see, no spiders, no birds nesting. In any other lush place there would be humming, chewing, rot. Here, nothing eats, nothing rots, nothing pollinates. The fronds propagate by flaking — a dry leaf breaks off, lodges a centimeter away, puts out white hairs and becomes a new rosette. Dead material doesn't decay; it just becomes more felt for the mat to stand on. It is a green that has learned to do without mouths.

That is the strangeness of it, beyond the first impossibility. The leaves grow wrong. They grow edge-on to light, face-on to vibration. Some of them have veins of actual verdigris, green copper salts laid down like blood vessels. If you hold the palest hanging straps up to the slit of daylight, you can see the transformer light shining through them from behind, as if they remember being lit from the other side. The moss in the deepest corner, where no light at all reaches, is still green — a blind, electric green.

And it never changes. The old linemen will tell you: it looked exactly like this in '82, in '64, when the tower was set. Photos prove it — same mounds, same rosettes in the same places, same height against the 7-B stencil. It does not advance. It does not die back in winter. It does not bloom or seed or wither. The dust hangs in the same column. The chain clanks, the hum holds, the cold holds. A settled form, weathered into perfect balance: as much growth as the mist can feed, as much wear as the dry can take, locked.

You crouch there with green pressing into your knees and elbows and the loud dark humming through your skull, three thousand meters up, in a cup of rock no bigger than a closet, and you understand the rules of this little world:

Nothing flows. Nothing drinks. Nothing is eaten, and nothing leaves.

Only the tower sings, loud and unchanging, and the dry garden listens, packed tight, holding on.
