It is too bright at first to see it.

You come up over the rim — lungs working too hard for the climb you've done — into a light that has weight. High light. Thin-air light. The sky is a burned-out blue-white, the sun a cold coin that doesn't warm, and the air is cool enough to make your nostrils stick, to make you zip your jacket even while your forehead sunburns. Every shadow is knife-edged. There is no haze. You can see too far.

That's the first lie: that this is empty.

From the rim it looks like desert. A high, cold, dry tableland rolling out past the curve of the world, pale tan and grey and bleached bone, with nothing on it. No water anywhere. Not a lake, not a creek, not a puddle in a hoofprint. No dark green to promise a spring. The air tastes of dust and resin and faint ozone, and when you breathe your mouth dries instantly. Your lips crack within an hour. Things don't rot here; they cure.

Then your eyes adjust, and the empty floor moves.

What you took for ground isn't ground. There is rock somewhere far below, old and weathered and rounded by a million years of wind until it stopped arguing — settled, balanced hills, long shallow basins, outcrops smoothed like sleeping animals. But you will never touch it. Everything you stand on is plant.

A mat, a felt, a substrate a meter thick and springy under your boots. Interwoven rhizomes thick as your wrist, papery lichens in sheets that crackle and heal, cork blisters, wax scales, dead lint compacted to peat that never got wet. It smells like an attic and a sawmill: turpentine, dry hay, hot paper. It is everywhere, to every horizon. This is not a place with plants on it. This *is* a plant, wearing the continent like a skin.

And then, far apart, the Towers.

They are sparse — that is the second shock. For all that living mass, there are so few uprights that you can count them. A dozen to the horizon, no more. Each one a kilometer or two from the next, each one vast enough to have its own weather. You thought they were hills until one turned slowly.

They are thistles, if a thistle grew to the size of a cathedral and forgot about water. A trunk of braided bast fiber, pale grey and twisted like old rope, thirty, forty meters around, rising a hundred meters up and splitting into arms that hold a head — a sour, bristling globe of dry bracts and spines and sails the size of barn doors. No leaves. Nothing green. Everything is straw, ash-white, parchment-yellow, rust-red. They do not photosynthesize like lowland things; they comb the wind. Their sails fray continuously into airborne down, tons of it a day, and their roots — you can see them where the mat tears — are not roots but anchors screwed deep into the old rock, drinking powdered mineral and fog-oil.

No one built anything here. There are no walls, no roads, no ruins, not even a cairn. The place is untouched in the way a heart is untouched.

And yet it is industrial.

Listen — you have to, you can't not. The sound comes before understanding.

It's deafening. Not loud like a crowd. Loud like inside a drum. Your sternum buzzes. You have to lean in and shout into someone's ear from a foot away and still lose words. At first you think it's wind. It is wind, but wind *played*.

Each Tower is a machine that was never made. Its hollow trunk is an organ pipe, a bellows. When the high plateau wind hits — and it always hits, this continent makes its own storms, sucking air up its warm center and flinging it outward — the Tower inhales through slits in its bark with a shriek, swells, and exhales in a long chuff-chuff-chuff that you feel in your teeth. The arms creak, ten tons of fiber grinding on fiber with no oil but wax, a groan that goes on for minutes. Seed-mills along the branches — hardwood pods like press-cylinders — clamp shut and stamp, stamp, stamp, extruding identical pale ingots of packed fluff that pile in neat windrows for miles downwind, scaled production with no factory floor.

Between gusts there is the crackle. The air is so dry, the down so full of static, that the whole moor hums with discharge. Blue sparks snap from spine-tip to spine-tip. At night — though day is blinding — you can see St. Elmo's fire crawling over the Tower heads, and smell the sharp metallic tang. The plants are electrified looms, weaving charge out of friction.

Nothing here is still. That is the law.

The Towers sway, shed, rebuild. A bract the size of a roof tears off with a sound like canvas ripping and tumbles bouncing for a kilometer, already unraveling into a comet of lint. A head bursts. Not metaphorically — a ripe head detonates at noon when the light is hardest, a dry pop you hear five miles away, loosing a black cloud of oilseeds that rises instead of falling, caught in the updraft and static, hanging like smoke. Within hours, scavengers arrive.

Because for all the emptiness between Towers, this place is full of animals. Abundant, frantic, hyperactive life, just pulsed.

They live *in* the plant, on the plant, because there is nothing else. Woolly grazers big as bison but light as deer, with wide splayed feet for walking on the springy mat, mow the lichen-felt in moving lawns, their teeth grinding audibly even under the wind-roar. Flocks of needle-beaked clingers strip wax from the trunks. Skyfish — pale, manta-like gliders with six-foot wingspans of skin-thin membrane — ride the Tower updrafts in hundreds, filtering down out of the air, shrieking thinly. And the underthings: copper-colored myriapods long as your arm that tend the seed-windrows like assembly workers, cutting, rolling, burying, in endless anthill lines that shift direction all at once for no reason you can see.

Everyone is shouting, grinding, chewing, clattering to be heard over the Towers. The combined noise at midday — wind + bellows-chuff + fiber-creak + stamping pods + static + herds + ten thousand wings — is a physical weather. You feel it flatten the tiny hairs on your arms.

And then the storm, daily, proves the scale. You watch it being born: lint and dust rising from three Towers at once spiral together twenty miles away, white at first, then grey, then towering, a dry thunderstorm with no rain in it. No water will fall. Instead at its heart lightning walks from cloud to Tower-head to cloud, and the Tower *answers*, venting a plume of scorched oil and spore that smells like bread and lightning. The wind front hits you minutes later as a wall of cool, blinding fluff. You can't see your hands. You breathe through cloth. The mat under you ripples. The grazers lie down and face it. The skyfish vanish. The whole continent shudders and sheds its skin, tons of living matter ripped up and redeposited, and an hour later the air clears, brutally bright again, and everything glitters with a thin frost of wax that will sublimate by evening.

That is the strangeness, and it is not screaming strangeness. It's a tilt. Water, the thing you expect to run everything, is forbidden here. Any hollow that would hold a puddle after the rare sleet is lined with hydrophobic wax; droplets bead and skitter off like mercury and evaporate before noon. The plants don't want it. Their chemistry runs on oil and air and light and ground stone. To drink would be to rot. Even your sweat dries too fast to cool you. You learn to carry water inside you like a secret.

The rules, once you walk a day between two Towers with your ears ringing, are simple:

Nothing touches stone except through plant. The mat is floor, food, house, grave.

Nothing was built. Everything manufactured was grown, extruded, stamped by living wood, and will unravel.

Nothing is quiet, and nothing stays. Climb a settled hill and it will have moved a hand's breadth of felt by morning. Count the Towers and one will have toppled in the night — they do, every few years, with a crash like a shipwreck — and already its fallen trunk is sprouting a white rash of new anchors, becoming a long low ridge, a nursery for grazers.

Stand still and you will sunburn in cool air while your boots hum with static and the ground faintly vibrates with work far away. Put your palm to the mat and feel the slow peristalsis of rhizomes tightening against the wind. Look up: the nearest Tower is turning its head a fraction to face the storm it helped make, sails cracking like sails, shedding a bright river of down that flows sideways for miles, feeding things you can't see yet.

It is a desert that is a jungle. A factory with no builders. A prairie the size of a country that is one body, sparse at the top and total underneath, roaring in bright cold light, making and unmaking itself faster than you can walk across it.
