The dark here is not empty. It has temperature.

Warm, first of all — a low, breathed-out warmth that sits on the skin and does not lift. No sun has risen here in a long time, or perhaps it never does. There is no moon to fix distance by. Your eyes keep trying to adjust and never quite do, so you learn to see sideways: by heat on your cheeks, by the give of ground underfoot, by the sheet of wet that condenses out of the air and runs down everything.

It is a low country. Flat enough that sound carries wrong. A shallow basin, valley-wide, town-wide, that goes on past where you can feel it end. Old sea floor, new mud. In the day — if there ever was a day — it would be a marsh-edge plain stitched with pans and ditches and slow channels, black water under black mud. Now it is mostly touch and echo.

The ground is young. That is the strangest thing to feel under your boots. It hasn't been smoothed yet. The ridges of slag and poured concrete are still sharp where they broke. The levees they threw up are still crisp-edged, not yet slumped. Rebar pokes out of a collapsed culvert like fresh quills, its rust only a thin orange skin. You can feel formwork grain still in the concrete of the low walls. This whole place emerged recently — pushed up, or drained, or dumped — and water is already taking it back. Every bootprint fills ankle-deep before you have lifted your foot. Every low spot holds a warm mirror that trembles.

Water is everywhere without being deep. It doesn't fall as rain so much as it exhales. The air is saturated; metal weeps, rocks weep. The pump sheds drip from their eaves in a constant slow bead. The catwalks drip. Your own arms drip. Underfoot the water table is a finger's width down — press your palm to the mud and warm water wells up around your fingers, smelling of iron and rot and hot oil. The channels move, but barely. A leaf dropped in a ditch will still be there an hour later, turned slightly.

Vegetation is thin for how wet it is. Clumps, not meadows. Stands of black reed with stems thick as thumbs, spaced like fence posts, their heads heavy and nodding though there is no wind. Low mangrove-like things on stilt roots holding onto the levee sides. Algae in slicks — olive, rust-red — spreading across the pans in a skin that breaks and reseals around your steps. Nothing tall. Nothing to block the openness. Between the clumps there is just warm mud, cracked in fresh plates, and glassy water, and slag.

What *is* dense is everything else.

The air is crowded. Clouds of midges and moths hang in columns over the water, so thick you walk through them as through curtains, they coat your lips and eyelashes. You can hear their wings more as pressure than as sound. Below them, the mud is paved with life: snails shoulder to shoulder, crabs the color of bone, tadpoles blackening the shallows until the water looks solid. Frogs call — thousands — but not in a chorus. One here, one far off, one under your foot. Eels slide in the ditches, fat as wrists, leaving V-wakes that persist forever in the still water. Nightjars and herons shift out in the dark, you hear the soft suck of their feet coming loose from mud, a wing-beat close to your ear that is gone before you turn.

And among them, scattered but unmissable, the machines.

They are not a city. They are a scatter — a pump house here, a transformer on a concrete plinth there, a low shed with its door gone, a stack a half-mile off breathing out a slow column of heat you can feel on your face long before you smell the sulfur in it. Narrow rails run out into the marsh and sink. A conveyor trestle marches on rusted legs toward the center of the basin and then simply stops, its belt still, its end hanging over nothing. Cables sag between leaning poles, beaded with water, ticking as they cool.

They are still running, most of them. That is the industrial part, and it is patient to the point of eerie. No one tends them, but they have not stopped. A big flywheel in an open-sided shed turns once, pauses, turns again — one revolution a minute, dripping oil into a pan that overflows into the mud where the eels gather. A pump clunks somewhere out in the dark, draws breath, pushes water from one ditch to another ditch for no reason you can see. Transformers hum so low you feel it in your molars rather than hear it. Everywhere the faint smell of ozone and hot grease under the rot. Pilot lights burn red and green on panels — pinpricks that do not illuminate anything, they only mark presence, like animal eyes.

There should be noise — engines, factories, frogs, water — and yet it is mostly quiet. The dark eats sound. The warm wet air muffles it. What you get are small, close things: your own breathing loud, a drip from a pipe hitting a puddle with perfect clarity, a snail shell cracking underfoot, the slow sigh of a pressure valve lifting and settling a hundred yards away. Even the frogs sound muted, as if calling through cloth.

And then there is the way the place does not hold together.

At first you think it is just the darkness playing with distance. That stack you walk toward for twenty minutes is no closer. That hum you left behind you is suddenly in front of you. But then it gets more specific.

Water beads hover. In the weeping places — under the catwalk, at the mouth of the culvert — droplets detach and then do not fall. They hang, warm, trembling, catching the red pilot glow for an instant before they slide sideways and vanish. Your footprint fills *before* you lift your boot, with water that is warmer than the water around it.

Animals double. You hear a heron lift off to your left and simultaneously see its heat-shape lift off to your right. You count a crab's legs — seven, nine — and when you look again it has the right number, but it is walking backward through its own trail. The moth columns sometimes pulse in unison, all wings at once, and for a half-second there is a shape in the air above the water that is too large and too jointed to be moths, and then it is moths again.

The built things do it too. There is a shed you pass with a caved roof and a stenciled number: 7. A half hour later, circling a pan you cannot see across, you come to a shed with a caved roof stenciled 7. The mud on its step holds your own bootprint, still filling. If you put your hand on a humming transformer, the hum stops — and starts again a moment later from the next pole down the line, as if it moved to make room for you.

No one lives here now, or no one admits to it. But there are faint marks of tending: a walkway swept clear of snails, a wick trimmed in a kerosene lantern that is never lit, a row of eel traps emptied and reset, their withies still wet from hands. Offerings, almost. On one concrete plinth someone has set out, in a neat line, a bolt, a frog skull, a red pilot bulb still warm.

The rules, if you stay, make themselves known slowly, the way everything happens here slowly:

Don't step where the water is warmest. That is where the mud is thinnest, and where the eels are thickest, and where something underneath breathes up.

Don't follow a light that moves. The fixed red and green ones are machines. The white ones that drift low over the pans are not.

If you hear your own footstep a half-second before you make it, stop. Wait until the marsh catches up.

It is a young land, still sharp, still deciding its shape — warm mud and fresh concrete and slag under warm dark, crowded edge to edge with small lives and slow engines, all condensing, all dripping, all waiting out a night that does not end.
