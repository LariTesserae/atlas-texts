It is not on any map because it is too small to map. You find it only by following the sound.

High up — high enough that your breath goes short and white — the ridge narrows to a notch, a missing tooth between two shoulders of rock. From below it looks like nothing. From above, weather closes over it nine days out of ten. But if the cloud lifts for an hour at noon, you can drop down into it.

It is a room. That is the size of it. Six, seven paces long. You can touch both walls at once if you stretch your arms. And yet it is submerged.

Not a lake beside the mountain. A piece of ocean that got lodged in the mountain.

The floor is gone. Or never formed. What should be ground is water — black-green, cold to the point of pain, moving too fast for its size. It fills the pocket from wall to wall, lip to lip, and it does not sit still. Fed from above by three white tongues of falls that come off the upper snowfield and squeeze through cracks in the cliff, and fed from sideways by seep and spray and the cloud itself condensing on every surface, it churns. It boils without heat.

The rock that holds it is wrong for its age. This is not old, smoothed stone. This is raw stuff. The walls are fresh-broken granite and dark basalt, edges still razor, faces still showing the glitter of just-split crystals. No soil has had time to gather. No rounding. In the corners, a pale milk of rock-flour swirls and never settles. You get the feeling the mountain cracked open last week and hasn't decided what this place is yet — pothole, tarn, throat — embryonic geology, still wet from being born. If you put your palm to it, grit comes off on your skin.

And because there is so little room, the water is everything. It is the floor and the wallsweat and the air. You are not beside water, you are inside it. Spray hangs so thick the air is drinkable. Your jacket darkens in seconds, your hair plasters, rivulets run down the back of your neck though it isn't raining — not exactly. It is atmospheric-water. The cloud that lives in the notch breathes in and out, and when it exhales the whole pocket whites out in a wall of droplets. The falls hit the pool and throw it back up. The pool hits the walls and throws itself at you. Even the stone sweats. There is nowhere dry to stand.

The light, oddly, is ordinary. That is the strangest mercy. High noon alpine light, clean and white-gold, comes straight down the chimney of the notch. It catches the spray and makes a short, furious rainbow that appears and is shredded and appears again, ten times a minute. It lights the upper meter of water to bottle-green transparency before the turbulence takes it opaque again. It does not feel like a cave. It feels exposed, daylight-bright, while drowning.

The cold is a clean cold. Not bitter, not killing — just cool enough that your fingertips go stupid after a few minutes in the splash zone. Enough that you can see your breath mix with the mist and lose track of which is which. Stone is cold through boot soles. Water, if you trail a hand — and you will — is knife-cold, snowmelt cold, with a mineral bite.

You will think, at first, that it is empty. And it is. Profoundly empty. For a place so small, the distances feel enormous. A tuft of moss here, then nothing for a long time, then a lichen there. The volume is mostly water and air and roar. Vast space between presences. You can stand for an hour and see no living thing move except water.

Then you start to see the sparse things that hold on.

Vegetation is an afterthought, a rumor. In cracks above the splash line: a cushion of star-moss, black-green and sodden, a few blades of wiry alpine grass flattened by constant wind, crusts of orange and bone-white lichen that look painted on. Nothing tall. Nothing soft. All of it beaded, dripping, shivering.

Fauna is rarer still. No fish could climb here, and yet — if you stare down into the churn during a lull — there are pale flickers deep down, eyeless white somethings, maybe amphipods, maybe young char washed down and trapped, rolling in the gyre, unable to leave. Once, a dipper comes. A small slate-gray bird that should not be this high drops shrieking through the spray, spears straight into the white water of the falls, vanishes, and bobs up in the pool shaking, with something wriggling in its beak. Then it is gone, and the place is empty again for a long time. A single white moth pastes itself to the leeward wall and pulses.

People have been here. Not many. Not recently, or not for long.

Scattered, half-erased: on the only ledge wide enough to stand without getting your feet wet, someone has stacked a low windbreak of dry stone — no mortar, just slabs fitted by hand, now tumbled at one end where water undercut it. Three hand-chipped steps lead down to the water's lip, the chisel marks still visible, fresh-looking because there has been no weather soft enough to blur them. An iron ring, hand-forged and hammered into a crack, rust bleeding orange down the fresh rock. From it, a rotted stump of rope made of twisted grass. A wooden dipper, split and gray, left upside down on a boulder. A tiny cairn of three stones, the top one balanced impossibly, already green with slime on its north side.

No machines. Nothing that needs more than hands. Whoever comes here comes to drink, or to mark, or to listen, and leaves.

Because of the sound. You cannot prepare for it in a place this small.

It is deafening. The falls hit from only ten meters up but in this stone throat there is nowhere for the noise to go. It stacks on itself. Low hammering from the plunge, high hissing from the spray, a mid-range roar that gets into your sternum and vibrates your teeth. You have to shout to hear yourself think, and then you stop trying to think. Conversation is impossible. Even your own breathing sounds distant. The rock itself seems to hum. If you press your forehead to the wall you feel it in the bone.

And it is never the same twice. This is a turbulent place in the truest sense — not just moving, but unmaking and remaking itself by the minute. The water level breathes. Every four or five minutes the pool sucks down — you see the dark walls exposed, slick and gasping — then surges back up with a slap that sends a wave over the steps and rearranges the pebbles. Eddies form and dissolve. A log that wasn't there arrives, spins three times, jams, breaks, leaves. The little rainbow tears. The cloud thickens and thins. You cannot step into the same pocket twice.

Here is the strange part, the part that keeps you longer than the cold says you should:

The water behaves as if the pocket were much deeper than it can possibly be. Drop a stone and you never hear it hit bottom, only swallowed. On calm seconds — and there are seconds, between breaths — you look straight down and the green goes down and down, darker than any six-foot pool has a right to go, with a slow tidal sway, as if this thimbleful were connected by a straw to the deep sea. Tides, here. A thumb-high tide line of foam that comes and goes without moon. And the echo arrives early. You clap, or the water slaps, and you hear the return a fraction before the sound, as if the walls anticipate.

The rule of the place, which you learn without being told: don't turn your back on the inhale. When the pool draws down, step up to the windbreak. Keep your hands out of the main gyre. Drink from the seep on the east wall, not the pool — the pool is rock-flour and old snow and will grip your stomach. Leave the cairn alone. Someone balanced it for a reason with numb fingers.

Stay until your clothes are soaked through and the roar has cleaned out everything in your head, until the ordinary daylight on impossible water starts to feel normal. Then climb out, up into thin, quiet, dry air, ears ringing, and the notch closes behind you in cloud again — a hidden lungful of ocean, high and cold and loud and almost empty, still deciding what it wants to be when the mountain finishes making it.
