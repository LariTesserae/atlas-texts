At first, from below, it looks like a hill.

You climb out of the warm clouds — and the air should be cooler this high, it *knows* it should be cooler, your lungs work a little harder for it — but it isn't. It stays warm, bathes-warm, held-warm, as if the mountain is breathing. The trail does not get lighter as you rise. It gets darker.

This is high country. You can feel it in the drop. The slope falls away on both sides of the ridge into nothing you can see, a warm black that smells of rain that fell somewhere else yesterday. Far below, valleys exhale mist. Up here the ridge widens into a long saddle, a mile or more across, between two blunt peaks you will never see in this light. Town-sized. Valley-sized. You could walk all night and not cross it.

And it is not rock. That is the second thing you learn, after the darkness.

At first glance in the dimness it has the shapes of settled land — rounded shoulders, gullies, terraces, overhangs, hollows where water would pool. Old shapes. Nothing raw or broken. Everything weathered smooth, sagging into itself, in balance. But put your hand down and there is no stone. There is no soil either.

There is *make*.

Every surface has been worked. The ground under your feet gives slightly and does not give way: a mattress meters thick of interlaced grass stem and stripped bark and rootlet, of mud pellets pressed beak-round, of chewed leaf pulp dried hard as paper, of silk and wax and feather-down felted together, of droppings compacted over centuries into a kind of warm mortar and then re-dug and re-plastered. Old stick platforms sag and have been built over with new stick platforms. Mud cups are stacked on mud cups until they form walls. Woven pouches hang from woven pouches until they form curtains. You think you see a bank, and closer — eyes adjusting — you see the weave. You think you see a cliff, and it is a thousand thousand swallow-cups cemented edge to edge, weathered by rain at the lip into long drip-teeth. You think you see a meadow hummock, and it is a termite rise grown house-high and gone green with moss and then tunneled by something else and patched.

There is no natural substrate left to stand on. Even the trees that hold parts of it up are hardly trees anymore. Their trunks are cores inside a thicker trunk of nests. Their branches are widened to three times their girth with thatch and guano and orchid root. What is lush here does not grow *beside* what is built. It grows *out of* it. Ferns uncurl from the side of an abandoned weaver pouch. Seedlings root in the damp crumble of old guano terraces. Vines stitch one hanging colony to the next until you cannot tell where plant stops and animal architecture begins. In the near-dark, with only the last bruised phosphorescence of sky, the green is black-green, wet-black, but everywhere — dripping, brushing your shoulders, closing overhead until the sky is a rumor.

It is damp, not wet. There is no river up here to hear — you would hear it if there was, you would hear anything that wasn't animal — but water is held everywhere in small kept amounts. A cup-nest after rain holds a mouthful for a day. The broad leaves hold beads along their midribs. Bromeliads wedged in the thatch hold little private ponds, frog-sized. Mostly it is condensation. Warm air rising from below hits the cooler leaves and the feathered bodies and beads. It drips all night, irregularly, from a thousand edges: plink on leaf, plink on mud, plink on chitin. If you stand still your shoulders go speckled. The gullies are not carved by floods but by feet and by this endless drip, worn smooth and polished in the pulp-paper and dung-plaster, curving to cisterns that are themselves made — old collapsed chambers lined with wax that hold water for a week after a storm and then go dry and cracked until the next.

The darkness is almost total but not quite. No moon gets through the cloud and canopy. Your eyes, after an hour, make a grey-green day of it. Shapes separate: the long fringe of hanging nests swaying almost imperceptibly; the pitted face of the mud-cup cliff; the soft domes. Now and then a faint cool glow from fungus in a rot pocket, or the blink-blink of fireflies threading the understory, biological lamps, nothing else. No wire. No cut wood. No glass. No metal. No right angles that were not made by a body folding grass back on itself again and again.

And the bodies. That is what the darkness is for.

You never see the full number, which is good, because the number would stop thought. You feel the density as pressure. The air is occupied per cubic foot. Things brush your cheek and are gone. Things land near-touching on the stem next to your hand and call. The thatch underfoot hums through your soles. Above, the pouch colonies shift with weight — thousands of weavers and finches and honeyguides shoulder to shoulder, rustling, settling, rustling again. Mid-level, the swifts and oilbirds come and go from the cup-cliffs in tireless relays, wings slapping close. Lower, frogs the size of thumbnails call from their bromeliad ponds, hundreds per bush, and crickets and katydids lay a high silver sheet over everything. Beetles work the floor. Ants in columns wide as your arm carry green confetti overhead on leaf highways worn smooth as rope. Bats pour out of the deeper chambers in a warm leathery river and return. Mites, moths, feather lice, nest beetles — presences inside presences. Nothing lives alone here. To land is to land on someone else's roof. To eat is to eat someone else's refuse or neighbor. The smell tells you: sweet rot and fresh grass and warm feather and ammonia-sharp guano and night flower, all layered, all thick enough to chew.

And the sound —

It is physical. Deafening is too thin a word. It is not one loud thing but all the volumes at once, high to low, filling the dark from ground to canopy. The insect trill is a constant needle at the top of hearing, unbroken, so high and dense it becomes a kind of silence until it wavers. Under it the frogs: bottle-chirp, bottle-chirp, in overlapping rounds that never resolve. Under that the birds: chatter, scold, liquid contact notes, the leathery flutter-thump of ten thousand wings adjusting. Now and then something larger — a nightjar's churn, an owl's soft saw cutting through — and the colony answers with a wave of alarm that moves like wind over grass, rising and falling. If you press your hands to your ears you still hear it in your teeth, in the thatch through your feet. You have to lean close to hear the drips.

Yet for all this frantic occupancy, nothing here hurries. That is the rule you learn last.

No one is building fast. A mud pellet tonight, a grass strip tomorrow. The great forms — the cup-cliff ten meters high, the termite hill turned to garden, the ground-mattress itself — were not made this season or this lifetime. They accrete, slump, are patched, sag a little more. A tunnel collapses and is routed around over weeks. A terrace edge crumbles in rain and is re-cemented dropping by dropping. Generations have lived and died without ever seeing rock. The young hatch onto weave, learn weave, add to weave. Even the erosion is slow and lived-in: gullies polished by millions of tiny feet, rain-lips rounded, old silk gone grey and papery, holding.

This is the law of the high saddle: everything is made, and nothing is made by anything but a mouth, a foot, a beak, a gut. If you need water, you find it where a leaf or a cup has agreed to keep it. If you need footing, you trust what ten thousand ancestors chewed and pressed. The mountain underneath is only a rumor now, a shape the living have borrowed and covered over until the cover *is* the mountain — lush, warm, dark, damp, dense, roaring softly and endlessly in the night, extending past where you can walk, past where you can hear, still building itself, one beakful at a time.
