You climb for three days before you understand you have arrived.

Below you the lowlands fall away into a white sea of cloud that never breaks. Above you there is no peak, only more up. The air should be getting cooler. It doesn't. It gets hotter, thinner, brighter, until your lungs work and your skin shines with sweat at the same time. This is a high place that forgot to be cold.

Then the trail of cracked rock simply stops being rock.

The ground gives under your boot.

Not soft like mud. Springy, resistant, warm. A felt, a weave, a braid of roots so dense and so old they have become soil. Olive and rust and deep bruised green, matted with fallen wax scales the size of dinner plates, with hair-moss, with lichen that has settled into long weathered swirls like contour lines. If you dig with your fingers — and the people who live here will tell you not to dig deep — you find no earth at all for the first meter. Only layer on layer of living and half-living fiber, damp in the middle, dry on top, smelling of tannin and hot resin. The whole continent is a substrate. It is plant all the way down.

That is the first thing to understand: this is not a forest on land. This is a plant the size of a country, wearing a forest like hairs on an arm.

And the hairs are far apart.

From any one point you might see three, four of them. No more. They rise out of the root-ground impossibly far from each other, across miles of empty, sun-beaten mat where nothing vertical happens except heat shimmer. Each one is not a tree so much as an architecture. A trunk as wide around as a village, buttressed, fluted, barked in long peeling ribbons that hang and rot and re-root. A crown half a mile across, tiered, made of leaves that are each as big as a sail, waxy on top to throw back the noon sun, felted white underneath. Between the giants: emptiness. You can walk all morning on the bouncing, creaking hide of the world-mother with the sky huge over you and nothing to hide under, your shadow a tight black puddle under your feet. Sparse. That is the law here. Abundance, but spaced.

Because for all its green, there is almost no water.

There are no rivers here, no lakes, no streams. The old erosion — you can see it in the long, settled swells and shallow braided troughs in the root-ground, in the way the mat has healed over its own gullies with scar-tissue bark — that erosion is not from water running now. It is from a wetter age, or from the plant's own growth shrugging. Now the damp lives in pockets.

It lives in the axils of the giants, where leaf meets branch in cisterns that hold twenty, fifty gallons of amber rain-tea, warm and full of tadpole-things and mosquito larvae and rot. It lives in cup-leaves at ground level, low rosettes the size of bathtubs that open at night to catch condensation. It lives at dawn, for an hour only, when the hot air coming up off the cloud sea below hits the cooler crown-air above and the whole plateau sweats. Dew beads on every hair, every thread, drips, runs, is gone by nine. Animals know this. People know this. You learn to lick it off leaves, to wring moss into your mouth, to never pass a sky-cup after a storm-head without drinking.

For the plateau makes its own weather. That is how big it is. By midday the transpiration of ten thousand square miles of leaf rises in a visible sheet. It gathers over the center, white and anvil-topped, bruising purple underneath. Thunder walks around in there most afternoons. If you are lucky you get ten minutes of hammering, steaming rain that drums on the sail-leaves and floods the root-mat and fills every wrinkle, and then it is gone and steam rises off the ground and everything screams louder. If you are unlucky you watch the storm rain itself out three miles away, a dark curtain dragging over another giant, while you stand in dry heat.

Heat is constant. Normal light — a yellow-white sun almost straight overhead, colors true, no weird tint — but the light feels pressed close. Thin high air, thick sun. The wax leaves throw it back in glints. The open mat radiates it into your knees. Sweat dries almost as it forms, leaving salt. Under a crown it is ten degrees cooler and a hundred times louder and smells green and rotten and sweet.

Loud. You cannot prepare for how loud.

From a distance each giant hums. Closer, it roars. Part of it is wind combing through sail-leaves, each leaf creaking on a stem thick as your thigh. Part of it is the trunk itself: many of them are hollow, storm-split in youth and healed around the wound, and wind pipes through the fissures and resin-holes, low organ notes that you feel in your sternum. But most of it is animals.

Fauna here is not a presence. It is a saturation. A condition of the air.

Every giant is a city. Clouds of stingless amber bees move like smoke between flower spikes as long as canoes. Cicada-things the size of your fist sit in ranks on the undersides of branches and crank their sides until the air shivers — that high metallic saw that never stops, that layers and layers until your teeth ache. Gliders with membranes like wet paper launch from the high tiers in flocks of thousands, circling the thermals, shrieking. Clingers, suckers, chewers, sap-drinkers with needle mouths, bark-runners, things that look like monkeys and things that look like half-finished birds. At the water pockets, wasps and flies form a living crust. At night — though it never gets cool — the shift changes and the grunting and barking things come out to strip moss and hunt each other in the dark under the leaves. Nothing is ever alone here. If you stand still for a minute on the open mat, the ground itself seems to move: beetles, ants in highways, mites, skinks that swim through root-hair. And overhead always, the gliders and the seed-eaters and the hunters that eat them, falling and rising.

Predator and prey are on a fast clock. You can watch it in an afternoon from a platform: a hawk-analog with four wings stoops into a glider flock, scatters it, misses, perches panting, while below a mantis as long as your forearm takes a bee off a flower, while a root-snake takes the mantis. Everything eating, being eaten, laying eggs in the leftovers.

And the strangeness — because there is a strangeness that keeps you from calling this just jungle — is that the line between plant and animal is thin here and sometimes crossed.

Many of the animals root. You will see them in the still heat of early afternoon when the chorus dips for breath: furred, feathered things perched on low branches pushing a tendril-like tongue or a true rootlet into a nectar well and going torpid, photosynthesizing through symbiotic algae in their skin, green-blooded, still for hours. And many of the plants move like animals. The throat-flowers, ten feet long, mottled pink and carrion-purple, gape at dusk and actively inhale — you can feel the tug of air — drawing in moth clouds, then close, then at midnight exhale a burst of small, sticky, blind fledgling-things that flutter and are eaten or eat. The sail-leaves turn to follow not just sun but sound; a sharp clap will send a ripple across a whole crown as leaves angle toward it, listening. The giants are slowly, almost imperceptibly, walking. Not with feet. With lean. Year by year they lay down root on the windward side and let the leeward rot, shifting a few meters a decade toward storms, leaving behind a wake of rich, raised mat where cup-rosettes flourish. Old maps, the people say, are useless for more than a lifetime.

People. There are people, after a fashion, and their marks are few and far between, which suits the spacing of the world.

You will not find towns. You will find a platform lashed in a fork eighty feet up, made of cut sail-rib and fiber rope, with a thatch of wax-scale, empty now, a cold fire pit, a cache of thorn-hooks. A day's walk on, a rope ladder down a trunk, its rungs worn smooth. A shrine: a cairn of shed claws and seed stones and a carved rib-bone, left where a sky-cup trail crosses a beetle highway. A drying rack for glider meat, collapsed.

They live light because the host does not tolerate deep wounds. Their tools are what the plant gives them and what the animals leave: thorn awls as long as your hand, still barbed, bone scrapers from glider wings, fiber cord twisted from root-hair, leaf-sails for collecting dew, gourds made from unopened throat-flower buds. No metal. No forging — nothing to burn but green wood and resin that burns too fast and too smoky. They cut shallow, they lash rather than nail, they move when a crown gets too loud or too quiet. They drink after midday when the water in the cisterns has been sterilized by sun. They never cut the mat deeper than a finger. That is the rule the world enforces: take the surface, or the surface will fester and you will lose your footing for miles as rot spreads.

If you stay, you learn the other rules quickly because your body teaches them.

Walk early. Rest under sail at noon when the exhalation comes — every giant at once releasing spores in a hot, peppery sigh that hazes the air gold and makes you cough and makes the insects go mad. Drink at every chance; there will be miles with none. Sleep off the ground; the mat crawls at night. Keep your mouth closed when the cicada ranks peak; the sound is physical, a pressure that can make your nose bleed if you stand too long in the focus of a hollow trunk. And listen for the change in pitch that means rain on its way, or a hunter flock turning, or a throat-flower opening behind you.

At evening, from the top of a ladder, the view is the thing you will never get used to. Giant after giant receding to a green-gray haze, each crown lit gold on top and black underneath, each trailing its own small cloud of wings and spores. Between them, the empty shining mat, miles of nothing but living skin, steaming after rain. Below everything, on all sides, the cloud sea turning pink, and you on an island in the sky that is itself alive, hot, humming so loud you feel it in your teeth, vast enough to have weather and old enough to have forgotten any other way to be.

You are very small here. The plant does not mind you. The animals barely notice you unless you bleed or carry water. The heat presses down. The chorus goes on without pause, deafening, indifferent, total.

You drink from a leaf with both hands. You keep walking.
