It is high up, and it is drowned.

That is the first contradiction you have to hold in your body to understand it. Far below the sun, kilometers of black water stacked over your head, there is a mountain. Not a metaphor. A real mountain of fresh basalt rising off the abyssal plain, and near its summit — still two thousand meters below any air — the slope flattens into a shoulder. A ledge. And on that ledge, a town.

You come up to it from below, because there is only below. Your lights find the cliff first: a wall going up and up out of range, unnaturally sharp. This rock is young. It has not learned to be soft yet. The cooling cracks are still hexagonal, edges like broken bottle, unrounded by any current because there is no current here. No sediment drapes it. No green films it. Pillars and knives and steps of volcanic glass stand naked, glittering faintly where your beam crosses them, black on black.

Then the cliff ends, and the ground levels, and you are in the town.

It is small. You could walk it end to end in ten minutes if walking worked normally here. A hamlet, really, but unmistakably urban — not scattered outpost pods, but a plan. Terraces cut into the sharp rock. A plaza. Streets that meet at right angles and then stop, respecting an edge you cannot yet see. Layered construction: under the walkways you can see through grates to a second level of conduits and culverts and root-like anchors going down into the mountain, and below that a third glow, fainter, where something else is happening.

The buildings are sparse. That is deliberate. Vast black water between each presence. Perhaps nine structures total, each the size of a chapel or a small tenement, set far apart as if they are afraid of crowding each other, or as if emptiness itself is load-bearing. They are not built *in* the water so much as *out of a disagreement with* the water.

Because here is the technology you do not have words for: the town does not keep the ocean out. There are no domes. No glass bubbles. The streets are open to the sea. The doors stand open to the sea. And yet —

The water inside the town is not the water outside.

Step over the boundary marker — a low row of standing stones at the lip of the ledge, humming almost below hearing — and the pressure lets go. Not gone, but translated. The cold remains, that deep bone-cool of four degrees that numbs your lips even through a suit, but the crush is edited out. The water becomes breathable in a way that has nothing to do with oxygen, more like the town has convinced a small volume of ocean to behave as if it were air that happens to be wet. You can stand in the plaza with your helmet open and take it into your lungs, cold and iron-tasting and perfectly still.

Stillness is the law here.

Outside, the ocean should move. It should have tides and turbidity and marine snow falling forever down. Inside the markers, nothing drifts. A particle of silt kicked up off the plaza hangs where you kicked it and does not settle, does not rise. There are no gradients. No warm vent, no cold seep. No rust creeping on the metal-ceramic walls, no pitting, no growth. The edges of the buildings are as sharp as the rock they stand on — grown, not assembled, extruded as lattices that pin water molecules in place until water acts as rebar, as wall, as roof. You can put your hand *through* what looks like a wall and feel it resist only your intention to pass, cool and slick and utterly unmoving.

This is why nothing erodes and nothing rots. Why the town looks both newly made and immensely old. Time has been put into equilibrium here. The maintenance threads — thin lines of pale blue light strung between buildings like laundry lines, the town's infrastructure made visible — do not flicker. They simply are.

Light is scarce because light is expensive and, here, slightly rude. The town does not illuminate itself. It marks itself. Each building has a single seam of light, a cold bioluminescent white-blue, just enough to trace its corners against the black. The plaza has a shallow pool of it in its center, a circle set into the basalt that does not cast shadows so much as suggest that shadows are possible. Beyond those seams, darkness is total, velvet, pressing. Your own lamp feels blunt and colonial when you turn it on. The darkness beyond the last building is not empty — it is the drop-off, where the ledge ends and the mountain falls away again into deeper black. If you walk to the edge and look out, you see nothing for a very long time, and then very faintly, far below, the abyssal plain catching nothing.

There is no vegetation. Not a blade, not a mat, not a smear. No kelp, no algae, no bacterial felt on the stones. The world is mineral: glass, basalt, grown ceramic, pinned water. Even the idea of green feels obscene here, a rumor from another physics. What lives here instead are patterns. On some walls, faint interference fringes crawl so slowly you only notice them if you stare for an hour — energy-patterns left by the stasis field, or perhaps the stasis field itself dreaming.

Animals are rare to the point of event. The water is too still, too clean, too negotiated. Once in a long while a blind white thing comes up from below — a shrimp, a hake with unformed eyes, something elongated that was never named — drifts to the boundary, touches the hum of the standing stones, and turns aside, confused by water that will not carry scent. The residents, if they can be called that, do not keep animals. What moves in the streets besides you are the cleaners: slow, manta-shaped deficits in the water, not quite machines and not quite animals, that pass along the seams and eat disorder. You hear them only as a displacement, a faint *tock* as stillness closes behind them. They ignore you unless you stand still too long, in which case one may circle you, tasting whether you are part of the equilibrium yet.

Sound: mostly you hear your own blood. The ocean here has been muted. No groan of pressure, no distant whale fall, no grind of tectonics despite the youth of the rock. Only, if you hold your breath and stay very still in the middle of the plaza: the low, almost-subsonic tone of the anchors holding this pocket of stillness open against the weight of an entire ocean, and beneath that, rarer still, a single ping — metallic, distant — from somewhere below the terraces, repeating at intervals that are not intervals. It does not echo. Echo requires movement. Here sound goes out a short way and simply stops, as if politely laid down.

The rules, as you learn them with your skin:

1. Nothing falls unless you mean it to.
2. Nothing changes temperature. Your body heat does not warm the water around you. It leaves you and is gone, taken laterally somewhere you cannot follow.
3. Do not go beyond the last light. Not because you will be crushed — you will, but slowly — but because outside the markers, distance behaves again, and you will realize how small this place is, a lit ledge on a high mountain in a dark, moving world, and how far you are from any other negotiation.

You stand there, cool to the marrow, in a small, sparse, impossible town high up in the deep, where sharp young stone meets engineering that treats physics as a suggestion, where nothing grows and almost nothing comes, where the water does not flow and the lights do not waver, and you understand that it was not built to be lived in. It was built to prove that a place can be held — exactly here, exactly this size, exactly this still — forever, against an ocean that wants everything to dissolve, drift, and darken.

And so far, it is winning.
