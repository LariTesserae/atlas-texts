You don't walk in. You kneel.

The hatch is knee-high, stenciled 3-B in paint worn to a rumor, set in the side of the mill between the big duct and the foundation. Past it the floor drops six inches and the ceiling drops to where your back remembers every bolt. It is a room that was never a room — a void left over when they boxed the dust-collectors, a maintenance crawl, long and low and forgotten. From outside you'd never know it was here. From inside you can't believe how much is here.

The air is cool first. Not cold-storage cold, just the permanent cool of concrete in shade, of earth pressing close. It moves. An old extractor fan at the far end still turns on its timer, pulling a slow, dry draft from the louvers to the dark. Your throat dries in a minute. There is no water here. No drip, no puddle, no condensation even on the steel. Everything that is wet has learned to stay inside a body.

Light is ordinary, which is strange. Two caged work-bulbs buzz on wire guards and a grimy skylight the size of a lunch tray lets in a flat gray day. Enough to see color true: gray concrete, gray steel, gray-brown feather, rust orange, whitewash. No green anywhere. Nothing grows. Nothing has ever grown here. Even the dust refuses mold — it is mineral, salt-sharp with ammonia, fine as talc.

The floor is not a floor anymore. That is the ancient part.

Decades of feet — boots first, then claws — have worn the concrete pour into shallow bowls, polished at the centers. Over that lies guano-crete, inches thick, troweled by ten thousand shufflings into flows and rims like cooled lava, hard as plaster where old, soft and chalky where new. It feathers at the edges into down, seed husk, hollow bone, beetle husk, all felted together. If you press your palm to it, it holds the cold and gives back a faint warmth from underneath where decomposition works without moisture. The whole terrain has settled into its final shape. Nothing sharp remains. Pipe elbows are sanded bright on one side. The corners are round. Even the fallen bolts have lost their threads.

And on every surface — that is the press of it — birds.

Not a flock. A filling. Pigeons wall to wall in a low, breathing mass, shoulder to shoulder on the two longitudinal pipes, packed three deep on the cross-braces, lining the cable tray like beads, carpeting the raised ledge where the collector hoppers nose down. Feral blues and checks and whites and a few rust-reds, heads tucked or turning, iridescent necks catching the work-light as they shift. Between them, sparrows stitch the gaps. Below them, on the floor, the young and the grounded shuffle and peep. Above them, when something startles, a sheet lifts all at once and the air becomes wing — a hot, dusty thunder that hits your chest and dies back into cooing before you can cover your ears.

Because it is loud. You expect a roost to be soft. It isn't. It is a factory floor made of throats. The baseline is a low, rolling coo, hundreds overlapped until it sounds like water would sound if there were water, a pouring murmur. Over that: wing-claps like thrown cards, squeaks of squabs, the dry skitter of claws on metal, the tick of falling seed husk, and underneath it all, always, the mill itself — the 60-cycle hum of the transformer on the back wall, the extractor's unbalanced wobble, and every eleven minutes the shake-down auger kicking on with a clank-shudder that runs through the pipes and into your teeth.

The machines still work. That is the deal here. No one tends them, no grain runs through them anymore, but the timers never got the memo. Lights on at six. Fans on purge. Auger shakes. A junction box clicks warm. And the birds have built their calendar around it.

They ride it. Watch: when the auger starts, the pigeons on that pipe don't flush — they lean into the vibration, feathers puffed, eyes half-closed, as if being dusted. The warmth is real. Eggs are laid deliberately in the lee of the transformer where the steel stays five degrees warmer; those chicks hatch early, naked and blind and shouting. The sparrows nest inside the dead motor housing, weaving in feather and wire-clipping and plastic tie. A broken pallet in the corner has become a cliff-town of nests. A single upturned hard hat holds three eggs.

Nothing is wasted because nothing comes in easily and nothing leaves except by wing. Foraging parties go out the broken louver in shifts — you can see the traffic lane in the dust, lighter where wings beat — and return with cropfuls of cracked corn from the rail siding, bread from town. What falls is eaten below. Rats work the floor margins, sleek and bold in the flat light, cleaning the dead and the addled eggs with surgical quiet. Meal moths work the feather-felt in silver flickers. A red-tailed hawk sometimes dares the louver at noon and the whole room inverts — floor to ceiling in half a second, a screaming feather-storm in a box too small for flight — and then settles, panting, one bird fewer.

That is the rule of this pocket: keep low. Do not stand — you can't. Move on the auger's time, not yours. Breathe through your mouth because the dust will pack your nose. Do not expect quiet or space or softness. The room is full to the thickness of a wingbeat, layered from guano floor to pipe to brace to cable, each level occupied, each body touching the next. If you sit still long enough, the mass reclassifies you as structure and flows around your shoulders, a pigeon landing on your boot, eyeing you with orange, unafraid, proprietorial.

After a while the flicker gets you. The work-bulbs pulse at just under the wingbeat frequency, so when a bird takes off nearby its wings seem to stall mid-air, hanging for a frame too long — a stutter in the world. You blink and it is normal again. Cool air, dry throat, the warm stink of bird and hot oil and old concrete. Coo-pour. Hum. Clank-shudder.

Outside is the rest of the mill, empty acres of silo and yard. Inside is this: a single, low, sunken lung, ancient and worn smooth, with no water and no green, lit like an office, humming like a factory, crowded beyond counting with hot, loud, jostling life that has learned to live off vibration and warmth and each other.
