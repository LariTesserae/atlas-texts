The place is a room that thinks it is an ocean.

You get to it by climbing — long after you should have stopped climbing. Up service ladders gone slick with condensation, up through thinning air and screaming wind, up past the last cloud deck where the sky goes hard blue and the earth below is only weather. The tower keeps going. It was built so long ago no one builds like that anymore, rivet by rivet, mile after mile, to wring water out of the high air. At its very crown, tucked under the intake crown like a secret kept from the sky, is Tank Nine.

From outside it is nothing. A blistered dome, a rusted hatch, a number you can only read by touch. Inside, it opens — and it is small enough to hold in your head all at once, twenty strides across, a cylinder with a domed lid — and it is completely full of elsewhere.

Everything here is made. There is no ground, no stone that was not carried up and bolted down. Floor plates, wall plates, ceiling plates, stanchions, pipes, catwalk, ladder rungs, portholes, valve wheels. Even the silt under your feet is manufactured: a soft, reddish-black flour of iron oxide, ground down over centuries until the seams and welds and catwalk diamonds have settled into their final, forgiving shapes. The edges are all gone. Stairs have become ramps. Bolt-heads have become smooth kisses. Stalactites of rust hang from the ceiling joists, but they are old stalactites, stunted and rounded, worn back down to nubs by warm rain that never stops. This place has finished eroding. It has reached the oldest stable state a made thing can reach — not collapsed, just softened, like a reef.

And it is drowned.

The air is water and the water is air. The condensers above are still running — no one ever told them to stop — and they sweat and drip and pour, industrial and patient. A wall of warm rain falls constantly from the ceiling grates, hissing white in the daylight from the oculus. The tank itself is flooded to the chest, to the chin in the middle where the floor plates have sagged into a shallow bowl. Bath-warm water, engine-warm. Under the floor, you can feel it through the soles of your feet: the low, stupid, faithful throb of the circulation pumps, *dum-dum, dum-dum,* shaking the whole volume. Every seven minutes the big overflow pipe gulps and the level drops a hand's breadth and then climbs back. The room breathes.

Light comes down normal and milky through the three-foot oculus and the ring of portholes. No drama to it. Just day. In the shafts of it the rain looks solid. At the portholes — thick glass, green with algae at the rims — you see only falling white and, far, far below, a slow churn of cloud. That is the only reminder of how high you are. A gull-feather vertigo. You are swimming at the top of the world.

Nothing is crowded here. That is the rule of the tank. A few presences hold a lot of emptiness between them.

There are four columns, widely spaced, weeping water from their flanges. There is the broken catwalk, only half remaining, its cut end smoothed to a soft, lipped mouth. There is a valve wheel the size of a cartwheel, furred in moss. Each one is an island, and each island is jungle.

Lush beyond reason. Because everything is warm and wet and lit and iron-rich, life has taken the metal and embroidered it. Long curtains of emerald filament algae hang from pipes and sway in the pump-surge like hair in a tide. Ferns grow out of rivet lines, their roots white and reaching down into the water. Water lettuce and duckweed raft in slow gyres around the columns. On the catwalk beams, where it is just above the splash, moss has built pillows six inches thick, and from them hang aerial roots and small white orchid-like flowers that open in the damp. You can push through a hanging garden and come out the other side into ten feet of open, dark, empty water. Then another garden.

And the animals. They are everywhere once your eyes adjust to the emptiness. Clouds of almost-transparent shrimp pulse in the light shafts. Armored pillbugs the size of thumbs graze the moss. Eels, black and muscular and warm as the water, slip between floor plates. In the deeper middle, a school of pale, blind catfish circles endlessly, following the pump gyre, mouths open. Above — because there is an above, a foot of humid air under the dome where the rain is born — swallows have come up from impossible distances to nest on the joists. They cut through the rain shafts screaming, then fold and dive straight into the water to snatch shrimp, bursting back out in an explosion of droplets. Their mud nests are made, remarkably, from rust-mud. Guano streaks the walls white-green and feeds the ferns. It is a whole food chain running on rust and rain and light, turning over and over, loud and busy.

For it is loud. The rain roar never stops. Under it: the engine throb, the clank-tick of expanding pipes, the seven-minute gulp and sigh of the overflow, the plink and patter of drips off a hundred softened edges, the shrill stitching of swallows, the sudden slap-splash of a diving bird or surfacing eel. You have to raise your voice to be heard by yourself. Your own breathing sounds underwater.

There is a strangeness here you only notice on the second hour. The water is layered and the layers refuse to mix. Knee-deep and below, it is faintly salty, dark, and still, smelling of iron and old oil. Chest-high and up, it is fresh rain, clear and moving. When you wade, your legs disappear into a different sea than your chest is in. The catfish live in the salt; the shrimp live in the sweet; the eels commute. And in the interface between them, a thin, shimmering skin forms — bacteria, oil, pollen from the high flowers — that holds footprints for a moment, that tears and heals. If you lie back very still and float, you are held up by that skin, cradled between two waters, staring up into the falling white while swallows stitch the air inches over your face, and the whole tower sways almost imperceptibly in the high wind, a pendulum nine kilometers long, and the water in the room stays perfectly level.

That is the law of Tank Nine: keep moving to stay still. The pumps must pump, the rain must fall, the overflow must gulp, the shrimp must rise, the birds must dive. If anything stops, the balance tips and this small, ancient, man-made sea — this hidden pocket ocean perched above the clouds — will remember it is only a leaking room.

So you do not touch the valve wheel. You hang your hammock from the catwalk nubs, above the splash line but below the swallows. You sleep in short naps between pump cycles. You eat watercress. You listen to the iron breathing around you, warm as blood, old as the idea of making things, holding its little emptiness faithfully against the sky.
