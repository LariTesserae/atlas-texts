It is warm before it is wet.

You drop through the green gloom and expect the cold to take you at twenty meters, at thirty — that familiar squeeze — but it never comes. The water stays bath-warm, blood-warm, held. It thickens around your limbs like broth. By the time your eyes adjust you understand you are not in open water at all. You are inside something.

A bowl. A sump. A drowned hamlet cupped in a collapsed place below the seafloor.

From above it would have looked like a dark pit in an otherwise pale shelf — a sinkhole, a caldera no wider than a cathedral nave, maybe a city block if cities were built straight down. Its rim is a ragged lip of limestone and coral rock, and then the sides fall inward, terraced by intention as much as by geology. Because the pit is occupied. Fully.

There is no empty water here.

That is the first rule you learn: there is no room between presences.

What from a distance read as reef resolves, as you sink, into roofs. Into walls jammed wall-to-wall, eave-to-eave, pipe-to-pipe. A workers' town that was once organized — you can still read the organization — two tight lanes crossing, row houses of brick and poured concrete for the hands, a larger hall for the boilers, a stack, a crane arm frozen mid-swing. Now everything is welded together by growth and by rust. Alleyways are not alleys but slots, a shoulder-width between buildings, choked with cable and kelp. Vertical shafts of pipework climb the pit walls like vines made of iron, manifolded, valved, flanged with brass that has gone green-black. Catwalks sag between second stories. Chains hang and do not swing. You could cross the whole place in fifty slow kicks, from rim wall to rim wall, if there were space to kick. There isn't. You have to pull yourself through, hand over hand along warm railings.

It is industrial, unmistakably, but an industry that has learned patience.

Low down, in the deepest pocket of the bowl where the heat wells up, sit the machines. Three boilers in a row, squat as beehives, their rivets bearded with white sponge. A flywheel taller than a man, its spokes furred in crimson algae. A stamping press, a lathe, a pump house with its mouth open. They are running. Or after-running. No one tends them, no coal feeds them — the heat is the earth itself, a slow vent breathing up through cracked basalt under the foundations, keeping the water at this unnatural summer and turning some trapped pocket of steam, some thermosiphon the old engineers tapped. So the flywheel turns. It turns so slowly you doubt it at first. You watch a single spoke and hold your breath and count your heartbeats and yes — it has moved a finger's width. The piston beside it sighs out a coin of oil that does not rise, but hangs. The stamping press lifts, hesitates for a long minute, and comes down on nothing with a soft, felt *thud* you feel more in your sternum than hear.

Because it is quiet here in a way factories should never be.

Water takes the clamor and folds it up. What would have been roar and shriek and whistle is reduced to a low bodily throb, a tick of expanding metal, the occasional *tink* of a chain link settling against another. Your own bubbles are loud going up — too loud — so you learn to breathe shallow and let them leak small. Silt hangs in the warm water and does not settle; every movement stirs a milk that stays. If you are still enough, you can hear the place creak as it breathes: brick sighing against iron, kelp sawing softly on a girder.

Light barely reaches. The sun is a rumor, a dirty teal disk very far above the rim, filtered through sixty feet of plankton and particulate snow. By the time it gets down into the bowl it is exhausted, green-gray, directionless. Shadows pool permanently under eaves and inside doorways. And yet the town is not dark. It makes its own dimness. From cracked conduit and drowned junction boxes, from cage lamps still sealed in their glass, a weak amber electrics leaks — not bright enough to see by, but bright enough to see. A filament here, a strip there, buzzing faintly, never quite dying, fed by whatever slow current the vent dynamo still coaxes out. The lights pulse, very faintly, all together. On a sixty-second cycle, dimmer, brighter. Like something asleep.

Everything weathered has stopped weathering. That is the second strangeness. The rust should have eaten the iron to lace by now. The brick should have slumped. Instead the decay halted mid-sentence and made a treaty. You see it in the edges: all corners rounded, all lettering on the signs — MERCY IRON & LIGHT, No. 3 SHAFT, CANTEEN — softened to braille, but still legible. Sponge has concreted over concrete. Oyster and barnacle have bolted plates tighter than rivets. The town is long-existed, settled into its own state. It is not a wreck. It is in balance.

And it is lush.

Wherever iron meets warmth and that dying amber light, life has packed itself in until there is no surface left unclaimed. Not jungle-lush, not riotous — crowded, upholstered lush. Ribbons of pale kelp, almost white for lack of sun, stream from every gutter and handrail, twenty feet long, moving in the faint thermal upwelling like hair in bathwater. Blue-green mats of algae quilt the roofs. Anemones cluster in the valve-wheels, opening and closing with geological slowness. Black mussels beard the crane cable from top to bottom. Tubeworms plume from the boiler seams where the water is hottest, their red gills the only vivid color in the whole bowl. Leaves and fronds and filters fill every gap between buildings so that to move through a street is to brush shoulders, hips, face against living tissue and warm metal at once. The smell — even through a regulator — is iron and iodine and hot stone.

Animals, though, are almost absent.

That is the third rule: the garden does not want guests.

In a place this fertile by all rights there should be schools of fish, jacks flashing, sharks patrolling the rim. There are not. The pit seems to repel them, or they have learned not to enter the warm broth. In an hour you might see three presences and no more: a single blind-white guppy flickering between rain-barrels, mouth working; a pale eel, thick as your wrist, living permanently inside the flywheel housing, emerging only to taste the water; and high up on the rim, a sea turtle that comes to look down but never descends, its silhouette holding against the light. No crabs scuttle. No urchins. Even your own body feels like an intrusion the water tolerates but does not invite. Your kicks stir up clouds of copepods that glitter briefly in a lamp-glow and then vanish back into the kelp.

The weirdness is not dramatic. It accumulates.

Bubbles, for instance, do not always rise here. In the hottest layer, just above the vent grates, exhaled air shatters and hangs, or sinks a few inches before deciding. Air itself is trapped where it should not be: duck under a low doorway into the canteen and your head breaks into a pocket of stale, warm air held against the ceiling by nothing but pressure and habit, thirty years old, smelling of oil and soup. It should have dissolved. It hasn't.

The machines, you realize after a while, are not just overgrown. They are photosynthesizing in a way machines should not. The algal mats on the boiler backs are plumbed — literally plumbed — into the condensate lines, green filaments growing *inside* copper tubing, visible through cracked glass gauges. The town has become a single organism that happens to have streets.

If you stay still long enough, pressed into a slot between a row house and a water tower, wrapped in white kelp, breathing slow so you don't cloud the water, you feel the cycle the place lives on. Not tides. Not day. Something slower. The thermal lifts, the flywheel turns a degree, the lamps brighten for their minute, the anemones open, the eel shifts in its housing, the press comes down on nothing it has been stamping for decades — perhaps a plate for a ship that will never sail — and lifts again. Warmth holds you without current. Dimness holds you without dark. The hamlet, packed to bursting and utterly empty, goes on producing its patient, useless work at the bottom of its bowl, below the sea, below the surface of the world, asking for no witness.

You will have to leave soon to find colder, emptier water. Your fingers are pruning despite the warmth. The silt you stirred on entry still hasn't settled.

The press comes down again. Softly. Through bone.

*Thud.*
