From a distance it looks empty.

That is the first lie of this place. A high, warm table of land — not a peak, not a range, but what is left when mountains give up. The old uplift worn down until it forgot it was ever jagged. You walk up for a day without ever really climbing, the grade so patient you only know you are high because your breath comes a little shorter and the clouds, in the morning, are below you.

The light is ordinary. That is important. No red sun, no violet haze. Midday white-gold, honest high-altitude light that flattens nothing and hides nothing. It shows the stone for what it is: dark basalt and pale sandstone both sanded to the same tired dun, pocked and honeycombed, rounded at every edge as if handled for a million years. Which it has been — by wind, by rare rain, by heat. There is no sharpness left here. Even the broken rocks have soft mouths. When you tap the larger boulders they sound hollow. Most of them are. The wind and the salt of ancient seas ate their centers out and left thin shells, tafoni domes you could crawl into, egg-thin stone.

The air is warm and thin. Warm on your skin, thin in your lungs. It smells of hot dust and a faint iodine sweetness, like kelp remembered by rock. This whole table was once seafloor. You can still find the ghosts of it — coiled ammonites weathered in relief on slabs, ripple marks frozen mid-ripple on what is now a hillside two thousand meters above any tide.

There is almost no soil. Just pavement: interlocking pebbles, iron-dark varnish, dust in the seams. And water — but not where you expect it.

There are no streams. No springs that run. Water here waits, it does not travel. The top of the plateau is dimpled with solution pits, tinajas, rock bathtubs from the size of a cupped hand to the size of a house. Most are dry, showing cracked silt and a white tidemark of lime. A few hold. After a rain — which comes sudden, hard, out of a blue sky, once every few weeks in the wet season — they fill tea-brown and opaque. Then over weeks they shrink, become clear, become damp mud, become dust again. In between, in the mornings, the cloud ocean below breathes up and over the rim, and for two hours everything drips. Condensation beads on every pebble, runs down into the pits, darkens the stone. You can hear it if you crouch: a tick, tick in the hollows. The place drinks fog.

Because of that, the vegetation is a rumor. Not a cover, but a punctuation. Grey-green cushion plants wedged so tightly into cracks they look like moss-covered fists. A succulent with a swollen, bottle trunk no taller than your knee, its leaves waxy to hold the morning drip. Black and orange map-lichens spreading a millimeter a decade across the boulders. A thorn scrub, leafless most of the year, that puts out tiny red flowers for three days after a pit fills. You could walk a hundred paces between green things and think this is a desert.

Then you look down, and the ground moves.

Not the stone. What is on the stone.

They are crabs. Pale amber, dust-colored land crabs, from thumbnail to helmet size. Millions of them. They cover the open pan from rim to rim, shoulder to shoulder in the hollows, layered two and three deep in the shade of the boulders, tucked into every pit edge, motionless. At first you mistook them for more pebbles. Their carapaces are matte, pitted by sun, many of them carrying a living coat of lichen, so they vanish into pavement until one shifts a leg a centimeter and the whole field shivers.

This is the density of the place. Not forest. Not grass. Animal weight. A living gravel. In the cool undercut of a tinaja rim you will find them stacked like dishes, the big old ones on the outside, the soft just-molted ones in the middle, all breathing slowly through the damp air, their gill chambers fluttering.

And they are quiet. Overwhelming, but quiet. That is the second rule here: make no noise you cannot afford. There is no birdsong chorus, though small mottled lizards flick between shells and a few high hawks circle, waiting for a straggler. The sound of the plain is mostly absence — wind smoothing over worn stone without anything to catch it — and then, underneath, the small sounds: the faint dry tick of chitin on chitin when a crab resettles, the soft scrape of claw on lime, your own boots crunching too loud, and in a pit that still holds water, the almost inaudible plip of a crab lowering itself in to wet its gills, staying for hours.

They move slow. Everything here moves slow. A crab might travel ten meters in a day, in a great, decades-long circuit around the basin, following the memory of moisture. You can see their highways if you know how to look: faint polished trails in the dust, converging on the deepest pits.

They build. Without tools, without fire, without anything you could call technology, but they build unmistakably.

Scattered across the pavement are low rings — circles of gravel, broken shell, and cemented lime, a foot high, three to ten feet across. From above they look like ruined foundations, like someone tried to lay out a village and abandoned it. They are dew-traps. The crabs pile the rubble grain by grain, and at night the rough surface condenses fog just a little faster than flat stone. The water trickles inward to a damp center where the juveniles huddle at dawn and drink through the tufts of setae on their legs. Some of the rings are fresh, sharp-edged. Others are ancient, collapsed, fused into the bedrock, lichen-grown, with generations of molted carapaces crushed into their walls until they are part geology. There are also middens: white heaps of picked claws and molts, and burrows under slabs, the entrances smoothed by a thousand passages.

Nothing else builds here. No fences, no cairns that are not crab work, no cut wood. If people ever came, the stone and the crabs took their leavings long ago.

The strangeness does not announce itself. It accumulates. That crabs should live this far from the sea, this high, in a place that is mostly dry — that is strange enough. But then you notice the old ones do not quite move like crabs. They have grown heavy, slow, their joints stiff with lime. Lichen grows on their backs so thick they carry little gardens. Tiny mites and springtails live in that garden. The lizards eat the mites, not the crab. The crab, in turn, will eat its own molt within hours of shedding it, and the molts of its neighbors if they are slow — calcium is the currency here, more precious than water, because to live so far from the ocean you must make your own sea inside your shell and never spill it.

Stay a full day and you learn the rules, because the place enforces them gently and without exception:

Stay low and pale or dry out. Move at night and dawn or not at all. Water is not a flow but a pause — find where it pauses and wait with it. Eat everything twice. And do not expect the land to change for you; it finished changing long ago, and now it only settles, flattens, hollows, waits for the next rain to fill the same pits in the same stone.

By late afternoon the fog is gone, the pits glint, the crabs have gone still again, sealed in. The warm stone gives back its heat. The plain extends past walking — a town-sized openness of pits and rings and hollow boulders and living pavement, ringed by low, utterly worn hills that were once cliffs. You can hear your own heart. Then, if you are very still, you hear the plain answer: a thousand tiny closings, a thousand breaths held until morning.
