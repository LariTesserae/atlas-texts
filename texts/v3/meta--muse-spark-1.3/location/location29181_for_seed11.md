You come up and the air goes thin before you expect it to be warm.

That's the first wrongness. At this height your lungs should be aching with cold. Instead the air is warm, wet on the back of the neck, equatorial noon-light lying flat and white over everything. You are very high — high enough that the horizon has bent, high enough that the clouds are below you in the mornings — and still you are sweating.

At first you think it's a plain. Then you walk it and understand it's the top of something.

An old mountain range, so old it has given up being mountains. Eons have ground it down to this: a tableland of pale whaleback rock, pink-grey gneiss and quartzite polished to a shine, domes and shallow pans stretching for a valley's width in every direction until the edge simply stops. No peaks. Only long, low swells like the backs of buried animals, split here and there by deep, narrow cracks, by round potholes big enough to lie down in. The stone is soft to the eye, all roundness, lichen-black and rust-orange in patches, gritty under the boots where wind has left a dusting of sand. If you kneel you can see the fine striations, the ripple of ancient rivers frozen in rock.

Nothing here is sharp except where it has broken yesterday at the rim. And at the rim, the world falls away two thousand meters straight into boiling cloud.

It is not empty the way a desert is empty. It is empty the way a huge room is empty. Space between things is the main material.

Between the domes are hollows. That is where the green lives.

Not a forest. Islands. Each damp declivity, each crack where seep has collected for a thousand years, has exploded into lushness so dense you can't see into it more than an arm's length. Tree-ferns ten feet tall, their trunks thick with moss. Wild ginger, orchids with waxy white throats, vines as thick as a wrist that drop aerial roots like curtains. Stunted cloud-trees, bent double by wind, their crowns matted into a single green felt. Leaves enormous, dripping even when it hasn't rained, because the cloud itself condenses on them. Inside one of those hollows the light goes green and the heat closes in and the smell hits you — rot-sweet, humus, crushed leaf, warm stone wet underneath.

Then you climb three steps out of it and you are back on bare, ringing rock with fifty meters of nothing to the next green pit. Lush and empty at once. The green does not spread. It waits where the water waits.

And water does not stay. There is no lake here, no river you can put on a map. There are only habits of dampness. In the morning everything weeps: the rock sweats condensation that runs in beads and gathers in the potholes, enough to cup in your hands and drink. Midday the seeps wake along the cliff lines, dark streaks where water bleeds out of the stone and feeds the hanging gardens — mats of moss and fern clinging vertical over the void. In the afternoon, almost every afternoon, the sky breaks.

You will learn to watch it. The turbulence never stops.

Clouds do not drift here. They boil. They come up from below, over the rim, pouring like slow waterfalls in reverse, white and grey and bruised purple, moving impossibly fast. The light changes every minute: full sun that burns your shadow into the rock, then a green gloom, then a shaft that lights one distant dome and nothing else. Wind gusts hit without warning strong enough to stagger you. Then rain — not a drizzle, a sudden vertical sheet, warm as blood, hammering the stone so hard it bounces knee-high, turning every shallow pan into a mirror, sending a hundred temporary streams hissing toward the cracks. For twenty minutes, waterfalls throw themselves off the edge all along the rim, white ribbons that shred to spray before they hit bottom. Then, as fast as it came, it stops. Steam rises off the rock. The potholes are full. By evening they will be half-empty, drunk by moss, evaporated. Damp, not wet. A place that is always drying out from the last drowning.

That is why it is so loud.

You expected high silence. There is none. The sound is physical, a pressure in the chest.

The base note never leaves: wind hitting the tableland edge and funnelling up through the chasms, a deep organ roar that you feel in your molars. Over it, the high whistle where wind is forced through natural arches and fluted pinnacles left by ancient erosion — a chorus of flutes out of tune, rising and falling. When the rain comes the drumming on bare rock is deafening, and the temporary falls add their own white-noise scream as they go over. And even in the lull, the vegetation rattles. The big leaves are stiff, almost like tin, and thousands of them clatter and shiver in every gust.

It is loudest in the hollows. The domes ring. The stone here is hollowed underneath in places, thin shells over old cavities, and footfalls boom. Shout and your voice comes back wrong — flattened, then a half-second later too loud, as if the rock threw it back at you. The old herders say the mountain listens. You understand why when you put your ear to the warm stone at dusk and hear a low hum, wind moving in chambers below, like the place breathing through its teeth.

There is almost no one to hear it with you.

Rarely, very rarely, something moves. A glider — huge, black, wingspan wider than you are tall — tilts high above the rim for an hour without a single flap, watching. You might see one a week. Frogs the size of a thumbnail live in the bromeliad cups in the green pits; you never see them, only, after rain, hear a single glass-bell note that is instantly swallowed by wind. Once a year the white moths hatch all at once from the moss in a blizzard that lasts an afternoon and then is gone, their bodies left papering the potholes. Footprints in mud show a small deer-like thing came to drink in the night and left. That's it. Life is concentrated, hidden, holding its breath between gusts.

People have been here, but lightly, and long ago, and still a little now.

You find them by tripping on them: a low dry-stone windbreak, waist high, curved like an arm around a hollow's edge. A bothy of stacked slabs with a turf roof, just big enough to crawl into, smelling of old smoke and damp wool. Cairns at the crossings — piles of flat stones with a stick thrust in, a scrap of cloth tied on that snaps violently in the wind until it shreds. Below one dome, a handful of tiny terraces, hand-built, their walls collapsing, where potatoes and quinoa were coaxed from grit and guano. A stone quern left upside down. A digging stick, polished by hands, leaning against a rock.

No metal, or almost none. Everything is stone on stone, wood, woven fiber. Things made to be mended with what is at hand, or abandoned. The paths are not paths, just places where lichen has been worn off by feet over centuries.

The rule of this place, once you stay long enough to feel it in your knees, is this: the ground is finished, the sky is not.

The rock has settled into its final form. It will not erode further in any time you can imagine; it is too hard, too round, too tired. It will just sit here, warm, humming, while everything above it — water, cloud, wind, light — rages and creates and destroys itself a dozen times a day. The plants understand. They don't try to conquer. They hunker in the damp pockets and grow furiously lush where they can, and let the vast polished emptiness go.

At night the wind drops a fraction. The roar thins to a hiss. Condensation ticks off leaves into potholes, drip, drip, drip, impossibly loud in the sudden space. The stars are close enough to hurt. Far below, lightning flickers silently inside the cloud sea. Up here the stone still holds the day's warmth under your back, and if you lie still you can feel it vibrate, very faintly, with the sound of water finding its way down through ancient cracks toward an edge you cannot see.
