First you think it is a bog.

From the lip of the low trough you look out over a valley a day's walk wide, flat as a plate, and impossibly green for how cold it is. The light is ordinary, midday-milky, a pale sun smeared behind high cloud. No drama in it. That is what makes the green so strange — there is no summer here to explain it. Your breath smokes. The rail you lean on is rimed with frost and already furred with moss on its underside.

Then the bog resolves into roofs.

It is a town, a whole packed industrial town down there in the raw, and the green is on top of it. Every roof, every wall, every pipe and shed and stack is quilted thick with living stuff. Not decorative. Necessary. From up here you can see the pattern: black timber terraces jammed wall-to-wall, no gaps, no yards, alleys thinner than a doorway, and over them all a continuous pelt of moss and dwarf fern and liverwort, white-tufted cotton and crowberry vine, spilling from one building to the next so you could walk half the town without touching ground. Between the houses, narrow braids of slate-grey water glint. Beyond them, spoil heaps and cinder cones, also green. The ground itself is new.

This valley floor was ice not long ago. Everyone knows it. The till is still blue-grey and unsorted, clay and shattered shale and dropped boulders, not yet soil, not yet carved by any river into any shape. It heaves. Frost lenses grow overnight into low white domes that crack the lanes and tilt the privies. In the morning crews come with screw-jacks and long levers and simply jack the houses level again. Everything here is built on skids, on rollers, on stacked peat blocks. Nothing has foundations. Foundations are a rumor from the south. Here the rule is: build light, build loose, let the earth finish being born under you.

That is why from a distance the town seems to shimmer and crawl. It is turbulent with motion, anthill motion.

Look longer and the quiet hits you. An industrial town should roar. This one doesn't.

Trams glide down the central grades on rubber-shod wheels, sparking faintly blue at the wire, full of standing workers in oilskin and wool, saying nothing. Belt-lines run overhead between factory mounds, carrying peat bricks, fish boxes, iron pigs, turning on tall wooden trestles that creak — only the creak never quite reaches you. It is swallowed. A crane swings a boiler plate across a yard and sets it down without a clang. A thousand chimneys, squat and moss-lipped, breathe white steam in unison. The steam does not rise far in the cold damp air. It sags, condenses, becomes drizzle, becomes rime on every wire and line.

The damp is not a lake or a sea. There is no big water here, only pockets and braids and condensation. The river comes in from the melt and immediately loses its mind, splitting into fifty shallow braids that argue around gravel bars and under houses on stilts. They shift every night. A street that was dry yesterday has six inches of clear, aching-cold water today with eels in it. Men lay duckboards without comment. Children pole little punts between warehouses. And even where there is no open water, there is water: it beads on every rivet, drips from every eave, sweats from the underside of every walkway. You are damp within an hour. Your coat gains weight. Your eyelashes bead.

Sound behaves oddly in that wet cold. The moss-quilts eat it. They are thirty centimeters thick on the factory walls, cultivated, combed, stitched with twine like blankets over engines. Lift a corner — and everyone does, once — and inside is black heat and scaled production: rows of lathes, dynamos humming so low you feel it in your molars more than hear it, women in felt boots feeding white-hot rivets to a press that closes with a soft *whump*. Then drop the quilt and the sound is gone. The whole town has agreed, by engineering and then by custom, to stay muffled. Sharp noise cracks the new clay, they say. It startles the ground-mud. It brings down rime. So no shouting, no whistles, no exhaust bark. Even the dogs have learned to woof under their breath.

And yet it is frantic. That is the contradiction you live inside after an hour down in it.

The market braid is shoulder to shoulder, but silent: fur traders, eel smokers, moss-cutters with their long green bales, ironmongers, wick-sellers, boys carrying live coal in insulated boxes. Everyone signals with hands, with elbows, with raised eyebrows. Above them, on the moss bridges between roofs, flows a second traffic — runners with message tubes, harbor foxes trotting purposeful lines with scraps in their jaws, iron-grey rats moving in disciplined columns along the pipes, long-tailed tits erupting in white flurries and resettling. The fauna is not decoration. It is labor. Geese in hobbled flocks weed the roof gardens and turn the small paddle-wheels in the culverts with their paddling. Eels keep the culverts clean. Foxes keep the rats honest. Rats — sleek, almost blue in winter coat — haul twine and find lost bolts. No one owns them exactly. They have joined the industry.

Everything is packed. There is no room between presences. Moss grows over iron grows over wood grows over moss. A fern grows from a crane joint. A birch, dwarf and twisted, grows from a gutter and has a pulley hung from it. On a single wall you can count: basalt block, tar paper, moss-quilt, fern, lichen, frost, a nesting box with tits, a steam pipe sweating, a vine of crowberry with black berries, a child's chalk mark, a new crack from last night's heave with raw mud welling up.

Ah — the mud. That is the strangest part, the part they don't tell until you see it.

In places at the raw edges of town, past the last sheds where the ground is still bare and steaming faintly despite the cold, springs well up. They bring up warm grey milk. In the cold air it skins over and hardens within hours into new stone, pitted and light. The earth is still manufacturing itself. Overnight a new hummock can rise knee-high in an alley, breaking duckboards. The survey boys mark it, the moss crews seed it, and by spring someone has built a lean-to on it. The town grows outward not by clearing but by waiting for ground to happen and then immediately occupying it, quilting it green before it can erode. There has been no time yet for gullies or cliffs or valleys-within-valleys. Erosion hasn't started. This is pre-erosion, embryonic geology, and the town is acting as its skin.

Rules, unspoken after a day:

Keep the quilt on. Never scrape a wall bare. Bare iron screams in the cold and the frost will take it.

Follow water, don't fight it. If a braid comes through your shop, cut a hole and charge a toll for punts.

Jack, don't fix. Nothing is level for long.

Stay quiet. Speak low. Let the tits and the drips and the soft whump of presses be the loudest things.

Because under the normal light, under that flat white sun, the place is working at full tilt without raising its voice — a lush, freezing, sodden engine-room the size of a valley, too new to have scars yet, too crowded to have empty space, steaming gently into its own condensation, greening over its own iron as fast as it can rust it, and shifting, always shifting, under your feet while you try to stand still and listen.
