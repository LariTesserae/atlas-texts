You get in through the service slit, sideways.

It's not a room anyone was meant to stand in. On the plans — if you could still call up the plans — it would be a sliver, a 2-meter void left between the outer weather-skin of the Stack and the inner pressure hull, up where the air is already thin. Floor 311's ceiling, Floor 312's floor. A mistake in tolerance that they kept because the building needs to sweat.

Outside, far below, the lowlands are in haze. Up here the wind wants to tear. In here it is warm as a mouth.

That's the first thing. The heat. Not hot like sun, but stored-body heat, machine heat, animal heat. The air is thick and does not move right. It hangs on your skin instantly, beads on your upper lip. You can hear your own breathing go shallow, and then you can't hear it at all because of the sound.

The sound gets in before the sight does. In a pocket this small it shouldn't be possible to be deafened. But the whole interstice is a drum.

There is a low roar that is the building breathing — air forced through the baffle-fins to keep the skin cool, a constant waterfall of atmosphere being shoved past a gap no wider than your shoulders. And layered on top of it, inside it, is living noise so dense it becomes physical. A shivering, clicking, chirring wall of it. The peep-peep-peep of a thousand throat-sacs going at once. The dry papery detonation of wings. The tick of claws on metal. You feel it in your sternum and in your teeth. You have to open your mouth to equalize.

Then your eyes adjust to the dim, and you see why it's so crowded.

Every surface is made. There is no stone here, no earth. Floor, ribs, ceiling are all built — a ribbed alloy, old ceramic baffles, a sprayed-on skin that was once smooth and is now pocked and scaled with age. The place has been here long enough to weather. White mineral lace has grown over everything where water runs, and rust-brown and verdigris films in the hollows. The corners are softened, not sharp anymore. Cables sag in catenaries furred with dust and down. Nothing is new, but nothing is failing either. It's settled into itself.

And everything is wet.

Water condenses straight out of the warm air on the cold baffles — the outer skin is night-cold even in day, because altitude. The engineers didn't fight it; they farmed it. The ribs are strung with field-condensers, black glass teeth that are colder than physics says they should be, that never need power. They weep constantly. Drops gather, swell, let go. The walls cry in lines. The ceiling drips. Underfoot — where you kneel because you cannot stand — the deck plate has a permanent skim of water an inch deep, warm as bathwater, rippling from impacts, draining sideways and somehow uphill along a capillary gutter toward the sump throat that glugs and gurgles, never quite overwhelmed.

You cannot put a hand down without putting it in water. You cannot lean without your shoulder brushing a wet rib. The whole pocket is near-touching.

Light is almost nothing. The work lights died decades ago. What remains are the standby dots — a string of amber maintenance photons embedded in the lower rib, dim as dying embers, and the cold blue bleed from the condenser teeth themselves. It is enough to see sheen, not color. Enough to see movement everywhere.

Because it is full.

At first you think the walls are moving. Then you see they are covered.

Moths, first. Pale, dust-thick service moths in their hundreds, plastered to the damp alloy around the dots, shivering their wings, drinking condensation. Midges in clouds so dense they look like smoke, parting around your face and re-closing. Roach-like cleaners, flat and mahogany, working the waterline in lines. Cave crickets with legs like wire, springing from rib to rib.

And the frogs. Little amber tree-frogs, no bigger than a thumbnail, that should not be two thousand meters up in a machine. But here they are, by the thousands. They sit in the mineral cups, in the cable trays, on every horizontal lip, throats pulsing. That's the peeping — a deafening amphibian chorus in a metal box, amplified by the ribs until it rings. They eat the midges. Something has to.

And the things that eat the frogs. Geckoes, translucent-bellied, crowded into the warmer seams near the inner hull, eyes like wet beads catching amber. Spiders the size of your palm have strung their sheets across the narrower gaps, beaded with water, sagging with trapped wings. In the darkest end, where the slit narrows to a hand-width and you can hear the outer wind scream, the swiftlets have mudded their half-cup nests to the vertical baffle — gray birds crammed shoulder to shoulder, chicks gaping, parents flickering in and out through the wind-slots with impossible timing, adding a constant leathery rush-rush to the roar.

There is almost no green. A smear of black-green biofilm where the water runs slowest. A tuft of etiolated moss in a bolt-hole, pale and leggy from no sun, surviving on lamp-glow and guano. A single fern, stunted, curling out of the sump grate, its fronds coated in lime. The plants lost here. This food web doesn't need them. It runs on guano and drowned midges and skin-shed and the endless warm rain that was never rain.

The smell is warm mineral, ozone from the teeth, sweet rot, bird musk, frog spawn.

The rules of the pocket make themselves clear fast:

Keep low. The ceiling teeth will kiss your scalp cold and leave a numb stripe if you rear up.

Don't block the gutter. If your knee dams it the water backs up hot around your shins in seconds.

Don't drink the bright water straight off the teeth — it tastes of metal and leaves your tongue buzzing. Drink the standing water where it's gone still and warm, if you must.

Don't swat. Everything here eats everything else and the balance is total. Knock a spider sheet down and the midges will cover your eyes in a minute. Startle the swiftlets and a hundred wings in a closed drum will deafen you past pain.

And don't stay still too long. The crickets will test your bootlaces, the geckoes will watch you with utter indifference, a frog will climb onto the back of your hand because you are the warmest, driest vertical in its world, and its tiny cold belly against your skin will be the most intimate thing you've felt in the Stack.

Outside the skin the air is thin and clean and kills. Inside this sweat-seam, four meters long and barely wide enough to crawl, life has packed itself layer on layer, wing over throat over claw, thriving in the dark warm drip, shouting to be heard over the building's endless exhale.

You back out with your clothes soaked through, ears ringing, flecked with moth-scale and down, and for an hour afterwards you still hear the peeping, faint, under every other sound — as if the pocket came out with you.
