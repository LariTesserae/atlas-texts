There is a room up high that was not built.

You find it because the slope stops. You have been climbing a long time through thin, cold air — scree giving way to turf giving way to bare, wind-scoured granite — and then the ground dips, just a few strides across, a bowl no bigger than a hamlet garden, walled on three sides by rock that leans inward. From above it would be nothing, a dimple. From inside it is everything.

It is night here, even when it is not night elsewhere. The walls are too high and too close and the cloud is too low. What light gets in comes grey and strained through mist, the color of water in a cupped hand. Your eyes never quite adjust; they keep trying and failing. After a while you stop looking for distance and start looking close, and close is where this place lives.

Everything is wet and nothing runs.

Water hangs in the air more than it falls. It beads on every moss tip and fern hair and then gets heavy and lets go, but not far. The rocks weep. They are old rocks — not sharp. Long-existed, settled into themselves, their edges rounded, their faces pocked into small cups and channels by ten thousand years of this same dripping. Each cup holds a dark mirror. Each channel feeds another cup. Nothing drains away. The bowl has no outlet that you can find. The water comes out of the stone, sits in the moss, lifts back into the air as breath and mist, and comes down again. Near-surface, under-surface, in-surface. If you press your palm to the ground, water rises around your fingers without you pressing hard. The soil, if it can be called soil, is a sponge of rotted leaves and liverwort and black peat, saturated to bursting.

It is cold. Not biting, not winter. The kind of cool that lives in high shadow — that makes your breath faintly visible and your fingertips go slow. The air smells of wet stone and crushed green and something animal, musky and sweet.

There is no bare ground. That is the first rule here.

Moss on rock on moss on peat on root. Cushions of star moss and feather moss piled two, three deep, so lush they swallow sound. Ferns — small alpine ones, hardy, their fronds no longer than your thumb — unfurl from every crack. Lichen, sage and ash-white and sulfur-pale, crusts the upper faces of boulders where the drip is lightest. Under the overhang, where it is darkest, the white threads of fungi lace everything together, and from them rise tiny translucent caps, wet as eyeballs. You cannot put a boot down without putting it on something living. So you don't. You crouch, you kneel on a stone that is already upholstered.

And they are here. All of them.

At first you think the ground is patterned. Then the pattern breathes.

Salamanders — black alpine salamanders, fat and slow, dozens to the square foot, piled flank to flank in the moss hollows, their skins slick with seep. Newts the color of wet bark, coiled around each other for warmth that isn't warmth. Toads no bigger than a knuckle, eyes like polished jet, throats pulsing but making no call. In the cups of water: clouds of tadpoles, black commas, so dense the water looks solid until it shivers. On the walls: snails with paper-thin, almost clear shells, moving a millimeter an hour, leaving silver trails that never dry. Midges that do not swarm, moths — pale, high-altitude moths — that do not fly because their wings are too wet; they walk, absurd and patient, over the backs of amphibians, over fern fronds, antennae waving.

There are so many bodies that the place is warm in patches from them, despite the cold. No room between presences. A beetle crosses a toad's head. A worm surfaces between two salamanders. In the moss, springtails pop like sparks when you breathe too close.

It should be loud. It is almost silent.

That is the second rule. No one calls. A throat pulses, no croak comes. A moth rubs its legs, no sound. What you hear, when you hold still long enough for your own blood to quiet, is water letting go: *tik* on a leaf, *tup* in a cup, the softest *sill* as a snail extends. And underneath, a low, collective wet breathing — hundreds of small sides going in and out — like wind in a distant pine, but it isn't wind. The air here does not move. The cloud sits.

No one built anything here. There is no wall, no cairn, no cut stem that is not regrown, no charcoal, no path. Your knees leave a dent in moss that slowly, very slowly, exhales back.

But there are tools, if you know how to look for tools.

On a flat anvil stone by the largest pool — a stone polished hollow by drips — lie a scatter of empty snail shells, all broken on the same edge, in the same way. A thrush, or something like a thrush, dark and almost flightless, hops down while you watch, picks up a snail in its beak, and knocks it, left-right-left, against the stone until it cracks. It eats and leaves the hammered shells for the next. In the pools, caddis larvae have built themselves mobile houses out of sand grains and spruce needles and tiny fragments of shell, perfect tubes they drag as they graze. Under a lip of rock, a shrew — impossibly dense fur, nose constantly working — has used a sharp flake of quartz, wedged in a crack, to wedge open beetles; the flake is stained, worn on one side from little hands — not hands — guiding. Nothing made, nothing kept. Hand-tools in the moment, then dropped. Primitive manufacture that leaves no monument.

The strange part is not any of this. The strange part is how it stays.

A drip falls from the overhang into the same cup it has fallen into for longer than you can imagine. The cup is not getting deeper. The moss at its lip is not getting beaten back. The salamander in the hollow below it has not moved, except to breathe. The moth walking across the fern has been walking across the fern. You watch for a long time, cold seeping into your knees through the sponge-ground, and you realize no succession is happening here. Nothing is colonizing, nothing is dying back, nothing is flowing downhill. It is in balance to the point of stillness — weathered into its final shape and then held.

Even your being here feels temporary in a way that will be edited out. The mist closes behind you. The water rises back around where your hand was. The breathing continues, quiet, packed, wet, in the dark up high where the world folded into a small, saturated room and decided to stay exactly as it is.
