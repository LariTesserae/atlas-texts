It's a pit, first of all. Not deep, not dramatic — a shallow bowl sunk into low country, the kind of place you walk down into without noticing until the wind stops.

From the rim you can see the whole of it at once. That's the size of it. Fifteen minutes to cross if you don't stop, and you will stop. A ring of fresh spoil heaps closes it in, black-gray and sharp as broken teeth. Nothing here is rounded. The stone was cut or dumped yesterday in geological time — quarry faces still showing drill marks, slag blocks with bright fracture planes, cinder paths that crunch underfoot and haven't yet been worn to dust. Even the ditches have hard shoulders. It is a young land, raw-edged. Rain hasn't had time to teach it manners.

Inside the bowl: a town that shouldn't be called a town. Six or seven big buildings, spaced far too widely apart, with a lot of nothing between them. That's the rule here — leave space.

There is a mill with its tall brick chimney, unlit most days but breathing a thin heat-shimmer. There is a machine shop with corrugated walls gone wavy with rust-patches. A boiler house, a warehouse with its doors permanently open to the weather, two rows of workers' houses with slate roofs. They are urban buildings, proper town buildings, with curbs and lampposts and manholes and a grid of streets that implies a much larger place. The streets go out from a central cross, too wide for the traffic, patched with tar snakes. Overhead, the emptiness is stitched shut: fat insulated pipes on trestles running building to building, catenaries of black wire sagging between poles, a belt-drive housing like a covered bridge. Underfoot, you can hear culverts ticking. Layered construction — the town is more connected above and below than it is on the ground.

Between the buildings is where the green lives. Because it is lush here, aggressively lush for an industrial yard. Not parkland, not planned. Moss up to the ankle on the north side of every wall. Ferns unrolling from mortar joints, from the gutters, from the cracked concrete pads where something was supposed to be built and wasn't. Bramble and buddleia and slender white birches leaning out of the spoil. Bindweed climbing the guy-wires. In the open lots the grass grows knee-high and stays wet until noon, bent with dew. The town is sparse, so the plants have taken the interval. They grow around, never quite over — as if they respect property lines.

It is cool here, even at midday. Normal daylight, bright overcast that makes colors honest — red brick very red, leaf very green, iron very black. But the air pools in the bowl and stays cool. Your breath shows until ten. The metal shows it longer. Everything metal sweats.

That's the water of this place. There is no river. No lake. Just damp as a condition. Night condensation beads on every pipe, every railing, every leaf, and by morning it all drips. Drip from eaves, from flanges, from the underside of the pipe bridges. The ground holds it in shallow, iron-stained puddles in tire ruts and in the footprints of the paving — pockets of sky lying in a larger dryness. Once or twice a week a thin rain comes through, not enough to wash, just enough to refresh the film. It smells of wet stone and hot oil. Your jacket will be damp at the shoulders by the time you leave. Your boots will be dark at the toes. Nothing ever quite dries, nothing ever floods.

And it's loud. That surprises people because nothing seems to be moving fast.

The mill engine idles all day. Not producing — just turning. A flywheel twelve feet across turning once every twenty seconds, slow as a minute hand, and each time the pitman comes around the whole building groans low in its foundations, a sound you feel in your molars. The boiler sighs and ticks. Somewhere a ventilator fan is out of balance and rattles its housing in a steady *tak-tak-tak-tak*. Steam is let off at irregular intervals with a white roar that startles the pigeons up all at once — a hundred wings clapping like laundry. Then the dripping resumes, and the starlings take over, and they have learned to do the machines perfectly: the squeal of a dry bearing, the hiss, the clank. The air is crowded with sound bouncing off the spoil ring, unable to leave the bowl.

Because the animals own the intervals. For so few people, there are so many lives. Pigeons in the chimney courses and on every warm vent, lined up by size as if placed. Starlings ribboning over the lots. Wrens in the bramble. Rats along the culverts, bold in daylight. Three-legged cats asleep on the transformer boxes where it's warm. Goats — someone's, everyone's — cropping the lots between the shops, bells dull with verdigris. Frogs in the ruts, fat and unbothered. Beetles under every lifted board. Moths around the lamps even in daytime because the lamps are always on, buzzing sodium-orange in the cool brightness. The fauna has moved into the industry the way the moss has — nesting in it, warming on it, drinking its sweat. A robin has built in the elbow of the steam exhaust, and the workers put a little shield over her so she doesn't get scalded. That's the level of negotiation here.

Change is patient. Rust blooms slowly under the condensation and is wire-brushed back slowly. Buddleia splits a concrete seam over three years. The flywheel turns, the goats eat, the drips fall. Nobody hurries. The work, if there is work, is maintenance: oiling, sweeping water, clearing leaves from grates, coaxing the old engines to keep idling. Production scaled down long ago to just keeping the place itself alive, electrified and mechanized but going nowhere in particular. A factory for keeping factories.

There is one unusual thing, and you notice it after an hour. The green follows the iron. Seedlings line up exactly under the pipe runs where condensation falls in a nightly line. Moss grows thickest on the vibration side of the machine shop, where the hum shakes the dew into the brick. Ferns lean toward warmth, not light — toward the transformer, the boiler wall, the culvert grate. Even the pigeons space themselves along the warm pipes at exact intervals. It's as if heat and water have replaced sun as the organizing principle. The town is a small, cool, damp sun made of engines, and everything lush and living has turned to face it.

Stand at the cross in the middle at noon. Cool light flat on your face. Mill groan to your left, fan rattle to your right, goats pulling grass with soft rips, starlings doing a perfect imitation of a wrench dropped on concrete. Smell of coal-smoke and chlorophyll and hot grease. Wet glint on everything. Sharp black ridge all around, holding the sound and the weather in. Wide empty streets with grass coming up through. Buildings waiting, patient, sweating, humming low.

You don't need to go further. There's nowhere further to go.
