You go down to it, and that is the first rule. Not up, not across. Down.

A service stair, poured concrete with the rebar showing where the edge has been knocked fresh — sharp, un-softened, yesterday-broken though no one remembers when yesterday was — drops thirty meters through salt and basalt into warmer air. Your ears pop. Your mouth dries out on the way down. By the bottom step there is no spit left.

First you think it is a factory basement. Then you think it is a cave. Then you stop trying to decide, because it is more itself than either.

It is a room. That is the size of it. Not a cavern system, not a mine. A single, bounded place you could walk across in three minutes if you could walk in a straight line, which you can't. Maybe a hundred and fifty meters long, half as wide, the ceiling low enough in places that the hanging pipes almost brush your hair. You can see the walls all the way around, all the time, curved and blasted and new. The rock faces are fresh, angular, unrounded by any water — because there has never been water here. No drip, no seep, no pool. The stone was cut or fell and then stopped. The fractures still have edges that will cut a palm. Dust hangs off them like frost, but dry.

And it is crowded. Impossibly crowded for a room this small.

From floor to ceiling it is packed, layered, near-touching. Vertical pipes in bundles of ten and twenty, fat ventilation trunks furred with salt, cable trays sagging with black cables, catwalks that end in rock, a belt conveyor that runs the length of the room carrying nothing but fine white dust from one pile to the identical pile on the other end, forever. Between the machines, the animals. On the machines, the animals. In the machines.

The air is warm — body-warm, breath-warm,  furnace-room warm without being hot — and dim amber, from caged sodium work-lamps crusted over with salt until they glow like coals. They do not flicker. They do not die. One red EXIT sign buzzes over a door that opens onto solid rock. The light doesn't reach the corners so much as stain them. Shadows pool under the motors and vibrate faintly, out of sync with anything.

There is almost no green, and what green there is has learned to be dry. In bolt-holes and weld seams sprout tufts of wireweed — black, copper-stiff filaments that tremble in the fan-wash. On the lee side of the pipes, salt-whiskers grow in white starbursts, brittle as glass. Rust-lichen spreads in orange scabs across the iron plates on the floor. That's it. No soil. No leaf that would wilt, because there is nothing to wilt with.

But everything else lives here, and loudly.

Furnace-crickets by the thousands pack the warm housings of the pumps — pale, eyeless, long-antennaed things that eat dust and lichen and each other and sing without pause, a high metallic sawing that sits on top of everything. Lamp-moths in clouds orbit each salted bulb, wings the color of ash, so many that the air itself seems to snow upward. Copper beetles with backs like rivets click along the conveyor, falling off, righting themselves, climbing back on. In the cable trays nest the rats — naked-tailed, pink-nosed, blind as thumbs — sleeping in piles for the warmth of the current, waking to gnaw insulation and lick salt.

You cannot move without brushing life. A cricket lands on your wrist. Moths hit your face, dry as paper. The air is dense with chitin-dust and salt-motes and shed wings; it never clears. You breathe it and taste ozone and hot iron and musk and old oil. Your teeth ache with it.

Because it is loud. That is the thing you don't expect until your sternum is in it.

Two big exhaust fans, each taller than a man, turn in opposite walls, pushing the same hot dry air around and around the room in a closed loop. Under them, the piston pumps thump — *whump whump whump* — that were built to lift brine from a sea that isn't here anymore and now lift only air and dust and keep lifting it. The conveyor rattles. The transformers hum. And over, under, through it all, the crickets and beetles and rats add their own engine-noise. It is not cacophony. It is a chord, held. Deafening, but perfectly even. You shout and can't hear yourself, but after a minute you stop noticing, because it never peaks, never drops, never changes tempo. It just *is*, like pressure.

Nothing here changes, and that is the strangest thing. Stranger than the animals tending machines, stranger than the dust that never settles.

The dust should settle. It doesn't. It hangs at chest height in a permanent haze, lifted by the fans exactly as fast as it falls. The conveyor should wear a groove. It doesn't. The belt is bright where the beetles polish it, but no thinner than it was. The moths that burn on the bulbs are replaced — somewhere behind the pipes you hear the faint tick of egg-cases hatching — exactly as fast as they die. The rats eat the crickets, the beetles eat the rats' droppings, the crickets eat the beetles' shed shells and the lichen eats the dust of them all. A closed mouth eating itself, warm and dry and perfect.

Scattered through it like afterthoughts are the human things, too small to matter now: a control kiosk with its dials all frozen at mid-range, needles trembling but never moving. A lunch pail, rusted open, full of salt-whiskers. A time-clock with no cards. A single boot. Faint white arrows painted on the floor pointing to doors that no longer exist. No one comes to read them. No one needs to.

If you stand still long enough — and you will, because there is nowhere to go — you notice the rules that hold the place together:

One, nothing is wet, ever. Sweat dries to salt on your upper lip before it can run. Oil is the only liquid, and it stays inside its lines, black and endless, circulating.

Two, everything works and nothing produces. The pumps pump nothing to nowhere. The fans cool no one. The belt delivers dust to dust. The animals work the night shift of a factory with no product, cleaning contacts with their tongues, nesting in warm relays, keeping the current flowing by being warm themselves.

Three, time does not pass. The rock stays sharp. The bulbs stay dim but lit. The chord stays loud. You will leave with dust in your lungs and ringing in your ears and the absolute certainty that if you came back in fifty years, the same moth would be hitting the same bulb, the same cricket would be singing on the same pump housing, the same warm air would be making the same loop around this small, dense, buried room that learned how to keep going without ever going anywhere.
