You hear it long before you see it.

In the low woods, where the ground has already given up and gone flat toward the bog, the river finds one last ledge — not high, maybe twice your height — and throws itself over. From a hundred paces off it is only a rumor in the trunks. From fifty it is a pressure. From ten, it gets inside your ribs and takes the place of thought.

Then you push through the alder screen, wet leaves slapping your face, and the fall is there, and the hollow is there, behind it.

It is not a cave so much as a withheld breath. The river has spent ten thousand years chewing back into soft dark sandstone veined with harder basalt, and then stopped, satisfied. The overhang it left is low and long, perhaps twelve strides wide and only three or four deep — a pocket, a room you could miss if you blinked because of the water curtain in front of it. You have to duck sideways through a gap where the fall thins against the left wall, where the rock shoulders out. Cold water slaps your hood, finds your neck at once, and then you are inside, and the forest is gone.

The light does not follow you.

What comes through the falling water is not daylight but a boiled, green-grey translucence. It moves. The whole back wall pulses with it as the river thickens and thins overhead, as wind worries the curtain. For a second you can see the grain of the rock, the wet shine; the next, you are in near-dark, feeling for footing. Your eyes never fully adjust. There is too little to adjust to. Darkness lives in the corners here permanently, under the ledges, in the scalloped potholes at knee level where the old floods swirled pebbles round and round until they drilled perfect bowls and then abandoned them.

The air is cold in a way that is not weather but place. A stored cold. Even in late summer it would be cold here. Now, near the edge of the year, it is just above freezing. Your exhale hangs and is torn away. The stone sweats. Everything sweats. This is not a wet place in the sense of standing water — the plunge pool is *out there*, beyond the veil — this is a damp place. Condensation. Seep. The fall hits and atomizes and the backdraft pushes a fine, icy mist through the gap constantly. It beads on your eyelashes, on your sleeves, on the rock until the rock is black and slick. It gathers in the pits and drips, with a slowness that feels deliberate: *plik... plik...* — if you could hear it. You can't. The roar erases it.

The sound is the size of the place. It is deafening in the true sense: it deafens. It is not loud like a shout, it is loud like being inside a drum. Low white continuous thunder from the lip above, and under it a thousand higher hisses and cracks as the water tears itself apart. You feel it in your sternum, in your teeth. You try to shuffle your boots on gravel and hear nothing. You clap your hands inches from your ears and feel the motion more than the noise. If you came with someone else you would have to put your mouth directly to their ear and scream, and still lose half the words to the water. So you stop trying to listen outward. Your hearing turns inward — blood, breath, the creak of your own jaw.

And once the roar has flattened everything else, the small things begin to stand out because there is so little else.

The room is almost empty. That is its first truth. Vast space between presences. A floor of cold rounded cobble and coarse sand, scoured clean, sloping almost imperceptibly toward the curtain. A ceiling of black rock, low enough in places that you stoop, smoothed by flood and frost until it feels like worked soapstone. Nothing in the middle. A hollow held open.

Life lives only on the edges, in films and fringes — but where it lives, it lives lushly.

The entire back wall is green. Not the green of summer fields, but the deep, light-starved green of moss and liverwort and fern that has learned to drink air. Pillow moss, star moss, mats of it inches thick, swollen with spray, so saturated that if you press a thumb in, water wells up around it and stays. Hart's-tongue ferns uncurl from cracks where no soil should be, their fronds stunted, leathery, beaded with droplets that never fall because the mist replenishes them. In the zones closest to the curtain, where the light pulses strongest, the green is almost luminous, algal, slimy to the touch. Further back, in permanent shadow, it darkens to black-green, then to black. It smells of iron and peat and crushed stems. It grows immeasurably slowly — a millimeter a year, a patient creep outward from the seep lines — and because nothing disturbs it except drip and frost, it has become deep, tufted, ancient. You understand that this is a climax community in miniature: it will not become forest. It will remain exactly this, forever, as long as the spray comes.

There are almost no animals. The roar has evicted most of them, and the dark and cold do the rest. For long stretches you will think you are alone with rock and water. Then — a flicker. A wren, brown as bark, darts in through the side gap with a grub in its beak, lands on a moss hummock, cocks its tail, vanishes into a crevice you hadn't seen. Somewhere out in the pool a dipper works the white water, but you only catch its white bib as a flash through the fall. Once, in the shallowest edge of the curtain where stones make a shelf, you might see the shadow of a trout holding impossibly still in the chaos, waiting. In the deepest dark, high up where the basalt has cracked, a few bats hang in torpor through the cold months, furred pebbles, breathing once a minute. They do not hunt here. They only store themselves here, borrowed.

People have been here. Not many. Not recently, but repeatedly. That is the other thing the emptiness makes visible.

Against the right wall, where the overhang is highest and the drip is least, someone has laid a low windbreak of river stones — flat slabs set on edge, two courses high, forming a shallow C open toward the back wall. Inside the C the gravel is darker, mixed with ash and tiny flecks of charcoal from a fire dead so long the stones around it are cold through. No metal. The sticks left are hand-cut: alder poles, ends crushed and split rather than sawn, lashed once with twisted bark fiber now rotted to a stain. A leaning frame, perhaps for drying a hide or sheltering a pack.

Further left, half-swallowed by moss, a small cairn: seven or eight stones stacked with care, the top one balanced. And wedged in a crack above it, a offering of some kind — a strip of leather, a carved wooden dipper, a flake of chipped flint still sharp enough to cut. The carving is simple, finger-deep grooves: wavy lines for water, a circle for — what? The pool? The eye? The maker did not need to be precise. On the back wall itself, where the sandstone is softest, there are faint scratched marks under the moss: tally lines, an outline of a fish. You can just fit your fingertip into them.

No one lived here. You couldn't. The cold gets into bone in minutes, the noise gets into mind, the damp gets into everything and never leaves. But someone came to get out of rain, to wait out a flood, to trap the trout on the shelf, to leave something for the water and take something away. Primitive work, hand work, things that could be carried in and carried out.

The rules of this place announce themselves without words:

Stay low and stay back. The rock is slick and the curtain pulls.

Do not expect to be dry. Expect to be damp to the core and then a little damper. Your clothes will gain weight. Your skin will go numb and then tingle.

Do not expect silence to return. Even if you press your palms hard over your ears, the fall comes through your skull. After a while you stop fighting it and let it become a kind of darkness you can hear.

Do not move quickly. Everything here — moss creep, drip hollowing, frost prying one grain loose per winter — operates on a time so slow it looks like stillness. You are the fastest thing that will ever enter, and you feel absurdly fast, absurdly temporary.

If you stand long enough, your eyes watering from spray, your shoulders hunched against cold, the curtain thins for a moment with a gust and a shaft of real, grey forest light spears straight through and hits the back wall. The mosses flare emerald. The droplets hang in air like glass. The trout-shadow flicks. And then it closes again, and the roar takes it all back, and you are left in the humming dark with the smell of wet stone and the sense that you have been allowed, briefly, to be inside the river's skull — a small, cold, green, empty room that exists only because the water allows it, and will exist exactly like this long after your fire-pit has washed away.
