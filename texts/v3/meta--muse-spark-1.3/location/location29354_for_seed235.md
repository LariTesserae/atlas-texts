Above the clouds, where the air should be thin and cold, it is thick and hot.

You climb — or the world tilts up under you — for a long time through white nothing, through cloud that scrapes cold on your skin, and then you break through on top and the heat hits you like opening a cooking pot. The light up here is ordinary daylight, a flat tropical noon without drama, which makes everything else more impossible. The sky is pale blue-white. The sun is where it should be. And you are standing on a roof thousands of feet in the air, sweating instantly.

It is a mesa, a table-mountain cut off on every side. Basalt black and travertine white. From where you stand you can see most of it at once, valley-sized and open — not a peak but a country set in the sky. A shallow bowl of land, slightly dished in the middle, rimmed by a lip of rock, and beyond that lip, nothing. A sheer fall on all sides down into the cloud sea. You can walk from one edge to the other in a long afternoon, if you can walk straight, which you can't.

Because this is a city. And it was never built.

The whole top is grown dense.

Not forest-dense. Street-dense. Layered, shoulder-to-shoulder, roof-over-roof, root-over-wall. Trunks as thick as houses rise and fork and fuse, trained by centuries of weight and water into arches, colonnades, towers, long vaulted halls open at the sides. Giant bamboo, green-gold and hollow, grows in perfect palisades ten deep to make walls. Strangler figs lace themselves into lattices, stair-ramps, bridges that sag slightly and hold. Broad philodendron leaves bigger than doors overlap like shingles. Between them, everywhere, the filler: moss a foot deep, liverwort slick, film algae, white bracket fungi stacked like plates, hanging curtains of lichen and air-roots that brush your face as you push through.

There are neighborhoods. You can read them. To the north, the Cistern Fields: a checkerboard of shallow sunken pans, each rimmed with living root, each brimming with warm black water, connected by grown aqueducts — hollow vines big around as your torso — that gurgle constantly. To the center, the Hollow Towers: fifteen, twenty trunks braided around empty cores, sixty, eighty feet high, their interiors cool and dripping, entered by oval mouths worn smooth by water, not hands. To the south, where the land dips marshy, the ground itself gives up and becomes sponge — you sink to the ankle with every step into hot peat and sucking warmth, into sedge and pitcher plants big enough to drown your arm, all gaping, all full of rainwater and drowned leaves, none of them ever catching anything. There is nothing here to catch.

No birds. No insects. No frogs in the marsh. No fish in the canals. No people. The lushness is animal-less, a green engine running without consumers. Pollen hangs visibly in the air because there is nothing to carry it. Leaves unfurl and never get chewed. Spores drift and settle and sprout on the spot. Things grow into each other and graft. A vine enters a wall of bamboo and exits as part of the wall.

And everything weeps.

Water is near the surface everywhere, under pressure, finding its way out. The rocks at the rim sweat continuously. The basalt cliffs on the outer lip are black with wet, streaked with travertine, with long green beards of moss that drip without pause. The ground squelches. The air itself condenses on your skin, on every leaf underside, beading and falling back before it can evaporate. Hot springs breathe up from cracks in low white mounds — not geysers that erupt, but perpetual simmers, eye-holes of milky turquoise water that shiver and steam. Canals run warm as blood. Waterfalls start a few feet from where you stand and do not stop.

At the rim, on all four sides, the marsh drains off the world. Hundreds of falls, some wide as rivers, some only a curtain a hand thick, pour over the edge in a continuous white roar down into nothing. You can walk right to the lip — there is no railing, only a thickened curb of roots — and put your hand in the sheet as it leaves. It is hot. It falls perhaps a mile to the clouds below and vanishes into white. The mist it throws as it falls rises back up the cliff face in a warm updraft and becomes the low fog that drifts permanently through the towers and fields, so the city is always raining slightly upward.

The sound is physical. Deafening is too small a word until you are in it.

It is not one waterfall but the sum of them, plus the hiss of steam from every vent, plus the low organ hum of wind moving through millions of hollow stems. The bamboo palisades vibrate. The big leaves shudder. It presses on your eardrums, on your chest, on the soles of your feet through the ground. You feel it in your teeth. You open your mouth to equalize and the roar gets inside. You shout and hear nothing of yourself, not even vibration in your skull — your voice is taken instantly, added to the total without changing it by a fraction. Clap your hands hard in front of your face; you see the motion, you feel the slap, you hear nothing distinct.

And it never changes. That is the strangest part, stranger than the height or the heat.

In any real place the roar would swell and ease, a gust would pass, a cloud would muffle. Here the needle is pinned. The volume you hear in this second is the volume that was here a thousand years ago and will be here. The water level in the cisterns never rises, never drops, though water pours in and out constantly. Leaves do not fall and rot, though everything is soft and wet. They hang, perfect, dripping, neither young nor old. The light does not move. Noon holds. Your shadow pools short and dark under you and if you stare, after a while it seems to thin at the edges and lean, very slightly, upward, toward the sun, as if tired of the ground.

The place is long-settled in its form. Nothing is raw. The travertine rims are rounded, the channels are smoothed to living curves, the roots have found their final braids. It is weathered into balance and then stopped. Erosion should be sawing this table apart — all that water, all that heat — but the edge is exactly where it is. Pieces do not fall.

Walk far enough in any direction and the geometry loosens.

That tower you left behind — look back through its hollow core and you see another tower perfectly aligned behind it, and through that another, a corridor of identical mouths receding though you know there is not that much land. Step into a canal and the water parts around your ankles flowing both ways at once, warm current tugging north on your left leg and south on your right. Hold up two wet hands and each leaf behind them has a faint double, a half-inch offset, a green afterimage that does not fade when you blink. If you walk toward the falls keeping them straight ahead, you arrive at a different section of rim than you aimed for, but the sound is identical, so you cannot prove it.

The rules here announce themselves without words:

Nothing dries. Stone, wood, skin — all stay filmed with water. Towel yourself and you are wet again before you finish.

Nothing quiets. Silence does not exist as a memory after ten minutes. You stop trying to listen past it and start feeling with it, like weather inside your body.

Nothing is made. There is no cut stone, no joint, no nail, no edge that a tool left. Everything that spans was coaxed. The city is infrastructure — streets of compacted root-mat slightly springy underfoot, plazas where the moss has been worn (by water, by nothing else) into shallow bowls that hold an inch of warm mirror, drains that are stems, roofs that photosynthesize — but no one built it. It grew into the shape of dwelling because dwelling is a stable shape for growth to hold water and light.

Nothing walks. There are no paths worn by feet. Where you push through hanging roots they close behind you immediately, dripping, indifferent.

Stand in the middle of the Cistern Fields at what must be midday forever. Heat shimmers off every surface but the air never clears. Steam rises in slow columns that do not disperse. The green above you is three, four layers deep — high canopy of braided fig, mid floor of bamboo vaults, low mats of moss and pitcher and fern — each layer dripping into the next. Light filters down green-gold, normal as a greenhouse, ordinary as summer. The water at your feet is skin-warm and perfectly still despite the roar. Your own sweat mixes with condensation and runs down your arms and drips off your fingers back into the system without loss.

Beyond the green, white on all sides: the curtain of falling water and rising mist, the edge of the world.

You are very high. You are very hot. You are very wet. You are in a town with streets and towers and plumbing and no inhabitants and no builders, loud as the inside of a drum and changeless as stone, holding its impossible balance above the clouds while everything drips, steams, hums, and stays exactly, forever, itself.
