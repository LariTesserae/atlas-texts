There is a place high up where it should not be hot, and hot where it should not be wet — a hanging valley set so far into the sky your lungs notice it before your eyes do.

You climb for a long time through thinning air, through black rock and switchback cloud, expecting the usual bargain of height: colder, barer, quieter. It does not come. The air stays thick. It gets thicker. By the time the ridge breaks open, you are sweating through your shirt at nearly four thousand meters, breathing hard in soup.

The valley opens below you and beyond you — not a peak, not a gorge, but a broad, shallow trough a few miles across, cradled between dark escarpments. From the rim it looks soft. That is the first lie of scale. It is not soft. It is saturated.

Everything here weeps.

The cliffs that wall the valley are not dry stone. They are black basalt, old and settled, rounded at the shoulders by long weather, not jagged-new. Water comes straight out of them. Not in clean waterfalls, though there are a few of those, white and muscular where aUpended streams throw themselves off. Mostly it is seep: a thousand slow sweats beading along lichen and fern-root, joining, sheeting, falling as constant drizzle even when it is not raining. The rock faces glisten as if oiled. In the morning sun they steam.

The floor is not ground so much as sponge. Peat a meter deep, moss a half-meter over that, water an inch under that, or over that — it is hard to tell where lake ends and land begins. Shallow pans of water, tea-brown and iron-orange and milky blue by turns, lie linked by braided trickles that never quite decide their course. Step anywhere and water rises around your boot, warm as blood. The air smells of warm rot and mineral and green crush — sulfur low, sweet orchid high.

It is hot with a high, white heat. This close to the equator the noon light falls straight down, normal daylight, no amber, no gloom, just full drenching illumination that makes every leaf edge flare. But the light does not stay clean for long. Heat rising off the marsh meets the cool breath spilling over the rim, and the valley makes its own weather every hour. Clouds condense out of nothing at chest height. One moment you can see across to the far wall, its hanging gardens and silver threads of fall; the next you are inside a warm white room, visibility ten paces, droplets forming on your arms, on your eyelashes, on every hair of moss.

Vegetation does not grow here. It piles.

From a distance the valley floor looks like meadow. Inside it is jungle compressed to knee and waist and head height, then suddenly flung twenty meters up. Giant groundsels and tree-ferns rise on fibrous trunks wrapped in moss sleeves thick as your forearm. Their crowns hold tanks of rainwater where frogs live entire lives. Under them, a second ceiling of broad-leafed Gunnera, leaves big as shields, dripping constantly. Under that, ferns, sphagnum hummocks, sundews, bladderworts, orchids white and magenta clinging to anything vertical. Everything grows on everything else. Bark is not bark; it is a vertical garden. A fallen log is not dead; within a week it is green over and fruiting mushrooms from its sides. There is almost no bare soil to be seen. If you part the fronds, the presences are layered and near-touching — leaf over leaf over root over water — high saturation, no gaps wasted.

There are no trails. No walls, no cairns, no cut stumps, no plastic, no wire. Nothing made. That is important because at first, with your eyes closed, you would swear this place is industrial.

It works like a plant that never shuts down.

Listen — and the valley is mostly quiet, muffled by all that moss and water and cloud, so you have to listen under. Beneath the drip-drip-drip from a million leaf-tips, beneath the low insect burr and frog-click, there is a deeper rhythm: a chuff, a sigh, a long exhalation. Then again. On the eastern flats, where the peat thins over a fault, the earth breathes through clear throats. Hot springs well up glassy and trembling, 50, 60 degrees, building low rims of silica and rust. Mud pots blubber and pop. A geyser — not tall, not showy, a workmanlike thing — vents every eleven minutes or so with a piston-thump you feel in your sternum, throwing a plume of steam that the wind shears sideways to become the next cloud. If you put your hand near a vent you feel the push-pull, intake and release, like a boiler lung.

The water does the hauling. Every rill is a conveyor. Leaves, petals, drowned beetles, silica grains, pollen in yellow slicks — all moving steadily down-gradient toward the central marsh-lakes, where mats of algae and duckweed run their green production lines at full tropical speed, doubling, doubling. The cascades off the west wall grind perpetually where they land, potholing basalt into perfect cylinders, clacking stones against each other with a sound exactly like distant machinery, couplings loose in a mill. And before storms, which gather almost daily by three o'clock, the air goes electric in a literal way: hair lifts, the smell of ozone cuts through the sulfur, far-off sheet lightning stitches cloud to ridge without thunder loud enough to carry.

This is the rule of the place: nothing leaves. Heat comes from above — that vertical sun — and from below — that shallow magma breath. Water comes from above and below too, rain and seep and spring, and it cannot drain fast because the lip of the valley is high and narrow, a single notched outlet where the whole surplus pours off into blue abyss. So water cycles tight and fast: sweated out of rock, drunk by moss, transpired by leaf, condensed as fog, rained in an afternoon hammering, sweated out again. Nutrients cycle even tighter. Things do not rot here so much as get reassigned. A dead bird is mossed over in days,Digested by fungi, flowered through by orchids.

Because of that, animals are everywhere, though you have to be still to see them. Not large animals — the sponge will not hold weight — but abundant in number and insistence. Clouds of minute flies that glitter when the sun breaks. Copper-green hummingbirds working the high flowers in straight, businesslike lines, wings a blur that adds to the engine-hum. Rails and snipe picking through sedge, almost too tame. Frogs the size of thumbnails calling with soft glassy *tink, tink* from every puddle, hundreds of them, but so hushed and ventriloquial the marsh seems to tinkle like rain even when dry. In the warm pools, amphipods and blood-red worms writhe in densities that make the mud look alive. Up on the drier hummocks, leaf-cutter highways run for fifty meters, green fragments moving steadily like parts on a belt — scaled production without foreman or factory wall. Butterflies gather at mineral seeps in trembling orange carpets that lift all at once if your shadow falls.

The unusual thing — the thing you keep turning over and cannot quite name — is how directional everything feels without wind. Mist here often moves against the wind, drawn toward heat. Water in the moss moves uphill by capillary pull stronger than gravity at this scale. Stones on the flats ring faintly when stepped on, because they are hollowed by hot water below. And at night, if you stay (and the heat lets you, no cold to drive you down), the valley does not darken so much as dim to blue-green, and the springs glow faintly with bacterial light, lines of soft fire marking where the earth is thinnest.

No one built this. No one tends it. And yet it has the throughput, the noise-under-noise, the ceaseless venting and condensing and conveying of an industrial district — only all the pipes are veins in leaves, all the furnaces are pools, all the flywheels are water circling, and the roof is cloud remade every hour from the sweat of stone.
