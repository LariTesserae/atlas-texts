The heat does not lift after sunset. It settles.

This is low country, a broad shallow basin that has been low for so long it has forgotten any other shape. The hills around it are not hills so much as long, tired swells, rounded by ages of rain into red-brown laterite and clay. Nothing here is sharp. Old meander scars curve through the forest floor like pale ghosts — places where a river used to go, a hundred, a thousand wet seasons ago. The ground is weathered down to its balance: mud, root-mat, leaf mold deep enough to sink a hand into, and underneath, smooth stones worn egg-soft. It does not erode anymore so much as breathe, very slowly, in and out.

In daylight this would be green and gold and steaming. Now it is dark. Not dusk-dark. True dark.

Cloud has sealed the sky. No moon, no stars make it through the canopy, and the canopy itself — triple-thick, leaf on leaf on leaf — finishes the job. You stop trying to see after a while. Your eyes stay open and useless. What light there is, is low and living: a net of fireflies pulsing far out over the valley, whole acres of them blinking on and off together, and underneath, the dimmer cold smolder of fungus on fallen logs. It is not enough to see by. It is enough to know how large the place is. The flashes go on past where you can walk, past where you can imagine walking, ridge after ridge of treetops blinking in sequence. A town-sized valley, a river-basin-sized valley, full to the brim with leaves.

The air is hot and still and close. Twenty-nine, thirty degrees at midnight, and so humid your own skin condenses. This is not a wet place, not now. It is a damp place holding its breath between rains.

Water lives here in pockets. A step to the left and the leaf mold squelches; a step to the right and it is powder-dry dust. In the hollows of buttress roots, black pools the size of a palm, the size of a cooking pot, sit perfectly still, filmed with pollen. Drops form on the underside of every leaf — not rain, just the air giving up — swell, and fall with a soft, distinct *tok* onto the leaf below. Some nights a brief shower passes far off and you hear it move like an animal through the upper leaves and then lose interest. The river itself is elsewhere, reduced to a chain of warm, oxygen-poor pools somewhere beyond hearing, where catfish and knifefish wait it out in the mud.

Everything that is not water or air is occupied.

That is the first thing your body understands before your eyes do. There is no empty ground. The leaf litter is not litter; it is a living carpet, layer upon layer. Leaves, living leaves drinking the dark, dead leaves softening, vines using other vines for ladders, mosses using bark, orchids using branches, fungi using everything. Put your hand down and you touch five growing things at once. The vegetation is not tall here — the low soil won't allow giants — but it is complete. Bushes crowd saplings crowd palms crowd ferns. The air between plants is also crowded.

Because of them.

They are everywhere and they are quiet about it, which is why you don't understand the numbers at first. The floor moves.

Land crabs, red and purple-clawed, shoulder to shoulder coming up from the estuary miles away, moving inland in their patient migration. Thousands. Tens of thousands. Their shells click faintly when they bump — a dry, soft sound like fingernails tapping — otherwise they make almost no noise. Between and over and under them: millipedes as long as your forearm, flowing in black-red ribbons over logs. Beetles. Roaches. Ants.

The ants are the industry here.

No people have ever built here — no cut stump, no nail, no path, no smoke. But production is happening at industrial scale, and it has been happening all night, every night, for longer than any factory.

A leafcutter highway runs across the slope, half a meter wide and worn smooth into the mud by millions of feet, disappearing both ways into darkness. Down one lane comes an endless green conveyor: each ant carrying a sail of fresh-cut leaf many times its own weight, holding it overhead. Up the other lane goes the empty return. It does not stop. It does not hurry. The column crosses over crab legs, under fern arches, around pools, without breaking. To stand beside it is to stand beside a machine that never idles.

Nearby, termite mounds rise like kilns, chest-high, clay-fired hard by sun and saliva. Put your palm to one and it is warm — warmer than the night air. Inside, millions of bodies and fungus gardens generate their own climate, venting through spires, drawing cool air in at the base and exhaling hot air out the top. In the dark they tick and rustle, very faintly. A furnace-room sound.

And over it all, low enough to feel in your teeth more than hear: the drone. Not a chorus. No frogs screaming, no monkeys, no night birds. Most of the multitudes here have no voice. What you hear is wings — thousands of beetles moving between trees, heavy-bodied, their wingbeats a thick, uneven idling, rising and falling elsewhere in the black — and the faint electric crackle of the firefly fields discharging, and your own blood.

If you stay still long enough, things climb over your boots without altering course. A crab tests your ankle with a claw and decides you are a stone, weathered, established, part of the settled form. A line of ants reroutes an inch to the left around you and continues.

Nothing happens fast. A leaf falls every few minutes somewhere out in the dark and lands with a sigh you barely catch. A pool shrinks by a millimeter as the mud drinks it. A vine tightens around a trunk over the course of weeks. Even the migration — for all its numbers — advances at a walker's tired pace. The basin has time. It has had a long time to get this packed, this lush, this hot and dark and teeming, and it is in no hurry to become anything else.

The rule here is simple and normal and unbreakable: live close. Share the leaf, share the drop of water in the root-hollow, share the dark. There is no room for emptiness, so nothing is empty. You breathe in warm spores and the iron smell of mud and the green oil smell of crushed leaves, and you breathe out, and that too is immediately used by something else.
