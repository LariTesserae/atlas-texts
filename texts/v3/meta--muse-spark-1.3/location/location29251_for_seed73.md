You climb for days to get here and then, impossibly, the ground levels.

Not a peak but a table knocked off the sky — a long, uneven plateau sheared off on all sides by cliffs a mile deep. From the lip you can see the world dropped away beneath you: a sea of cloud far below, white and broken, and through the breaks, dim lowlands in green and brown that look drowned in blue air. Up here the air should be thin and cold. It isn't.

It is hot. Soup-hot. Bath-hot. The kind of heat that gets into your knees.

The first breath on the Table hits wrong. It is heavy, sweet, too thick for the altitude. Your lungs expect a gasp and get a mouthful instead — wet, perfumed, faintly metallic, as if you inhaled through a hot towel left on flowering rot. Sweat starts at your hairline and does not stop. Nothing ever dries up here.

This is the Weeping Table. A high marsh in the sky.

The plateau is not flat. From the edge it looks flat, but once you are inside it resolves into open country, valley-sized, several hours to cross if there were trails: a rolling mosaic of shallow basins, low forested ridges, black rock spines, and everywhere, water sitting close to the skin of the world.

Water does not run here so much as seep and hang. The rock — dark, pitted limestone and old basalt, rounded by ages of weather into solution cups and flutes and sleeping whale-backs — weeps continuously. Every overhang drips. Every cliff face in the interior glistens with a slow film that crawls sideways as often as down. In the basins the water table kisses the surface: tea-colored pools under sphagnum, sedge marshes that quake when you step near, narrow black channels that move so slowly you only know they move by the leaning of the duckweed.

The heat lifts it back up as fast as it gathers. Midday you can watch it happen: steam rising off every pool in slow ribbons, condensing a meter up into a low, drifting ground-mist that never quite becomes fog and never quite burns off. The light comes through it ordinary and white — a normal sun, high and yellow-white — but diffused, softened at the edges, made to shimmer. Shadows are short and a little too dark, as if the light is thicker than it should be.

Vegetation has taken that bargain — heat + water + light at the top of the world — and gone lush and mad and layered.

There is no single forest. There are many vegetations occupying the same volume. On the drier ridges, a low, wind-combed cloud forest: gnarled trees no taller than a house, trunks swollen and bark peeling in copper sheets, crowns flattened and dense with epiphytes. Every branch is itself a garden — mosses a hand deep, filmy ferns translucent as fish fins, orchids white and blood-red clinging in clusters, bromeliads big as buckets holding their own private ponds where tree-frogs live their whole lives and never touch ground.

Between the ridges, the ground opens into marsh and meadow. Giant rosettes dominate — frailejón-like, but here sun-scorched and steaming: thick woolly stems topped with crowns of silver-green leaves that funnel rain and dew into their hearts. Under them, a near-touching mat of sphagnum, sundew, bladderwort, sedges with saw edges, and flowers that only open in heat: yellow, magenta, a violent orange. You cannot put a foot down without stepping on ten living things.

And over everything, the density. Spores, seeds, insects in the air in such numbers the light has texture. Vines through vines. Roots growing up into the air to drink the mist. Fallen logs that are not fallen but nurse-logs, sprouting rows of seedlings along their backs, already half dissolved into peat. The smell is fecund and heavy: warm peat, crushed green, honey rot, ozone after rain that hasn't fallen yet.

Because the fauna is everywhere, and loud.

You hear the Table before you see its interior. From the cliff edge it is a dull roar — wind shearing up the abyssal walls a thousand feet below, mixing with water. Inside, the roar resolves into layers that never stop: the high electric sizzle of cicadas in the ridge forest, so constant it becomes a pressure in the teeth; the glassy *plink-plink-plink* of dripping rock; the low, abdominal chorus of toads in every pool, a slow *whoop... whoop... whoop* that seems to come from the mud itself; and underneath, the shear and twitter of birds that should not live this high and do.

They are abundant to the point of carelessness. Hummingbirds the size of your fist, emerald and copper, hang in front of your face, unbothered. Flocks of small black parrots tear screaming over the marsh at dusk. Lizards the length of your forearm bake on black rock, throats pulsing. In the pools, things flicker — transparent frogs with visible hearts, salamanders with external gills like red lace, beetles that row upside-down under the surface film. At night, the leaf litter rustles with something heavier — a highland coati-like animal, long-nosed, moving in troops, turning over moss for grubs. They stare at you without fear. Nothing up here has learned to be scarce.

If you stand still long enough, you notice the rules are slightly off. This is the strange part, and it grows on you slowly.

Water beads climb. On certain smooth rock faces, droplets will merge and then run *up* a shallow incline in a thin, deliberate film, against gravity, to vanish into a moss lip. Compasses spin lazily and settle 20 degrees off. Your voice carries too far across a marsh and then stops dead at a forest edge, as if cut. And the shadows — at noon, things cast a faint double, a second, paler shadow offset a finger's width to the east, that fades if you stare.

That is because the Table is kept.

Scattered, half-swallowed by green, are the structures. Not many. You could walk a day and see only five or six, which makes them worse. They are not ruins in the normal sense, though some have fallen.

The Needles first: seven of them, spaced around the plateau rim, each perhaps thirty meters tall, slender as a pine, dead black, without joints. They are not metal, not stone. Touch one — everyone touches one — and it is warm as skin and faintly yielding, and your fingerprint stays for a second as a pale afterglow before fading. They hum. Not loudly, but you feel it in your sternum when you are near, a low note that beats against the cicada sizzle. On still afternoons you can see the air above them distort, heat-haze rising even when the stone at their base is cool.

Between them, smaller artifacts: low cairns where someone has stacked weeping slabs, and in the stack, a lozenge of that same black material, veined with light that moves slowly like blood. Trail markers that are not markers — thin rods driven into peat that project, a hand's breadth above their tip, a hovering mote of light that stays in place in wind. Step-fields: patches of marsh where your footfall lands a half-second late, a soft lag in the world. A collapsed dome on the eastern ridge, its ribs open to the sky, inside it a floor that repairs itself — you can see the seams where moss was pushed aside by something growing back, smooth and grey and warm.

No one lives here now, or no one admits to it. A single hermit shelter of lashed poles and tarp, long abandoned, its notebooks swollen to pulp. A rope bridge across a black channel, its ropes now thick as your wrist with moss and orchid roots, still holding. Faint cut steps in a rock spine, worn almost smooth.

The old story the lowlanders tell — and up here, sweating, slapping at golden flies, you believe it — is that the Table was made to be a cistern and a garden. That before physics settled, someone lifted this block of country into the sky and wrapped it in machines that breathe for it: exhaling dense, hot, wet air to keep it tropical against all altitude, inhaling the cold thin wind and wringing it. The Needles are lungs. They keep the inversion trapped. That is why the air is thick enough to hold hummingbirds at four thousand meters. That is why it rains upward some afternoons, the mist condensing on leaves and falling, then rising again as steam.

Whether that is true or not, the effect is of a place in long balance, changing only on the slowest clocks. Erosion here has finished its dramatic work. The cliffs are old, stabilized, blackened with lichen maps centuries across, their rockfalls colonized before they stop moving. Peat accumulates a millimeter a year in the basins, burying pollen, beetles, lost sandals, Needle-shed — layer on patient layer. The forest does not advance or retreat; it breathes in place, a tree falling and a hundred epiphytes continuing on the log. Even the machines pulse slow: the hum deepens on an eleven-year cycle the frogs seem to know, chorusing louder in the deep years.

By late afternoon, the light goes gold through the steam and the whole open country glows. Pools turn to mirrors, then to white. The double shadows lengthen. The toad chorus swells until the ground seems to throb. The air gets hotter, if possible, before it cools — that last exhalation of the day — and the scent of flowers intensifies to something almost narcotic.

Stand on a rock spine then and look across: marsh to forest to marsh to Needle to marsh again, all steaming, all buzzing, cliffs on every side dropping to cloud, sky huge and ordinary above. You are very high up. You are very low down in green. You are somewhere that should not, by all ordinary rules of mountain and heat and breath, exist — and does, lush and loud and patient, weeping water into hot air, holding itself in the sky by old, scattered, incomprehensible care.
