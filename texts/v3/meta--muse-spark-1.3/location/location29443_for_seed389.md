You come up to it slowly, because there is no fast way up.

The rail-crawl takes the better part of a day, switchbacking up a cone that wasn't on the old maps. Below, the lowlands flatten and haze out. Above, the air should get cold. It doesn't. It gets thinner and hotter, until breathing feels like drinking from a warm cup, and the light turns white and level — a normal noon that never seems to move.

This is a mountain still being born.

The ground up here has no age to it. No softness, no soil, no rounding. Everything is sharp. Ridges of aa lava like frozen surf, black and rust-red, glassy ropes and slabs that ring under your boots. Between them, still-warm fissures breathe. In places the street — if you can call it that — gives slightly under your weight, warm tar-plastic. Children press handprints into the cooling curb and come back a month later to find them lifted an inch higher. That is how fast the hill grows: inches a year, a patient swelling. The surveyors re-chalk their marks every spring and find them wrong.

There are no plants. Not a blade, not lichen, not even the green-black stain of moss in a seep. The chemistry is wrong and the heat is wrong and the stone is too new to have learned how to host anything green. What looks from a distance like vegetation — olive smears on the north walls, dark velvet in the gullies — comes close to reveal itself as mineral: sulfur bloom, copper salts, thin flows of obsidian that cooled with a sheen. It is a mineral world.

And on it, dense as barnacles, a town.

Not a mining outpost. A town the size of a small valley, spilling around the crater rim and terracing down the outer flank in layered steps, roof touching roof, wall sharing wall. From the approach you first mistake it for more geology — black cubes and rusted sheds, kiln stacks and water towers — until you see the repetition, the intention. Streets are stairways cut between buildings. Buildings bridge clean over cracks where heat shimmers up. Over everything, the infrastructure: low tramlines on iron trestles that creep at walking pace, endless copper and ceramic pipework, canvas sun-sails strung between parapets, cisterns like fat black seeds tucked into every courtyard.

It is industrial, unmistakably. The terraces are factories. Long sheds with open sides where rows of dark ingots cool from cherry to black over days. Electrolysis yards humming faintly behind spark screens. Machine halls where enormous flywheels turn — so slowly you have to watch the painted spoke for a full minute to be sure it moved. These are heat engines, not fire engines. They run on the mountain itself: on the gradient between the upwelling rock below and the thin sky above. Pistons the size of houses sigh once an hour. Chains inch ore buckets up from the fissure quarries. Everything scaled for production, everything electrified — you can see the thick braided lines, smell ozone after a surge — and yet:

It is quiet.

That is the first strangeness that settles on you. A dense city of ten thousand roofs and furnaces should roar. This one ticks. The new basalt pings as it contracts. Dripwater patters into tin. A flywheel bearing groans low enough to feel in your molars rather than hear. Men and women call to each other in low voices, not from politeness but because the thin hot air carries badly and shouting leaves you winded. Even the tram, when it passes a meter from your shoulder, is mostly a warm displacement and a click-click of expansion joints. The quiet piles up until noon feels like the inside of a kiln after the firing — saturated, held.

Water rules here. There is no river, no spring in the lowland sense. This high, the peak snags clouds. For about an hour after dawn, before the sun burns through, everything drips. The sails, the pipes, the cold frames left out overnight bloom with condensation, and the whole town wakes to harvest it. Gutters chatter. Children with squeegees herd beads into funnels. Women unplug the overnight drip-bags hung under every eave. By eight it stops, as if a tap were closed, and the stone steams dry by nine. The rest of the day's water lives in pockets: steam traps where fissure vapor is led through cool coils, cistern cellars cut into older, cooler flow, roof-tanks painted white to hold the morning against evaporation. You learn quickly not to ask for water. You wait to be offered from the household jar, and you do not spill it. To spill is to insult the cloud that bothered to stop.

Away from the harvested damp, the larger dryness asserts itself: ash fines that get in your teeth, wind that smells of hot iron and machine oil and faint rotten egg, sun that burns the back of your neck while your lungs work for air that isn't there.

Animals are rare enough to be news. A pale, almost translucent lizard that lives under the cooling sheds and licks condensate off pipes. Swifts, very high, circling the thermal for hours without flapping, crying once, thinly, before they drop away. Every few months someone swears they saw a dog gone feral in the ash fields, living on scraps and drip. You live mostly among people and rock and slow machines.

The second strangeness is the light. At midday the heat lenses the air over the caldera. Distant quarters of the town — a kiln row, a water tower half a mile off — detach and hover, mirage-city floating a hand's breadth above its foundations, rippling. You learn to navigate by the real shadows, not the floating doubles. Old-timers say the mountain dreams its future shape in the air.

And because the ground moves, the architecture moves with it. No foundation is trusted. Every substantial house sits on jackscrews and stacked basalt shims, re-leveled when doors jam. Pipes have loops and bellows to take up the swell. In the plaza of the Upper Tier there is a doorway built perfectly plumb twenty years ago that now leans north by six degrees, preserved as a kind of clock. People touch it for luck on the way to the kilns.

There are rules that are not written but are harder than stone:

Rest in the white hours, when handrails will brand you. Work early, work late.
Do not build straight across a warm crack. Bridge it, leave it room to breathe.
Keep your drip-line clear. Your neighbor's jar depends on your roof.
Speak low. Walk slow. The mountain is working slower than you, but it is stronger.

By evening the light finally slants, ambering the black glass to bronze. The factories exhale their stored heat in long shimmers. The coils begin to cool, and if you put your ear to a standpipe you can hear the first tick of tomorrow's water condensing inside, drop by patient drop.
