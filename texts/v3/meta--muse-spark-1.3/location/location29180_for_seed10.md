It is high, but not sharp.

That is the first wrongness you have to unlearn. High usually means peak, ridge, fall. Here high means flat. An old, old table set above the weather — a mountain range that has lived so long it has forgotten to be mountains. The rock underneath is pink-grey, rounded like molars, licked smooth by a billion rains. There is almost no bare rock left to see. What you stand on is not ground. It is plant.

The plateau stretches out past what you can walk in a day. A shallow bowl of living sponge, three or four miles across before it drops off into cloud on all sides. No trees. No walls. No posts, no cairns, no cut anything. Nothing has ever been built here.

And yet everything works.

The light is dim and does not change. A cold, late blue-grey that might be dawn or dusk but never commits. The sun is a paler smear behind low cloud that never lifts, only thins. Because the air is thin and wet at once, light diffuses and hangs. Distances go soft. A hundred yards out, a moss hummock and a grazing animal have the same grey weight. Shadows do not fall. Everything glows faintly from wetness.

It is cold. Not killing cold. Working cold. The kind that makes your nostrils stick and your breath hang and not dissipate, because the air is already full of water. Exhale and your breath joins the general exhalation and stays there, condensing on every leaf hair, every feather, dripping back down. Inhale and you taste iron and peat and ozone. Frost needles grow in the shade sides of every tussock even though it is technically thaw. At night — if this is day, there must be night — small black tarns skin over with ice, and by midday they crack again with a sound like distant rifle fire.

Water is everywhere and nowhere deep. There is no river here, there is no need. The whole plateau weeps. Step and water rises around your boot from the moss that is ten feet deep and saturated like a lung. Lift your foot and the print fills instantly, dark. Every hollow holds a tarn, tea-black or sky-grey, rimmed with trembling sundew. Rocks weep — the few ancient boulders that poke through, glacial erratics worn to eggs, have steady rivulets running down their lee sides even when it has not rained, because the fog condenses on the cold lichen and runs. The lichen themselves are orange and pale green and they drip, drip. The air condenses on your eyelashes. If you stand still you get wetter than if you walk.

Vegetation is not on the land. It *is* the land. Sphagnum in russet and gold and acid green, built up in domes and ridges six, ten feet high, separated by wet black flarks where water shows. Between them, cotton-grass nods — thousands of white heads shivering at once. Dwarf willow no higher than your ankle but a hundred years old, twisted into mats. Cranberry vine threading through everything. And pitcher plants, huge here, cold-adapted, knee-deep pitchers full of rainwater and drowned midges, top-heavy and steaming faintly.

It looks sparse at first. That is the trick. From the rim you think: empty bog, a few humps, a lot of sky. Then you get down into it and realize each hump is a city. Each dome is dense, layered, crawling with life, ten thousand stems to a square foot, mites and springtails and fungal threads and rootlets, and then you crest it and there is fifty yards of almost nothing — black water, wind — before the next dense island. Presence scattered in a huge emptiness. The sky does most of the work here.

Because then you hear it. It is loud.

Not city loud. Organ loud. The wind first, combing constantly over an open table with nothing to stop it, roaring low and then whistling through cotton-grass. Under that, the work-sound.

Every large moss dome breathes and pumps. You can feel it through your knees if you sit. A slow *chuff — chuff — chuff* as the living sponge takes in melt and rain and squeezes it through itself, hydraulics on a massive slow scale. Some domes sigh and vent. Methane and cold air hiss out of blowholes in the peat with a kettle shriek, and for a second you smell deep time, rot sweet as honey. Elsewhere water is fired from one pool to another through natural pipes of hollowed peat and old root channels — a sudden gurgle-gush, a throat clearing. And underneath, almost subsonic, a thump, thump that is not your heart. If you put your ear to the moss, it is a boiler room down there: gurgle, knock, expansion, contraction.

And crackle. On the highest tips of the moss, in the dim light, blue sparks crawl. The whole bog makes electricity the way a storm does — charge separated by wind and mist and vast mats of photosynthesizing tissue. On still moments you hear it ticking. At dusk-dim, you see St. Elmo's fire trembling on every cotton head. Pools where flatworms and black newts gather pulse faintly. If you trail your hand in a tarn, your fingertips go numb with the weak current.

Nothing is built, and yet scaled production happens everywhere. The sundews make glue by the acre. The sphagnum makes acid by the ton, pickling everything, stopping rot, laying down peat inch by century. Conveyor vines of cranberry move nutrients foot by foot across the domes. It is industry without intention. Engines without engineers.

Which is why the animals are here, and there are so many of them.

In the cold and the dim you would expect quiet. Instead the plateau seethes. Herds of pale reindeer-kin, shaggy white-grey with moss caught in their coats until they look like moving hummocks, drift between domes, thirty, fifty at a time, tearing at sedge, grunting, calves bawling. Their hooves churn black water to soup. Flocks of small dark geese wedge overhead, honking, then drop all at once into a tarn with a slap that echoes. Ptarmigan burst from willow mats in white explosions. And insects — impossible clouds of them for this cold — midges in pillars that bend in the wind, crane flies bouncing, huge slow bumblebees the size of your thumb working the cotton-grass, their drone joining the general hum. In the pitchers, shrews dive and swim. On a boulder, a fox the color of peat watches, and watches.

It is turbulent. Nothing stays. Frost heave lifts whole plates of moss overnight into new small cliffs that collapse by noon with a wet *whumpf*, exposing steaming black peat that is instantly colonized by green. A tarn drains sideways — you watch it — through a peat pipe while you blink, leaving stranded newts flipping, and an hour later refills from fog. The reindeer tear a path, the path floods, the sphagnum grows visibly over their prints. The cotton-grass heads all turn at once though there is no sun to follow. Anthill energy in slow motion, but relentless. You cannot photograph the same place twice.

And then, the breakdown.

Because this place is too old, and too wet, and too charged, and the world wears thin here.

Rules slip.

First, sound precedes cause. You will hear the geese land a full second before they come over the rim. You will hear your own boot squelch a half-step before you lift it. The bog's thump sometimes beats in double — *thump-thump ... thump-thump* — and the second beat is slightly *earlier*.

Second, distance is negotiable when unwatched. That white herd was a quarter mile off. You look down to wring water from your sleeve. You look up and they are sixty feet away, breathing fog, staring, too close, and they do not seem to have walked. The moss between you is untrampled. If you stare directly at a distant dome and walk toward it counting steps, you will count too many. Always too many.

Third, water runs wrong when the charge is high. After a venting, when the air smells of ozone, rivulets on the weeping rocks will for a minute run up, beading upward against gravity, before falling back down with a little shake, as if embarrassed. Tarn reflections lag. You move your hand; your reflection moves a breath later.

Fourth, the dead do not stay put. You will find a reindeer skeleton picked clean and sunk in sphagnum, antlers hung with sundew. You will walk around a dome and on the other side find the same skeleton standing, flesh on, grazing, with peat still in its ribs. If you blink it is gone, but the midges are still parted around where it was.

No one made this. That is the rule that holds all the others: *nothing is built, everything works.* There are no tools to fix the glitches because there was never a builder to assume the world should behave. The moss does not care if time runs backward for a second. It keeps pumping. The geese do not care if their cry arrives first. They keep landing. The plateau has settled into its final form — flattened, soaked, cold, humming — and in that final stability it has become free to be unstable in every small way, all the time.

Stand still long enough and you become part of the substrate. Water will condense on you and run off you. Sparks will gather on your shoulders. Midges will pillar above you. Your breath will not dissipate. Your weight will make the dome you stand on sigh and settle and *chuff* a little faster, industrious, indifferent, alive.

And beyond you, in the dim, mile after mile of wet breathing islands going out to cloud, thumping and hissing and crackling, herds appearing and disappearing without crossing the space between, water rising from below and falling from the air to meet in the middle, in the plant that is the earth.
