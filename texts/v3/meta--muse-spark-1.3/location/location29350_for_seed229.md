You come up over the rim and the wind stops meaning anything. Below you is the place.

From above it looked empty — a shallow bowl, maybe three hundred paces across, scooped out of high basalt and filled with dust. High enough that the lowlands are only a rumor, a paler brown far below, and the air is thin enough to make your ribs work. The light is wrong for that height. It should be bright, scoured. Instead it is dim, copper-gloomed, as if the sun has been wrapped in gauze. Dust hangs permanently over the high country, and here it eddies and settles, softening everything to ember and umber.

There is no water here. Not in the way you understand the word.

No spring, no seep, no well, no damp dark line under a rock to lick. No dew even at dawn. The ground is dry mineral — ash and pumice and wind-polished salt that squeaks under your boots and sticks to your lips and cracks your tongue. If you dig, your hole fills with powder, not darkening. If you kill something, it does not bleed. It oozes warm wax and then dries to husk within an hour.

And yet it is lush.

That is the first contradiction your eyes refuse. From the rim you saw bare dust. Descending, you realize the lushness was hiding in plainness. The green you expected isn't here. What grows here is black-purple and rust and deep felted grey, colors that in the dim light look like shadow until you are almost touching them. Then they erupt.

They stand far apart, as things must where nothing is shared. A hundred paces of open singing dust between one and the next. That is the rule of sparseness — much emptiness to hold a few presences upright.

Each presence is huge for its kind. A thunderleaf: a squat trunk of corked, weather-worn basalt-textured bark — old, settled in its form, not growing fast so much as having grown long ago and now maintaining — crowned with leaves the size of blankets. Not thin leaves. Thick, furred, succulent without juice. If you tear one, which you won't after the first time because of the smell, there is no sap. There is a dry white pith that smells of resin and scorched hair, and fibers that pull like wool. The plant is lush because every frond is curled, layered, overlapping, a single individual carrying a jungle on its back.

Between them, lower, the ground fur: bellows-moss, which is not moss, and carpet-lichen that puffs up when stepped on and sighs. Chime-trees — spindly, ten feet tall, with hollow gourds hanging from every branch, each gourd paper-dry and full of loose seeds.

Nothing here drinks. What would it drink? Instead they eat the air. The wind that comes over the rim is full of fine mineral salt and ash and pollen-husk and powdered insect shell. The great velvet leaves comb it. You can hear it if you press your ear close: a faint electrostatic crackle as dust strikes fur. The leaf holds the dust, dissolves what it needs with exhaled oils, and seals the rest into wax. Wax is the water here.

Where water would run in veins and drip off tips, wax accumulates — amber beads along leaf margins, heavy drips that never fall but harden into knobs. Animals come to chew the knobs. They excrete fine dry pellets that crumble back to dust for the leaves to re-catch. It is a closed, dry circle, perfected over a long, weathered time. The rocks themselves show it: rounded, fluted, hollowed into organ-pipes and honeycombs, not raw, not crumbling, but in-balance, in-its-own-state. Wind has played them for ten thousand years and they have learned the shape of wind.

Because in dimness color is useless, everything here shouts.

The bowl is loud. Unreasonably, beautifully loud for a place so small you could walk rim to rim before your sweat dried — if you sweated, which you soon stop doing.

First, the rock itself. The rim is broken into teeth and flutes, natural organ stops. When the high wind hits, which is always, the whole crater hums and moans and whistles, a low chord that you feel in your molars. Then the plants answer. The chime-trees shake in even a light gust and their gourds clatter — thousands of dry maracas, arrhythmic, insistent. The thunderleaves, when the gust strengthens, slap their great blanket-ears together with a sound like sails.

Then the animals ride over it all.

They are abundant, though you would not think it in any given minute. The emptiness tricks you. Then the air tears. A swarm of ash-locusts rises off a thunderleaf all at once, a black glittering sheet, wings rasping like files on slate. Veil-gliders — manta-thin mammals with furred wing-membranes — launch from the lee of a rock and scream to each other, high needle whistles to navigate in the gloom. Pouch-mice boil from under the moss, chittering, pouches bulging with stolen wax-knobs. Dust-moths the size of your hand thud against your chest, drawn to your heat.

It never settles. This is not a place that rests. A thunderleaf you watched sleeping suddenly shudders and splits a seed-pod with a gunshot crack, firing a plume of hooked down into the wind. A chime-tree is stripped bare by locusts in four minutes and stands rattling naked, and by evening it has already pushed out pale new furls to catch the next dust fall. Gliders chase moths chase locusts chase gliders. Molts drift like snow — transparent wing cases, shed fur — catching on the moss, becoming soil. Everything is eating, molting, rattling, mating, dying dry and being unmade to dust again within sight. Anthill energy. Bazaar energy. You cannot stand still in it; your ears keep turning.

People have been here. Not many. Not a town, not even a camp — scattered marks, half-erased by establishment.

A low ring of stacked basalt, wind-fallen on one side, with a fire-pit in the middle that has never held water to boil. The charcoal is wax-wood, which burns hot and smokeless and smells sweet. Beside it, a tripod of knotted chime-wood poles with strips of glider-hide tied on with sinew, cracked bells of fired clay hanging from it to call or to scare. A cairn with a skull on top — glider, long-snouted — facing the wind. A bone fence, ribs interwoven with fiber, built to funnel pouch-mice, broken and half-buried. A comb carved from obsidian, teeth worn smooth, left on a rock where someone scraped wax.

Hand-tools. Nothing machined, nothing smelted beyond a simple pit. Whoever lives here — hermit-herders, wax-tenders, or something not quite you that learned to tie knots — lives light and moves between the great plants, chewing wax for food and for the little bound moisture exhaled in breath, never drinking, tending, harvesting, listening.

Because listening is the rule.

In a place with no water to reflect light, with dim air and far-apart giants, you do not find your way by sight. You find it by sound. The hollow gourd that rings fullest has the most seed. The thunderleaf whose slap is deepest holds the most wax on its lee side. The silence between gusts is where predators glide.

If you stay until your throat aches from dryness, you learn the other rules without being told:

Nothing flows. Everything accretes.
What is lush is not wet.
What lives does not drink; it chews, and breathes powder, and listens.

By the time you climb back out, your clothes are full of dust, your ears are ringing, and behind you the small bowl keeps roaring to itself in the warm gloom — those few huge furred crowns shaking and clattering far apart in all that singing emptiness, turbulent and strange and perfectly at home without a single drop.
