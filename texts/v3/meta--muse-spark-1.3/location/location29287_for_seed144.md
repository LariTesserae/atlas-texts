Up here the air should be cold. It isn't.

You feel it first in the lungs — that high, thin pull when you try to take a full breath and can't quite finish it — and then in the skin, because the sun has no filter. It comes down white and ordinary, noon-light, no strange color to it, and it hits rock that has been in the sun since morning and will not cool until well after dark.

The tableland extends past what you can comfortably walk. Not a peak, a place. A broad, broken mesa maybe a day across, rimmed on three sides by a drop you don't want to look at for long. Below the rim is a slow sea of cloud that burns off by ten and boils back up by four. From the edge you can hear it faintly moving, far below your boots. Beyond the far rim there are other high tops floating like islands. This is high country in the way that makes maps lie — the elevation is in your knees, in your ears popping when you swallow, in the blueness of the shadows.

The ground itself is packed. That's the first wrongness your body registers: there is nowhere empty to put your feet.

What looked from a distance like flat pavement resolves, as you get close, into teeth. Thousands upon thousands of close-set pillars of dark basalt, shoulder to shoulder, hip to hip, most no taller than a person, some twice that, weathered not jagged but settled — rounded shoulders, pitted flanks, cups worn into their tops. Wind and old water have had a long time here. Nothing is fresh-fallen, nothing raw. Every stone leans into its neighbors like it has found its balance and intends to keep it. You have to turn sideways to slip between them. The slots between are in full shadow and smell of dust and old feathers.

And every top is occupied.

Sparse is the wrong word for green here, because there is almost no green to be sparse. A wiry tuft of straw-grass in a crack every twenty steps. A smear of orange lichen, a black crust lichen, both clinging so tight to the north sides of the pillars they seem painted on. That is the vegetation, done. It could not feed a goat.

It feeds something else indirectly, or rather, it is irrelevant. Life here does not run on leaves. It runs on water that isn't supposed to exist and on each other.

Each pillar-top holds a shallow cup, natural or wind-widened, and in the morning each cup holds water. Not rain — there has been no rain in weeks except for one black shaft that crossed the far end of the mesa yesterday afternoon, dumping for eleven minutes and vanishing. This is condensation. At night the stone cools just enough, the cloud-sea rises and breathes against the high rock, and by dawn every hollow is brimmed with a finger of clear water, trembling. By midday under this hot sun most of it is gone to steam. By three the cups are dry white rings of mineral and guano. But for a few hours the whole plateau glitters.

And in those few hours it is saturated with animals.

Low, first: mosquito-thick gnats and copper-backed flies rising off the cups, water-boatmen in water that will not last the day, blood-worms curling. Lizards the color of hot rock — throat-fans flicking pink, pink, pink — wedged three to a crevice, waiting. Furred rock-runners like fat guinea pigs with naked tails, squeezing between pillars, drinking in shifts. Above: swifts. Thousands of them. They nest in the vertical slots, mud-and-guano cups plastered to shadow, whitewash streaking every pillar flank below a nest-hole. They come off their nests at dawn in a soundless lift, more bodies in the air than air, wheeling, snapping flies. Higher still: the hunters. Pale kestrels that hang motionless in the thin updraft off the rim, then drop between the teeth with folded wings.

It should be loud. A colony that dense should stink and scream. It doesn't. The thin air eats sound, and the rock teeth break it up. What you hear, mostly, is quiet: your own boots scraping, your own breath too fast. Then, if you stand still long enough, the place sorts itself out into small noises. Wing-whirr as a flock turns all at once. A pebble ticking down into a slot. The faint high peep of lizards. Wind fluting low across the pillar-tops, a breathy organ note that comes and goes.

Through it all, underneath, the machines breathe.

They are scattered and low on purpose, so at first you miss them. A rust-red pump hut no bigger than an outhouse, half-buried between pillars, its door propped with a stone. A line of stout black pipe running at ankle height for half a kilometer, humming faintly when you put your hand on it. A fog-net — a rectangle ofMesh strung between two galvanized poles — sagging with morning wet, dripping into a covered cistern. A solar array tilted to the sun, dust-filmed, ticking as it tracks. A cairn of sorted bolts, a coil of wire, a warning stencil bleached unreadable. No town, no factory wall. But this is industrial ground. The whole mesa has been plumbed.

Someone, some company or cooperative generations back, realized the cups could be scaled. So they drilled the pillars — you can see the bore-holes, neat and dark — and set one-way wicks. They laid the pipes to catch the night-drip before the sun takes it. Down at the saddle of the mesa there are three buried tanks you can hear echo if you stamp; by the gauge-wheels they are still working. The electricity is still on: a faint ozone smell near the transformer box, a green LED blinking patiently in the dust. Twice a day the pumps kick, a deep sigh-shudder you feel more in your molars than hear, and move the harvested sky-water to storage.

The unusual part — the thing that keeps catching your eye — is that the animals have joined the system without permission.

Swifts nest on the warm transformer box because it is always warm. Rock-runners drink from a slow pipe-joint leak in preference to the natural cups. Lichen grows thickest in a green line following the buried pipe where it sweats at night. And the stones themselves are warm. Even before sunrise, if you press your palm flat to basalt, there is a residual heat from deep down, geothermal, patient. The mesa is a thin lid over something hot. In the coldest hour before dawn you can see steam ghost off the eastern fissure field, a low crack-zone where the pillars are stained yellow with sulfur and no one has laid pipe because the water there smells of eggs.

That is the rule here, unspoken but enforced by everything living: don't waste the morning water, don't block a drip-line, don't make noise at midday when everyone is sheltering. The human marks obey it too. Walkways are single-plank boards laid across pillar tops to avoid crushing cups. A hand-painted sign on a hut, sun-crazed: CHECK VALVES / LEAVE CUPS FULL TILL 9. Even the kestrels obey — they hunt at the edges in the heat, not over the center where the swifts are panting in shade with beaks open.

By afternoon the dynamic shifts. The cloud-sea rises fast, a white wall climbing the cliffs, cool and damp against your sunburnt neck. Light goes flat and pearly for twenty minutes. Condensation beads instantly on every pipe, every net, every whisker. The flies get a second wind. The lizards emerge. The pumps kick again. Then the cloud sinks, the sun returns with a slap, steam lifts off a thousand wet pillar-tops at once and the whole packed field shimmers, mirage-water dancing between real water already gone.

At dusk the mesa empties upward. The swifts funnel out over the rim to feed in the cooling air below. The rock-runners vanish into slots. The gnats settle. The machines tick as they cool, metal pinging. The stones hold their heat longest — you can sit on one at midnight and feel it through your clothes, faintly alive — while above you the sky, huge and ordinary and star-crowded, does nothing strange at all.

There is no room here that is not used. Every hollow holds morning water or a nest or a pipe. Every shadow holds a lizard or a sensor. Even the quiet feels occupied, packed in between the teeth of the rock, waiting for the cloud to rise again.
