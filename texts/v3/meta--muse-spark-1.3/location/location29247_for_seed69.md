It is high up and it is night that never ends.

Not night as in evening. There is no evening here, no morning to come. The height does not bring you closer to stars because there are no stars. The cloud deck below and the cloud deck above sealed together a long time ago, and the world on top learned to do without sky. You notice the altitude first in your chest — the air is thin up here and at the same time too thick to breathe easily, warm as blood, so wet you have to chew it. Three kilometers above whatever sea used to be, on a tableland so wide it makes its own weather. Countries could fit up here and be lost.

You cannot see. Understand that first. There is nothing wrong with your eyes. There is simply nothing for them to do. No moon leak, no horizon glow, no ember. The darkness is complete and material, like being inside warm peat water with your eyes open. After a while you stop trying to look and your other senses take over, which is how everyone here — animal, plant, machine — lives.

Everything weeps.

The rock of the Heights is old, very old, rounded down to patience. This is not sharp young mountain. This is a settled place, eroded into balance long before anyone built anything. Basalt domes worn to the softness of shoulders, broad shallow valleys that braid and unbraid, peat basins a dozen meters deep that quiver when you step. Water does not sit in lakes so much as lie in sheets, in films, in a slow skin over everything. It seeps out of the stone itself. Cut a bank and it bleeds. Put your hand on a boulder and your palm comes away wet, even when it hasn't rained for an hour — which is rare. Rain is frequent and warm, hissing down in curtains, then stopping, then condensing straight out of the air onto your skin, your hair, every leaf. Droplets hang in the air without falling, held by the updraft of heat from below and the density of the air itself. You walk through a permanent drizzle that has no source.

Smell is iron and rot-sweet and hot oil. The ground gives underfoot: sphagnum, black mud, decaying leaf-mat a meter thick, then stone. The whole plateau breathes heat from underneath. Geothermal seams run like veins, keeping the marsh warm from below while the wet air keeps it warm from above. Your sweat doesn't evaporate. Your boots never dry. Your feet learn the difference between living mat that will hold you and sucking hollow that will take your leg to the knee.

It is lush here in a way that should be impossible without light.

Nothing here photosynthesizes. There is nothing to photosynthesize with. The forest of the bog learned another trade. The leaves are black, rubber-thick, waxy, hot to the touch — some of them faintly warmer than your hand. They spread wide, not to catch sun but to catch sound and heat and mineral rain. Great ear-ferns unfurl to the size of doors, ribbed and trembling, drinking vibration. Fungal trees rise twenty, thirty meters, their trunks fibrous and damp as lungs, their canopies a dense interlock of shelves that drip continuously. Pitcher plants big enough to drown a dog stand in the shallows, lids open, not for flies but for condensate, for iron-rich seep. Moss covers everything that stays still for more than a day. Vines as thick as your thigh run along the ground instead of climbing, rooting every meter, forming a second ground above the ground that bounces when the herds pass.

It is crowded. That is the rule. No empty volume remains empty for long. Between the leaves are spores, midges, droplets, seeds with tiny propellers that never land. Between the trunks are roots, rhizomes, fungal cords pulsing. Between the sounds are other sounds. The air is saturated — with water, with bodies, with noise. Reach out in the dark and you will touch something living within seconds: a leaf, a flank, a wet cable, a wall of rain.

And it is loud. You will never hear silence here.

First there is water: the constant patter-hiss of rain on ten billion leaves, the gurgle of runnels, the suck and sigh of mud, the drip-drip-drip from canopy to understory to peat — a vertical river that never stops.

Under that, the animals. They are abundant and they are blind, or near it. Eyes are a rumor here. What use? Instead they sing. The frog-things in the pools trill in layered choirs so dense the water itself shivers. Insect swarms tune to each other in metallic sheets of buzzing that rise and fall as you walk through them. High waders — tall, stilt-legged grazers with barrel bodies and wide splayed feet for the bog — boom low through the dark, infrasound you feel in your molars, locating each other across kilometers. Packs of smaller chitterers sweep through the fern aisles, echolocating in clicks, hunting the swarms. At intervals, something large displaces mud with a long wet tearing sound and then is quiet.

Over that, the machines.

They are why it is still dark. The industry here runs blacked-out, by compact, by taboo, by survival. A light would deafen the ecology that navigates by sound, would scatter the herds, would silence the pollinators. So the crawlers go dark.

You hear them long before you feel them: a deep, slow throb, piston-breath, tread-crush. The Harvest Engines. Each one is a factory on legs or on wide mud-treads, the size of a hill, moving at walking pace across the marsh on routes worn over centuries into the old riverbeds. They take in peat, water, fungal timber, iron seep; inside their lightless bellies they cook, ferment, distill, generate. They exhale columns of clean steam that rise and become cloud and become rain. They trail thick umbilical cables behind them that hum with current you can feel in your teeth if you stand too close. They groan, they vent, they clank. Valves pop. Chains slap. Their feet make a particular sound in mud — *chuff-suck, chuff-suck* — that every animal knows and moves aside for.

There are not many of them, and the land is huge, so most of the time you are alone with biology. Then the ground begins to tremble faintly in your feet, the frog choir shifts pitch to make room in the spectrum, and one passes like weather. The heat coming off it warps the rain around it. The smell of hot oil and sterilized mash rolls over you. If you put your hand out you might brush its flank: riveted iron gone soft with moss, hot, vibrating, alive with its own internal processes. Some of them have gone feral, their crews dead generations ago, still walking their circuits, still processing, tended by birds that nest in their joints.

The only buildings are for the machines, and they are scattered, apologetic. A pump-house on stilts, its diesel puttering, its shutters dogged tight against any light leak. A maintenance shack sunk to its windows in peat, full of tools hung by position so they can be found in the dark. A way-station: a stone dome with a cistern that pings as it fills, with rope guides running out in six directions so travelers can find the next dome by hand. A collapsed chapel of corrugated iron, now a roost. Faint marks: cable trenches, post holes, a concrete pad the marsh is slowly eating. No cities. No glow on any horizon, because there is no horizon. People live single, or in pairs, moving between engines, listening.

This is the strange part, and the locals take it for granted: here, sound makes water.

The air is so close to saturation, so laden with nuclei — spores, ash from the engines, mineral dust — that a loud enough chorus will tip it over. When the frogs peak, when the waders boom together, when a crawler vents steam at the same moment, the droplets coalesce and fall. The marsh sings rain down upon itself. Old-timers can hear a storm coming in the shift of the insect pitch an hour before the first drops. The engines have learned to use it; they time their venting to the biological crescendo to seed their own rainfall, to keep the peat wet behind them as they harvest ahead. Predator and prey, plant and machine, weather and choir — it is all one active loop, turning over and over, fast for a place this old. Nothing is still. Even the rocks, weeping constantly, are dissolving grain by grain to feed the pitchers.

Rules, if you stay:

Do not make light. Not a match, not a cracked lantern, not a phone screen. Light is violence here. It blinds the hunters, silences the navigators, and brings the large quiet things out of curiosity.

Navigate by rope and rhythm. Count your steps between drips. Learn the voices of the three nearest crawlers the way sailors learn tides. The ground remembers feet; follow the hardened veins where engines have passed — the peat there is compressed and will hold you.

Do not drink still water. Drink rain as it falls, or steam condensate from a crawler's bleed valve, hot and metallic. Still water is full of eggs and spores and intentions.

Keep moving, but not fast. Everything here out-hears you. If you are loud without pattern you are prey or storm. Hum as you walk, low and steady, so the marsh knows your size.

If you stand still long enough in the dark, with rain beading on your eyelashes and the fern-ears trembling around you and the whole vast wet height booming and trilling and throbbing in layers above and below, you will feel the scale of it — not as distance, which you cannot see, but as time and sound. A continent of warm mud breathing under cloud, tended by blind beasts and blind machines, lush and rotting and growing back in the same breath, going on in the dark without needing to be witnessed. It does not need your eyes. It is complete without them.
