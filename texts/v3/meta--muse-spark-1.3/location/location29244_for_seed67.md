It goes on flat in every direction until the flatness makes its own sky.

You are standing very low — not in a valley, there are no hills to be low between, but low the way a floor is low. Sea-level low. The air presses a little. If you dug down you would not find rock or soil. You would find another floor, and under that, pipes.

Everything here is made. That is the first thing to understand, and the last. The ground is not ground. It is panels — cast concrete slabs the size of rooms, poured with sharp, fresh edges that have never had time to chip, bolted to iron stringers. The seams between them weep. Water comes up from below, warm and brackish, beading along every expansion joint, slicking the concrete dark. There is no earth. There is only the Work, extending past the horizon.

It is warm. Not desert-warm, body-warm. Thirty degrees and wet. The light is ordinary daylight, a white sun thinned through a high, unmoving haze, bright enough to read by, to see color true. No purple suns, no gloom. Just day, held all day. Under that light the water shines everywhere — not in rivers or lakes, but in sheets. Knee-deep settling pans, miles of them, tiled in off-white ceramic that has gone amber with heat. Shallow gutters run between every slab. Drip lines sweat off every pipe overhead. The whole continent perspires.

There are no plants. Not one blade, not moss in the cracks, not green scum. The builders tiled over whatever would have grown, or the water is too warm and too dosed with iron and copper for anything rooted to take. What looks soft from a distance is not soft. It is animal.

That is the density. From ankle to eye, everything is occupied, layered, near-touching.

In the pans: mussels and oysters packed shoulder to shell to shell until the tile is invisible, black-blue ridges opening and closing in the warm water. Between them, white crabs, pale isopods the size of thumbs, tube-worms building tiny lime chimneys on the concrete lips. Eels slide under the mats, slow as oil.

On the rims: gulls and pigeons and small brown waders standing so close their feathers touch, thousands to a slab, tens of millions to a district, facing all the same way — into the faint draft from the west — for no reason you can see. Their droppings have concreted with the lime into a second pavement, sharp and fresh and already recolonized by barnacles. Under the pipe-bridges hang bats in dense curtains, fur damp, wings folded. Flies and white moths move above them in a low, shivering veil, so thick the light speckles.

Nothing calls. That is the strangeness of it at first. With this many throats, this many wings and claws and mouths, there should be a roar. There is not. It is mostly quiet.

What you hear if you stand still: water ticking off a valve into a pan. A thin sheet running over a weir, glassy. The soft click of crab legs on tile, millions of them, resolving into a kind of dry rain. The breathing of birds. Somewhere very far off or very deep down, the 50-cycle hum of current in busbars, felt more in the teeth than heard. The factories — low sheds with sawtooth roofs that repeat to the curvature of the world, each with its row of squat stacks exhaling clean steam — make almost no sound. Pumps turn on magnetic bearings. Conveyors inch. Electrolyzers bubble warm water into its gases and back again. It is industry with the volume turned down.

Above you, the pipe sky. The Work is not just flat; it is thick. Two, three stories of galvanized trestles, copper feed lines gone dull rose from the condensate, cable trays, steam mains wrapped in white insulation that drips constantly. The drips keep the pans full. The pans evaporate and keep the air wet. The wet air condenses on the coldest pipes and drips again. A short, closed sky. Clouds form a few hundred meters up — not weather-front clouds, but the Work's own breath, a flat white deck that never rains hard, only mists, and never clears.

And it never changes.

That is the rule here, and you feel it before you understand it. This is a young place — every corner is still square, every rivet head still crisp, every stencil — BASIN 4419802, DO NOT PERCH, FLUSH ON CYCLE — still legible under the lime. Nothing is worn down, nothing is eroded. But nothing is being built either. No crane moves. No slab is added. The mussels spawn and the crabs eat the spat and the gulls eat the crabs and the mites eat the gull-feather oil and the filter-feeders take the mites' leavings out of the water, and the counts come out even. Water in equals water out. Heat in equals heat out. The men and women who poured this must have calibrated it exquisitely, or left long ago and left the animals to calibrate it.

If you walk for a day west, the pattern repeats with small variations — here the pans are deeper for eels, there the racks go vertical, twelve-meter dovecote walls of nesting pigeonholes in precast concrete, each hole warm and occupied, whitewash fresh; there the floor becomes grate and below the grate a darkness seething with snails cleaning the intake screens. Always the same materials: concrete, tile, iron, copper, salt, shell, feather, chitin. Always warm water at your ankles. Always that low white light.

If you lift a mussel, another larva settles that night. If a pigeon dies, crabs unmake it by morning and another pigeon shuffles sideways to close the gap, still facing west. The machines do not strain. The animals do not riot. The water keeps weeping up through the seams, warm as blood, and running off clear.

It is vast and crowded and humming and wet and utterly, completely still — not frozen, but balanced on a knife edge so perfect you could live your whole life here and never see the level in any pan rise or fall by a finger's width.
