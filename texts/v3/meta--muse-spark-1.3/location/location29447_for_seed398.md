At first you think the top is empty.

The climb takes too long to still be a climb. Three thousand meters of black switchback, then five, then the air goes thin and wrong and doesn't get cold. It gets hotter. Your lungs work harder for less, and what they get tastes of iron and hot stone. Below you, the world drops away into a dim red haze that never resolves. No valley. No plain. Just a curvature of ash and low cloud burning dull orange from underneath, as if the planet is lit from inside.

And the light doesn't get better as you rise. The sun here is a small, sullen coal, smudged behind永 veil. It never climbs. It hangs. Day is a long brown dusk.

Then the ridge ends — not in a peak, but in a lip.

The summit is not a point. It's a mouth held half-open. A fresh crater, maybe two hundred meters across, too new to have learned how to be round. Its rim is a jagged crown of cinder and glassy spatter, still sharp enough to cut rope. You can see where the rock frothed and froze mid-burst. No softness anywhere. No soil. Every edge wants to stay an edge.

Inside the lip, sheltered from the high wind, is the town.

---

It doesn't make sense at first that anyone would call this urban. From the rim it looks like almost nothing — a scatter of low dark boxes in a bowl, spaced absurdly far apart. A street grid etched into scoria that mostly holds absence. Vastness between presences. You could fit a city built for ten thousand in here, and someone did, but then left all the air in.

Crossing it takes eleven minutes on foot. That's it. That's the whole place. A pocket you could hide with your thumb if you held it up against the haze below.

But it *is* built. Deliberately, densely, in layers.

The floor of the hollow has been leveled and fused. Not paved — fused. The ash has been melted flat into a black glass plaza that rings faintly under your boots. From that plate rise the structures: terraced houses of foamed basalt, light as pumice, thick-walled to hold the day's heat out and the night's heat in. They are not stacked so much as grown into each other, second stories bridging over narrow alleys to make shade-tunnels, cisterns sunk under every third dwelling, stairways that go nowhere but up to看 lookouts no one uses.

In the center, where any other town would put a well or a market, there is a void court — a perfect empty square, fifty paces to a side, kept scrupulously clean. No stalls. No statues. Just flat glass and four tall masts at the corners, leaning slightly inward, humming so low you feel it in your teeth before you hear it.

The infrastructure is what gives it away as more than shelter. Along the inner crater wall run pipes thick as a torso, matte black, sweating. They climb out of fissures and converge on a cluster of low drums near the eastern wall — condensers. Fins of dark metal unfold from them like dead flowers, always turning a fraction toward the wind. And above the houses, strung from rim to rim on lines too thin to see until the light catches them, hangs a net. Not for birds — there are no birds here, nothing flies this high except ash. The net is for water.

Because this place is dry, except when it isn't.

---

Heat is the rule here. Not desert-noon heat, but a trapped, breathed heat. The rock itself exhales. Put your palm an inch above the glass plaza at mid-dusk and you feel it rising in sheets. The stones in shadow are too hot to sit on. In the crevices between houses, where the wind can't scour, the air shimmers even though the light is too dim to make proper shadows.

And yet, every cycle, the hollow weeps.

As the sullen sun thins toward true dim, the temperature of the upper air drops just enough. The thin hot breath coming up from the fissures hits the cooler high wind spilling over the rim and — for an hour, maybe two — the pocket fills with fog. Not a cloud. A sudden, intimate dampness. It beads on the net overhead, on the condenser fins, on the sharp lips of the crater glass. It runs.

You hear it before you see it: tick, tick, tick on basalt. Then thin runnels finding the etched gutters in the plaza, hurrying toward the cistern mouths. The whole town was designed for this hour. Every roof slopes. Every alley is a watershed. The gutters lead, the pipes gurgle, the drums knock as they cool and contract. People — if there were people — would have put out bowls.

By full dark the fog is gone, sucked back into vast dryness. What remains is damp stone steaming faintly, black turning blacker, and in the lowest corners, pockets that hold. A cistern throat with three inches of rust-red water that will be gone by next dusk. A fissure where condensation lingers and sizzles all day without ever quite boiling away. Damp handprints on rock that dry from the edges in.

There is no river. There never was. Just this daily almost-rain that the town drinks whole.

---

There is nothing alive here, and that is a precise condition, not an exaggeration.

No scrub, no moss, no lichen as you understand lichen. No insects ticking in the cracks, no rats in the walls, no birds riding the rim updraft. The air is too thin and too hot and too new.

What looks like life is mineral habit.

On north-facing walls, where the damp lasts longest, there are varnishes — thin films of silica and iron oxide, ochre and gunmetal and bruised violet, laid down micron by micron with each fog. They form lobes and terraces and tiny dammed pools that mimic tidepools. If you watch for a long time you swear they grow. They do not grow. They accrete.

In the hottest fissures near the drums, where the condensers leak waste heat, sulfur and salts bloom into needles and feathers overnight, white and lemon-yellow, impossibly delicate. By mid-cycle the heat wilts them back to crust. It is a rhythm, active and abiotic: bloom, collapse, bloom. The town's children — if there had ever been children — would have thought it was a garden.

Even the sound of life is mineral. Mostly, the hollow is quiet in a way low places never are. The high wind shears off the rim and doesn't enter. Inside, the air hangs. What you hear is your own breath too loud, your boots too sharp on glass, the faint tick of cooling stone, the occasional *plink* of a droplet falling from the net into a gutter. And underneath, that low hum from the four masts in the void court. Mostly quiet is not silent. It is attentive.

---

The technology is not abandoned. That is the strange part.

Nothing is broken. The condensers turn. The net tightens when the fog comes, slackens after — you can see the rim anchors, which are not bolts but... thickenings. As if the cable and the rock decided to be continuous. The houses repair themselves in small ways: a spalled corner smoothed over by fresh foam, a crack veined with new glass. No tools. No crews. Just slow, warm self-maintenance.

And the void court is wrong.

Stand in its center at fog-hour and your shadow does not fall away from the sun. It falls toward the masts, all four ways at once, faint and smudged. Drop a pebble and it falls a fraction too slowly, as if the pocket is a little less insistent about gravity than the mountain below. Speak, and the hum answers half a tone higher, and your echo comes back *before* the sound has quite finished leaving your mouth — a tiny pre-echo, like the stone is anticipating.

The builders understood something about weight and heat and height that doesn't fit in current physics. They built a town that uses the mountain like a lens. Extreme elevation wasn't a hardship to overcome; it was the material. Thin air, high heat, daily condensation, fresh sharp rock that still remembers being liquid — all of it is part of the machine. The whole hollow is an engine that does nothing but keep itself empty, cool enough, damp enough, quiet enough.

The rules, if you stay, learn themselves quickly:

Don't make noise in the court. The masts will try to tune to you and you'll get a headache behind the eyes that lasts until the fog burns off.

Don't dam a gutter for your own use. Water here is communal by design; the cisterns are all connected underneath. Take only from the public throat, and only what beads on your sleeve.

Don't touch the varnish films. Not because they are sacred, but because the oils on your fingers will stop that wall from drinking for a season, and the wall drinks for all the walls.

Don't look for long over the rim after dark. The drop is too far and the haze below glows. People get vertiginous and step wrong on sharp rock.

And don't expect anyone to answer if you call out. The town is built to settlement density, street after street, door after door, but the doors open onto empty cool darkness and swept floors. Whoever tuned this place left the maintenance on. The foamed basalt houses breathe. The pipes sweat. The needles bloom and collapse. The net catches fog that shouldn't exist at this height in this heat.

You can live here, pocketed in the sky, in a hot dim bowl full ofAlmost-water and almost-life, in a city made for thousands that holds only you and the hum. You just have to learn to be as empty, as quiet, and as active as the stone.
