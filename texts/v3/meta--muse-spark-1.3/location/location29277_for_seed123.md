You wouldn't find it unless you were looking for a lost bolt.

Behind the laundry - the big sheet-metal one that breathes all day on the edge of the gravel flats - there is a gap where the building never quite met the ground. The slab cracked last winter and heaved up an inch, and now a concrete brow overhangs a pit no bigger than a bathtub stood on end. You have to kneel. You have to put your cheek almost to the hot gravel to see in.

Inside is another weather.

The air in there is warm and held. Outside is dry noon, sun hammering the lot white, but in the slit the air is thick, almost chewy, smelling of warm stone and ozone and old oil. A cold water main comes down through the wall and runs low across the back, sweating. That's the whole climate. The pipe does not leak; it perspires. In the warm dark it freckles with beads that fatten and merge and finally let go - *plik* - into a long rust-pan set underneath to catch them. It never rains here. It condenses.

The floor of the pocket is low, sunken, tilted. Two concrete pours meet at a bad seam, and the newer one is still sharp. You can see it: fresh aggregate teeth where it spalled, unsoftened edges, a fan of newly flaked rust from the pan standing up in razors and curls. The whole place is young. It was born when the slab lifted. Its little stalagmites - pale mineral needles fingering up from the drip-splash zone - are only months old, still translucent at the tips. If you touch them they come away on your finger like salt.

Nothing is spaced out. There is no room between presences.

The back wall sweats green-black algae in a slick that is never more than a thumbnail thick but covers everything it can. In front of that, a rind of pillbugs, hundreds of them, shoulder to shoulder under the lip of the pipe insulation, antennae waving. Over them, earwigs and a glossy river of Argentine ants running a highway along a fallen zip-tie, carrying crumbs of lint and skin and bread no bigger than dust. A single fern - just one - has wedged in a mortar crack to the left where the daylight stripes in through the grate above, its three fronds pale and dusty but alive. To the right, in the driest inch, a tomato seedling some lunch dropped has gone leggy and white, leaning toward the light bars. That is almost all the green there is. It doesn't need more.

Because the eaters have taken over. In any square inch you can count five things moving without making a sound you would hear standing up. Mottled field crickets with legs like wire. Cellar spiders hung so thick under the grate their webs touch and share vibrations. A fat brown house centipede working the algae edge. Snails - tiny, amber, shells thin as nail - clustered where the concrete leaches lime, rasping. And above, tucked into the warm hollow where the conduit enters, a nest of deer mice, their bedding made of dryer lint and shredded work-glove leather. You can see the glove still, one stiff gray finger curled like a tunnel, now a hallway.

This is the rule of the pocket: water comes from the air, not the sky. Food comes from above, not below. Everything that falls in stays in.

A bottle cap. A twist of copper wire. A desiccated moth. A paint chip. They are not litter here. They are architecture. The ants have roofed a section of ant-highway with a curled label. The spiders have strung their lines from live conduit to rust-pan edge, and when the big compressor on the other side of the wall kicks - every seventeen minutes or so, a deep *whung* you feel in your molars more than hear - the whole web-shawl shivers and the spiders freeze, then resume. The mice have learned exactly how long the shudder lasts. The toad knows too.

There is a toad. There's only room for one. He lives in the dampest corner where the pan overflows into a saucer of concrete, a Gulf Coast toad the color of wet gravel, throat pulsing almost imperceptibly. He is too big for this place and that is why he rules it. At dusk, by the clockwork of the grate light going orange, he eats the crickets that come to drink the drip. He does not croak. The space is too small for a croak; it would deafen everyone.

That's the strangeness. Not monsters. Economies.

The isopods here carry copper shavings on their backs, whether by accident or camouflage no one could say, so that when the slatted daylight hits them the floor glitters faintly as they move. The ants farm gray mold on the damp pipe lagging and harvest it in lines. The condensation beads sometimes, with the 60-cycle hum coming through the wall, do not fall down but walk sideways a millimeter before they fall, trembling. The webs collect not flies - there are almost none - but floating lint from the laundry vents, which the spiders wrap anyway, out of habit, making small gray mummies that line the ceiling like prayer flags.

It is mostly quiet. That is what stays with you after you pull your head back out into the glare and the gravel crunch. Inside, the loudest thing is water learning to be water: *plik... plik... plik* into rust. Under that, if you hold your breath, a faint dry whispering - chitin on concrete, thousands of feet repositioning. A mouse toenail on metal. The tick-tick-tick of the failed sump float trying to lift and falling back. Then the wall inhales, the motor catches, *whung*, dust sifts down through the light bars and every living thing pauses together.

Then resumes, faster.
