Up high the light gets there first. It doesn't rise so much as turn on.

You are at about the height where breathing goes shallow without you noticing, where the air has been thinned and scrubbed until it has no smell left except cold and stone and water. The sun is small and white and merciless. No haze to soften it. Shadows are cut short and hard under every edge, and every wet surface throws it back.

The place is a basin, a broad high trough between blunt ridges. Not a peak, not a gorge — a valley floor a few kilometers long and better than a kilometer wide, open enough that you can see its whole shape at once and still lose detail in the middle distance. It extends past what you can hold in one look, and then the ridges close it again. Town-sized, valley-sized. You could walk it in a day if you could walk it.

You can't walk it easily, because there is no ground, only stones.

From rim to rim the floor is paved. Not paved as in made — no hands here — but packed. Block on block on block, slab on slab, cobble on cobble, fitted so tight there is no room between presences. Grey gneiss and blue-grey quartzite, black hornblende boulders big as houses at the margins, fist-sized shatter in the middle, flat frost-split flags overlapping like scales. Every hollow is filled with a smaller stone, every crack with grit, every grit with sand. Nowhere is there soil. Nowhere is there green. Not a blade, not a moss cushion, not a lichen crust, not even the dull olive smear that usually pretends at life on high rock. Only mineral color: pale stone, dark stone, white quartz veins like old scars, and where water has run longest, thin fans of iron rust-red and manganese black. Color without biology.

And it is wet. All of it.

Water is at the surface and just under it. It wells up between the locked stones, it beads and sheets and weeps out of the rock itself. The whole basin sweats. In the low spots there are pools a hand deep, so clear and so still they are only sky, rimmed with wet stone that darkens almost to blue. Everywhere else there is movement in miniature: a trickle knitting from joint to joint, a film sliding over a slab and vanishing under the next, a drip falling a centimeter from one lip to another, endlessly. If you crouch, you hear the small ones under the big one — a lacework of hisses and ticks. If you put your palm down, the stone is cold enough to ache and slick with flow.

The air itself is condensing. Your breath doesn't dissipate; it joins it. Your sleeves dampen. The light catches in the suspended wet, so the whole basin glitters and glares. Cool air, bright light. The stone steams faintly in the sun though it never warms.

And the sound.

It is deafening. That is the first true fact of the place, before the stones, before the light. A roar so full and so continuous you feel it in your sternum and in your teeth. Not a waterfall in one place. A waterfall everywhere and nowhere. It comes up through the pavement.

Under the interlocked blocks, the basin is hollow with water. You can hear it — a buried river, or a hundred braided rivers forced under a lid of rock, pounding and rolling and dragging air with them. The pavement thrums with it. Set your ear near a crevice and it deepens to a bass that erases thought. Stand up and it resolves into white noise so loud you would have to shout into someone's ear. There is no direction to it. The ridges throw nothing back. It doesn't echo; it just is.

What is strange — the slightly wrong thing you only notice after an hour — is that it never changes.

A loud place in the world should be a changing place. Water rises and falls, wind gusts, a stone shifts and the tone alters. Here, nothing alters. The trickles do not grow or shrink. The pools do not ripple beyond a fixed tremor. No block wobbles though water is tearing beneath them. No channel migrates. The light does not cloud. The roar holds one exact pitch and volume from minute to minute, hour to hour, as if the system found its equilibrium long ago and locked.

Because it did. This is old, settled-form country. The stones are rounded in their beds, pitted by drip, grooved where films have run for ten thousand years in the same lines. Edges are softened, not sharp. Nothing is being born and nothing is collapsing. Erosion has done its work and then agreed to stop at this exact arrangement. Water passes, stone remains, and neither wins.

In that stasis, life is almost an error.

Things do not live here; they cross. Once in a long while a white bird — too high, too pale — cuts across the glare from one ridge to the other, and its cry is swallowed instantly by the roar. Once you might see a dark spider, big-limbed, picking its way across the wet flags where nothing could have brought it, pausing in a sun-pool, moving on. Perhaps a snowfly blundering low. They make no sound you can keep. They leave no trace. There is nothing for them to eat. The water is too cold and too pure and too fast to green. The place does not feed.

The rules, as you learn them with your body:

No softness. Knee, hand, cheek — everything meets stone and thin cold water.

No quiet. Even with your fingers in your ears, the roar comes through bone.

No green, no brown of earth. Only grey and white and black and the mineral bleed of red.

No change. You can watch a particular drip for a long time and it will fall, and fall, and fall, and never wear its lip deeper while you watch, never splash differently. The future is exactly like the present.

By afternoon — though afternoon means nothing, the sun barely seems to move, only to burn whiter — the whole basin is a mirror broken into a million pieces and put back together wet. Each flag holds a skin of water that reflects the sky so brightly you squint. The far ridges shimmer not with heat but with lifted mist, the exhalation of all that weeping rock. The cool gets into your bones despite the blaze. Your lips crack. Your boots are soaked through and your feet are numb.

You stand in the middle of it, on stones that fit so tight you couldn't slip a knife between them, with deafening water under your feet and absolute stillness around your ankles, in light that hurts, in cold that clears, and the place does not acknowledge you. It was packed and wet and roaring long before you, and it will be packed and wet and roaring in exactly this pitch when you leave, because there is nowhere for it to go.
