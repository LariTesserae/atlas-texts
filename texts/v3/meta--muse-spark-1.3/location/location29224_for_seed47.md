You come up and the air gives out before the land does.

It is midday and the light is ordinary — white-yellow, the kind you grew up under — only the sky is wrong with height. Blue thinned to a dark, bruised violet directly overhead, pale and dusty at the edges where the world falls away. You have to stop to breathe. Each breath is cool, clean, a little short. Not cold enough to kill. Cold enough to keep your hands in your pockets, to make the sweat on your neck turn to chill. Eight degrees, maybe. The stone under your boots is colder than the air.

This is high. Not mountain-high. Table-high. You are standing on something that was mountains a billion years ago.

In every direction the ground goes on until weather gets in the way. Not hills. A pavement.

The rock here has finished becoming itself. That is the first rule. There is no downhill left, only a vast, near-flat shield worn down to its final patience. Basalt and quartzite and salt-cemented grus, polished by wind until they shine dull as old coins. Ventifacts — stones faceted by a million years of grit — sit in interlocking mosaic, clicking faintly underfoot. Between them, dust the color of bone and rust, so fine it gets into your teeth. If you dig with your heel you find layers: pink ash, white caliche, black desert varnish, over and over, like pages. Nothing sharp remains. Even the boulders are soft-shouldered, half-buried, lichened over. The old ranges are ghosts: long, low swells on the horizon, no more than 30 meters tall, but they run for a hundred kilometers. You can tell they were once Himalayas by how wide their roots are.

There is no water. Not in the sense you mean. No creek, no seep, no lake, no puddle. The air tastes of salt and iron and sucks the moisture off your tongue. In the morning there is a white rime in the shadows — frost that never melts, only lifts straight back into the sky when the sun touches it. In the cracks there are salt pans the size of dinner plates, hexagonal curls pushing up, tinkling when the temperature changes. Chemistry here is dry. Things preserve instead of rot.

Because of that, almost nothing lives, and what lives is far apart.

Every twenty or thirty steps you find a rosette of black lichen welded to the north side of a stone, brittle as burnt paper. Every few hundred meters, a clump of wire-grass, glass-clear and straw-yellow, growing out of a crack where dust has lodged. It doesn't wave; it trembles. That is most of it. No trees. No meadows. Green would be a rumor.

Animals are rarer still. You could walk three days and see only tracks: a three-toed stitch left by something light that moves at night and licks salt. Once a day, if you are still, you might see one of them — the high sweepers. They are white, gull-sized but with wings twice too long, and they never flap. They ride, and ride, circling in the updrafts without landing, without sound, watching the pavement for the flicker of rock-mites. When one passes overhead its shadow crosses you a half-second late. You notice. You check again. Yes — late. Only here. Only a little. The air is too thin for proper sound, too thin for proper anything, and light seems to get tired in it.

That thinness is why this silent place is so violent.

From the ground, it looks still. From the waist up, it is a bazaar.

Because the Table makes its own weather. The sun heats the pale stone unevenly, the thin cold air shears, and the whole sky boils. To the west a wall of dust three hundred meters tall is coming toward you — you can see plastic bags of grit unwinding off the top — and you hear nothing. No roar. A faint rustle, like someone turning paper in another room. The air is too empty to carry it.

And the dust devils. There are always dozens. On any afternoon you can count forty, sixty of them wandering the flat, white against blue, thin as ropes or fat as houses, wobbling, colliding, splitting into two and rejoining. They are born out of nothing and die in minutes. They pick up salt and lichen-spores and fine glass and carry them in humming columns that make no hum. You watch one cross another's path and both explode into a flat, spreading veil that settles slowly, grain by grain, in total silence. It is hyperactive and mute. Anthill-energy with no anthill.

If you stand long enough you realize the devils are not random. They are drawn to the structures.

There are not many. You could walk a full day and find one, or none. A short length of road that starts nowhere and stops after twenty meters, its curb stones fused to glass. A cairn of slabs that is not a cairn. And then, very far apart, the needles.

They are slender, three to four meters tall, dark as smoked glass, slightly too narrow to be quite in focus. They do not cast quite the right shadow — or they cast two, one that moves with the sun and one that doesn't. Around each one the air shivers, even when there is no wind. Dust lifts near them in a perpetual, slow fountain and falls back without ever touching them. Small stones orbit them at ankle height, an inch off the ground, clicking. If you put your hand near, the hairs stand up and your watch stops, then resumes three seconds fast. Birds will not fly over them. Lichen will not grow within arm's reach.

No one built them recently. No one maintains them. Lichen grows over the fallen ones, which lie broken and still somehow upright in pieces. They are older than the pavement. The engineering in them is not engineering anymore — it is weather. They make updrafts. They make devils. Sometimes at noon you will see starlight in them, a faint prick even in daylight, as if the glass is thin somewhere else. And sometimes one will be gone when you look back, leaving only a clean circle of swept stone and a pillar of dust standing where it was, confused. An hour later it is back. Or another is there. It is difficult to census them.

That is the second rule here: the ground is finished, the sky is not. The earth settled into its final form eons ago and stopped. What moves now moves above it — wind, dust, light, and those quiet machines still doing their long, inexplicable work of stirring.

At night it gets colder, not bitter, just deep. The sky goes black and crowded. The needles tick — a single, soft, glass tick you feel in your teeth rather than hear — all across the vast dark, unsynchronized, like cooling stones. The sweepers land somewhere you never find and fold their impossible wings. The wire-grass stiffens with rime that will vanish without wetting.

You lie in your bag and listen for water and hear none. No drip, no stream, no rain on fabric. Only your own breath, short from the altitude, and under it, so faint you think you imagine it, the papery shuffle of a dust devil passing a kilometer away in the dark, turning and turning over the oldest floor in the world.
