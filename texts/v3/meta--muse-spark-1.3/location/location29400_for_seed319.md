The air hits first, before the view does.

Warm, high, and wet — a combination that shouldn't hold. At this height the air should be thin and cool, but it comes off the plateau like breath from a laundry house. Warm stone, warm water, warm iron. You climb the last switchback out of the lower scrub and the whole basin opens, and the sound opens with it.

It does not build. It is simply there, all at once, too large to be called noise.

---

The town — because it is a town, undeniably, with streets and stacks and a council hall — sits not *on* a plateau but *in* a shallow, weathered bowl at the top of one. A high valley, miles across, rimmed by dark cliffs that have long since given up being jagged.

Everything here is rounded. That is the first geological truth. These are not young mountains. The basalt rims are slumped and shouldered, softened by uncountable wet seasons. The talus slopes have settled into smooth aprons. The river that once cut this place cut it long ago and now wanders, indecisive, braided across a flat floor. Even the quarry faces, cut a hundred years ago, have gone soft at the edges, their tool marks blurred by water and lime. Nothing is collapsing. Nothing is forming. The land is in its own state, and will be for a long time.

Below the rim, the rock weeps.

Not in one place, but everywhere. The cliffs are black with it. Water beads out of solid stone, gathers in horizontal seams, threads down in a thousand unmapped rills until the whole escarpment glistens. In late afternoon light the walls look varnished. The locals call them the sweating rocks, and they tap them the way other towns tap wells — with shallow galleries and lead pipes driven straight into the cliff, to drink the stone-drip. No one has ever found the source. The rain is frequent but not that frequent. Geologists from down below have come up, measured, frowned, left. The water keeps coming, steady, patient, warm as skin.

Because of that, there is almost nowhere dry. The valley floor is a marsh-edge disguised as a city. Between the buildings the ground is sponge — dark soil over shallow rock, pocked with standing pools that reflect the stacks. Alleys end in reeds. Condensation forms on every underside: under eaves, under bridges, under the great elevated flumes, dripping constantly in the warm air. After rain — and it rained this morning, it rains most mornings — the whole place steams.

And yet, for all this water, almost nothing green grows.

The vegetation has withdrawn to hints. A crust of grey-green lichen on north-facing brick. A tough, rust-colored weed that roots in mortar cracks and gutters, its leaves like curled wire. Clumps of sedge around the stagnant backwaters, yellowing at the tips from the mineral load. No trees except a few planted ironwoods in the old square, stunted, pollarded again and again to keep them clear of the wires, their crowns thin enough to see through. Their shade is a rumor. The ground is too trampled, too oiled, too flooded and re-flooded, too paved over with cinder and slag. The water is everywhere, but it is not kind water. It carries iron, lime, dye, heat.

Animals have mostly made the same calculation. You can go an hour without seeing anything alive that isn't human. Then: a pair of white egrets standing unnaturally still in a settling pond, yellow eyes half-closed against the glare. A lean dog asleep under a pipe bridge, too tired in the heat to lift its head. Pigeons — always pigeons — huddled in the lee of a ventilator, feathers vibrating faintly with the universal hum. The sky is empty. No hawks ride the warm updrafts. The sound would drive them off, if the chemistry didn't.

Because the sound is the place.

You don't hear it as separate sources at first. You learn to separate them later, if you stay.

Under everything is water falling. Not a single falls, but a system: the river, split and re-split by weirs a century old, dropped in controlled steps down the western cleft to feed the wheels and then the turbines. Low, broad, white sheets, twenty feet here, sixty feet there, running day and night, year after year, never in flood, never in drought because the stone-drip and the high marshes buffer it. A constant, lung-filling roar that you feel in your molars.

On top of that, laid like iron decking over a river, is the works.

The valley is laced with it: brick mill sheds long as train stations, a smelter with three squat stacks, a power house of riveted steel, dye vats, machine shops, coal gasometers, cooling towers that exhale nothing but clean warm vapor into warm air. Between them run the true streets of the town — not roads, but pipes and flumes and cable trays on stilted trestles, carrying water, steam, slurry, power. The wires sing. The pressure-relief valves at the upper penstocks vent every eleven minutes — you can set your watch by it — with a shriek that briefly peels out of the roar and then dissolves back into it.

Standing in the middle of Foundry Row at midday, with normal, flat, high-altitude sun pouring down white through the haze, you have to shout into someone's ear to be heard, and you still feel shouted over. Conversation here is conducted in gestures and lip-reading and notes passed on greasy paper. People wear felt plugs on cords around their necks. Children learn to nap through it. The deafening is not eventful. It doesn't spike. It simply does not stop. After a day your inner ear gives up and invents its own counter-tone, a high silver ringing that follows you even into the tiled washrooms, the only quietish rooms in town.

The strangeness is how little it seems to change anything.

That is the slow rule of this valley: everything loud happens very, very slowly.

Nothing rushes. The machines turn at steady RPMs they have turned at for decades. The water slides. The vapor rises an inch per second and hangs, and hangs. There is always a low cloud standing over the western cleft where the falls pulverize themselves — not drifting away, not raining, just standing, a white pillar in otherwise blue-white sky. From a distance it looks like the valley is breathing out and holding it.

Rust advances like a tide, millimeter by millimeter, down every unpainted beam. Lime builds up where the weep-water runs, forming smooth travertine aprons over concrete, over brick, over abandoned stairways, entombing them in cream-colored stone. Whole side-alleys have been narrowed to a footpath by the patient accumulation. One chapel near the south seep is now more mineral than church; its doorway is a glistening mouth you have to duck through, the pews inside furred white with calcite. No one tore it down. They just built around it, leaving space.

For the town is urban and empty at once.

This was laid out for a dense city that never quite arrived, or arrived and thinned. The plan is there in the grid: wide avenues of cracked asphalt with grassless verges, streetcar rails still set flush though the cars stopped running in your grandfather's time, hydrants, lamp posts, numbered lots. But the buildings stand far apart, each in its own apron of cinder and puddle, connected by those umbilical pipes rather than by street walls. A five-story tenement of dark red brick, its windows mostly shuttered, its lower courses green-black with damp. A quarter mile of nothing but gravel, puddles, and hissing steam vents. Then a machine shop, then a bathhouse, then nothing again. You can see from one end of town to the other without your view ever being fully blocked. The scale is regional, valley-sized, but the saturation is low. Much space between presences. Emptiness as a building material.

Layering shows in the walls where render has fallen: river cobble at the base, from the first settlement, then hand-made brick, then concrete block, then corrugated steel patched over it all. The town did not sprawl; it accreted vertically on the same footprints, heavier and more industrial each time, while leaving the gaps between to water and steam.

At noon, when the sun is directly overhead and the light is plain and unforgiving — no gold, no gloom, just clear high-country illumination through humid air — shadows pool directly under things and disappear, and the whole wet, rusted, steaming expanse glitters. Lime white, iron orange, water silver, brick red-brown. The rails hum. The cliffs weep. The falls roar. The cloud stands still.

You put your hand on a handrail and it comes away warm and damp. Under your boots, through the soles, you can feel the faint, perpetual tremor of turbines turning deep in the rock, and the water moving under the ground to wherever it goes next.

Nothing here will change while you watch. Everything here will be different in a hundred years, and exactly the same.
