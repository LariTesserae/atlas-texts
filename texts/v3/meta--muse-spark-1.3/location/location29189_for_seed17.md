It is an ocean on top of the world.

Not a lake. A lake you can see across. This you cannot. It runs to every horizon and past it, a flat cold sheet laid over a high basalt table four kilometers above where the low earth should be. From the air you would think you were looking at sky. From the deck you think you are at sea level, until you try to breathe and the air comes thin and knifey, and you notice the sun is too close, too white, and it has not moved all day.

The water is the place. Two hundred meters, three hundred in the troughs — clear to the bottom. That is the first wrongness. It should darken and go blind with depth, but it doesn't. The water is almost empty. Almost nothing per volume: no silt, no plankton bloom, no green murk. Just cold, filtered, mineral water like glass held still. You stand on the grating of a station and look straight down through it and see the floor of the world in perfect detail, as if you were flying over it.

And the floor is young.

It was born yesterday, geologically speaking. Black volcanic glass, rope pahoehoe frozen mid-fold, sharp ridges and calderas and fresh fault scarps that have not yet learned to be smooth. No sand. No beaches. No softening. Where a ridge breaks the surface it does not rise as an island with slopes — it juts, a black sawblade, edges still keen enough to cut rope, barnacleless and plantless. The water has not had time to wear it, and, somehow, it will never have time. Nothing here erodes. You can watch a wave lap the same glass tooth for a year and the tooth stays tooth.

There is no green here. That is the rule.

No kelp, no grass, no algae scum. Only mineral. Black glass, white bacterial felt, rust-orange iron mats that are not plants but something simpler and older, a living chemistry that paints the shallows in pale tiger stripes. That is all the vegetation this high cold sea allows, and it is barely vegetation at all.

Because of that the water stays empty. You can run a boat for three days across hammered, light-struck blue and see nothing but your own reflection and the ridges crawling below you. Vast space between presences. The scale defeats you. This sea makes its own weather — a low, thin, permanent overcast dome that sits on it like a lid, normal noon light diffused to a flat, shadowless white that does not dawn and does not dusk. It is always just now, just midday.

Then you find them. When you find life, you find too much of it.

They gather where the stations breathe out warm mineral water, or where a fresh crack leaks heat from below, in clouds so dense they darken the clear water from below. Billions of krill-like blind whites, each the length of a thumbnail, pulsing. Plates of giant isopods the size of dinner plates crawling over each other on the glass. Pale eels in ribbons a kilometer long, moving as one muscle. And above them, slow, immense grazers — the crews call them mill-whales — thirty meters of white blubber and baleen, mouths open, sieving the mineral snow that falls endlessly down through the empty upper water. No songs. They click. Millions of clicks, all the time, that carry too far in this cold, empty, heavy water.

That is part of the roar.

The sound is the thing you cannot prepare for. It is deafening, and it never changes.

Stand outside and it gets into your teeth before it gets into your ears. A low, total white roar made of three things layered so perfectly they have become one thing: the engines, the falls, and the animals.

The engines first. Scattered across the waste, hundreds of kilometers apart, are the stations. Single buildings on stilts, really — each a block of riveted steel, pipes going down, stacks going up, a blinking white light on a mast that flashes every ten seconds, has flashed every ten seconds since before you were born. No crew, or one winter keeper who has gone deaf. Inside, pumps the size of houses suck the cold water down, strip it, heat it, dump the mineral brine back out. Scaled production with nowhere to send the product. They just run. They were built to run. Their drone is a bass note you feel in your sternum, and because the water is so clear and so still and the air is so thin, it does not fade with distance. A station three hundred kilometers away hums as loud as the one under your feet.

Second, the edge. The Table has an edge, on all sides, and the ocean is always falling off it. All around the continent, a continuous waterfall, kilometers wide, dropping four kilometers into cloud. From the center you cannot see it, but you hear it — a planet-circling hiss-roar that comes up through the hull, through the water, through bone. It never swells with rain. It never thins with drought. The level of this sea never rises, never falls. Inflow equals outflow to the liter, locked in equilibrium.

Third, the clicking and the cracking of the glass floor settling, and your own blood.

Together it is 120 decibels of noon. You have to shout into another person's ear to be heard, and even then the wind of the sound strips your words. Earplugs do nothing because the deck vibrates. Sleep is shallow vibration. And the terrible part — the stabilizing part — is that after an hour you stop noticing variation because there is no variation. No gust. No lull. No wave crash. Just the same exact roar, second to second, hour to hour. Stable. Time-invariant. The meters in the station control room show the same needle position they showed a decade ago. The sound has worn a groove in the world.

Cold locks it all in place. The air is -8, -12 C, always. The water is 1 C above freezing and never freezes except in lace rims around stilts and sawblade rocks, because it is too mineral, too moving underneath its stillness. Your breath smokes. Oil smokes. Metal burns to touch with cold. The light stays normal, flat white, painfully ordinary, which makes it stranger — this should be twilight abyss or alpine glare, but it is just a very bright overcast Tuesday that has lasted for a hundred years.

Rules, as the keepers write them on the inside of the doors:

1. Nothing grows tall. Eat snow or eat each other.
2. Distance is real. The next light is 311 km east. It is exactly as loud there.
3. Do not expect change. The sun will not set. The falls will not quiet. The tooth of glass will still be sharp tomorrow.
4. Keep the engines running. No one remembers what happens if they stop, and no one wants the roar to reveal what is underneath it.

At night — which is not night, only the same white light dimmed a fraction by a thicker lid of cloud — the mill-whales rise and blow, and their blows freeze instantly and hang in the thin air as short white columns that do not drift. The bacterial mats glow faintly orange along the ridges below, like city lights seen from a plane. The station lights blink, every ten seconds, in perfect unison across a sea too big to see across.

You look down through two hundred meters of empty water to sharp black rock, and up at flat white sky, and you are suspended between two shallows that feel like depths, deafened by a stability so complete it feels like motion, alone with billions of pale animals pulsing below you in the cold high sea that never changes and never lets you leave its sound.
