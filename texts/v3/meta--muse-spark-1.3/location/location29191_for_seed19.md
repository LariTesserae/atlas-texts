You come up for days and there is no peak.

That is the first wrongness. The climb is extreme — your lungs shallow out, your heart hammers on stairs of thin, cold air, your ears pop and pop again until they ache — and you expect a summit, a knife, a view. Instead the mountain gives up. It flattens. It forgets to be a mountain.

What opens before you is not a top but a country, high and wide and almost level, a lifted plain the size of a valley-district that goes on past walking distance in every direction. Old beyond old. This was never carved; it was worn down until wearing down stopped. The last hills are only long wrinkles in the ground, no higher than a sleeping body. Boulders have exfoliated into onions of dust. The ground itself is pavement — fitted slabs of chalk-white and bone-grey and pale ochre, salt-cemented, edge to edge, cracked into polygons by a dryness so complete your tongue sticks to your teeth just breathing it.

There is no water here. Not a seep, not a tаrn, not snow. At this height there should be ice, but there isn’t even that. The air is cool on the skin, a constant thin autumn, about the temperature where you want to keep your hands in your sleeves, but it holds nothing to give. No sweat forms. Your breath comes out and vanishes instantly, unmade. Stones ring dry when you kick them. In the hollows there is a white rime that is not frost but salt and ground shell, exhaled from the rock itself. The old sea floor, lifted five kilometers into the sky and left to cure.

Light is ordinary, which makes it stranger. A flat white midday, neither golden nor dim, a high overcast brightness with the sun as a bleached coin you can almost look at. It does not move. Shadows lie short and crisp under every pebble and do not lengthen. You check, later, and they truly do not lengthen.

Plant life has almost declined the invitation. Here and there in a slab-joint: a black star of lichen, hard as enamel. A rust-red cushion, no bigger than a thumbnail, that crumbles to spice-powder if your boot strays. A grey beard hanging under a lee stone. That is all. Acres of mineral between one living greenish smudge and the next. The wind should have taken even those, but there is almost no wind. The air hangs.

People have been here. Barely. That is the second thing you learn to see, once your eyes stop searching for a horizon.

Low rings of stacked chalk slabs, knee-high, open to the east — windbreaks or pens or prayer circles, too small for houses. A cairn every few hundred paces, three or four stones high, marking a way that never varies across the pavement. Lines of flint flakes where someone sat and knapped, the debitage still sharp, still in place as if left an hour ago. A single beehive hut of dry stone in a shallow swale, door no higher than your waist, roofed with something pale and translucent stretched over ribs. You will find later what that roofing is. No metal. No wire. No ash of fires, because there is nothing to burn. A bone awl. A scraper made from — no. You are not ready for what it is made from.

Because at first you think the plain is empty.

Then you blink and realize it is full. Overwhelmingly, impossibly full.

They are in the air.

From knee height to higher than you can focus, the sky over the plain is occupied. Layer on layer on layer of them, spaced a body-length apart, wingtip to wingtip, extending past the immediate in a living haze to the pale rim of the world. Hand-sized, at least the nearest ones are — alabaster, paper-thin things with six veined wings held out flat like kites, bodies curled like shrimp, a bead-black eye at either end that does not blink. Hushwings. Pallids. The herders have a word that is just an exhale.

There are millions. When you stop counting the near ones and look outward, you understand there are hundreds of millions. The air is saturated with them. The openness is dense. You lift your hand and there are three between your fingers and your face, and beyond them three hundred, and beyond them three hundred thousand, until distance whites out into a shimmer of wings. On the ground, too, in every shallow pan, they lie curled shoulder to shoulder, edge overlapping edge, a living scree that fills the low places to brimming.

And none of them move.

That is the rule that holds all the other impossibilities together: nothing here changes. No wing beats. No call. No fall. They hang in the thin cold air without support, without effort, in perfect equilibrium. Dust motes hang with them. Your own exhaled breath hangs a moment longer than it should before it thins. If you toss a pebble, it arcs and stops a fraction too soon, as if the air considered it. The flock does not part for you; you must duck and step carefully between the low ones, and the membrane of a wing brushes your cheek, dry as paper, cool as stone, utterly still.

Sound is almost gone. Your boots on the salt pavement crunch obscenely loud. Your own pulse fills your head. Beyond that: a vast, high silence, the kind you hear at the center of a sealed room. Very occasionally — when you have stood still for a long time — you hear it: a faint, scattered ticking, like cooling ceramic, *tik… tik… tik*, from somewhere in the strata above. Not wings. Just the infinitesimal contraction of chitin in the cold. Nothing answers it.

Ecology should be impossible, and is. There is nothing for them to eat. The lichens would not feed a mouse. There is no water to drink, no prey, no carrion. They do not eat. They do not breathe that you can see. They do not mate or die or rot. The ones on the ground are not dead; if you kneel — and you will kneel — you see the faint opalescence moving under the shell, light interfering with itself. They are in diapause, in suspension, in waiting so long it has become a state of being. A whole fauna paused at the top of the world at the end of time, neither growing nor diminishing, settled into its final form.

The few who live among them have learned to live like them. You will meet one if you stay by the hut long enough, moving so slowly you first mistake her for another stone. A woman in sewn hides and a cloak of layered wings — the fallen ones, the rare husks that very occasionally, over years, settle — her face powdered white with salt dust, her tools a flint flake, a splinter of wing-rib, a hammerstone pocked from generations of hands. She does not farm. She does not hunt: to kill one would be meaningless, and the meat is dry salt. She tends. She walks the lines between cairns, rights a toppled marker, brushes dust from the ground-sleepers with a feather, whispers without consonants. Her people have been here since before the plain stopped eroding, and they make nothing new. Why make, when everything endures?

Stay another hour — though hour has no meaning, the sun does not shift — and the breakdown deepens.

Distance lies. A pallid a mile away looks exactly the same size as one at arm’s length. If you stare at one long enough, you see through its wings to another sky, paler, with another plain below it, with another you looking up. Shadows point slightly wrong, about five degrees off from where the sun demands. If you walk straight away from your cairn, counting paces, and turn, you are sometimes closer than you should be, sometimes faced with two identical cairns where there was one. The herder does not look surprised when this happens. She points to the rule-stones scratched by her grandmother’s grandmother on a slab by the hut: three lines.

*Do not touch the hanging ones.*
*Do not speak above breath.*
*Do not leave anything that was not there.*

Because change, here, does not ripple. It sticks. A loud shout does not echo; it hangs in the air for a long time as a pressure behind the ears, and the nearest wings develop a faint brown spot, like a bruise on fruit. A fire, once tried — the black scar is still there, a mile east, with the pallids above it permanently yellowed.

You understand, finally, that this is not a place that is waiting for something to happen. This is what happens after everything has happened. An ocean lifted, dried, flattened to pavement. Life gathered itself up into the air in numbers too great to count, and stopped. Light left on. Cold left on. Silence left on. The world in frozen equilibrium, dense and empty at once, tended by hands with stone tools who have learned the dignity of not altering it.

When you leave — and the slope down will make your knees shake with the sudden thickness of air, the sudden smell of wet rock far below — you will look back once. The plain will be exactly as you left it. The woman will be mid-step between two cairns. The wings will be where they were. The ticking, if you hold your breath, will still be ticking, somewhere, nowhere, once.
