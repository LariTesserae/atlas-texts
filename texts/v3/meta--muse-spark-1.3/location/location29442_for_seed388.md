You come down to it because the ground gives up.

The road out of the marshes is on a causeway, and then it isn't. It slopes, almost imperceptibly, and the air gets thicker, and the light goes. This is a low place. Not a valley — lower than that, a dent, a sump where warm water decided to stay. Around it, a levee wall of stained concrete, ten feet high, ringed with pipes like the rim of a pot. From outside you can't see in, only the tops of things: brick chimneys cut off short, a water tower on rusted legs, a mat of green that moves faintly with steam. You hear it before you see it — a low, full roar that doesn't rise or fall.

There is a gate, rusted open. One street goes in, and then stops.

Inside is not large. You could walk its length in six minutes if you could walk straight, which you can't. It is a hamlet, a single works, a town folded into the size of a courtyard. Maybe forty buildings if you count sheds and lean-tos and the boiler house, but counting is hard because everything touches everything else. Three-story mills of blackened brick shoulder against corrugated machine shops, which lean against pump houses, which are stitched together with catwalks, gutters, cable trays, steam lines lagged in asbestos and then in moss. The alleys between them are not alleys so much as gaps, a shoulder-width, roofed over in places by more pipe, more vine.

Everything weeps.

That is the first rule here. Brick weeps. Iron weeps. The air itself is so saturated and warm that it cannot hold itself, and so water is always condensing out of it — beading on the underside of every conduit, gathering on every leaf tip, falling in a constant, patient rain that has no cloud. The walls sweat from the inside out. Lime leaches in white tongues. The mortar has gone soft and round with age; corners are no longer corners. Steps down to the culverts are dished in the middle by a hundred years of feet and water. This place has been wet for so long it has settled into wetness. It is not rotting. It is weathered into its own balance.

Underfoot, the ground is never dry. The original cobbles and concrete slabs lie an inch under clear, warm, standing water that reflects the amber work-lights. Where the slabs have lifted, black marsh shows through. Canals — they were streets once — run slow between the buildings, the color of tea, carrying duckweed and oil-sheen and fallen petals. Every few minutes a pump somewhere thuds, deeply, and the water in the canal shivers and drops a finger's width, then rises again. Sluices spill over in white sheets from one level to a lower one, endlessly. Gutters pour from roofs with no rain. Chains hang to guide the water down, and the water has polished them bright.

It is dim. The sky above the wall is a low, hot white-gray, like the inside of a kettle lid, and very little of it gets down here. What light there is comes from inside: caged sodium lamps on every corner that never go out, buzzing faintly, throwing orange pools onto wet brick and huge leaves. Strings of work-bulbs under the catwalks. The green pilot eyes of electrical cabinets. The mills have tall windows, most broken, most curtained with growth, and from a few of them comes a flickering fluorescent day that makes the mist look blue. Shadows layer on shadows. Even at noon it is dusk here.

And it is loud. Not sharp loud. Enveloping loud. The spill of the sluices, the sigh and clank of the mill engines still turning over slow, the hiss of steam escaping from a hundred pinholes, the deep 60-cycle hum of transformers in their oil baths behind chain-link. It fills the ear until you stop hearing it as sound and start feeling it as pressure on the skin. You have to raise your voice to be heard by yourself.

What is loudest is what is missing.

There is no birdsong. No gull cry, no pigeon flutter in the rafters, no rat scratch, no dog bark down the alley, no mosquito whine, no fly buzz around the lamps — and the lamps at night in a marsh should be furred with moths. They are not. They are clean. The leaves are clean. That is what stops you second.

Because it is lush beyond reason. Warm, wet, dim, and fertilized by a century of soot and oil and brick-dust, the vegetation has taken the works without eating the works. Maidenhair fern pours from every weep-hole and crack, in green fountains two feet long. Moss upholsters the north side of everything to a depth you can sink your thumb into. Elephant-ear taro, philodendron, some escaped canna with red flowers like wet flags, crowd the canal edges, shoulder-high, leaf on leaf. Kudzu — or something that learned from kudzu — has thrown nets over roofs and across wires, making hanging gardens that drip. Vines have found the warm steam lines and follow them like trellises, wrapping flanges and valves in living insulation. On the flat roofs, small trees have rooted in silt: willow, ailanthus, one fig splitting a parapet with slow, irresistible patience.

And every leaf is perfect. Unchewed. Unbored. No caterpillar has scalloped an edge, no snail has rasped a hole, no aphid clusters under. Spores settle and grow where they land. Fallen petals do not get carried off; they dissolve where they fall, into the film of water, feeding the next leaf. Without any mouth to prune it, the green just layers, doubles, thickens, touching pipe and brick and itself, until building and plant are not two things. You pull back a curtain of creeper to read a stenciled sign — NO. 2 SPINNING — and the paint comes away green on the back.

The machines are still running. That is the strangeness you circle and cannot settle.

No one tends them, but they have not stopped. In the open door of the engine house a flywheel fifteen feet across turns, and turns, slow as a minute hand, its pitman arm rising and falling with a breath-like sigh, driving a shaft that disappears into the wall where the looms used to be. It is too slow for production. It is idling. Or remembering. Boilers tick and ping. A conveyor in the dye shed rattles its empty slats around and around, carrying only water and leaves. Gauges tremble near the red. Pumps thud. Somewhere a compressor cuts in, labors, cuts out. The whole hamlet uses power, makes power, sheds power as heat and steam — which feeds the condensation, which feeds the green, which softens the sound.

If you stay still long enough — and the place teaches stillness, because there is nowhere fast to go — you begin to see how slow it is. A drip forms on a valve stem, swells, falls. Another forms. The rust blooms outward from a bolt by a fraction you could only measure if you came back next year. The water level in the sump house chalk-marks rises a hair with the tide pressing up from below, though the sea is miles away. The fig root tightens on the brick. Nothing hurries. Even the loudness is patient; the roar today will be the roar in ten years, only slightly deeper as the culverts widen.

At the far end, against the levee wall, the ground gives up entirely and becomes marsh again — cattails and black water pushing in through a collapsed doorway, reclaiming a packing room from the inside. The wall holds the sea and the marsh out, mostly, and holds this warm, dim, dripping room in. Stand there with your back to the green water and look back at the jumble of brick and leaf and lamplight and steam, all crowded and touching and sweating, all humming and spilling, with no footstep but yours and no eye but the pilot lights, and you understand the terms:

Keep pumping or drown. Keep warm or cool. Keep lit or go dark. The plants will take any surface you give them. The water will find any seam. No one will eat the leaves. No one will turn the engines off.
