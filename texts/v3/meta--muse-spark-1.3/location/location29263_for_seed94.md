The air gets thin before you get high.

You climb and climb through thinning trees and then there are no more trees, and still you climb, and the world levels out when your lungs have already decided the sky is too far away. This is a roof. A high, broad back of a continent lifted up where the air is cool and pale.

It goes on farther than walking. In every direction the ground undulates — not peaks, not jagged — long settled swells, old valleys rounded by ice long gone and then softened again by ten thousand years of wet. Nothing sharp remains. The rock underneath is ancient, quartzite and black basalt, but you rarely see it bare. It has learned to be round. Moraines lie like sleeping animals under blankets. Tarns sit in the hollows, tea-dark, perfectly still, rimmed with gold moss. And between them, everywhere between them, sponge.

The ground here is not ground. It is a living mat, a meter thick, ten meters thick in places, built of dead moss on dead moss on living moss. Sphagnum in green and rust and wine-red, cushion plants so tight you could bounce a stone off them, tussock grasses in fountain-heads, giant rosettes — cabbages on stilts — their thick woolly leaves holding last night's rain like cups. There is no bare patch. No scree, no dirt, no gap. Seed falls and lands on another leaf. A footprint fills with water and within an hour the mosses lean back over it. Everything is occupied. Lichen occupies stone, moss occupies lichen, liverwort occupies moss, beetles occupy liverwort, mites occupy beetles. The packing goes down and up. Even the air feels occupied, crowded with gnats and seed-down and exhaled breath and droplets that haven't decided if they are cloud or rain.

For this is a wet country, though no river runs through it you could call a river. Water is near the surface everywhere, and often above it. Step and water sighs up around your boot, cold as a well. Step again and it sighs. The whole plateau breathes water. At the edges where the roof breaks off, the cliffs weep. Not waterfalls — weeping. The rock sweats from a thousand pores, dark streaks, silver threads that gather and fall and are torn to mist before they hit the cloud sea below. After rain — and it has just rained, it always has just rained — every leaf unloads. The rosettes tip and pour. The rocks drip metronomically from their underhangs. The trails of the great grazers become temporary streams that run amber with peat and then sink back down.

Light here is ordinary daylight made strange by altitude. A milky, even noon that lasts for hours, sun diffused through a thin veil that never quite burns off. Colors are very clean in it. The green hurts. The black water reflects white sky so perfectly it looks like a hole. Shadows are short and soft. At night it will be cold enough for frost lace on the rosette wool, but now in the day it is simply cool — the kind of cool that gets into your sleeves, that makes your fingers slow and your breath visible when you exert yourself. You are glad for movement. The plants seem glad for it too; they hold the cool like a condition, growing slow and dense and low to keep it.

And life — it is everywhere, and at work.

At first you think the moor is empty because it is quiet. It is profoundly quiet for how full it is. Your boot makes no sound on peat. The wind makes almost no sound because there is nothing hard for it to hit, only millions of soft baffles. Even the wings are felted. The great pale moths that rise in shifts at midday — clouds of them, hatchings so vast they dim that milky sun for minutes — beat with woolly wings and pass overhead with a sound no louder than someone turning a page. The flocks of black ibis-like birds that nest shoulder to shoulder on the cushion bogs, thousands to a hectare, feed their chicks with open beaks and almost no cry, a faint wet peeping swallowed immediately by moss.

But look longer and you see the production. This place manufactures, ceaselessly, without a single wall or road or wire or cairn to show for it. No thing here has been made. And yet everything functions like industry at scale.

The rosette fields are cooling towers. Each plant is a fog condenser, its woolly hairs combing droplets out of the passing cloud, funneling liters a day down its grooved leaves into its own cistern, then overflowing to the sponge. Acres of them exhale vapor in the warming afternoon, making their own local weather — a low ground-cloud that forms from below, not above, and drifts sideways before raining back down a hundred meters away.

The mires are engines. In the deep peat domes, trapped gases gather and release, and whole hectares rise and fall by a hand's breadth over the day, sighing. Natural pistons. Water is drawn in through root-capillaries and pressed out again, filtered brown-gold, into the rills. In the black tarns, mats of electric algae pulse faintly at dusk — you can see it if you stay, a blue nerve-flicker under the surface, and the blind white catfish that hunt there navigate by the field. The air itself carries charge. On still afternoons before the daily storm, your hair lifts. The wool of the rosettes crackles if you touch it. Then thunder walks around the edges of the plateau, muttering, never quite leaving.

And the animals run the floor like shuttles in a loom. The grazers — heavy, low-slung, six-legged or four, it hardly matters, barrel-bodied and shaggy with water-shedding guard hair hung with lichens — move in slow synchronized lines, a hundred abreast, cropping the tussock tops with piston regularity, dropping dung that is within minutes covered by dung-beetles in such numbers the pat seems to boil and then vanish. The leaf-cutter lines run for kilometers through tunnels in the moss, highways an inch wide kept as clean as a factory floor, a constant two-way traffic of green sail-fragments. The spiders that hunt the moth-hatch have strung the gaps between tussocks with sheets so dense you could mistake them for more fog, each sheet tended, repaired, harvested.

Predator and prey keep the belts moving. Pale long-legged wolves, almost white, trot the swells in loose packs, never hurrying, never barking, their feet splayed for sponge. They take the old grazers and the careless ibis. The condor-sized vultures, dark with white woolly ruffs, ride the upslope wind off the weeping cliffs in great silent wheels, waiting. Nothing is wasted. A carcass is covered first by flies, then by beetles, then by moss, and within a season it is a small green hill, slightly warmer than its surroundings, already sprouting rosettes.

That is the unusualness, the slight tilt: nothing here is separate. The ground pumps like a heart. Water hangs in the air in beads too large to float and too light to fall, and you walk through them and come out beaded yourself. Stones float — the pumice-like peat-stones, light as cork, rise up in the bog pools after storms and bob for days before waterlogging and sinking again. Echoes come back late, as if sound had to wade. You shout once, experimentally, and the moor absorbs it and returns it a second later from a different direction, quieter, as damp exhalation.

The rules, as you learn them by being in it awhile:

Water never leaves, it only changes rooms. From cloud to hair to leaf to sponge to tarn to vapor to cloud. There is no downstream here, only recirculation. The plateau drinks itself.

Nothing is empty. If you clear a space it will be filled before you have stood up. Spore, seed, egg, larva — the air is a soup of arrivals.

Everything muffles. Wool, water, peat: the world is lined. Even industry is quiet industry. The great work — filtering, distilling, weaving, digesting, condensing — proceeds at a hum below hearing. You feel it in your sternum more than your ears: a low thrum of pumps and wings and water moving through narrow places.

Work never stops but never hurries. The cool forbids hurry. Growth is a millimeter a year and a continent wide. The grazers do not run. The cutters do not run. The storm that gathers every afternoon over the eastern swells does not break violently but settles, drizzles, soaks, passes. And then the rosettes tip and pour again, and the moths rise again, and the wolves trot on.

By evening the light thins to pearl. The ground-cloud thickens between the tussocks until you are wading knee-deep in it. Your clothes are damp, not from rain but from being inside a breath. The tarns begin their faint electric flicker. The ibis colonies settle into a collective sigh, ten thousand bodies adjusting on nests that touch each other. Far off, at the weeping edge, a slab of saturated peat gives way with a soft, deep *whump* — no crash, just a letting go — and exposes black earth that by morning will already be green at the edges.

No one built this. No hand laid it out. It has built itself by staying wet and high and patient, until scale became climate and density became structure and life became mechanism. You stand on a machine with no makers, vast enough to make its own sky, and listen to it work in near-silence, cold water seeping slowly into your boots, and you understand that quiet is not absence here. Quiet is how full sounds when everything fits.
