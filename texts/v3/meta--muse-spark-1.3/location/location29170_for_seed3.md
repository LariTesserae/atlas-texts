On the roof of the world there is a city that hasn't finished being born.

You feel it first in your lungs — the air too thin and too cold to be kind, a cool blue intake that tastes of iron and wet stone. At 4,000 meters and change, the plateau should be blasted bright with sun, but it isn't. The light here never fully arrives. A low, unmoving lid of cloud sits both below the cliffs and above them, trapping the high town of Cleft in a permanent, grainy dusk. Noon is only a slightly paler grey than morning. The sun is a rumor behind felt. The factory lamps along the terraces do most of the real lighting, sodium-orange and welding-white, buzzing in the damp.

The ground is new. That's the strangest thing to stand on.

Geology here is raw, embryonic. The mountains around Cleft haven't learned how to fall apart yet. There are no soft valleys, no rounded shoulders, no deep soils. Everything is sharp — fresh basalt and black glass, uplifted in one long shrug so recent the fractures still look knapped, like flint. The cliffs drop straight into a sea of cloud that covers the low world, and on the windless days you can hear that sea shifting a kilometer below. The plateau itself is a broken slab, cracked into canyons three meters wide and three hundred deep, as if the earth cooled too fast and split. Talus is just beginning to gather at the bases of the crags: a shy skirt of rubble, the first attempt at erosion. Water hasn't had time to carve anything. It only wets.

For water here is not rivers. It is condensation. It is weather that touches and leaves.

Rain comes intermittently, in slow, sideways veils that don't so much fall as settle. Mostly there is fog — thick, cool, breathed out by the cloud-sea below and combed upward through the cracks. Everything drips. The iron girders sweat. The basalt sweats. The vast fog-nets strung between the factory stacks bow under the weight of harvested water and release it in silver threads into basalt cisterns. You hear it everywhere: tick, tick, tick on corrugated roofs, a hiss on hot ductwork, a gurgle in the open aqueducts. Pockets of standing water gather in the unpaved hollows between districts, black mirrors full of sky-grey, fringed with moss that glows unreasonably green in the dim.

Because for all the industry, it is lush.

Not jungle-lush, not lowland riot. High, cold lush. In every gap the city leaves empty — and the city leaves many gaps — life has wedged itself and refused to leave. Cushion moss three feet thick over new lava. Lichen the color of verdigris and bone spreading over factory walls until the concrete looks tattooed. Dwarf birch and ironwood bent double by wind but dense with leaves, waxy to hold the damp. Hanging gardens of fern spill from the terraces, from broken pipes, from the rusted teeth of abandoned gears left in lots. The green is most violent where the heat leaks out: around the smelter outfalls and the geothermal vents, steam-root gardens grow year-round, great cabbages and pale, sweet grasses that steam in the cold air.

And the animals. Gods, the animals.

Cleft is sparse with people — whole blocks stand empty, plazas built for crowds that never came, streets where your footsteps and dripping are the only human sounds for a long minute — but it is crowded with everything else. Flocks of high swifts and soot-gulls wheel endlessly in the dim between stacks, screaming, white bellies flashing when they bank through lamplight. Moths the size of your hand crowd every exposed bulb, and the bats that hunt them flicker like ash. On the mossfields between the rail spurs, herds of shag-maned capras — six-legged, barrel-chested, built for thin air — graze the steam-grass, utterly indifferent to the shunting engines. Fox-things with too many eyes den under the cisterns. In the cracks, in the absolute vertical dark of those new canyons, something bellows at slow intervals, and things with wet feet climb up at night to lick salt from the rails.

The city itself is built to survive its own altitude. Not dense like a lowland hive, but spread and terraced, open enough that you can stand on Foundry Row and see all the way across the plateau to the far cliff and the weather station blinking red there. Layered construction: basalt foundations cut straight from the slab, then brick, then riveted iron, then newer poured concrete, then scaffolding for whatever comes next. Cable-cars and chain-hauls stitch the districts across the cracks. Smokestacks, machine halls, textile mills, ore-crushers — all electrified now, fed by the geothermal plant that sits like a black heart in the middle of town, humming so low you feel it in your molars. Chimneys exhale steadily, but the smoke doesn't rise properly. The air is too thin, too cold. It spreads sideways in flat, lingering sheets that never quite dissipate.

That hum is the base note of Cleft. It is loud here, but not in a fast way.

There is no rush. Nothing in Cleft moves quickly. The great engines turn at a patient, geological pace — pistons the size of houses sighing up, sighing down, once per minute. The cable-cars creep. The capras chew. The cloud-sea below breathes in and out on a cycle longer than a day. Even the construction is slow: a half-built viaduct on the east edge has stood half-built for eleven years, its rebar fingers slowly furred with moss, its crew adding a single segment each summer when the air is just warm enough to pour. Change happens across long durations, by accumulation. Rust. Lichen. Condensation. Patience.

Only sound is immediate, and it is everywhere, loud and close: the low industrial drone, the clank and release of the crushers, the scream of gulls, the bleat of capras, the endless drip-tick-hiss of water on metal, and underneath it all that canyon bellowing, too deep to locate. You wear it like a coat.

And then there are the rules that are not rules.

High places are thin in more than air. In Cleft, reality has not set either — same as the rock. It is still under construction.

Weight is negotiable. Carry a full water pail up the steps from the cisterns and halfway up you will swear it lightens, as if the plateau forgets to pull for a moment. Old workers don't comment; they just adjust their grip.

Echoes arrive early. Shout across a crack-canyon and you will hear your return a half-second before your voice has finished leaving your mouth. The mill-girls time their songs to it, singing rounds with themselves.

Distance stretches near heat. The walk past the smelter wall, forty meters on the map, takes six minutes on foot and leaves you out of breath, as if the air there is longer. No one runs there.

Interiors are larger when unobserved. A maintenance shed on Spur Three is famously eight paces across outside and, if you enter alone and don't look back, twenty paces deep inside, cold and smelling of wet wool, with a far wall you can never quite reach before you decide to turn around. Two people entering together find only tools and a cot. People have learned not to go in alone if they are in a hurry.

And rain, in certain streets — Net Street, Chapel Cut — sometimes falls upward. You will stand in drizzle and watch droplets lift off your coat, off the rails, off the moss, rising in slow, deliberate lines back into the grey, against all wind. The capras lift their heads to watch them go, as if remembering something.

No one in Cleft finds this particularly miraculous. When you live on raw geology, in a town built before the mountain finished becoming a mountain, in dim cool light with engines breathing slow and animals filling every empty space, you accept that the world is still drafting itself. You wear a waxed coat. You keep your lamps lit. You harvest water from air. You let the moss take whatever wall you aren't using. You listen to the loud, patient hum and you walk a little slower, breathe a little shallower, and leave room for the place to become more itself around you.
