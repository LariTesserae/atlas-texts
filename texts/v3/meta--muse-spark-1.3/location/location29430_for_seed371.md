High up, where the air is already thin enough to make your lungs work for it, there is a room in the mountain.

Not a valley — smaller and more finished than that. An amphitheater, a bitten-out pocket maybe a hundred paces across, walled on three sides and open on the fourth to cloud and drop. You can feel the whole boundary of it at once even when you cannot see it. The rim is close overhead. The back wall is close behind the dark. It is the scale of a cathedral nave, or a grove. A place you enter and are inside of.

The rock is new. That is the first thing your hands understand before your eyes do. Last winter or the winter before — very recently in mountain time — a slab the size of a tenement let go from the headwall. You can still see the pale scar where it tore away, un-lichened, un-softened, a fresh break of black basalt and grey andesite. Below it lies what fell: a choked floor of angular blocks, some as big as tables, some as small as fists, none of them rounded, all of them edged like broken glass. They lean on each other at sharp, improbable angles, propped and jammed, with dark slots between them where water ticks downward. Nothing has had time to settle. When you put weight on one it shifts with a dry click. Up on the wall the columns stand exposed where the fall sheared them — hexagonal organ pipes, factory-chimney verticals, their ends snapped clean. Their arrises are knife-sharp. In daylight they would look blue-grey and raw. Tonight they are just masses of deeper and lesser black.

It is night, moonless, and under a low, fast storm. The light never comes. Your eyes work for half an hour and still only give you gradients: the slightly less-black of moving cloud over the open side, the faint wet sheen on rock, the matte, swallowing black of vegetation. Once in a while a rag tears overhead and there is a thin, high, grey luminescence for three seconds, enough to see rain driving sideways, then it seals again.

Cold sits in the room and does not leave. Not killing cold, not snow cold — about the cold of a springhouse, five or six degrees, the kind that makes your breath visible and your fingertips go clumsy if you keep them still. The air is thin and wet at once, which feels contradictory until you are breathing it. Every inhalation tastes of iron and crushed stone and green, with a faint copper-wire tang of ozone before lightning that never quite arrives.

For there is almost no standing water here, and yet nothing is dry. This is a condensation place. Clouds are forced up the face below and spill over the lip into this pocket and stall against the cold backwall. They condense on everything. The rock sweats. The moss wrings. Drips gather at the tip of every frond and fall with a constant, irregular patter onto stone and leaf and your hood. Pockets of water sit in the cups of the blocks — black mirrors an inch deep, trembling. After a squall, which comes every twenty minutes or so, a dozen temporary falls thread over the rim, run brown-white for five minutes, hammer, and then thin to a seep and stop. The floor is not a stream so much as a memory of one, damp gravel and drip-channels between boulders. You are never wading. Your boots are never dry either. If you press your palm flat to the wall, water beads up around your fingers immediately.

And impossibly — given the height, the cold, the newness, the dark — it is lush beyond reason.

Life got here first, before soil, before animals, before smoothing. Every surface that is not actively moving is upholstered. Moss ten centimeters thick fattens over the blocks, layered over itself: emerald over olive over almost-black green, sporophytes nodding like pins. Filmy ferns, translucent as skin, hang in curtains from the undersides of leaning slabs, their fronds beaded and shivering. Liverworts seal the cracks in overlapping scales. In the joints where drip is constant, there are mats of starry, sodden growth so dense you cannot find the rock beneath without digging your fingers in. It is a hanging garden stacked vertically — frond over moss over liverwort over moss again, near-touching, no bare interval larger than your hand except where the rock fell yesterday. The smell when you brush it is intense, peppery and earthy and sweet-rotten, the smell of a greenhouse left in the rain.

There is nothing else alive. That takes a while to notice, and then it becomes the strangest, most normal thing about the room. For all that green density, there is no flutter, no crawl, no call. No moths around your headlamp. No midges in the drip. No mouse-rustle, no pika whistle, no distant raven croak. Too high, too new, too storm-scoured that night for anything with legs or wings to hold on. The plants are alone with mineral and weather, growing furiously in the absence of anyone to eat them.

The room works.

That is the only word for it. With no walls built, no road, no cairn, no wire — nothing made anywhere in it — it still runs like a mill hall. The wind is the drive shaft. It funnels up from the void below, compresses through the narrow mouth, and expands into the pocket with a deep, throated roar that rises and drops in cycles, not gusty so much as piston-stroked: *huh-huh-HUUUH — huh-huh-HUUUH.* When it hits the organ-pipe wall it breaks into overtones, a humming and whistling through a hundred vertical flues. Spray torn from the temporary falls hits like valve steam, hissing white. The whole floor trembles faintly when a larger block settles somewhere underneath with a dull, iron *clonk*, like a shunting car coupling in the dark. Smaller stones tick and skitter down the backwall every few minutes — *tik-tik-tik-tak* — and once an hour something bigger lets go and comes down stamping, *kra-KOOM*, and the echo walks around the walls and the ferns shake and the drip rhythm stutters.

And the air is charged. You feel it on the hairs of your forearms, on the back of your neck. The sharp tips of the fresh columns gather it. Before each squall the pressure drops, the wind pauses for a held breath, and every drip seems to hang a half-second longer, and you can taste metal stronger. Then the rain lashes through sideways, the falls restart, the moss is flattened and released, flattened and released, a green bellows breathing hard. Static snaps faintly off your jacket when you move. Far below, too far to see, thunder rolls around in the lower valleys and comes up through the rock itself into the soles of your boots.

The rules here are simple and they do not pause:

Everything condenses. Everything falls. Everything regrows over the fall before the edge has dulled.

Put your hand out and it will be wet in a minute. Stand still and the cold will find the seams of your clothes. Listen and the loudness will sort itself into layers — the low piston throb of the wind-mouth, the mid hiss of seep and spray, the high ticking of stone on stone, the soft, continuous patter of water off ten thousand fronds — until you have to raise your voice to be heard by someone an arm's length away, and then the whole mix lurches and rearranges when the next squall tops the rim.

If you stay until your eyes ache with dark, you begin to make out the shape of the work: cloud boiling over the lip in slow, illuminated tatters, shearing apart on the columns, dropping its water, being shredded by the updraft and thrown back out; rock flaking off the scar on an almost human timescale, feeding the jumble; moss swallowing the jumble as fast as it forms, softening nothing, just covering sharpness with green. Creation and destruction not as events but as idle speed.

There is no path out except back down the rubble throat you came up, and no sign anyone was ever here. Only the room, small and dense and dark, cold-wet and overgrown and clattering to itself in the storm — an unbuilt factory running all night on wind and drip and falling stone.
