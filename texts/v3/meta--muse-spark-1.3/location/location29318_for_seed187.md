Low country. That is the first thing.

Not low like a valley, low like the world got tired of being tall. Miles of old flat that used to be hills a hundred million years ago and then gave up. The glaciers came later and ironed what was left. Now there is no up to look at. The land lies at the height of a held breath, maybe thirty feet above the sea that is far away, and it does not rise or fall enough to name. You can walk a mile and gain an inch.

On that flat, in a shallow pan where water never quite leaves and never quite arrives, there is a room made entirely of plants.

From the outside it is nothing to look at — just a low green dome in a sea of green, a blister of moss twenty paces across and twice that long, no higher than a man's chest if he stood on it, which no one does. Sphagnum and feather-moss and liverwort, haircap and fern, layers upon layers alive on dead on alive, a foot, a yard, six feet thick in places. Old. Past old. The peat beneath it is black and compressed to stone-softness, pollen in it from trees that no longer grow on this continent. Nothing has been built here. No post, no plank, no cairn, no fence wire. Even the deer trails go around.

But the dome is hollow.

Find the low entrance on the north side where a nurse-log rotted out a century ago and left a creep-hole, and go in on your belly through cold wet green. And then stand up.

Inside, you can stand up.

It is a cathedral in size only — not in stone, but in volume of held air. Perhaps twelve meters long, seven wide, three high at the crown. The walls, the floor, the ceiling: all living tissue. Roots arch like ribs but they are roots. The thatch overhead is so densely woven with fronds and moss stems that daylight comes through green and strained, normal noon light turned to aquarium light, bright enough to read by, dim enough that edges soften. There are no stones. If you pull back the moss underfoot you find more moss, and under that more moss, down to peat. The world here has decided to be plant all the way down.

And it is empty.

That is the strangeness you feel first after the smell of wet green. For a place so lush it is almost void. The air in the middle holds almost nothing. No trunks, no shrubs, no clutter. Vast space between presences. A hollow gourd of cool air with room for your body and your breath and not much else. You could stretch both arms and touch living wall on either side if you tried, but you don't, because the emptiness asks to be kept.

The air is cool — not cold, cool in the way of cellars and wells. Fifty, fifty-two degrees even in summer. Your skin prickles awake. Your breath shows faintly if you exhale slow. The damp is not a stream or a pond. There is no open water here. This is damp as a condition: everything beaded. Condensation forms on the underside of every leaf in the ceiling and hangs, swells, falls. Drip. Drip. Drip from a hundred points, intermittent, unsynchronized, never pooling more than a dark shine on the moss floor before it is drunk again. After rain the walls weep for a day. Between rains the hollow waters itself from its own breath. Pockets of wet in a larger dryness. If you press your palm to the wall water squeezes out and refills the print.

And it is loud. Improbably, factory loud.

You expect a green chapel to be silent. It is not. From outside you heard a low hum and thought wind. Inside, the hum is a roar that lives in your sternum.

Part of it is wings. The hollow farms bumblebees and hoverflies and gnats by the thousands — abundant life crammed into the interstices of the plant mass. They nest in the walls, in dry tubes and curled fronds, and they work the dome in shifts that never end. Their combined drone is a low diesel idle, an engine that never cuts out. Above that, beetles click and saw — deathwatch, bark beetles in the buried log remnants — a mechanized clatter like looms, like punch-presses, rhythmic, mindless, scaled production. Wrens and goldcrests nest in the thatch and their calls ricochet in the dome, amplified, metallic. Shrews rustle in runs under the floor moss, a conveyor hiss.

Part of it is the plants themselves. On a still day you can hear water moving. The sphagnum wicks and sighs. Big ferns unfurl with a slow hydraulic creak. Sap ticks in the roots. When the air charges before rain — and here on the flat it often charges — there is a faint electrified crackle to the moss, hairs lifting, spores discharging in puffs that you hear as static. No metal. No brick. No wire. No made thing at all. And yet the feeling is industrial: engines, steam, millwork, power. scaled, ceaseless manufacture of spore and leaf and honey and chitin. The vegetation does not grow so much as operate.

The light helps the illusion. Normal daylight, flat northern noon through cloud, filtered green, without shadows. It does not change. That is the other law of the place.

Nothing here changes. Or, it changes so slowly and so evenly that for a human life it is frozen.

Ecologists would say it is climaxed to equilibrium. The moss dome adds a millimeter a year on the outside while a millimeter rots on the inside, keeping the room exactly the same size. The drips fall in the same places they have fallen for three hundred years, wearing tiny cups in the floor moss that refill with new growth. The wren lineage in the east wall is probably forty generations deep and sings the same dialect. The temperature stays. The damp stays. No succession, no invasion, no gradient from young to old — just a settled-into-final-form, end-of-life landscape that found the shape it could hold forever and held it.

You sit, because there is nowhere to look except inward, and the loudness settles around you like a blanket in a machine shop. A gnat lands on your wrist. A beetle crosses your boot, purposeful as a forklift. A drop forms overhead, catches green light, grows, lets go, hits the moss by your knee with a soft tick lost in the roar. Another forms in its place.

The rules are simple and unposted:
Stay low. Breathe slow. Drink nothing but air.
The walls are alive and load-bearing.
What is made here is made by living things, at industrial volume, without pause, without progress.
It was flat when you came. It will be flat when mosses have sealed the creep-hole behind you and opened another somewhere else, same size, same cool, same roar.

Outside, the ancient plain goes on being flat under a normal sky, and the dome goes on being a small empty loud green engine-room with no builders, humming to itself, damp and cool and full to bursting and holding nothing at all.
