On the roof of the world, there is no world left. Only roof.

You feel it first in the lungs — the thin pull, the way breath comes a little short even though the air is warm. Warm, that is the first wrongness. At this height the air should bite. Below, a sea of cloud breaks and reforms a thousand meters down, white and indifferent. Above, the sky is a hard, rinsed blue. The sun does not filter here, it hammers. It bounces off whitewash and galvanized iron and wet leaves until your eyes water. And yet the heat holds. It lives in the brick, in the low thrum of engines that have not stopped in living memory, in the press of bodies — human, animal, plant — all breathing together in a high, walled warmth.

This is Cumbre, though no one calls it a city anymore. A town the size of a valley, a valley made entirely of town.

There is no ground here. Not anywhere. That is the rule you learn with your feet. Scrape under the moss in a courtyard and you find mortar. Pry up a courtyard flag and you find a ceiling. Drill down and you find another room, another cistern, another level of dwelling, going down and down into the cloud. The original mountain — basalt, granite, whatever it was — has been completely swallowed. Encasement, cladding, terrace upon terrace, until no natural substrate remains to touch. Everything is made: brick fired a century ago and gone soft at the edges, poured concrete gone pitted and honeycombed, iron beams bleeding rust that stains the walls in long orange tears, timber balconies blackened by sun and oil, tile, asphalt, copper pipe, cable.

And it is old in the way a riverbed is old. Nothing is collapsing, nothing is new. Corners have been worn round by hands and hips and baskets. Steps dip in the middle. Rust has found its equilibrium with paint; paint has surrendered to vine. The whole mass has settled into itself, weathered into balance. Maintenance is religion here, not progress. You do not build — you patch, you replaster, you rewire.

Because on every made surface, something is growing.

From a distance Cumbre looks green, impossibly green for a place with no soil. Then you get closer and see the trick: lushness without earth. Every flat roof is a field. Every wall is a hanging garden. Beans and squash sprawl over factory parapets. Banana leaves slap against smokestacks. Bougainvillea spills in magenta cataracts off stairwells seven stories tall. Moss packs the mortar joints like grout. Figs root in gutters, splitting concrete with slow, patient strength, and no one cuts them down — you route the gutter around them. Planters made from cut oil drums, bathtubs, engine blocks, line every ledge. The compost is human, animal, leaf-litter; soil is manufactured here, carried in buckets, treasured, never wasted. The air smells of hot vegetation, of tomato vine and wet lime and machine oil.

Water is why.

There is no river here, no lake, no spring to speak of. There is damp. Mornings come with cloud. Not rain, exactly — the high fog arrives just after dawn, thick and white, and it catches on everything. The city is built to catch it. Between buildings stretch great sails of woven net, gray with age, sagging with dew. They drip, all morning they drip, into tin gutters that tick and ping as they cool, into downspouts that feed cisterns buried under every courtyard. You hear it everywhere before you see it: *plip-plip-plip* on metal, on leaf.

By midday the cloud burns off and the light turns brutal. Then, if you are lucky, a short, furious shower builds over the eastern terraces — fifteen minutes of hammering rain that sends everyone running to pull awnings, to open cistern hatches, to stand in it with arms out. Children shriek. Chickens scatter under eaves. Then it passes, and steam rises off the hot tile, and the city exhales. Pockets of water remain: a courtyard tank green with algae, a factory cooling pool where women wash clothes, a sagging roof that holds a permanent shallow pond where ducks paddle and dragonflies hunt. Damp lives in larger dryness here — always intermittent, always saved.

That damp plus heat plus leaf equals animals. Abundantly, shockingly many.

Pigeons, first and always. Flocks that darken the bright sky when the shift whistle blows at the pressing plant, rising in a single decision. They nest in engine housings for warmth, in the hollow letters of old factory signs. Swifts cut the air above the terraces, screaming. Small bright finches work the bean rows. Lizards — fat, orange-throated — do push-ups on every sunlit wall. At night, geckos click in the thousands.

And larger things. Cats, semi-feral and lordly, asleep on warm transformers. Troops of small, soot-fingered monkeys that learned generations ago to pry open lunchboxes and turn stopcocks; they travel the power lines like highways, chattering. Goats on the upper scrub-roofs, bells clanking, trimming the grass the gardeners miss. Pigs in pen-yards behind the dye works, fed on market waste. Bees everywhere, kept in box hives stacked on stair landings, drunk on bougainvillea. Cockroaches and beetles in the damp under-levels, and the spiders and toads that eat them, and the chickens that eat everything.

People are just another dense layer. The streets — really alleys, really staircases, really rooftop causeways — are near-touching. Laundry lines cross above your head, dripping. Market stalls press against machine shops press against shrines. You cannot stretch your arms without touching tended life: a pot of chilies here, a child there, a goat, a humming electrical cabinet fenced with marigolds.

The sound is loud, but it never spikes. It is a held chord.

Under everything, the industrial bass: the low, patient idle of big engines, generators, press-drives, water-pumps, cable-car winches. A 60-cycle hum from the substations that you feel in your teeth. The slow clank of the ore-buckets on the long incline that still climbs from the cloud-base to the summit works, carrying up compost and scrap and carrying down nothing much — it runs because it has always run. Mid-tones: hammering from the tinsmiths' terrace, the rattle of looms in the textile sheds, the hiss of steam vents, the whine of bicycle chains, the bells. High tones: swifts, vendors calling, children, goats, water dripping off a thousand eaves in the morning fog, wind worrying the fog-nets so they thrum like sails.

Nothing hurries. That is the strangest thing for a place so loud and crowded. The cable cars move at walking pace. The factory line moves at the pace of hands sorting beans. The vines move slower still, prying a millimeter a year into the concrete. Change happens across long durations here. A roof garden becomes, after forty years, a roof thicket. A rust stain becomes a map everyone knows. The light shifts, the fog comes, the shower passes, the cistern level falls a finger's width a day until the next cloud.

The rules, unspoken, hold it all together:

1. Catch water before it falls.
2. Grow on what is made. There is nothing else to grow on.
3. Keep the heat. Close doors, bank engines, compost hot.
4. Do not build new. Tend what holds.

At the summit — which is not a peak but a broad, paved plaza of cracked tile, the highest made ground — you can stand in blazing sun with sweat on your neck and look out over a mile of stepped roofs falling away in green and rust and white, terraces and water tanks and net-sails and smokestacks with parrots on them, alleys plunging down into green shadow, cable lines sagging toward other spurs of the same endless structure that disappear around the shoulder of cloud. It extends past seeing. It is open and crowded at once. Thin air, bright light, warm stone underfoot that is not stone.

Below you, the winches groan. Somewhere a cistern lid slams. The fog-nets drip their slow arithmetic. A fig tree, rooted in nothing but a wall and fifty years of dust, holds its leaves up to the hammer-sun and does not wilt.

No mountain. Only the work that replaced it, weathered, humming, growing over itself.
