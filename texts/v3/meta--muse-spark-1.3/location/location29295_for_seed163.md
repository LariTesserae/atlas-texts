Up high, past where the road gives out into gravel and then into wet tire ruts, the valley opens and holds the sky.

It isn't a peak so much as a hanging shelf — a broad, tilted moor at maybe nine, ten thousand feet, walled on three sides by still-white ridges. The air is thin and brutally clear. Light comes down without filter and bounces back up again. Everything glints. Even on an overcast thought of a day, you squint here. The sun is white, not gold, and the cold makes it whiter.

The ground is young. That is the first strange, normal thing you learn with your boots. This whole shelf was under ice a hundred years ago, fifty years ago in the hollows. The glacier pulled back and left nothing finished. No soil to speak of. No true valleys. Just raw geology — pale granite slabs still scratched with striations, round-shouldered till, gravel fans that haven't decided where their channels are. The streams haven't learned how to erode yet. They don't cut down; they spread out, sheeting thin over rock, braiding and re-braiding every spring. Pre-formation. A landscape still wet from being made.

And because it is so wet and so bright and so cool, life has taken it all at once, low and total.

Not forest. There is almost nothing upright here. What covers the shelf from edge to edge, between boulders and over them, is a sponge. A living mat a foot thick, three feet thick in the dips — sphagnum moss electric green and rust-red, liverworts black-green as wet leather, starry saxifrage and cushion moss and dwarf willow pressed flat to the ground as if by the weight of the light. You step and water rises around your boot and the whole mat sighs and settles. You step again and hear the squelch sucked back down. Underneath, stone. On top, plants using plants as ground. Roots in moss, shoots in humus that is barely humus yet, flowers growing out of the corpses of last year's moss. In the hollows the pools are rimmed with cotton grass, thousands of white heads nodding. The vegetation doesn't fill the air. It fills the floor. It is the floor.

Water is everywhere but never deep, except where it leaves. The water table is an inch below the moss. Every footprint fills. Every slab weeps. The granite itself sweats — after rain, and it rains or mists most afternoons when the clouds catch on the rim, the rock faces run with a thousand temporary threads. The air smells of cold iron and peat and crushed green. Your breath shows even at noon. Your fingers go numb if you kneel too long to look at sundews, tiny and red, waiting in the wet.

Because the shelf ends.

On the eastern side the young land simply stops, cut off in a scarp that hasn't yet eroded into a proper cliff. And all that sheet-water, all that seep and braided melt, gathers for a few hundred yards and then throws itself off. Not one waterfall but a fringed curtain — twenty, thirty white plumes depending on the hour, dropping eight hundred feet into the lower valley. From where you stand among the moss you can see them all at once, across two miles of rim. You can see the spray columns rising back up into the light.

You hear them before you see anything. That is the rule here.

It is deafening. A low, full-body roar that lives in your sternum, compounded of falling water and wind and something else underneath. The wind comes up off the drop and over the sponge with nothing to break it — no trees, only a few scattered, stunted spruce flagging hard east, knee-high after decades — and it tears at your jacket. But beneath the wind and water there is a mechanical drone, steady as a held organ note. It took me a day to place it.

The built things are so sparse you miss them at first. That is also the rule: much space between presences.

A concrete intake box half-swallowed by moss, its steel grate humming with suction. A low weir, algae-slick, metering a braid into a buried penstock. A rusted cable pylon marching off toward the rim, then down. And down near the lip, three small flat-roofed buildings of weather-gray concrete with narrow, bright windows — a catchment station, a turbine hall, a store. No road to them now. No people most weeks. But they are alive. Lights blink green on the door. A low vent breathes warm air that condenses instantly. Inside, a turbine the size of a truck spins on meltwater, governed, automatic, feeding power down to the town you can't see in the haze below. An electric hum under the roar of the falls. The smell of ozone and hot oil for ten feet around the door, then gone, swallowed by peat and rain.

Everything else is empty in the vertical. One shepherd's hut with its roof caved and moss growing through the slats. One cairn. One weather mast ticking in the wind, its guy wires singing. A mile between each. Otherwise only the mat, the stone, the water, the light.

Animals are rare to the point of event. No herds. No flocks. The ground is too wet and too young for burrows, too exposed for cover. Once in a whole afternoon you might flush a ptarmigan, still mottled white-brown for summer, that clatters off and is gone. A water pipit works the edge of a pool, impossibly loud for a second between gusts. Midges rise in a brief glitter and are blown flat. In the pools themselves, nothing moves but tadpoles, slow as minutes. The ecology is plant and water and stone. The animals haven't arrived yet, evolutionarily speaking. They are still scouting.

Nothing here happens fast, except the falling. The moss grows a millimeter a year. The streams shift an inch a season. The slabs settle by fractions. Even the industrial things operate on a patient cycle — gates opening a centimeter to let a spring surge through, turbines spooling up over hours, reservoirs that are really just widened bogs filling and draining across weeks. The deafening is constant, but it is not urgent. It is the sound of a slow place with a loud edge.

Stand still long enough and your boots sink half an inch. Water wells up clear and brown as tea. The light gets brighter as the cloud thins. The curtain of falls throws up another rainbow and then another, doubled in the spray. The cotton grass bows all one way. The green lights on the turbine hall blink, indifferent. The whole shelf hums, weeps, glints — a high, cold, saturated sponge of living matter laid over unfinished stone, roaring softly, endlessly, off its own unfinished edge.
