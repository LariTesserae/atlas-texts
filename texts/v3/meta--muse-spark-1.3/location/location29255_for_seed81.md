Up high, where the air should be thin and cold, the heat finds you first.

You come over the rim and the whole valley opens below — a town-size sprawl fitted into a shallow bowl of dark rock, terraces and stacks and low flat roofs stepping down toward a center you can't quite see for steam. The elevation is in your lungs and ears, a faint pop and a shortness, the horizon too far and too sharp. But the air is hot. Not sun-hot. Ground-hot. It rises off the stone in visible sheets and makes the far side of the city waver, as if you were looking at it through water.

The light never gets stronger than this. A dim amber dusk that holds all day. The sun is a dull copper coin somewhere behind the lid of cloud that never lifts — cloud made half of weather and half of the place itself exhaling. Steam from the cooling towers, smoke from the pilot stacks, vapor off every hot puddle. It hangs low and filters everything. Edges go soft. Sodium work-lamps left on along the boulevards burn all the time, little orange halos in the gloom, and here and there the blue-white flicker of a transformer still holding charge. The furnace mouths down in the industrial terraces glow a deeper red. Without them, you would lose depth entirely.

There has never been anything green here, or not for a very long time. No trees, no grass, no moss even in the wettest cracks. The rock is basalt and cinder, weathered round and settled, old lava flows worn down to smooth-backed ridges. What grows instead is mineral. On every shaded wall, salt and sulfur bloom into white and yellow cauliflowers, fragile as frost. In the gutters a slick, rust-colored biofilm spreads — not plant, not quite fungus, just a living varnish that can take the heat and the metals in the water. It peels in sheets when it dries and reforms overnight.

This is a wet place, impossibly, for how hot it is. Water is near the surface everywhere. It weeps straight out of the cut rock along the upper roads, darkening the concrete in long vertical tongues. It condenses out of the air onto every cool pipe and cable and then rains back down in a constant, localized drizzle — you hear it before you see it, ticking off corrugated awnings, plinking in cisterns. The city was built to move it. Canals, culverts, aqueducts on stubby legs, half-pipes bolted to the sides of buildings, cisterns like grain silos. The lower streets run with an inch of hot, clear runoff that never seems to drain or evaporate entirely; it just circulates. Puddles throw back the orange lamps and the red furnace glow and shiver when a vent sighs underneath.

And it is empty. That is the strangest weight of it.

This is unmistakably urban — organized, layered, dense with intent. A grid up top for housing, blocks of three- and four-story tenements with laundry lines still strung, shutters half-open. Then the middle ring of workshops and depots, roller doors rusted partway up. Then down in the heat of the bowl, the industrial core: factories with sawtooth roofs, engine houses, pump stations, lattice towers of cable, smokestacks banded in faded white and red. Stairs and catwalks and freight rails stitch it all together. It was built for thousands of movements a day.

There is no one to make them.

Doors stand open onto rooms with a chair, a kettle, a bed made. A tram sits mid-street, its pantograph still up, its windows fogged from the inside. Vast space between any two living presences. You can walk a whole boulevard and be the only footfall, your steps slapping back off the wet walls too loudly. Inside the buildings it is even hollower — the kind of hollow that has its own pressure in the ears.

The machines did not all stop. That is the rule here: the city idles, it does not die.

Every few minutes something cycles. A pump house shudders awake, groans water uphill through a fat insulated main, then cuts out with a metallic *tink-tink-tink* as it cools. Louvers on a cooling tower breathe open and shut. A vent on a rooftop lifts and releases a white jet with a soft *whoosh* that condenses instantly and falls as hot rain on the street below. Transformers hum, low enough to feel in your teeth. Otherwise, it is mostly quiet. Mostly drip and tick and your own breathing, thin and fast. Once in a long while a sheet of corrugated siding, expanded by heat, pops free somewhere out of sight with a report like a gunshot, and the echo takes its time crossing the bowl.

Rare things live in the margins. Pale moths, almost translucent, that feed on the sulfur bloom and gather in clouds around the work-lamps, casting huge, slow shadows. A heron-like bird, featherless-looking and gray, that stands statue-still in the outflow marsh where the city's water finally spreads and cools at the lowest terrace — a shallow, steaming flat of black mud and standing water, rimmed with white crust. You will see one, maybe two in a day. Heat-ticks click in the expansion joints. Nothing calls. Nothing sings.

The weirdness is not in any one thing but in how the place holds together despite its contradictions. Water here doesn't quite obey. In the stillest heat of the afternoon, when the vents are all closed, droplets will hang in the air at chest height over the lower plazas — thousands of them, suspended, catching the lamp light — neither rising nor falling for a full minute before settling all at once as if decided. Your shadow is doubled even where there is only one lamp. The hot runoff in the canals runs cold for a few feet where it passes under certain bridges, cold enough to steam in reverse, and then goes hot again. The old-timers, whoever they were, painted marks on those bridges: white hands, warnings. Don't drink from the ground pools. Don't sit on metal after the siren. Be under cover at venting hour.

If you stay until the dim dims further toward night, the city becomes almost legible. The pilot flames — hundreds of them left burning on walls and stacks to keep the gas from pooling — come out as a constellation, outlining streets and stairways and the rims of the great cisterns. The steam glows faintly from below. The water keeps weeping, condensing, running, steaming. The stone sweats. The iron sweats. You sweat instantly and it does not dry.

It is a place built to be full and loud and green with work, and instead it is high and hot and wet and empty, running itself through its cycles with no one left to tend it but the water and the heat.
