It is down.

Not deep in the mythic way, not cavern-system deep. Just below. A bite taken out of the ground and not yet healed over. You find the rim by the heat coming up off it before you see anything — a warm breath rising in the night, smelling of iron and wet green.

From the lip, it is small enough to hold in one look, once your eyes stop trying. Maybe forty paces across. An oval. A blasted sump, a test pit, a collapsed service shaft — the walls are too straight in places to be natural. Poured concrete on one side, still showing the grain of its formwork. Raw rock on the other, fresh and sharp. This place is young. The breaks haven't softened. The talus cones at the foot of the walls are still angular, black basalt and cinder with edges that will cut a boot. No river has ever run through here to round them. Nothing has had time.

The floor is ten, twelve meters down. A ladder goes down, bolted to the concrete — rungs sweating, orange with rust that hasn't yet flaked. A stair of cinderblock that ends halfway, where the wall sheared. You have to climb the last part hand-over-hand.

Down there the air changes. Hot. Not desert hot, not sun hot. Enclosed hot. 2 a.m. boiler-room hot that gets into your lungs and stays. Your shirt sticks in a minute. Your breath comes back to you.

There is almost no light to see by. That is the first rule. The sky is a narrow, cooler lid far above, a bruised slot with no stars because of the steam. The work-lights strung on the catwalk died years ago — their caged bulbs are blind with dust and moth husks — no, not moths. There are no animals here. Just the husks, left from before. What light remains is industrial and involuntary: a pilot flame behind a cracked grate, blue then guttering orange. A seam in a boiler where the heat leaks out, a dull cherry line. A single red maintenance bulb still burning over a doorway that goes nowhere, fed by something that hasn't yet given up. It is enough, after ten minutes, to see shapes. After twenty, to see color.

Water does not flow here. It hangs. That is the second rule.

There is no stream, no pool that lasts. The damp comes from below and from the air itself. Waste heat from the machines meets the cooler rock of the walls and sweats. Every pipe, every cable, every cold edge beads and drips. *Plink.* Long silences. *Plink.* The concrete weeps in dark streaks. In the low spots between slabs, shallow pans of black water appear and vanish over days — you can see their old shorelines as pale mineral rings, overlapping like tree rings. If you put your hand to the wall, it comes away wet, smelling faintly of oil and sulfur.

And because it is hot and wet and sheltered, it is lush.

Not lush like a jungle. Lush like isolated, unhurried exuberance. Sparse and lush at once. Between expanses of bare, hot cinder and empty concrete, there are islands of green so dense they look black in the red dark.

Ferns, mostly. Huge, pale things. Fronds three meters long, translucent at the edges where they have never needed sunscreen. They grow not toward light — there is almost none to grow toward — but toward heat, toward damp. They lean to the pipes. Their roots have found the lagging around the steam lines and pried it open, white rootlets combing the condensation. In the clefts, mosses make cushions thick as mattresses, acid green, lime green, a white-green that seems to make its own faint phosphor. Pitcher plants hang from the catwalk, their trumpets full of rain that is not rain, their lids perfect and unchewed.

That is the strangeness you feel before you name it: everything is whole. No bite marks. No webs. No droppings, no tracks, no buzz, no scuttle. No animal life at all. Spores fall straight down and germinate where they land. Dead fronds do not get eaten; they collapse into sodden felt and slowly melt back. The air has no wingbeats in it. Even your own steps sound wrong here, too loud, too animal.

The machines are still here, and they are still, barely, working. That is why the place hasn't cooled and died.

Along the north wall, on a raised plinth to keep it out of the intermittent flooding, sits the plant: three horizontal boilers the size of culverts, jacketed in riveted steel, plumbed into the rock with pipes that disappear downward. One is cold and open, its firebox door hanging, full of fern croziers. One ticks as it cools, has ticked for years probably. One is alive. You can hear it if you hold your breath — not a roar, a low, patient churn, a pump lifting something hot from deeper still and letting it sigh through a valve every few minutes. *Hssss.* A white feather of steam that is gone before it reaches the rim. The pressure gauge needle trembles just above zero. A belt drive turns a shaft, slowly, so slowly you think at first it is still, and the shaft turns a fan that no longer moves air so much as stirs it.

Scattered around it, the faint marks of people who left in a hurry and then never came back to tidy: a tool shed with its door ajar, inside a bench, a row of jars clouded with condensation, a logbook swollen shut. A single chair facing the boilers, as if someone liked to watch. A catwalk that goes two-thirds around and stops at a fall of rock. Extension cords thick as snakes, coiled and abandoned. A hard hat, upside down, become a planter for moss. No graffiti. No camp. Just enough to say: we were trying to keep this warm.

The processes here are slow. That is the third rule, and it governs the others.

Rust creeps a millimeter a month. Condensation gathers, falls, evaporates, gathers again. The ferns unroll a centimeter a week. The mineral rings widen. The heat never spikes, never drops; it just persists, a weak gradient from the living boiler outward to the cold walls, and life has arranged itself along it in bands — closest, the scald-mosses that can take near-boiling seep; then the ferns; then, out in the dark corners where it is merely warm, pale shelf fungi like stacked ears, listening.

If you stand still long enough your own sweat begins to join the cycle, beading and falling from your elbows to feed the moss. Your breathing fogs and clears. The red bulb hums — a sound at the very edge of hearing — and then, for a long time, nothing. A drip from the catwalk. The tick of contracting steel. The almost inaudible sigh of the valve.

Above you, the rim is a hard line. Beyond it, the dry world continues, cool and starved and noisy with animals. Here, below, the world has made a small, hot, dark exception for itself: a room-sized greenhouse with no sun and no keepers, run by one tired engine, watered by its own breath, growing extravagantly in the dark with no mouths to feed.
