You are under, and you stay under. There is no shore in this place.

The light tells you first how deep — no, how high — you are. It is ordinary daylight, sun-white gone green-gold through water, strong enough to read by, to see your hands go pale and then dark. It comes from impossibly far above, a rippling white ceiling that is the surface of the sea. You are not in the deep sea. You are on the heights.

Below you, the continent rises.

This is a high country drowned. A plateau lifted kilometers above the abyssal plain, its edge falling away on all sides into blue-black that never ends. You stand — you float — over its shoulder at maybe two hundred meters depth, and beneath you the land itself is not land. The rock is long gone, buried, forgotten. What holds you up is living mass.

The substrate is plant.

It is a mat, a felt, a peat of interwoven holdfasts and fallen stipes and root-rhizomes thick as your thigh, many fathoms deep, springy and cold to the touch. It has been here so long it has taken the shape of hills and valleys, weathered into settled forms. It does not crumble. It does not grow higher. Gulleys run through it, rounded as old riverbeds, where the current has polished the living tissue smooth as leather. If you tear a piece — you won't, it's tough as belting — underneath is only more plant, black-green and dense. There is no stone to find. No sand. The world has replaced mineral with vegetable and then stopped.

And out of that mat rise the stalks.

Not a forest as you know crowding and underbrush. This is a sparse forest, a cathedral forest. Each trunk — kelp is too small a word, but kelp is the closest — is thirty, fifty, a hundred meters around at the base, rising straight up for three, four hundred meters toward the light. They are spaced wide, a hundred meters, two hundred meters between presences, so that between any two giants there is nothing but cold green water and distance. You can see for a long way and see almost nothing, and then another pillar crosses your vision, dark, barnacled with smaller algae, with mussels and anemones clinging in living bark.

Look up their length and they vanish into haze. Look along them and you understand the scale: this goes on past the horizon. Rank after rank after rank. A continent of pillars holding up a sea. The water between them has its own weather. Marine snow falls steadily, sideways in storms — slow white blizzards that gather and disperse and gather again over days, internal waves the size of hills rolling through without ever breaking the surface above. You can feel pressure fronts pass like cold breath.

It is cool here. Not freezing, not tropical. The kind of cool that gets into your jaw and stays. Seven, eight degrees. The water tastes of iron and sap and salt. Your exhalations, if you have them, go up in silver beads that rise and rise and never seem to arrive.

There is nothing built here. That needs saying because your ears will argue otherwise.

It is loud. Very loud. From the moment you drop below the thermocline you hear it in your sternum: a low industrial roar, a working sound. Creaking on a scale that makes you think of harbor chains, of dock gates. Deep rhythmic whoomps, hisses, suctions, the knock of wood on wood. At first you look for engines. For mills. For wreckage.

There is none. No cut stone, no rust, no rope, no ruin. Untouched.

The sound is the forest working, and it works like industry because it *is* industry, without ever having been made.

Each giant stalk is a pump. Its bladders — gas sacs the size of houses clustered at intervals up the stipe — inflate and deflate with a slow hydraulic sigh, drawing cold nutrient water up from the mat and venting it higher. You can see the pulse travel: a tightening, a release. *Huuush-thump. Huuush-thump.* The fronds, broad as sails, thirty meters long, beat and feather the current into flumes, into millraces between the pillars. Water is forced through natural constrictions where two holdfast hills lean together, and there it accelerates, turning eddies that spin perpetually, grinding fine plant-detritus like grist, sorting, conveying.

Elsewhere the stalk joints knock. When the surge comes — and the surge always comes, same strength, same interval — the upper trunks lean and strike their neighbors with a deep wooden boom that carries for kilometers: *tock... tock... tock...* like a line of pistons out of phase.

And the light flickers with electricity. Not metaphor. The water here carries a faint charge. Schools of small silver fish discharge as they turn, and the great fronds store and release. On overcast days — surface days, far above — you will see St. Elmo's fire crawl blue along a bladder seam and vanish with a crackle you feel in your teeth. No wires. No dynamos. Just tissue that learned to be a battery because in a place this vast, chemistry becomes power.

The fauna live inside that machine, abundant, unafraid.

Ribbons of fish, thousands strong, pour between the pillars in perfect conveyor streams, mouths open, filtering the ground meal the eddies make. Spider crabs the size of tables pick along the mat, slow and deliberate. Nudibranchs bright as warning flags graze the bark. Above, shadows pass — great filterers, whale-like, but flattened, mottled green, moving with impossible slowness between the trunks, their calls adding bass to the roar: long moans that vibrate the bladders and come back altered.

Nothing hunts in a frenzy. Everything feeds, excretes, is filtered, feeds again. A closed belt. The whales eat the fish-fall, the crabs eat the whale-fall, the mat eats the crab-fall, the stalks lift it back up. You will see a crab tear a frond and the sap well out milky, and within minutes small fish arrive to clean the wound, and the wound closes. Not healed — restored.

Because this is the rule that holds all the contradictions together:

Nothing changes here.

Oh, it moves. God, how it moves. Water rushes, bladders pump, fish school, snow falls, wood knocks, current roars like a factory floor at full shift. But measure it and it is the same. The current at this gap is always 2 knots. The surge always leans the same stalk six degrees. The temperature never warms, never cools. The pillars do not grow taller, do not rot, do not topple. The mat does not thicken. The fish you saw loop around that far pillar yesterday — last year — a thousand years ago — will loop around it again tomorrow in exactly the same number, same spacing.

It is erosion brought to equilibrium and left there. Long-existed, weathered into its final shape, and then time stopped pushing. Not frozen like ice. Frozen like a held breath that never needs to exhale. If you release a bubble and watch, it will rise, wobble, catch the light, join the stream of other bubbles — and if you wait long enough, another bubble will take exactly its path again. And again.

That is the strangeness, beyond the size and the sound. You expect wilderness to be messy, to succeed and fail. This wilderness has the regularity of scaled production with no product, no waste, no progress. An engine that runs perfectly because it is not trying to go anywhere.

Float in the middle distance between two giants, where the water is empty and green and loud, and you can feel it: the cold pressing in, the daylight filtering down, the slow *huush-thump* of the forest breathing around you — inhale, exhale, inhale, exhale — always moving, never different.

No one built it. Nothing will unbuild it. It just works, forever, high above the dark, drowned in light.
