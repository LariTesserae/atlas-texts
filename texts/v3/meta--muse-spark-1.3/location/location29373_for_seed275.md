You go down to get there.

Not down like a cave. Down like a town that missed the surface. The plateau goes on flat and pale for miles — cracked clay and wind-comb — and then it simply stops. A rim, raw as a broken tooth. And below you, a valley that shouldn't be there. Not carved by any river, because there has never been a river here. Cut. Blasted. Split open last year, geologically speaking. The walls are still sharp.

That's the first thing you notice: nothing is smoothed. The terraces stepping down are too straight, too fresh. The rock faces show drill spirals and blast flowers, unsoftened by rain because there is no rain. Talus hasn't had time to pile. Dust still hangs in the air where the last cut fell. It is a place just-coming-into-being, still deciding what shape it will be.

And then, at the bottom of all that raw mineral — green. Not a patch. A substrate.

The floor of the sunken basin, two or three kilometers across, is not floor at all. It is a living thing.

A dry forest, if you can call it a forest when there is no water in it at all. A single interwoven mat of root and rhizome and bark-plate, thick as houses in places, that has taken the flat of the pit and made it into itself. Everything you walk on gives slightly and creaks. The ground is cork and fiber and lacquered stem. Where the miners scraped too deep you can see the cross-section: pale woody flesh veined with black, utterly dry, layered like phyllo dough, still growing around the foundations they poured into it.

Cool air settles down here and stays. Your breath shows in the morning. The light is ordinary — that is the strange part. You look up and there is just a long slot of white sky between the rims, and the sun comes straight down at noon like a lamp lowered into a box. True colors. You can see the rust on a bolt, the yellow edge of a leaf. No gloom. The pit is deep but not dark. The light does not have far to fall.

The town is in it. Not on it — in it.

They didn't clear the plant mass. They hollowed it. The stems here grow thicker than grain silos, fluted and hollow, twenty meters tall before they branch, their skins hard as kiln-dried timber. Someone long ago realized you could cut a door in one and it would heal around the frame. You could run a flue up through the pith and it would seal around the pipe. So the streets are not rows of houses so much as a scattered manufacturing grove.

A boiler-house grown inside a seed-pod the size of a chapel, its iron belly glowing through slits cut in the husk. A machine shop strung between three trunks on cables and timber decks, lathes humming under a canopy of papery leaves. Chimneys — squat, brick, industrially serious — poking up through branch-forks, letting out a thin, dry smoke that the leaves seem to lean toward. Wires sag from crown to crown. Catwalks of grating and rope. Water tanks, repurposed, because there is no water to put in them — now they hold oil, and sap-milk, and compressed air.

It is urban, undeniably. Layered construction, signage, shift whistles, freight elevators that groan down the outside of stalks. But it is sparse. That's the second thing that unmakes your expectations. A city should crowd. This one doesn't. Between one factory-stem and the next there might be a hundred meters of nothing but resonant bark-ground and wind-blown husk, and a single path worn pale across it. Much space between presences. You can stand in the middle of the production district at midday and feel alone, hearing only your own boots on cork.

Because the plant is everywhere, density stops meaning what you think. The living mass fills the basin wall to wall, but it is mostly empty architecture — vast dry chambers, curled dead leaves big as sails, hollow runners you can crawl through for an hour. The built part, the peopled part, is scattered thin through it like knots in wood.

And there is no water. Learn this quickly or you will keep looking for it and go mad.

No creek, no well, no damp in the soil. Your lips crack within an hour. Metal doesn't rust here so much as dust over. The air smells of resin and hot iron and stone dust — never of wet rot, never of mud. The plants do not want water. They are insulted by the idea.

Their leaves are not green in the wet way. They are sage-grey and straw and a dusty olive, thin as tin, stiff, edged with salt-crystals that glitter. When wind moves through the open basin they do not flutter; they clatter. A whole hillside of them sounds like distant cutlery. They drink light and mineral dust and what the factories exhale. Their roots etch the raw rock walls, splitting fresh blasts with hair-fine tendrils that secrete acid strong enough to taste metal in the air. Their sap is not watery — it is slow, milky, flammable if you get it hot enough. The mechanics use it to cut oil. The old-timers chew the root-shavings for the moisture that isn't moisture, a chemical coolness that fools the mouth.

This is the rule: nothing here cycles water. Everything cycles dryness.

And yet it is alive, obscenely alive. Abundant in a quiet way.

The fauna don't drink either. The hoppers — knee-high, six-legged, the color of bark — shear leaf-edges with scissor mouths and store the shavings in their cheek pouches where they ferment into heat. The veil-moths, big as your hand, come at shift-change to crowd around the warm exhaust vents, laying eggs in the soot. The soot-worms eat it. The wirebirds eat the soot-worms. Up in the high branches, things that look like desiccated monkeys but aren't — thin, grey, huge-eyed against the bright sky — pry open seed-pods with little crowbars they steal from workshops, scattering the downy seeds that the wind carries up and out over the rim to nowhere.

Predator and prey, but muted. A hunter here doesn't splash or crash. It is all papery rustle and pounce. You will see a dust-fox flatten itself against a root-rib, ears turning like dishes, and then vanish into a hollow runner after a squeaking nest of chewers. The machines, in their way, are part of it. The stamping presses thump on a slow rhythm, and the seed-pods have learned to pop open on that frequency. The factories pollinate the forest with vibration and smoke. The forest houses the factories and muffles them.

That is why it is so quiet for an industrial town.

You expect clangor. You get absorption. The living wood drinks sound. The thick cork floor, the husked walls, the acres of dry leaf — they take the edge off every engine. You hear a lathe three stems over as if through a pillow. A freight chain rattles and stops. Mostly what carries is small and close: the creak of a living beam adjusting its load, the tick-tick of cooling iron, the skitter of claws on bark, the high thin whine of moth wings in a vent shaft, someone shouting a name two clearings away and the plant swallowing half the vowels.

Activity, yes. Constant motion. Growth against cutting. The forest extends a new runner overnight across a walkway and the day crew saws it back by noon, and both sides seem content with the arrangement. A wall of the pit flakes off in a dry slide — no mud, just slabs and dust — and within a week white rootlets are already stitching across the scar, holding it. Children run messages along the root-paths. A crane swings a boiler plate through the air between two crowns. A herd of hoppers moves through like a tide, stripping a patch bare to silver stem, and behind them the leaf-buds are already unrolling, dry as paper fresh from a press.

It feels embryonic. Unfinished on purpose. The rock is still raw, the town still sparse, the organism still testing how big it can get without water. If you climb back up to the rim at dusk and look down, you see it best: the cool blue shadow filling the cut first, the scattered warm windows lighting on one by one far apart in the green-grey mass like sparks in tinder that refuses to burn, the smoke rising straight up in the windless cold, the vast quiet working.

Below the world, a dry jungle that learned to be a city. Above it, nothing but plateau and sky.
