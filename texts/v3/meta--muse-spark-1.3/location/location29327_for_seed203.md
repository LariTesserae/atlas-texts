You go down to get to it. Not up.

The light should have died a hundred meters below the lip. It doesn't. That is the first wrongness you have to unlearn.

The sky up there is white, not blue — a flat, burning white that hurts to look at. The rift is so deep the walls should be in twilight. Instead the light comes down all the way, bounced and bounced and bounced again until the whole depth is full of it. Bright cold light, like sunlight through clean ice. On the snowfields above, noon is blinding. Down here, three kilometers below the surface, noon is also blinding. Your shadow is short and hard at your feet on nothing.

Because there are no feet, really. There is no floor.

The world is a wound. A fresh one. Somewhere under the icecap the ground split — not long ago, geologically speaking. Yesterday, in planet-time. The edges are still sharp. You can see it in the stone where the green hasn't covered it yet: black basalt teeth, glassy, unweathered, edges you could cut your hand on. No river has had time to soften them, no wind. The split runs beyond seeing in both directions. People who have walked the upper rim for a season say it keeps going, that it has its own weather inside, clouds that are born in the rift and die in the rift and never come out. Continental in scale. You can stand on one rim on a clear morning and not see the other side, only white haze and light.

And everything inside is alive.

Not alive *with* plants. Alive *as* plants.

From the lip down as far as you can follow with rope and eye, the walls are not rock with moss on them. The moss *is* the wall. Meters thick. Fathoms thick in places. A living substrate of frost-moss, lichen-wool, vine-cable, leaf-sail. It holds itself up. It grows on itself, dead layers underneath still clinging while new layers root in the corpses. In the upper thousand meters it is low and tight to the stone because of the wind shear — a pelt of silver-green, crunchy with frost, beaded with ice. Lower, where the air stills and warms a fraction — still cold enough that your breath smokes, cold enough that your fingers go numb if you take your mittens off to pick — it lets go.

That is where the forests hang.

They do not grow up toward the light. That is the second wrongness. Up is too dry, too scoured. The water is down here, in the air itself. So they grow out and down and *in*, away from the sun, into the void. Great braided cables of stem as thick as your torso shoot straight out from the wall for fifty, a hundred meters, then droop under their own weight, sprouting secondary branches, tertiary nets, leaves the size of tents, translucent, veined with white. Whole hanging meadows. Terraces of green suspended over nothing. From across the abyss they look solid, like you could walk on them. Up close you see light shining through gaps, through layers, and below them only more light and more falling green and then white mist.

Because the middle is empty.

This is the thing the numbers can't tell you until you're in it: for all that mass of living tissue, the rift is mostly air. A vast hollow. Almost nothing per volume. You could drop a stone from a hanging terrace and watch it fall for a minute before it disappears into the mist, hitting nothing. You could shout — though no one does — and the moss would eat the sound before it crossed a tenth of the distance. The presences are far apart. A grove here. A curtain-forest a kilometer down and half a kilometer over. Between them: bright, cold, damp emptiness.

Damp, not wet. There is no river down here, no lake. The old ice above sealed most melt away. What water there is comes in pockets and moments. Condensation beads on every leaf-edge in the morning and evaporates by midday. Seeps bleed black from cracks in the young basalt and freeze into ribbons by night. Sometimes a warm pocket rises from below — no one knows what's at the bottom, if there is a bottom — hits the cold wall and becomes rain. A sudden, local, vertical rain, a gray veil a few hundred meters wide, falling sideways, freezing as it falls, ticking faintly on leaves. And then it's gone. The leaves cup the drops, the mosses suck them up. Temporary waterfalls form after such rains and stop mid-air as icicles, glinting like chandeliers off a vine, until the next warm breath melts them and they go on falling.

It is young, this place. Everything is sharp and unfinished and moving too fast.

You expect a cold place to be slow. This one is turbulent. Hyperactive. You can *watch* it. Sit on a cable-root for an hour, miserably cold despite your furs, and you will see a tendril near your hand uncurl, lengthen, find a purchase, thicken. Spore-storms boil off the curtains without warning — vast, silent, glittering clouds that rise and obscure the far wall, then settle. And collapse is as fast as growth. The substrate gets too heavy, too wet. A whole acre of hanging meadow will sigh — you feel it in your knees more than hear it — and tear loose. It goes down slowly at first, turning as it falls, leaves still catching light, and then faster, into the white below. It does not crash. There is nothing to crash on that you can see. It just goes into the mist and is gone. Within a day, pale new shoots are already combing over the scar, bright green against dark rot.

And it is quiet. That is the strangest mercy of it.

With that much creation and destruction there should be roaring. There isn't. The moss eats noise. The distances eat noise. The cold air holds still. What you hear, when you stop breathing hard from the climb, is close-up and small: the tick of a melt-drop landing on a leaf below you. The creak of a vine tightening in frost. The soft papery rustle as two sail-leaves touch. Your own blood in your ears. Far away, very occasionally, a deep, dull *whump* as something large detaches, softened to a sigh by three kilometers of air. Mostly quiet, so quiet you start to hear the plants drinking.

Almost nothing moves in it that isn't plant.

Rare fauna. That's the word the old pickers use: *rare*, said like a blessing. Once a week you might see one of the blanket-moths — white, silent, wingspan wider than you are tall — riding the updraft along the wall, trailing frost from its wing-hairs, looking for spore-clouds to graze. They never flap if they can help it. They just hang there, drifting. If you are very lucky and very still, you might see a wall-goat, shaggy and gray-green with lichen growing in its fur until you can't tell animal from plant, picking its impossible way across a vertical bog, dislodging little avalanches of green that fall and fall. There are mites in the moss, and black beetles under the leaves that the pickers eat. That's about it. No flocks. No herds. Long, bright, empty hours where the only animal is you, breathing smoke into cold light.

People are here, but thinly, like the animals. Scattered.

You find their marks before you find them: a cairn of sharp black stones stacked on a moss hummock to mark a safe anchor. A cut vine, cleanly sliced with a stone edge, weeping sap that has frozen into amber. A rope left hanging — not manufactured rope, but twisted living fiber, harvested green, chewed soft, braided while still pliable, left to freeze stiff. It will hold a person's weight for a season before the wall reclaims it.

The camps cling like burrs. A half-dozen leaf-pods, each woven from living sail-leaves pinched together at the edges so they keep growing into a watertight tent, lashed to a cable-root. A platform of dead stem laid crosswise over a dip in the moss. A fire-pit lined with basalt chips where they burn dried vine-pith and rendered fat — the smoke rises straight up in the still air for a long, long way, a thin gray thread you can see from a kilometer away. That's how you find them, those threads.

Their tools are simple because anything complicated dies here. Chipped stone blades — basalt holds an edge and doesn't shatter in the cold the way scavenged metal does. Bone hooks for climbing. Hand-looms for fiber. Wooden combs. They are pickers and pruners by necessity: they harvest fiber, sap-sugar, beetles, the cup-water from giant leaves after rain. They move constantly because the wall under them moves constantly. No one builds to last. You build to be abandoned when the meadow tears. You leave the cairn for the next band.

Rules of the Hollow, as they will tell you if you share fire:

Nothing rots. It's too cold and too bright. Things are either living or frozen. If you leave food out it freeze-dries. If you drop it in the moss it gets incorporated.

Don't trust solidity. Step only where you have probed with a pole. Green lies about depth.

Don't shout. Not because something will hear you, but because the quiet is what holds the hanging forests together. At least, that's what they say.

And: don't go down to find the bottom. The mist below is not cloud, they say. It's the breath of the place, warm and damp, where the next rain is being made. Ropes are not long enough. Moths go down and do not come up. The bottom, if there is one, is still being grown.

At dusk — though dusk up on the surface is only a dimming of white to pearl — the light in the rift turns blue and horizontal. The far wall, which you could barely see at noon for glare, suddenly stands out in impossible detail: every hanging terrace, every tear-scar, every thread of smoke from every scattered camp, suspended in vast, empty brightness. Frost begins to form on the sail-leaves with an audible faint crackling, like tiny glass breaking. Condensation pearls along every edge and catches the last light.

You sit in your leaf-pod, cold to the bone despite the fire, listening to drip, creak, breath. Above you, kilometers of living wall rising to a slit of white sky. Below you, kilometers of living wall falling into white mist. Around you, nothing but bright cold air and green, growing and falling in silence.

Vast. Empty. Totally alive.
