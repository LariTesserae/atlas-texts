At first you think it's flat because it's dark.

Then you stay, and your eyes adjust, and you realize it's flat because it's finished.

This is low country in the oldest sense — not just near sea level, but near the end of shape. A basin the size of a small county, planed down by so much time that even hills have forgotten how to be hills. The rock underneath is black and purple and iron-red, worn soft as river eggs, polished to a dull sheen. There is almost no relief. A rise of three inches is a landmark here. A shallow pan two feet deep could hold a lake a mile wide.

And does. Water is everywhere, but never deep. It doesn't run in rivers — there's not enough slope left for rivers. It sits in skins. It seeps. After eons the stone sweats. Every low boulder, every slab weeps from its underside, black water beading and crawling and dripping without rain. The air itself is cold-saturated, too cold to hold what it holds, so it is always condensing out on everything: on stone, on breath, on fur and shell and iron hide. Your eyelashes ice in minutes. The ground underfoot is never dry — a quarter inch of supercooled melt, slush, black glass-ice with water moving atop it in slow, directionless sheets that shiver when the wind touches them.

No plants. Nothing green has ever taken here, or nothing green was allowed to stay. No grass, no moss, no brave lichen crust. Only mineral color: graphite shine, rust-bloom orange where iron water oxidizes, white salt beards where seeps evaporate in the cold wind, oily blue-purple bacterial films that are not plants and do not photosynthesize so much as stain. The world is stone and water and cold light.

The light is the color of late dusk held for hours. No sun you can point to, just a low, smeared brightness behind cloud — a pewter sea of stratus that never breaks, never lifts. Visibility goes a long way sideways because there is nothing to stop it, a mile, two miles, out to where fog and breath-smoke swallow distance. You can see far, and far is more of the same.

And it is full. Impossibly full.

Packed is not the right word until you are in it. From a distance you think the basin floor is cobbled, pebbled, textured. Then the texture moves. Then the texture breathes.

There is no room between presences.

Shoulder to shoulder, flank to flank, back to back, for acres and acres and acres, out past where you can hear individual voices into a single massed hum — the herd. The flock. The pack. The colony. The fauna, abundant beyond counting, have occupied every square foot of this wet flat. Low barrel-bodied grazers the size of oil drums, on six short piston legs. Taller waders on stilts that pick their way with hydraulic delicacy. Things like vast slugs armored in boiler plate that drag themselves through the slush leaving polished wakes. Things like penguins made of soot and copper wire that huddle in shivering thousands and click.

They touch. They have to touch. There is nowhere to stand without standing on someone, leaning on someone, being breathed on by someone. And yet no one fights for space. Space has been abandoned as a concept.

Listen — because you hear them long before you see detail — it is loud here. Not forest-loud. Works-loud.

Clank and hiss and low bellowing. The wheeze of bellows-lungs. The rhythmic chuff-chuff-chuff of a thousand walking engines. Metal on stone squeal. Water sloshing under ten thousand feet at once. High pressure whistles that start in one part of the plain and are picked up half a mile away and carried onward. Under it all a deep, felt-more-than-heard thump, as if the basin itself were a drumhead and something huge were walking just below.

Because none of this was built, and all of it is industrial.

That is the thing that takes longest to understand. There are no buildings. No walls, no fences, no roads, no ruins, no scrap, no wires strung by hands. This place is untouched in the human sense. Unmade.

The industry is in the animals.

Open one — not that you would, but if a carcass splits in the cold you can see — and there are no soft guts. There are chambers, fireboxes. Stomachs that smelt. The grazers nose down to the rust seeps and the iron water and lick the stone clean, and inside them you hear a stoke and roar as cold mineral and cold water become heat. From the short stacks along their spines they exhale. Not breath. Exhaust. Great plumes of white steam that in this cold do not rise but spill sideways, low and heavy, to join the ground fog. The air smells of hot iron and sulfur and wet hide and ozone.

Their joints do not bend, they drive. Pistons. You can see the rods slide in oil-slick sheaths at knee and hip. Their hearts are pumps with a two-stroke beat. When herds move — and they are always moving, shifting weight, shuffling a foot to let water pass, predator and prey trading places in slow rotation — the whole plain rings like a shunting yard.

And they make light. Dim ambient dusk is not enough to live by, so they carry their own. Between the upright horns of the larger bulls, between the antennae of the waders, blue sparks crawl and snap. St. Elmo's fire. When two herds press together flank to flank, static discharges pop in chains across wet backs, a running lace of white-blue that illuminates the steam from inside for a second. The young, hairless and pink-hot, glow faintly at the seams where plates haven't closed.

They eat stone and heat and each other. The grazers filter iron and sulfur bacteria from the weep. The hunters — lean, low, eight-legged things with furnace mouths that open sideways — do not chase so much as overheat, pressing their white-hot throats against a grazer's side until the plates buckle. The scavengers, clouds of chittering crab-moths, descend on cooling bodies to strip waste heat before the cold can steal it, clustering so thick on a fall that the fall seems to move again. Nothing is wasted because cold punishes waste. Production never stops because stopping is freezing.

You could call it an ecosystem. You could call it scaled production. Both are true.

But the numbers don't work. You notice it when you try to count. That hollow between two slabs should hold six bodies. It holds twenty. That steaming back you were watching — you blink and there are two of it, slightly out of phase, one a half-second ahead in its chuff. The whistle you hear arrives before the throat that makes it moves.

This is where the place stops pretending to be normal.

Reality here is thin from age, worn like the stone. Distance folds. The far side of the basin is both two miles away and pressed right against your face in the fog. You walk ten steps toward a stack-plume and it is no closer, but the sound of it is now behind you. Water sometimes weeps upward, beads leaving stone to join the low cloud. Steam hangs and thickens and, if you stare too long, takes on weight and eyes and legs and walks away to join the herd — a newborn condensing out of everyone else's breath.

Time stutters in the cold. The herd is packed not only in space but in time. The same beast occupies the same slush several times over, overlapping itself, which is why there is no room and never crowding. They phase through each other at the shoulder with a spark and a wet shudder and keep going. Predator and prey can be the same individual, minutes apart. The loudness is loudness piled on loudness — past chuffs not quite gone when present chuffs arrive.

The rules, insofar as you can learn them by standing knee-deep in slush with rime forming on your sleeves while a boiler-slug drags past your leg, sparking:

1. Nothing grows up. Everything spreads out.
2. Heat is food, food is heat. Keep moving or give your heat to someone who is.
3. Nothing was made here. Everything was precipitated, like salt from too much water, like steam from too much breath.
4. Do not look for edge or source. The flat goes on until fog takes it, and fog gives it back. The noise goes on until your bones learn it, and then it is silence.

Stay long enough and your own breath joins the low cloud. Your own footsteps fill with black water. Your own joints start to ache in rhythm with the pistons around you — chuff, hiss, step — and you understand why nothing green needed to come here. Iron and water and cold and endless, overlapping life were already enough to keep the works running without builders, without end, on the flattest, wettest, oldest floor in the world.
