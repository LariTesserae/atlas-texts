The town inside the leaf.

That's the first thought you have, and it's wrong, but not wrong enough to throw away. It's not inside a leaf. It's inside something that decided, a very long time ago, to be a town the way other plants decide to be leaves.

You come down into it — because everything here is down, low, settled into a basin of warm air — through a slit that closes behind you like a lip. Outside there was forest, presumably. You don't remember. Inside, the light doesn't change much. Normal daylight, gold-green and filtered, coming from everywhere and nowhere. The ceiling is high above you, translucent. You can see the veins in it branching, thick as avenues, with light pulsing slowly through them. That's the sun, through skin.

The scale is small. You can feel the boundaries all the time. A hamlet. A single room the size of a hamlet. A cathedral that got confused and grew neighborhoods instead of pews.

The floor gives under your feet. That's the first rule: **nothing here is dead.** There is no stone, no wood in the dead-timber sense, no dirt. The ground is living tissue, matte green-black and worn smooth and shiny in the middle of paths by ten thousand generations of feet, claws, pads, bellies. In the hollows between paths it is moss-velvet, springy, warm to the touch. Warm like an animal flank. The air itself is warm, lowland warm, that presses sweat out of you and then doesn't let it evaporate.

Terrain, if you want to call it that, is anatomy. The town is organized the way a leaf is organized because it *is* that organization. A broad central midrib runs end to end, a raised causeway of tough fiber, slightly ridged, slightly bouncy. From it branch the streets, smaller veins, then vein-lets. They are sunken gutters where sap runs clear and sweet after a rain, and things come to drink. The neighborhoods sit between them: clusters of hollow growths.

Houses are not built. They are budded. Curled bracts the size of huts, gall-apples hollowed by beetles and then re-lined with wax, pitcher-rooms hanging in rows from the lower ceiling, swaying slightly, each with a lipped doorway. Seed-pod tenements split open and furred inside with floss. There are two, three layers of them in places — one grown over the scar of an older one, balconies of fungus-shelf, staircases that are just the spiral of a tendril thickened with age. It is unmistakably urban. Intersections. Alleys that dead-end in a drip-pool. A little plaza where the midrib widens, the floor polished to a pale green translucence, worn by dancing or fighting or both. No tools made it. No cut edge anywhere. Everything was coaxed, chewed, secreted, grown around a need until it held the shape.

And yet it is empty. That is the strangest thing and the truest thing. For a town, there is almost nothing in it per volume. The great middle air is hollow. Vast space between presences. You stand on the midrib and shout — though you wouldn't hear yourself — and the vault above you is just *lung*, fifty, sixty feet of nothing but warm, lit, trembling air. The houses cling to the edges, to the floor, to the low walls, like barnacles on a whale, leaving the center void. Plant fills the space only in the sense that plant *is* the space. Walls, floor, sky are all one continuous living mass. You are walking around inside a single hollowed body.

Water is not a river here. There is not enough of it for that, and too much plant to let it be. Water is intermittent. It lives in pockets. In the axil of every big leaf-wall there is a cup, waxy, holding a palm of brown tea-water where tadpole-things flick. Dew condenses endlessly on the underside of the ceiling and hangs there until it is too heavy, and then the town has weather. It rains indoors, in sudden, localized showers — a patter, a hiss, a brief drum on taut membranes — and then stops. The floor drinks it immediately, swells, sighs. In the mornings, which are not mornings but exhalations, fog breathes out of pores in the walls and drifts knee-high down the streets, smelling of crushed green and iron. You learn to lick moisture off the walls rather than drink from the cups. The cups are nurseries. Things live in them that will resent you.

Fauna. Oh, there is fauna. Abundant is too small a word. The emptiness is alive.

They are mostly small, which is how the town stays empty and crowded at once. Clouds of midges that move like a single cloth. Jewel beetles big as your thumb, working the gutters. Tree-frogs the color of fresh sap, hundreds of them, throats going. Geckos flattened against the walls, eyes like wet seeds. Moths with wingspans like handkerchiefs sleeping in the pitcher-rooms until dusk-light triggers them. Up high, in the void, swifts or something like swifts — not birds, not bats, something with membrane wings and a keel of chitin — carve endless circles, screaming. And mites, springtails, ants in highways along the handrails of veins, wasps that have learned to roll leaves into doors.

And predators, because this place is active, in motion, not a diorama. The mantids hang at intersections, perfectly still, perfectly the color of the doorway they haunt. The frogs eat the midges. The swifts eat the moths. Something under the floor — you can feel it moving when you lie still — prunes the roots from below, keeping the streets from closing over. Sap runs, leaves unfurl with an audible creak, a pitcher-room that was closed yesterday is open today and occupied, a gall bursts and releases a chittering brood that becomes the new weather for an hour. Cycles happen fast here. Birth to death while the ceiling-light shifts once across the sky-vein.

The sound. You cannot prepare for it.

It is deafening. Not loud like a machine. Loud like being inside a throat that is singing with a thousand other throats. The baseline is a cicada roar so continuous it becomes a pressure on your sternum, coming from the walls themselves — the walls *are* the singers, vibrating. Over that: frog chorus in overlapping rounds, ant-mandible clicks amplified by the hollow, wing-buzz dopplering through the void, sap hissing in pipes, the groan of fiber as the whole town shifts its weight, the plink-plink-plink of interior rain. You feel it in your teeth. Conversation is impossible beyond a lip-read distance. Your own thoughts go hoarse. After a while you stop trying to filter and let it hold you up. That is the second rule: **the sound is structural.** When a swarm lifts all at once and the pitch spikes, the ceiling bellies slightly outward, the houses tremble. The old-timers — frogs, mostly — sing in the key that keeps the roof taut.

And then the weirdness, which is not decoration. It's load-bearing.

Directions don't quite hold. Walk the midrib end to end and you count seven side streets on the left. Walk back and there are nine. Not trickery; the plant is slowly peristalsing, rearranging. An echo arrives *before* the call that made it in the plaza, because sound travels faster through living fiber than air and takes a shortcut through the floor. If you stand very still in the center void and look up, you can see yourself from above, reflected not in water but in a lens of clear sap in the ceiling — tiny, on the causeway, mouth open against the roar — and the reflection blinks half a second late.

Gravity is negotiable near the walls. The walls are floor if you commit. Geckos know this. Drunk beetles know this. You learn it on your third day when you slip and instead of falling, stick, and walk three steps sideways up a house-wall before your weight remembers itself and drops you, laughing and ringing, onto moss.

Most unsettling: the town remembers being larger. In the weathered places — and everything here is weathered, settled, long-existed, polished by touch, in balance — you find doors that open onto solid flesh, streets that narrow to a scar and stop. The plant has closed districts off. Or outgrown them. And at night, when the light through the ceiling goes indigo and the chorus drops half a tone, you can hear traffic in those sealed places. Footsteps. A market. Something still living its urban life on the other side of the skin.

You don't leave quickly. The slit you came through is now just a seam, and prodding it makes the whole street flinch. You sleep in an empty pitcher-room lined with someone else's shed down, rain pattering on its hood, frogs singing you deaf, warm sap-smell thick enough to chew. You learn the third rule: **don't dam the gutters, don't drink the cups, don't sing against the choir.** The town is not hostile. It is simply total. It has no outside tools, no devices, no dead matter to hide behind. Only living mass, and hollow, and noise, and hunger in balance.

In the morning-exhalation, fog in the streets, light coming green-gold through the veins, swifts screaming circles in the great empty lung above the little dense-built hamlet clinging to its own ribs, you understand it all at once: this is not a place where a town was grown. This is a place where growth learned the idea of a town, and liked being crowded and empty and deafening all at once, and never stopped.
