It is low here. You feel it first in your knees and in the air — the press of warm air that has settled into a basin and decided to stay.

A broad, shallow valley, valley-sized, town-sized, the kind of distance you can walk in a day if the ground would hold still. Above, a normal late-afternoon light, white-gold and unremarkable, comes down in sheets. Below, there is no ground. Not really.

There is green.

Not green on things. Green as the thing itself. Stem on stem on leaf on root on flower on bark, layered ten, twenty deep, near-touching everywhere, filling the volume of the valley from the damp undermat to a hundred feet up where the canopy closes and opens and closes again. You look for soil and find only more plant: mats of moss thick as mattresses, root-plates braided like muscle, trunks that are not single trunks but bundles of a hundred saplings fused at the flank.

And it is a city. That is the second thing you understand, after *green* and just before *loud*.

Because this mass is organized. Avenues run where giant petioles have arched and flattened into roads, their upper surfaces worn smooth and pale by nothing but rain and their own flexing. Walls rise where aerial roots have descended in colonnades and fused. Blocks and tiers stack: low warrens of hollow ginger-like rhizomes big enough to walk through, stooping; above them, mid-level galleries of interwoven branches that form floors, railings, vaults; above that, leaf-roofs, each leaf the size of a house awning, overlapping in shingles, veined like roadmaps, dripping condensation from their pointed tips.

No stone. No metal. No cut timber, even. No tool marks anywhere. Everything is still alive, still attached, still growing. The doorway you duck through is a stomatal pore in a living wall that dilates when you approach. The stair you climb is a spiral of thorns that softened into steps an hour ago and will harden again tonight. The plaza ahead — a broad, shallow bowl made by six immense leaves cupped together — holds three inches of water at its center, sky-reflecting, warm as tea. All architecture is anatomy.

It is damp, but not wet. This is not a swamp. Water exists here in pockets and passages and temporary gifts. The air itself sweats. Warmth lifts moisture off the endless transpiration, and it condenses on every underside, beads, falls in brief, local rains that last a minute and stop mid-drop. You will walk through a dry colonnade of bark, turn a corner of vine, and be caught in a sudden patter from a leaf-tip above, just that one leaf raining. In the hollows between rhizome-bulks, cisterns have formed: cup-fungi the size of bathtubs, bromeliad tanks you could bathe in, pitcher-structures big enough to drown in, all full of brown-green water that shivers. A day ago — an hour ago — they were elsewhere. Water is borrowed here, never owned. The mass drinks it and moves it on.

Beneath all this living weight, the young earth keeps pushing up. This valley is new. In the gaps where roots split for a moment, you see it: sharp, black, unweathered teeth of basalt, fresh lava-crags from a recent upwelling, edges so fresh they would cut. They have no time to round or soften. Within days, sometimes hours, tendrils find them, wrap them, pry them apart. A shard that stabbed the sky naked this morning is by evening sheathed in green, used as a foundation pylon for a new tower. The land is sharp because it hasn't learned to be old yet.

You have not heard the quiet because there has been no quiet since you entered.

It is deafening.

There are no animals here. No birds to call, no insects to buzz, no throats at all. Listen anyway, and you will have to cover your ears.

The sound is the plants themselves, at city-density, at storm-speed. Close up, it resolves: the high, almost ultrasonic tick-tick-tick-pop of xylem cavitating as water columns snap and reform in a thousand stems at once, like fat frying all around your head. The slow groan of trunks in torsion, wood fibers slipping, a bass note you feel in the molars. The rip and sigh of new leaves unfurling — a wet, tearing sound, repeated a million times. Seed pods detonating with rifle-cracks, firing their seeds into bark walls where they stick and immediately germinate. Sap moving: not a trickle but a rush, a riverine roar inside the larger boles, audible when you press your ear to them.

And from further off, where the districts shift, a deeper percussion: collapse. Because this city is turbulent, hyperactive, unable to stop. Growth here is not seasonal. It is minute-by-minute. A tower of bamboo-like culms shoots thirty feet in an afternoon, leans, overbalances, crashes down across an avenue with a thunder that drowns all other thunder, splintering into pulp. Before the echo fades, creepers are already over it, using the wreck as trellis, raising a new arcade from the corpse. Streets close. Plazas open. A wall you leaned against at noon has become a ceiling by dusk. The whole borough churns like a bazaar where every stall is eating its neighbor and building a new stall from the pieces.

Stay still long enough and you feel the rules change.

This is the part that unmakes it:

1. Inside is larger than outside. The hollow rhizome you crawl into, ten feet long from without, runs for a hundred feet within, curving down through warm, chlorophyll-lit chambers that pulse faintly. You emerge from its other mouth onto a canopy walk you swear was *above* you a moment ago.

2. Paths remember and repeat. Walk down a root-avenue away from the central leaf-plaza, turn left at the forked strangler-fig, turn left again, and you return to the same plaza but from above, looking down at the place where you started, where a version of the light still holds your wet footprint in a leaf.

3. Growth is direction, not time. Things do not age here; they head. Young shoots point toward futures that have not happened yet, and when you follow them you find half-formed rooms, stairs that end in curled fists of meristem, waiting. Old tissues do not die so much as fold back into elsewhere, resorbed, the walls thinning to membrane and then to nothing while still bearing weight.

The light stays normal through all of it. That is the strangest mercy. No red sun, no purple gloom. Just ordinary daylight filtering through ordinary chlorophyll, making everything glow from within as if lit by lanterns. Motes of pollen — plant, not animal, endless, wind-borne, no bee needed — drift in the shafts, thick as dust in an attic. Your skin in this warmth is constantly filmed with sweat and sap and fine condensation. Everything you touch leaves a green smear and sticks slightly.

If you stand in the plaza at the center — if there is a center — and turn slowly, you see the extent: tier on tier of living tenements, bridges of fused lianas sagging and rebounding under their own hurried weight, turrets that are unopened flowers the size of silos, their petals still sealed but throbbing faintly with pressure, ready to blow. Beyond them, more of the same to the valley rim, and beyond the rim, a haze of green spires continuing. It does not end so much as repeat until light gives out.

There is nowhere to sit that is not alive. Nowhere to be that is not crowded. A fern grows from a fern grows from a moss grows from a bark crevice on a branch that is itself growing from a leaf. Epiphytes have epiphytes. Even the water in the cups has a skin of duckweed and, beneath it, filaments of algae knotting into tiny forests.

And over it all, that roar — vegetative, mindless, exuberant — the sound of a town with no citizens except its houses, building and unbuilding itself forever in the warm, damp lowland, too fast to map, too dense to leave, too alive to ever be finished.
