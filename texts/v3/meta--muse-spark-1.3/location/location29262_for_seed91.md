You come down into it by a road that drops and doesn't come back up.

From the rim it doesn't look like much. A shallow, enormous bowl in the tundra, a few kilometers across, rim to rim. The light is low and stays low, a flat grey-blue that could be morning or evening. It never quite decides. The wind up on the lip is clean and thin and cuts straight through your coat. Then the road tilts down, switchbacking through raw black banks where the earth has been cut fresh.

The cut is young. You can tell. The edges haven't softened yet. The peat faces along the road are sharp and vertical, layered like pages — living green on top, then brown, then almost black, dripping. Frost is already needling out of the exposures in white fans, even though it isn't deep winter. Stones stick out at angles where the dozers left them. There are no rounded hills here, no old slopes. Everything looks recently uncovered, as if the lid was lifted off not long ago and the air got in.

At the bottom, the ground changes entirely.

You step off the gravel onto plants, and after that you never step off plants again.

The whole floor of the basin is living mass. Not forest — there are almost no trees. Not meadow. A deep, continuous, springing carpet of sphagnum moss and lichen and dwarf willow pressed flat by cold, unbroken for as far as you can walk except where men have laid something over it. Olive and rust-red and acid green, gone grey with frost. It gives under your boots and comes back up. Water squeezes up around your soles with every step, cold enough to ache. If you kneel and pull a handful apart, it's wet down a foot, down two feet, a sponge of half-rotted stems and ice crystals and black water smelling faintly sweet, like tannin and iron.

This is the substrate here. There is no soil to speak of that isn't plant, past plant, future plant. Hummocks rise knee-high, hard with frost on their north sides, soft on their south. Hollows between them hold black mirrors of water no bigger than a dinner plate — sky trapped in peat. In the mornings, and most of the day is morning, every moss tip is beaded. Your pants are soaked to the thigh within a hundred yards, not from rain but from brushing through it. The air itself condenses. It beads on zippers, on railings, on the underside of pipes, and falls in a constant slow drip.

Drizzle comes at intervals and passes without drama. No storms. Just a fine wetness that thickens the grey and then thins again.

And over it all, the sound.

You hear it before you see any building, before you are even halfway down the road. A low, physical roar that sits in your chest. Not one engine but many, layered, bouncing off the low cloud and the bowl walls until direction stops meaning anything. Diesel generators. Pump screws. Ventilation fans on tall stacks turning and turning without hurry. The 60-cycle hum of transformers under load, a whine on top of the rumble you feel in your teeth.

Down on the floor you understand why. Scattered across the moss, a half-kilometer apart, a kilometer apart, stand the stations. Small, utilitarian boxes of corrugated steel and poured concrete, rust bleeding from every bolt, frost feathering up their shaded walls. Each one sits on pilings driven down through the living mat. Each one has a stack, a vent, a set of pipes that run out low over the moss on trestles and then dive down into it. Each one breathes. Each one shouts.

Inside — you can see through an open service door at one — it is hot and bright and deafening in a different way, close and metallic: a big green engine block shuddering on its mounts, gauges trembling, a man in ear defenders checking oil with his mouth moving and no words audible. Outside, the exhaust ripples heat into air so cold your breath plumes. The contrast makes you shiver harder.

The pipes sweat and drip. Around every footing, the moss is trying to climb. Tendrils of green already over the concrete lip, over the first rung of ladders, into cable trays. Someone has cut it back recently with a strimmer — you can see the pale fresh cut — and it is already returning. That is the rule here, and everyone who works here knows it: if the machines stopped for a season, for a year, the water pooled in the hollows would join up, the frost would heave the pilings, and the moss would close over road and pipe and platform and not leave a seam. The basin wants to be a bog. Only the roar keeps it a floor you can walk on.

Between the stations there is almost nothing, and that almost-nothing goes on for a long time. A boardwalk of black, wet planks running straight until perspective swallows it, already soft with lichen. A line of power poles marching, wires humming audibly, vibrating a fine mist off themselves. A single abandoned fuel drum half-sunk, its rim become a garden for moss. Tire tracks filled with dark water that show no bottom. Distance fools you. Sound fills the space so completely that your eyes keep expecting crowds, traffic, a town. There is no town. There is just the huge, dim, green-black flatness and the far rim all around like the edge of a saucer, pale in mist.

Living things other than plants are scarce, and shy of the noise.

Once in an hour you might startle a ptarmigan, white already, bursting from a willow hollow with a clatter that is instantly eaten by the drone. Ravens cross high, tilted by wind, and do not call — or call and cannot be heard. Fox tracks stitch in a straight line from one hummock to the next, pressed sharp in frost, gone soft by noon. Under a lifted board you find white springtails ticking like dust. That is mostly it. No herds. No flocks. The cold and the constant shaking of the air keep everything else near the rim.

The cold is not dramatic. No blizzard. Just a steady, seeping zero that finds cuffs and collars. Puddles skin over with ice as thin as a contact lens and crack underfoot and re-form. Your fingers go clumsy on zippers. Metal sticks faintly to bare skin. In the hollows where water stands, ice needles grow up out of the moss overnight in columns, lifting tiny caps of peat on their tips, then collapse at midday into glitter. Slow work. Everything here is slow work.

The moss grows millimeters a year. Rust creeps a little farther down each stack each season. The sharp peat banks along the road slump grain by grain. Water moves sideways through the sponge, not in streams but as a pressure, a dampness seeking level. Men check levels, top up fuel, change filters, cut back green. The engines do not hurry and do not stop.

By what passes for evening the light thins further to indigo. The station windows go amber. Vapor from the stacks leans sideways and merges with low fog that begins to form just above the moss, knee-high, then waist-high, glowing faintly around each floodlight. The roar does not lessen. If anything, in the dark and the fog, with the rim vanished and only the next light visible as a halo, it grows larger — the whole basin humming like a held note.

You stand on living ground that is cold and soaked, between machines that will not be turned off, under a sky that will not get bright, and you feel the scale of it in your body before you understand it: kilometers of plant holding water, and a handful of hot steel boxes shouting to keep it from rising.
