You are under water, and you are high up.

That's the first wrongness you have to get used to. Your body knows depth as low, as down. But this water sits on top of a mountain — two kilometers of black rock underneath you holding up a shallow, sunlit bowl. If the ocean were drained you would be standing in alpine air, light-headed. As it is you hang in cool blue, fifteen meters below the sky, with daylight falling in straight pillars all the way to stone.

The bowl is small. You can see all of it at once, rim to rim, like a room. Maybe sixty meters across. A crater, and a fresh one. The rock is too young to have learned how to be soft.

Everything is sharp. The rim is a jagged crown of basalt teeth, glassy where it quenched, still with the rope-swirl of lava frozen mid-flow. Inside, the walls step down in clean, unworn terraces, black and rust-red and sulfur-yellow. No rounding, no sanding, no cobbles. Flakes and shards and needles. Where a chimney has fallen it hasn't broken into pebbles — it has shattered into knives. The whole place looks recently emerged, still deciding its shape. You do not put your hand down anywhere without looking first.

There is no green. Not a blade, not a frond, not even the slick of algae that usually films sunlit stone. The water is too mineral, too new, too hungry. This is a mineral world. What covers the rock instead is animal, and then more animal on top of animal until the rock is only a rumor underneath.

That is the second thing: how crowded it is.

The water itself is thick. Not empty blue but a snowstorm that never settles — marine snow, mineral smoke, larvae, molts, eggs, drifting white, all lit from above. And in it, bodies. Layered, near-touching, high-saturation life. Clouds of small pale shrimp, thousands, tens of thousands, hanging in curtains between the light shafts, each one transparent except for a hard red eye. Below them, on every ledge, white anemone-mouths packed shoulder to shoulder, tentacles open, closing slow as lungs. Below them, brachiopods stacked like dishes, and bristle-worms in white tubes, and flat cog-crabs wedged into every crack, legs folded like jackknives.

No one swims fast here. That is the rule. Everything pulses, filters, clicks, but moves as if through thicker time. A crab lifts a claw over the course of a minute. A ray of shrimps parts around you and closes again long after you've passed. Even the light seems patient.

And yet it is loud. Very loud.

At first you think machinery. You can't help it — the rhythm is industrial. There is a low, continuous grinding as the sea forces itself through the notches in the fresh rim, a surge that inhales and exhales through stone flues. There is a hissing from the chimneys in the center. There is a metallic clanking, irregular but constant, as sharp slabs shift a millimeter and settle. And over it all, the biological rivet-gun: the shrimp. Millions of snaps per minute, their tiny claws cavitating. Close up it is not a background hiss but a factory floor — snapping, hammering, sizzling. You feel it in your sternum more than hear it.

The center of the bowl is where the industry lives. Not built. Nothing here is built. No wall, no wreck, no cable, no cut stone, no artifact at all. And still — smokestacks.

A cluster of chimneys, five or six of them, three meters tall, fresh and rough, the color of rusted iron. From their tips pours not black boiling heat but a cool, milky fluid, heavy with dissolved mineral. It doesn't roar upward; it billows and slumps and spreads sideways in slow layers because the surrounding water is almost as cold as it is. Cool on your exposed skin, cool in your mouth through the regulator — not tropical, not polar, that high-mountain-lake cool that wakes you up. Around the base of each stack, the water shimmers faintly with charge. If you hold a fingertip close — you don't — the hairs lift. Iron and sulfur precipitating out of solution make tiny, endless lightning, too small to see, big enough to taste as metal.

The animals farm this. That is their engine.

The chimneys are pumps and the currents are conveyor belts. The milky plume rises a meter, stalls, and sinks outward in a slow doughnut, and every mouth in the bowl is turned to meet it. The anemones on the high terraces get first pass, the tube-worms get second, the crabs scuttle — slowly, sideways, like forklifts — to clean up the fallout. Nothing is wasted. The water that leaves through the rim notches is stripped clear for a moment before the ocean pushes new snow back in.

Look closer and the industrial mimicry gets stranger. The worms do not just sit; they piston. Each white tube has a crown of red gills that extends, twists, retracts on a hydraulic pulse, all the worms on a ledge slightly out of phase so the ledge ripples like a line of pistons firing. The cog-crabs really do look cogged — their carapaces are toothed gears, and when two meet they don't fight, they mesh, edge to edge, turn a quarter-turn, and separate. The shrimp carry tiny, mirrored gas bladders that flash when they snap, so the whole curtain strobes faintly, on-off, on-off, like warning lights on a production line.

No one designed this. That's the eeriness. The mountain erupted last year or ten years ago — yesterday in geologic time — and life poured into the fresh wound and immediately organized itself into scaled production, electrified, mechanized, without a mechanic.

The boundaries hold. The rim teeth keep the bowl separate from the vast blue outside. Beyond them you can see the mountain falling away into dark, empty water, but inside, everything stays. Larvae rise and sink and are caught again by the doughnut current. Even sound stays. A snap made here echoes off the sharp walls two, three times before it escapes, so the bowl is always louder inside than it should be.

Rules, as you learn them hanging there:

1. Everything is either erupted or grown. Nothing is made.
2. You do not get space. Crowding is mandatory. To live here is to live touching.
3. Green is not an option. Energy comes up from stone and down from sun-lit snow, never from leaf.
4. Change is slow despite the noise. The chimneys grow a centimeter a month. The terraces fill animal by animal. The rim will keep its knives for a thousand years before the sea learns to soften them.

Stay long enough and your eyes adjust to the density. You start to see individuals: a single shrimp hovering, beating its swimmerets so slowly you can count them; a single anemone closing around a flake of iron; a single crab, white and blind, polishing a patch of basalt with its mouthparts until it shines like a gauge.

Above you the surface is a bright, wrinkled ceiling, normal daylight, noon-bright, throwing the shadows of waves across the whole crowded room. Below you the engine breathes. Around you the water, cool and heavy and full, clicks and hisses and grinds.

A high place, drowned. A small room, full to bursting. A factory with no builder, running patiently in the sun.
