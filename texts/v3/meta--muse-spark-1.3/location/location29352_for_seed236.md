On top, past the loose scree where your boots stop slipping, there is a rim.

Not high as rims go — a dark, fresh lip of basalt not yet rounded by anything. You can see where it broke. The edges are still glassy in places, sharp as broken bottle, too young to have learned how to be a hill. From below this whole mesa looked barren, a rust-black cinder pile too steep and too dry for anything. No snow, no seep, no creek line. No shine of water anywhere, not even the dark stain where water used to be.

And then you step over the rim and drop down into the bowl, and it is full.

Not full of water. There has never been water here. The stone is thirsty in a way lowland deserts don't understand — no buried aquifer, no morning dew that lasts past sun-touch, no mud memory. The dust between the clinkers is pale mineral flour, pumice and ash that sucks the sweat off your hands if you touch it. But the bowl is crowded, lush, jammed stem-to-stem and roof-to-roof with living tissue. A dry lushness. A lushness that doesn't need to drink.

At first you think forest. Then you think town.

It is both, and it is small enough to hold in one look. Two hundred paces across, maybe less, an oval cradled by the crater wall so the wind goes over and not through. That shelter is the whole reason. Warm air gets trapped here. At this altitude the air should be thin and cold, but the black rock holds the sun and gives it back all day and half the night, so it stays late-summer warm, close and still, smelling of hot stone and resin. The light is ordinary — white-gold, midday, no strange suns — which makes what grows here stranger for being so plainly lit.

What grows here grows thick.

The ground is not ground. It's a mat, ankle-deep then knee-deep then waist-deep if you leave the path, made of felted air-mosses, grey-green and silver and dusty sage, none of them rooted in soil because there is no soil. They root in each other. Their leaves are tiny spoons coated in wax and fine hair, designed not to lose anything but to catch everything: the faint oil-fog that rises off the sun-warmed rock, skin cells, spores, sloughed chitin. They drink the air without water in it. You sink a little as you walk and the mat sighs back up.

Out of that mat rise the towers.

A dozen, maybe twenty, clustered like a hamlet with streets between them. They are not built. They are secreted, woven, grown. From a distance they look like termite spires, but up close they are plant and animal at once — hollow columns of chewed fiber, lava grit, and living vine, five to fifteen feet tall, leaning slightly toward the sun. Their outer skin is a living bark of overlapping scale-leaves, thick and succulent, pale jade and amber where the sun hits, storing not water but a clear aromatic sap that burns slow if cut and seals instantly. Inside, each tower is hollow, cool, dim. Things live in them.

Between the towers there are plazas — worn, flattened places where the felt mat has been pressed into felt rugs by thousands of soft feet — and narrow lanes just wide enough for one person, or one large animal, to pass without brushing the walls. For the walls are alive and bruise easily. Overhead, the towers lean toward each other and send out bridges: living arches, rope-thick tendrils braided and re-braided until they hold weight. From those bridges hang gardens: curtains of beard-moss, bladder-leaves that never fill with water but with air, dark seed pods that tick faintly in the heat. Layer on layer. Ground, midstem, roof, air. Every cubic foot is occupied. You cannot put your hand down without touching three different lives.

And the lives are everywhere, once your eyes adjust to the quiet.

Nothing here moves fast, nothing here shouts. This is an abundant place that practices silence.

On the tower skins, whole herds of flat, velvet-backed grazers — big as your palm, slow as sap — inch along, rasping algae-wax with mouths underneath their bodies. They leave pale polished trails. In the shadow lanes, blind white millipedes as thick as your wrist turn the mineral flour, aerating the mat. On the hanging curtains, moths the color of ash hang with wings open to collect heat, not flying but gliding for seconds at a time from bridge to tower when the air finally stirs. Little lizards with translucent throats bask on the sharpest rocks, the only sharp things left uncolonized, and they watch you without fleeing because fleeing costs moisture they don't have.

Up highest, on the rims of the tallest towers, sit the builders. They are not ants, not people, but they have that settlement logic. Cat-sized, soft, six-limbed, covered in hooked hairs to which they deliberately stick seeds and cuttings. They farm their own backs and then transplant from each other. They mend the towers with pellets of chewed moss and grit, pressing them in with their foreheads, patient as masons who are also the bricks. They have no tools. No cut stone, no metal, no fire, no wire. If you look for a door hinge, a nail, a knife mark, you will not find it. Everything is lip, tooth, claw, tongue, secretion, patience. And yet the result is urban: drains (for sap overflow, not rain), middens tucked in the lee where the old leaves are composted into gritty humus, nurseries deep in the coolest tower where the grubs pulse almost imperceptibly, common roofs where the builders gather at warm dusk to touch antennae and exchange cuttings.

It is unusual not because it is grotesque, but because the categories are wrong. The houses photosynthesize. The gardens walk around on the backs of animals. The streets breathe. If you stand still long enough — and here you must stand still, the air punishes hurry — you feel the whole hamlet inhale as the sun passes zenith and the scale-leaves open their pores in unison, a faint warm exhalation of turpentine and honey.

Sound: there is almost none. No water to babble, no leaves to rattle hard, no birds to call. Your own breath is loud. Under it, if you crouch and hold that breath, you hear what the place uses instead of noise: the microscopic tick-tick-tick of seed pods dehiscing in the heat, a soft creak as a bridge vine takes up thermal slack, the nearly subsonic rasp of a grazer, the dry whisper of your sleeve against wax. The builders do not vocalize. They drum, once, twice, with a hind foot on hollow fiber when alarmed — you feel it in your sternum more than hear it. Mostly they are quiet to keep the moisture in their mouths.

Nothing here happens quickly. That is the rule.

The towers grow perhaps a finger-length a year. A bridge takes a decade to become walkable. The felt mat advances over a new fall of cinders grain by grain, softening the razor glass without ever quite rounding it — this place is too young for rounding. You can still see the fresh fracture faces everywhere you look where the crater blew open not long ago, geologically speaking, and life rushed in before erosion could even start its work.

Other rules follow from dryness:

Do not expect to drink. There is no water to find, beg, or steal. The succulents will give you sap that smells like pine and will blister your tongue and seal your lips. The animals get their solvent from that sap and from each other. You are the only thing here that needs water, and the place knows it. Bring your own and keep it sealed.

Do not break the skin. Every leaf, every tower wall, is a sealed bottle. A tear doesn't bleed water, it bleeds future. The builders will come and stare at the wound with their blank, heat-loving eyes and begin to patch it with their own chewed bodies if they must, and they will remember your shape.

Move slowly, and low. The lanes are not for striding. In the heat and thin high air you will tire fast anyway. Better to learn the hamlet pace: a shuffle, a pause, a look. The grazers will not move for you; step around.

Stay till the light slants. In normal noon the hamlet looks merely dense, a green-grey town in a black bowl. In late afternoon, when the sun drops below the western rim and the shadow pours in like something poured, the towers glow from inside — the succulent leaves transluce amber — and for an hour the whole small city is a lantern, lit, warm, silent, crowded to bursting with slow lives that have solved living without water by living very close together and never, ever wasting a breath.
