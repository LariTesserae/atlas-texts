It is not on any map because it is not large enough to be on one.

Above, there is desert — white saltpan to the horizon, heat-hammered, empty. If you walk it at noon your boots leave a print that the wind erases by evening. In one place, where a slab of basalt has cracked and tipped against another, there is a slit no wider than your shoulders. You have to turn sideways and let yourself drop.

You land below the surface, and the drop is only six feet, but the air changes completely.

Warm. At once, bath-warm. Not the dry blast-furnace of the surface, but a close, held warmth, like putting your face into cupped hands that have been breathing for a hundred years. It does not rise or fall. It has no source and no draft. It simply is, everywhere in the pocket at once,  blood-warm on your forearms, on your eyelids.

The room is small. You could cross it in twenty slow steps if you walked straight, but you won't walk straight. It is about the size of a chapel, or a large living room sunk into the earth. A single chamber, ovoid, its walls still sharp.

This is new rock. That is the first thing your hands understand. The floor and the low vault are fresh-broken, unsoftened. Black glass and cinder, edges like chipped knives, bubbles frozen mid-pop, needles of feldspar glittering where they snapped yesterday in geological time. Nothing has been rounded. There is no sand here, no dust drift, only angular grit that crunches and stays where it falls. The ceiling hangs low in places, in daggers and fins, so you must duck. Everything smells of hot mineral and faint sulfur, clean, absolutely dry.

There is no water here. Not a seep, not a stain, not a dark line where water once ran. The air will take the moisture off your lips in a minute and give nothing back. Your sweat never beads; it is wicked straight out of you and gone into the thirst of the stone. If you spit, it would vanish before it hit. This place has never known rain, river, spring.

And yet it is lush.

Not lush as a jungle is lush. Lush as a held breath is full. Scattered across the sharp floor, with wide bare stretches of black glass between them, are islands of growth — each one dense, extravagant, knee to shoulder high, and utterly dry to the touch.

They are not green, or not only green. The dominant things are plume-bushes: stalks the thickness of a finger, made of a waxy, amber fiber, branching into antlers that end in feathers. The feathers are leaves, if that word still works — thin vanes of rust-red, sulfur-yellow, deep bruised purple, covered in a velvet down that catches the dim light and holds it. They rustle when you brush them, with a papery, insect-wing sound, and they do not wilt, do not dampen. Beneath them grow mats of something like liverwort but crisped: overlapping scales of pale jade and ash-white, dry as moth wings, piled lushly three inches thick. From cracks rise whips, six feet tall, jointed, tipped with lantern-pods of translucent skin that glow faintly from within.

There is no soil. They root straight into glass, into salt-rind. Their roots are white cords that secrete acid and drink stone. They do not drink water because there is none to drink. They drink heat and vibration. You can see it if you stay still long enough: the down on the plume-leaves trembles in sympathy with the sound, harvesting it.

For the sound is loud.

In so small a room it should be impossible. It is loud the way a beehive inside your skull is loud. A single, massive, unbroken chord made of a million stridulations — the fauna.

They are everywhere once your eyes adjust to the dim. The light is low, a dusk that never deepens nor lifts: a sourceless amber-gray exhalation from the lantern-pods and from the rock itself, where veins of some phosphor run like milky seams. In that gloom the air is thick with wings.

Crickets the size of your thumb, blind, milk-white, with hind legs like tuning forks, lined up on the plume branches shoulder to shoulder, sawing without pause. Ash-moths, huge and slow, gray as ash, looping the vault in endless circuits, shedding scales that hang in the air and never settle. Dust-skinks, translucent, belly-warm, flicking between islands, mouths open to catch moth scales. Mites like red beads clustering on the stalks. Everything singing, chittering, clicking — but after a minute you realize it is not many sounds. The echoes in this hard little egg of a room have folded into each other until they lock. One tone. One dense, buzzing, warm tone that presses on your sternum. It does not get louder or softer. It does not stop between breaths. It has, the old marks suggest, never stopped.

Between the plant islands, in the bare spaces, are the structures.

Small. Scattered. A cairn of balanced glass shards no higher than your knee. A lean-to made of plume-stalks lashed with twisted gut, just large enough for something dog-sized to curl under. A rack of tiny bones — skink ribs, cricket femurs — tied in a lattice with copper-dark fiber and left leaning against the wall. A circle of knapped flakes, stone tools: scrapers, a hand-axe of obsidian still sharp enough to shave with, a mortar stone worn smooth in the center from grinding pods.

Who made them? Not people, not now. Perhaps the skinks have hands enough. Perhaps something visited, stayed a season, left. The tools are primitive, hand-work, no metal but that native copper thread, no writing, only a few scratched lines on a flat slab: tally marks, or sound waves. They are dusted with moth scale but not buried. Nothing buries here. Nothing decays quite right in the dry heat — a dead cricket mummifies where it falls, intact, singing legs still cocked, and stays.

That is the rule of the pocket, and you feel it within an hour: nothing changes.

Out in the desert above, wind erodes, sun bleaches, things live and die. Down here, equilibrium holds. The temperature does not gradient — the floor is as warm as the ceiling is as warm as your skin. The sound does not decay — the crickets that tire are replaced by others picking up the exact phase, so the chord holds seamless. The plants do not grow larger or die back; a feather-leaf that breaks off is caught by mites and carried back to the mat and re-woven. Even the dust hangs. You can see moth scales suspended in your headlamp beam, motionless despite the deafening vibration that should shake them down. They hang.

Time has pooled. This chamber broke open once — a gas bubble in cooling lava, cracked through to the surface by a quake — and in that instant everything that could live here arrived, arranged itself, and locked. Warmth in, sound in, life in, no water needed, no waste accumulating. A closed loop humming to itself.

If you sit very still on the sharp glass, back against the warm wall, plume-down brushing your cheek, the loudness stops hurting and becomes a surface. Your own breathing, your heartbeat, your stomach growling — they don't echo. They are absorbed and added. You become, for a little while, another instrument holding the same note.

You will have to climb back out to drink. Nothing here will give you water. The lantern-pods, if you crush one, yield only scented dust and a brief brighter glow that fades back to exactly what it was. The skinks watch you with eyeless heads tilted to your vibration.

When you leave, squeezing up through the slit into the white glare, the roar cuts off all at once, like a door shut. Behind you the pocket remains — small, hidden, warm, dry, crowded and empty, fresh as a wound and old as a held breath — humming its one loud note to no one, unchanged and unchanging.
