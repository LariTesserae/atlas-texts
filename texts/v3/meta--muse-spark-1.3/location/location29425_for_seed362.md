It is flat first. That is the first thing you have to unlearn about forests.

No hills. No valley to gather anything in. The world does not rise. It spreads, low and level and impossibly wide, until the curve of it is only a theory, and the brightness swallows distance. You stand on it and the horizon is not a line but a white fraying, like paper held up to a lamp.

There is no ground under the green. That is the second thing.

What you are standing on is made. Woven. Grown and then grown over. A mat three, ten, thirty meters thick of interlashed stems, rhizomes, trunk-rafts, root-cables, leaf-thatch pressed into felt and then into board and then into floor. If you cut down — and the maintenance pruners do, in long slow slots — you do not hit soil or stone. You hit underfloor: sealed, dun-colored mineral, smooth as a kiln shelf, warm faintly from the engines below. No dirt. No mud. No water to make mud from. This whole continent is a building that decided to be a plant.

And it is dry. Completely, constitutionally dry in a way that has nothing to do with desert.

No rivers. No ponds. No rain. The air is cool — refrigerator-cool, greenhouse-in-winter cool, the kind that makes your breath show for a half-second and then takes it away — and it smells of cut hay and hot oil and ozone. It does not smell wet. Nothing rots here. Things cure. Things husk and flake and powder.

The plants learned another chemistry. Their leaves are not wet inside. Snap one and it doesn't bleed. It cracks, and inside is a dense white starch, or a coil of oil-resin that smells like turpentine and machine grease, or simply air in honeycombed chambers. Their veins carry dust and light, not sap. They drink brightness. And there is so much brightness to drink.

The light is total. A low white sky, not blue, not cloudy, just luminous, as if the whole dome were a lamp shade. No single sun you can point to. Light comes from everywhere, cool and even and too strong, bleaching color out at noon and sharpening every edge. Shadows, when they exist, are thin and blue and lie directly underneath things, like spilled ink trying to hide. Your eyes water, then stop watering, because there is no moisture to spare. You squint and the world glints: millions of dry surfaces catching and throwing back.

Because it is dense. Not jungle-dense. Factory-dense. Shelf-dense.

From above — if you could get above, which you can't for long because the canopy weather takes you — it would look like a circuit board made of leaves. Thatched plains of silver-green strap-leaf, acre after acre, stitched through with darker lines: the conveyor-vines, thick as your torso, running straight for kilometers, carrying vat-fruits the size of barrels slowly along their length. Clumps of scaffold-trees, their trunks fluted and gray as concrete, grown in perfect grids, their crowns pollarded flat to form second floors, third floors, roofs. Between them, the chimney-stalks: hollow, jointed, a hundred meters tall but still low against that huge sky, breathing out slow plumes.

Not smoke. Lint. Pollen. Chaff. Dry spores that hang in the cool air and catch the light until whole districts glitter. That is the weather here. With no water there are no clouds, but the continent makes its own weather anyway out of shed material. When a sector flowers — and some sector is always flowering — the air fills with gold dust that moves in low, silent fronts. Visibility goes white-gold. You hear, very faintly, the patter of it landing on leaves: shhhh. The ground-mats thicken by a millimeter. The filters in the scaffold-trees clog and shudder and clear themselves with a soft cough.

Everything is working, and almost nothing makes noise.

That is the strangest part for newcomers. It looks industrial. It *is* industrial. Walk any maintenance aisle — those long, straight cuts between leaf-fields where the underfloor shows through like a polished path — and on either side: pistons of laminated wood rising and falling in bark cylinders, looms of living tendrils weaving new floor-mat even as you walk on it, press-fruits dropping with a dull thud into husk hoppers, being milled by toothed rollers into fiberboard, being carried off again as beams. Scaled production, endless. Electrified: you can see the coppery stamens arcing faint blue in the dim under-mat service tunnels, hear the sixty-cycle hum through your teeth.

But it is all padded. The leaves absorb. The felt absorbs. The air itself, dry and cool and full of fiber, absorbs. A stamping mill the size of a house operates ten meters from you behind a curtain of hanging moss-paper, and you hear only a soft, rhythmic *whump... whump...* like a heart through blankets. A conveyor-vine hauling a ton of seed-cotton sighs as its hydraulic fibers contract. Somewhere far off, a chimney-stalk exhales: *hahhhh.*

Human voices, if there were crowds, would carry. There are no crowds. There are almost no animals at all.

Rare fauna. You can walk for days and see only the plants and their machines, which are the same thing. Then, once, a maintenance beetle — or what you think is a beetle — as long as your forearm, matte black, jointed, not quite chitin, not quite casing, picking its way along a vine with deliberate, silent steps, pruning a sucker with its forelegs. It does not look at you. Another time, a flock — three, four — of paper-thin white moths, each the size of a hand, drifting on the lint wind, landing on a light-collector leaf and folding shut. They are so quiet you feel you imagined them. The ecosystem does not need them. Pollination here is done by air jets and shaking combs and electrostatic curtains that snap once an hour with a tick you feel more than hear.

The ecology is plant-eats-plant-makes-plant. Vat-trees grow vats in their crotches: huge, resin-lined gourds where cellulose pulp is digested by engineered fungi into oil. Oil runs through root-pipes to engine-nodes, where it is burned coolly to make electricity and warm mineral. Warmth rises to ripen the high fruits. The high fruits drop fiber to make more floor. The floor grows more trees. Nothing is wasted because there is nothing else to waste. Fallen leaves do not compost wetly; they are gathered by rake-vines into windrows and fed directly into shredder mouths that chew all night with a sound like someone turning pages very far away.

It has been like this a long time. You can tell. This is not a new greenhouse, bright plastic and sharp edges. Everything is weathered into its own state. The scaffold-tree bark is deeply furrowed, lichen-dry, patched with graft scars where a limb was redirected a century ago and the redirection stuck. The edges of the floor-mats fray into threads. The underfloor, where exposed, is scuffed by millions of passages. Vines sag in established catenaries. The whole system is in balance, not expanding aggressively, not collapsing, just maintaining, growing exactly as fast as it wears out. Long-existed. Settled.

Until you walk far enough, and it stops making sense.

Because the scale breaks it. Anything this vast and this bright and this alive starts to generate its own physics.

First: distances. You can see a cluster of chimney-stalks that should be two kilometers away. You walk toward them along a straight aisle for an hour, cool air on your face, rustle underfoot. They do not get closer. You look back. The scaffold-tree you started at is still right there, fifty meters behind you. You were walking but the mat was growing backward under you at walking pace, laying down new weave to keep the spacing correct. No one ordered it to. It's just how it keeps tension.

Second: interior/exterior. Many of the larger trunk-halls — hollowed scaffold-trees joined by leaf-membrane hangars — are bigger inside than outside. You duck through a slit door no taller than you into a sorting hall that runs two hundred meters, lit from above by translucent leaf panes, full of slow-moving sorting arms, all silent. You exit the far end and realize you have come out three trees over from where you entered, though you walked straight.

Third: light lag. In the deep thatch canyons, where the mat has folded over itself into layered arcades to save space, the brightness pools. It arrives before it is made. You will see your own shadow enter a crossing a full second before you do, cast by light that hasn't reached you yet. The maintenance crews — mostly automated pruners, a few masked human tenders — don't comment on it. They prune around it.

Fourth: duplication. The pattern repeats too perfectly. The same arrangement of three scaffold-trees, one vat-tree, and a kink in the conveyor-vine occurs every eleven kilometers, rotated slightly, weathered differently but topologically identical. As if the continent memorized a successful module and tiled itself. If you climb — carefully — up a scaffold ladder onto a crown roof and look out, you see the tiling shimmer in the heatless glare: gold-green-gold-green, repeating to the white edge of the world.

Rules, insofar as visitors learn them:

1. Do not look for water. There is none unbound. Even your sweat will be wicked from your skin by the air and taken. Drink from your sealed bottle. Piss into the sealed reclaimer. The mat will try to drink you if you let it. Not maliciously. It drinks all loose moisture.

2. To build is to prune. If you need shelter, you do not assemble. You tie back, you stake down, you redirect. Cut a strap-leaf and it will curl into a tube within an hour. Lash six together and you have a tent that is still alive and will slowly tighten around you unless you talk to the tender.

3. Walk on the aisles, not the fields. The fields are working surfaces. They can support you — they support tons — but they notice, and they will grow around your footprints to record you, and the record itches.

4. When the flowering front comes, stop and cover your mouth. Not because it is toxic. Because it is silent. The gold air deadens even your own breathing, and people have walked off the edge of service slots because they couldn't hear their own steps. Sit down. Watch the light move sideways through the dust. It is beautiful in a way that has nothing to do with you. Cool motes on your eyelashes. The faint ticking of electrostatic combs discharging. The distant, felt *whump* of the mills continuing underneath it all.

At night — though night is only a dimming, a graying of the white to pearl — the place does not sleep. It glows faintly. The light-collector leaves release what they stored, a cool phosphorescence that runs along the conveyor lines like slow traffic. The chimney-stalks exhale warmer plumes that rise and flatten against the invisible ceiling and spread. You lie on the floor-mat, which gives slightly and is surprisingly warm from the engines below, and you listen to the quietest industrial continent imaginable thinking to itself in rustles and sighs, growing itself and building itself at the same time, with no water, no animals, no hills to interrupt it, only leaf upon leaf upon made leaf, bright, cool, vast, and patiently, actively alive.
