You don't find it unless you are looking for somewhere else.

Up on the moor it is dry grass and wind-scoured grit, and then — between two slabs of heather-rooted basalt — a crack no wider than your shoulders. A rabbit-hole that isn't. Cold air comes up out of it even at noon. If you lie on your belly and put your face to it, you can see brightness down there where there should be dark.

You go in feet-first, hands scraping.

It is not deep. Three meters, four, a chimney of raw stone that has not learned how to be a cave yet. That is the first thing: how new it is. The edges are still sharp enough to cut. The walls haven't been rounded by water or feet or time. Flakes of rock flour come off on your palms. When you press your thumb too hard it leaves a print that slowly fills back in, the way warm bread does. The place is still making itself. Dust sifts down at intervals for no reason. You hear — no, you feel — a faint tick as a flake lets go above you.

Then your feet touch, and you are in.

The room is the size of a pantry. The size of being held. You can touch both sides if you spread your arms, you have to duck to not hit your head, and still you are afraid to move too large. It is entirely below the world, a pocket, an air-bubble left when the lava cooled wrong and then opened last winter when the top collapsed. From above, nothing. From inside, everything.

It is blinding.

A shaft of daylight falls straight down the throat, at midday so white it hurts. Outside the light is ordinary moor-light, grey-blue and wind-torn. In here it pools. It doesn't scatter like it should. It lands on the floor in a perfect ellipse, about as wide as a washtub, and stays there, thick. Dust motes hang inside it and do not drift. The surrounding stone, black basalt and rust-veined shale, gleams around it with condensation — ten thousand beads — each one lit from within. The air is cool, cellar-cool, your breath just barely fogs even in the brightness. Cold sinks and stays here. Above your head where the shaft narrows it is warm; at your knees, winter.

There is no water, really. That is the dryness of the wider moor following you down. No stream, no seep. Only damp. The walls sweat. Every morning and every evening they exhale beads that grow, tremble, fall. They land with a tick you feel in your teeth more than hear. In the cupped hollows of fallen blocks, tiny lenses of water last an hour: sky-colored, dust-filmed, gone by the time the light moves off them. Once a day, if a cloud crosses fast up above, a three-minute rain finds the hole — a sudden vertical pouring, a whole weather system shrunk to the width of a chimney — hisses on the warm stone, fills the cups, and stops. The smell after is iron and moss.

Life should not fit here, and does.

Vegetation is almost nothing. Four ferns, bleached translucent green-white from trying to live on a sunbeam, clinging to a ledge where dust has gathered. A cushion of moss no bigger than your fist, so bright it vibrates. A thread of liverwort along a drip-line. That is all. Great distances — for this scale — between them. Long stretches of bare, wet, newborn stone.

And then the animals. Too many animals.

The walls move if you soften your eyes. Pale cave crickets with legs like eyelashes, dozens, each holding its own territory of stone, spaced exactly apart as if measured. Blind white springtails in the moss like salt spilled. Two small frogs the color of wet concrete, throats pulsing, sitting in separate rain-cups and not looking at each other. Moths — a drift of them, tissue-paper, eyeless — clinging to the dark side, wings dusting the air with scales that catch the shaft and glitter. A salamander, pink-gilled, curled in the dampest crack. Centipedes hunting. Beetles like black seeds.

It is crowded and it is empty at the same time. Abundant, but everyone keeps a careful distance, much clean stone between presences. And it is silent. That is wrong for how much is happening. A centipede takes a cricket three inches from your ear and you hear nothing. No rustle, no squeak. The moths lift and settle without a whisper of wing, like pages turning behind glass. The only sounds are yours — your breath too loud — and the tick, tick of water condensing and falling. The rock eats the rest. The moss, perhaps.

Nothing here is human. There is no tool, no wire, no plastic, no hum. And yet there are buildings.

On the floor, out of the bright ellipse, stand three little chimneys of mud, each as tall as your thumb, each perfectly round. Cricket turrets, built pellet by pellet from rock flour and spit to guard their burrows. Near the moss, a dome woven from rootlets and moth-wing scales, a mouse nest abandoned last season, tight as a chapel. Along the ledge, someone — a beetle? the frogs? — has arranged a spiral of white quartz pebbles, one by one, decreasing in size. No purpose you can name. Faint marks everywhere if you look: claw-script on the soft stone, drag-lines, old egg-cases like tiny paper lanterns. Scattered architecture, made and left by bodies, not hands.

You stay still long enough and the place stops pretending to be normal.

First: the shadows fall wrong. Yours, and the fern's, lean *toward* the light shaft, not away.

Second: the bright ellipse on the floor has weight. When you put your hand into it, your hand is warmer — and heavier — than it should be. The light resists, faintly, like water. The insects walk upside-down on nothing about a foot above it, on a membrane you cannot see but where warm air meets cold: a whole inverted highway of springtails crossing mid-air.

Third: the echo comes early. If you — against the etiquette of the place — click your tongue, you hear the tiny return a fraction *before* you click. As if the room answers in anticipation, to keep the silence intact.

And if you step fully into the ellipse at noon and look up, you will see for a half-second not the grey moor sky through the crack, but another sky — deeper blue, a different hour — and the silhouette of your own head looking down at you from above, before you climbed in. The light here is not just light. It is a place where light has pooled so long it has worn thin into somewhere else.

The rules, as far as you can learn them in an hour on your knees:

1. Keep quiet, or the brightness dims. Sound and light are in opposite pans of a scale here.
2. What falls in stays until the rain. Moth scales, pebbles, your skin cells. Then the little storm resets it, washes the cups clean, erases footprints.
3. Do not take the center for long. It belongs to the cold-blooded at noon — frogs, salamander, beetles all edge inward to bask in the heavy light, then retreat to their spaced stations. There is a truce, and you are part of it while you are down here.

When you climb back out, your knees shaking from crouching, the moor is enormous and loud and ordinary. Wind. Grass. A skylark tearing sound open. Behind you the crack is nothing, a dark line you could step over and miss forever.

Below, the white room goes on ticking, sweating, hunting in silence, building its mud towers in blinding light while it is still young enough to be soft — a whole active world, the size of a held breath, waiting for the next three minutes of rain.
