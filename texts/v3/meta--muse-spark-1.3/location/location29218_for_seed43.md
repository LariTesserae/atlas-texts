Down here the sky is a lid.

You come to it first as an edge — a low parapet at ankle height in a flat glare of dry country, and then the ground simply stops. Not a cave. Not a valley. A made thing, square-cut and stair-stepped, going down.

It is not large. You can shout across it and be heard too well, and you can learn every face in it in a week. Perhaps forty paces across at the rim, narrowing as it descends in broad, uneven terraces, each terrace faced with block and plaster until there is no earth left anywhere to see. Limestone, lime-wash, fired brick patched over older stone, timber props black with smoke and handling. Even the floor at the bottom is paved. If a weed wants to live here it must split mortar or live in a pot. That is the rule: nothing is natural and everything leaks a little anyway.

The heat finds it and stays. Warm air pools in the pit by mid-morning and does not leave until well after dark, held by the mass of sun-baked masonry that gives it back all afternoon. The light is ordinary daylight — no gloom, no crystal — but it behaves differently down here. It comes straight down at noon and then goes to bouncing, wall to wall, until the shadows climb the steps in order and the bottom goes amber and green. From the rim you can see all the way to the water. From the bottom you can see only a cut-out square of blue, crossed constantly by wings.

Water does not fill this place. It haunts it.

There was once, you can tell, more of it — a dark tide-line halfway up the lowest course, mineral-white and moss-black, and the great cistern mouth at the center bottom, a round throat wider than a house door, ringed with rope-grooves where a thousand buckets have been hauled. Now the throat holds only a shallow, ankle-deep pan that never quite dries and never quite rises. What the pit gets instead is damp: seep and sweat and drip.

It sweats from the walls themselves. The stones exhale at night when the air above cools, and just after dawn there is an interior weather that belongs only to the pit — a fine, cold condensation that beads under every lintel and on the undersides of the reed awnings and then falls as a brief, private rain. It pocks the dust, darkens the plaster, fills the foot-worn hollows in the steps for an hour before the sun takes it back. When true rain comes, rarely, the whole hamlet becomes a funnel; the terraces turn to cascades and gutters roar for twenty minutes and everyone is out with jars and hides and cupped hands, laughing, shouting, because for a little while the cistern runs chocolate-brown and full. Then it settles, seeps away through old joints into deeper tanks you cannot see, and the damp resumes.

Nothing is new here and nothing is ruined. That is the other thing you feel in your feet. Every step is dished in the middle where generations of bare feet, goat hooves, wet jars have passed. Handholds at the corners are polished to a greasy shine. Rope has sawn inch-deep notches into the coping stones. Lime plaster has bubbled and been patched and bubbled again, and salt blooms in white beards along the north-facing courses. Swallow nests, built and rebuilt for longer than anyone remembers, follow the same horizontal crack all the way around the third terrace like a frieze. The place has arrived at itself. It erodes only as fast as hands repair it.

And hands are everywhere, because every horizontal inch is occupied. This is a dense place, layered, near-touching.

Look at a wall and it is not a wall. At the bottom: a goat pen made of lashed poles, three brindled goats chewing imported straw. Above it: a ledge with clay cooking hearths, smoke staining the plaster above each in a black tongue. Above that: a reed awning strung on timber, under it a loom clacking, under that a sleeping niche just deep enough for a curled body, with a rolled mat and a copper bowl. Across the void: rope bridges and pole scaffolds, laundry lines, gourd trellises, pigeon lofts made from stacked jars with their mouths knocked out. Pots everywhere — chipped red earthenware crammed with chili, onion tops, basil, a stunted fig kept small as a dog — because soil must be carried down in baskets; there is none to dig. Children run up and down the great stairs with impossible sureness, baskets on heads. Old men sit in doorways that are really old water doors, mending nets that are really pigeon nets.

Vegetation, left to itself, is miserly. Maidenhair fern in the drip-lines, caper bushes exploding sideways from a joint, a slick of moss where a spout never stops weeping, lichen like spilled paint. Everything else is tended, watered by the spoonful from the pan, guarded from goats. Sparse, stubborn, precious.

Animals have no such restraint. They own the vertical.

Pigeons by the hundred — grey, white, rust — pour out of the lofts and wall holes in a clatter that fills the pit like thrown gravel, circle the square of sky, and funnel back. Swallows cut lines through them all day, screaming. At dusk the shift changes and bats take the air, leather-quiet after the day's noise. Lizards, banded and quick, work the sunlit courses for flies. Rats work the night courses for grain. Two lean cats, temple-thin, patrol the fourth terrace and are paid in offal for their patrol. A kestrel has learned the trick of the place: stoop from the parapet, use the walls to bank, take a pigeon in mid-pit while the whole flock thunders in echo. In the pan at the bottom, toads the color of wet mortar sing on warm evenings, and mosquito larvae wriggle in every footprint puddle, and swallows skim them.

It is loud. You have to understand that the shape is an ear as much as a bowl. A bucket clanked at the bottom arrives at the rim as a bell. Goats bleat and it comes back doubled. Pigeon wings, loom beaters, a copper pot dropped, a mother calling a name, a mason tapping with a hand-stone to test plaster — it all stacks and lingers. At midday when everyone is down in the shade of awnings and the goats doze and even the pigeons sit panting with open beaks, there is still the metronome drip from a dozen spouts: *tok — tok — tok*, unsynchronized, amplified. Visitors raise their voices without meaning to, then shout to overcome themselves. Residents have learned to speak low and listen for the return.

Tools here are simple enough to mend with hands: stone hammers, wooden mallets, copper awls gone green, bone needles, coiled rope, reed mats, clay. No wheel, no pulley larger than a hand-span. If something heavy must rise — a block, a water jar, a sick goat — it goes up by rope and back and shoulder, step by step, with chanting to keep time. What goes down must be carried up, including shit, which goes up in sealed jars to the dry fields outside and comes back as onions. Water, by unspoken law, belongs to the bottom. You may dip at the top spouts for washing, but drinking water is drawn from the pan and no one fouls it. You do not dig. There is nowhere to dig to. You maintain.

There is one strangeness, small but persistent, that everyone here takes for granted and visitors notice first: the stones sweat in patterns. On certain mornings the condensation beads not randomly but in lines and whorls that follow old repairs, outlining blocked doors and filled channels you cannot otherwise see, as if the pit remembers its earlier plumbing and draws it in water for an hour. The old women read weather in it — if the ghost-door on the east wall sweats complete before sunrise, rain within three days on the outside world. They are usually right. No one can explain it to your satisfaction. The masons shrug: different mortar, different cold. The pigeon-keepers say the birds know before the stones do; they go quiet an hour before the sweating starts.

By evening the light goes horizontal and cuts the pit in half — one side honey-gold, one side already indigo — and the swallows feed frantically and the toads start and cooking smoke cannot rise straight out but hangs in a blue layer at the second terrace until a night draft pulls it up like a cork. Lamps are lit in niches, little clay saucers of oil, and the walls, all that made, worked, hand-plastered surface, flicker. From the rim, if you lie on your belly and look over, it looks less like a ruin or a well than a room that decided to become a village and dug just deep enough to catch the sky.
