High up, where the air thins and cools, there is a valley that has no ground left in it.

From the rim you would think at first it was natural — a broad, shallow bowl between two blunt ridges, maybe a mile across, filled with grey. But there is no slope, no outcrop, no scree that has not been touched. Everything has been lifted, split, and set back down by hands.

It is bright here past comfort. The light is high-mountain white, unfiltered, bouncing. The sky is a thin washed blue, the sun a hard coin that gives light without warmth. The air is cool enough that your breath stays visible, that your fingertips go dull in minutes, that the water numbs. Below the rim, clouds sit in a flat sea, so the valley feels like an island lifted clear of weather — and yet it is wet. Impossibly wet.

Water is everywhere, and no soil to take it.

It comes not in rivers but in weeping. The whole built bowl sweats. Cold air condenses on cold stone. Fog that you can't quite see ghosts through in the morning and leaves every surface beaded. Rain, when it comes, is frequent and horizontal and brief, and then gone, and still the stones drip for hours after. There is water near the surface everywhere, under every slab, pressing up. You hear it before you see the pattern: a thin, constant stitching of trickles under the quiet.

Because it is quiet. No birds. No insects. Nothing moves on legs or wings. No green at all.

That is the first strangeness you have to adjust to: a place so thoroughly made, and so empty of living things. No moss in the joints, no grass in the cracks, no lichen except a crust so pale and thin it might be mineral stain — ochre, rust-black, sage-grey films that do not rise above the stone. No trees, no shrubs, not even rot. Nothing to rot. The stone is young, fresh-broken, sharp. The ridges themselves feel recently lifted, the fracture faces still glittering where they were split. Edges have not had time to round. You cut your hand if you brace carelessly. The builders — whoever, whenever — worked this fast, fresh rock with stone on stone, and left peck-marks and wedge-notches on every block. No sawn planes. No poured smoothness. No metal. Only split slate and basalt and pale quartzite, fitted dry without mortar, chinked with flakes.

And they fitted everything.

Down in the bowl the density is total. Roof touches roof. Wall becomes terrace becomes stair becomes channel becomes cistern lip. There is no room between presences. Alleys are shoulder-wide slots where two wet walls nearly kiss, and light falls straight down in a white stripe. You do not walk on streets; you walk mostly on roofs, because the structures are low, beehive and box and longhouse-low, all about the same height, stepping down the valley in tiers like scales. Each roof is also a catchment. Each wall is also a dam. The whole town-valley is one machine for slowing water down.

That is the rule here, and once you see it you see it everywhere:

**Every surface sheds to the next.**

A roof slab tilts a finger's width to a groove. The groove runs to a spout — just a hollowed stone tongue sticking out. The spout drips into an open runnel that runs along the top of the next wall down. The runnel drops through a chink into a sunken cistern, round, stone-lined, dark water four feet down, cold as glass. Overflow from the cistern seeps out its base into the next terrace's pavement, which is cambered to sheet-flow to the next groove. Down and down, slow. Patient. There are thousands of these steps, and the fall from top to bottom is maybe three hundred feet, so the gradient is weak, the movement minimal-flux. It takes days for a given cup of water to work its way from rim to sump at the center. You can watch it hesitate at each lip, bulge, break, fall a half-inch with a tap like a clock ticking too slowly to time.

The materials are all local and all cold to the touch. Dark blue-grey slabs that ring when tapped. Lighter rubble packed behind facing stones. Doorways — and there are many doors — are low trapezoids, lintelled with a single massive slab, and most are shut with another slab leaned against them, or open onto blackness that smells only of wet rock. No smoke stain. No hearth. No midden. No carving except the occasional hand-sized cup pitted into a sill where water collects and overflows in a perfect thread. If tools were used here they were hammerstones, antler picks, hide ropes, human backs. You can see where a block was too big and was fire-cracked: a reddened patch, a star of fractures. Primitive work, but endless. To pave a valley this size by hand would take generations.

From the middle distance, standing on a roof near the center sump — a large, still, rectangular pool lined so tight it looks cut from one stone — you can read the other features that make it more than one repeated cell. To the north, a long stepped spine like a causeway, slightly higher than the roofs around it, running rim to rim, its sides weeping in regular vertical streaks. To the east, a cluster of taller cones, beehives with their caps missing, open to the sky, each holding two feet of sky-mirror water inside. To the west, a field of low cairns, each man-high, each exactly as wide as a man with arms out, packed so tight you cannot walk between them, only over their saddles. To the south, the terraces widen into broad shallow pans, ankle-deep after rain, that drain with a skin of movement so slow you only know it moves because your reflection warps.

No animals have left sign. No droppings, no nests in the wall holes, no spider line across the slots. The water in the cisterns is too clean, distilled and stone-filtered, with a faint mineral bite. Nothing swims in it. Nothing skims it. The ecology, if you can call it that, is stone and water and light. The pale crusts grow a millimeter a year. In a still pan, a film of silica forms and cracks into polygons when disturbed and reforms. In the deep shadow of a covered channel, a white fungal fringe hangs like frost, not plant, not animal, just a simpler appetite for damp, and it does not spread beyond the dark.

Sounds are few and therefore large. Your boots squeak on wet slab. Your jacket rasps too loud. Under that: drip — pause — drip from a hundred spouts not in rhythm. A faint hiss where sheet-flow crosses pavement. Wind combing over the rim, cool on the ears, flipping a curtain of seep into spray off a high wall. And at long intervals, a deep, soft *tock* from somewhere underfoot as the whole packed mass settles a fraction, stones finding each other. You feel it more in the soles than hear it.

The light stays bright even as the air stays cool, and that dissonance never resolves. Your eyes water from glare off wet stone while your nose runs from cold. Shadows are short, hard-edged, utterly black in the slots. Wet rock is almost black; dry rock, where wind gets it for an hour, pales to dove grey. So the whole valley flickers as clouds above thin and thicken — dark patches blooming and fading where wind-drying was undone by fresh weep.

If you stay until late, the weirdness sharpens by a degree. Water continues to run, to drip, to fill threads and runnels long after any rain has stopped, long after fog has burned off. The stones themselves seem to exhale. Put your palm flat on a north-facing wall and in a minute there is a damp print of your hand, and then beads beyond it. It is as if the mountain is breathing up through everything that was built on it, and the building, for all its completeness, cannot cap it — only slow it, terrace it, honor it step by step down to the center pool.

Nothing hurries here. The young sharp stone will take ten thousand years to soften. The cistern levels rise a finger in a week. The crusts creep. The water goes down, evaporates in bright cold, condenses again at night on a thousand roofs, and begins again.
