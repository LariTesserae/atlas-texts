High, first. That is the first thing the body knows.

The air is thin and clean to the point of hurting. Light comes down hard and white at noon, without warmth in it. You breathe and get less than you asked for, so you learn to ask for less — shorter steps, slower shoulders. Every exhale hangs a moment in front of you and then is taken by the wind. In an hour the whole city will have exhaled a weather of its own.

Below you, or around you — there is no below, really, because you are already on the roof of the world — the plateau runs out in all directions until distance itself gets tired. Pale gold-brown, grey, white. A vast shallow bowl of frost-shattered stone between young mountains. The mountains are too sharp to be old. They stand up all around like fresh-broken teeth, black rock with snow caught in the cracks, scree slopes that have not yet learned to soften. The wind has not had time to round them. The rivers have not had time to carve them. Everything still has edges.

And across the whole bowl of the plateau: the City.

You do not see it end. At first you think those darker patches on the plain are outcrops, or herds, or low cloud shadows. Then your eyes adjust and you understand scale. It is built. It has been built for a very long time, by many hands, without pause.

Low, dense, sprawling. No towers. Nothing dares to go high here where the wind lives. Instead it spreads and thickens, house against pen against wall against house, for miles and then tens of miles. Dry-stacked stone the color of the ground it was pulled from — slate grey, rust brown, lichen pale green. Flat roofs of sod and packed earth, of felt stretched over willow and bone, weighted down with stones against the wind. Smoke holes. Small dark doors facing away from the wind. Between them, a labyrinth that is not a labyrinth if you live here: drover avenues wide enough for a herd, then footpaths narrow enough that two people in winter coats must turn sideways, then courtyards, then pens, then again houses.

It was not planned. It was accreted. A family built a windbreak, then a neighbor leaned their wall against it to save stone, then someone roofed the gap between to make a stable, then someone else built on top of that stable because the ground was already warm from the animals below. Layered construction, generation over generation, all by hand. You can read it in the walls: older courses at the bottom, mossy and sunken, stones fitted with impossible patience; newer courses on top, sharper, still showing chisel marks from antler picks and stone hammers. No sawn timber — there are no trees up here to saw. Beams are bone, long leg-bones and ribs of the herds, or drift willow carried up from far below, precious as iron. Lashings are hide and sinew. Everything else is stone, earth, dung, felt, skin.

 sparse green holds on where it can. In the cracks between stones, orange and grey lichens like spilled paint. Cushion plants, tight and hard as fists, hugging the ground for warmth. In sheltered south-facing terraces behind walls, small fierce plots of barley and root, no taller than your ankle, leaves thick and waxy against the cold. Outside the city, the steppe is mostly stone pavement and frost-needled soil, with tussocks far apart. Not enough to feed what lives here, you think — until you remember the herds move.

For this is a city of animals first.

They are everywhere, dense as the stones. Shaggy, barrel-chested grazers the size of small bison, with sweeping horns and breath that plumes — the foundation of everything. Flocks of black goats that climb the walls as if walls were cliffs. Long dogs, ribby and silent, curled in doorways for heat. Overhead, and on every roof-ridge, birds: heavy ravens, white ptarmigan in winter dress, small brown finches that live on spilled grain and dung-seeds. In the walls, pikas and voles rustle, storing hay in the thatch. The city rustles and shifts and breathes with them. In a single courtyard you might count forty bodies pressed flank to flank — grazers in the center for warmth, goats on the periphery, dogs along the walls, people moving among them, hands constantly working: milking, combing, checking hooves, carrying dung cakes to dry.

People are outnumbered here and do not seem to mind. They dress like the animals: layers of felt, hide with the hair turned in, faces dark and wind-burned, eyes narrowed against glare. They move slowly. Everyone moves slowly. At this altitude, to hurry is to fall to your knees gasping. So the whole vast urban mass proceeds at a walk, at a herd's pace. A child leading a grazer by a nose-rope walks for an hour to cross what would be three streets elsewhere, and does not think it far.

Water is not a river here. There is no river big enough to see from far. Water is intermittent, secretive. In the morning everything is iron-hard with frost — puddles in hoofprints like lenses, laundry stiff on lines, the edges of felt tents rimed white. By midday, under that hard bright sun, the frost gives up a little. Ice needles in the soil melt and the ground weeps. The roofs drip, one drop at a time, into carved stone gutters and then into covered cisterns. Along the main drover roads run shallow channels, no deeper than your hand, where grey meltwater trickles and is led from pen to pen, from garden to garden. Women break the thin ice with a heel. Animals lick condensation off the north sides of walls where the wind has breathed moist night air onto cold stone. In hollows outside the city there are pockets — small tarns the color of sky, fringed with ice even in summer, where herds drink in turns, patiently waiting. Dampness lives in corners, not in abundance. Enough, if you know how to keep it.

And it is quiet. That is the strangest thing for a city so large, so crowded.

There are no engines, no metal to clang, no wheels on pavement. Sound is swallowed by felt and wool and snow-dust and distance and thin air. What you hear, if you stand still: wind combing over a million low roofs. The soft, continuous crunch of hooves on frozen grit. A low wooden bell, then another, far off. Breathing — so much breathing. A dog shaking out its coat. Stone set on stone as someone, somewhere, mends a wall by hand. A child humming. The trickle of midday water. Ravens calling once, twice, and then deciding not to.

Even the market places, dense shoulder-to-shoulder with people and beasts, are low-voiced. Bargains are done by touch under a blanket, finger-pressure for numbers, because to shout wastes breath.

Nothing here happens fast. Change is glacial, seasonal, generational. A wall takes a summer to build. A herd takes years to grow. Dung for winter fuel is cut in summer, stacked in neat geometric hives to dry, and burned crumb by crumb through the long dark. Barley is harvested one handful at a time with bone sickles. The dead — animal and human — are laid on high platforms outside the city for the birds, and the bones come back as tools and beams. Nothing is wasted because nothing can be replaced quickly.

At dusk the light goes amber and then blue, very fast. The temperature, already cold, drops like a stone. Frost begins to form again on every surface. From every smoke hole rises a thin column of dung-smoke, sweet and acrid. The columns do not dissipate; in the still, heavy evening air they flatten and merge a few hundred feet up into a single man-made cloud that holds the city's heat for a little while. Under it, bodies press closer. Grazers lie down in circles, calves in the middle. Dogs crawl into the circles. People crawl in among the dogs. The stones of the houses, warmed all day by sun and by animal heat, give their warmth back all night.

From a ridge above, you would see lights — not electric, never that, but thousands and thousands of small fat-lamp flames, dung-fire glows in doorways, flickering through hide curtains. They go on to the horizon in every direction, thinning finally into scattered herder outposts, stone bothies with a single pen, then nothing but steppe and sharp young peaks and stars so close and numerous they seem to press down on you.

It does not look possible. A city this big, built without machines, without forests, without rivers, in air that starves the lungs. Sparse moss and lichen for plants, a little barley, and yet tens of thousands — hundreds of thousands — of warm bodies, breathing together.

But it holds. It holds because it is slow, and low, and dense, and patient. Because it learned the rules of this high cold place and obeyed them utterly: keep low against wind. Share walls, share warmth. Move with the herd, not faster. Save every drop of melt. Burn only what the herd gives. Build only what hands can lift. And stay together, flank to flank, until the thin air itself feels, for a moment at midday, almost thick enough to live in.
