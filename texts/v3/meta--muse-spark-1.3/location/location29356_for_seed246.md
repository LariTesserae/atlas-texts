On the high plateau you walk a long way between things.

The air is thin and cold enough to hurt the inside of your nose. Even at midday in the bright season your breath comes out white, and the light — it is not dim, not polar dusk, not underworld gloom — is plain, high, white-gold daylight, the kind that makes edges hurt. The sky sits very close. But the ground does not rise and fall anymore. This country is too old for that.

You are up very high, almost two kilometers above any tree that dares to be a tree, on a table of rock that has been weathered since before weather had a name. Everything sharp has been taken away. The bedrock swells here are rounded like the backs of sleeping animals, polished, pitted with lichen that has had ten thousand years to eat in. There are no cliffs. No peaks to speak of. Only a vast, wind-scoured flatness of pale grit and frost-shattered slabs, with so much nothing in it that sound carries wrong.

And then, in the middle of that nothing, a shallow bowl.

You don't see it until you are almost at its lip because it is so shallow, so settled into its final form. A slight subsidence, perhaps two hundred paces across — hamlet-sized — rimmed by a low collar of smooth, whaleback stones. Inside, the ground changes completely. That is the whole place. You can stand in the center and see its boundaries in every direction. Beyond the collar, emptiness resumes.

Inside the bowl it is green. Impossibly green for this cold.

The first rule here is: cold does not mean barren. It means low.

Nothing grows up. Everything grows *in*. The centers of the ground are mattresses of moss so thick your boot sinks to the ankle and rebounds — haircap and peat and star moss, liverworts like dark green tongues, cushion plants, saxifrage and moss campion pulled into domes as tight and hard as fists. If you kneel and part the moss with your fingers it is a miniature forest in there: stems like red wire, leaves like scales, spore capsules on stalks thin as hair, all beaded with water that never fell as rain. Each cushion holds its own summer. Put your palm flat and you can feel it, a degree or two warmer than the air, damp, breathing.

The second rule is: water is not a river here. It is a breath.

There is no lake in the bowl, no stream that lasts. What there is: condensation. Every night the cold settles, rime feathers the stones, fog pours over the low collar and pools. By morning every moss dome is heavy with it, every stone sweats. As the normal daylight strengthens — not hot, never hot, just bright — the rime melts and runs, not in channels but in seeps, a filigree of trickle that gathers for a few hours in the cracks between things and then is gone. Pockets. A dark mirror the size of your hand here, a black wet gravel throat there. You hear it before you see it. Drip, seep, tick. The stones tick as ice lets go in their pores. If a true rain comes, once every few days, it does not soak so much as ring — it strikes the taut moss and the bare rock and vanishes into the sponge within an hour, leaving the whole bowl steaming faintly in the cold light.

Which brings the third rule, the strangest: this place is built.

No one built it. There is no tool-mark here. No cut wood, no stacked wall, no wire, no glass, no nail. Not even a cairn. And yet from the lip, looking down, you see a town plan.

Frost made it. Over uncountable winters, freeze and thaw sorted the ground the way a careful hand sorts seed — fine soil heaved to the center, stones shoved outward to the edges. Polygon after polygon, cell after cell, a honeycomb of sorted circles and nets, each two or three strides across, each rimmed with a curb of upright, lichen-grey slabs, each center filled with black soil and that furious lush moss. From above they read as blocks and streets. The curbs are the walls. The troughs between them, filled with gravel and intermittent meltwater, are the streets, the gutters, the plazas. In places the earth has humped itself into loaf-shaped mounds — thufur — shoulder-high, moss-roofed, hollow-sounding when you knock them, like houses with no doors. In other places the stones stand taller, frost-jacked into little pillars and tors that catch the wind.

It is urban in density and emptiness at once. That is its contradiction and its truth. Within the bowl, structure is everywhere, organized, layered — you can walk avenues that turn at right angles for no reason except ice physics, you can get lost for a minute between moss-houses. But there is almost nothing *in* it. Vast space between presences. A beetle crossing a plaza is an event. You can stand still for a long time and see no movement except water and light. It is a city with no citizens, hollowed out, waiting.

Because almost nothing lives here that moves. That is the fourth rule. On the plateau the fauna is rumor. Once a day, perhaps, a white ptarmigan cuts low over the collar, wings whistling, and is gone. Stoneflies hatch for an hour in a melt pocket and freeze again. Under a lifted slab you might find springtails, white as salt, popping like static. Once I saw hare tracks pressed into soft moss, the prints filled with clear water, going from one side of the town to the other and out, as if it had just been passing through and did not stop. No herds. No flocks. No predators to hear. The green does all the living, slowly and without permission.

And yet it is loud.

You expect high cold emptiness to be silent. It is not. The bowl amplifies. The wind comes off the flat in a continuous low roar and then breaks on the collar of stones and on the little standing tors, and it splits into voices — whistling through the curbs, humming in the hollow thufur mounds if you put your ear to the moss. The meltwater in the stone gutters talks constantly at midday, a bright chattering under the wind-bass. And underneath, at intervals, a deep, soft *thunk* as a buried needle of ice expands and shoves a slab a millimeter higher. If you lie down with your cheek near the moss you can hear the capsules popping in the sun, thousands of infinitesimal lids flipping open. It is an active place. Freeze, thaw, heave, settle, condense, evaporate, grow a fraction of a millimeter, die back, grow again. Nothing is still, even when nothing is there.

The atmosphere gathers all of this into something you feel in your chest. Damp wool and stone dust and green iron smell. Light that is normal but too clean, so colors vibrate — the orange map-lichens on the curbs almost buzzing against the green. Cold that makes your fingertips ache even while your face is sun-warm. And the weird, quiet insistence that this pattern is *meant*. Walking the gravel streets between the moss blocks, you find yourself lowering your voice, as if you are intruding in rooms. You find yourself not stepping on the centers. The geometry is too good. Some polygons are almost perfectly hexagonal. Some curbs align for ten, twelve cells in a row before joggling. It feels settled, final, as if erosion finished its work long ago and then life moved into the ruins and learned how to be a city without ever inventing a tool.

Stay till late afternoon and you will see the daily unmaking. Fog begins to spill back over the collar. The light flattens to pearl. Condensation starts to bead again on every leaf. The trickles slow and stop. The wind drops half a tone and the stones begin to tick in reverse as ice re-forms. The green town darkens from emerald to black-green and closes. The water pockets skin over with ice thin as a contact lens. The bowl becomes a shallow cup holding cold mist, and the vast empty flat around it disappears entirely.

You are left with a room, a grove, a hamlet of moss and sorted stone, humming faintly in the wind, holding its damp against the enormous dry cold around it — ancient, low, lush, empty, built by no one, busy with its own small weather.

If you leave a footprint in the moss, by morning the frost will have lifted it smooth again.
