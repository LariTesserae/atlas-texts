There is a country that is not on the ground.

From a long way off, below it, you would think it was weather. A flat-bottomed, warm cloud that never quite lifts, sitting too high up where the air should be thin and cold. It is not a cloud. It is a weave.

The Loom — no one now remembers a word older than that for it — is a continent of living braid, kilometers thick in places, strung between mountain spines so distant you could walk a lifetime and not reach the stone. You never reach the stone anyway. There is no stone to reach. Everything that can be touched has been touched, bent, grafted, tied.

It began, the old songs say, with simple tools, and it never got more complicated than that. Stone adze and bone awl and fire-hardened dibble and hands. A cut made to coax, never to fell. A root led over a root. A sapling bent when green and lashed with its own bark-strip to its neighbor, and then to a hundred neighbors, until the bend held after the lashing rotted. Then again. For thousands of years. Generation after generation of small, patient work has made a thing so large it has its own sky.

Because it is all plant. Not forest *on* something. Forest *as* something.

Trunks the diameter of council halls rise — no, are *trained* up — in slow corkscrews for two, three thousand feet, flaring into buttresses that are themselves hollow and inhabited, then splitting into limbs thick as aqueducts that run horizontally for miles before they are allowed to rise again. Between them, the infill: aerial roots as thick as your wrist and as fine as hair, vines, lianas, stranglers, mosses laid upon mosses until the mat is deep enough to hold water. Leaves the size of sails and leaves the size of eyelashes, layered fifty, a hundred deep above you, shingled and stitched with living thread so rain runs where the weavers want it to run. Even the ground is not ground. It is floor. A springy, giving, endlessly repaired lattice of intergrown limbs packed with humus and felted root, and under that more air, and under that another floor.

If you cut down through, which you do not, you would pass floor after floor, chamber after chamber, going down into warmer dark.

For all its mass it is empty.

That is the first shock. You expect jungle press, green crush. Instead you step out of a low root-tunnel onto a braided causeway and the world opens and keeps opening. The great pillars stand so far apart that the mist between them is blue with distance. The voids are the true terrain here — vaulted hollows a mile across, three miles across, where nothing is except air and slow light and a single Gordian cable of vine crossing like a horizon line. You can shout — people don't — and hear nothing come back for a long time. Density is a rumor. A family may tend a territory the size of a lowland county: one hearth-pocket, a few water-knots, a garden ledge, and leagues of tended emptiness they patrol with soft feet to tighten a lashing here, redirect a sucker there.

You learn to read emptiness as built. The absence of tangle *is* the architecture. The air has been cleared by pruning and by weight and by centuries of things falling and being caught lower down.

The light never gets bright. Warm, yes — a bathhouse warmth that sits on your skin despite the altitude. Outside the Weave, above the upper leaf-skin, the wind at fifteen thousand feet is thin and vicious and cold enough to kill. Inside, transpiration and rot keep it summer. The canopy layers steal the sun and give it back as green dusk. Light filters down in shafts the color of weak tea, full of drifting seed-fluff and midges, and fades before it reaches the lower floors. There is no noon here, only a long, amber late-afternoon that dims toward brown at night without ever becoming black. Things glow faintly instead: foxfire on the undersides of limbs, the pale lantern-pods the children hang and tend, the eyes of animals.

Water is everywhere but rarely as river. The air itself sweats. You wake wet. Your shoulders are wet by midmorning from brushing leaves. The great leaves overhead collect cloud and exhale cloud; mist rises from below as the lower rot breathes, meets the cool skin above, condenses, and falls again as a perpetual soft rain that never quite reaches the bottom. It patters from leaf to leaf, gathers into runnels along the trained grooves of branches, runs sideways for a hundred yards along a limb-highway, then drops in bead-curtains from a drip-tip city into a cistern-knot.

Those knots are the lakes of this country. Where three or four massive limbs were forced to grow around each other long ago, the bark cupped and moss sealed it, making perched bogs suspended in air. Black water, warm as blood, rimmed with sphagnum and pitcher plants big enough to drown a dog in. Frogs chorus there without sound — throat sacs pulsing — and eels like green ribbons slip between pools by way of damp moss bridges. Every trail is edged by weeping. Every cut root bleeds clear sap for days. You never dig for water; you listen for the weight of it overhead, and tap.

It smells of wet fiber and sweet rot and crushed leaf and smoke that has nowhere to go.

And it is old. Not wild-old. Settled-old. The bark under your hand on the mainways is polished smooth as riverwood by ten thousand years of bare feet and buttocks and carrying-slings. The drip-channels have worn half a foot deep into wood harder than oak. The lashings you see now are the great-great-grandchildren of lashings, replaced fiber by fiber without ever untying the structure. Nothing here is sharp or new. Even collapses — and limbs as big as ships do sometimes tear loose in storm-growth — do not look like wounds for long. A fall is caught three floors down in a net of daughter roots already growing to meet it; within a decade the wreck is a garden, within a century it is a thickening in the floor. The Weave is in balance with itself, healing at the pace of wood.

That pace is the motion of the place. It is not still. It is active in the way a body is active while sleeping.

Things grow audibly, if you put your ear to them: a creak, a tick. Tendrils advance inches a night, seeking the tension-stakes left by tenders. Leaves track the dim sun in slow unison, turning whole ceilings from dark to silver-green over an afternoon. Storms of a sort pass through the voids — pressure differentials between warm lower rot and cool upper skin send fogs rolling for miles, white walls that soak you in minutes and vanish. Flocks move through them. Moth migrations like slow snowfall, each wing as wide as your hand, silent. Beetles clicking only with their shells, no voice. Birds here do not sing. They have no need and perhaps no air to spare; they whistle with feathers, a low thrumming as slotted primaries cut damp air, signaling in rhythms you feel in your sternum.

Fauna is everywhere once you learn to see quietly.

Grazers the size of tapirs, dappled and six-limbed, with prehensile lips for stripping moss, moving in loose herds along the high limbs, stepping with impossible care. Their calves are parked in cradle-forks while mothers feed. Their predator is a long, flat, leaf-green cat-snake that does not chase but grows — it lays itself along a trail for weeks, moss growing over its back, until a hoof brushes it. Then a blur, then silence again. There are frog-things in every cistern, salamanders in the drip-curtains, fish that live in bark-pools and walk on stiffened fins to the next pool during fog. And people, sparse as owls. Dark-skinned, barefoot, with sap-stained hands, moving without talking, carrying stone blades and coils of living cord to graft. Their villages are not clearings but tightenings — a dozen hammock-pods hung in a fork, a smoke-ledge, a child-tended nursery of air-plants braided into toys and snares. You can walk three days on a tended way and meet no one, only the signs: a freshly tightened knot, a redirect leaf pinned with thorn, a footprint filling slowly with water.

It is quiet because the Weave eats sound. Moss an inch thick on every surface, humus pockets, layered leaves — they take footfall and voice and give back almost nothing. People learned long ago not to waste effort. They speak in hand-signs across voids, in rope-tugs along tension-lines, in low hums felt through wood. The loudest regular sound is water: the universal tick-tick-tick of drip from ten thousand tips, the sudden soft rush when an upper leaf dumps its load, the glug of a cistern overturning in rain. After that, the creak of living tension, the soft pop of pods, the leathery shuffle of moth wings passing like a thought.

And then the wrongness, which everyone accepts the way you accept weather.

The Weave is too big and too hollow and too high for the world to sit straight in it. Surveyors — patient old women with knotted cords — will tell you, without mysticism, that distance cannot be trusted here.

Walk a straight limb Causeway for an hour outward, count your paces, turn and walk back: you will return in three-quarters of the time, as if the way back were downhill though the limb is level. Sleep in certain fork-hollows and you dream the memories of the tenders who slept there a hundred years before, not as vision but as muscle habit — you wake knowing how to tie a knot you never learned. Water sometimes falls *up*. In the largest voids, on warm evenings after fog, columns of mist detach from the bog-knots below and rise too fast, too straight, humming faintly through feather-slots, and carry small frogs and leaves with them to the ceiling where they stick and start new gardens.

Gravity leans. Everyone knows that down is mostly down, but near the oldest, thickest braids — the Mother Cables, wider than rivers, humming with sap — down leans a few degrees toward the wood, as if the mass of living water pulled at you. Plumb-bobs hung there do not hang true. Old tenders navigate not by sight in the deep dusk but by the pull on their inner ear and by the tension-singing of cords: a healthy load-bearing vine thrums at a pitch you can feel in your teeth if you bite it.

Most uncanny are the returns. There are loops in the Weave where topology has grown through itself — a limb grafted to its own granddaughter, a tunnel of roots that passes through a knot that is also above it. Enter with a lit lantern-pod and walk steadily; you will come out behind yourself, see your own back fading ahead, and arrive back at the entrance to find your pod still warm where you left it, though you have been gone an hour. Hunters use these places to rest: game will not follow through. Children are forbidden them until they can tie their own lifeline, because occasionally someone comes back *thinner*, as if a part of them decided to keep walking.

No one calls this magic. The rule here is simple and total: *everything was made, and everything is alive, and living things do not hold shape the way stone does.* Shape drifts. You tend, you do not command. You build with stone tools because metal would poison the sap and because, frankly, stone is enough when you have centuries. A cut is a suggestion. A lashing is a prayer the plant may or may not answer over fifty years.

So you learn the manners: never cut a load-line without planting its replacement first. Never dam a drip, only divert. Never shout in a nursery void — not from superstition, but because the startled moths will rise in thousands and clog the cistern filters for weeks. Piss only in the rot-pockets, where it feeds heat. Bury the dead in the floor-gardens, shallow, so the roots take them quickly and the way stays springy. Leave emptiness empty; the voids are load-bearing as surely as pillars.

At night — if you can call it that when the amber only deepens — the whole country ticks and sighs and settles, dripping and growing. Far above, beyond the leaf-skin, real wind screams over the top, and stars, thin and furious with altitude, wheel over a sea of leaves. Far below, between the lowest hanging root-curtains, you can sometimes glimpse through a rent in the fog the terrible far-down world of rock and cold rivers, impossibly distant, irrelevant.

Up here it is warm, and dim, and wet, and hollow, and endlessly, patiently made. You put your palm to a Mother Cable and feel sap move under bark like blood in a throat, and the faint vibration of someone, miles away, tightening a knot, and the answering tremor of the plant taking the suggestion, and you understand that you are not in a forest and not in a city but in something that refused to choose, and grew large enough to forget the difference.
