It is not big. You can see the whole of it at once, if you stand right.

A shallow saddle high up, hung between two long shoulders of old black rock. Below, on both sides, the world falls away into blue heat-haze valleys, but up here the air stays put. Warm and close. Thin enough that climbing the last ten steps leaves you breathing through your mouth, thick enough that your shirt sticks to your back within a minute. A high tropical hanging garden that never quite dries.

The bowl is maybe a stone's throw across — a room with no roof. On three sides low ribs of basalt rise like the rims of old bowls, rounded, lichen-crusted, softened by an uncountable number of wet seasons. They are not cliffs. Nothing here is raw or new. The stone has settled into being stone. Cracks have become soil. Soil has become sponge. On the fourth side the lip drops, but even there a tangle of roots and fallen slabs slows any water that tries to leave, so most of it doesn't leave. It stays.

The ground gives underfoot. Not mud, not quite meadow — a deep mat of liverwort and peat and rotted leaf, saturated from an inch down. Every depression holds water, tea-colored, sky-reflecting. Put your weight down and water sighs up around your boot and then, slowly, sucks back down. Between the wettest hollows rise little dry islands, only a hand high, where the peat has heaped over old rock.

And on those islands, spaced far apart, the lush things grow.

That is the first strangeness — how lush and how empty at the same time. This is not jungle. There is a great deal of air between one living thing and the next. Each plant has its own circle of emptiness around it, as if by agreement.

A dozen, maybe fifteen great rosettes. Leaves as long as your arm, as wide as your chest, thick, waxy, veined like hands, a green so saturated it looks almost blue in the folds. They cup upward to catch rain, and they are always full. Ferns grow in the axils, orchids cling to the undersides, moss beards everything that does not move. A few crooked trees — no taller than two men — stand alone near the rock ribs, their trunks swollen with stored water, their crowns flattened by wind into umbrellas. Their bark is smooth and peeling in wet paper strips. Everything that lives here looks well-watered to the point of bursting, and alone.

Nothing has been built here. No wall, no cairn, no path even — only the faint compression where deer or people once stepped and the moss has since erased. If you hunt, you will find exactly one made thing in the whole saddle: a flake of pale chert half-sunk in peat by the middle pool, its edge still sharp under the green film. Someone sat here once, struck a stone twice to make an edge, cut something, and left. Years ago. The moss is already closing over it. The place otherwise has no use for tools, though the materials for them lie everywhere — straight sticks, loseable stones that fit the hand too well.

It is always wet, even when it is not raining. Especially when it is not raining. The rocks weep.

The basalt ribs sweat beads that grow, tremble, and fall without any rain to feed them. The underside of every leaf is beaded. The air itself feels on the edge of condensing — warm breath you can see if you look sideways into the light, though the light is perfectly ordinary midday light, white-gold, falling straight down through a thin veil of high cloud. You expect, at this height, coolness, clarity. Instead, a low warm cloud clings to the saddle itself, not as fog but as humidity made almost visible, catching on your eyelashes, salting your lips with mineral water.

So the sound. It is loud here, disproportionately loud for a place with almost nothing moving.

A thousand drips out of sync. Plink from rock lip to pool. Thock from leaf tip to leaf below. The glug and gurgle of water moving under the peat mat where you can't see it. The sudden heavy slap when a rosette, overfull, tips and empties a cupful all at once. Wind combing the umbrella crowns makes a dry rustling above all that wet percussion. It fills the little room and bounces off the ribs, so a drip behind you sounds like it is in front of you. You find yourself turning for animals that aren't there.

Because there are almost none. A place this green should hum and crawl. It doesn't. No swarm, no flock. Once an hour a small brown bird crosses the saddle in a straight line, as if unwilling to land. High up, swifts cut the air with thin screams that do not echo. In the pools, if you crouch very still, you will see the faint pulse of a frog's throat — one frog per pool, widespread, patient, the color of peat. That is all. The lushness is vegetable, mineral, aqueous. The animal world has mostly declined the invitation.

Everything happens slowly. Nothing erodes anymore, nothing builds. Water does not rush; it seeps, it hangs. Watch a bead on a rock for a full minute and you will swear it has not grown, then blink and it is gone, fallen. The level of the middle pool rises and falls over weeks with some deep breathing of the mountain's water table, leaving pale bathtub rings of silica on the stones. Fallen leaves do not rot quickly here despite the wet; they bleach and flatten into paper and become part of the mat, legible for months. Time is measured in saturation.

Stay a while and the other strangeness settles in.

The drips are too cold. On your warm skin, each drop lands with a shock disproportionate to its size, as if it fell from much higher and colder than the leaf above you. And the water holds light a beat too long — a bead will catch the sun and keep it for a second after the sun has gone behind cloud, a tiny lingering lamp. If you cup your ear, the bowl has a low tone underneath the dripping, a hum you feel more in your sternum than hear, that stops when you hold your breath, as if it were your own blood, but returns when others listen too.

The rules here are simple and unnegotiable:

You will not get dry. The rock, the leaf, the air will find a way to wet you again.

You will not get lost. The boundaries are always visible — black rib, blue drop, green rosette — a room you can cross in forty paces.

You will not see change, only maintenance. The water leaving by seep equals the water arriving by sweat. The plants do not spread and do not retreat. They hold their circles.

You will make noise no matter how careful you are, and the place will make more noise than you, covering you.

When you leave, climbing down out of the warm wet breath back into ordinary thin mountain air, your boots will be heavy for a hundred steps, and then light again. Behind you the dripping continues, loud, patient, unheard by anyone, keeping the little saturated room exactly itself.
