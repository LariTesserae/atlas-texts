It is low country, though you would not call it flat.

From the first rise — and there are only first rises here, nothing ever gets high enough for a second look — the valley opens the way a hand opens: wide, dark, palm-up to the sky. Town-sized, valley-sized. Far enough that the far edge disappears not in distance but in weather. Close enough that you can see it is all the same thing repeating.

At first you think it is water. Then you think it is stone. Then you stop correcting yourself.

The ground is young. That is the strangest thing to feel under your boots. It has not been smoothed. No river has had time to forgive it, no wind has sanded it down. Everything is fresh-broken. Black rock, blue-grey rock, glass rock that catches what little light there is and holds it for a half-second too long. Slabs tilted up like teeth. Shards standing on edge. Chimneys and blades and bowls, all near-touching, all crowded. You cannot put your foot down without touching three or four separate stones, and between the stones there is no soil, no moss, no grass — nothing green at all, not a root, not a seed. This place is before that, or after that. Mineral only.

It is dense here in a way a forest is dense, but without any living to do the crowding. Thousands of presences per glance. Layer on layer: the sharp field underfoot, the shallow pans of water between, the low pillars rising out of the pans, the mist lying on the pillars, the low cloud lying on the mist. Everything nearly touches everything else.

And it is wet. Not with rivers, not with rain, exactly — though it rains often enough that the rock never fully dries. It is wet from underneath and from inside the air itself. The water table breathes just below the surface; you can see it in every hollow, black mirrors that shiver when you step near. The rocks weep. That is the only word. Their sides are always beaded, always running with slow trickles that gather, hang, fall. The air is so saturated that your jacket, your hair, your eyelashes bead too. Breathe out in the cool and your breath does not dissipate. It joins. Condenses. Becomes part of the general damp that never lifts.

Cool, always. Not killing cold. Work-with-gloves-on cold. Numb-fingertip cold. Stone-sucks-the-heat-from-your-palms cold. Your sweat goes clammy and stays.

The light never fully arrives. It is late-afternoon-during-an-eclipse dim, a grey-blue that flattens shadow and makes distance unreliable. There is no sun, only a brighter smear where the sun ought to be, diffused through kilometers of low, unmoving cloud. Colors go muted except where water catches them — then the black rock flashes bottle-green, oil-purple. In the pans, the sky stares back at you, paler than itself.

You hear it long before you understand it.

It is loud here. Unreasonably loud for a place where nothing moves quickly.

From the rise it is a low roar, like a highway or a distant falls. When you descend into it, it breaks apart into its sources and does not get quieter. Everywhere: ticking, plinking, dripping, gurgling, sighing. Water moving a centimeter at a time through a thousand stone throats. Wind caught and whistled and fluted through sharp holes and narrow gaps between slabs — the young rock is full of natural pipes and it plays them constantly, low notes you feel in your molars. And underneath, a deeper resonance when you stand still: your own weight pressing water out of the ground into new channels, stone settling a millimeter against stone with a hollow clunk that carries.

Nothing hurries. That is the rule. The drips fall one by one by one. A pool takes a season to rise a knuckle's width. The accretion on the rocks — pale crusts of silica, rust-orange films that are not plant, not lichen, not anything you have a name for, just a slick living chemistry — grows slower than fingernails. Even the mist moves slowly, not drifting so much as thickening and thinning where it stands, condensing on one pillar, evaporating off another. The whole valley is patient to the point of indifference. It will still be weeping here long after your boots have rotted.

Because there is almost nothing alive to witness it. No trees, no shrubs, no grass to soften the sound. Occasionally — rarely — something moves. A pale, eyeless crustacean flicks in a pool and is gone. A high, thin bird crosses the grey without landing, crying once, and the sound is swallowed by the louder water. At night, if you crouch very still, you might see the films pulse faintly, iridescent, a kind of non-biology or proto-biology laying down color in rings. It is not vegetation. It does not photosynthesize so much as precipitate. Do not mistake it for life as you know it.

And the structures. You will notice them only after your eyes adjust to the repetition, because they are made of the same rock.

Scattered, half-sunk among the blades: low cells, beehives, windbreaks. Dry stone stacked without mortar, without tool marks, without metal or wire or anything made. One or two courses still standing, most collapsed back into the field they came from. Inside one you find a flat slab for sitting, a niche where water has been allowed to gather into a drinking bowl. No hearth. No trash. No path leading to or from. As if someone, with bare hands and a lot of time, tried to make a brief dryness inside the wet, and then stopped. Or as if the stones themselves occasionally, by chance, fall into the shape of shelter.

Do not expect them to be warm. They are colder inside than out, the wind cut but the damp doubled. But in the loudness, they are strangely quiet — the stacked walls break the whistle just enough that you can hear your own pulse.

If you stay, you learn the etiquette of the place:

Step only on what is already wet. The dry-looking edges are the sharpest; they are still fresh enough to cut leather. They also ring the loudest when struck, and the sound carries wrong.

Do not drink from the stillest pools. Drink from the weeping sides, where the water has just been born out of rock and air. It tastes of stone and cold iron.

Do not stack stones. There are already too many, too close together. Adding yours will not be noticed, and somehow that feels worse than being forbidden.

And do not try to wait for change. You will swear nothing moves, then look down and find the water has risen over the toe of your boot without you seeing it rise. The valley does everything slowly except surround you, which it does all at once.

By the time you climb back to the first rise, your clothes are heavier. Everything you brought is beaded. Behind you the field ticks and roars and weeps, dense and dim and treeless, extending past where you can see, making its patient, loud music out of stone and shallow water — a young place, newly made, still sharp, still deciding what, if anything, it wants to become next.
