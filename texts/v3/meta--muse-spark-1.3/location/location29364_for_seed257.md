You climb for a day to swim for three meters.

That is the first rule of the place. The path goes up and up through thin, cold air, switchback over bare scree, until the hill flattens to a rim — and there, sunk into the summit like a tooth socket, is the water. Warm. Black. To the brim.

It is not a lake. It has edges. It ends where hands ended it.

The High Tank. The Drowned Court. The old ones on the lower slopes have other names, but they all mean *the room full of rain at the top of the world*.

From above, in the last grey light before you duck under, you see it whole because it is small enough to see whole — hamlet-small, courtyard-small, a single made hollow you could cross in thirty slow strokes. An irregular oval, maybe forty paces long, walled all around with a parapet at chest height to keep the mountain out. Inside the parapet, steps go down. And down. And down, until the dark takes them.

Everything is made. That is the second thing you learn with your feet. There is no shore, no mud, no rock showing through. The bottom is tile. The walls are block. The steps are block. Even where the plaster has slumped off in sheets, underneath is only more dressing — chisel-marked limestone fitted so tight a fingernail won't go in. The builders faced the whole summit. They lined the world. Generations of hands with stone mauls and copper chisels and baskets on ropes. No quarry is visible anymore; the hill itself was eaten to make its own lining.

And it is old. Not ruined — settled. That is different. Erosion here has not broken, it has softened. Every arris is a pillow. Every step sags in the middle like a hammock, worn by ten thousand bare feet and then by ten thousand years of warm water moving a finger's width a day. The mortar joints stand a little proud because the stones around them have dissolved back. If you run your palm along a wall you feel scallops, solution pockets, the slow licking of water that has sat here longer than any lineage. Corners have become coves. Doorways that once led out to the sky now lead only to more water, their lintels thick with travertine cauliflower. The place has reached its final shape and decided to stay.

You slip in. The warmth is a shock after the wind.

It is bath-warm, blood-warm,  body-warm without being hot — the kind of warmth that erases the boundary of your skin after a minute. High water stays cold, usually. Not here. The tank holds its heat. The stone itself gives it back. Whether it is the sun trapped under the lid of still water, or the press of many bodies, or something deeper sweating up through the tiles, no one remembers. It simply does not cool. Steam stands over it on cold mornings in a low, flat sheet, and then is gone.

Light does not go in. That is the third rule.

There was once an open sky over it. There are still the stumps of the roof — six massive slabs laid across the span to check evaporation, the old priests said, now collapsed inward at odd angles, leaving only three choked oculi, round as wells. By day a little grey-blue pushes down through them in tilted columns and dies four arm-lengths down. By night, nothing. It is almost always night inside. Your eyes open wider and learn nothing. After an hour you stop trying to see and start to feel the room with your cheeks, your fingertips, your hair.

What you feel is crowd.

The water is dense with others, near-touching. This is not empty holy water. It is inhabited to saturation. Shoals of pale loaches, blind and fat as thumbs, brush your ribs in a single decision and turn. Whiskered catfish the length of a forearm work the steps with their barbels, mouthing the biofilm. White salamanders with external gills like ferns hang in the water column without moving, waiting. Freshwater shrimp come in clouds that part around your hand and close behind it. Crabs — small, bleached, long-clawed — cling to the walls in thousands, shoulder to shoulder in the pits, clicking very softly when you come too close. Snails the size of lentils cover the ceiling slabs underneath, rasping.

No green. Nothing green has ever held here. No weed, no moss, no algae string. The light forbids it and the old law forbids bringing it. What grows instead is animal and simpler than animal: a grey-white felt of fungus and bacteria over every stone, slick as tongue, which the snails and shrimp mow, which the catfish crop, which the eels stir. The water tastes faintly of iron and skin. The food chain is detritus, slime, grazer, predator — and then, at the top, slow, diving people.

Because there are people. You hear them before you feel them — which is to say you almost don't hear them.

Sound here is a failure. Water should carry sound, but this warm, still, enclosed water swallows it. A kick two bodies away is a soft displacement. A stone tool tapped on tile is a dull *tok* that dies instantly. What remains is close: your own heartbeat in your ears, the sigh of bubbles leaving a nose, the faint rasp of a shell on stone, the brush of a fin on your calf. The divers learn to move without sound at all, to conserve. They hang motionless for minutes.

They live amphibious between water and ceiling. That is the unusual thing, the one tilt in the world: there is no air in the Tank except what the Tank keeps for itself. Under the collapsed roof slabs and in the vaulted corners of the old gatehouse, pockets are trapped — shallow domes of stale, warm air, silver-black from below. The divers know them all by touch: Pocket of the First Step, Pocket of the Broken Lintel, Long Pocket that runs like a lung along the north wall. They rise without splashing, nose up into the pocket, breathe slowly, mouth the condensation off the stone, and sink again. Children are taught the map of airs before they are taught names.

Their tools are simple because nothing else works here. Woven milkweed rope, knotted every cubit, strung from parapet to slab as hand-lines — you follow them in the dark. Stone sinkers grooved for a toe-loop. Bone fish-gorges, shell scrapers, gourds stoppered with wax for carrying air down to work a crevice. Clay oil lamps are found in niches, rows of them, unlit for centuries; fire has no place under the surface. Copper awls green-black with age, kept because copper does not rot. Everything is tethered to a wrist with cord. If you drop it, you lose it to the felt and the crabs.

Movement is slow. It has to be. Stir the silt — the fine lime powder that never quite settles — and you blind what little touch-sense you have for an hour. Breathe too fast and you waste the pocket. Swim too hard and the loaches scatter and do not return for a day. So the work of tending the Tank goes at the pace of accretion: scraping felt, lifting fallen blocks a finger's width a year with levers and bladders, re-laying a tile, feeling the walls for a new seep. For there is always seep. The level breathes.

Once a day, though no inlet has ever been found and the oculi are choked, the whole small sea rises the breadth of a hand and falls again. Plaster marks, renewed with charcoal, record it on the gate pier — thousands of overlapping lines. No one knows if the mountain inhales or the sky presses or the stone exhales heat. It just breathes, slow as sleep.

If you stay long enough, your sense of up and high inverts. You know intellectually you are on a summit, that below you the slopes fall away for three thousand feet to cold rivers. But your body learns a different geography: a warm, dark, bounded room where down is tile and up is stone and air, where the horizon is never more than an arm's reach away, where presences are layered — shrimp over snail over crab over catfish over human — all suspended in the same amniotic volume, all patient.

It does not feel like drowning. It feels like being held inside something very old that was made by hands to hold water, and then the water decided to hold everything else.

When you climb out at last, shivering in the wind, the cold air hurts your teeth. The parapet drips. Behind you the Tank closes over your entry without a ripple within seconds, black glass again, steaming faintly. From the outside it is nothing — a low walled oval on a bald hill, full to overflowing with night.

You can put your hand in it and feel them down there, moving slowly, brushing past each other in the dark, tending the softened stones.
