You wouldn't find it if you weren't looking for shade.

Up here the world has finished becoming. This is old rock, older than shape — a high tableland worn down to almost nothing, pink-grey granite rubbed smooth as molars, the joints between boulders softened and filled with red dust. From a distance it looks flat and empty. A dry pavement under a too-close sky. Wind moves over it in one long sheet and doesn't stop. Nothing tall has grown here for a million years. Only lichen, black and orange and pale green, mapping the stones, and quartz veins standing up a finger's width proud because everything around them has washed away.

Warm, for this high. The sun at noon is ordinary — white-yellow, normal light, no strange suns, no purple filter. That is part of what makes it disorienting. The air is thin enough that climbing twenty steps leaves you breathing through your mouth, but the stone radiates heat into your knees and palms. It smells like hot dust and ozone.

And then, in the middle of that flatness, the ground gives. Not a valley. Not a crater. A cup.

A shallow bowl, maybe forty paces across, six or seven deep at its center, as if a giant had pressed a thumb into clay while it was still soft and the rock had remembered. You drop down into it out of the wind and the temperature jumps five degrees. The sound jumps with it.

From above it was silent except wind. Down here it is loud.

First you hear the hum. Low, through the soles of your boots. Then the insects take over — an unbroken, layered roar. Not one swarm but hundreds, stacked on different frequencies: the high electric needle of midges in columns over the damp, the sawing of crickets from inside the moss, the wet-motor drone of jewel-green flies. Under that, frogs. Dozens of them, throats puffing in the shade, calling in bursts that overlap until you can't separate one voice. And birds, high and sharp, cutting through. The bowl focuses it. The curved stone throws the sound back at you so it feels like it's coming from inside your skull.

This is the damp place in a dry world. There is no stream. No lake. Water here doesn't flow, it appears.

Three arches stand in the cup, and they are the reason.

They are not large — the tallest barely reaches your chest — slender loops of something matte black that is not metal and not stone, half-sunk in peat, set at odd angles to each other like thrown quoits. No seams. No base. No cables. Cold to the touch even at noon, and if you rest a hand on one you feel it all the way up your arm: a deep tuned vibration. In the morning they are beaded with water that was not there the night before, fat droplets running together and falling, and falling, and falling. They make rain out of thin air. By mid-afternoon the cloud that has built over the plateau every day for ten thousand years snags on the lip of the bowl, and for twenty minutes there is a real, vertical shower, hissing on hot rock, filling the rock-pocks. Then sun again. Steam rises. The arches go back to sweating.

No one built them recently. Around them are the other marks — fainter, human-scale and failing. A low ring of stacked slabs, collapsed on one side. A cairn with a rusted piton driven through it. A square of dark soil where a tent or shelter was pegged a long time ago and the peat still remembers. Someone left a coil of wire, a chipped mug, a strip of solar cloth now brittle as a leaf. Offerings, or just litter. The arches ignore them.

Where the water lands, life is lush and feral, but sparse — that's the pattern here. Not a carpet. Islands.

Between the islands is bare, warm stone and red grit that crunches underfoot. You can walk five steps and touch nothing living. Then your foot sinks to the ankle in moss.

The moss forms deep cushions, emerald and rust, so thick they hold your footprint and slowly exhale it back. Out of them rise things that should not be this high or this hot: dwarf trees no taller than your knee, their trunks twisted horizontal by old winds that no longer reach them, their crowns dense with waxy leaves and white orchids that smell like vanilla and rot. Pitcher plants crowd the wettest hollows, throat-open and drowned — each one half-full of brown water and buzzing wings, their lips slick pink. Sundews glitter. A fern unrolls from a crack that is always in shadow.

Nothing is alone for long because the fauna is overwhelming for a space this small.

The air above each moss island shimmers with midges, thousands rising and falling in a single column that holds its shape even when you wave through it. Jewel wasps — blue-black, the size of your thumbnail — work the pitchers, drunk and fearless. Tiny amber frogs, no bigger than a knuckle, sit in rows along the arch-bases where the drip is steadiest, throats pulsing, eyes like wet beads. They don't hop away when you come; there are too many of them to be afraid individually. Higher up, hovering: two or three hummingbirds, copper throats flashing, so territorial they scream at each other and at you, the slap of their wings audible over the insects. And under the leaf litter, things moving — beetles with mirror backs, pale centipedes, a mouse-like thing you glimpse only as a ripple in the moss.

It's a complete food web compressed into a room. You can watch it happen in an hour: fly drowns in pitcher, frog snaps fly, bird takes frog, mite eats bird's dropped feather. Multiple cycles turning at once, fast because warm, because wet, because small. The edges of the moss are always advancing a millimeter and retreating a millimeter. A pitcher opens. A pitcher rots. A frog clutch hatches in a rock-pool no deeper than a saucer and is gone by evening — eaten or evaporated.

That's the rule of the cup: nothing leaves. Water condenses, falls, is drunk, is breathed out, condenses again. A feather falls and is unmade by afternoon. Even sound stays, circling the bowl.

And the strange part — it is not very strange, which makes it stranger — is the tuning.

The frogs don't call randomly. If you sit long enough to let your own breathing slow, you hear it: their pulses line up with the hum in the arches. Three beats, pause. Three beats, pause. The midge columns lean, all of them, very slightly toward the nearest arch, not toward the sun. The dwarf trees lean that way too, though the light is straight overhead. When the daily shower ends, the arches ping as they cool — a clear, glassy note — and for a second every insect goes silent at once, as if listening, before the roar slams back louder.

Your heartbeat does it too, if you stay. You notice on the way out, climbing back up into the wind and the huge empty flat, your pulse still keeping that three-beat time for a few steps after you've left the water behind, until the dry air takes it out of you.

From the rim, looking back, it's almost nothing. A darker smudge in the pavement. A pocket of green you could cover with a house. The wind wipes the sound clean off the top — two steps up and the roar is gone, replaced by that long thin hiss over stone. The plateau stretches to every horizon, ancient, settled, flat, indifferent.

Below you, hidden in its little room, the cup keeps sweating, dripping, buzzing, eating itself alive in the warm ordinary sun.
