You see the edge of it before you see it — a low levee, a chain-link fence gone slack with rust, and beyond that a flatness that doesn't rise.

It is not large. You could walk it end to end in ten minutes if you didn't have to stop for the puddles. Maybe nine buildings in all, brick sheds and one taller mill with its windows punched out, set far apart from each other on a cracked concrete apron like teeth with gaps. Between them: nothing but open yard, pipe-racks, a canal that moves too slow to be called moving. From any doorway you can see all the other doorways. From the middle you can see where the place stops. That is the size of it — a hamlet that was once organized to be bigger.

At first you think it's abandoned.

Then the sound corrects you.

Low first — a thrum you feel in your sternum before you hear it. The pump house, the mill engine, the transformer on its pole all humming the same held note, day and night. They never rev and never idle down. On top of that, layered: chain clatter, a loose corrugated panel banging its own arrhythmic time, water ticking off a hundred pipe joints onto concrete, and above and through everything, animals. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands.

There is no green here. Not a weed in the concrete seams, not moss on the north walls where by all rights there should be moss. The chemistry is wrong for it — oil in the soil, ammonia whitewash of guano on every ledge, cold, and a constant tremor from the engines that shakes seedlings loose. What grows instead is mineral: rust blooming in orange lichen-circles on steel, salt whiskers on brick, white dripstone of pigeon lime under every girder. A post-life, constructed-only palette. Grey concrete, black oil-rainbow in the puddles, red brick gone soft at the corners with a century of weather, white everywhere the birds have sat.

And the birds have sat everywhere.

Pigeons mostly, slate and checkered and rust-red, packed shoulder to shoulder along every pipe, every catwalk rail, every window mullion of the mill. Gulls — big herring gulls with pink legs — standing in the puddles and screaming. Starlings in strings that lift and settle and lift again. When you step into the yard the sky takes off. Not in panic. In circulation. They wheel between the sheds in a thick, low river that never empties, wings slapping, thousands of wingbeats making a wind of their own that smells of cold feathers, hot oil, wet stone. The air between the sparse buildings is not empty at all. It is saturated with flapping bodies. You duck without meaning to.

Below them, the other citizens: rats trotting the canal edge with complete indifference, moths — pale, winter-moths — orbiting the sodium yard lamps that have been left burning even though it is daytime. Midges in columns over the still water. The lamps hum. The moths never land, never die, or if they do another takes the orbit. Everything hums.

It is cool here. The kind of cool that lives low to the ground. Your breath shows. The brick sweats. Every cold pipe is beaded with condensation that gathers, swells, and drops in a metronome tick onto the puddles below, and the puddles — shallow, oil-skinned, reflecting the white sky and the wheeling black specks — never get bigger and never dry up. The canal is the same level always, lapping one inch below the green stain on the concrete. It rains sometimes, a thin misting rain you barely feel, and then it stops. The damp remains.

This is an old, settled place. Nothing is collapsing, nothing is being built. The steps are worn into smooth troughs in the middle. The handrails are polished where hands used to be and now where feathers polish them. The bricks have lost their sharpness. The engines have worn themselves and their housings into a perfect fit, so they run without friction, without progress. The mill's belts turn. The pump pumps water from the canal back into the canal. A fan in a broken louver turns and turns, moving the same cool air.

That is the strangeness, when you stay long enough to notice it — and you will stay, because the noise pins you at first and then erases itself:

Nothing changes.

A pigeon lands on a valve wheel. The wheel shudders a quarter-turn under its weight. Steam sighs. The pigeon takes off. Another pigeon lands. The wheel shudders back. The belts carry nothing. The hooks on the overhead chain come around empty, around again. The gulls scream the same scream. The starling river flows clockwise around the mill, counterclockwise around the pump house, figure-eighting forever without tangling. If a feather falls, and feathers are always falling, sifting down like slow snow and lodging in the puddles and on your shoulders — if a feather falls, there is already another in the air to replace it.

Perched, they all face the same way. You will check this three times because you won't believe it. On the long pipe-rack, two hundred pigeons, all facing east. On the mill sills, all facing east. As if listening to the hum that comes from everywhere.

The rules here are simple and you learn them with your body:

Stay out of the drip lines unless you want to be wet.
Don't look for a center — the yard between the buildings is the center.
Don't wait for quiet, or for a shift whistle, or for night to clear the sky. The lamps will stay on. The engines will stay at that one note. The birds will stay in the air.

It is loud, and cold, and damp, and utterly stable — a small industrial town that stopped needing people and kept, instead, the machines and the flocks, balanced against each other like two weights on a scale that never tips.
