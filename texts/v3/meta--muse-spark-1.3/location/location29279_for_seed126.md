The light gets to you first.

It is not sunrise, it is just *day* — a high, white-gold brightness that comes from everywhere. The sky is too pale to be blue, bleached to a luminous milk by dust and by the mirrors still turning far overhead. They are too high and too old to see as objects; you only know them because the light has a managed quality, even after millions of years. No glare, exactly. No hard sun-disc to squint at. Just an immense, warm bath of photons that flattens shadow into a thin blue line under everything and makes the air itself seem to glow.

The air is warm against the skin of your forearms, dry to the point of abstraction. Warm in the way a kiln is warm hours after firing. 30, maybe 35 degrees, and utterly without moisture. Your mouth dries in minutes. Your sweat evaporates before it beads. There has never been water here, or not for so long that the concept has worn out of the stone. No riverbeds, even fossil ones. No shorelines. Only the shapes that dryness makes when it has forever to work.

You are standing very low in the world. Not in a valley — there are no highlands left to define a valley — but on a continental plain so broad and so level that you feel the curvature of the planet in the way the distance goes slightly *up* at the edges. The surveyors of whoever built this would have called it Datum Zero. Everything was built down to it, and then everything wore down to it again.

Because this is not ground.

Under your boots is a sinter of pale materials, warm and faintly resonant: crushed ceramic, fused glass aggregate, powdered polymer-stone, precipitated lime from dissolved towers. It crunches very quietly and then stops crunching, compacted. Scuff it with a toe and you expose a layer fractionally darker, fractionally harder. Dig a meter and you'd find laminated flooring. Dig ten meters and you'd find a ceiling. Dig a hundred and you'd find another city. There is no bedrock here. There never was, or it is buried so deep it no longer counts. The planet is made, all the way down as far as anyone has ever cut, of previous versions of itself.

And it is crowded.

At first from a distance it looks like flat desert — a white-tan waste running unbroken to the white horizon. Then your eyes adjust and you see the density. The plain is not empty. It is *set* with things, close-set, shoulder to shoulder, as far as you can walk in a lifetime.

Stumps. Fins. Slabs. Pylons. The worn-down teeth of a machine-forest.

They rise eight, ten, fifteen meters, no higher, because higher did not survive the age. Wind and dust and time and their own slow subsidence have planed them to a common height, rounded them like sea-stones. Each one is a few meters from the next, tilted at slightly different angles where the substrate has slumped. Their surfaces are pitted, honeycombed, exfoliating in paper-thin mineral skins. A material that was once wall, vehicle, conduit, computer, all fused by deep time into one geology. Some are hollow and you can step inside them into a cool, dim, ribbed chamber that hums just below hearing. Some are solid. Some are perforated with thousands of small, flute-like holes that were windows, vents, light-guides.

You walk between them and the scale keeps resetting itself. What looked like a nearby cluster is a day away. What looked like a ridge on the horizon is just the next ten thousand rows. The field makes its own weather. Heat rising off the pale floor in the afternoon builds slow, stately dust devils that wander for kilometers between the fins, sifting fine white powder from one place to another. Higher up, long banners of electrostatic haze gather and discharge without thunder, in utter silence, as sheets of St. Elmo's light crawling from fin-tip to fin-tip at dusk.

This is an ancient place in the way a beach is ancient — not broken, but finished breaking. Edges no longer get sharper, only smoother. A fallen cornice the size of a house lies half-buried beside you, its ornament — faces, letters, circuits, all three at once — worn to soft ripples you can barely feel with your palm. And yet it is not dead. That is the strangeness.

Put your hand flat on a fin at noon and you feel it *breathe*. The whole dense field expands almost imperceptibly with heat, each slab leaning a millimeter closer to its neighbor, the narrow slot-canyons between them tightening. At night they tick, contract, exhale the day's stored light as a faint warmth that keeps the air from ever cooling properly. In the narrowest slots, where two slabs nearly touch, the air shimmers even when there is no wind — gravity is thin there, or the floor still remembers how to repel dust. Dropped grit falls *slow* in those places, drifting sideways before it lands.

Look closely at the lee side of any fin and you will see the sparse green-black script of the only plants that matter here. Wire-lichen. Glassmoss. They do not need soil, because there is no soil. They need light and dust and time. The wire-shrubs are exactly that: tangles of black, hair-fine photosynthetic wire, a meter across, anchored in a pit in the ceramic by an acid etch. They do not sway. They tremble at a frequency you feel in your teeth when the wind is right. Between them, in shade-lines that never move until they do, are patches of moss that is not moss but colonies of silica lenses, each the size of a pinhead, focusing the brutal brightness down to a tolerable ember for the algae living beneath. Step back and each fin wears a dark mustache on its shadow side, a living contour line.

Animals are rarer than plants, and you will hear them before you see them, which is to say you will hear almost nothing.

Once a day, perhaps, a glider passes — a pale, meter-wide thing like a torn plastic bag given intention, riding the thermal streets between the fins without flapping. It is watching for maintenance-beetles: fist-sized, dome-backed grazers, half animal, half leftover machine, that chew the wire-lichen and excrete fresh ceramic pellets. You find their trails everywhere, dotted lines of perfect spheres. You almost never see the beetle itself; it hears the crunch of your boots through the floor from fifty meters away and shuts down into perfect stillness, indistinguishable from rubble.

Quieter still are the things underneath. At night, if you lie with your ear to the warm floor, you hear it: not a sound so much as a pressure, a slow peristalsis far below, the deep structure redistributing load, sealing a crack that opened during the day, growing a new buttress by a micron. The ground here repairs itself. Cuts you make with a knife are gone by morning, filled with a smooth, enamel-like scar. It does not do this for you. It does this because that is what it was designed to do before its designers became a layer.

The rules here are simple and you learn them with your body:

One: Do not look for nature underneath. There isn't any. The dust is pulverized building. The sand is pulverized building. The horizon is building, worn to the shape of weather.

Two: Light is a material. The ancients poured it, stored it in the slabs like heat in a brick. That is why the shadows are so sharp and so cool, why the moss grows only in certain angles, why on some afternoons the gaps between the fins fill with a golden fizz that stings the eyes and makes compasses spin. You are walking through a battery the size of a continent, slowly discharging.

Three: Keep quiet, because the place does. Sound dies fast here. Your footsteps are swallowed by the powder. Your voice does not echo off the fins, it is absorbed by those thousands of little holes, drunk down into the structure. Even the wind makes almost no noise as it combs through — only, if you stand perfectly still at midday, a very high, very faint choir, as air moves across flutes tuned by erosion to just above human hearing. The beetles can hear it. The gliders navigate by it. You can only sense it as a lightness behind the ears.

By late afternoon the light goes amber and incredibly level, and the dense field extends its long blue shadows in perfect parallel ranks to the east, fin after fin after fin to the curve of the world. Dust risen by the day's devils hangs and catches fire. The warm air smells of hot stone and faint ozone, clean to the point of sterility. Somewhere far off a slab, undermined by a night of self-repair, settles with a soft, powdery sigh you feel through your soles more than hear — the only landslide this flattened world still permits.

You could walk for years and not leave it. People have. Their shelters are small lean-tos built in the lee of fins from fallen skins of building-stone, almost invisible. Their cairns are little stacks of beetle-pellets. They learn, as you are learning, not to fight the brightness or the dryness or the crowding, but to move the way the glider moves: slow, low, between the teeth of the world, pausing in the thin lines of shade where the glassmoss grows, listening for the almost-nothing that tells you you are not alone, and that the vast, white, built desert, settled into its final form, is still, very quietly, awake.
