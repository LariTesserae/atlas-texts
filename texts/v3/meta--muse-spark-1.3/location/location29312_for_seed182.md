You find it first by the heat coming up through your shoes.

On the plain there is nothing — dry cracked laterite, thorn scrub, white sky — and then the ground simply stops. A mouth. Not wide. You could walk its rim in twenty minutes if there was a path, which there isn't, because the rim is houses.

The Pit at Marrowell is not old. You can tell. The rock hasn't learned how to be a cliff yet. Forty years ago this was a blasting cut for copper, or for coal, stories differ, a straight-sided bite taken straight down. The walls are still sharp where they broke, black basalt and red cinder with the drill-marks still in them, little half-moon scars in rows. Fresh rebar teeth stick out where a gallery collapsed. Nothing is smoothed. Even the rockfall talus at the ledges looks newly fallen, edges you could cut your hand on.

And into that fresh cut, the town poured itself and never stopped pouring until there was no room left.

It is small. That is the first strange thing for how much is in it. From the lip you can see the whole of it at once, if the steam lets you — a hamlet, really, a single throat maybe two hundred meters deep and half that across, terraced all the way down in rings. But there is no empty wall. Not a meter. Every ledge, every blasting shelf, every vertical face has been floored, walled, roofed, bridged. Houses of poured slag-concrete and corrugated iron sit on top of older houses of timber and brick. Catwalks stitch across the void. Pipes run like veins, thick as a man's torso and then thin as a finger, cased in moss. Staircases that were once mine ladders are now enclosed in lean-tos and kitchens. There are no streets. There are only roofs that serve as streets, and rooms you agree to walk through to get to the next roof. To go down to the sump you must pass through eleven other people's parlors, and leave your shoes at each third one.

Light barely gets in. At noon, for perhaps an hour, a solid shaft stands straight down the center, full of dust and seed-fluff and vapor, and hits the bottom water and turns white. The rest of the day the pit is in its own shadow. The walls shade each other. What light there is comes sideways and tired, or comes from below: strings of amber cage-bulbs, furnace doors left open, the dull orange seam where the geothermal plant breathes. Everything is seen in half-light. Edges glow green and then go black.

Because it is hot. Brutally, greenhouse hot. The plain above is hot in a dry way. Down here the heat has nowhere to go. It rises from the bottom — from the vent and the engines and the composting depth of leaves — and hits the narrow rim and sits.  It sits on your lungs. Air down there is thick enough to chew: hot iron, hot oil, crushed green leaves going sweet-rot, sulfur just under it all. Your glasses fog instantly. Your shirt sticks. Metal handrails are warm to the touch at midnight.

And so it sweats. That is the water here. There is no river in the Pit, no lake, though visitors always expect one. There is damp. The walls weep along horizontal seams where the blasting cracked an aquifer. Black seeps that never quite become streams. Condensation beads on every cold pipe and then runs before it can drip. Every morning — if morning means anything down there — a low cloud forms at about the third terrace down and hangs, caught, unable to lift out over the lip. It condenses on washing lines and taro leaves and runs down chains of bottle-caps hung for that purpose into cisterns. Children are sent out with rags to wring out the air. Once in a while, when a storm passes over the plain, rain falls straight in, sudden and hissing on hot roofs, and for ten minutes there are waterfalls off every catwalk. Then it stops, and the steam eats it.

Where there is heat and sweat, there is green. Impossibly green for a hole in iron. Vegetation down there is not decorative; it is structural. Giant elephant-ear taro with leaves big as doors shades the western stair. Maidenhair ferns pour out of every bolt-hole. Creepers — morning glory gone thick as rope — have taken the old ore gantry and turned it into an arch. Figs have split two cisterns and are holding them together with roots. Moss does most of the work: it deadens, it seals, it pads the boiler housings so they don't ring. The people encourage it. They train vines along hot pipes to cool them, they thatch roofs with cutters-grass, they grow gourds in hanging buckets to shade windows. The mills at Level Six pulp fiber for paper and cloth, and the smell of boiling leaves is always somewhere.

Iron and leaf have stopped being separate things. That is the strangeness you notice after your eyes adjust. A handrail is half rust, half stem; you can't tell where one gives up. Leaves near the smelter come up copper-edged, stiff as tin. A support cable left alone for a season will be found furred in moss and then, cut open, the moss will have sent root-hairs *into* the wire's grease, drinking it. The old-timers call it green-iron. They no longer paint things. They prune them.

For all the industry, it is quiet. This is the rule of the Pit, and also its physics. Sound does not travel well through wet leaves and steam and packed rooms. And the machines themselves have been throttled down to a patience. The great Cornish pumps that once dewatered the mine still turn, but now they lift seep-water to the gardens one slow stroke at a time — *keh — ... — keh — ... — keh* — you can time your pulse between. The main cage-winch that lowers the market platform from rim to sump takes all morning to descend; the women ride down knitting, selling from their laps as they go. The dye-works, the wire-drawing shop, the oil-press, the canning shed for pickled shoots — all electrified, all humming behind moss-baffles at idle. Now and then a pressure valve sighs and lets go a plume that whites out a whole terrace, and everyone pauses. Drips tick in cisterns. A goat bell, somewhere, but muffled by leaves. Mostly, feet in cloth slippers on warm wood. Low voices, because loud voices come back off the walls and embarrass you.

Animals are rare. Too hot, too closed, too little to eat that the people haven't already claimed. No flies — the sulfur and the oil see to that. Sometimes a crow circles the rim, looks down at the steam, thinks better of it. Down inside: a few pipe-rats, hairless-tailed and shy, a couple of blind white lizards that live behind the boilers, chickens kept in wire boxes on roofs, panting constantly. Once a heron came down with the rain and stood on the sump rail for three days while everyone brought it scraps, until it lifted out again. People still talk about it. People are the fauna here.

Slow is the law. Rust blooms over a season, not a night. The vines take a wall in a year and give it back in ten when the house beneath is rebuilt. The sump at the very bottom — a scummed, steaming eye of water no bigger than a courtyard, ringed with pipes and taro — rises and falls over months, not days. When it rises, the lowest floor is abandoned without argument; when it falls, it is swept and reoccupied. No one hurries the winch. No one shouts. If you live packed this tight, cheek to leaf to boiler, you learn to move like sap.

Stand on a roof-terrace near the middle at dusk, when the cage-bulbs come on one by one down the spiral and the shaft of sky above has gone violet. Below you, the steam glows from underneath. Above you, the lip houses jut out over the void like teeth, with laundry still dripping condensation. The whole packed, sweating, green-rusted hamlet breathes out once — a long, hot, plant-sweet exhalation that lifts the hanging cloud a meter and lets it settle again — and holds.
