There is a heat that does not come with light.

You go down into it. Not up — the land falls away in a long, almost imperceptible tilt toward sea-level and then stops falling and just spreads. A basin, miles across, flat enough that you cannot tell where the center is. The air gets thicker with every step down until breathing feels like drinking warm tea. It is midday, it must be midday, but the light never arrives. It hangs high above in a low, yellow-gray steam, filtered, chewed up by three layers of leaves before it can touch ground. What reaches you is green, then green-black. A dusk that sweats.

The heat is not weather. It is the place. It comes from above as damp pressure and from below as a slow exhalation. The rock under everything is still young.

That is the first strange thing you notice with your hands: the ground has no soil. Not really. A few centimeters of black crumble and then — stone. New stone. Pillow basalt and cracked pahoehoe, porous as bone, edges still sharp because nothing has ever been washed or winded into smoothness. There has been no time for valleys, no time for gullies or sand. The geology is embryonic, just come up out of the earth still warm in its core. You can feel it through your soles if you stand still: a faint, animal warmth in the rock itself. And it weeps.

Water does not fall here so much as appear. It beads on the underside of every leaf, on your forearms, on the black rock pores, gathers and slips and falls again. The stone sweats continuously. Shallow sheets stand everywhere a foot is low — not lakes, not streams, just skin-water, ankle-deep, bath-warm, brown-green and opaque, holding the sky's heat. It does not flow anywhere. There is nowhere lower for it to go. It sits, it evaporates an inch, it condenses a foot above and rains back up from leaf-tip as drip. Drip is everywhere. A soft, endless plinking under the louder things. If you wring a handful of moss you get a cupful. If you squeeze the air you would get the same.

And the moss is everywhere. That is the second layer. On new rock that should be barren, life has thrown itself all at once, too fast for succession. Velvet mosses in acid green and black-green felt the stone so completely you don't see rock unless you peel. Above the moss, ferns — thigh-high, chest-high, tree-high — their fronds so close they overlap like shingles. Above the ferns, broadleaves with plates for leaves, elephant-ear and split-leaf and things with no name, glossy with condensation, angled to catch the little light there is. Above them, the canopy proper, interlocked, fifty, sixty feet up, closing the world. From below you see only trunks, black and slick, straight as columns, rooting directly into cracks in the basalt with white finger-roots. No deep earth. No need. Water and rot are right at the surface.

Because rot does not accumulate. That is the rule here.

Leaves fall constantly — you see them helicoptering down in the dim — but there is no leaf litter. A leaf hits the sheet-water or the moss and is taken. Within hours, sometimes minutes, it softens, darkens, is covered in white mycelium lace, is eaten by springtails, by pillbugs the size of thumbs, by fungal threads. The basin digests itself as fast as it makes itself. Nothing piles up. Nothing washes away either, because there is no current to carry it, no slope to erode. Sharp rock stays sharp. Warm water stays warm. The place holds itself in perfect, humming equilibrium. Morning would look like evening would look like ten years from now. Hot, dim, streaming.

Which is why it can be so full.

You cannot move without displacing something alive. That is not exaggeration, it is density. The air at face height is a soup of midges and gnats and clear-winged flies moving in slow vortices, so thick your exhale makes a temporary tunnel. On every fern frond sit frogs — thumbnail treefrogs in orange and lime and wet slate, throats pulsing, dozens to a plant, thousands to an acre, their bodies touching. In the sheet-water, tadpoles in black ribbons, and fish no longer than your finger that live in water barely deeper than their backs, mouths up, drinking oxygen from the film. Leeches inch along submerged stems. Crabs the color of moss click sideways across basalt noses. Snakes, thin as vines, hang motionless where vine should be.

Nothing builds here. That is the other rule. No mounds, no dams, no burrows that last the night — the water table is too high to dig and the fungus takes anything chewed. So everything clings. Lizards cling to trunks. Orchids and ferns cling to lizards' larger cousins, to branches, to each other. Birds — small, dark, red-throated birds that never leave the basin — do not nest so much as perch their eggs in the fork of a leaf and sit, and then leave, and another sits. Ants in endless living chains pour over roots, carrying green cuttings many times their size, but they make no hill; the colony lives in the hollow of a fallen trunk that will be gone by next week, and then they will live somewhere else. Even presence is temporary, but the *amount* of presence never drops.

And the sound. You hear it before you understand it. Not jungle-loud in crashes, but loud as a held chord. At the base, a low insect drone, millions of wings at slightly different frequencies merging into a single hot wire of sound. Above it, the frogs: not croaking, really, but bell-tones — *plink, plink-plink, plink* — different species in different keys, so regular you begin to think they are keeping time for each other. Above that, the drip-drip of condensation from leaf to leaf to water, and the occasional heavy *plop* of a fruit giving up and falling, and the rustle as something small is eaten by something slightly larger, and then the drone again. If you stand long enough your own heartbeat starts to sound late.

The weirdness is quiet. You expect rain and it never rains. You expect shade to be cool and it is hotter under the leaves than outside. You put your palm to a weeping boulder and the water coming out of the pore is warmer than the air, faintly mineral, smelling of iron and green. Roots grow upward as often as down, into the damp air, to drink what never falls. Light motes, what few there are, hang and do not drift, as if the air is too thick to let them settle. At night — if it can be called night, for the dim only deepens by a shade — the frogs glow faintly at the belly, not enough to see by, just enough that the understory sparkles when you move, thousands of tiny cold lights going out and on where your foot disturbs the dark.

Walk a mile in any direction and it repeats. More flat black rock, more moss felt, more ankle-water mirroring nothing, more columns, more frogs. A palm that has fallen and already become a garden for seedlings sprouting from its trunk while it is still green. A clearing where water stands a little deeper, knee-deep, and lilies with leaves like dinner plates crowd so tight you could walk on them, and under them the water boils softly with tadpoles. A slightly higher nose where the basalt humps up, bare for a moment, steaming, colonized first by orange lichen like spilled paint, then by moss, then — a few feet away — already by ferns. You are watching geology being covered in real time, but the cover never finishes, never deepens into soil, never changes the balance. Construction and erasure happening at exactly the same speed.

Stay until your clothes are soaked through — which is minutes — and you understand the stasis is not emptiness. It is fullness held. Water near surface and always near surface. Heat that does not peak or break. Light that does not brighten or fail. Life that does not invade or retreat but presses, layer on layer, breath on breath, in warm, dim, dripping contact.

Nothing is being made here except more of itself, immediately, forever.
