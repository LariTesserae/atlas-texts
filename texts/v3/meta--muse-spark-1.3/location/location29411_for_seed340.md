You come to it from above, because there is no other way. The plain runs flat and pale to every horizon, cracked salt-clay and wind-combed grit, and then it simply stops — a lip, a raw edge, a long oval mouth dropped out of the world.

Below you is the Hollow.

Not a cave. Not yet. A collapse so recent the geology hasn't learned how to be old. The walls fall two hundred meters in sheer, fresh slabs where the ceiling of some older world gave way. The rock is unweathered, unlicked by rain — because there is no rain, has never been rain here. It is sharp as broken pottery, ochre and bone-white and rust-red in unsoftened strata, dust still sifting off the ledges in slow veils. At the rim great blocks hang half-toppled, held by nothing but their own weight and indecision. Everything smells of hot stone and cut hay. Your mouth dries in three breaths.

Sunlight gets in. That's the first surprise. A wide ragged skylight runs the length of the Hollow, and noon pours straight down in a solid column, normal daylight, gold-white and warm on the skin. It doesn't reach the walls. Blue shadow pools under the overhangs, but the center burns.

And the center is alive. Entirely alive.

From the rim it looks like a dense yellow-green sea, a valley-sized mat of vegetation filling the basin from wall to wall, town-big, restless. Only when you start down the scree trail — a switchback cut by someone's hands, with thorn-rope rails — do you understand the scale and the dryness.

There is no water here. None. Not a seep, not a damp darkening under a stone, not a memory of mud. The air sucks. It pulls the wet from your eyes, from your lips, until you breathe through cloth. The dust underfoot squeaks, fine as flour, mineral and utterly anhydrous. And yet everything below you is plant.

Not trees. Not as you know them. Thatch-forest. Paper-forest.

Millions upon millions of dry stalks, taller than a man, packed near-touching, layered, climbing over each other toward the light slit. They are the substrate and the architecture and the inhabitants all at once. Stems as thick as your wrist, as thin as wire, jointed like straw, hollow, ribbed, ochre and straw-blond and bleached white and here and there a feverish green at the growing tip — the only green, and it lasts a day before it cures to yellow. Leaves that are not leaves: dry ribbons, curled shavings, frayed banners that never wilt because they were never wet. Seed heads like thistles made of glass-fiber, huge puffballs of lint that tear open with an audible pop.

The whole mass is meters deep. It floats, almost. Under the standing stalks is a springy, creaking mattress of fallen stems, woven molt, husk, chaff, never rotting — nothing rots wet here. Things only shred finer and finer until they become dust and the dust is caught again by spider-fine anchor threads and hauled back up into new growth. You step off the scree and onto it and it gives, boings softly under your boots, then firms.

It is warm. Body-warm, breath-warm. The sun heats the enclosed air and the walls throw it back and the sheer friction of so much life moving keeps it warm. Sweat doesn't bead; it vanishes. The heat shimmers above the seed heads, and with it rises the chaff: a perpetual low blizzard of fluff, pollen-dust, wing-scales, powdered husk. You walk in a golden haze. Light catches every mote. It itches gloriously.

Then the sound hits you, once you are below the lip.

Deafening. There is no other word, and even that fails. It is not one sound but a physical pressure.

First the plants themselves: in the dry heat the mature pods cure and coil and burst. Pop. Pop-pop-pop. Thousands per minute across the valley, a ceaseless crackling frying, like fire without heat. And the stalks rub. Wind funnels through the skylight and saws the forest against itself — a vast dry rustle, paper on paper, a shushing roar that swells and drops and swells again.

And under, through, over that — the fauna. Overwhelming. You thought the place was plants until a stalk beside you shudders and lifts.

Everything is inhabited, and everything inhabits everything else. The air is thick with them: cream moths big as hands, black wasps in ribbons, needle-flies, buzzing beetles armored like seeds, locust-things in clouds that darken the sun shaft for seconds at a time. They do not fly so much as boil. Their wings make a high electric whine that drills through the lower rustle. On the stems, legions: ants the length of a finger sawing with audible clicks, hump-backed crickets drawing their legs across ridged backs — chirr-chirr-chirr, millions in imperfect unison until the valley rings like a struck gong — mantis-things in dead-leaf cloaks, spiders strung between stalks on lines that sing in the wind.

And larger rustlers deeper in the mat: pill-bugs the size of dogs rolling and unrolling, shedding plates with a clatter; burrowers you never see, only hear, threshing through the thatch mattress. Molted skins hang everywhere, translucent, still clinging to stems, rattling.

Nothing here drinks. That is the rule you learn slowly. No blood as you know it. Cut a stem and it does not bleed water; it exhales a puff of resin-dust and a bead of thick amber oil that hardens before it can drip. Crack a beetle and inside is powder and fiber and oil-sacs, dry to the touch. The whole economy runs not on water but on dryness itself: on dust, on oil, on wind-borne flour, on sound and movement. The plants photosynthesize light and heat and scavenge the nitrogen of endless shed chitin. The animals eat pith and lint and each other and excrete fine pellets that crumble instantly to fertilizer-dust. To be wet would be to die — wet means clogged spiracles, gummed wings. The Hollow keeps itself dry by constant thrashing.

It is turbulent because stillness is death. Nothing rests. Watch any patch for a minute: a green tip spears up visibly, uncurling, an inch while you watch, pale and hungry. A wave of ants finds it, swarms it, strips its ribbons in seconds, the stalk left shivering naked. Then the locust shadow passes, then the seed head beside it pops and fires its fluff into the updraft, and a moth lays a seam of eggs in the wound, and a spider is already stringing a net across the gap. Creation and destruction are the same gesture here, fast as breathing, anthill-fast, bazaar-fast. The topography of stalks changes while you stand in it. Paths close behind you.

This is why the hollow stalks are drums. That is the strangeness you only feel in your teeth after an hour.

Many stems are perfectly hollow, with thin windows, with taut membranes stretched across nodes. The fauna play them. Crickets back into them to amplify. Ants drum in code — tap-tap-tapause-tap — and whole columns turn. Woodpecker-like beetles hammer for grubs inside, boom-boom-boom, and the stem resonates like a bassoon. The forest is an instrument being played by its parasites, and the plants listen. You can see it: seed heads cock toward a fresh drumming, growing tips bend toward vibration, not light. Sound is a kind of sun here. Loudest patches grow fastest.

People have been here. Scattered, light-handed people, or something close enough. You find their marks once your eyes adjust past the boil.

A platform lashed ten feet up between three massive joint-stems: woven mat floor, a low rail of thorns, a ladder of knotted vine that sways. Empty now except for husks and a cache of thorn-needles, stone flakes, a hammer of dense knuckle-chitin. Further on, a snare line — nooses of twisted fiber set across a beetle run, now sprung and full of dust. A hut like a wasp nest, single-room, made of chewed pith plastered over a frame of stems, door facing away from the wind, inside a sleeping hollow lined with moth-wing blankets that shimmer, still warm. Hand-work everywhere: cut stems, notched at the same height, twine, simple, effective, repairable. No metal. No machines. Just cutting edges and leverage and profound knowledge of when to move and when to lie flat and let the swarm pass.

No one is in them now. Or they are hidden, painted, quiet — which in this roar is its own kind of invisibility. You get the sense the structures are seasonal, or tidal, put up when a patch ripens and abandoned when the mat eats them. Already one corner of the platform is being colonized: a green tip has pierced the weave, a spider has guy-wired it.

If you stand still in the center at midday — in the full normal light shaft, in the warm chaff-storm, with the popping and whining and drumming hammering your ribs — you understand the final rule: the Hollow is embryonic. The walls are still falling, grain by grain. The mat is still learning how to be a forest without water. It has filled the space given to it completely, densely, loudly, and is now building upward toward the mouth, stalk on molt on stem, trying to climb out. In a year the floor will be ten feet higher. In ten, the skylight will narrow. It is under construction, raw and yawning and brilliant, a dry womb shouting at the sky.

You leave with your ears ringing, your clothes full of lint and wing-scale, your throat parched beyond thirst, oil under your fingernails instead of sap. Behind you the roar does not diminish with distance; it is cut clean when you crest the lip, as if a door closed. The pale silent plain. The wind-combed grit. And below, hidden, the warm, waterless, teeming thicket continuing its deafening business of making and unmaking itself.
