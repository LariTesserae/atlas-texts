It goes on flat longer than your legs want to believe.

Not flat like a field. Flat like something that gave up on having a shape. You stand on it and your boots make the same sound in every direction: a dull iron tock, then a wet suck underneath. There is no ground here. Not underneath. There is deck, and under the deck there is older deck, and under that is slag poured and set like lava and then worn smooth. If you pried a plate up — and many plates have been pried up by time, curled at the corners like dry skin — you would find not soil but cinder, ash-concrete, the ballast of a thousand demolished walls tamped down into a new earth. Everything is made. The world has no bottom except older making.

This is low country, though no river made it. A basin a few meters above the idea of sea level, wide as a small town and ringed not by hills but by the dim bulks of worksheds settling into themselves. In the dark you can’t see the far edge. You can feel it as a change in the roar.

Because it is dark. Not night-dark that will lift. A permanent industrial dark, cool and thick. The sky is a low lid of cloud and smoke and steam lit from below, a bruised orange-black that never quite becomes day. The only true lights are low and man-made and scattered far apart: a sodium lamp hissing on a leaning mast, a row of green pilot lamps along a pump house that never blink off, the sullen cherry glow from a furnace grate left open to the rain. They don’t illuminate so much as stain the wet air around them. Between them are long reaches of nothing where your eyes adjust and you realize you are walking on a mirror.

Water is everywhere and it has nowhere to go. That is the first rule here.

It rains often, a fine cold drizzle that is half rain and half condensate falling back out of the steam, and because the plain is so old and so near-level, the water doesn’t run. It stands. It sheets. In the pitted concrete aprons and the dished iron plates it stands in films an inch deep that reflect the lamps in smeared streaks. In the low places — the settling basins, the old cooling ponds with their collapsed rims, the borrow pits where material was once dug to make more floor — it stands deeper, black and still and skimmed with oil that makes slow rainbow halos. Pipes weep. Everything weeps. Valve stems, rivet seams, the flanks of boilers gone cold but still sweating. The concrete itself weeps, darkening from the inside out, dripping from its undersides where the deck is jacked up on rusted piers. You hear it even under the roar if you listen for the higher frequencies: tick, plink, trickle, the constant seep finding the crack.

Cold gets into the metal and stays there. Your breath smokes. The air smells of wet iron, cold grease, ozone, and a faint sweet rot from where plant life has found a purchase. It is cool enough that steam hangs instead of rising. Plumes from vents and relief valves don’t climb to the sky; they spread sideways a few meters up and become fog, rolling slow across the open, catching lamp-light, wetting your face. Condensation beads on every surface, on handrails, on chains hanging from gantries, and falls in a perpetual secondary rain.

And the sound — you feel it before you understand it. It is not one sound. It is a weather made of machines still working with no one to work for.

A low fundamental you feel in your sternum: great pumps somewhere out in the dark, thudding on a cycle that never resolves, moving water from one low place to another low place. Over that, the white scream of steam bled continuously from a manifold, so loud it erases thought. Closer, the arrhythmic clang and shudder of a press or a crusher operating empty, chewing nothing, its jaws meeting with a boom that rolls across the sheet-water and comes back off the shed walls. A conveyor rattles somewhere to your left, carrying nothing, its rollers squealing. Chains slap. A fan with a bent blade thumps the air in a lopsided wobble you can time with your pulse until you lose it. It is deafening in the literal sense: after ten minutes you stop hearing individual things and hear only pressure, and after an hour your own voice in your head sounds distant and faked.

Nothing lives here to compete with it. That is the second rule. No gulls, no rats, no insects ticking in the dark, no dogs barking between sheds. The noise and the oil and the cold and the lack of any true soil have scoured it. Even your own footsteps sound wrong without any other animal sound to cushion them. It is a place built at human scale that has outlived the human ear.

Only the sparse green persists, because green is patient and does not need quiet.

Moss in the expansion joints, black-green and swollen like a sponge, hissing faintly with absorbed water when you step near it. Lichen — pale gray, sulfur yellow — crusting the leeward sides of tanks, following the drip lines down. In the cracks where plates have heaved apart by millimeters over a century, tufts of wire-grass, dark and salt-stiff, shivering in the fan-wash. A few birch-thin saplings, white-barked and stunted, growing out of a clogged gutter on the side of a machine shop, their roots visibly wrapped around rusted bolts, their leaves small and curled and dripping. No meadow. No thicket. Just punctuation. Life as a stain in the seams of the made world, creeping a centimeter a year while the machines shake the floor around it.

Time here does not erode like wind on sandstone. It erodes like age on iron. That is the third rule, and you can read it everywhere.

The sheds are the hills now. Their roofs have sagged into shallow bowls that hold rain. Their corrugated walls have thinned and bellied, rusted through at the bolt holes so the whole skin hangs loose and drums and booms in the wind from the fans. Corners that were once sharp are now rounded, handled smooth by a hundred years of wet and vibration. Stairs lead up to platforms that no longer overlook anything because the railing has rusted away and the floor plates have cupped. A gantry crane spans a yard, its rails spalled, its wheels frozen mid-travel decades ago, its hook block hanging on a wire rope that sings in the wind — still hanging, still swaying, still not falling. A cooling tower, squat and blind, its wooden louvers rotted to lace, exhales warm wet air from its throat in slow breaths. Everything leans a degree or two toward the center of the basin, settled into final form. Not ruined. Ruin implies an event. This is settled. This is what industry looks like when it becomes geology.

Walk outward and the pattern repeats with variation, town-size, valley-size: another apron, another basin of black water trembling under steam-rain, another hulk of a boiler house with its doors gone and its fire still somehow banked and breathing inside, another field of pipes on low trestles marching to the dark and dripping at every flange, creating a line of little permanent puddles, each with its own oil rainbow, each with its own fringe of moss. Between presences, long empties where there is only wet deck and fog and lamp glare reflected in sheet-water and the roar coming from all sides so you cannot point to its source. Sparse, but total. Nowhere to step off. The emptiness is also built.

Strangeness gathers in the low places. Because the ground is dead level and vibrating constantly, water sometimes refuses to drain and instead shivers into standing wave patterns around pipe feet, tiny concentric ripples that hold for hours without wind. Because the lights never go out and the fog never lifts, you see afterimages that are not afterimages — dark shapes moving at the edge of vision that resolve, when you turn, into steam folding over itself. Because everything is iron and water, compasses spin lazily here, and radios fill with the crackle of the works, bursts of static timed to the pumps. And once, in a settling pond, you will see the reflection of a lamp ripple and hold, and the ripple will keep its shape after the water calms, as if the water remembers being shaken.

It is active, though nothing is born here. Pumps lift water and drop it. Steam rises and falls as rain. Rust blooms and flakes and is washed into cracks where grass takes it up into green. Concrete spalls and exposes rebar like bone, and rebar rusts and swells and cracks more concrete. The cycle is slow, mechanical, hydrological, botanical. No predator, no prey. Just maintenance without maintainers.

Stay long enough and your boots fill with cold seep. Your shoulders dampen through. The roar becomes your baseline, and the moments when a relief valve slams shut and leaves a half-second of relative quiet feel like falling. In that half-second you hear what is really here: water ticking off a thousand edges onto iron, onto concrete, onto black grass, onto black water. Plink. Plink. Plink. All across the open dark, the made plain weeping itself back into a marsh it never was.

Then the steam screams again, the pumps thud, the empty crusher booms, and the place resumes itself.
