It is so flat you have to learn to read it all over again.

At first it looks like nothing has happened here. The ground goes out in every direction with barely a lift or fall to break it, a low warm plain only a breath above the water table. But if you stay, if you walk it slowly, you begin to see that everything has already happened here, a long time ago. This is old country in the geological sense — not old like a mountain, but old like after mountains.

There are no hills left, only ghosts of them: long, almost imperceptible swells two or three feet higher than the hollows, broad as a town. The soil on the swells is pale and gritty, bleached sand mixed with pea-gravel of dark ironstone, rattling underfoot. In the hollows it is black and greasy, peat and silt that sucks at your boots and does not let go cleanly. Scattered here and there are stones, but they are not outcrops. They are the last hard bits, duricrust nodules and broken pans of rust-red laterite, rounded to eggs and loaves by more rain than you can imagine. When you break one open the inside is banded yellow and blood-red. They sit half-sunk in the mud, sweating. Even hours after the rain has stopped they weep water from their pores, darkening the ground around them in a halo.

Water is everywhere, but not as a river, not as a lake. The slope is too small now for rivers to bother. Water sits near the surface and thinks about moving.

After rain — and it has rained recently, the air still smells of it — the whole flat shines. Every low spot holds a sheet, six inches deep, two feet deep, tea-colored or sky-colored depending on what is under it. They connect by slow, almost invisible threads of flow through sedge and over sand, too shallow to have banks. You can stand and watch a leaf for a full minute and not be sure if it moved. In the slightly deeper pans, old meander scars that have lost their river, the water stays year-round: warm, tannic, still, with a skin of pollen and insect castings. If you wade in, your foot sinks first through warm water, then through a cool layer near the bottom, then into silt as fine as flour that puffs up around your ankles in a cloud.

It is warm and full of air you can drink. Not hot like desert. Warm like breath. The light is bright, full daylight, but softened by evaporation. The sun is up behind a high white haze, so there are no hard shadows. Everything glows a little at the edges. Distant clumps of trees shimmer and double themselves in the sheets. Your shirt sticks to your back in minutes. Condensation beads on every stem and on the undersides of leaves, gathers, falls with a tick into the water below. The plain is steaming very faintly, though you only see it if you crouch and look sideways across a pan.

For how wet it is, there is surprisingly little green.

That is the age of it. The soil has been rained on for so long that almost everything edible has been washed out of it. What grows here grows thinly, spaced out, careful. Low sedges in tussocks, their blades harsh and olive-yellow, each tussock separated by bare wet sand or black mud. Scattered paper-barked trees, none tall, trunks pale and peeling in strips that hang and soak up water like wicks. Their crowns are sparse, small hard leaves held edge-on to the sun, casting almost no shade. Between them, sundews and bladderworts, tiny carnivores making a living where roots cannot. A patch of reeds where seepage is stronger. Otherwise, openness. You can see a long way — a mile, two miles — to where the land dissolves into water-light and water dissolves into sky. The emptiness is not absence; it is the main thing. Each living thing has a lot of space around it.

Life is not in the plants. Life is in the water and the mud and the air above them.

Because it is loud here. Unreasonably loud for a place that moves so slowly.

First the frogs. They are everywhere in the pans, invisible, throats pulsing. Not one chorus but three or four layered: a high metallic trill that goes on without pause, a lower bottle-knock, a long groaning bleat from the deeper pools. When one patch quiets, another starts. Then the insects — clouds of midges and mosquitoes hanging in columns over the water, dragonflies the color of hot metal hawking through them, and underneath it all the constant sawing of cicadas from the scattered trees, rising and falling like heat. In the mud at the edge, crabs click and plop back into their holes as you pass. Small silver fish dimple the surface of the larger sheets, taking flies, and where they gather, birds come.

Birds are the large movement here. Egrets and herons stand motionless for so long you mistake them for stumps, then spear. Ibis probe in lines, black and white. A harrier quarters low, wings barely moving. At the far edge of hearing there is always the gabble of ducks from a pan you cannot see. Nothing hurries. Even flight seems slow, deliberate, wings beating in the thick air.

If you stand still long enough, the mammals show themselves in the interstices: a rat swimming strongly from tussock to tussock, nose up; wallaby tracks, maybe, pressed into the drying silt on a swell, already filling with seep.

The rule of this place is simple: nothing runs off, everything soaks, settles, evaporates. Change happens at the speed of rust and rot. A fallen tree does not rot away in a season; it sinks, waterlogged, and becomes a dark bar in the peat that will be there for decades, sprouting ferns from its back. The ironstone nodules grow a millimeter a century. The pans migrate a few feet a year, eating at one soft bank and filling in at another with sedge rhizomes. The water itself barely flows, but it is always working, dissolving, staining, leaching the last iron down to a hard pan a foot under the sand that rings hollow when you tap it with a stick.

You can feel the end-of-form in it. This landscape is not building toward anything, not eroding toward anything. It has arrived. Rain will come again this afternoon — you can smell the weight building behind the haze — and fill the tire-track depressions of your own boots with brown water. Then sun will take it back up. Frogs will get louder. The weeping stones will weep a little faster. Nothing will look different, except everything will be wetter, and then, slowly, exactly as wet as before.
