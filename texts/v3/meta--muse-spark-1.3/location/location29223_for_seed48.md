It is low country first. That is the first thing under everything else.

A flat that goes on and on until haze takes it, a degree or two above the marsh and the sea, so water doesn't have to fall to be here — it seeps. It comes up through the cracks. After rain it stays.

This is a town made on that flat. Not a downtown, not a center. An industrial edge-town, the kind that spreads instead of rising: one-story warehouses and sheet-metal sheds and long sidings and tank farms and a power station with its stacks cold, all set far apart from each other with wide lots of broken concrete between them. You can stand in the middle of the four-lane service road and see a half mile in either direction and see no one. The buildings are hollow. Doors hang open or are gone entirely. Inside is dark and empty volume, oil stains on the floor, pallets stacked and rotting, nothing worth taking left.

That is why it feels empty even though it is built everywhere you look. There is too much air for the things in it. Sky is huge and low and the color of hot milk — white-grey-yellow with heat in it. The light never gets bright. It stays at that hour-after-downpour dimness, sun a dull coin behind vapor. It is hot, a wet heat that does not lift. The concrete gives it back. The puddles give it back as steam. Your shirt sticks. Metal burns to touch.

Everything weeps. That is the water here.

It rained this morning, hard, and it will rain again tonight, and in between the whole place drips and sweats. Gutters pour long after the clouds pass. Weeps of rust-water bleed from every bolt and seam and run down corrugated walls in orange-black tongues. The low lots — old laydown yards, parking fields that never got striped again — are shallow lakes, a few inches deep, reflecting the dim sky. Wheel ruts hold water. Potholes hold water. The rail spur is two rust lines disappearing into standing water, sleepers dark and sodden. Near the canal edge the ground itself is saturated; step off the pavement and your boot sinks to the ankle in black mud that smells of diesel and rot and salt.

Nothing green gets a hold. That is the strange part until you smell it and understand. The soil is fouled with oil and brine and slag and heat. The puddles have an iridescent skin. The lots were sprayed for years to keep them clear and the flood in spring killed whatever tried since with brackish water. So there are no weeds in the cracks, no grass verges, no trees. Just mineral: concrete gone pale and pitted, asphalt softened and rutted, gravel, cinderblock, rust scale, glass, wire. The weather has worked it all for a long time. Corners are rounded. Rebar shows like bone. Paint has chalked to ghosts of letters: BAY 7 — NO PARKING — KEEP CLEAR. It is a settled ruin, not collapsing, just sitting in its own weathered state.

And it is not empty. Not at all.

At first you think it is. The quiet fools you. There are no engines running, no backup beepers, no voices. Only one pump somewhere to the south still chugging on automatic, a low put-put-put you feel more than hear, moving floodwater from one ditch to another, forever. Otherwise drips. Plink from an awning into a puddle. Hiss of evaporation off a hot roof. Your own breathing loud in the damp air.

Then you notice movement and you cannot un-notice it.

The puddles are alive with fiddler crabs and shore crabs come up from the marsh, thousands, side-stepping across the concrete, clicking very faintly, claws raised. Gulls and white egrets stalk among them, jabbing, swallowing whole. On the dry patches, rats run — not one or two, but runways of them, brown bodies pouring along the base of walls, along pipes, disappearing into culverts. Above them, on every beam inside every open warehouse, pigeons. Hundreds to a building, thousands in the district, cooing low, shuffling, dust sifting down from their wings. Their droppings have whitewashed the floors. Feral cats slip between pallets, thin and watchful. A pack of three lean dogs works the far road, noses down, scattering rats, then losing interest in the heat and panting in the shade of a tank.

Down near the ground the air shivers — mosquitoes, midges, flies in columns over stagnant water, over carrion, over anything still. Cockroaches boil under lifted boards. On the canal wall, mussels and barnacles in a black band, and herons standing motionless as valves.

No one feeds them. Not anymore. They feed each other. Crabs eat gull droppings and drowned midges. Gulls eat crabs. Rats eat pigeon eggs fallen from rafters and garbage mummified in dumpsters. Cats and dogs eat rats and pigeons. Flies eat everything dead. Hawks — two, three kestrels hovering over the hot lots — eat whatever moves too slowly in the heat. It is a whole food web built on concrete and rainwater and leftovers and tide, with no green in the middle of it at all.

The rule here is simple and normal as any tideflat: stay out of midday heat, drink from the sky not the ground, don't waste movement. So at this hour — hot, dim, steaming — most of it waits. The dogs lie down. The gulls stand on one leg in the shallows. The pigeons tuck heads. The crabs keep going because they must. The whole town ticks, clicks, rustles, very quietly, under a vast empty sky.

If you stand still long enough you become part of the waiting. Water ticks off metal. Steam rises off the road in thin ribbons and is gone. Far off, beyond the last shed, the marsh exhales — salt and sulfur and warm mud — and the haze beyond it is the sea.
