The bar comes into view first as a smudge of smoke that doesn't rise.

It's low — lower than the banks on either side — a long gravel tongue left in the middle of a braided, cold river where the current split and forgot to rejoin. From the shore you would think it was too small for a town. From the shore you would be right, and there is a town there anyway.

That is the first rule of the place: there is no room, so everything touches.

Sixty, maybe eighty roofs crammed onto a hundred paces of fresh ground, shoulder to shoulder, gable into gable, second stories leaning out over the first until across the lanes the thatches almost kiss. There are no streets. There are plank runs, no wider than a man's shoulders, laid over black mud and raised and re-laid every week as the ground settles. Where two houses can't both fit, one is built on top of the other — a loft on stilts, a platform of green-split pine reached by a notched log. Ladders, bridges, trapdoors. Gutters made of split reeds carry roof-rain from one roof to the next to the next until it falls, finally, into a stave cistern. The whole hamlet drinks its own roofs.

The ground itself is new. You can tell by putting your hand on it.

The stones haven't learned to be round yet. The flood that laid the bar was only a few generations back — a slip upstream, a damming and a bursting — and the cobbles are still sharp-edged, grey and blue and rust-flecked, biting through thin soles. The banks where the channels cut are clean vertical slices, peat over sand over clay, with roots hanging out in the air like unfinished thoughts. Lichen has only just begun, a dust of sulfur-orange dots on the north side of the retaining walls. Everything smells of crushed rock and wet wood, not of humus. There hasn't been time for rot to sweeten it.

And the place is always settling. Doors that fit in autumn jam by spring. A corner post will have a fresh wedge of slate shoved under it, then another. The houses tilt together like drunks holding each other up, and the people let them. To build straight here would be to misunderstand.

Water is under everything, and in everything.

Not deep water — near water. Press your palm to the mud between planks and it wells up around your fingers, cold. The river arms slide past on both sides, milky-green and talkative over the shallows, and smaller runnels finger straight through town, under floors, between walls. You step over them on boards worn slick and dark. After rain — and it rains briefly almost every day, a thin cold veil around mid-afternoon — the puddles stay for hours, skinning over with sky.

The stones weep. Even on a dry morning, the big foundation boulders dragged up from the channel are beaded with droplets that gather and fall without any rain to explain them. The air itself condenses. Wood never fully dries; an axe handle left out overnight will be damp to the core by dawn. Hide cloaks hung by the door stiffen and soften with the hour. People's breath hangs a little, even in summer, and mixes with the low mist that never quite lifts but thins to a brightness by noon and then thickens again at dusk.

Light here is ordinary, workmanlike. A flat, pale brightness through high cloud, no shadows to speak of, enough to see the grain in split pine and the silver flash of a minnow. Colors stay muted until you're close: then the saturated green of fresh thatch, the red-brown of daub still showing fingerprints where it was pressed, the blue-black of mussel shells piled for lime.

Materials are all within carrying distance. That is the second rule.

Driftwood and green pine, adze-marked, still bleeding resin. Wattle of willow and alder. Daub of river clay mixed with chopped reed. Thatch of reed from the marsh edge upstream. Twine of twisted bark. Querns of rough basalt. A little copper, hammered thin, carefully mended — a knife, a brooch — but mostly bone and chipped stone and fire-hardened wood. Smoke from peat and damp kindling hangs low and gets into everything: clothes, hair, the underside of the planks, the taste of stored grain.

There is almost no plant life that isn't useful or trampled.

A clump of reeds holds on at the downstream point where the current won't let a house stand. Moss, bright and spongy, colonizes the shady sides of stilts and the tops of unused posts. A single willow, stunted and wind-bent, grows sideways out of the retaining wall on the north arm, its roots gripping stone. Its leaves are nibbled. Everything else has been walked on, cut, eaten, woven. Between houses there is no soil left for weeds — only mud, planks, stored bundles, drying racks.

Animals are scarce in the way of places fully occupied by people.

No herds, no packs. A grey heron stands downstream most mornings, motionless, and is ignored with great respect. Eels live under the town, fat in the dark runnels, and on warm nights you can hear them shift against the floorboards. A few ducks nest in the thatch and are tolerated because they eat slugs. Once a day the old brown dog makes a slow circuit of the planks, sniffing, and then lies down again. Midges rise in a thin column at dusk despite the cool, and are eaten by two or three bats that flicker between roofs and vanish.

People fill the space that animals would.

At any hour someone is doing something with water or wood. Bailing a bilge-pit with a wooden scoop. Re-shimming a post. Splitting reeds, scraping a hide, knapping a flake, stirring a pot. Shuttles click in a doorway. A child carries a bucket that slops with every step, carefully, because spilled water has to be swept back into the channel. Talk is low. The river and the thatch and the close walls soak up sound. What you hear is mostly quiet, layered: drip from eave to barrel, creak of a laden plank, lapping underfoot, the soft knock of a mallet wrapped in cloth so as not to wake the baby two walls over, the long sigh of someone straightening their back. Once in a while a laugh carries across three roofs and stops, embarrassed at its own loudness.

The unusual part you notice only if you stay till evening.

The puddles lag. Step away from one and glance back — your reflection takes half a breath longer to leave than you do. Everyone knows it and no one mentions it except to children, who are told not to stare. And the two arms of the river, for all that they look the same, do not mix where they meet at the tail of the bar. There is a visible seam, a line of clearer and milkier water running side by side for a hundred yards, refusing each other. The fishermen set their traps exactly on the seam. They say eels cross it only at night, traveling overland through the town, from one water to the other, wet tracks glinting across planks in the morning to prove it.

At night the hamlet contracts further. Shutters ofhide and wicker close. Small fires are coaxed up in stone hearths — there is never enough dry wood to be lavish — and the smoke that would not rise by day now crawls sideways between roofs, blue in the moonless dark. The river gets louder, or the quiet gets deeper. Planks cool and tighten and tick. Someone coughs. Someone bails, always someone bailing, because the water never stops coming up.

It is a wet, cold, crowded, unfinished place, holding on to a scrap of new land in the middle of moving water by the sheer stubbornness of staying packed tight.
