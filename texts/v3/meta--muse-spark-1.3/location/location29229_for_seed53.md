At first you think the brightness is snow. It isn't.

It is altitude and air so thin it barely counts as air, and sun coming through it unfiltered. The blue over your head goes deep, almost violet at noon, but the light on the ground is white and total. It gets into the seams of your eyes even squinted. Everything throws a shadow with a hard edge, a blue so dark it looks wet.

You are very high. You can tell by the way your own body has become unreliable — breath shallow after three steps, heart hammering on nothing, a dull ache behind the forehead. The climb up was loose scree and old, weathered granite, the kind of mountain that has long ago settled into being what it is. Not jagged, not crumbling. Rounded shoulders, sorted stones, frost-heaved into nets and polygons that have not moved in centuries except by millimeters. An old mountain. In balance with wind and cold.

And then the ground levels, just for a little while.

It is a saddle, a shallow bowl between two higher ribs of rock. Maybe a hundred paces long, half that wide. You could see the whole of it at once if you stood on either rim. It has boundaries — behind you the trail falling away, ahead the trail falling away, to left and right the ground rising into pale cliffs streaked black with manganese and rust-red with iron. Nothing grows here to soften it. That is the first strange thing your lowland brain keeps trying to correct: there should be something. A tuft, a lichen, a smear of green in a crack. There is none. Not even the orange crust lichen that clings everywhere else on this range. The wind scours too hard, the freeze comes every night even in summer, the ultraviolet burns. It is mineral only. Stone and ice and light.

What fills the bowl is dense.

Knee-high, waist-high, in places shoulder-high, a crowd of them, so close together you cannot walk straight through but must turn sideways: blades of ice and firn snow, thin as hands, leaning all one way. Penitentes, though no one here calls them that. They have grown not as drifts but as knives, sharpened by sun and sublimation. The sun hollows the pits and leaves the ridges, over and over until the whole flat is a congregation of white teeth, tilted a few degrees north, all pointing at the midday sun like plants would if plants could live here.

Between and under them are stones. Hundreds of stones stood on end by no human hand — frost-heave pushing them upright — and hundreds more stood on end by hands. That is the second thing you notice when your eyes adjust.

People come here, or came. They did not build much. They could not stay. There is a low ring of dry-stacked slabs on the lee side, three courses high, open to the east, just enough to crouch behind out of wind. The slabs are fitted without mortar, chinked with smaller flakes. Hand work. You can see the peck marks where someone knocked them square with another rock. Beside it, a collapsed niche, lintel fallen. Inside, protected from the worst of the sun, a few offerings that have not rotted because nothing rots this high and cold: a twist of copper wire, a wooden bead carved with a face worn featureless, a square of woven cloth, its red and blue almost bleached white, tied to a short stick and wedged between rocks. It snaps in the wind with a dry tick-tick-tick.

And cairns. Not the big trail ducks from below. Small, obsessive ones. Dozens, maybe a hundred, clustered where the ice blades are thinnest, on the slightly higher, drier gravel. Each is a fist-sized tower, five to nine flat stones high, balanced impossible in the wind. Some have fallen and been rebuilt. Some are joined at the base, forming little villages. From a distance they look like more ice blades. Up close they are warm gray granite glittering with quartz. Past travelers adding their stone so the place gets denser every year. The rule here seems to be: add, don't take. Make it more crowded.

Water exists, but not as you expect. There is no stream, no lake. The air itself is damp in the afternoons when cloud pushes up from the valleys thousands of meters below and condenses the instant it hits this cold bowl. Rime feathers everything — the north sides of the ice blades go furry white, the cairns grow white beards, your jacket sleeves go stiff. For an hour everything drips. At two in the afternoon, when the sun is straightest despite the cold, the pits between the blades hold an inch of melt, sky-reflecting and impossibly clear. You hear it before you see it: plink, plink, deep under the crowd, water threading down through firn into gravel. Tiny rills, a finger wide, run for three or four meters and then vanish under stones. By four the cloud lifts, the temperature drops ten degrees in twenty minutes, and every drop skins over into black glass. By night it is all locked again, ticking.

Because despite the quiet, this place is busy.

It is mostly quiet. That is true in the way high places are. No birds live up here — too high to nest, nothing to eat. Nothing buzzes, nothing calls. Even your boots on the gravel sound muffled between the ice blades. But if you crouch down and stay still until your breathing slows, you hear the work.

Wind threading through a thousand narrow slots makes a low, reedy hum that changes pitch when you move your head. Ice expanding and contracting ticks like cooling coals. Every so often a sharp *tock* as a blade tip, undercut by sun, snaps and falls. Meltwater percolating. A pebble somewhere under frost-heave clicking against another pebble. The little cloth flag worrying its stick. Your own blood in your ears, loud because there is nothing else.

Nothing here is still. That is its ecology, without plants, without animals. Sun eats ice, ice shades stone, stone holds night-cold into day, melt loosens stone, night freezes melt and jacks the stones a millimeter higher, wind sharpens everything again. The blades migrate slowly south over the season, the patterned ground sorts itself, the cairns lean and are righted. Predator and prey replaced by sun and shadow.

Stay until late and you see the unusual thing that makes people stack stones here. As the sun lowers, the light doesn't just get golden. It gets trapped. The west-facing sides of the ice blades go translucent honey, then rose, and each blade throws a long blue shadow across three others. For a few minutes the whole bowl glows from inside, as if lit underneath. The granite flecks spark. The little pools that haven't yet frozen catch fire. The shadows are so dense and overlapping you lose depth. It feels, absurdly, crowded with presence — though you are alone, though nothing lives.

Then the sun clears the rim and it ends. The temperature falls off a cliff. The water glasses over. The hum stops. The white goes blue-white, then gray. Your fingers, even in gloves, go stupid with cold.

You understand why the shelter is only a windbreak. You don't sleep here. You add your stone to the top of a cairn — choosing a flat one that fits your hand, setting it carefully so it will survive the night's cracking — and you go down, breathing hard in the thin bright air, leaving the dense, damp, lifeless bowl to its own quiet business of freezing and melting and sharpening itself.
