It is first a bowl.

Not a valley — too small and too deliberate for that. A shallow pan in dark rock, perhaps a half-mile across, rimmed all the way around by a fresh, broken lip. The rock there hasn't learned to be soft yet. Every edge is still sharp enough to cut. You can see where it came up: plates of basalt tilted and jackstrawed, black glass still showing the bubble-holes, pale dust settled only in the lee of the shards. Beyond the lip, nothing continues. Flat, pale hardpan to the horizon, wind-scoured, empty.

Inside the lip, it is lower, and therefore dimmer and cooler. The light doesn't really get in. It stays up above, a thin grey-blue wash, sun diffused through high dust to the brightness of late evening that never becomes night and never becomes morning. Down here the air pools and cools. It smells of cold stone and faint iron. It dries you instantly. Your lips crack within minutes. There is no water here and there never was — not a seep, not a stain, not a green ring to say water stood. The ground rings under your boots like fired clay.

And in that bowl: a town.

Not built. Grown. Or shed.

From the center outward run streets — you have to call them streets because they are too regular to be anything else. Broad, empty avenues radiating and then ringed, packed smooth by wind more than feet. They are far too wide for anything that lives here now. You could lie down across one and not touch the edges. The dust on them is rippled in long silent waves, unbroken by footprints except for needle-thin stilt tracks that cross in straight lines and are gone.

Along the streets stand the houses. Tall, hollow, thin-walled things the color of bone and wasp paper, ten, fifteen, twenty feet high, clustered in some places two or three together, elsewhere standing alone with vast space between. They lean slightly inward, as if listening. Their walls are layered — you can see it where one has split open: sheet over sheet of cemented fiber, salt crystal, excreted mineral, dust glued with wax. No cut marks. No nails, no bricks, no wires, no glass, no metal of any kind. No right angles. Every seam is a curve, a lip, a healed-over tear. Near the base each one has a mouth, a dark oval taller than you, that opens onto the street and breathes out air a degree colder than the air outside.

No one made these with tools. Something secreted them, the way a termite secretes a mound or a moth secretes a cocoon, only at the scale of architecture. And left them. Most are empty. If you put your head inside one and whisper, the sound doesn't come back. The walls drink it. The interior is ribbed, faintly warm to the touch despite the cold, and in the upper dark you can see the slits where light should come in but doesn't.

That is the emptiness of the place. It is urban and hollow at once. Infrastructure without inhabitants. Up on one wall someone — something — has laid down a second skin of pale lattice to reinforce a crack, still tacky. A fallen spire has been neatly hollowed and its material carried off in pellets to patch another. Drainless gutters of hardened slime run alongside the avenues, not for water but for wind: they channel dust, sorting it by weight, piling fine powder in drifts against the houses where it can be harvested.

Vegetation is sparse and has learned the same economy. Between houses, out of cracks in the pan, come tufts of black wire-grass, stiff as bristles, and low cushions of grey lichen that look like ash but are alive, and a kind of tumble-thorn that doesn't root at all but rolls very slowly around the bowl, pushed by drafts, combing static out of the air. Nothing is green. Everything holds its moisture — if it has any — in wax. Break a stem and it doesn't bleed. It seals, instantly, with a bead.

Animals are rarer still. For a long time you think there are none. Then you see movement at the edge of vision and it stops when you look. The tenders: tall, pallid striders, all leg and eye, bodies no bigger than your fist suspended four feet up on stilts. There are maybe six of them in the whole bowl. They move without sound across the dust, bending to lick salt from the walls, to paste a pellet over a hole, to pluck a wire-grass seed. They are both the builders and the predators. Once, while you watch, one freezes, then unfolds impossibly fast and spears something from under a dust ripple — a blind, fat grub, white as wax, that had been eating the foundation of a house. The strider eats it whole. No struggle you can hear. The whole food web here is that: dust feeds lichen feeds grub feeds strider feeds house when the strider dies and is walled into a crack.

And yet the place is active, constantly in motion, only motion without noise. The wind is the weather, the river, the season. Every afternoon — if afternoon means anything in this light — the air in the bowl turns over. Dust lifts in silent sheets and re-sorts itself. The thorn-rollers begin their circuit. The houses creak, a sound so low you feel it in your teeth more than hear it, as their walls expand and contract a millimeter with the cold. Fresh fractures appear overnight along the rim, shedding bright flakes that smell like struck flint. A new bud, the size of a thumb, pushes up from a crack near the center, already hollow, already ribbed inside — the start of another house that no one will live in.

The rule here is simple and absolute: nothing leaves, nothing evaporates, because there is nothing to evaporate. Water never enters the accounting. What matters is surface, and salt, and wind, and wax. To live here is to keep your inside sealed and trade only in dry things, quietly, in the cold blue dusk, in a too-large town made by bodies, waiting for bodies to come back and fill it.

If you stand in the central crossing where all the avenues meet and turn slowly, you can see the entire rim from any point. The town has edges. It ends. Beyond the lip the hardpan goes on empty to a horizon that shimmers without heat. Inside, the houses wait, breathing cold air out of their mouths, one after another, out of sync, like sleepers.
