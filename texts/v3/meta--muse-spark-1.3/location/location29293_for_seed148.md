It is small. That is the first rule you learn, because you will not believe it.

From the rim it looks like nothing — a low, cool dip in the land, hamlet-sized, the kind of hollow where cold air pools and stays. Bramble and blackthorn make a closed circle around it, a boundary you can walk in ten minutes if you keep moving. Inside that circle: darkness that is not night.

Light does not travel here. It falls a few feet from any source and gives up, thickens, settles. The sky above the dip is not sky, just a low lid of cloud and leaf-shadow, starless. What illumination there is comes late and sideways: a sodium lamp still burning somewhere on a rusted pole, furred over with moss until it is only an orange bruise. Strings of old industrial wiring sag between trunks and terraces, humming at 50 hertz, spitting a weak bead of phosphor now and then. Fungal freckles. The eyeshine of animals. You learn to see by touch and by drip.

Because for all that it is small, it is empty in a way that should not fit. The volume is wrong. You stand on what was once a high street — you can still read it: curbstones, a drain grate, the terraced fronts of two-up two-downs in soot-dark brick — and across to the other side should be twenty paces. It is not. The street goes out into a hollow gloom and goes on. Your voice does not echo back. The houses across stand too far away, thinned to facades, separated by gulfs of cold air. The place has the scale of a room and the spaciousness of a void. It is mostly nothing, held up by very little.

That very little is alive.

Vegetation here is not cover. It is substrate. It is the town.

Everything built has become grown, and everything grown has learned to be built. Ivy did not climb the mills; the mills are ivy now, or the memory of mills kept in leaf-shape. Brick terraces persist as hollow lattices of root and runner, courses and lintels still legible, but if you touch them they give like bark and breathe. Roofs have unroofed into canopy — vast burdock and nettle leaves the size of doors, black-green and dripping, layered a hundred deep, forming vaults and aisles like a cathedral nave. Floorboards are moss ten inches thick. Staircases are bracket fungi ascending in a spiral. The cotton mill at the low end, four stories, its chimney still upright, is a single organism: its walls a weave of creeper stems thick as a man's thigh, its windows empty eyes fringed with ferns, its interior one tall hollow chamber where the old line-shafts and looms still hang, suspended in aerial roots.

It is urban, unmistakably. This was organized. Streets still cross at right angles. Alleyways still run behind. Culverts, retaining walls, iron footbridges over nothing, lampposts, a chapel with its slate roof now a living skin of liverwort. Layer upon layer: cellar holes filled with black water and newts, ground floors open to the air, upper floors collapsed into hanging gardens, attics become nests. Infrastructure density without population. No people. The town kept its shape after its matter left.

The matter left because it was never much to begin with. This is raw geology. The ground under the moss is not soil — not yet. It is shattered, just-born stuff: sharp shale chips, cinder, crushed brick, clinker from the boiler houses, all angular, unrounded, as if the world here started yesterday and water has not yet had time to learn how to carve it. There are no valleys, no worn beds. Erosion has barely begun. In places the root-mat pulls back and you see the pale, embryonic under-rock sweating. Pebbles have edges that cut. The land is under construction, pre-formation, still deciding what a hill is.

So water does not flow. It condenses.

This is a damp place, not a wet one. No river runs through the hollow, though the iron bridge pretends one does. Instead, moisture arrives intermittently and stays as bead and breath. Cold air holds it until leaves grow heavy. Every surface sweats. At intervals — no schedule you can keep — a soft rain will hang in the air without falling, a mist that thickens on your coat and eyelashes, and then it passes. The rest of the time it is drip: from leaf-tip to leaf-tip to moss, *plik... plik... plik* in the dark, each drop finding a different route down. Pockets of water sit where they should not: a bathtub on a third-floor root-platform half full of black tea-colored water and wriggling larvae. A cellar become a pond two feet across, perfectly still, with a drowned lamppost reflected in it. Gutters made of curled bark that carry a thumb's width of flow for an hour and then dry. Your own breathing condenses and returns to you. You are part of the cycle for a while.

It is cool always. Your breath shows even when you are working. Sap, when you break a stem, is cold and clear and smells of iron. The air has the mineral chill of a cave church. It preserves. It keeps the brick from crumbling entirely and the machines from seizing entirely.

For the machines have not stopped.

This is the strangest industry: quiet, plant-driven, tended by no hands you see. In the mill-hollow the great wheel — once water, now sap — turns slowly, its paddles grown over with moss, its axle greased with rot. Leather belts, now strips of tough mycelium, still connect shaft to loom, and the looms still move, a half-inch per hour, weaving nothing, or weaving air, or weaving root-hair. A small engine house chuffs, very faintly, once every few minutes: a piston of heartwood in a cylinder of rust, driven by fermentation gas from a composting pit. The town's old electrified grid persists in a diminished form — low voltage harvested from decay, enough to keep that one lamp bruised-orange, enough to make the wires sing if you put your ear close. Factories here do not produce. They maintain. They are part of the metabolism, like hearts.

And they are not unattended. The place is abundant.

The emptiness is volumetric, not biological. Between the far-spaced facades, the air is crowded. Moths the size of your hand move through the dark in silent snowfalls, their wings dusted with luminous scales that shed and drift. Slugs as long as forearms patrol the moss streets, leaving silvered census trails. Pale woodlice cluster under every lifted leaf in hundreds. Eels, blind and white, work the cellar ponds and the bathtub seas. Something fox-like — too thin, too quiet, its coat green-tinged with algae — slips between the ground floors, hunting rats that hunt moths that hunt spores. Bats stitch the upper vaults. On the hanging wires, wrens have built bottle nests from machine lint. Every drip-pool blooms with springtails that explode outward at your footfall and settle again. Life fills the hollow volume the way dust fills a sunbeam, except there is no sunbeam. It eats darkness, it eats rust. The plants here do not photosynthesize so much as they slow-rot and re-knit; great leaves unfold blackly to catch not light but condensation and sound.

For it is mostly quiet. That is the second rule.

Sound attenuates like light. A footstep on moss makes almost nothing. The engine chuff, the wire hum, the drip, the soft *furr* of moth wings — these are close sounds. They do not carry across the street-gulf. Predator and prey negotiate in whispers: the rustle of a fern as the fox passes, the sudden cessation of dripping where something large has inhaled. Sometimes, deep in the mill, you hear a groan as a root-beam takes up new load, or a clink as a slate made of leaf slips and falls a long way without landing loudly. Mostly you hear your own swallow, your own joints. The town enforces quiet the way a library does. The active things — sap rising inches per night, leaves turning to track the dead lamp, eels breeding, belts tightening, foxes learning — happen without announcement. This is an ecosystem in motion at full pace, but muffled, as if under wool.

Until reality slips, and then you hear it most clearly: a street that was empty has an extra house in it that was not there before, and the house is both ruined and newly grown. If you count the chimneys on the mill terrace you get seven going one way and nine coming back. Distances re-tie themselves when unobserved. Walk downhill following drips and you arrive slightly above where you started. A door you left open is now a wall of woven hazel with your handprint still wet in its sap on the other side. Time pools like cold air — a moth you watched land yesterday is still landing. Gravity is negotiable for seeds and for lost buttons; they hang for a long while before deciding to fall sideways.

The breakdown is not violent. It is botanical. The world here is using plant logic to solve town problems: how to span a void? Grow across it thinly. How to house emptiness? Make walls that are mostly air. How to keep industry without workers? Let the building be the worker. How to be both small and vast? Be a seed: tiny from outside, endless from inside.

Rules, then, if you stay, which you should not for long because the cool gets into the marrow:

Do not trust boundaries. The thorn circle that looked ten minutes wide will take you hours to cross from inside, and will be ten minutes again when you finally emerge, with moss in your shoes and iron filings on your tongue.

Do not follow water downhill. Water here does not know downhill yet. Follow moss upstream toward the driest brick — that is where the ground is oldest, a few days older than the rest, and will hold your weight.

Do not count houses, or moths, or drips. Counting fixes them, and they resent it. They will change the number to remind you.

Do not make loud noise. The quiet is structural. The hollow is held up by thin living tension. A shout does not echo; it is absorbed, and something absorbs it back — the foxes stop, the looms pause mid-inch, the leaves turn, all together, toward you. You do not want that much coordinated attention in the dark.

Do not eat the blackberries. They are abundant, huge, shining like oil, growing straight out of mortar. They are not for you. They are the town's way of remembering sweetness. The eels get them when they fall. You get condensation.

If you must leave a mark, leave it in iron. Scratch your initials on a rusted grate. The plants cannot read iron yet; they will grow around it respectfully. Anything written in wood, leaf, or skin will be rewritten by morning into a different word, in a different hand, in sap.

In the deepest hollow, under the iron bridge that bridges no river, there is a place where the moss is thinnest and you can put your ear to the raw shale and hear the whole small-vast borough working: sap lifting, wires humming, water beading, roots tightening on brick, moth wings dusting down, eels turning in their tubs, the slow cold engine taking another breath. It sounds like nothing. It sounds like a town holding its breath to stay empty enough to stay alive.

Stay until your breath fogs in rhythm with the drips, and then go back up to the rim before you learn to photosynthesize darkness too.
