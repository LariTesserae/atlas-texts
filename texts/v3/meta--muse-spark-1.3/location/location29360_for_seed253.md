You are under water, and you are very high up.

That is the first wrongness you have to let in. The cold has weight here, a thin, clean weight pressing on your cheeks and the backs of your hands. Not crushing. Not deep-sea weight. High weight. The water is cold enough to slow you — breath comes short even though you are moving slowly, and your fingers go soft-numb at the tips. Above you, there is not sky. There is more water, blacker and colder the farther you look, until looking up and looking sideways are the same thing. Below you, impossibly far below you, there are clouds. You know this without seeing them. The light tells you.

There is almost no light.

What light there is comes down filtered through fathoms of black-green, a bruised dusk-blue that never gets brighter and never fully goes out. It is not day. It has never been day here. Maybe far above the water thins into storm and starlight leaks in, or maybe the water itself remembers light. You can see perhaps twenty meters in any direction before shapes dissolve. After that, darker shapes. After that, nothing. It is enough to navigate by, once your eyes stop hunting for a sun.

You are over a plain. A drowned summit plain.

Once — so long ago the rock has forgotten how to be sharp — this was a mountain range. You can still feel the idea of mountains in the long, almost imperceptible swells under you, broad as whales' backs. Erosion has had its way for an age upon age. No peaks. No cliffs. No scree. Everything that could be broken has been broken, rolled, smoothed down to a table. The stone that remains is pale, pitted, soft as old bone, full of shallow bowls where water has worried at it for ten thousand years. You never see it bare.

You never see anything bare here. That is the second thing.

The floor is alive. Entirely.

From edge to edge of seeing, to the limit of the valley and past it, the ancient stone is sheathed in a living mat — felted, layered, many hands thick. Dark olive mosses, black kelps flattened by cold, root-hair felts the color of wet slate, sponges that give faintly under your fins or hands. It breathes, almost imperceptibly. If you press your palm to it, you feel the slow push back. Nothing is dead. Fallen leaves do not rot here; they are caught and re-woven before they can settle. Silt does not accumulate; the mat drinks it. There is no mud, no sand, no exposed rock. Even pebbles are wrapped in green-black skin. The world has no natural substrate left. Everything you touch has been grown, tended, placed.

And out of that mat, far apart, the towers rise.

Sparse. That is the word your body understands first. For long stretches there is only the glowing mat below and the empty black water above — a volume so open you feel you could swim for an hour in any direction and meet nothing. The scale is a valley, a small country: multiple swells, multiple darks, but all the same system. You drift over emptiness that is not empty, water so clear and so dark it feels like flying at night.

Then a shape condenses ahead. A stalk. A house. A tree that is also a town.

They rise one by one, a hundred meters apart, sometimes more: immense hollow stems, twenty, thirty meters thick at the base, tapering as they climb out of sight into the dark above. Not wood — something between kelp and reed and muscle, ribbed vertically, banded with old leaf scars. At their bases they flare into buttresses that merge with the floor-mat, indistinguishable. Higher up they branch, and branch again, and each branch has been bent, grafted, woven into rooms. Flared cups big enough to sleep in. Latticed causeways spanning between limbs, swaying almost imperceptibly. Funnel crowns at the top that open and close with terrible slowness, sieving plankton and snow from the water.

Nothing here was built. Everything was grown into building. There are no tools, no cut edges, no rope, no stone blocks, no metal, nothing dead made sharp. If a wall is needed, two living branches are coaxed together until they fuse. If a door is needed, the tissue is taught to leave a hole. You can see the pruning: clean, healed lips where gardeners have bitten stems back. You can see where a walkway has been thickened by years of treading — by fins, by bellies, by many small clawed feet — the living fibers calloused into a path.

For there is abundant life. It just keeps its distance until you are inside it.

At first you think you are alone in the black between towers. Then your eyes adjust and the water fills.

Clouds of small, pale crustaceans hang like dust in the light shafts, clicking. Schools of long, eyeless white fish pour around a tower base in a single decision, turning flank-on to catch what little light there is, flashing all at once. Slow newts as big as canoes haul themselves along the mat on six soft limbs, grazing, leaving brighter trails that close behind them within minutes. In the cups and lattices above, things nest: coiled eels with translucent throats that pulse, colonies of bell-animals that retract when you pass, crabs with mossy backs that tend the walls, nipping away parasites.

All of them are gardeners. All of them are tenants. The newts prune. The fish fertilize. The crabs stitch. The towers feed them, shelter them, and in return are kept in shape — rooms kept open, sieves kept clear. No single species made this place. The place is the agreement they all keep.

It is loud. In air this would be deafening; in water it lives in your chest.

There is a constant low thrumming as the slow summit current pushes through a thousand sieve-crowns — water moving, always, at the same unhurried pace, straining through living mesh. Under that, the towers themselves creak: deep, wooden groans as the immense stalks flex a centimeter and return. And the animals never stop. The eels sing — long descending moans that travel for kilometers in the cold and come back off distant towers. The crustacean clouds click and hiss like static. Somewhere out of sight, something large and slow beats water rhythmically, thump … thump … thump, never hurrying, never stopping. You feel it in your teeth. The water carries everything to you. There is no quiet direction to turn to.

And yet nothing changes.

That is the oldest feeling here, older than the smoothed stone. The current does not gust or slacken. It loops around the summit basin in a great, cold gyre and has looped so long the towers lean into it permanently. The funnel crowns open a fraction, close a fraction. The fish school pours around the same base, in the same direction. The mat neither advances nor retreats. Growth and grazing are perfectly balanced, have been balanced so long that a stem thickens on one side exactly as fast as it is eaten on the other. The temperature does not rise or fall. The dusk-blue does not brighten or dim. Even your own movement feels absorbed — wake dissolving behind you in seconds, as if the water insists on forgetting disturbance.

The rules, as you learn them by drifting:

Stay in the water. There is no out. The surface, if there is a surface, is far above where light is, and nothing goes there.

Touch only living things. There is nothing else to touch. If you scrape the mat, it heals under your hand.

Do not expect sharpness. No tooth here is sharp for long; no edge stays. Everything rounds, fuses, softens.

Make noise or listen — both are the same. You will be heard. You are already part of the chorus, your breath and kick adding to the thrum.

Do not wait for morning. Morning ended here before the mountains flattened. There is only this cool, black-blue, endless late hour, and it is enough for everything to live by.

Swim on a little farther and another tower will emerge from the dark ahead, exactly as far away as the last one was, its crown filtering, its rooms full of pulsing throats and clicking legs, its base flared into the endless living floor. Between you and it: black water, cold on your face, full of small white lives drifting like snow that never lands. Below you, the ancient bone of the world, wrapped and kept warm by green. Above you, more water, and beyond that, stars you will never see, over clouds you will never touch.

High, drowned, tended, dark, and awake — the summit sea keeps itself.
