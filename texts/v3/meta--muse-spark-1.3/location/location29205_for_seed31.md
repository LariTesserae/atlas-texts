On the high ground, the air stays warm.

That is the first wrong-feeling thing, and then you stop feeling it is wrong. You have climbed for days to get here — the cliffs falling away below in grey-green forest, the switchbacks, the thinning — and you expect cold. Instead the plateau opens and breathes warm damp air against your face, the same temperature as your skin, so you stop noticing temperature at all after a minute.

It is a broad bowl in the sky. Not a peak, a place. You can see most of it from the rim where the trail comes over: three or four miles across to the far rim, a shallow dish of dark young rock cupping light. Outside the rims, on all sides, the world drops. Clouds hang below the level of your feet out there, white and flat-topped, and the lowlands are a blue haze beyond them. In here, above them, the light is plain midday light, high and even and uncolored. Nothing theatrical. The sun is almost straight up through a thin veil, so shadows are short and soft under every stone.

The rock is new. You can tell even without knowing geology, because nothing is rounded yet. The far rim is a saw of black teeth, unweathered, with fresh scars where slabs have sheared off and lie below exactly where they fell, angular, edges still sharp enough to cut a hand. The streams that drain the bowl have cut narrow slots straight down, with hard lips where they drop in white threads. There are no wide valleys, no smoothed hills. Even the soil, where there is soil, is thin and gritty over bedrock, black to brown, with bright mineral faces winking in it.

And water is everywhere in it.

Not in one lake, but near the surface everywhere, as if the whole bowl is saturated and just barely holding. The center is marsh — open sheets of knee-deep water with sedge islands and causeways over them — and around the center, in every low pocket, taro and reed beds in standing water that mirrors the sky. Springs come straight out of the cliff feet all around the bowl, not gushing but weeping, in dark wet streaks down the basalt where the moss is almost black-green with wetness. Where the trail cuts under an overhang, water beads and falls steadily, drop by drop, darkening the stone. In the mornings, they say, and in the evenings, mist condenses out of nothing and settles, and leaves drip even when it has not rained. Your clothes never quite dry here. Your footprints fill with water behind you in the low fields.

Between the wet places are the dryish places, and that is where the green is lushest. Not forest — clumps. A cluster of tree-ferns in a hollow, twenty feet tall, trunks shaggy with old leaf bases, crowns spreading. Ferns and broad-leafed herbs waist high where a seep feeds them. Moss thick as a mattress over fallen blocks. The gardens people have made are the lushest of all: mounded beds of sweet potato vine, gourds on trellises of thin poles, bananas with torn leaves in sheltered corners, stands of a tall reed they use for everything. All of it very green, wet green, with water shining between stems.

But scattered. That is the rule here. Nothing crowds. A house, then a hundred yards of marsh and garden, then another house. A clump of ferns, then fifty yards of bare scoria and shallow pool with sky in it. The town — for it is a town — is spread so thin you almost miss that it is planned until you see the lines.

From the rim you can read them: two long straight causeways crossing near the middle, built of fitted basalt slabs laid without mortar, raised three feet above high water, wide enough for six people to walk abreast. Smaller causeways branching off at right angles. Canals alongside them, straight too, with simple sluices of wood and stone where a single plank can be lifted to flood or drain a field. Low walls marking field edges. Near the crossing, a large level plaza of pounded earth and stone, with a low stepped platform on one side and a cistern sunk beside it, stone-lined, always full to the brim with clear seep water. Further out, houses: perhaps two hundred of them, though you would have to walk all day to count.

Each house is small and alike — a rectangle of dry-stone wall to waist height, and above that woven reed and mud plaster, and above that a steep thatch of the tall reed, tied in thick layers that shed water in sheets when the mist thickens. Many stand on short stone piers over damp ground, with a notched-log stair and a narrow porch where baskets sit, and gourds hang drying from the eaves. No chimneys. Cooking fires are small and outside under a lean-to, and the smoke rises straight up a few feet and dissolves in the damp.

Everything is hand-made, and you can see the hand in it. The basalt blocks are not sawn but pecked and split; their faces show the pits of hammer-stones. The wood of the sluices and bridges and trellises is adze-smoothed, silvery with constant wet. Baskets are coiled and twined, tight enough to carry mud. There is no metal glint anywhere, no wheel, no draft animal, no plow — only wooden spades, digging sticks fire-hardened at the tip, stone hoes, carrying straps. The work is slow and can be seen half-finished and left: a wall going up course by course, a new canal ditch with the spoil still wet beside it.

There are people, but few for the size of the plan. You might walk the main causeway for half an hour and pass only one woman mending a sluice, shin-deep in water, lifting and dropping the plank to judge the flow, and a child carrying a bundle of cut reeds, bare feet silent on wet stone. They do not call out. They nod. The plaza, big enough for thousands, holds a dozen people sorting tubers into piles, working without talking.

And there is nothing else alive that moves on its own.

No dogs. No chickens. No goats. No birds circling the rim. No frogs in the marsh at dusk, no fish fin in the canals, no dragonflies, no mosquitoes even — something about the newness and the height, the isolation of this lifted bowl. The colonists brought seeds and cuttings up on their backs, grain in baskets, tubers wrapped in leaves. Animals did not follow, and nothing wild has found its way up the cliffs yet. So the gardens have no manure, only rotted green matter and ash, and no pests to pick off. The broad leaves are whole, uneaten. Pollination, where wind does not do it, is done by hand, with a feather or a fingertip, patiently, plant by plant.

Because of that the quiet is complete in a way you have never heard.

Stand still off the causeway, in the reeds. What you hear is water, only water, and even that is quiet: the seep and trickle of the springs, the thin fall of the rim waterfalls, the plink of condensation from a banana leaf into standing water, your own breathing, a little louder than usual because the air is thin. No buzz, no chirp, no bark, no song. Human sounds do not carry far in the damp — a stone set down, a footstep in mud, the soft knock of a wooden tool — and then they stop, and water takes it back. Even the wind is muted, broken by the rim into slow stirrings that move the mist but make no noise in the thatch.

Nothing changes here, or changes so slowly it might as well not. This is the other rule you learn after a day. The water level in the cistern is the same morning and evening. The clouds below the rim form and dissolve in the same place. The temperature at noon and at midnight differs by only a few degrees — warm, damp, breathable — the equatorial highland holding its equilibrium. There is no season for flood or drought; the springs weep at the same rate, the marsh holds the same depth, the thatch rots and is replaced at the same pace as it has always been. Lichen spreads on the new rock at a finger-width per decade. The town does not grow or shrink; they build a house when a child marries, they let one melt back into mound and reeds when its old people die. The causeways remain straight. The plaza remains swept.

If you stay, you fall into it. You learn to step from stone to stone without splashing. You learn to keep your tools dry under the eave, to clear a ditch with your hands, to hand-pollinate a gourd flower in the still noon. You learn not to listen for animals, and after a while you stop expecting them. The world narrows to stone underfoot, water at ankle height, green at waist height, warm air, plain light, and the far black rim holding it all up out of the clouds — a wide, sparse, carefully kept place that asks for nothing but maintenance, and gives back water and green, day after day without variation.
