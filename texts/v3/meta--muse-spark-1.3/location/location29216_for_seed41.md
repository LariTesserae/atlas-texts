It doesn't look like anything from above.

The plain goes on — dry, sun-beaten, the color of bleached bone and lion grass — until it simply stops. A mouth. Not wide. You could throw a stone across it if you threw well, maybe thirty paces rim to rim, raggedly oval. From the heat-shimmer you would walk right past it, except the air changes a few steps before the edge: cooler on your ankles, then suddenly hot and sweet.

The ground gives way inward.

The pit drops straight and then bellies out, like a throat swallowed halfway. Forty meters, maybe fifty. Limestone, old collapse, long since settled. Nothing raw about it. The edges are not jagged anymore; they are rounded, lipped, softened by centuries of rain and root. Weathered ribs of rock show through moss the way old bones show through skin. Everything leans in. The figs at the rim lean in. The light leans in.

You have to climb down, hand over hand along roots. There is no path now, though there was once the idea of one — shallow footholds cut into the north wall, each one cupped now with soil and fern, each one holding a little green garden. No rope, no ladder, no device left. At the bottom, what might have been a low stone curb, a cistern lip or a hut foundation, lies tipped and split by a strangler fig. It is not a ruin so much as a suggestion that people once knew to come here for coolness and then stopped needing to.

Halfway down the heat changes. Above: dry wind, cicadas screaming. Below the rim: no wind at all. The air gets trapped and cooked. Hot, dim, bathhouse-hot. Sweat doesn't evaporate, it accumulates. It runs down your spine and your forearms come away wet without having touched anything.

Light comes only as a single slanted column, and only for a few hours around midday. It doesn't illuminate so much as hang. Dust and spores and tiny white midges drift through it very slowly, as if falling through honey. The walls remain in a permanent green dusk. Your eyes adjust and then keep adjusting; there is always more detail than you thought. Lichen the color of old copper. Black wet streaks. Pale roots as thick as your thigh coming down a hundred feet from the rim trees, never touching soil until the very last moment, then exploding into a broom of feeders.

It is damp, but not wet. That is the rule here.

There is no stream, no lake. This is a place that drinks from the air. In the morning, everything is beaded. The underside of every leaf, the belly of every vine, the overhangs of rock — all furred with condensation that has formed overnight when the hot trapped air finally kisses the cooler stone. It gathers and swells and drops. *Plip.* That is the loudest sound you will hear.

Pockets of water in larger dryness. A shallow pan in the limestone floor, no deeper than your palm, black with tannin. A second, smaller, choked with fallen flowers. Seep-lines where the rock weeps continuously, dark green with algae and liverwort. If you press your cheek to the stone you can smell it: mineral, iron, rot-sweet. Between rains — which may be weeks apart up top — this is enough. The pit makes its own rain. Water falls upward first as vapor off the hot leaf-litter, hits the cool wall shadow, and returns as dew.

And because of that, it is lush in a way the plain above could never afford. Not tall jungle. Dense, low, layered, near-touching. From rim to floor in three tiers.

Up top: the hangers. Lianas, curtain fig, wild grapevine, dropping straight down, some with leaves only at the very tip, twenty meters of bare cord. Orchids cling to them midway, white roots exposed to air.

Mid-wall: the mat. Ferns in every crack, mosses a foot deep, pillowy, swallowing sound. Begonias with waxy leaves turned, all of them, inward toward the light shaft, like a crowd turning faces to a speaker. Fungi like ears and shelves.

Floor: the rot. Leaf litter knee-deep in places, steaming faintly. Seedlings so crowded their stems are white from reaching. Fallen fig fruits fermenting, smelling of wine and sick. Aroids with leaves bigger than a doorway unfurling in the dim.

You cannot take two steps without touching five living things.

That is why it is so full — and so quiet.

At first you think it is empty. Then you see movement everywhere, but you hear nothing.

Moths, huge ones, the size of your hand, pale green and owl-eyed, lift off bark and settle again without a wingbeat you can hear. The moss deadens everything. Geckos, translucent-bellied, crowd the seep-walls shoulder to shoulder, throats pulsing, snapping up midges with a soft, wet *tick* that barely carries three feet. Millipedes as long as your forearm move in slow processions through the litter. Land crabs the color of wet brick work the drip-pans at night, their claws muffled by slime. High above, swifts and cave swallows cut the mouth of the pit in screaming loops — but down here their cries don't reach. Only an occasional feather spirals down through the light, turning and turning.

Even predation is silent. A golden orb-weaver has strung a wheel two meters across between roots; the silk is so thick with dew it looks jeweled. A mantis the color of dead leaf waits on aroids. A vine snake hangs motionless among the lianas, tasting air. No buzzing hive roar, no frog chorus. A hush, enforced by the shape of stone and moss. Sound goes into the walls and does not come back. If you clap, it dies instantly. The place eats noise.

That is its small strangeness. Things behave slightly off-plumb. Shadows lean toward the center even when the sun has moved. Dew forms only on downward-facing surfaces. Leaves on the north wall are larger than on the south by half, though both get equal light. The snails here — and there are hundreds, clustered in the dampest crevices, amber and banded — all coil the same way, left-handed. No one taught them.

Scattered through all this green are buildings, but none made by hands.

Mud-dauber wasps have potted the underside of every overhang with rows of tiny clay jugs, each sealed. Spiders have folded leaves into tight retreats and lashed them with silk. Ants have built paper galleries along a root the size of a road, with off-ramps and refuse piles. A bowerbird — lost, or choosing to stay — has cleared a court no wider than a platter on the drier side of the floor, decorated it with white snail shells, blue beetle elytra, and one inexplicable red plastic cap that must have fallen from above years ago. It is the only bright manufactured thing here, and he polishes it daily.

Everything is working, all the time. This is not a still place, though it is silent. Roots tighten and split rock grain by grain. Figs drop fruit, fruit ferments, flies breed, geckos eat flies, snakes eat geckos, fungi eat everything that falls. The light shaft crawls across the floor an inch a minute, and the whole photosynthetic crowd tracks it. At dusk, the air lifts as bats pour out of a slit in the lower wall you never noticed during the day — hundreds of them, funneling up and out without a sound you can catch, gone to the plain, returning before dawn to plaster the slit with guano that feeds the ferns below. Condensation, drip, evaporation, drip again.

The rules are simple, and you feel them in your skin within minutes:

Stay low. Stay damp. Make no noise.
Drink what condenses. Eat what falls.
Do not try to leave except by climbing toward the light.

If you sit long enough at the bottom, with your back against the warm stone and your breathing gone shallow to match the heat, the pit stops being a hole and becomes a room. A grove-room. A cathedral with no roof but a small bright eye. You can see its entire boundary at once — wall all around, sky like a coin above — and yet you cannot take it all in, because every square inch is occupied three deep, leaf over insect over fungus over stone.

Above, the plain is vast and empty and loud with wind. Here, the world is small, crowded, hot, half-dark, and listening so intently to itself that it has decided, collectively, to whisper.
