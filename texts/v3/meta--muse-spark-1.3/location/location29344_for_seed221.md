There is a room you can only get to on your knees.

Not on any plan the station hands out anymore — a pocket left over between things, high up where the mountain plant gets thin to stand the wind. You unbolt a hatch the size of a breadbox in the back of Level 9, past the penstock gallery, and you crawl three meters through square duct, and then the duct simply widens a hand's width on every side and stops. That is the place.

It was never meant to be inhabited. It was meant to be thickness. Outer skin of riveted steel to take the weather at 3,700 meters, inner skin of poured concrete around the surge tank, and between them a service void for a man to stand — if he hunches — to reach the valves. A closet inside the mountain's machine. Everything in it is made. There is no rock here, no soil, no outside. Even the dust tastes of cement and paint. Floor is galvanized grate over a sump. Walls are plate and concrete. Ceiling is a forest of pipe.

Everything is close. That is the first law. You cannot extend your arms without touching three temperatures of metal: the cold feedwater main sweating on your left, the lukewarm tracer line on your right, the conduit bank overhead humming faintly warm with 400 volts. Valves like iron artichokes. Junction boxes. Mineral wool bulging from split lagging. Chains. A ladder that goes nowhere, bolted flat to the wall because the spec said ladder. It is dense the way a switchboard is dense, layer on layer, near-touching, no air wasted.

The air that is left is wet and cold enough to hurt.

Cold first. This high, unheated, wrapped in wind, the steel radiates winter straight through your jacket. Your breath stays. Your fingertips go stupid in a minute on the handwheel. The cold has a metallic edge, a clean burn, cut with ozone from the transformers and a sweet rot of ammonia and feather-dust and machine oil. It is dim the way deep water is dim. One caged bulkhead lamp, greenish, behind wire glass, coated in moth-scale and never cleaned. It does not flicker. It has not flickered, the log says, in eleven years. And a second light: a vertical slit where the outer louver doesn't quite meet, no wider than a thumb. Through it, vertigo — white, moving fast. Cloud deck a kilometer below, blue shadow of the range, a sound of wind so far down it is only pressure in the ears. You have to remind your knees you are inside.

Water is everywhere and has nowhere to go.

It does not rain in here. It exudes. The cold feedwater main sweats continuously in beads that grow, merge, run in crooked courses down the paint and fall from the lowest bolt. The concrete weeps in dark patches. The ceiling seam drips — if you put your pail under it — at a rate that the old maintenance men measured once at forty-two seconds between drops and then stopped measuring because it never changed. Under the grate, the sump holds three inches of black water that never rises and never falls. Condensation equals seep equals evaporation. The atmosphere itself condenses on your eyebrows, on the backs of your hands. Drip pans have grown lace collars of pale mineral, little nipples of calcite no longer than an eyelash. First rust blooms under every screw head like dried blood, a perfect orange halo, and stops. Paint blisters have risen like smallpox across the lower plates and never popped.

This is geology that hasn't decided to start yet. Everything looks poured last week and left to cure for a hundred years. Raw, embryonic. No erosion, only the hint of it. The promise of stalactites. The promise of rust-through.

What lives here should not be able to live here.

At first, in the dim, you think the walls are upholstered. Then the upholstery breathes.

Moths. Thousands of them, high-altitude winter moths the color of wet paper, shoulder to shoulder on every warm surface, especially the conduit bank, layered two and three deep, wings folded, antennae shivering almost imperceptibly. Between them, smaller black midges that breed in the sump slime rise in a permanent low cloud that never disperses. Harvestmen knot in the corners in writhing beards of legs. House spiders, pale from generations without sun, have strung sheet webs from pipe to valve to lamp cage, sagging with husks. Silverfish ripple through the sodden lagging.

And the birds.

Rock doves, maybe eighty, maybe a hundred — counting is impossible because they do not move away, only shift — crammed on the pipes, on the valve stems, on the lip of the drip pan, on each other's backs. Dirty white, blue-barred, iridescent green at the neck gone grey with dust. They are puffed to twice their size against the cold. Their feet, vivid red, grip the freezing steel. They watch you with orange eyes without alarm. A few pale squabs, almost translucent, gape silently from a nest made of wire ties, moth wings, and mineral wool tucked behind a flange where no nest should fit.

There is almost no green. Only where the louver slit throws its knife of daylight across the concrete for an hour that never moves — it never moves — a smear of black-green algae, a thread of moss in a bolt-hole, three fern prouts no taller than your fingernail, white for lack of light, leaning toward the bulb that is always on. They have been that tall for as long as anyone remembers.

The sound will make your teeth ache before your ears understand it.

Through the concrete on one side comes the plant itself: the surge tank and the penstock below it, a full-bore roar of water falling two thousand meters inside steel, and the 50-cycle throb of the transformers. It is not loud the way a crash is loud. It is loud the way weight is heavy — a bass you feel in the sternum, in the marrow of your shinbones against the grate, perfectly steady, without swell or fade. No startup. No shutdown. The needle never moves.

Over it, inside the steel box, the fauna makes its own weather. Cooing amplified to a hollow moan by the plates. A hundred claws ticking. Sudden panicked flutter when a bird loses its footing — a detonation of wings in a closet, thunder in a drum — then settling. The high, endless whine of midges, thousands of tiny wings, that finds the resonant frequency of the duct you crawled in through and sings there. The drip, when it lands in the pail, is not heard so much as felt as a brief hole punched in the roar, and then the roar closes again.

You expect it to change. It doesn't.

That's the strangeness, beyond the simple fact of a dovecote in a machine. Nothing cycles. The sump never overflows. The bulb never burns out. One pigeon leaves through the louver slit into that terrible white wind and — within minutes — another squeezes in, or the same one returns, so the number on the pipes is always the same. The moths on the lamp die and are replaced by moths from the wall. The spiders eat the midges, the mice — yes, there are two mice, you will see them eventually, moving like slow thoughts along the conduit — eat the spiders' leavings, the doves eat the moths stunned by the light, the mites eat the dove dander, the fungus eats the dead. Water in equals water out. Heat in from the cables equals heat lost through the skin to the storm. The fern sprouts neither grow nor die.

Even the birds have learned the equilibrium. They all face inward, toward the tracer line, tails to the louver draft. When they coo, they coo together in a low pulse that, for a half-second every few minutes, cancels a harmonic of the transformer hum so perfectly that a pocket of true silence opens behind your eyes — and then fills again. The old hands say if you stay long enough your own breathing joins it without your permission.

You will not stay long. The cold gets into the knees on the grate, the wet gets into the lungs, the roar gets into the bones until you feel you have always been hearing it. You back out on your knees through the duct, re-bolt the hatch, and the pressure in your ears clicks once for the altitude.

Inside, the lamp stays on. The drop forms on the bolt. Forty-two seconds. The moths shiver. The doves puff and resettle, shoulder to shoulder, warm spots in a freezing, weeping, man-made womb bolted to the sky, waiting for nothing to happen — which is exactly what happens, continuously.
