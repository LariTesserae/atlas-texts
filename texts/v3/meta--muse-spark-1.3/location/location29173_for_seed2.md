It is not an ocean. It remembers being a hole.

You stand for a second on the rim — though rim is the wrong word because there is no natural edge left. There is a guardrail, a haul road wide enough for four trucks, a concrete lip poured straight as a ruler that runs past curvature until haze takes it. Behind you, flat land at grade, cool wind, low factories and transformer yards still blinking. In front of you, the ground simply stops being ground and becomes architecture going down.

The Terraced Sea.

It drops in steps. Each step is a story of industry tall — a hundred meters of cut wall, then a bench as wide as an airfield, then another wall, then another bench, spiraling down and down until blue takes distance and depth together. It is too large to see across. On a clear cool day you can see three, four terraces down on your side, the opposite wall only as a pale vertical weather, and below that, water haze and cloud. It makes its own clouds. The scale does that. Warm air off the rim factories hits the cold breath coming up off a million acres of cool water and condenses and hangs, a thin low stratus that lives only inside the pit.

Everything is made. That is the first rule. There is no shore, no beach, no mudflat that wasn't poured or blasted. The walls are fresh-cut rock faced with shotcrete, still sharp. The corners haven't softened. You can see the drill marks, half-moons in rows, where the last blasts went. You can see the rock bolts, galvanized heads in grids, each with a drip of rust only a few inches long because this is all young. The benches are old haul roads: crushed rock compacted, then concrete panels, expansion joints still clean, painted lines still yellow-white under ten feet of water. The water hasn't had time to round anything. It breaks against a 90-degree edge and sheets off clean.

Because it is drowned. The sea was let in — or the rivers were, and the rain, and the groundwater that the pumps stopped holding back. Now water owns almost all of it. From the second bench down, everything is submerged. Cool, clear, green-grey water, 12 degrees, the kind that takes your breath even through a wetsuit and leaves your fingers stupid in minutes. Up top, where light is full daylight, you can see straight down through thirty feet: the road markings, a dropped wrench, a line of empty ore cars sitting on rails, patient under water.

Light comes straight down. Normal noon light, overcast-bright. It doesn't feel like the deep sea. It feels like a rainy day in a quarry that got too big. Sun through high cloud, diffused, losing itself meter by meter. The first terrace down is bright aquamarine. The second is jade. By the fourth, it's slate blue and the sun is only a white ache above. Yet even deep, the work lights are on. That is the strange continuity — the industrial plant didn't drown so much as it kept running underwater. On every third bench, a pump house, a gantry crane, a row of sodium and LED floods on steel masts, still burning in the daytime, still fed by cables from the rim. Their cones cut through the water full of motes. At night from the rim you can see the whole spiral glowing faintly under water, like a city seen through ice.

It is mostly empty. That is the second rule. The volume is so vast that nothing fills it. You can swim out from a wall over a drowned bench and there is just... distance. Poured concrete extending to a vanishing edge. Water above you fifty meters to the surface, water to the side hundreds of meters to the next wall. No fish, no weed, just the tick of cooling concrete and your own bubbles. Then, all at once, life.

Because for all the emptiness, it is abundant where it decides to be. The biology hasn't read the plan. With almost no plants — only a sparse, stubborn skin of things: a velvet green biofilm on the sunlit concrete, a few cold-water kelps clinging where a bolt head gives them purchase, their stipes no longer than your arm, waving white-tipped in the current, a tuft of ditch grass rooted in a cracked expansion joint — with almost nothing green, there should be nothing else. Instead there are millions.

Mussels came first. They crust the sharp edges, blue-black clusters thick along every wall corner from the waterline down to the light limit, prying into the fresh shotcrete. Where mussels are, eels are. Long, grey-green, cool-water eels that live in the drain galleries and hunt at dusk along the benches. And above them, the silversides — huge schools, tens of thousands, moving as one slow muscle through the empty volumes. In the clear upper water you hear them before you see them: a sudden rain-click of thousands of tails turning. They flash when the work lights catch them, turning a whole submerged airfield silver. Diving birds work the top hundred feet — cormorants and gulls and terns that nest on the dry rim cranes and commute down, stabbing into the schools. Their cries are thin and lost against the larger noise.

The noise. It is loud. Always. Not surf-loud, machine-loud. Water pours over the rim in a dozen places where rivers were diverted to feed the pit: white engineered waterfalls that drop a hundred meters to the first bench pool and pound it to foam, the roar bouncing wall to wall until you can't tell direction. Deeper, the low octave: the big dewatering pumps, reversed now, still turning, moving water from bench to bench to keep the level stable, a felt-more-than-heard thrum through the concrete and through your chest if you're submerged. Add the groan of gantry cranes swaying almost imperceptibly in pit wind, cables thrumming, sheet metal on a half-drowned maintenance shed banging in a slow rhythm, and the constant drip and trickle and runnel of seep. Even underwater, you hear it: the concrete transmits. A crackle of mussels feeding, the whine of a distant turbine, the boom when a seiche wave — the pit's own slow tide, meters high, sloshing back and forth over hours — slaps a vertical wall.

It is active. Nothing is still for long. The weather cycles inside. Morning mist burns off the upper water by ten. By afternoon, wind funnels along the spiral roads and chops the surface white, and the chop stacks against the lee walls and breaks in concrete surf — no sand to soften it, just spray on vertical. By evening, cold air sinks, fog rebuilds from the center out, and the work lights bloom in it. Currents run constantly, driven by intakes and outfalls: a slow, cool gyre that carries larvae and oil sheen and concrete dust in a great circle around the pit, bench by bench descending. Storms spin up that never exist outside; rain that falls on the surrounding plain falls harder inside, wrung out by the cold pool.

Materials are simple and everywhere: concrete, basalt aggregate sharp under gloves, steel, painted safety yellow now blistered, rubber from tires, copper cable, glass from broken cab windows. The bottom — no one has seen the bottom in years, but sonar says it is flat. A final poured pad, three kilometers down, with settling ponds and sump pits and the foundations for a crusher that was never installed. Fresh, still. Sharp rebar chairs sticking up waiting for a pour that will now never come, colonized by white anemones like frost.

If you go down, in a cage elevator still running on its rails down a wall face, or in a hardhat dive, you feel the cold increase, the light thin, the pressure build in your ears, and the strange comfort that this place, though drowned and empty and too big, is still legible. A stair is a stair even underwater. A road sign — BENCH 14 — 30 KM/H — CURVE — is still reflective when your lamp hits it under twenty feet of green water, and a school of perch shelter in its lee. A lunchroom with its windows gone holds eels in the lockers.

No part of it is natural, and no part of it is finished. The edges are too sharp to be old. A flake of shotcrete falls and there is bright new grey underneath. A rockfall from an upper wall exposes unweathered stone that smells faintly of explosives still. The sea is young here. It hasn't learned how to round a corner yet.
