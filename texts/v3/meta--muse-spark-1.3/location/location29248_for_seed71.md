This is the Table. That is what the few who live here call it, because there is no other word that holds.

A continent lifted up and left there. High enough that the air is thin and goes bright and hard in your lungs. Cold — not the sharp killing cold of deep winter, but the old, patient cold that never quite leaves. Even at noon in summer your breath shows. At night frost feathers everything. The sun is normal. A pale white-yellow disc, ordinary light, no strange color to it. It just has farther to fall.

It was mountains once. You can tell. Under everything, the bones of mountains. But that was so long ago that the world has finished with eroding. Ridges worn down to long swells. Valleys filled with their own dust until they are only shallow sighs in the land. Bedrock come up through the thin soil like the backs of huge buried animals — granite and slate and quartzite polished by wind and ice until they shine faintly when wet. Nothing is jagged anymore. Everything has settled into its final shape. Walking here is walking on something finished. The stones do not roll. The slopes do not slide. The land has decided.

And because it is so vast — because it goes on past the horizon in every direction and makes its own weather in the middle — you can walk for days in that finished quiet and see nothing change except the light.

Sparse. That is the law here.

Life does not cover the Table. It gathers.

In the hollows, in the lee of those worn stone backs, where condensation collects, there is green so dense and so soft it hurts to look at after so much grey. Not trees as you know them. Cushion-plants, moss a foot deep, liverworts black-green and slick, dwarf willow no taller than your ankle but a hundred years old, lichen in rust and bone-white and sulfur. In late summer, which lasts two weeks, everything exhales spores at once and the hollows steam faintly. If you kneel and put your ear close you can hear the trickle of water moving under the moss, and if you stay still long enough you can watch the moss move. It grows while you watch. Tendrils creeping over stone an inch an hour. Fronds unrolling. This place is active, biologically furious in its slow way. Without anything to eat it, the green just keeps building on itself, peat over peat, until the hollows bulge upward like rising bread.

Between the hollows: miles of nothing but wind-scoured stone pavement, frost-cracked polygons, thin grit that squeaks underfoot. Emptiness with a texture.

Water here is not lakes, not seas. It is dampness that comes and goes. Mornings, the whole Table drowns in fog — a white sea that pours between the swells, that rivers through the air. You wade through it to the waist. By midday it burns off and condenses on every blade and wool thread into beads that fall with a ticking sound. Afternoon, brief rain, always brief, that never quite reaches the ground before the wind shreds it. Evening, frost again. And scattered, impossibly clear, are the tarns: shallow, black, perfectly still pans of water sitting in stone bowls with no inlet and no outlet. They are fed only by air. You can drink from them. They taste of stone and sky. Sometimes you find old shorelines ten feet above the water, and ten feet below the water, stairs going down.

Where there is water and green, there are the towns.

From a distance you mistake them for more bedrock, because they are made of the same stone, and lichen has taken them. But as you come closer the pattern resolves: dry-stone walls, terraces stepping down into a hollow to trap the damp, roundhouses with turf roofs so thick the moss on them is deeper than in the wild, cisterns lined with clay, causeways of flat slabs laid across the bog, cairns, field walls running for miles to nowhere.

No metal to speak of. A few hammered copper pins, green with age, holding a door. Everything else is hand-work: stone mauls, antler picks — though where the antler came from no one will say — basalt flakes, bone awls, woven willow, felted hair. Primitive work, but organized. Layer upon layer. You can see where a wall was built, fell, was built on top of itself five, ten times. Streets worn in the stone by feet over centuries. A city, in scale, if not in crowd. Some of these settlements would house thousands, by their granaries and sleeping platforms.

Almost no one lives in them.

You may walk through a town of three hundred houses and find three hearths smoking. An old woman scraping lichen into a pot. A man mending a wall, taking a stone down and setting the same stone back up a fraction straighter, as his father did. Children — very few children — stacking pebbles into towers and knocking them down without a sound. They are quiet people. They have to be. Sound does not carry right here, and loud noise feels dangerous.

And there are no other animals. None.

No birds in the sky. No gulls over the tarns. No sheep, no goats, no dogs beside the hearths. No flies over the middens. No worms in the soil when you dig — just roots and roots and cold. No footprints but human ones. The silence this makes is not like a forest at night. It is absolute in a particular frequency. You hear wind in moss, which is almost no sound at all, like breathing through cloth. You hear your own joints. You hear water ticking off leaves. You do not hear chitter, or bark, or buzz, or call. The people here do not keep animals and do not seem to miss them. If you ask where the animals went, they look puzzled, as if you asked where the second sun went.

The green has taken over those jobs. Flies are replaced by drifting spores. Worms by frost-heave, churning the soil each night. Scavengers by moss, which covers a dropped tool, a fallen wall, a dead body within a season, gently, completely, without judgment.

If you stay long enough, you notice the Table is wrong.

Not haunted-wrong. Broken-wrong. Reality here has worn as thin as the mountains.

Distances do not hold. A town you walked to yesterday in four hours is six hours away today, though the road has not moved. A tarn reflects a sky with two moons, though there is only one. If you climb to a high swell to survey, you will see the same distinctive ruined tower — a broken ring of tall slabs — in three different valleys at once, and when you walk to the nearest, the other two are gone.

Echoes arrive before the sound. You will hear your footstep on stone a half-second before your foot lands. The fog sometimes flows uphill, or stops mid-air in ribbons and stays there all day, solid enough to cast shadows. Water in the cisterns at dawn is warm, though the night was below freezing. The old people stack stones not to build higher, but to pin the place down. "Weight helps," they say. "The light places drift."

The worst — or the kindest — is the doubling. On quiet days, especially when the fog is thick, you will see another settlement superimposed on the one you are in. Same walls, but whole, taller, crowded. Smoke from every roof. Figures moving in the mist that make no sound and leave no footprints. If you stand very still and do not look directly, you can hear, very faintly, a market, a song, stone on stone, hundreds of hands working. Then the wind shifts and it is gone, and there is only the empty terrace and the one man rebuilding the same wall.

No one is afraid of this. They treat it like weather. Because it is weather, here. The Table is large enough to have weather of its own, and some of that weather is time.

Rules, insofar as visitors learn them:

Drink only running or daily-condensed water. Do not drink from a tarn that is showing you a different sky.

Do not build with new stone. Take from a fall. The Table does not like new holes.

Sleep under turf if you can. The moss muffles the doubling and you will dream quieter.

If you hear voices before mouths move, keep walking. Do not answer an echo that comes early.

Leave cairns alone. They are not markers. They are weights.

And at night, when the frost comes down and the green freezes stiff and tinkles faintly if the wind touches it, and the towns go dark except for one or two peat fires guttering low, lie still and listen. You will hear what passes for wilderness here: the slow crack of stone contracting, the sigh of peat settling deeper, the almost inaudible creep of moss over everything humans have stacked up, patient, lush, cold, covering it all again.
