On the rim you stop because the air changes first.

From below, climbing up the long basalt road, it was dry heat — thin, high-country heat that smells of dust. Then you crest, and the plateau opens, and the heat gets wet. It sits on your skin. It gets into your mouth when you breathe. The light is too white to look at directly for long.

It is a wide, shallow bowl, a valley the size of a small town and then some empty space around it. Maybe three miles across, rim to rim, worn smooth at the edges as if it has been holding water for a very long time. And it has. The whole floor glistens.

There are no trees here. No grass, no weeds in the cracks, no green at all. That is the first wrong thing your eyes keep trying to fix. A wet place this hot and bright should be jungle. Instead it is mineral.

Pale stone. Cream and bone and rust-red. Terraces of silica sinter laid down by centuries of hot springs, rounded, lipped like frozen waterfalls, weathered not jagged. Long-existed stone, settled into itself. Between the terraces are flats of cracked mud and salt pan, and everywhere water is coming up. Not in rivers — in seeps. The cliffs weep. The ground weeps. You can watch a dark stain spread across a white wall in minutes, bead, run, drip into a channel cut for it a hundred years ago. Steam lifts off every surface by mid-morning and does not quite burn off, so the whole valley hangs in a trembling, condensing haze.

The town is in it, but loosely. Not a dense city. A sparse scattering, as if the ground was too hot to build on continuously and people learned to leave room.

Low buildings the color of the terraces themselves, made from cut sinter block, flat-roofed, thick-walled against the sun. They sit up on short piers where the ground steams. Between them: empty lots of steaming gravel, fenced pools, pipe-bridges, washing lines, cisterns. Everything is connected by waterworks more than by streets. Covered aqueducts on arches carry milky-blue hot water from the upper springs down through the settlement in steps, cooling as they go. Open runnels alongside every walkway carry the used, warm water back out to the pans. Bridges are everywhere because you are always crossing water that is too hot to step in. The tallest things are not houses but the works: squat cooling towers that vent nothing but shimmer, long sheds with louvered sides, a row of slow-turning ventilator cowls, and downhill, at the outflow gap, the pylon line marching away with its wires humming below hearing, carrying power made here down to the lowlands.

It is industrial, unmistakably. You see the settling tanks crusted orange and teal with precipitated minerals, the salt-harvest plots raked into perfect pink ridges, the glassworks where they blow the local silica into pale blue jars, the machine shops with their doors open and lathes standing idle in the noon hour. Pumps tick. Turbines turn. Water moves on belts and screws. But you have to stand very close to hear any of it.

That is the second wrong thing. A place of engines and water and animals should be loud. This valley is almost silent.

Partly it is the stone itself — porous tufa and pumice that drink sound. Partly it is the air, so loaded with vapor it muffles the way snow muffles. The big falls where the upper aqueduct drops ten feet into a basin make only a low, private sighing, as if heard through a wall. Metal expanding in the heat gives a single *tick* and then stops. Voices do not carry twenty feet. Even your own footsteps on the damp boardwalks land soft. The people here have learned to move quietly, to signal with a hand. The machines were chosen for quiet: sealed steam, electric pumps sunk underwater. After a while you realize the quiet is not absence. It is a rule the place enforces.

Because there is life here. Abundant, impossible life, with nothing green to eat.

Look at any pool that is not scalding and it is rimmed with living skin — not plants, but mats. Slicks of bacteria and archaea in impossible colors: tangerine, mustard, copper green, blood red. They grow in the heat, in water that would poach an egg, feeding on sulfur and silica and sun. They stink faintly of eggs and iron. And everything else feeds on them.

The shallows boil with small things. Translucent shrimp the length of a fingernail flick in clouds. Blind white fish nosing the mats. Flat crabs the color of bleached bone picking across the terraces, their shells crusted with salt, clicking so softly you feel it in your teeth more than hear it. On the mud, legions of springtails like spilled pepper spring and resettle.

And above them: birds, in numbers that make no sense until you see what they are eating.

They are everywhere and they make no call. Flocks of needle-billed white birds — stilts, spoonbills, a kind of ibis with glassy, heat-warped feathers — stand leg-deep in the warm outflow, sweeping side to side. When something startles them they rise all at once, a white sheet lifting off white stone, wings beating with a sound like cloth shaken far away. They circle and land again without a cry. On the rooftops, doves the gray of steam coo with throats that barely vibrate. In the shade under pipes, swallows with sooty, heat-proof bellies dart after the other great abundance: insects.

The air at human knee-height shimmers with them. Tiny midges that breed in the mats rise in columns. Larger dragonflies with wings like cellophane, bodies the bright blue of pool water, hover without a whir you can hear. Beetles with mirrored backs walk the handrails to drink condensation. At dusk, which comes bright and sudden — the sun drops but the stone keeps radiating — the flats go pink and the crabs come out in thousands, a slow pale tide, and the owls come out too, white as the birds of day, hunting by sight alone.

Nothing here sings. Even the predators are silent. You might see a lanky, dog-sized lizard, dusted white with silica, standing motionless by a runnel for an hour, then taking a crab with one liquid snap. No growl, no splash. Communication, when you watch closely, is in flashes: the crabs wave a bleached claw, the fish turn a flank to catch the sun, the dragonflies tilt to signal with polarized light. It is a bright, loud-looking ecosystem turned down to mute.

The water makes the rhythm of the day, and the day is active, cycling fast. Morning: clear glare, steam low, workers raking salt with wooden rakes, birds feeding thick. By eleven the heat is punishing — 40, 42 in the shade, hotter on the white pan where reflected light burns the underside of your chin — and the town goes still under awnings. The air gets heavier until your shirt sticks and the stones sweat beads that join and run. Early afternoon, almost every day, the vapor that has been rising all morning gathers into a low white lid and squeezes out rain. Not a storm so much as a release: warm, heavy drops that hiss faintly when they hit hot stone and run immediately into the channels. For twenty minutes everything drips, patters, overflows. The mats brighten. The pools rise a finger’s width. Then the sun breaks through again, steam doubles, and the whole cycle starts over: evaporation, condensation, weep, flow, evaporation.

If you stay, you learn the etiquette, which is mostly about water and quiet and where not to put your feet.

Do not step on the orange mat — the bright orange is the living edge, a degree cooler than death, and a boot destroys a year. Stay on the boards. Do not shout; sound startles the flocks and wastes water, somehow — the old women will tell you noise dries the springs. Drink only from the blue-marked taps, where water has cooled through three terraces and been cut with rainwater from the roof cisterns. The straight-from-the-ground water will fur your teeth with silica and grip your gut. Wash in the evening runnels, not the morning ones. And at noon, rest. Everyone does. Even the crabs slow.

There is a strangeness here you only notice on the second day. The light lags a little. Shadows on white stone keep a faint after-image when a cloud passes, as if the stone itself remembers brightness for a second longer than it should. People say it is the silica, phosphorescing. The birds know it — they hunt the edge of a moving shadow because fish linger in that remembered light. And the water, in the closed loops of the works, sometimes moves without pumping for an hour or so in late afternoon, climbing the screws on trapped steam alone, as if the valley has decided to keep the circulation going by itself.

From the rim again at evening, looking back, it does not look like a factory town. It looks like a pale inland reef, all terraced fans and glinting pools, with a few human structures set lightly among them, spaced wide, joined by thin lines of pipe and water. Steam rises in pillars that catch the low sun and go gold. The flocks make one last silent lift and settle. The wires downhill hum in a frequency you feel behind your ears. Below your feet, the rock you stand on is damp to the touch, weeping quietly, warm as skin.

No forest. No fields. Just heat, water, stone, and the white, watchful abundance that has learned to live on light and scald and utter quiet.
