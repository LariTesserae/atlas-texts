High up, where the mountain has already given up on being a mountain and become a tooth — black, sheer, still steaming at the seams — there is a dent.

You would miss it if you didn't already know to look. From below it is nothing. From ten steps away it is nothing. It is a pocket, no larger than a small room, cupped into the leeward shoulder of the ridge, sheltered by a lip of fresh rock. Around it, the world falls away into thin blue air and far cloud. Inside it, the air stays.

The rock here is new. That is the first thing you feel through your boots. It hasn't learned to be rock yet. Its edges are too sharp, glassy where it broke, foamy where it froze mid-boil. No soil. No roundness. No gullies or dust or memory of rain. Olivine crystals wink green out of the black like unblinking eyes. When you shift your weight a flake snaps off with a clean *tick* and smells faintly of iron and lightning. This place is still under construction. It is still deciding its shape.

And it is hot. Not sun-hot, though the light here is plain, ordinary midday white — normal light, unfiltered by the altitude, laying everything out flat and honest. The heat comes from below, breathing up through the porous stone. It shimmers. It makes the lip of the bowl waver if you stare too long. It dries your mouth in a minute. Nothing here has ever been wet.

There has never been water here. Not a puddle, not a seep, not a bead of dew. If you poured a cup it would scream off into vapor before it touched ground and never return. The stone is hydrophobic, dusted with pale salts and sulfur yellows that repel even the idea of rain.

And yet it is lush.

Not lush like a jungle. Lush like a few things that have decided to live *too much*. Spaced far apart in the bowl — seven or eight of them, with great empty heat between — stand the green engines.

At first you think they are plants. They have roots, stalks, crowns of broad leaves that overlap and drink the light. Then you get closer and the categories come undone.

Each one is three or four meters tall, thick-stemmed, fleshy. The stem is bark and boiler-plate at once, ribbed with rivets that are not rivets but knots, dark and smooth. Coppery veins run up it, pulsing very slowly with something amber and viscous — not sap, not exactly oil, but a coolant blood that catches the light. The roots do not dig. They spread on the surface like pipes, half-fused to the rock, hissing faintly as they draw heat straight out of the stone.

The leaves are enormous. One leaf would shade you fully. They are green — a deep, wet-looking green that should be impossible in this dryness — but the green is overlaid with iridescence, peacock oil-slick purples and brassy edges. They are thick as canvas, veined like hands, and they tremble very slightly though there is no wind in the pocket. When you touch one, it is warm as skin and humming, a low, almost subsonic tremor you feel in your teeth more than hear.

Because they are factories. There is no other word. No one built them — there is nothing built here at all, no wall, no ruin, no cut mark, no path. The place is untouched. And still, industry happens.

Along the underside of each frond, in rows like gills, hang small chambers that swell and release with a patient sigh, once an hour, once a day. A puff of dry steam that smells of hot metal. At the crown, where a flower should be, there is a valve — a whorl of red petals stiff as sheet metal that opens with a slow *clack* when pressure peaks, venting a plume of black smoke that does not rise. In this thin, hot, broken air, the smoke falls sideways, pools for a moment at knee-height, then unravels.

Seed-pods form like ball-bearings, perfect spheres of dark ironwood that clink faintly when the windless air moves them. When ripe, they do not drop. They *discharge*, with a soft cough, rolling a few inches to lodge in a crack of new lava where they will sit for years, ticking as they cool.

At the tips of the youngest shoots, on dry afternoons, electricity gathers. Little blue coronas, St. Elmo's fire, crackling for a second — natural spark gaps. You smell ozone.

There are no animals. Not one. No flies on the leaves, no lizards under the pipe-roots, no birds overhead. The lushness is uninterrupted, uneaten. Pollination, if it happens, is done by heat itself: convection currents lifting a fine dust of iron pollen from one valve-flower and letting it settle, slow as snowfall, onto another. You can watch a single mote take ten minutes to cross the bowl.

It is mostly quiet. That is the strangest thing for a place that is, in function, a machine shop. The background is absence — no buzz, no rustle, no water. What sound there is, is slow and spaced so you almost invent it: the tick of cooling rock, the deep once-per-hour exhale of a leaf-bellows, the faint metallic *ping* as a stem expands in the sun, the soft sputter of a corona discharge. If you stand still long enough you hear your own blood, and then, a minute later, you hear your footstep arrive late, as if sound took a detour.

Because reality is thin here. The bowl doesn't quite hold.

Shadows don't behave. At noon they should be puddled directly underneath. Instead some of the leaves throw shadows upward, onto the air, faint green afterimages that hang for a breath after you blink. If you look at the farthest engine from the corner of your eye, you sometimes see two of it, overlapping, one a half-second ahead as it sighs. The empty spaces between the plants feel larger than they measure; you cross in twelve steps but remember it as longer. The lip of rock that shelters the pocket hums at a frequency that makes your hands feel like they are in two places.

The rules, as far as you can learn them in an afternoon:

1. Nothing liquid that is water can exist. Oil, sap, molten metal — yes. Water — no. It is refused.
2. No thing is made. Everything is grown, even the things that look made. Do not look for a builder.
3. Growth is patient. A frond unfurls over a whole hot season, inch by inch, creaking. What looks still is only slow.
4. What is industrial does not stop. It idles. Even at night — though you have not seen night — the stems would stay warm, ticking down degree by degree, waiting for sun to stoke them again.

Sit long enough at the edge, with your back to the warm new stone and the whole blue drop of altitude beyond, and the place becomes more itself. The sparse green engines, each alone in its halo of heat-shimmer, breathing metal, sweating oil, flowering sparks, rooted in newborn geology that has never known rain. Too much life in too little space, high above the world, humming quietly to itself, making and unmaking without hands.

You leave no footprint — the sharp rock springs back, the salt dust resettles. Behind you, an hour after you've gone, one of the valve-flowers will open with its slow iron clack, vent its sideways smoke, and close again.
