It starts with going down.

Not a slope, a descent. The access ramp is a poured concrete helix a lane and a half wide, stained black down the center where meltwater and oil have found the same path for years. Your ears pop twice on the way. The air gets colder as you drop, not warmer the way earth should be. By the third turn you can't see the grey slot of sky you came in from, only the string of caged bulkhead lights curving away below you, orange, orange, out, orange.

Then the ramp ends. And opens.

The Basin.

It is too big to be called a room and too made to be called a valley, but it has the size of a valley. A mile or two across, walled all the way around in stepped, terraced concrete, disappearing up into dark. Somewhere far above is a lid — a roof of cross-beamed steel and corrugated panels that was put over the pit decades ago to keep the snow out and the heat in, and failed at both. You can tell it's there only when a freight train crosses it, and dust sifts down through the light shafts, or when the weather shifts and the whole lid ticks and groans.

Nothing here is ground. The floor is the floor. Slabs, plates, grating, ballast. Even where it has cracked, it has cracked onto more making: under the broken slab is compacted slag, under the slag is a membrane, under the membrane is another slab. There is no soil to reach. The survey marks painted on the pillars say -47 METERS, -52 METERS. Everything is below the world.

And everything is spaced too far apart.

That is the first strange emptiness of it. For a factory town it is not crowded. A cooling tower stands alone in a half-acre of empty, frost-heaved concrete. A long, low assembly shed squats a quarter mile off, windows dark except for three. Between them: nothing. Just flat, wet apron, expansion joints ticking with ice, yellow painted walk-lines faded to ghosts. A substation hums by itself behind a chain-link fence hung with icicles. A stack vents white steam that never rises, just spreads sideways and becomes cloud. The plan, if there ever was one, was for more. More sheds, more towers, more people. What got built instead is scattered — big industrial animals left far from each other to steam in the cold.

Because it is cold. Bitter, basement cold. The kind that lives in concrete. Your breath comes out and does not leave. It hangs, joins the general haze. Puddles have skins of ice even where the water is moving. Along the north wall, where the sun shafts never reach, the seep has built curtains: six-foot beards of dirty ice hanging off conduit and bolt-heads, growing a millimeter a day. Men have painted a red line there: DO NOT PARK UNDER ICEFALL.

Wet is the rule here. Water is near everywhere, even when you can't see it.

It weeps out of the walls. The terraced sides of the Basin were supposed to be waterproofed. They aren't. At every seam, at every tie-hole and cold joint, water beads, darkens, runs. In the dim light the whole stepped wall glistens, as if sweating. It runs down in black threads, collects in the perimeter canal — a ten-foot-wide channel that rings the entire floor — and the canal runs, always, to the Pump House at the low end.

You hear the pumps before you see them. Three big vertical turbine pumps in a brick house with its windows blown out and replaced with plywood, running and running. They never stop. If they stopped, the Basin would become a lake in eighteen hours. Everyone knows the number. It's stenciled on the Pump House door along with the high-water marks from the two times they did stop: a brown line at knee height, a black line at waist height.

From the Pump House, the water is lifted in screaming pipes up the wall, through the lid, and discharged somewhere up in the real world as clean-ish. Here, it leaves behind rust, and algae, and a smell of cold iron and wet cement.

The ceiling rains.

That is the weather in the Basin. Warm exhaust from the works below rises, hits the freezing lid above, condenses, and falls again as a fine, endless drizzle mixed with rust flakes and dust. It doesn't fall everywhere at once. It moves in fronts. You can watch a veil of it coming across the empty apron toward you, hissing faintly on the hot transformer casings, beading on your coat. Then it passes, and for ten minutes the air is clear enough to see all the way to the far stairs. Then another front comes. The old-timers call it inside-rain and don't carry umbrellas. You just get damp and stay damp.

Light is scarce and low and pooled. There is no day down here, only variations of dim. Far overhead, the light shafts — round oculi cut in the lid and lined with corrugated culvert — drop columns of real grey daylight that never quite reach the floor. They dissolve halfway down into the haze. The rest is electric: sodium floods on hundred-foot masts, each one making a perfect orange island in the dark, with moths long dead caked in the fixture pans. Between islands, blue shadow. Fluorescent tubes over doorways, flickering at 60Hz, buzzing. The red aircraft-warning eyes on top of the stacks, blinking slow. Your eyes never fully adjust. You walk from glare to gloom to glare.

And it is loud. Not city loud. Machine loud, with space for echo.

The base note is the Pump House and the substation hum — a deep 50-cycle throb you feel in your molars. Over that: the clank and sigh of the automated press line in Shed 4, still running on a contract nobody cancelled, stamping out chassis parts for trucks no one orders, stacking them, banding them, moving them by conveyor to a yard where a forklift with no driver parks them in perfect rows to rust. The hiss of steam traps venting. The rattle of the lid in wind. The occasional crack like a gunshot when a slab lifts with frost and a new sharp edge is born — the concrete here is young, in geological terms. It hasn't rounded. Every spall is fresh, pale gray inside, edges like knives, rebar teeth bared. Rust blooms overnight, bright orange on gray.

You can walk for twenty minutes and see no one.

Life has thinned to what can stand cold iron and inside-rain.

Vegetation: in the expansion joints, where grit has collected, there is a black-green moss, tough as felt, rimed with frost. In the lee of the warm pipes, pale etiolated weeds — lambsquarter, a dandelion or two — grow leggy toward the sodium lights, leaves almost translucent. Under one perpetually dripping flange, a little hanging garden of liverwort has established itself on concrete, improbable and lush as a wound. Nothing is planted. Everything is volunteer.

Fauna is rarer still. A pair of crows nests on the stack catwalk, huge and shaggy in the cold, commuting up through a ventilation shaft to the world above and back down with fries and insulation. Rats run the canal edges, fat on press-line grease. In the settling ponds by the water treatment skid — three open concrete tanks, green-black and steaming faintly — live blind white carp, dumped as fingerlings years ago to control algae and now huge, slow shadows that surface only when the aerators stutter. Once, at the edge of your light, you might see the feral tabby the night shift feeds, its whiskers beaded with condensate.

No one looks up for birds anymore. They listen for other things: the change in pump pitch that means intake icing, the rattle that means the lid dampers are freezing shut, the sudden quiet that means a breaker has tripped in Shed 4 and the whole line is cooling and ticking.

The rules of the Basin are simple and posted everywhere in stenciled capitals, overpainted many times:

1. KEEP WATER MOVING. Do not block channel, culvert, scupper. Report weep over 5 L/min.
2. DO NOT DIG. FLOOR IS FINAL. There is nothing under to find.
3. STAY IN LIGHT BETWEEN SHIFTS. The dark between masts is -15C with wind.
4. NO OPEN FLAME NEAR SHAFT 2. Methane collects in cold pockets.
5. IF YOU HEAR THREE BLASTS, GO UP. Not across. Up.

People obey them because the place enforces them. Leave a pallet in a scupper and by morning it is iced in and the foreman is chipping it out with a bar, cursing. Try to pry up a floor plate and you find the membrane and the sensor wire and the alarm.

And yet, for all the concrete and procedure, it feels strange. Not haunted, exactly. Mis-placed. The scale deceives you — sounds arrive late, flattened by cold air and distance, so a hammer clang a hundred yards away sounds like it's next to you, and footsteps behind you are really half a mile off on the terraces. The light shafts move, but too slowly, so noon up above is a dim brightening at 3 p.m. down here. Pigeons that come down the shafts circle and circle, confused by the lack of horizon, until they land, exhausted, on a warm pipe and stay.

Stand in the middle of the empty apron at shift change, when the press line pauses and the pumps throttle back for thirty seconds to clear the intakes, and there is a moment — brief — of relative quiet. You can hear the drips. Thousands of them. From the lid, from the wall weeps, from the ice beards, from the handrails. Plink on steel, splat on concrete, tick on ice. The Basin settling, sweating, freezing, cracking, pumping. A made place, with no nature left in it, still acting like weather.

Then the pumps ramp back up, the press slams down again, the sodium lights buzz, the inside-rain comes on, and the cold closes over your head like water.
