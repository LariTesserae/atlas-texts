It is not under the sky. It is inside.

Far down where the soil turns cold and stops smelling like soil, there is a bulb. Not a metaphor. A bulb the size of a small room, the size of a held breath, left by something that stopped growing so long ago it has flattened its own hill above it into moor. From the surface you would walk over it and never know. That is the whole of the place: a single sealed pocket, low and lowly, embedded and hidden.

You get in — if you get in — through a split in the outer tunic where the fibers parted like old linen, and then you are in the dark that is not night-dark but burial-dark. Absolute. No leak, no seam, no phosphorus, no eye-adjustment after. The dark here is a building material.

And the room is larger than it should be because it is empty.

The floor sags almost flat. That is age. Everything old in this place has settled into its final form. There is no hill left inside the bulb, no wrinkle left sharp. The inner skin, which is also the ground, the walls, the ceiling, has sagged over ten thousand slow seasons into a shallow bowl, polished by nothing but time and dry air. You kneel and your palm finds it cool — cellar-cool, cheek-cool — and bone-dry and faintly ridged, like the inside of a vast onion that decided, centuries ago, to stop being wet.

Because there is no water here. That is the first rule. Not dry for a season. Dry as a principle.

No seep, no drip, no cistern sweat, no well. The air when you breathe it takes moisture from you. Your mouth goes papery in minutes. The smell is not rot-wet but rot-dry: sweet garlic, old paper, dust of stone that sifted in through a crack a thousand years ago and never left. The plants here do not drink. They have learned another chemistry. They eat cold and vibration and the slow slough of their own skins.

And everything is plant.

That is the second thing you learn with your hands, in the dark. The floor is plant. The walls curving up and over you to make a low dome are plant — layer on layer of tunic, parchment-thin, lignified with age until they ring like drumhide under your knuckles. What you first take for streets are the great flattened veins of the bulb, pressed by the weight of ages into boulevards, pale and fibrous, running in a radial pattern from a sunken center. What you take for masonry is pith, cut and stacked.

For this is a town. A complete, organized, layered town, no bigger than a pantry, and you are crouching in its outskirts like a god with your head brushing its sky.

Along the veins, houses. Not grown — made. Someone cut blocks of dry pith and heart-fiber, someone lashed beams of blackened root-wood with cordage twisted from leaf hair. The work is primitive if you feel the edges: hand-scraped, wedge-split, lashed not nailed, chipped with stone. Early tools. Patient tools. Doorways no higher than your thumb, windows the size of a slit for a letter. Terraces step down toward the middle in three tired tiers. A plaza, flattened and swept clean of dust in a perfect circle. Around it, what can only be called infrastructure: dry channels, not for water but for air — shallow gutters to coax the cold to circulate. Dry cisterns, bell-mouthed jars woven from rootlets and sealed with wax-dry sap, built to hold nothing but still, cool darkness. Granaries, still quarter-full of seeds hard as shot, that will never sprout because sprouting would require what is forbidden.

It is urban in the way a honeycomb is urban, but clumsier, hand-made. You can read the zoning with your fingertips: weavers here, knappers there, a meeting hall with a long low bench grown straight out of the living floor and then faced with cut slabs.

No one lives here now. No one has lived here for a very long time. That is the third rule: nothing that walks, crawls, flies, or breathes with lungs is here. Not a mouse, not a mite, not a moth, not you for long. Your presence is a trespass the place tolerates because you are quiet and temporary. There is vast space between things here. A house, then ten paces of nothing but resonant floor. A tower — a three-story tower no taller than your shin, leaning — then a whole empty boulevard. Hollow, hollow. The town was built for crowds and now holds distance.

What lives here is only what can live without light and without water and without eating other living things: the blind flora.

In the dark they are white, or the memory of white. Air-beard hangs from the dome in threads so fine you only know them when they break across your face, cool and dry as cobweb without the spider. Dust-moss furs the lee sides of the houses, not green, but a pale grey-yellow, feeding on stone-dust and settling spores. Lampweeds — misnamed, for they give no light — rear up at street corners, thin stalks with closed buds like fists that never open, that orient not to sun but to sound. And along the walls, shelf-brackets of dry fungus-tough leaf flesh, layered like shingles, creaking as they very, very slowly thicken.

They all grow patiently, almost not at all. The dynamic here is slow to the point of stillness. A crack widens in a lintel over a hundred years. A new air-beard thread reaches the floor in a decade and snaps. Nothing fluxes, nothing flows. The place has settled. It is at the end of its becoming.

Except for sound.

You expect a sealed dark room to be silent. It is loud. That is what replaces the missing things.

The bulb breathes — not with lungs, but with tension. Cool outside, slightly less cool inside, fibers taking up and giving off the dryness of the air, and as they do they move. And because everything is taut and papery and hollow like an instrument body, every move is amplified.

So you crouch in absolute black in an empty miniature city made of plant, and you listen to it boom.

First, the near-constant bedding: a low papery rustle as air-beard trembles in the circulation from the air-gutters, a hiss like distant surf. Then the ticks — sharp, startlingly close — as a tunic layer somewhere overhead releases. Then, every while, the groan. It starts as a deep creak in the walls, travels around the dome, gathers in the sunken center, and goes off like a slow plank breaking: CRACK — that echoes and re-echoes off pith houses and dry cisterns until the whole town hums. You feel it in your sternum. The lampweed buds shiver. Dust-moss sheds a faint dry sift.

The builders knew. You can feel it in how they built: doorways facing away from the main resonance, granary jars nestled in moss to deaden them, the meeting hall at the exact acoustic focus where a whisper spoken at one end arrives clear at the other. In a place with no light, they navigated and governed by knock. A code of taps on the floor-veins would tell you which street you were on by how it answered. Sound was load-bearing. Even now, empty, the town is still performing for itself.

If you stay still long enough — and the cold seeps into your knees, and your own breath sounds obscenely wet and animal-loud — you begin to understand the strange economy: the plants feed, in part, on this. Not on water, not on sun, but on the slow release of strain, the vibration that shakes loose spores and splits seed coats. The loudness is the weather. The crack is the rain.

Do not bring water. Do not bring light. Do not make a fast move. Those are the courtesies.

When you back out through the split tunic, up toward the cool moor wind and the grey that passes for day, your hands will smell sweet-dry for hours. Behind you, sealed again, the hidden town will go on being what it has become: a total plant that learned to be a city, a city that learned to be empty, booming patiently in the dark to keep itself alive without ever drinking.
