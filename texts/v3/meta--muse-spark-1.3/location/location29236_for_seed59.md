You come up over the rim and drop into it all at once.

It is a bowl, no bigger than a hamlet field. A high shoulder of the mountain slumped a very long time ago and never moved again, so the ground has settled into itself — old, sodden, rounded. Behind you the ridge path, in front of you the fall: far below, the lowlands in haze. But here, at cloud height, the air is thin and cool enough to wake you. Your breath shows in the morning. By midday it will be just cool-damp on the skin, the kind of chill that lives in sleeves and never quite leaves.

The hollow holds mist. Not a storm, not rain exactly, but water in every other form. It beads on everything and falls again. It seeps out of the black rock on the uphill side in a hundred weeps and trickles, it stands in the low places shining, it condenses on leaves until the leaves tip and pour. The rocks weep. The air weeps. Your boots are wet in three steps. The ground gives under you — not mud, but a deep mattress of root and moss and rotted leaf over old stone. If you press your palm down you can feel water just under the surface, waiting.

There is no ground that is not alive. That is the first rule here.

The forest that took the bowl did not leave room for anything else. Huge, cool-temperate trees — beech-like, oak-like, but shaggier, bearded — crowd stem to stem, crowns knitted shut overhead. Between them every gap is filled: saplings, ferns as tall as a child, curtains of moss hanging a forearm long, vines as thick as your wrist. Leaves layer on leaves until the light comes down green and broken. And in and through all of it, people live.

Not clearing it. Inhabiting it.

The village is not built on the forest. It is built *of* it, and in it, and it is dense as a honeycomb. Twenty, thirty families in a space you could shout across. Platforms lashed between three trunks, then another platform above that, then a ladder up to a loft in the crotch where the branches split. Walls are not planks but living wattle — hazel and willow withies woven while green and then left to keep growing, so the wall leafs out in spring. Roofs are trained leaves: great broadleaf boughs bent down and tied, overlapping like shingles, still attached, still alive, shedding water in sheets off their tips. Where a trunk was needed for a post, it was left standing and notched. Where a trunk was in the way of a doorway, the doorway was moved. You do not cut a pillar here. Everyone will tell you that.

Because everything is close, everything touches. A bridge of two poles and vine rope goes from one family's floor to another's roof. A chicken coop hangs under a walkway. A water trough is a hollowed log still sprouting at one end. Stairs are carved directly into a buttress root, the steps dished by generations of feet and slick with moss. Below, at true ground level, narrow duckboard paths wind between roots over standing puddles, and beside them little hand-dug rills run everywhere, stone-lined, carrying the seep from the uphill rocks down through the kitchens and the washing places and out to the boggy garden terraces on the lip where taro and cress and bitter highland beans grow in black muck.

All of it is weathered. None of it is new. The lashings are dark with algae. The handrails are polished wood under the green. The stones in the rill edges are round as eggs. Stone adzes, bone awls, flint edges, fire-hardened digging sticks — you can see the toolkit in the work: joints bound with flax and nettle cord and pine pitch, not nails; pots of coarse grey clay; cloaks of felted wool and woven bark. Smoke rises from half a dozen small hearths tucked under leaf-eaves, and the smoke does not rise far before it tangles in the canopy and hangs, blue-grey and sharp, smelling of wet wood and fat.

And the animals. Gods, the animals.

They are not background. They are a weight. The canopy boils with small birds — warblers, finches, jays — in such numbers the branches seem to twitch even when the wind is still. Frogs the size of thumbs and frogs the size of fists crowd every puddle and rill, calling without pause. Leeches inch on the undersides of leaves. Beetles and moths and midges fill the air in columns in every shaft of light. Red squirrels run the ropeways as confidently as children. A troop of small grey mountain monkeys works the upper village every afternoon, stealing, grooming, scolding, leaping from roof-leaf to roof-leaf and shaking down showers. Underfoot, in the thatch, in the grain baskets: mice, voles, shrews. Above the chickens — scrawny, loud, everywhere underfoot — the tame pigeons circle and return to dovecotes hollowed in standing deadwood. Bees are kept in hollow logs stacked like cordwood near the gardens, and their hum is a constant bed under everything. The people do not seem to shoo much away. You make room. You cover the food and accept the rest. In a place this alive, keeping anything out is wasted work.

It is loud. You notice that after the greenness. Water drips and patters and gurgles from fifty points at once. Leaves rustle and dump their loads. A baby cries, a dog barks itself hoarse at the monkeys, someone is splitting kindling with a stone maul — *tok tok tok* — someone else is fulling cloth by beating it on a root. The frogs keep a high metallic trill that never stops. The chickens argue. The pigeons whistle with their wings. The bees drone. High up, wind combs the crown and brings down a fresh cold shower even when it isn't raining.

And nothing stays still. That is the second rule here. With so many people and animals in so small a wet place, everything is maintenance and barter and traffic. A woman comes down a ladder with a basket of grubs and trades it across a bridge for a bundle of dry kindling kept under cover — dry being the true currency. Two boys haul a dripping pole, shouting to clear the path. An old man re-ties a sagging vine bridge while people keep using it around him, stepping over his hands. A girl mends a living wall by weaving in a fresh green shoot and tying it with a strip of bark. Goats — three, impossibly, on a platform — butt and bleat. Someone guts fish, tiny black highland minnows from the outflow pool, on a flat stone while cats and jays gather at a respectful, vibrating distance. Paths are blocked and unblocked. Water is diverted with a sod, then diverted back. It has the energy of a market and an anthill at once, cheerful and quarrelsome and damp.

The light, for all the cover, is ordinary day. No gloom, no glow. High mountain noon comes through in pale shafts where the crown thins, catching the mist and the midges and the drip-sparkle, then clouds over again. Colors are true: green moss, brown bark, grey stone, the red of a child's cap, the white of washing hung under an eave where the drip won't quite reach it.

If you stand still long enough at the center — on the big root-plait plaza where three paths meet over the main rill, where the ground thrums faintly with water underneath — you can feel the whole bowl breathing. Inhale: mist gathers, leaves fill and tip. Exhale: drip, run, gurgle down to the gardens and over the lip. The village rides it, lashed on, growing, rotting, re-tied, re-woven. It does not conquer this place. It keeps itself afloat in it.
