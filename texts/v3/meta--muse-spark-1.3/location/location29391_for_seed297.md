You come down into it without climbing. The land simply lowers — a broad, shallow basin the size of a small country you could walk across in a day if it let you, which it won't. The air gets warmer as you descend, not hot, but held-warm, like the inside of a held breath. Afternoon light, full and white-gold, and it does not move.

At first you think this is a forest. That is the wrong word but your eyes make it anyway because there is nothing else for that much green, that much crowding, that much leaf-on-leaf layering.

It is lush the way a reef is lush. Dense to touching. Stem against stem, frond over frond, canopy braided into canopy until you cannot see soil for three hundred meters in any direction. But there is no water here. Not hidden water. Not waiting water.

This is a dry lushness, and after a minute your body understands the contradiction before your mind does. Your lips crack. Your nostrils dry from the inside. The ground under the thicket is not mud, not humus. It is fresh mineral — pale salt pan and puffed gypsum and black basaltic glass so young it still has edges. Every crystal is sharp. Nothing has been rounded. The whole basin looks precipitated yesterday, extruded, flash-cooled. You kneel and the shards bite your knees through cloth and do not dull.

The plants do not mind. They do not drink.

They are not green the way Earth plants are green. Up close the color breaks. The low carpet — ankle-high, liverwort-thick, spongy under your boots but shedding no juice when you crush it — is a deep copper-green, almost black. Above that, waist-high whips and fans in sulfur yellow and oxide red. Above that, the canopy, ten, twelve meters up: broad plates the size of serving trays, translucent, veined with light. When the normal sun hits them they ring faintly, like struck glass, and throw down a dapple that is warm on your shoulders without any damp in it.

Break a leaf and there is no sap. There is dust. Fine, dry, metallic dust that smells of cinnamon and hot circuitry and ozone. Inside the stem is not xylem, not water column. It is filament — thousands of dry hollow hairs, and in each hair a bead of oil that is not oil, that never evaporates, that climbs by static alone. The plants drink charge. The basin floor hums very faintly with a telluric current, and their roots — if you can call the fused glassy feet roots — dip into that current and pull up ions. Light does the rest. They photosynthesize without transpiration. They have solved thirst by refusing it.

That is the first technology, and it is everywhere, and no one built it. There is no building here. No wall, no path, no cut stump, no artifact. Even your bootprints fill behind you with lifted dust as if the place smooths out intention. And yet everything is engineered beyond any engineering you know.

Hold still under a plate-leaf and look at its underside. The veins are too regular. They branch at exact angles, re-branch, re-branch, down past what your eye can follow, down to a shimmer that is not texture but interference. A single leaf holds more switches than a city. When wind — which almost never comes — brushes it, the leaf does not flutter. It computes the flutter and decides not to. It stores the light that hits it, holds it a fraction of a second too long, and releases it slightly slowed. The whole forest is doing this. That is why the light feels thick. That is why your shadow falls half a step to the left of where it should, and stays there even when you shift your weight.

The forest knows where photons are supposed to go and edits them.

This is why nothing changes. The place is in equilibrium so perfect it looks frozen.

No leaf falls. You will see dead leaves suspended in the understory, brown lace caught between live stems, hanging for — how long? There is no rot here without water to rot with. They just hang, weightless in dry air, neither falling nor decaying. You will see seeds, pale dandelion clocks the size of your fist, halted mid-air two meters off the ground, each filament perfectly still. If you touch one it does not scatter. It rings, very quietly, and resettles exactly where it was, as if time nudged and then thought better of it.

The sharpness persists because erosion has barely begun. The salt roses at the basin's edge are still perfect spirals, fragile as breath, unbroken by rain because rain has never come. The black glass needles that jut up between roots are still needle-sharp. The world is young and has decided to stay young.

It is mostly quiet. At first you think silent. Then you hear what quiet is made of here: a high thin ticking just at the edge of hearing, the leaves switching. A soft papery knock when two plate-leaves almost touch but do not. Your own breathing too loud, your own swallowing loud because there is no water in the air to soften sound. Far off, very rarely, something moves.

Fauna is rare to the point of rumor. You will walk for an hour in the dense thicket and see no animal, no insect, no bird. No webs, because no flies to catch. But there are grazers — if that word still works without drinking or pissing.

Once, if you are still enough, you will see one: tall as a deer, narrow as a folded umbrella, made seemingly of the same plate-leaf material as the canopy, legs like dry reeds that do not bend but fold at impossible joints. It does not eat so much as tune. It presses its flat head against a frond and the frond dims slightly, passing charge. The animal shivers with stored light and steps — without sound — into denser green and is gone. It leaves no print, no dung, no breath-fog. It sweats nothing. It is anhydrous meat, oil and glass and slow electricity. You understand it can go its whole life without a sip, because sipping was never invented here.

Stay longer and the weirdness stops being background and becomes the land.

Distance does not add up. You can see across the basin — open, regional, valley-wide — to the far pale rim where the thicket thins and the salt shows white. You walk toward it for twenty minutes through dense, touching stems that part without wetness against your arms, and the rim is no closer. You turn and your entry slope is no farther. The forest is larger inside than its basin. Or space is folded here, leaf over leaf, the way leaves fold light.

Look up. The sky is normal blue, normal sun. Look down at a puddle of shade. In the shade there are two suns reflected where there is no surface to reflect them. Look at your hands through a plate-leaf and you see four fingers slightly offset, as if you are slightly out of register with yourself. The rules, as you learn them by walking, are simple and unbreakable:

1. No water will come. Clouds that cross the rim thin and dissolve. Your sweat dries before it beads. Even your tears evaporate on the lash.

2. Light is food, water, and weight. Everything eats it, drinks it, stores it.

3. Nothing is allowed to finish. Growth halts mid-gesture. Death halts mid-fall. The seeds hang. The salt roses do not crumble. You will not get thirstier after the first hour, nor less thirsty. Your body, too, is paused at the edge of need.

4. You are being processed, gently. The ticking quickens almost imperceptibly when you pass. The canopy above you brightens by a fraction as it reads your heat, your capacitance, your strange wet interior, and files it away without judgment.

By late — though late never comes, the light stays exactly where it was — you stop trying to find water, stop listening for animals, stop expecting wind. You sit on sharp warm crystals in the dense dry shade and let the copper-green press close around you, near-touching, layered, saturated. The air smells of hot dust and pepper. Your skin is powdered white with salt. A suspended seed hangs at eye level, trembling without moving.

It is a jungle that never drank, a machine that was never built, a young world that refuses to age. And in its perfect, quiet, thirstless equilibrium, it holds you — lush and dry and warm and strange — without ever needing to change to keep you.
