On the top of the world that has grown tired of being a mountain, there is a room.

Outside, the mountain is very old. Not jagged-old, but worn old. Three thousand eight hundred meters up, where the air is thin and cold enough to make your lungs ache, the summit does not rise to a peak. It softens. Ridges have slumped into long swells. Boulders have rotted in place into red clay. What was once basalt and limestone teeth is now a high, rolling moor of peat and low wind-combed grass, eroded past drama into patience. You can walk for an hour and gain or lose only ten meters. The rock under the peat, where it shows, is pitted, honeycombed, polished by a million years of rain into shapes like sleeping animals.

And then the ground stops. A fissure, no wider than a doorway, breathes warm air into your face.

You slip in sideways, down worn steps that were not cut but persuaded — old water work, widened later by hands — and after nine meters, the light dies.

Not fades. Dies.

It is not night. Night on the moor above still has stars, still has the gray sheet of sky. This is a darkness with weight and intent. Photons go in and do not come back. If you cup your headlamp and thumb it on for half a second, you get a smear of brown-green at your boots and then nothing, as if something drank it. Your eyes never adjust. There is nothing to adjust to.

Stay still. Let the cold leave your jacket. It is warm in here.

Warm as a mouth. Warm as a greenhouse after rain. Twenty-four, twenty-six degrees, and so wet the air condenses on your eyelashes, on the hairs of your arms, and runs down. You hear it before you understand the shape of the place: water, everywhere, falling slowly from a great height to strike stone and leaf and shallow pools with a crack that echoes too long.

This is the Weeping Hollow.

It is small. You could cross it in three hundred slow steps if you dared to walk straight, which you won't. The floor is almost flat — the final form of a cavern that finished collapsing ten thousand years ago and has been filling in ever since. Rounded breakdown blocks, each the size of a hut, have settled and been buried to their shoulders in black humus. Between them are pans of water, ankle to knee deep, warm, mineral-tasting on the air, fringed with marsh. The ceiling is sixty, seventy meters above you — you know this not by sight but by time. A drip lets go up there, and you count: one, two — impact. The delay is cathedral. The sound rolls around the walls and comes back from three directions. The center of the room is empty air. A vast, hollow lung. Almost nothing per volume. Just warm fog and falling water and the long travel of sound.

The walls are not bare. That is the second thing your hands learn.

Everything that can hold a root is bearded. In absolute dark, the vegetation should not be here, and yet it is lush to the point of choking. Not green — there has never been green here. The leaves are white, cream, translucent pork-pink, grown huge to catch condensation, not sun. Great blind elephant-ears, as wide as your torso, slick with water, drooping under their own weight. Mats of moss a foot thick that squelch and sigh when you step and spring back slowly. Fleshy vines as thick as your wrist that run up the weeping walls and hang back down in curtains, rooting again where they touch the floor. Fungi like cold dinner plates. The air smells of them: sweet rot, crushed celery, wet paper, iron from the rock.

They do not photosynthesize. There is nothing to synthesize. They drink. The rock weeps constantly — seeps welling out of a thousand pores in the ancient limestone, lime-warm, loaded with dissolved minerals — and the plants comb it from the fog with hairs and cups and funnels. Their leaves are gutters. Their roots excrete acid and eat stone. Under your boots the soil is deep, springy, almost entirely organic, built leaf by leaf in the dark over millennia. If you kneel and press your ear to it, you can hear it fizz faintly.

And it is crowded. That is the third thing.

You are alone in a big empty room, and you are surrounded. The fauna here is abundant to the point of uproar, only you will never see a single one.

Frogs first. Hundreds of them, in the pools and on the leaves. They do not croak so much as ring — high, glassy notes, each male holding a few inches of territory, so close together their calls braid into a pulsing sheet that rattles your sternum. Between their pulses, crickets saw, blind white crickets the length of your thumb, calling from the vine curtains. Something larger moves in the water with a slow suck and plop — salamanders, pale as grubs, as long as your forearm, hunting by smell. Above, in the void, wings. Small leathery beats, circling, circling. Bats, or birds that have become bats. Every few minutes one snaps a moth out of the air inches from your face and you feel the pressure wave and hear the papery struggle.

Nothing has eyes, or nothing uses them. They find you by heat and vibration and go silent in a circle around you as you move, then close in behind you. If you stand perfectly still for ten minutes, the frogs will resume on your boots. The loudness is total. Drips like pistol shots. The frogs. The trickle of rills between pools. Your own breathing embarrassingly ragged. Your own heartbeat. The rock takes every small sound and keeps it, rolling it around the dome for seconds. Move too fast and you make a storm.

Move slowly. That is the rule here. Everything here is slow.

Water falls grain by grain. Leaves grow a millimeter a month. The pools rise and fall over seasons you cannot feel. The boulders settle a finger's width per century. Even the animals are patient — the salamander in the pool by your left foot has been waiting in the same posture since you arrived, and will wait an hour after you leave.

There are things in here that are not rock and not plant.

Scattered, half-buried, easy to trip on if you are careless: low platforms of something that is not stone. Three of them, arranged in a loose triangle around the largest pool. Each about the height of a stool, about as wide as a door, perfectly flat on top, cold to the touch even in this warmth. No seams. No lights. No hum you can hear, but a pressure you can feel in your molars when you lean close. And between them, strung at ankle height on a line you cannot see or feel until your shin finds it — beads. Twelve black beads, each the size of a walnut, weightless. If you touch one it warms very slightly and the darkness gets, impossibly, deeper for a breath, and the frog chorus stutters.

Nobody built a house here. Nobody lives here. Someone installed something. The darkness is not an absence; it is maintained. The old guides from the hamlet below the moor — four stone huts and a sheepfold, the only human mark for a day's walk — will not enter. They say the Dark Eats The Lamp. They say the plants grow on sound. They are not far wrong. Whatever those platforms do, they drink every photon and exhale a low, steady field that the white leaves have learned to eat instead of sun. Root-acid and vibration and heat. You can feel it on your skin as a faint oiliness, a metallic taste at the back of the tongue. Post-rain, post-sun, post-everything we know how to wire.

The unusual part is not that it is dark and alive. The unusual part is how ordinary it feels after an hour. Your feet learn the difference between moss and leaf-litter and lip-of-pool. Your hands learn the curtain-vines. You learn to navigate by echo — cluck your tongue and the room returns you its shape: wall left, close; wall right, far; open water ahead; block above. You learn the rule of the place: do not bring light, do not make sharp noise, do not step in the center pools where the frogs breed. Drink the weep if you must — cup your hands under a seep and it is warm and chalky and good — but spit back the first mouthful for the salamanders.

If you lie on your back on the deepest moss, between the roots of a giant white ear, and hold your breath, you can feel the whole small world working: drip, ring, saw, wingbeat, drip, your heart slowing to match it. High above the cold thin air. Deep inside the oldest stone. Warm, wet, black, crowded, empty, patient.

It does not need you to see it to be completely itself.
