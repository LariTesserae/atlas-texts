Above the clouds, in a bowl cut out of the roof of the world.

You come up over the last shattered ridge and the air simply gives out — thin, cold enough to make the inside of your nose stick together, dark even though your body insists it should be day. The stars do not fade here. They stay nailed up, hard and small. The sun never clears the ring of peaks. This valley lives in their shadow, a permanent blue-black night at noon, lit only by starlight and what lives below.

It is high. High enough that distance misbehaves. The far wall, ten miles off, looks close enough to touch, black teeth against black sky. High enough that your breath does not plume and dissipate but falls, heavy, tinkling faintly as it freezes. The ground crunches then sighs under your boots.

And it is — impossibly — lush.

Not the lush of jungle. The lush of tundra left alone for ten thousand years to do exactly what it wants. The valley floor and its terraces are upholstered meters deep in moss and liverwort and cold-fern, black-green and iron-red, swollen with stored night. Clubmosses as thick as your wrist crawl over old lava and make new soil out of themselves. Cushion plants, tight as fists, dome the basalt, each one a hundred years of slow patience. When you press a hand to them, they give like lungs and come back cold and damp. Under starlight their wet tips glitter. Everything glitters here, because everything wears a skin of rime.

There is almost no water here, not as you understand it. No lakes, no river, no rain that falls. The air is dry as bone and cold as iron, and yet the moss is wrung wet if you squeeze it. This is a condensation world. Moisture does not fall, it arrives sideways, as fog that climbs up from the cloud sea below and spills over the low col at the valley's throat, and as breath.

You notice the damp first on your own face — beads forming on eyelashes, on the wool at your collar. Frost feathers grow an inch long overnight on any stone that stands up. The plants drink this. Their leaves are combed and pitted and hairy with cold-traps, built to coax droplets out of passing vapor. In the hollows between terraces, where the fog pools thickest, the ferns grow taller than you, arching over in black cloisters, their undersides jeweled with hanging drops that never drop, they just swell and shrink, swell and shrink. The valley has learned to live without rain.

It looks empty. That is the first rule. For a long time you will think nothing is here. The scale is wrong for eyes. The terraces step down for miles — old flows, long cooled, weathered into staircases and polygons and sorted stone circles — and between one living smudge and the next is a vastness of frost and starlit rock where nothing moves. You can walk an hour hearing only the faint squeak of your own steps in snow that is more like iron filings than snow, and think yourself utterly alone in an untouched place. And you are right about the untouched. There is no cut stump, no cairn, no path, no wire, no wall. Not even a broken twig stacked wrong. No made thing at all.

Then you see the lights, far off, and understand the valley is not empty, only spaced.

Low to the ground, amber, moving slowly in a loose line. Three, then seven, then twelve, spread over a half-mile of moss. They pulse. Dim, bright, dim.

They are the herds.

Up close — and you must be patient and very quiet to get close — they are not what you expect. Each is the size of a musk ox, low and barrel-bodied, six-legged and shaggy with hollow, glassy hair that frosts white and sheds water inward toward the skin. Their backs are overgrown with the same moss they eat, so that at rest they look like hummocks. Their sides glow.

That is the only word. Through slits in their flanks, between ribs you can see when they breathe, there is furnace light. Orange. Heat-shimmer in air that should kill heat instantly. They are boilers walking.

They crop the deep moss with radial, silent mouths, tons of it in a night, and inside them something that is not quite digestion and not quite smelting does scaled, industrial work. You can hear it if you lie belly-down on the cushion plants and hold your own breath: a low, deep, double-thump, like a distant piston engine turning over in a closed room. *chuff — chuff — chuff.* A sigh of a valve. A tick-tick-tick of cooling metal, though there is no metal. Their blood runs hot with dissolved iron and copper; their exhalations come out through paired stacks on their necks as jets of steam that plume ten feet up and then collapse, raining fine, warm drizzle in a circle around them. That is the rain here. Each animal is a walking weather.

The moss where a herd has passed steams faintly for hours, trampled, fertilized with ash-rich dung that glows dully for a minute before going dark, then erupts, days later, in brighter green than before. The valley is farmed by furnaces that grew themselves.

Nothing here is built, but everything is mechanized. The herds clank softly as they walk — not bone on bone, but something like shutters opening inside, plates seating. When a bull challenges another, they do not roar. They vent. A sudden white column, a bass pressure-wave you feel in your sternum more than hear, and a crown of St. Elmo's fire crackling blue around their stack-tips. The air smells of hot stone and crushed greens and ozone.

And they are not alone. Where there are furnaces, there are things that hunt warmth.

The pale ones. Tall, stilt-legged, blind, white as rime, with wide dish-faces that are all radiator-veins. They drift between herds at impossible slowness, making no sound at all in the snow, listening for heat, for that piston heartbeat. When they strike they do it by smothering — folding their sail-like membranes over a young boiler-beast to hide its infrared bloom from the rest, cooling it until its fire gutters out. You will not hear the kill. You will see, from a ridge a mile away, one amber light among many slowly dim to red to black. And then, hours later, a new pale light, faintly amber at the core, standing up and walking away, warmer than before.

The plants, too, are in on it. The lamp-moss, which grows only on the leeward side of boiler trails, stores the stray photons leaking from their flanks and releases them in slow green afterglows to lure the iron-midges — clouds of almost invisible gnats that live their whole lives in the two-foot warm envelope above a herd animal's back. The ferns lean, over generations, toward the habitual migration paths, not toward light that never comes.

This is an active place, furiously active, but it does its business in silence. Thin air carries poorly, snow eats echo, and evolution here has selected for quiet. Wind through the natural basalt organ-pipes on the north scarp — columns forty feet tall, hexagonal, perfect as factory chimneys though no hand raised them — makes a sound like someone humming far away with their mouth closed. Ice settling in polygons ticks like a cooling stove. Your own heart is the loudest engine for miles until you learn to slow it.

Stay still long enough and the cold finds the seams. Your exhaled water freezes to your face and the moss drinks it gladly. The stars wheel very slowly over the teeth of the rim. Another herd crests a terrace far off, a string of breathing lanterns, venting small private clouds that hang a moment and then settle as rime on the ferns, feeding them.

No one made this. No one maintains it. Boiler, moss, midge, pale hunter, fog, frost — it has settled into itself, weathered and balanced and strange, a factory with no walls, an industry with no owners, turning cold and darkness and almost-water into meat and light, night after night, without a sound.
