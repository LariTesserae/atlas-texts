High up where the air should be thin and cold, it is warm instead.

A shallow bowl sits in the shoulder of the mountain, not a crater and not a valley — just a hesitation in the slope, perhaps forty strides across, cupped on three sides by low cliffs and open on the fourth to cloud. Below it the world drops away into white. Above it, nothing but sky. You can stand at the lip and see the whole of it at once, the way you can see a room.

The rock here is old. Not jagged young rock. It has been rained on for longer than shape remembers, until all its edges have softened. The cliffs are rounded, pocked, bellied out. Solution pans have been worn into the flat slabs, smooth as basins. Water made them and still keeps them, patiently. Everything in the bowl is in agreement with water.

It weeps. That is the first thing.

The back wall — a seven-foot head of dark basalt veined with something paler — never dries. Even when it is not raining, and it is not raining now, beads gather along its undercut lip, swell, and fall. The stone sweats from inside. Seeps thread down through moss and leave black wet trails. In places the water doesn't even fall as drops but hangs in the air as a cool exhalation that condenses on your skin, on leaves, on everything. The ground itself is saturated to just below the surface. Step, and your foot sinks half an inch into green-black sponge and a clear print fills instantly behind you.

Warmth holds the wet up. It is greenhouse warm, bath warm, breath warm. The light is ordinary midday light, neither gold nor blue, but it comes through humid air and seems to diffuse and linger. Mist doesn't burn off; it thins and thickens, slowly, like something thinking.

What grows here grows apart.

There is no jungle press. No tangle. Instead, presences set far from each other in the emptiness, each one lush unto itself. A fern the size of a person, its fronds arching out and dripping from every tip, stands alone on a hummock with ten feet of bare wet stone around it. Farther on, a clump of broad-leafed things — leaves as wide as shields, veined, cupped to hold rainwater after the rain — rises from a crack. Between them, nothing but moss and liverwort and film.

The moss is the true ground. Deep, springy, many greens at once. It swallows sound. It mounds over boulders, softens the rims of pools, climbs a little way up the cliff and then stops, as if by agreement.

And there is nothing else alive in the animal sense. This is the strangeness you feel before you name it.

In a place this warm and wet and green there should be whine and flicker. Midges over the pools. Beetles under leaves. A frog's throat pulsing. Birds, at least, passing over. There are none. Not one.

The pools are perfectly clear and perfectly empty. Three or four of them, knee-deep at most, set in the stone floor like offered bowls, fed by drip and seep, draining by slow overflow through moss. You can look straight to the sandy bottom and see no wriggle, no tadpole shadow, no water-boatman dimple. Leaves fall into them and do not rot as they should; they bleach and settle, intact, layer on pale layer. Spores drift on the surface and stay.

Because of that absence, the plants have arranged themselves differently. No flowers waiting for bees. No fruit. Only the ancient ones that need no go-between: ferns unrolling their fiddleheads with geological slowness, mosses fruiting with hair-thin stalks tipped with red-brown capsules, liverworts spreading in flat green skins, fungi pushing up white and ochre ears after wet nights that collapse back into slime by noon. Everything propagates by wind and water and spore. Everything can do it alone.

It is quiet in a way low places never are.

No buzz, no call, no rustle of small feet. What sound there is is water moving at the speed of stone: the metronomic tick of a drip from the overhang into a pan — *tink... tink... tink* — with long silences between that make you hold your breath; the soft, heavy *plip* when a fern tip releases its gathered drop onto moss; once in a long while, the faint sigh as a saturated cushion of moss settles under its own weight. Your own breathing sounds intrusive. Your footsteps are muffled to nothing within two paces.

Change here is almost too slow to witness. A frond yellows over weeks. A rimstone lip around a pool grows a millimeter a year. The cliff recedes grain by grain. Even the mist moves as if reluctant to leave the bowl that made it, circling the walls, lifting a few feet, settling again.

If a maker ever came, they would find everything ready and nothing made.

Along the talus at the foot of the wall lie nodules of dense grey stone that frost and fall have flaked cleanly, leaving edges sharp as shell, fit for scraping and cutting. Straight, hard-wooded stems have fallen and seasoned where they fell, unrotted in the clean water. Fibrous bark peels in long strips. Clay the color of rust collects in one seep hollow, smooth and workable. All the invitations for early tools are here — edge, haft, cord, vessel — scattered as nature left them. No hand has taken them up. No shelter, no hearth, no cut mark, no stacked stone. Only the materials, waiting in their first state.

The rules of this place are simple and unbroken:

Keep water close to the surface. Let stone sweat. Keep distance between green things. Make no noise that lasts. Do not hurry. Need no other life but your own kind to continue.

Stand still long enough and the condensation will bead on your arms. A drop will gather at your elbow and fall to the moss, and the moss will take it without a sound. The fern across the hollow will release its drop at almost the same moment, and for an instant you and it will be doing the same thing — holding water, then letting it go — in the warm, high, empty bowl above the clouds.
