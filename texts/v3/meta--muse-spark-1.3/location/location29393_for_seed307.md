You would walk past it. Everyone does.

Up that high — above the last scree where even lichen gives up, where the air is too thin to shout in and the midday light comes in white and unfiltered — the new ridge is just black broken teeth. A fresh spire, still sharp from when it cooled. No soil. No green. Nothing built, nothing stacked, no cairn, no wall. Just volcanic glass that hasn't learned to be round yet.

And then, in the lee of the wind, a crack wide as a hand. If you kneel and put your face to it, you find the room inside is bigger than it should be. Not much bigger. The whole place would fit in a washtub. You could cup it if it weren't so hot.

It is hot. That is the first rule here.

Outside the wind is cold enough to chap your lips. Inside the crack the air sags, heavy and wet and heated from below. The rock sweats. It weeps continuously from a thousand pores, beads gathering on black glass, swelling, breaking, running down in slow fingers to the bottom. The stone itself is too young to be smooth. Every edge is a razor. Obsidian blades, basalt needles, silica crusts like frozen foam. You learn fast to not put your weight down. The water makes everything shine.

This is a wet place in a dry sky. Clouds catch on the spire all morning and get pulled in through the crack. By noon they are dripping off the ceiling. There is weather here, a whole weather, contained in a bowl you could step over. Condensation forms, falls, evaporates off the hot floor, rises, condenses again. Some drops never make it down. They hang in the heat column, shivering, then climb a little before falling again. Post-rain, forever. Marsh-edge, but miniaturized: at the bottom, where the walls pinch in, there is a palm-deep pan of milky hot water, rimmed with sulfur-yellow and rust-red slime. Not plant. No moss, no grass, no leaf, nothing that photosynthesizes in any way you would recognize. Just bacterial skin, iridescent, smelling faintly of eggs and iron, slicking every submerged edge. Pre-life, or after-life. It pulses faintly when the seeps surge.

And the animals. That is the second thing, after the heat, that doesn't make sense for how small this is.

They are overwhelming. Dense to the point of touch.

The air in the pocket is crowded. A slow, white snow of tiny midges, thousands of them, wingless or nearly so, riding the steam up and drifting down, so pale they are almost transparent. They make no hum. You expect a whine and there is none. Outside the wind screams over glass. Inside it is muffled to a held breath.

The walls are crowded. Blind white crickets, the size of rice grains at the smallest and your thumbnail at the largest, cling shoulder to shoulder on the weeping rock, heads down, lapping the slime and the mineral water. They shift and jostle and climb over each other in a continuous slow tide. When one moves, three move.

The water is crowded. In the pan, copper-red amphipods, soft as shrimp, flick and coil by the hundreds, layered belly to back, grazing, cannibalizing, mating in the same motion. You can't look at the pool without seeing eyes — black pinpricks all turned up.

It shouldn't be able to feed this many mouths. It does because the mountain feeds it. Every day the updrafts throw stray flies, moths, wind-blown gnats up from miles below and they blunder in through the crack and drown in the condensation or get stuck on wet glass. The residents fall on them in silence.

Silence is the third rule. For all that motion, the pocket is nearly quiet.

No chirp, no buzz. The crickets have no song. The midges beat their feathery wings without sound. What you hear, if you hold your breath and press your ear close enough that steam wets your cheek, is water and glass: the tick of a bead detaching, the faint sigh of steam through the crack, and underneath, intermittently, the tiniest *click* — glass on glass.

That is the tools.

Nothing here builds. There are no nests, no hives, no webs, no dams, no burrows. The rock is too hot and too wet and too sharp to allow anything to stay. Everything is carried.

The larger crickets carry. In their forelimbs or gripped in their mandibles they hold flakes of obsidian no bigger than an eyelash, chipped to an edge. Fresh flakes — you can see the conchoidal scars on the walls where they have been knapped off with their jaws. They use them as knives. A cricket will pin a drowned fly larger than itself, saw at the wing joint with its shard until it can drag the meat away. Another will scrape slime off a too-sharp ridge it cannot lick directly. After a kill, the winner often steals the loser's flake as well as the meat, carrying two now, awkwardly. No workshops. No storage. Just hand-tools, held for a life and then dropped to become part of the floor.

Predator and prey happen fast here because there is nowhere to go. The pale crickets eat the midges that land to drink. The red amphipods in the pan lunge half out of water to take crickets that slip. A blind, white, almost-translucent centipede, long as your finger, works the rim endlessly, taking crickets and amphipods both. You can watch a whole cycle in an hour: graze, hunt, eat, be eaten, shell left to be scraped clean by the next wave.

And the place will not hold still to be counted. That is the weirdness, and up here with the thin light and the heat-shimmer it gets bad.

The pan reflects a sky that isn't above you. Tilt your head and the reflection shows cloud moving the wrong way. Shadows fall toward the heat, not away from the sun. Your own exhaled breath seems to detach and hang a second too long before it dissipates. Droplets run sideways along invisible seams and rejoin each other.

Mostly it's number. You try to count the crickets on one patch of wall — twelve, thirteen — you blink from the steam, and there are nine, and one of them is chewing with a flake you swear was in another's mouth. You try to follow a single midge in the air column and it passes *through* another midge, slightly, like two ghosts, and both continue. The interior feels fractionally larger when wetter, as if water adds space. Put your finger near the back wall and the heat bends it; for a moment there are two fingers, and both are bitten by steam.

It doesn't resolve. It just keeps sweating, scraping, dripping.

Young rock. Hot blood under it. Water that never leaves, only circles. Life with no green in it, packed shoulder to shoulder, silent, armed with broken pieces of its own house, eating windfall from a world miles below, in a room the size of cupped hands, perched near the top of the sky where nothing should live at all.
