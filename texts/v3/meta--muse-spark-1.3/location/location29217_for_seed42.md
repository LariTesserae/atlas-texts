On top of a mountain, under the sea.

That is the first thing you have to hold in your head to be here. The rock under you is high ground — a basalt summit that once stood in wind. Now the sea has taken it, but not deep. Fifteen meters of cold green water above the highest terrace, no more. At noon the sun comes straight down through it in pillars. You can see the surface from the streets, a hammered ceiling of light. And if you swim to the edge of town and look out, the mountain keeps going down without you — flanks falling away into a blue that gets darker until it forgets color. Everyone who lives here gets vertigo the same way mountaineers do.

It is a hamlet, nothing larger. You can cross it on one breath if you are strong. Eighty, maybe a hundred paces of almost-flat summit ringed by a broken wall of old stone, and beyond that wall, the drop. Inside that small boundary, everything is plant.

Not plants growing on things. Plants *as* things.

The summit is capped with holdfast. Generations of bull kelp have rooted into every crack in the basalt until root over root over root has made a second ground, a springy, ochre-brown mat a meter thick. It gives under your feet, or under your hands when you pull yourself along. White shell paths cross it where the bottom has been worn to bare gravel and crushed urchin — the only bare places. Off the mat rise the stipes, as thick as a man's thigh, dark and slick, rising straight for ten, twelve meters and then fraying into long fronds that go all the way up to lie flat on the surface, making the roof.

The town is in the forest, and the forest is the town.

There were stone houses here once, before the flood. You can still see their bones: low rectangular foundations, terrace walls, a causeway of fitted slabs, all rounded now, softened by a hundred years of water, pinked over with coralline algae. Nothing sharp left. They are in balance with the sea, neither crumbling nor growing. The people — cool-skinned, big-lunged free-divers with cropped hair and oil on their shoulders against the cold — did not clear the kelp. They learned to tie it.

Each house is a bell.

About thirty of them, scattered sparse across the plateau with a lot of blue-green emptiness between, so that from the middle you can count every neighbor but you have to swim to visit. A bell begins as five or six living stipes coaxed to lean inward and lashed with braided kelp rope. At the top, where they meet, the gardeners have grafted and pruned dozens of pneumatocysts — the gas bladders — until they swelled and fused into a single translucent chamber, amber in the sun, big enough to crouch in. A living diving bell. Air breathed into it from below stays. The walls sweat oxygen, smell faintly of iodine and cut grass. Inside, on a platform of woven frond, they keep dry things: sleeping furs of seal, fire-stones, bone needles, a child.

From a distance the hamlet looks like nothing built at all. Just a grove of kelp a little too regular, with amber fruits glowing among the stipes, and thin lines of rope sagging between them where baskets and tools hang, and smoke — no, bubbles — rising from each bell in a slow leak: exhaled breath making its way up through the canopy.

Because it is cold. That is the second thing. Not killing cold, but the patient highland cool that gets into your joints. Eleven, twelve degrees. Light comes down normal and gold, dappling everything, but it does not warm. The water is full of fine snow — detritus, larvae, spores — drifting sideways in a current so slow you only notice it when you hang still. Everything here moves slow. The tide breathes in and out twice a day, lifting the whole forest three meters and laying it down again. The stipes lean with it, the bells sway on their stone anchors, the ropes creak. Growth is measured in years. A new house takes seven seasons to train. You do not hurry a plant that holds your air.

You cannot build with dead things here. That is the rule the mountain teaches. Wood floats away. Metal rusts to red scabs in a month and poisons the holdfast. Cut frond rots in days. So everything is living, pruned, guided, stitched while alive. Knives are chipped basalt and black chert, hafted in bone. Needles are gull bone. Rope is three-strand kelp, re-braided every spring. Even the anchors for the bells are just the old house stones, re-used, with living holdfast gripping them tighter than any mortar.

And it is loud. People imagine the underwater as silent and it is not. It is a cathedral of noise.

First, the forest itself: stipe rubbing stipe with a wet wood groan, bladders popping as pressure changes, fronds snapping like wet sails in the surge. Then the animals, abundant to the point of insolence. Urchins carpet the wall-tops in purple-black, clicking their teeth day and night, a constant static. Mussels and barnacles tick. Schools of black rockfish and copper surfperch pour through the streets in rivers, thousands at a time, turning on a single thought and blocking the sun. Crabs the size of platters tend the holdfast, clipping rot. An octopus lives under the causeway — everyone knows her, rust-red, old, she steals tools and leaves shells in return. Harbor seals drop from above like stones to nap in the glades between houses, barking bubbles. And far, far below the edge, sometimes so low you feel it in your sternum before you hear it, a gray whale passes along the flank of the mountain, calling.

The ecology is closed and total. There is no outside food. The fronds are food, and the holdfast is farm. Between the bells are tended clearings where dulse and sea lettuce are weeded like gardens, where the children herd urchins with sticks to keep them from eating the young bladders, where baskets trap crabs. Even the dead are planted: wrapped in frond and weighted with stone in the holdfast, to feed the stipes that will become someone's house.

There is one unusual thing, though visitors stop noticing it after a day. The bells remember breath. If you press your ear to the wall of an old bell — one ten, twelve years grown — you can hear a faint fizzing chorus, air moving through living tissue, and the wall is warm, a degree warmer than the sea. At night, when the light goes blue and then black and the fish settle into the fronds like birds, the bells glow very faintly from within where phosphorescent copepods have colonized the condensation. Each house becomes a lantern. From the edge of the plateau, looking back, the little town looks like a constellation that has sunk and caught on a mountain.

Then the tide turns, slow as thought, and all the lanterns sway together, and the forest lays itself down, and you hold onto a rope and let high water take you, cold on your skin, loud in your ears, suspended over an abyss and rooted, at once.
