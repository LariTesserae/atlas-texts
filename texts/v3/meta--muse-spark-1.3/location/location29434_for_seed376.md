It is high before you know it is cold.

The air comes thin and clean over a long shoulder of old granite, and the light is plain daylight, a flat white-grey that makes distances honest. No glare, no gloom. You can see across the whole of it — a valley, broad and U-shaped, the kind only a glacier makes and then abandons. A town could fit in here. A small weather system could fit in here. The ridges on either side are weathered down to their manners, lichen-pale and rounded, long settled into themselves. Nothing raw. Nothing new. Stone that has been stone for so long it has learned how to be soil at the edges.

And then, below the stone, green.

Not a meadow. Not a forest. A fill.

The entire valley floor is living mass, brim-full, a deep mat of plant that has risen up to meet you. From the rim it looks almost level, like a frozen lake. Closer — when your boots leave the scree and find no dirt, only give — you understand there is no ground here in the normal sense. There is plant upon plant upon plant, layered and near-touching, dense as a crowd with shoulders pressed.

Cushion moss and sphagnum in hummocks the size of armchairs, dwarf willow no taller than your ankle but a hundred years old, its trunk thick as your wrist and horizontal, crawling. Liverwort black-green and slick. Reindeer lichen, grey and brittle and branching like tiny antlers. Sedge tussocks. Star moss with its upright bristles. Everything low, everything tight, everything holding onto everything else. If you push a hand in, your fingers disappear to the wrist and do not find earth, only more stems, more thatch, more living and half-living and long-dead-but-not-rotted fiber. The valley is upholstered.

It is cold. Not the killing deep cold, but the working cold — one or two degrees above freezing at midday, breath showing. Your fingertips go dull. The damp gets in first through your cuffs and collar. Because for all the mass, it is not wet. It is damp. Water here does not lie in lakes or run in a proud river. It pockets.

Between the hummocks there are eyes of black water, no larger than a plate, no larger than a room, mirroring the grey sky perfectly and then disappearing under an overhang of moss. A rill surfaces for ten meters, chatters over peat, and dives again. Condensation beads on every leaf, on every hair of moss, and does not fall so much as hang. Your steps squeeze water up around your boots and then it is gone, sucked back into the sponge. In the hollows the sphagnum drips, steadily, pointlessly.

This should be still. A high peat sea in the cold, far from anything made — and there is nothing made here. No wall, no cut, no path, no post, no wire. Not even a cairn. If a boot print fills with water behind you, it fills with brown peat water, not memory.

And yet the place works.

That is the first unusualness you feel before you can name it: this untouched valley has the throughput of a mill district.

Nothing is built, but everything is processing. The mat breathes like a bellows, very slowly. Frost needles grow overnight in the thatch and jack whole cushions up an inch by morning; by afternoon they melt and the cushion slumps, sighs, resettles an inch to the left. Peat forms under you even as you stand there — the lower layers compressing under their own weight into black butter, squeezing out tannic water. In one hollow a bank of moss has slumped forward overnight, fresh green over black, and already the pioneer liverworts are filming over the wound. In another, a dwarf willow has been strangled by its own nurse moss and is bleaching, white twigs up like wires.

If you stay still long enough you see it is not still at all. It churns. A sedge tussock swells with taken-up water until its blades splay. A cushion splits down the middle from ice pressure and the split fills within hours with new shoots. Water finds a new route under the mat, domes it up, and a new eye opens, black and blinking, while an old eye a few meters away silts green and closes. Creation and rot at anthill speed, but without scurry. Growth you can almost watch. Death you can smell — sweet, iron, vegetable.

It is industrial the way a heart is industrial. Pumping, pressing, refining. Hydraulic. The thick stems of the willows are hard as pipe. The sphagnum heads are acid vats, pickling everything that falls in so it does not fully decay. The whole valley is a slow cold refinery turning light and rain and air into more mass, and more mass, and more mass — scaled production with no smokestack, no shed, no hand on the lever.

And it does it in near-total silence.

That is the second thing. With so much change, there should be noise. There is none. The moss eats sound. Your own footsteps are a dull compression, a wet kiss, then nothing. The little rill that surfaces does not babble; at this altitude, in this thick air, it ticks, drip by drip. Sometimes a cushion exhales when you step off it — a faint, low *huh* of released air that is gone before you are sure you heard it. Sometimes, deep under, there is a creak as ice lets go of root, too low to quite hear, felt more in the soles.

Even the wind, which you expect to rake a high open place, is baffled and shredded by the million tiny surfaces. It passes as a cool hand over your face, moving the condensation beads but not shaking them loose. Far above, cloud condenses out of nothing and un-condenses. Your own breathing sounds loud.

Fauna, if you want it, you have to wait for it to be rare.

No flocks. No herds. A single white ptarmigan picking between tussocks a hundred meters off, so still you mistake it for lichen until it blinks. The tunnel-hum of a vole under the mat, traveling, never surfacing. A crane fly hanging in the cold air, legs dangling, improbable, alone. Life here is mostly plant life so complete it has become geography, became substrate, became architecture — hummocks for hills, hollows for valleys, thatch for bedrock, living leaves for roof. An animal is a visitor, a rumor.

The rules, as you learn them walking:

1. Do not look for ground. The ground is alive and many feet thick and still growing. Rock is only at the edges, where the old valley shoulders shrug out of the green.

2. Water will not stay where you leave it. It is interstitial, shy, moving sideways through sponge rather than downhill. Follow a rill and you will lose it. Step back and a new seep will well where your boot was.

3. Cold is the engine. Not heat. Freeze lifts, thaw drops, and between the two the whole mat kneads itself, preserves itself, eats itself. Needle ice in the morning is the night shift.

4. Everything touches. There is no bare spot larger than your palm that stays bare. If ice opens it, water fills it. If water opens it, moss films it. If moss opens it, willow roots find it.

Stand in the middle of it at midday, with the plain light coming straight down and the granite rims/miles off left and right, and you feel both exposed and embedded — in an open regional bowl that is also a dense thicket knee-high. The smell is peat and rain-water and green iron. Your socks are damp. Your breath makes small clouds that do not rise fast in the thin air but linger and then tear.

Behind you, your footprints are already closing, green water welling up to level them. Ahead, the mat goes on, hummock and eye and rill and cushion, repeating but never quite the same, all the way to where the valley narrows and rises into cloud.

Nothing has been built here. And still, under your feet, silently, at full capacity, the cold factory of the living keeps running.
