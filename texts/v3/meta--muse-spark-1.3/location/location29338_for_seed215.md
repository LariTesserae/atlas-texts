It is high before it is anything else.

You feel it in the lungs first — air a little too thin, a little too clean, cold enough that each breath has an edge. The plateau runs out in every direction until weather gets in the way. Not a peak, not a valley. A table of the world lifted up. Three thousand meters, maybe more, a basalt table still raw from being made.

That is the second thing you learn: this ground is new.

It hasn't been smoothed. There are no deep river cuts, no soft hills. The rock is black and rust-red and ash-grey, broken in clean slabs and sharp rubble, as if it cooled last week. Glaciers pulled off it not long ago in geologic time and it is still rising back, a centimeter a year, shrugging. You can see the shrug in the cracks — long, straight tension fractures that run for kilometers, unweathered at the edges, no soil in them yet. Piles of scree that haven't slid anywhere. Lava ropes frozen mid-flow. Ash cones with their rims still crisp. Everything is pre-erosion. Water doesn't know where to go yet.

So water goes everywhere.

It sheets. It stands. The permafrost is only a hand's breadth down in most places, and the summer melt has nowhere to drain, so the whole plateau sweats. Shallow pans of sky-colored water between black rocks. Braided threads that are a centimeter deep and twenty meters wide, shifting around pebbles by the hour because no channel has been worn to hold them. Marsh-edge without a marsh, really — just wet tundra, sponge-moss and cotton-grass holding melt like a soaked blanket. Cliffs and road-cuts weep. Even on dry days the cut faces drip steadily, and boulders sweat beads that never quite fall. Morning is a low fog that condenses on everything: on stone, on railings, on your coat. You wipe it off and in ten minutes it's back.

Vegetation is holding on by its fingertips. No trees. No real fields. Lichen in pale green and orange maps the tops of stones. Black moss in the seeps. Cushions of dwarf willow no higher than your ankle, and sedge in the wettest threads. From a distance the land looks barren — stone first — and then when you kneel you see it is prickled all over with life, just low, just sparse, built to survive wind and a short summer. It smells like wet stone and peat and cold.

And spread across all of that, so thin you almost miss it, is the city.

It is not dense. That is its rule. No downtown, no crush. It is a city the size of a small country with the population of a large town, drawn out in lines and knots along the high ground. You walk for an hour over moss and sheet-water and you come to a cluster: six or twelve buildings, low and heavy, their backs to the wind, connected by a raised path. Then another hour of emptiness, then another cluster. Then a long transit thread stitching them together.

Because the ground is still moving, nothing is allowed a deep foundation. That is why the technology stopped looking like technology. The buildings ride. Each one sits on a triad of inertial moorings — dark pylons that go down only a few meters and then hold by field, not weight, constantly micro-adjusting as the basalt heaves and settles. Inside, the floor never tilts. Outside, you can sometimes see the whole structure breathe up a finger's width over a season. No one comments on it. It is plumbing.

The walls are grown-poured from the rock itself: crushed basalt fused and re-laid, rough on the outside so lichen will take, smooth and warm-wood-colored inside. Heat is siphoned from deep below, from the same hot spot that made the plateau, and run in silent loops under footpaths so that the main walkways are always just above freezing even when rim ice forms on the ponds at night. Condenser nets — tall, almost invisible meshes strung between poles on the ridges — pull drinking water straight from the fog. They sing faintly when the wind is right, the only high sound for miles.

Between the knots run the threads: pale, matte guideways a meter off the ground, for the driverless carriers that glide without touching. They make no engine sound, only a displacement of air and a soft tick of cooling. At night they carry freight. By day they carry people, and schoolchildren, and veterinary kits, and fence parts.

Because this is still, functionally, migration country.

Nothing about the poor grazing would suggest it, but the sky and the ground fill daily. The herds come through on ancient memory, even though the land is new — long-legged russet grazers with wide splayed feet for marsh, moving in lines five and ten wide and a kilometer long, picking at sedge, dropping manure that is already becoming the first soil. White geese and black-necked cranes stage in the shallow pans by the tens of thousands, turning water white and black. Small quick foxes follow. High above, the big soaring predators turn slow circles, waiting for the weak.

The people have organized themselves around not getting in the way. That is the urbanism here: not to concentrate, but to shepherd. Low drift-walls, hardly higher than your knee, angle across the tundra for miles to nudge herds away from condensers and nurseries and meltwater intakes. Crossing gates open and close on their own as the herds approach. The transit threads pause. The whole sparse city inhales, holds, lets the animals pass, exhales. Predator and prey play out right between the houses. You can stand on a warmed path with a cup of tea and watch a chase cross a sheet-flood, water flying, moss tearing, and then stillness again.

It is very quiet for a city. That is deliberate and also natural. The air and the moss swallow sound. The machines were built to be silent. Voices don't carry in the cold damp. What you hear, if you stand still: wind low over stone. Drip from a weeping cut. The far, thin crying of geese, like wind through a cracked door. The crunch of your own boots on cinder. The long sigh of a herd moving, hooves sucking in wet peat. Occasionally a carrier passing with a soft shush. Occasionally a deep, almost subsonic groan from the ground itself as it rebounds — not an earthquake, just settling.

Light is ordinary and therefore startling. A pale high sun behind thin, fast cloud. No color theatrics. Shadows short at noon, long and blue in evening. The wet rock doubles everything. Ponds become perfect mirrors until a wind line ruffles them. Far off, a weather cell of its own makes rain you can see coming for an hour — a grey curtain moving across the table, soaking one district while the next stays dry. By the time it reaches you, it is just drizzle, cold on the face.

At dusk the clusters light up, but not brightly. Warm windows, path markers at ankle height, the red blink of a mooring check. From a ridge you can see them strung out to the horizon: little warm knots in black vastness, each alone, each clearly part of the same thought. Smoke doesn't rise — there are no fires. Breath rises. Fog rises off the standing water as the temperature drops, and the condenser nets go white with frost.

If you stay a few days you learn the rules without being told:

Stay on the warmed paths when the sheets are flooded. Let the animals have right of way. Don't stack stone — the surveyors need to see fresh movement. If your door sticks, don't force it; the house is leveling, wait an hour. And listen.

The place is still becoming itself. The rock hasn't decided where the rivers will be. The moss hasn't decided where the meadows will be. The city hasn't decided where its center is, or if it needs one. It is all embryonic, active, wet and cold and huge and mostly empty — and in that emptiness, full of motion.
