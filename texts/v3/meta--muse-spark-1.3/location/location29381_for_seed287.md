You go down to it.

Not far, as distances go — the street just gives out, the curb crumbles into rebar teeth, and then there are stairs cut straight into the earth, brick stairs gone soft and bellied with moss. Twenty meters down the light changes. Thirty meters down the air changes. By the time your shoes hit flat ground you are fully below the world. The sky is a ragged lid far above you, a hot white slit. You can see the whole rim if you turn in a slow circle. That’s the size of this place: one hollow, one room, that you could walk across in five minutes if there was anywhere to walk.

It was a works, once. Or a cluster of works that subsided together when the ground drank itself empty underneath them. The retaining walls are still there all around, three stories of blackened brick and poured concrete, bulging inward like cheeks holding breath. They weep. That is the water here — there is no river, no pool that lasts. There is dampness as a condition. The walls sweat, the pipes sweat, the leaves sweat. Heat rises off the floor plates in shimmering sheets and meets the cooler air sinking from the rim, and all day it condenses and drips and evaporates again before it ever lands. Every surface is beaded. Touch a handrail and your palm comes away wet and rust-orange. Touch a leaf and it is warm as skin.

Because it is hot. Not summer-hot, but held-hot, kept-hot. The hollow traps it. The machines make more of it than can escape up the narrow throat to the sky. It sits on your sternum. It makes your shirt cling in a minute. The air is thick soup, iron and chlorophyll and hot oil, and breathing it feels like drinking through cloth.

Dim down here, even at noon. Up top the sun is white fury; down here it arrives filtered, thinned. Part is the canopy — not trees, exactly, but what has learned to be trees in a room with no sun. Giant elephant-ears, black-green ferns ten feet tall, kudzu-thick vines as thick as your thigh. Part is the haze. Steam and smoke and transpired breath never fully clear. What light there is comes sideways and amber: a string of caged work-bulbs still burning on extension cords, green emergency EXIT signs in sheds with no exits, and a low red glow from the grates where the floor gets thin over whatever is still hot underneath. Shadows layer on shadows. Everything is edge-lit with wet shine.

And it is crowded. There is no empty ground. In the space of a hamlet — half a dozen leaning sheds, a pump house, a boiler with its side blown out, a catwalk collapsed into a V — everything else is filled. Pipes run at ankle height, at shoulder height, overhead, bundled like roots. Boilers shoulder up against fern brakes. Vines thread through chain-link and lift it. Moss upholsters the catwalk steps until they are soft. Leaves press against hot metal and do not burn; they have come to terms. It is dense in the way a jungle is dense and a junkyard is dense at the same time: too many presences per breath. You have to turn sideways to pass. Fronds brush your face, hot pipes brush your back.

The buildings are scattered, small, half-sunk. None taller than one story now because the upper stories are gone or buried. Brick sheds with corrugated roofs bellied under creepers. A concrete kiosk with its windows punched out and stuffed with roots. A control booth tipped ten degrees, its levers still labeled in flaking paint: INTAKE, BLEED, RETURN. Faint marks elsewhere — a painted arrow on a wall pointing into solid vine, a number stenciled on a pipe that disappears into the ground and comes up twenty feet away, a lone boot on top of a valve wheel, laced, as if someone stepped out of it. No streets. Just worn paths where the condensation drips most and keeps the growth a little lower.

Because the machines are still running. Slowly. That is the industry here — not production anymore, just maintenance of itself.

In the center, sunk to its knees in black muck and roots, is the engine. A horizontal piston engine the size of a cottage, flywheel maybe twelve feet across, turning — barely turning. One revolution every twenty seconds. You can watch a single rusted spoke come around, disappear into fern shadow, come around again. *Chuff... chuff... chuff.* With each stroke a plume of steam sighs out of a cracked gasket and the whole hollow exhales. Pumps knock in the sheds around it. A compressor somewhere kicks on with a shudder that you feel in your molars, runs for a minute, sighs off. Relief valves hiss at irregular intervals, long white jets that vanish into leaves and return as rain. Belts slap. Drips tick on tin roofs by the thousand. It is loud. The bowl shape holds the sound and throws it back at you. You have to raise your voice to hear yourself. But it is a slow loudness. Nothing hurried. Patient iron, keeping time for plants.

The greenery is lush beyond reason for so little light and so little water. It should not be this green, this urgent. Fronds unfurl the size of doors. Mosses in velvet banks up the walls, emerald over rust over black. Fungi shelf out from boiler seams in overlapping ivory plates. Vines flower with waxy trumpets, deep crimson, that open and close almost imperceptibly with the heat cycle. Everything drips, everything gleams. If you stand still, you hear the vegetation moving — a creak as a stem takes up water, a soft pop as a seed pod bursts in the heat.

And nothing eats it. That is the first wrongness you notice after the heat, after the noise.

No insects. No flies over the muck. No spiders in the miles of web-like vine. No rats in the sheds, no pigeons on the high rim, no birdsong falling in from above — even the sound from up there does not reach down. The trumpet flowers are unchewed, perfect, and they drop their petals whole to rot where they fall. A place this lush, this hot and damp, should be crawling. Here, leaves stir and there is nothing underneath them but steam. The loudness is all inorganic: iron, water, air through an orifice. No chitter, no buzz, no breath but your own, and the engine’s. It makes the green feel watchful, or lonely. As if life tried the animal experiment, withdrew it, and left only the vegetable half running.

Weathered, all of it. Nothing is new, nothing is collapsing either. Rust has stopped being decay and become a finish, pitted and smoothed by decades of condensation, the color of dried blood polished by hands. Brick has rounded at the corners. Concrete has spalled to show aggregate like knuckles. Roots have grown around pipes so tightly the two have taken each other’s shape — you find bark embossed with diamond-plate, and iron pipe bellied like bamboo at the joints. This is an old balance, long settled. The jungle does not strangle the machine; the machine does not burn the jungle. They use each other.

And the longer you stay, the stranger the terms of that use become.

Leaves here have learned metallurgy. Some of the ferns come up with a copper sheen that is not just shine; hold one to the dim bulb-light and you see actual veins of verdigris in the tissue. The big elephant-ears near the boiler have rivets — small, hard, round nodules along the midrib, spaced exactly like the boiler plate behind them. The fungi growing from the gasket crack pulse, very faintly, in time with the piston: swell on the chuff, slack on the return. Condensation does not always fall down. Near the hot grates, beads lift off the underside of pipes and rise a foot or two before vanishing, as if the heat has reversed a small local gravity for water.

The rule, if there is one, is exchange. Metal sweats like skin here. Leaves conduct like wire. If you lean your back against a warm pipe long enough, you will feel your heartbeat slow to match the flywheel — twenty seconds, turn, turn — and the fronds nearest you will angle, almost imperceptibly, toward the sound of your breathing, the way they angle toward steam leaks. Do not fight it. Drink the air slowly. Move slowly. Everything here is on the long cycle: drip, unfurl, oxidize, turn. Haste reads as heat, and there is already too much heat.

At the far wall, behind the pump house, there is a place where the vines part and you can put your hand flat on the retaining wall. It is warm through the moss. You can feel, through brick and earth, the slow knock of something deeper still working. And above you, through the slit of white sky, a cloud crosses — for a moment, true rain spatters down into the hollow, hissing where it hits hot metal, beading on a thousand leaves at once. It lasts a minute. Then it stops. The dripping continues, from walls, from pipes, from leaf-tips, as if the hollow had learned rain and decided to keep doing it itself, indefinitely, in the dark, with no animals to witness it but you.
