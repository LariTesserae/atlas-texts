The Summit Cistern

You come up out of the clouds and it is there, all at once — small enough to hold in a single look. A hamlet-sized bowl perched where no bowl should be.

From the outside it is a mountain, or the stump of a tower. Sheer concrete falling away on all sides into white weather, into nothing. The air up here is thin and cool. Then you step over the rim wall and down, and you are in warm water up to the chest.

Inside, the whole world is made. There is no ground, no soil, no rock showing through. Floor, walls, rim, walkway — poured concrete still showing the grain of its formwork, tiled in cracked white ceramic, bolted steel plates, sharp new welds beaded like fresh sutures. The edges have not had time to soften. Rebar ends bristle unrusted. Valve wheels still have their crisp knurling. Everything is young, recently emerged, as if the place was welded together last spring and then immediately drowned.

Because it is drowned. The bowl holds a shallow, impossible sea, three meters deep and not spilling. Warm as bathwater, clear as glass in the noon light. Normal daylight — high mountain sun straight down — but filtered through three meters of green and iron so it arrives in slow ribbons and netting, shivering across every surface. Above the waterline the air shimmers with steam; below, the light holds still.

And it is packed. There is no empty water here.

The floor is a forest of industry, planted stem to stem. Boiler tanks squat like iron pumpkins. Dynamo housings. Transformer boxes sweating oil that never quite surfaces. Standpipes and gooseneck vents and a slow conveyor chain that crawls inches per hour along the bottom, carrying trays of seedlings that will never be harvested. Copper coils gone verdigris. Rubber hoses fat as pythons. Switchboards blinking patient green and amber behind glass that algae has filmed over.

And on every inch of that iron, the lush.

Moss upholsters the pipes in velvet an inch thick. Ferns unfurl from expansion joints. Water hyacinths mat the surface so densely you could almost mistake it for lawn, their bulbous roots dangling down like lungs. Strangler figs have found the warm seams of two boilers and split them with pale roots. Orchids, white and magenta, bloom straight out of a fuse box, their roots drinking condensation off live bus bars. Kelp-like vines — not kelp, something freshwater and insistent — spiral up every vertical, leaf after leaf after leaf, until the whole submerged factory looks like a drowned conservatory. Algal felt, duckweed, lily pads the size of serving trays, hanging curtains of pondweed that move, barely, in a current you cannot feel.

There is no room between presences. Leaf presses against pipe presses against leaf. A lily pad grows around a pressure gauge and swallows it, leaving only the needle visible, twitching. If you lift your arm, watercress tangles your wrist and a warm cable brushes your elbow.

It is an industrial place still running, but running slow and drowned-quiet.

You expect roar and you get almost silence. Water takes the engines. What remains is a low, felt hum more than heard — a 60-cycle throb in your sternum from the big pumps under the floor, pulsing once every few minutes: *tuh —* and a column of warm water sighs up from a grate, lifts the hyacinths, lets them settle. The tick-tick-tick of hot metal contracting underwater. An occasional bead of gas detaching from a valve with a soft *plik* and then rising — but rising wrong.

Nothing here rises straight. That is the first strangeness. Buoyancy is tired at this altitude. Fallen leaves do not fall; they hang suspended for hours, turning over almost imperceptibly, before deciding to sink another inch. Bubbles wander, sidle sideways, pause, join into trembling clusters under lily pads and stay there, shivering. Pilot lights burn underwater behind little glass cages, blue flames flattened and steady, warming the water immediately around them where the most tropical mosses crowd closest, as if around campfires.

The second strangeness is the trade the two kingdoms have made. The leaves here are hard. You brush a fern and it clinks, its fronds stiff as enameled tin, edged sharp enough to nick. And the machines have gone a little photosynthetic — the copper pipes blush green in sun and pale at night, the insulation felt grows faint fuzz toward the light. You cannot always tell, at a glance, what was built and what grew. A ribbed conduit and a vine stem, side by side, same diameter, same green.

And the third strangeness is the absence. There are no animals. No fish flicker between the pipes, no snails on the tiles, no water beetles, no mosquito larvae wriggling, no birds landing on the rim to drink, though the rim is low and the sky is open. Not even a buzz. A leaf falls, and no mouth eats it, so the water should be choked — but it isn’t. The machines prune instead. Once an hour a slow rake on a track drags itself the length of the tank, gathering deadfall into a hopper where a grinder sighs it to pulp that the roots drink. The system tends itself, plant and engine, without appetite.

The rules here are simple because there was never any wilderness to negotiate with:

1. Everything roots in the made. There is no earth to return to, only rust, ash, felt, grout.
2. Water stays. Despite heat, despite sun, despite being perched a mile up in thin air over a drop of clouds, the level never drops a finger’s width. Evaporation beads on the inside of the steam and falls back as warm rain that never quite breaks the surface tension.
3. Change is patient. A valve opens a quarter-turn per season. Rust blooms by millimeters. A fig root widens a crack by a hair a year. You can watch, if you stand very still with water at your chin and sun netting on your face, the whole packed bowl breathing — almost imperceptible — in, out.

From where you stand you can see its boundaries in every direction: the circular rim wall, chest-high above the water outside and knee-high inside, tiled, moss-capped, with ladders and overflow spouts that have never overflowed. Beyond it, only sky and the tops of clouds moving far below, proving how high you are. Gulls should be screaming. There is only the soft knock of a lily pad against a standpipe, and your own breath, loud in the steam.

If you stay until the light slants, the amber pilot cages glow brighter, the green blurs deeper, and the entire small sea — factory, garden, warm bath — holds its heat into the cold mountain evening without losing a degree, humming to itself so quietly you feel it in your teeth.
