At first you think it's a cirque. Then you think it's a room.

It is that small. You can stand in the middle of it and see all of its edges at once — a black, jagged rim all the way around, not weathered, not softened, teeth still glassy from where they broke. The air is thin up here. Your lungs have to work for it. Breath comes out white and doesn't go away right away; it hangs. That tells you two things: how high you are, and how cold and wet the air is.

The light never gets better than this. A low, blue-grey dim, like the hour before sunrise stretched out and nailed in place. No sun, no shadows with hard edges, just a luminous overcast pressing down close over the rim. Everything condenses out of it. Your jacket beads. Your eyelashes bead. The rocks bead.

The ground is young. That is the strangest thing to feel under your boots. It hasn't learned to be ground yet. Sharp aa lava, black and rust-red, fractured into plates and daggers and hollow blisters that ring when you step near them. No soil to speak of, no smoothing. Between the shards are pools — not streams, not flowing anywhere, just black mirrors the size of a table, a door, a bed. The water table is right there, a finger below the stone. Every depression is full. It should overflow. It doesn't.

Everything weeps. The inner walls of the bowl sweat constantly. Not in runs or waterfalls, but in a uniform ooze, thousands of drops forming, swelling, trembling, never quite falling. If you touch the rock your fingers come away cold and smelling faintly of iron and hot oil. That smell is everywhere — ozone and diesel and wet pennies — though there is nothing here that could make it. No road. No cairn. No cut stump. No wire. No nail. No one has ever built anything here.

And yet it is industrial.

You hear it before you understand the shape of the place. A low, loud, layered working that fills the bowl and doesn't echo the way it should. A chuffing, piston-regular, from the water: seeps in the wall gulping air and then sighing it out, gulping and sighing, dozens of them out of sync until the whole rim seems to breathe like bellows. A turbine whine where the wind shears over the sharp rim — the young rock is full of flutes and needle-eyes and it sings, not like wind, like bearings. And under that, a constant high electrified crackle. On dry days at altitude you get St. Elmo's fire; here in the wet it never leaves. Blue sparks crawl on moss tips, on feather tips, on your knuckles. Your hair lifts. The air tastes metallic.

It should be deafening at the center. It isn't fading with distance, either, which is wrong. Ten steps or a hundred, the loudness stays the same, as if the sound isn't traveling at all but just existing, everywhere, at equilibrium.

Scattered across the black floor, far apart with a lot of nothing between them, are the green ones.

Lush is too small a word, but sparse is true. There might be twenty of them in the whole bowl, no more. Each is an island. Great domed cushions of moss five feet high and ten across, so saturated they quiver, so green they look lit from inside in this dim. Liverworts like wet leather, ferns as tall as you, their fronds unrolled and heavy with water, drip-tips always about to drip. From a distance each mound steams faintly in the cold. Up close you see why: they are furnaces. The moss is warm to the palm. Inside each cushion there is a low churning, a fermentation, a composting that never completes. Beetles work the surface in shifts, black and copper, thousands per mound, clicking like type-keys, like rivet guns. They carry droplets from the underside to the top and back down again. They never stop. The pile never gets bigger, never gets smaller.

Nothing here ever changes. That is the rule you learn slowest.

The fauna is abundant — overwhelmingly so, once you stop looking for big animals. The air between the green islands, which looked empty, is full. Clouds of white midges hang in fixed columns, each column humming at a slightly different pitch, together making a chord that hurts your teeth. They don't disperse. They don't get eaten down. Moths the size of your thumb, ash-grey, flap in place over the pools without ever landing, without ever tiring. High above, circling the rim and never leaving the bowl, are birds — hundreds — white ptarmigan-things with black eyes. They fly a conveyor circuit: up the east wall, across, down the west wall, across, up again. Always the same loop. Their wingbeats add a canvas-flapping, belt-slapping rhythm to the piston-breath of the rocks. Sometimes one opens its beak and lets out a perfect steam-whistle, two tones, and another answers. The echo comes *before* the call. You will check this three times to be sure.

On the ground, between pools, the whistlers. Marmot-like, but too long, too wet-looking, their fur spiked with condensed water and crackling blue. They sit up on shards and pump their chests — bellows, bellows — and scream. Not alarm calls. Sustained notes, loud, mechanical, like saws biting stone. Their throats throb. They do not move from their rocks. You will remember which rock each one sits on, because hours later they will still be there, mid-scream, same posture.

And production without product. That is the industry here.

Watch the birds long enough and you realize they are carrying nothing, making nothing, delivering nothing. The beetles lift water only to set it back down. The moss burns sugar only to stay exactly as warm, exactly as big. The water seeps in from the weeping walls into the pools, and the pools steam back into the cloud ceiling, and the cloud condenses onto the walls. Level never rises. Level never falls.

Even the sharpness doesn't dull. You cut your finger on a flake — it is that fresh, that newly broken — and the blood beads, bright, and the midges find it, and the cut in the rock where your blood fell stays exactly as sharp. No rust. No lichen creeping over. The green islands do not seed outward into the black. The black does not crumble inward. It is a young place that refuses to age.

The weirdness isn't a flourish on top of this. It *is* this. Stand still long enough in the cold — and you will stand still, because the thin air and the chord make you a little nauseous, a little outside yourself — and you notice the small breakdowns:

Your breath fog hangs too long, then vanishes all at once, not dissipating.

A drop on a fern tip swells, detaches, and stops an inch below the tip, hanging in air, trembling, never landing.

A bird crossing overhead casts no shadow on the moss, but the moss briefly dims as if remembering shade.

When you turn your head fast, the turbine whine lags half a second behind, catching up with a click.

There is no night and no day here, no weather front moving through, no season. Post-rain without ever having rained. Wet without flow. Work without wear. The bowl holds its cold, its dim blue light, its oil-and-ozone smell, its loud equilibrium like water held in a cupped hand.

You can walk it rim to rim in ten minutes if you step carefully between the pools. You will not want to leave a mark. There is nowhere to leave one that would stay, and nowhere that needs one. The place is already full — not with things, but with process. An untouched mill. Engines with no housing, made of breath and beetle and bird-circuit and weeping stone, running at full power forever to keep everything exactly as it is.
