You come up onto it without climbing — the ground simply is high. A tableland so broad you stop thinking in slopes and start thinking in distance.

The light is ordinary, midday-white and warm on the skin. It does not seem to come from any particular hour. Shadows lie short and sharp under every stone and do not lengthen while you watch. The air is thin enough that your breath works a little harder than it should in warmth, and so dry it takes the inside of your mouth. There is no damp anywhere to correct it.

This is the first rule: there is no water here. Not hidden water. Not waiting water.

No riverbeds, even dry ones. No lake rings. No clouds, no mist, no dew in the mornings because there is no morning. The rock has never been rounded by rain. The dust under your boots is not sand — it is cinder, glass-shard, raw flour of crushed basalt and rust, black-red and ochre, so completely anhydrous it squeaks and does not pack. It does not smell like earth. It smells like hot stone, iron, faint ozone.

And the geology is new. That is the second thing you feel once your eyes adjust to the emptiness. Nothing has had time to wear down. The plateau is all fresh edges: cooling cracks still razor-straight, blisters where the crust bubbled and froze mid-breath, pressure ridges like black rope, shallow pans of salt and copper-salts laid down in crystalline fur that no foot, no rain, has blurred. There are no valleys. There are suggestions of where valleys will one day be — a slightly darker line, a gathering of cracks — embryonic drainage that has never carried anything. The world looks under construction and then abandoned at the exact moment it became habitable.

It is mostly empty.

From where you stand you can see for kilometers to a pale, unbroken horizon, and in all that space there is almost nothing per volume. Stone, light, still air. Then — green.

Not a wash of green. Knots of it. Islands.

Half a mile off, then two miles beyond that, then near the edge of sight, dense clumps of vegetation sit on the bare mineral like something spilled and taken root. From a distance they look impossibly lush for where they are: rounded, closed canopies, layered, dripping with leaf, dark emerald and verdigris and young lime. No transition, no meadow thinning out. Barren glass to jungle in one step.

Walking toward the nearest one, the silence gets to you. There is no animal life to fill it. No insects ticking in the heat, no birds, nothing rustling away from your approach. Your own boots on scoria are embarrassingly loud. Your breathing is loud. When the wind should move, it barely does — a slow, warm displacement that lifts the finest dust and sets it down again a few meters on.

Closer, the lushness resolves into something wrong in a useful way.

These are not water plants. They cannot be. Nothing here has water to give them, and they do not ask for it.

The grove is perhaps forty meters across, and within that circle the density is tropical: broad leaves overlapping broad leaves three and four deep, vines roping between trunks, low sprouts crowding for light under the canopy. But the trunks are not wood. They are vertical columns, dark iron-red and swollen at the base like boilers, bark flaking off in enameled plates. Where a trunk has split, you do not see sapwood. You see chambers, pipes, fibrous wadding soaked in amber oil that catches the light and does not evaporate.

The leaves are thick, waxy to the point of being ceramic. Green, yes — a deep, oiled green — but veined with copper. They do not transpire. They do not wilt. They hold heat. If you press one between your fingers it is warm from inside, and it leaves no wet mark, only a faint smear of machine-smell: hot varnish, kerosene, crushed geraniums without any water in them. Some of the larger leaves have curled into funnels that act as condensers, ticking faintly as they cool. The undersides are dusted with a fine yellow powder — spores, pollen, product — manufactured by the thousand and released not to wind or bee, because there are no bees, but to pressure. Every so often a frond shivers and puffs it out in a short, silent exhalation and the powder settles straight down onto the cracked rock to begin again.

Because this is industry without builders.

There is no structure here that was made. No wall, no road, no ruin, no bolt, no weld, no foundation cut into the stone. And yet everything in the grove is engine.

Roots, thick as your thigh, leave the trunks and dive not down for a water table that does not exist but outward and shallow, fracturing the newborn basalt, secreting acid-oils that pit and crumble the glass. You can hear, if you kneel and hold your breath, the work: a very quiet tick-tick-tick of contraction, a sigh as a root-valve opens and closes. The whole plant maintains pressure. Stems swell and release. Seed-pods, round and riveted-looking though no rivets hold them, fatten on high limbs in perfect, scaled production — hundreds identical, then thousands — each filled with the same amber fuel and a coiled embryo waiting for a crack to open.

They make soil without rot. Fallen leaves do not molder — there is nothing to molder them, no fungus gnat, no bacteria film of damp. They lie where they fall, crisping in the warm light, then cracking into flakes that the oil-soaked rootlets creep over and dissolve chemically. Nothing is wasted because nothing can afford to leave the system. The grove is a closed factory that eats rock and light and exhales itself.

Stand up and step back ten paces and you are out of it again. The edge is that abrupt. One foot in litter of enameled leaf-fall, the next in sterile cinder. Behind you the canopy continues its quiet, ceaseless chemistry; ahead the void resumes.

From the edge you can count them: seven groves visible from this one rise, each a dark green engine-idling in the red-black flat, each alone, each complete. Between them, nothing moves. No creepers advance across the gap. No seeds blow over — the spores fall straight. The pattern does not spread, does not shrink. It holds.

That is the last rule, and the strangest: nothing changes. Not in the way of death, but in the way of perfect balance. No growth spurt, no die-off, no succession. The groves do not get larger year by year because there are no years. The rock does not erode because there is nothing to erode it, only the slow prying of roots that crack and fill in equal measure. The light stays where it is. The air stays warm and thin and still. The silence is not an absence of event — it is the sound of a system in equilibrium, humming so low and so constantly you mistake it for quiet.

If you stay long enough, your ears tune to it: the faint metallic ping of a leaf cooling, the soft exhalation of spore-release, the almost subsonic thrum in the soles of your feet where pressure builds and vents deep under the plateau.

No one built this. Nothing eats here. Nothing drinks. And yet in the middle of a high, dry, newborn desert of glass, the green is lush — dense, layered, productive as any mill town — manufacturing itself out of stone and sun, island by island, with vast, sunlit emptiness left reverently between.
