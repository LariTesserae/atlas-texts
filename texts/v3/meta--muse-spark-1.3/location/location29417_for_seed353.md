The Dry Stacks on High Night Mesa.

At first you think it's a forest. Then you think it's a city. Then you realize it's both and neither — it is a crop that learned to be a building, and a building that learned to keep growing after its builders stopped coming.

There is no water here. That is the first rule, older than any sign. No river, no well, no rain that matters. The air at noon — if noon meant anything under this sky — is hot and paper-dry,  and it rasps the inside of the throat. The ground doesn't squelch, it rings: slag-concrete, kiln-brick, rusted mesh, all furred over with dust so fine it gets into the seams of your teeth. Where a low spot dips between terraces, the dust has cemented with plant resin into a salt-pan glaze, cracked in hexagons, black-amber and shining like cooled slag.

And yet everything is alive. Total, choking, wall-to-wall alive.

The terraces climb and fall for kilometers across the top of the mesa, a town-sized bowl of stepped growing-beds, trellis-towers, pipe-bridges, vat-houses. Nothing is natural. Even the "soil" is manufactured — centuries of shredded vine-mat, coke-ash, crushed furnace brick pressed into felted slabs. But there is no bare patch left to see it. Blackvine covers it. Redstem covers the blackvine. Bladder-moss — which is not a moss — covers the redstem.

The plants here don't want water. They gave that up a very long time ago. Their leaves are not green. In the dark they look black-purple, thick as belt leather, edged with a waxy amber keel. They drink heat. They drink soot. Their roots go not down but sideways, into the warm breath of the heating ducts that run under every walkway, and their broad blind leaves turn their faces not to any sun — there is no sun to speak of — but to the low infrared glow of the service pipes, the furnace seams, the dull orange cracks in the boiler houses. Thermosynthesis. Hold your hand near a mature leaf at "night" and you feel it faintly cooler than the air around it, stealing the warmth out of the wind.

It is always night here, or something like it. The sky is a sealed lid of smog and high dust, the color of cold tea, holding in the day's furnace heat long after any day should have ended. No stars. No moon. The light is what leaks: a seam of furnace-orange from a half-open stoke door three terraces down, a string of dim red maintenance lamps along a catwalk, strangled almost immediately by leaves, a phosphorescent freckling where ripe spore-pods split. Your eyes adjust and then give up. You navigate more by heat on your cheeks than by sight. Shadows are total between the stacks, and the stacks are everywhere.

Because it is high. You feel it in the lungs despite the heat — the air is thin up here on the mesa, and hot-thin is a strange suffocation, like breathing through warmed wool. At the edges of the Stacks, where the vine has overrun the guardrails and poured itself over the rim in frozen cataracts of stem, you can sense the drop rather than see it: a colder updraft cutting sideways through the hot, carrying mineral dust that smells of distant stone. The whole mass sits up in the sky like a burning barge, isolated and dry.

It is ancient. Nothing here is sharp anymore. The concrete steps that were once poured square have been rounded by two hundred years of root-pressure and bootless machine treads into swells, like dunes. The iron trellises, originally lattice towers ten meters high, have sagged under the wet — not wet, the *weight* — of generations of vine into leaning arches, elephantine ribs, knuckled bridges. Paint, signage, rivets: all gone, digested into the felt. Even the machines look grown. The pruner-drones that crawl the rows on rubbered tracks are so muffled in leaf-litter felt and resin drips that you only know them by the slow hump they make under the canopy as they pass, and by the fresh-cut smell that follows.

Packed. That is the word the lungs understand. There is no *between*. Stem against pipe against leaf against wall against leaf against leaf. The walkways are not paths so much as tunnels chewed through living mass and kept open by constant cutting. If you stand still for an hour — you won't, it's too hot — tendrils will find your bootlaces. The density is structural: the plants hold up the buildings now, and the buildings hold up the plants. Pull one and the other sighs.

And for all that, there is nothing with eyes. No birds, no rats, no insects even. The heat and the resin and the absolute absence of free water starved them out a century ago. Pollination is done by shudder: at irregular intervals a whole sector will tremble as the air-pumps reverse and send a dull thump through the ductwork, shaking pollen like black pepper into the hot wind. Fertilization is industrial. The only things that move with purpose are the machines — boilers, conveyors, augers, cutters — running on looped programs no one remembers writing, feeding shredded vine back to the boilers to make more heat to make more vine.

It is mostly quiet. That is the strangest part. With that much life and that much engine, you expect roar. Instead the felt of living mass eats sound. What reaches you is low and close: the creak-tick of stems tightening in the heat, the soft *pop* of a seed-pod splitting somewhere overhead, the long exhalation of a pressure valve, the distant clank-clank-clank of a conveyor carrying cut bundles to the furnaces, muffled as if through blankets. Your own footsteps are deadened. You hear your own blood.

But don't mistake quiet for still. The Stacks are turbulent, storm-state alive. Growth here is violent in fast-forward. A pruned tunnel will close six inches by the next shift. A trellis-tower overloaded with fruiting mass will groan, lean, and then give in a slow, terrible collapse — no crash, just a choking slump — opening a sudden pit of orange furnace-light from below, steam, a rush of scalding air that smells of scorched cinnamon and ozone. Within hours, new shoots, pale white-pink and blind, will be spearing straight for that light-heat, crossing each other, knotting. The cutter crews — squat, heat-shielded automata with spinning discs — will converge, humming low, and take it back down. Cut, collapse, regrow, burn, regrow. The whole valley breathes like that, in and out, without ever cooling.

The rules, if you stay long enough to learn them, are simple and unbeatable:

1. Nothing drinks. Any transpiration is theft. Leaves seal at their edges with wax; to tear one is to smell sharp resin and see not sap but hot oil bead and instantly skin over.
2. Keep to the cut paths. The mass remembers pressure and grows toward it.
3. Do not bring animals. They die. The air will not support them and the plants will not feed them.
4. Feed the furnace. Everything pruned goes down the chutes. The heat must stay up or everything — plant, machine, tunnel — stalls and bakes into one dead brick.

At the center, if you climb — hand over hot hand over vine-rope — to the oldest part, the First Boiler House, you find the final form: walls completely replaced by lignified stem as hard as ironwood, boilers nested like seeds inside a fruit, their riveted skins gone green-black with heat scale, still thudding. Around them the vine has grown into shapes that are no longer trellis or tower but something settled, near-geological: domes, saddles, hanging curtains fifty meters thick that sway faintly though there is no wind. Spore-dust hangs in the lamplight, slow, glittering, dry as ash, never settling.
