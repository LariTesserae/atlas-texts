You come down into it before you see it.

The trail is not earth. That is the first thing. Long before the light gives out, the ground stops being ground. It becomes laid stone, then plank, then stone again — rough blocks still showing the tooth-marks of the point, mortar still dark and damp between them, never given time to cure. Low country. A basin shallow and wide, the air settling cold into it and staying there. Your breath goes white. Your boots do not touch soil anywhere.

Below, the Works.

At first in the dark you think it is a hillside of rock, until the rock moves. Until you understand the whole valley floor, as far as the dark lets you guess — a town's worth, a small region's worth — has been floored over and built up, and built up again. Nothing natural left to stand on. Platforms on posts on platforms. Wattle huts with daub still wet. Dry-stone pens and walls only waist-high before the next ladder starts. Towers of stacked slate that lean, all of them, slightly inward, like reeds in still water. Rope bridges sagging between them dense as loom threads. Scaffolds everywhere — raw poles lashed with hide, the bark still on some, but no living wood. No green anywhere. No moss, no grass, no tree. Only old black timber brought from somewhere else long ago and used and reused until it shines with hands, and stone, and bone, and mud.

It is unfinished. That is the second thing. The erosion here is not wearing down, it is just beginning. Every surface is fresh-broken. Chisel dust in drifts in the corners. Flakes of limestone sharp underfoot that have not yet been rounded by feet. A wall half-raised with the mason's string still tied across it. A floor with a hole sawn for a ladder that hasn't arrived. The place smells of wet rock flour and cold smoke, of lime and tallow and unwashed wool. Where a roof should be there is open frame, and through the frame the dark drips.

For water does not flow here. It condenses.

The air is cool — constantly cool, the kind of cool that lives in stone — and damp in a way that has nothing to do with rain. You rarely see rain. Instead the cold surfaces sweat. The underside of every plank beads. The stone weeps. It gathers and falls. Plink. Plink. In the dark you learn to walk with your shoulders hunched, because at any moment a cold drop will find your neck. The people here have learned to farm the air.

Everywhere, troughs. Hollowed logs, split bones, folded hides pitched with tar, half-pipes of fired clay still rough from the hand — all set to catch what the night air gives up. They run along eaves, along walkways, along the rims of cisterns. The cisterns are the only deep places: stone-lined pits sunk into the platform, covered with skins to keep the soot out, holding black water a foot or two deep that shivers when a drop hits. You hear it even over the hammering — that low, separate *tok* of water into water. In the larger dryness between, everything is only slick. Puddles in the hollows of stone that never quite dry. A sheen on the ladders where hands have polished the damp. Your own clothes grow heavy by the hour.

And it is loud. Unreasonably loud for darkness.

Hammer on stone. Maul on wedge. *Tak-tak-tak* from a hundred directions at once, unsynchronized, overlapping into a continuous clatter that the low cold air holds close to the ground instead of letting it rise. Timber creaks under weight. Rope groans. Somewhere someone is always chanting to keep time for hauling — a short hoarse call and answer — and somewhere water is being poured from skin to trough with a long guttural gurgle. Drips amplify in the cisterns into bells. A dropped chisel rings like a coin. Your ears never adjust. The dark makes it louder, because you cannot see what is coming.

Light: there is almost none. No sky to speak of — whether roofed by smoke or by night itself you cannot tell. The darkness sits low and complete, cool on the eyes. What light there is is ember-light. Small tallow dips in clay cups, shielded by hands. A forge-pit here and there where copper softens, a dull orange that throws huge, unsteady shadows up the scaffolds and makes the leaning towers seem to sway. Fire is rationed; wood is dead and precious. People move mostly by touch and by memory, by the feel of a familiar knot in a rope. You see them as densities in the dark more than figures — shoulders, backs, hands — near-touching, layered. A child asleep in a hammock strung under a walkway while feet pass inches above her face. Two men passing a block hand to hand on a ladder so narrow their chests brush. An old woman scraping condensation from a hide awning into a jar with a shell. Everyone close. The Works does not allow distance.

Animals are rare. You notice their absence by its length. No birds calling. No dogs. Only now and then: a pale rat picking along a trough, too thin. A blind white fish, no longer than a finger, flickering in a cistern when a lamp passes — put there on purpose, to eat the wrigglers. Once, high on a pole, a knot of torpid moths the color of ash, wings furred with damp, not flying. Life here is mostly human, and human is maintenance.

Because the rule here, unspoken but absolute, is this: you build, or you get wet, or you fall through.

Nothing stands by itself. Stone settles. Lashings stretch in the damp and must be re-tightened. Daub slumps before it dries. A trough tipped by a finger's breadth will spill its night's harvest onto a sleeping family below. So the hammering never stops. Even as you watch, a boy takes apart the very walkway you just crossed to replace a rotted plank beneath it with a fresh-split one, his hands moving in the dark by habit. Behind him another boy is already re-lashing what he lifted. The place is active the way a hive is active — not fast, but without pause. Sweat in the cold. Breath steaming. Condensation forming on warm skin only to be wiped away.

And the strange part settles in slowly.

The echoes come wrong. You hear a hammer strike a half-second *before* you see the arm fall in lamplight across the way. Or the drip from a trough above you falls *up* — no, not up, but sideways, drawn along a rope by capillary until it runs along the underside of a beam to drop somewhere else entirely, as if water here has learned the architecture better than the builders. And the buildings, for all their crude handwork — stone pecked with antler picks, holes bored with sand and a stick, rope twisted from hair — repeat themselves too perfectly. The same lean, the same three-pole joint, the same trough hung at the same angle, valley-wide, though no one seems to be directing it. As if the damp and the cold and the dark only allow one shape to stand, and the hands have found it over and over.

If you stand still long enough, someone will press a tool into your hand. That is the other rule. There are no watchers here. The density does not allow it. To be present is to be part of the layering, to add your warmth to the air so that later, on cold stone, it can condense and fall and be drunk.

Above you, out past the last lamp, the clatter continues into blackness where more platforms are being laid. You cannot see the edge of it. You can only hear that it does not end, and feel the cold beads forming on the back of your hands, and hear, underneath everything, the patient *tok — tok — tok* of water gathering where no river runs.
