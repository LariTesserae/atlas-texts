The bowl first. You notice the edge before you notice what's inside it.

You are high — high enough that the air is thin and cool in your lungs, high enough that the slopes below have fallen away into cloud. The ridge you stand on is sharp, unsoftened. This is young rock. Black basalt still edged like broken glass, scoria that crumbles under boots, a cirque not yet worn smooth by ice or water. It hasn't learned to be round. It juts and cracks. A rockfall from last spring still sits where it landed, lichen just beginning to freckle the undersides, orange on black.

And it is small. That is the strangest thing. After the long climb through empty alpine fell, you expect vastness. Instead there is a room. A single scooped hollow, no wider than a hamlet, maybe three hundred paces rim to rim, walled all around. You can see its entire boundary from where you stand. A cathedral nave made of mountain.

Light barely gets in. The cloud cap sits low here most of the year, and the walls shade the floor. What daylight comes is filtered, blue-grey and diffuse, like evening that never decides to become night or morning. Shadows don't have hard edges. Colors stay muted until you are close — and then they are violent.

Because inside the room, it is lush.

Not everywhere. That is important. Most of the floor is bare: cold black sand, frost-split pavement, young stone too fresh to hold soil. Emptiness with a lot of space between things. But where life has caught, it has caught excessively. In the lee of boulders, in cracks, in the drip-line under overhangs, moss has piled on moss until it is knee-deep and spongy. Ferns uncurl taller than a child. Dwarf cloud-forest trees — stunted, wind-bent, bearded with old-man's lichen — cluster in five or six dense groves, islands of green in the dark rock sea. Their leaves are huge, waxed, cupped to catch what little water there is.

There is no stream here. No lake. This place is only damp. It rains occasionally, hard and brief, and then for days there is nothing but condensation. The cold rock breathes out; the warm air trapped at the bottom breathes in. At dawn and dusk the fog pours over the low notch in the rim like a slow waterfall and fills the bowl to your waist, then burns off. Everything drips. You hear it before you see anything else — a solitary plink from a fern frond to a stone, another from an overhang. The trees sweat. Stones sweat. Your own coat, if you stand still, will bead with water that was never rain. In the hollows of basalt there are pockets, tea-colored pools no bigger than a cooking pot, where midges skate.

The town is built around that dampness.

You didn't see it at first because it is the same color as the rock. Low, round houses of dry-stacked basalt and turf, half-sunk for warmth, terraced up the more sheltered wall in three uneven tiers. Flat slate roofs weighted with stones. Rope bridges of twisted moss-fiber sagging between them. Narrow stone gutters, built pebble by pebble by hand, run from every roof to a central chain of cisterns — deep, lidded pits lined with clay. Above the highest houses, on poles of bent tree-limb, hang the fog-nets: loose weaves of hair and plant fiber that go stiff with collected dew and drip all day into troughs.

It is unmistakably urban. Planned. Layered. Paths paved, retaining walls, communal drying racks, a tiny plaza of trampled ash where fire pits are shared. But everything is hand-made. No sawn timber — the trees are too precious, too slow. No iron. Stone adzes, bone awls, slate knives, wooden digging sticks polished by generations of hands. Ladders lashed with sinew. Felted moth-wool cloaks hung over doors. The sparse part is the people. You might count twenty hearths but see only three old women mending nets, a child carrying water in a skin bag too big for her. Much space between presences. The town feels larger than its inhabitants, as if it were built to be tended in shifts, or as if most of its citizens are elsewhere.

They are. They are in the air.

The fauna here is overwhelming, and utterly quiet.

When the fog lifts, they rise. Moths — pale, hand-sized, ash-grey and luna-white, in numbers that turn the dim air to falling snow going upward. Thousands. Tens of thousands. They pour out of the moss, out of the groves, off the undersides of leaves where they roosted as caterpillars fat as your thumb. Their wings make no sound, or a sound so soft you mistake it for your own breath — a hush-hush-hush that is more pressure than noise. Among them drift other things: long-legged crane flies like dangling seeds, furred bumblebees slow with cold, tiny flightless birds like white mice with beaks that hop between fern stems, and above them all, circling without a single wingbeat you can hear, the owls.

The owls are white as the moths. Small, round-faced, fur-footed. They hunt entirely silently, as owls do, but here the silence is absolute. No screech. No snap of beak you can hear. You just see an owl open its talons and a moth is gone, and another, and another, and the cloud of moths closes around the gap and continues.

Everything is in motion, but muted. On the moss islands, the caterpillars are eating — you can see the fern edges scalloped overnight — and the flightless birds are eating the caterpillars, flipping leaves with quick jerks. The people are eating the caterpillars too, selectively, harvesting the largest and leaving the rest, and spinning the cast-off cocoons into that grey wool. The owls are eating the moths and the birds. The moths are drinking the dew from your shoulders. Drip, grow, spin, hatch, hunt. Predator and prey cycling at a frantic biological pace, all under a blanket of quiet so deep you hear your own swallow.

That quiet is the rule of the place, and it is also its strangeness.

The moss eats sound. Literally, almost. The deep sponge, the felted lichen, the fog itself swallow echoes. Shout and it falls flat three paces away. But the people will tell you — in whispers, always in whispers — not to try. After fog-drop, when the moths are thickest, loud noise confuses them. They fall. And if they fall, the owls go hungry, and if the owls go hungry, they take the chicks from the drying racks and the caterpillars from the farms. So: speak low. Step on stone, not moss, because moss holds nests. Leave water where you find it; carry only what the nets give you. And never, never take a white feather from the ground — those are the owls' tithe, left to rot back into the sponge.

There are other strangenesses you only notice if you stay. The basalt in the deepest part of the bowl is still faintly warm to the palm — this young earth hasn't cooled through. That is why the condensation gathers there, cold air kissing warm stone, and why the lushest grove grows in a ring around nothing, around a warm bare patch where no snow ever settles. The moths navigate by it. On windless nights you can see the whole cloud slowly revolving above that warm navel, like water going down a drain in reverse.

And if you stand very still in the dim blue light, with the cool damp soaking through your sleeves and the hush-hush of ten thousand wings moving the air against your cheeks, you realize the town is not really built for people at all. The terraces follow the moth flightlines. The cisterns are placed where dew falls heaviest for caterpillars, not where it is convenient to carry. The houses are low so as not to break the circling air. People are the sparsest thing here — tenants, tenders of nets and gutters — in a city whose overwhelming, silent, active citizenry has six legs or feathers, and who were here first, farming the moss long before the first stone was stacked.

You can walk the rim in an hour and see it all. And still, when you descend again into the bowl, the fog closing behind you, the dripping ferns rising to your waist, the pale snow going up and up and never thinning, it feels larger inside than out. A room that contains weather.
