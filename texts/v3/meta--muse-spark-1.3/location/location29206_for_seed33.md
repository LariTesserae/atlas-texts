At first it is only the air that tells you how high you are.

It is too thin to finish a breath in. Cool, not cold — the kind of cool that lives in shade even when there is no shade — it pulls the moisture straight off your lips, off your eyes, off the inside of your throat. You breathe in and your chest has to work for it, and the air you get for the work tastes of dust and old stone. No damp in it at all. Not the memory of rain. Your exhalation makes a brief white puff and then it is gone, ripped apart, as if the sky drinks it.

You are on a roof.

Not a peak. A roof — a vast, tilted tableland hanging above everything else, so high the clouds are a floor far below, a slow dirty-white sea that breaks against the black flanks of the massif and never reaches you. From up here the horizon is wrong. It bends. It falls away too fast on all sides, and the sky presses down close, dim and bruised, a perpetual late blue-grey with a small, pale sun stuck in it like a coin left in ash. It never climbs. It never sets. It gives light without glare, enough to see far, not enough to make shadow sharp. Everything is half-lit. The stars do not fully go away; they sit faintly through the day, lost grains of salt.

Underfoot: dry mineral. Utterly dry.

A plain of bone-white dust and shattered shale, salt-rimed, ringing faintly under your boots. No soil. No stream bed even, no dry wash, no ice. Even frost will not settle here, though the air is cool enough for it — there is nothing to freeze out of the air. The wind has swept the plateau clean for so long that the stones are polished into shapes that look settled, at ease with themselves. Low fins and domes and balanced slabs, weathered to softness, edges rounded as if by a hand that rubbed them for a thousand years and then left them. The land is old in its own state. It is not crumbling. It has arrived.

And then, as your eyes adjust to the dimness and the distance, you see it is not empty land.

It is streets.

What you took for natural fins resolves, with a sick lurch, into walls. Avenues. Blocks. For a valley's width in every direction the plateau is occupied — terraces stepping down toward the cloud-sea, towers standing apart with enormous intervals between them, arches spanning nothing, plazas of flat-packed dust, stairways wide enough for processions climbing the sides of low hills and stopping at the sky.

A town. A city. Regional, multi-centered, extending past what you can walk in a day. You can see three, four distinct quarters: a dense honeycomb of low domes to the north, a field of tall needles to the east, a sunken grid of courtyards to the south where the dust lies deeper. Infrastructure, undeniably — ramps for something heavy, gutters for something that never flows now, cisterns roofed over, layered construction where a second street bridges over a first, and a third bridges over that, all without mortar.

But there are no tool marks. That is the second lurch.

Come close to a wall and it is not cut. It is not stacked. It is grown, or secreted, or accreted — fine laminae like shell or tooth, calcium and chitin and volcanic ash fused into a matte, warm-grey stone. No chisel lines. No brick seams. No metal, no glass, no wire, no hearth-blackening, no hinge, no nail-hole, no rubbish. Nothing that was ever a device. The city is pure biology and geology. Pre-tool. As if something soft and huge once oozed here in organized streams and then hardened, or as if the wind itself learned architecture.

And it is hollow. That is the emptiness you feel in your teeth.

For all its size the place has almost nothing in it per volume. Vast space between presences. Towers stand a quarter-mile apart with nothing but humming air between. Plazas are enormous and bare. You walk an avenue fifty paces wide and your footsteps make no echo because there is nothing to echo off of for a long time. Even the buildings, when you find a doorway — low, oval, lip-like — open onto interiors far larger than their outsides should allow, and empty. Shell-rooms. The walls are thin. You knock and they ring like pottery. The whole city sounds, when the wind gusts, like an empty vessel being blown across the mouth of.

There is no plant life to soften it. Not a blade, not a root. Only, in the lee of walls, a matte black and rust-orange crust on the stone, thin as paint. It is not moss. It flakes like dried mineral when you touch it. If it is alive it lives so slowly as to be geology. That is all.

Life, where it is, is rare and moves above you.

Every hour or so a veil passes overhead — a glider, six feet across, translucent as a jellyfish skin stretched over a kite of bone, riding the updrafts off the cliff edge without flapping. They make no sound. Later you see one land on a needle-tower and fold itself into a slit in the stone and disappear. Lower down, in the dust, things no bigger than your thumb leave braided trails between courtyard and courtyard: dust-eaters, armored pill-shapes that sift the sterile powder for whatever falls from above — spores, dead gliders, salt. They freeze when you approach and become indistinguishable from pebbles. You could walk all day and count ten living things.

And yet the place is not still. It is active, ceaselessly, quietly.

The wind is the weather cycle here. It comes in long, cool tides, smelling of iron and distance. It lifts the white dust in sheets that move like slow water across the plazas — the only water this world will ever have — and lays it down again in new dunes against the walls. The towers tick and groan as they contract in the cool, a sound so low and infrequent you think you imagined it until a flake of stone pings off a terrace. In the sunken quarter, whole courtyard floors subtly breathe: they rise a finger's width by afternoon and settle by the dimmer hours, lifting dust in rings. Once a day the cloud-sea below boils up through a blowhole in the cliff and exhales a plume of cold vapor that never condenses, that falls apart into dryness before it touches the city.

If you stay long enough to walk it, the city stops behaving.

That is the rule here, and you learn it with your legs before your mind admits it.

Straight lines do not hold. An avenue that looks a half-mile long takes three hours to walk, and then you arrive back at the arch you started from, but from the other side. A stair you climb up twenty steps leaves you looking down on the top of your own head from a terrace you swear was below you a moment ago. Inside the larger shells, gravity goes negotiable: dust falls sideways along a wall, your pack pulls gently toward the ceiling, then corrects itself with a soft jolt that makes your stomach drop. Shadows fall upward in the needle-field at certain angles, detaching from their towers and pooling on the underside of nothing. And sound — what little sound there is — arrives wrong. You hear your boot scrape a half-second before your foot moves. You hear a glider's wing-snap ripple through the air after it has already passed.

Distances lie most of all. Two towers that seem adjacent will, as you walk toward them, recede until the space between them is a plain. A doorway that seemed shoulder-high becomes a vault you can stand in once you duck through. The city is bigger inside than outside, emptier the deeper you go, as if space here were thinned by altitude until it tore and was stitched back loosely.

No one built this with hands. No hands were ever needed. The city grew its avenues the way a reef grows its channels, for currents — only here the current is thin cold air and time. It maintains itself. It sheds skins of stone in papery curls that the wind stacks in corners. It opens new slits for the gliders to roost in and seals old ones. It waits, in dim light, in near-silence, on its high dry roof above the clouds, for the rare slow traffic of living dust to pass through its hollow streets, and it shifts, almost imperceptibly, under your feet while you stand still trying to understand it.
