It is not a lake. It is not a sea floor. It is a height, drowned.

Far below the surface that you will never see, a long mountain shoulder rises out of black. A high valley between two blunt ridges, the kind of country that used to hold weather — and now holds water instead. The rock remembers air. You can tell by the shape of it: not sharp volcanic newness, not abyssal plain, but settled, lived-in stone. Terraces cut and then rounded by centuries. Riverbeds that now carry current instead of river. Scree slopes softened to mud and moss. Everything weathered into its own balance long before the water came, and weathered again since.

The water is total. Cold enough to feel like a hand on the back of the neck, even through imagining. Two, three degrees above freezing, clear and heavy and absolutely dark. Light does not so much shine as leak — a bruised, deep-blue diffusion from impossibly far above that never quite becomes day. It fades out twenty, thirty meters out into ink. After that, the valley continues by feel and by glow.

Because the forest glows.

It is lush here, impossibly lush for cold and dark. Not the green riot of tropics, but a dense, slow, black-green abundance. From every flat, every old field-wall, every roof that held, rise the stalks. Wide-bladed kelps thick as leather straps, thirty, forty feet tall, their stipes dark umber and ridged like old rope. They do not rush for the surface; there is no surface to reach. They lean with the current, vast slow fields of them, interspersed with low woolly mosses in grey-white drifts, and with shelf-fungi the size of doors clinging to rock, and with hair-fine filament weeds that hang in curtains off every edge.

It is sparse, though. That is the first strangeness to settle. For all its lushness, there is so much space between lives. A tower of kelp here, rooted in a cracked foundation. Fifty feet of empty black water. A meadow of moss there, mounding over what was a road. Then nothing but cold mud rippled like corduroy, a fallen lintel stone, silence. The valley is town-sized, valley-sized — you can sense it extending past what you can see, ridge to ridge, bend to bend — but it is never crowded. Each presence holds its own sphere of emptiness. Even the forest respects distance.

And there is no eye in it.

No fin. No flick. No click or dart or scuttle. No gull-cry distorted down, no silver school turning. Not even a crab, not a worm-tube, not a speck you could call a swimmer. Only plant, and stone, and water. The absence gets loud after a while. You keep listening for what should be there in a place this green and hearing only the place itself. Dead fronds are not eaten. They fall and lie where they fall, in great slowly composting mats, because there is nothing to take them. They pale to yellow, then to translucent white, then to a fine silt that smells faintly sweet and iron. The forest feeds itself, leaf to mud to root, without intermediary. Growth is slow in the cold, but relentless. A stipe will split a dry-stone wall over a decade, patient as ice.

Scattered through that slow green are the built things.

Small. Hand-sized in intent. A hut of stacked slate, roofless now, its doorway facing down-current. Another, collapsed to three walls and a corner. A long low wall, hip-high, running fifty paces along an old terrace and then stopping where the ground slumped. Cairns — deliberate piles, too neat for rockfall — on promontories, each topped with a flat capstone. Stone circles where something was threshed or stored or marked. Rope, impossibly, still: braided lines of kelp-fiber, thick as a wrist, strung from post to post, now colonized by moss until they look like green veins. Tool-marks on the slate: the short, chipped percussion of hand hammers, of prying, not cutting. No metal. No glass. No machine-memory at all.

No one lives in them. No one has lived in them for longer than the water can remember.

And yet — here the place refuses to be simple — some of them are dry.

You come to a hut intact enough to hold its roof slabs, its doorway a low black arch facing you, kelp leaning away from it. Inside the doorway, water stops. Not ices, not films — stops, as if cut. Beyond the threshold, air. Old, cold, still air that smells of stone and smoke from a fire a hundred years drowned. You can see dust hanging in it, lit faintly from the water-light at the door. The floor inside is dry mud, cracked. A stone bench. A niche in the wall with a coil of fiber rope, dry as bone. If you put your hand through (you cannot, but if you did) your hand would be dry on the other side, and freezing, and the water would close around your wrist like a tight collar, refusing to follow.

There are several of these. Not all. Only the ones that were well-built, and closed, and loved, perhaps. They keep their last breath. The forest knows. Pale rootlets reach in through the roof cracks toward the air but never pierce it; they flatten against the invisible skin and spread, drinking condensation. At night — which is always — the air-rooms faintly tick as pressure shifts.

Sound here is a body.

The valley funnels the deep current. It comes over the northern ridge in a slow, enormous pour, cold water heavier than the water below it, and it falls — yes, a waterfall inside the ocean, silent at first and then roaring as it hits the valley floor and splits around the forest towers. The roar is low, organ-deep, felt in the sternum more than heard. Under it: the creak and groan of a thousand stipes bending and recovering, leather on leather. The clack of slate shifting on slate when the surge tugs a wall. The hiss of silt moving. Occasionally, a deep stone boom when a slab up on the ridge finally gives way after a century of loosening and tumbles, end over end, down into moss, sending up a cloud that hangs for hours in the cold stillness.

Light behaves wrong. The little there is from above dies quickly, but the plants make their own. Not bright — no carnival. The moss drifts exhale a faint milk-blue phosphorescence when the current touches them hard, so that the whole meadow breathes light in waves as surges pass. The edges of kelp blades are beaded with pinprick white lights, like dew that never drops, spore-knots ready to release. When they do release, in slow pulses every many hours, the water fills with drifting green sparks that rise a little and then, impossibly, sink, settling back into the forest that made them. Nothing eats them. They land and become.

The rules, as you learn them by staying:

First, nothing looks back. You will never be seen here. That is a kind of mercy and a kind of dread.

Second, weight is different. Things fall slowly, as if the water is thicker than water, more like cold air made liquid and thoughtful. A dislodged stone takes a long time to decide to land. A frond takes forever. Silt hangs.

Third, the high places are the shallow places, but shallow means nothing. The ridge tops are only a little closer to a light you cannot reach. Altitude here is measured down from light, not up from depth, and this whole valley is high — a mountain pasture under a mile of night.

Fourth, what was made by hands persists because the forest allows it. Kelp does not grow through an air-room door. Moss mounds up to a wall and stops a hand's breadth away, as if polite. Then, elsewhere, with no malice, a root splits a capstone in two. There is no plan. There is only the long active working: current and rot and growth and collapse, over and over, town-sized, patient, loud in the dark.

If you stay long enough — and cold slows thought until staying feels like the only option — the valley stops feeling empty. Not inhabited, exactly. But full. Full of green attention without eyes, of stone memory without minds, of water moving through old streets the way wind used to, lifting the hair-weed like laundry lines, belling the kelp forests out and then letting them fall.

A drowned high pasture. A forest with no herd. Houses holding their breath forever under the sea.
