You expect desert and you get it, at first.

Low white land going out flat to heat-shimmer, a pan of fine mineral dust and salt-crackle that lifts with every step and does not settle. No creek line, no well stain, no green promise on the horizon. The air itself feels wrung. Your mouth dries in minutes, your tongue finds your teeth. Even the stones look dehydrated — pumice-light, pitted, bleached bone-yellow and rust.

Then the mound.

It doesn't look built. It looks dropped: a vast, weathered pod, longer than three men, half-buried in the pan, its shell dark amber and iron-hard, polished by years of wind to a low sheen. It could be seed, could be fruit, could be the egg-case of something that left long ago. Old. Settled into itself. The seams where its valves once closed have split and resealed with dust and resin. Near the top, three breathing holes, round as flutes, no wider than your wrist.

You have to crouch. You have to turn sideways to get in through the split seam.

Inside is bigger than outside — not by magic, exactly, but by hollowness. All the volume is kept.

You stand in a room that is also a town.

The light stops you first. The shell is translucent. Sun comes through it the color of honey and hot wax, bright to the point of white at noon, so bright there are no true shadows, only amber and gold and thinner gold. Dust hangs in it and does not fall. The air is warm and held, lowland warm that pools and stays, smelling of heated fiber, turpentine, toasted seed-fluff.

The second thing is the sound, and it does not stop.

It is deafening. Not loud like a crowd. Loud like weather. A low organ-drone from the three holes where the outside wind catches and funnels and turns the whole pod into a resonating chest, a note you feel in your sternum and molars — and over it, a ceaseless high dry rattle, millions upon millions of papery edges shaking. You want to cover your ears. The people who live here don't bother. They sign with their hands.

Because it is inhabited, or was built to be. That is the third thing, and it takes your eyes a while to sort it.

The interior wall is not wall. It is terraces.

All the way around the ovoid hollow, clinging to the curve, are levels: narrow streets of compacted husk-fiber, plazas no larger than a sleeping mat, ladders of thornwood pegged with bone pins, rope bridges sagging across the void, granary baskets, sleeping lofts like swallows' nests daubed with mud and chaff, a tiny shrine of stacked seed-coats. Layer on layer, up to the crown where the holes let in blades of white sky. Primitive work — you can see the hand in it everywhere: lashings, knots, adze marks, loom-woven matting, flint-cut edges — but the planning is urban. Drains that never carried water. Gutters for dust. Alleys that turn to let the drone pass. Someone thought about flow, about neighbors, about a thousand bodies moving, and built for it.

There are no thousand bodies. There are almost none. That is the strangeness of the density here: a city built for a crowd, holding mostly air.

The center is empty. Deliberately empty. A vast hollow column from floor to crown where nothing is built, where you are not supposed to linger. The houses press back from it, the bridges cross it quickly and sway. In that void the sound lives thickest, and the light columns cross, and the slow thermal turns over and over — hot air rising along the sunward wall, cooling along the shade wall, falling, rising again. Seed-down rides it endlessly, never landing.

And everywhere, lush.

That is the impossible thing you keep coming back to. No water. Not a cup, not a seep. The floor dust, when you rub it, is talc and salt and will suck the oil from your skin. And yet the walls are furred. Bearded. Gardens hanging in curtains ten feet deep: silver felt-leaf, grey-green feather-fern that is not fern, amber veils of air-moss, bladder-worts swollen not with water but with scented oil, vines with leaves like beaten tin, soft and clattering. Lush is the wrong word and also the right word — not jungle-wet lush, but abundance, profusion, every peg and beam tufted, every terrace edge spilling. When you crush a leaf it does not bleed green. It bleeds clear resin, hot-peppery, that beads and does not evaporate. When you tear the moss it parts like wool.

They drink sound. The tenders will tell you this later, by signs, matter-of-fact. These are phonotrophs. Where there is no water, vibration will do. Roots — fine white hairs — reach not down but toward the nodes where the drone is strongest, toward bridge-anchors that hum. Leaves are tympana, stiffened to shiver. The endless rattle shakes loose a fine sugar-dust that coats everything, that the plants reabsorb through their felt. The whole garden is tuned. Different patches prefer different pitches; the silver moss thickest near the low hum by the floor, the paper-bells up high where the wind-whistle shears.

It is an ecosystem in motion, just not one with much meat in it.

Fauna is rare to the point of rumor. No flocks, no herds, no buzzing clouds. Once a season a great dust-moth, wingspan wider than a child is tall, finds a breathing hole and forces itself in, dusting the air with scales that glow in the honey-light, laying eggs in the resinous thatch and leaving before dawn. The tenders harvest half the eggs and leave half. Otherwise: a single pale lizard that lives under the floor planks and is considered a citizen. Mites in the dust. Nothing else dares compete with the plants for the sound.

The people — seven when you come, though there are nests for hundreds — are dry people. Lean, oiled skin, hair cropped short to keep from trapping the sugar-dust, throats wrapped in cloth against the roar. They do not speak inside; they have a fluent, rapid hand-talk, and at night, outside on the salt pan when the wind drops and the pod quiets to a murmur, they sit in a circle and talk in hoarse whispers to remember what their voices sound like. Their tools are bone awls, fire-hardened digging sticks, basalt flakes, simple looms strung between beams. They do not dig wells. They chew resin for moisture and press oil from bladder-leaves for cooking and lamp, though lamps are hardly needed. Their craft is maintenance: re-lashing bridges before the vibration saws through, combing the moss so it doesn't choke its own shiver, sweeping the dust-gutters, patching the shell with daub where sun has thinned it.

They have rules, and the rules are the place:

Do not bring water inside. Even spit is wiped and taken out. Water rots the fiber, silences the felt, invites the wrong green.

Do not dwell in the center. Cross quickly, eyes up. The center belongs to the note.

Tend the loud. If a corner goes quiet, clear it, re-tune it, plant to it. Quiet is death here.

In the late afternoon the light deepens to copper and the drone deepens with it as the outside air cools and thickens. The whole town thrums. The hanging gardens lift and shiver all at once like an animal raising its hackles, and the rattle rises until you can feel your skull buzz pleasantly, horribly. Dust rises off the floor in sheets and hangs. A bridge sways and sings on its ropes, a high string over the bass. The tenders move along their narrow streets with practiced indifference, bare feet silent on matting, hands flashing, checking knots by feel, because to hear a fray you would need impossible ears.

Standing there, small in that huge hollow, with green felt brushing your shoulders and a city stacked around you like the inside of a beehive made for giants who never came, you understand it all at once: the pod is an instrument, the town is the fretting, the garden is the player, and the song is what keeps everything, in the absence of water, alive.
