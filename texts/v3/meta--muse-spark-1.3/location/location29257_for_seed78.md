It is high up. That is the first thing your lungs know before your eyes know anything.

The air is thin and cold enough to ache, and black. Not twilight, not overcast — true darkness, the kind that lives at altitude in the long polar winter. Above, the stars are needle-sharp and too close. Below, there is nothing below, or rather, there is cloud, a slow white sea turning far, far down in the abyss where the lowlands should be. You are on a roof lifted out of the world.

And the roof is alive.

The ground under your boots is not ground. It gives, slightly, and springs back. Knee-deep, waist-deep, house-deep in places, it is moss — black-green and iron-dark, frost-rimed, woven so thick over sharp young rock that you never actually see the stone except where it has most recently broken through. This whole country is new. The mountains here haven't learned to be round yet. They came up fast, a basalt upheaval, and they are still all teeth: dikes like sharks' fins, cinder cones split open, lava spines that end in a knife's edge. The moss has thrown itself over everything in a hurry, trying to soften what cannot be softened. Where the rock is too sheer it hangs instead — curtains of lichen and rope-vine hundreds of meters long, swaying in the dark, freezing and thawing, freezing again.

It goes on to every horizon. That is the second thing to understand. This is not a forest. It is a continent of plant. Trunks you took at first for hills are trunks, pale and barkless and as wide as city blocks, rising out of the moss-mat and disappearing straight up into the black where you cannot follow them. Their branches do not branch so much as build: cross-beams, arches, lattices. The leaves are not leaves. They are bladders, sails, cisterns — some small as your hand, some big as a house, hanging thick and trembling with stored water. In the dark they seem to breathe.

It is impossible, you think. Cold like this, dark like this, high like this — nothing should grow. But put your hand in a crack in the fresh rock and you feel it: warmth leaking up. The ground is young and still remembers being molten. In every fissure, steam sighs out and immediately becomes dew, becomes frost, becomes drip. The plants crowd those seams, black roots drinking heat and mineral water. Away from the seams they drink the air itself. The whole vast mat exhales during the brief thaws and inhales the cloud-sea when it boils up over the plateau edge, combing gallons out of fog on millions of hair-fine fronds. It rains here, but not from the sky. It rains from below, and from sideways, and from the plants themselves — condensation gathering, swelling, falling in cold fat drops from leaf to leaf to moss to stone pocket.

Pockets. That is how water lives here. Not lakes, not rivers, though now and then melt finds a sharp channel and runs black and fast for a kilometer before vanishing into moss. Mostly water is held: in bladder-leaves that slosh when the wind hits them, in cup-fungi that freeze into cloudy lenses, in hollows lined with ice that never quite melts. You learn to listen for it, to tip a leaf and drink. The world is mostly dry frost and stone, and then suddenly, unbearably damp — your sleeves soaked from brushing one weeping wall of hanging moss, your breath white, droplets running off your hood and freezing in your eyelashes.

The emptiness is the strangest part. For all that everything is living, there is almost nothing here per volume. The scale is wrong for a human body. Between one great trunk and the next there might be a mile of open night air, cold and hollow as a cathedral. You walk for hours across springy moss-tundra under nothing, your footsteps muffled, and then the ground rises and you realize you are walking up the shoulder of a root. The canopy, if there is a canopy, is so far overhead it is weather. Clouds form up there, caught in the lattice, and detach and drift down through the dark to brush your face. The forest makes its own wind, its own fog, its own brief sleeting showers that patter on a million bladder-leaves with a sound like distant applause. You are tiny, moving through the interstices of a body too large to see.

Then sound reminds you how large and how alive it is.

It is loud here. In darkness, sound is size. The wind never stops moving through that vast lattice — a low organ groan that rises and falls, and under it, higher: the creak and crack of freezing fibers, the tick-tick-tick of ice shedding off leaves far above you, falling and falling before they strike with a brittle smash. Water drips everywhere, amplified by hollows. Every few minutes something booms — rock contracting in the cold, or a bladder-leaf bursting, or a trunk settling — and the boom rolls for a long time between the pillars, echoing back changed.

And the animals. You do not see them at first because there is so much empty air for them to be lost in. Then you hear wings.

They come in multitudes when they come — pale moth-birds with wingspans wider than you are tall, blind white and furred against the cold, riding the updrafts along the warm seams. Flocks of thousands pass overhead like slow snow, and the beat of their wings is a soft thunder that goes on and on. Below them, on the moss, the grazers: low, shaggy, six-legged things with shovel heads that shear moss in swaths and ferment it in their barrel bellies — you smell them before you see them, sweet rot and musk. They move in loose herds, grunting, calves whistling thinly to mothers. And where grazers go, the hunters follow — lean, dark, almost silent except for the click of their claws on fresh basalt and the sudden, shocking scream when they take something. The scream bounces from trunk to trunk and seems to come from everywhere at once.

No one moment is still. While you stand, the moss at your feet is growing — you can hear it, if you crouch, a faint fizzing as frost crystals form on new shoots. A vine beside you, thick as your wrist, is visibly tightening around a rock flake, lifting it. Further off, a whole hanging curtain has torn loose under ice-weight and crashed down, exposing steaming rock that hisses where drips hit it, and already the spores are on it, a green-black dust in your lamplight. Predator and prey, freeze and thaw, uplift and collapse. The plateau is still rising, millimeter by millimeter. You can feel it in your teeth when a new crack opens somewhere deep.

People have been here. Or something that uses hands.

You find the marks scattered and half-swallowed: a cairn of sharp basalt flakes stacked to mark a warm seam, now tipped and mossed over. A low bothy woven from living vine, trained in a dome and left to keep growing — the doorway still open, the inside lined with shed moth-fur for insulation, a cold fire-pit of stone where bone-char glitters. A bridge — if you can call it that — of braided leaf-fiber strung between two root-ridges over a breath of void, sagging with ice, clearly made to be crossed in darkness by touch. Stone scrapers. A needle of sharpened hunter-bone. A child's small mitten, felted from grazer wool, dropped and frozen stiff.

No villages. No fields. Nothing that tries to hold this place. Whoever lives here moves the way the herds move, from seam to seam, from pocket to pocket, drinking from leaves, sheltering inside the plant itself. You get the sense their craft is not to build against the world but to coax it: to tie a vine here, to prune a bladder there, to persuade the living roof to make a room for one night. In the morning they leave it, and the moss takes it back.

Stand still long enough and your eyes adjust to the dark and you see the faint wrongness that keeps everything alive. The moss glows — not brightly, not like a story, but a deep, bacterial ember-green along the warm cracks, pulsing very slowly. The bladder-leaves overhead, when the wind turns them, show undersides freckled with that same light, constellations drifting. It is not enough to see by, but it is enough to know the light is not coming from the sky at all. It is coming from the plant, from the rot, from the living chemistry of a place that decided it would not wait for the sun to return.

Cold bites your nose. Drip falls down your neck. Somewhere a mile off in the black, a herd grunts and moves, and above them ten thousand wings beat, and above them the great lattice groans and sheds its ice, and above that the stars wheel over a continent of frost and leaf that breathes heat out of young stone and holds water in its cupped hands against the night.

You are very small, and very awake, walking on the back of something enormous that does not know you are there, but keeps you warm anyway.
