It is low country. Barely above the water table.

You notice it first by the air getting heavier, warmer on your skin. Not hot, not a wind — just bath-warmth that stays. The ground underfoot softens, then firms again. You are in a shallow hollow, maybe thirty paces across, cupped on three sides by rock no higher than a house wall. The fourth side opens, but not really; a sheet of pale vapor hangs there where the marsh must be, and closes it.

This is the whole place. You can see its edges from anywhere inside it.

The rock is old. Not craggy, not broken. Long water has worked it. The walls are low and bellied out, smoothed into vertical flutes and round pockets where seeps have run for so long the stone has learned the shape of running. Sandstone, iron-stained, the color of damp bread. At the lip the stone rolls over instead of breaking. Nothing is falling. Nothing looks like it will fall.

Everything weeps.

Not in streams. In beads. The walls sweat continuously. Water gathers in the pits and along the undersides of the overhangs, thickens, lets go. A tick, a tick, a tick from a dozen points, never in rhythm, never stopping. Between the drips the stone is dark and glistening. If you put your palm to it, your palm comes away wet and warm and faintly gritty, and within a minute the dark print you left has beaded over again.

The air itself is wet. It is packed — there is no dry in it. Each breath is warm and mineral. Condensation sits on every surface that can hold it. The light is dim, not because it is night but because there is so much between you and the sky: low cloud, the rock lip, and the vapor itself. A grey-amber dusk that does not change. Shadows have no edges here. You can look straight at the sun's place and see only a brighter smear.

The floor is what holds you.

There is no soil showing. No mud, even. The entire hollow is paved, edge to edge, with stones — water-worn cobbles, flat chert and quartzite pebbles, from fist-size down to thumbnail — fitted so tight you cannot fit a fingertip between them. They were sorted by water a long time ago and have settled. They do not wobble when you step. A film of warm water, a millimeter deep, moves over them without seeming to move, connecting shallow pans no deeper than a saucer. Crouch and you see your distorted face in every pan, and the sky-smear, and the beads overhead about to drop.

There is no room for anything else. That is the rule of the floor. Stone to stone to stone, water to water to water.

That is why there is nothing green. In any other warm wet place there would be rushes, moss, algae in mats. Here there is none. No grass at the seams, no moss on the walls, not even lichen. The stones are too close, too constantly filmed, too warm and faintly mineral — iron and a whisper of sulfur — for seedlings to catch. If you look close there is a thin slick on the submerged stones, rust-orange and dull olive, slippery under your thumb. Not plant. Something simpler, that lives in the film and asks for nothing else. It does not spread. It does not recede. It just stains.

It is very quiet. After the first minute you stop hearing the marsh beyond the vapor. Inside, only the ticks of water letting go, the faint skin-sound of a bead running down stone, your own breathing made loud by the closeness. If you stand still long enough you hear the pans: an almost inaudible sigh as inflow and evaporation balance.

Because nothing changes here. That takes a while to understand. Water is everywhere but the level never rises. The pans never overflow, never dry. The seeps never quicken, never slow. The vapor at the mouth neither comes in nor goes out. It is post-rain without the rain, marsh-edge without the flood. Warm water comes up from below — you can feel it welling cool-warm around your ankles if you stand in a pan — and warm water leaves into the air. In balance. The stones have been exactly this wet for a very long time.

People have been here. Not many. Not now.

Near the back wall someone stacked a low platform of flat slabs, two courses high, without mortar, to make a dry seat above the film. The slabs have darkened and beaded like everything else. Beside it lies a trough, hollowed from a single log by hand, adze marks still visible under the water skin. It is full to the brim and does not spill. On the platform: three river stones, one split, flakes of chert around them where someone knapped an edge, and a small bowl pecked from sandstone, half full of clear warm water. A broken withy hoop leans against the wall. A cold fire-spot, a ring of blackened pebbles with ash washed to a clean grey paste.

Hand work. Nothing sawn, nothing nailed. Things made to dip and carry and sit.

There is no one to use them. There is almost nothing living to see. Once, while you watch a pan, a small brown water beetle oars across it, legs making V's that heal instantly. Later, from under the platform, a single frog the color of wet stone blinks and does not move again. You do not hear birds. You do not see tracks except your own, filling slowly behind you.

Stay. The warmth gets into your clothes until they cling. Droplets gather on your forearms, on your eyelashes. Drips tick on the trough, on the platform, on the back of your neck. The vapor at the mouth holds its shape. The pans hold their faces. The walls sweat and do not dry and do not erode.

It does not ask you to leave and does not invite you to stay. It is complete, packed full of stone and water and warm air, with just enough room left for a few hand-made things set carefully on top.
