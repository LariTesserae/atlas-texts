You come up and there is no more up.

The climb ends not in a peak but in a giving-out. The road switchbacks for a morning through younger scree and then simply levels, as if the mountain got tired of being a mountain. This is old country, older than sharpness. The highland spreads in front of you, broad and whale-backed and nearly flat — a tableland worn down over impossible time until the summits slumped into domes and the valleys shallowed into pans.

It is still immensely high. Your lungs know it before your eyes do. The air is thin and cool and always a little wet in the mouth, and every breath leaves a brief ghost. Your ears pop for no reason. The sky, which should be huge and blinding up here, is low and held down. A deck of cloud sits on the plateau like a lid, pearl-grey and unmoving, and light filters through it as if through dirty linen. No shadows. Only a dim, even, late-afternoon brightness that never quite becomes day or night.

The rock under everything is ancient. Not craggy, not dramatic — tired. Gneiss and quartzite so old the crystals have loosened and gone sugary at the edges. You can crumble it with a fingernail if you try. It is grey-white and pink-grey and black where it is wet, which is often. There are no cliffs left, only long tors like the knuckles of buried animals, rounded and flaking in onion-skins. Between them, the ground hollows into shallow basins that hold everything that matters.

Because there is no great river here, no lake worth naming on a map. The water of this place is intermittent and shy. Most days it doesn't rain so much as condense. Fog rolls in off the drop on all sides in the late evening, thick as wool, and by morning every surface is beaded. It drips from wires, from eaves, from leaves. It seeps out of the rock itself in dark lines that glisten and then vanish by noon. In the pans there are tarns — black, still, a few meters across — pockets of water in a larger dryness, fringed with peat. After a rain squall they connect for an hour via bright rills that chatter over the smooth stone, and then they disconnect again. The people here don't talk about rainfall. They talk about catch.

And catch is easy, because everything is lush. Not tall — nothing can afford to be tall in this cold wind — but dense, low, and insistent. A continuous mat of moss and cushion-plant and liverwort covers every horizontal surface that isn't swept daily. It is saturated green, yellow-green, rust-green, inches thick, springy under boot. In the damp gullies, ferns unroll to waist height, and pale lichens crust the stones in maps of sage and sulphur. The vegetation doesn't politely stay outside town. It climbs walls, swallows abandoned sheds, softens rooflines until from a distance you can't tell where moor ends and settlement begins. When you press your hand into it, cold water wells up around your knuckles. It smells of peat and iron and crushed green.

The town sits in three of those shallow pans, spilling over the low saddles between them, a dense urban knot in all that openness. From the rim you can take in its whole extent at once — a valley-sized organism of stone and brick and iron — and still not see to its edges for fog. Houses stand shoulder to shoulder, two and three stories, built of dark local stone and soot-stained brick, with slate roofs made heavy to resist wind and now heavy again with moss. Between and above and behind them, the industrial layer: mills with sawtooth roofs, low chimneys that never seem to plume straight up, cableways on iron pylons striding from pan to pan, water-tanks, gasometers, terraces cut and revetted where the slope demanded it. Pipes run like vines along alley walls, lagged in sacking that drips constantly. Wires sag with weight of dew.

It is unmistakably a working place. You see the scored loading yards, the stacks of peat bricks drying under tarps, the dye-works with its vats stained impossible colors, the machine shops where something clanks in rhythm. The foundries here don't smelt new ore — the rock is far too old and picked-over for that — they rework, repair, re-roll, spin wool, press lichen for dye, mill grain brought up from below, build the fine condensation-nets that strung between poles harvest fog on cold nights and tinkle faintly as they drain into gutters. Large-scale, mechanized, electrified: amber bulbs burn in cages over every doorway even at midday, because the light is so poor, and at dusk the whole pan glows in a reticulation of warm dots. The generating station hums down by the lowest tarn, where the rills have been trained into leats.

For all that, it is mostly quiet. Sound does not carry here the way it should at altitude. The moss eats it. The fog eats it. The rounded land offers nothing to echo off. What you hear, if you stand still, is a low composite: the deep thrum of turbines more felt in the soles than heard, the occasional sigh of steam vented sideways and torn away by wind, boot-squelch on saturated paths, endless dripping from eaves into barrels — plink, plink, plink in different keys down the street. A shift whistle, but muted, as if blown through cloth. Far off, a cable bucket groans along its wire and then is gone. People speak quietly, necessarily. To shout would feel obscene.

Animals are rare. You notice their absence before you notice them. No flocks, no herds, no chorus. Once in a while a pair of high crows tilts across the grey, calling once, thinly. There are stone-mice in the walls, quick grey flickers. In the tarns, something small and dark noses — newts, perhaps, or freshwater shrimp left over from when this plateau was seabed. Midges hang in columns over the wettest moss on windless moments, and then the one or two swallows that live under the mill eaves come out and work them silently. That is the predator-prey cycle here, so small and so fast you could miss it blinking. The real activity is botanical and meteorological: moss visibly swelling after fog, ferns unfurling over a week, peat forming, water moving in its brief bright threads, weather fronts stacking and unstacking overhead in an hour — sun for eleven minutes, hail, fog again, a blade of cold blue light that makes every wet surface flash.

There are rules to living up this high on such old ground, and the town obeys them without posting them.

Build low and clumped. Nothing stands alone against the wind. Share walls.

Never trust a level foundation. The ancient rock, for all its hardness, flows on long time. Old walls have bellied, old lintels have sagged like wax. Doors are re-hung every generation. You learn to step over the threshold-rise where the house has settled into the moss.

Save water when it gives itself. Every roof drains to a butt. Every yard has a cistern lid like a well-eye. Waste is a rumor from the lowlands.

Let green stay. A roof stripped of moss will drum in hail and leak in thaw. A wall scraped of lichen will weep and crack. The town has learned to be farmed as much as built.

And there is the strangeness, which no one remarks on because to remark would be to admit it. Iron here rusts green, not orange, in the lichen air. Certain slabs of the oldest stone ring underfoot — a clear, bell-like tone that sustains a second too long — and the children know which ones and hop from one to another to make a tune. Condensation sometimes beads on the *undersides* of the nets first, gathering upward against gravity before it grows heavy and falls. Compasses are useless; the rock holds too many old magnetisms, and needles wander and settle wherever they like. At night, when the cloud thins just enough to show stars smeared through ice crystals, the electric lights seem to dim of their own accord, as if the plateau were breathing out and taking voltage with it. People leave an extra cup of tarn-water on the sill during fog. Not offering, they will tell you if asked. Just catch.

Stay a day and your body adjusts. Your steps get shorter, your hearing sharper for small sounds. You learn to read dampness by smell, to tell by the color of the light filtering through cloud whether the fog will lift by two or settle for the week. You find yourself touching the moss as you pass, wringing a sleeve, stopping to watch a rill that wasn't there an hour ago braid itself across smooth stone and then sink again into green.

From the town's highest alley — a narrow stair between overhanging, dripping houses where ferns grow from mortar and cable shadows stripe the wet cobbles — you can look back out across the whole open tableland. Moor, pans, tarns winking dull pewter, tors surfacing like backs, cloud-wall on every horizon where the world drops away thousands of feet to places with trees and noise and bright sun. Up here the land has finished becoming, and life, dense and low and clever, has tucked itself into its final wrinkles: lush in the cold, busy in the quiet, lit amber against the dim, drinking fog because there is nothing else to drink, and making, mending, humming on.
