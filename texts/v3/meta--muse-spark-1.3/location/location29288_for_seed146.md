You go down a long time before you understand that down has ended.

Not a tunnel, not a cave in any sense you have for caves. The shaft you came down — a fresh rip, edges still sharp enough to cut rope — simply stops having walls and becomes a ceiling. A ceiling so high and so black your lamp doesn't find it. You swing the beam up and it dissolves. You swing it out and it dissolves faster. Warm darkness takes it.

This is a place below the world, and it is larger than the world above it.

The air is warm. Not hot like a furnace, but blood-warm, skin-warm, the exact temperature of something living held close. 38 degrees and utterly dry. It doesn't move. There is no draft here, no cold sink, no warm rise. The air was warmed once, a long time — or a short time — ago by the rock itself, and then it stopped. It stays.

Under your boots is new rock. You can tell newness by feel even in the dark. Nothing is rounded. Nothing is smoothed. This whole vault was cracked open recently, geologically yesterday, and the floor is a fresh break: plates of black basalt and volcanic glass tilted up like broken ice, salt rind white-gray over everything, sharp mineral teeth, dust that has never been blown because there has never been wind to blow it.

It crunches once under your weight and then the sound is swallowed. The dark here is heavy and textured, not empty.

Because the dark is full.

At first you think the floor is moving with salt crystals, with scree. Then your dim red lamp — you learned fast not to use white, white hurts them and hurts you with the reflection — catches an edge and another edge and you realize you are not looking at rock at all.

They are packed shoulder to shoulder, layer on layer, as far as your light reaches and, you suspect, much farther. A living pavement. A living wall. A living ceiling somewhere above, too high to see, but you can feel the faint, collective warmth raining down without moving air.

The fauna of the Still Deep.

They are small — to your knee if they stood, which they don't. They crouch, they cling, they lean into each other. Pale, hairless, eyeless. Skin translucent and damp-looking but dry as paper to the touch, stretched over long forearms and delicate many-jointed hands. Wide nostrils flared for a world without light. Wide ears that never twitch. Each one holds stillness so completely you think it is dead until you see the flank lift, once, slowly, every few minutes. Inhale. Hold. Exhale, warm into warm.

There are millions of them. The density is absurd. You cannot put your foot down without stepping near a hand, a head, a back. They do not move away. They do not startle. They tolerate nearness the way stones tolerate nearness. Juveniles sleep tucked into the armpits of adults. Adults sleep with their foreheads pressed to the back of the one in front. In places they have climbed on each other three, four deep, forming soft mounds and ridges that from a distance look like geology.

And there is no food here, as you understand food. No plants. Never were. No green, no moss, no mold, nothing photosynthetic, nothing that needs sun. No water — no drip, no pool, no seep. The salt under your nails stays dry. Your mouth dries in minutes.

What do they eat? They eat dust. They eat salt. They eat each other's shed skin and the fine mineral powder that sifts eternally — but not moving, just hanging — in the air. Or they eat almost nothing at all. Their metabolism is a held breath. In this equilibrium, a single lick of salt, a flake of chitin from a neighbor, can last a season that never comes. When one dies — rarely — it does not rot. Nothing rots here. The body simply dries a little drier and stays in the pile, and the others shift their weight around it by millimeters over years, and its hands still hold what they were holding.

Because they hold things. This is the strangest part.

Almost every third or fourth one has something in its hands: a flake of black glass, knapped to an edge by no metal, only stone on stone. A shard of long bone — from what giant ancestor, you cannot guess — ground to an awl. A bead of salt, rolled round by endless dry handling. They do not use them while you watch. They just hold them, the way you might hold a prayer.

And scattered among the bodies, at intervals too regular to be accident and too sparse to be a town, are their works.

Little cairns. Knee-high towers of balanced basalt shards, each stone placed edge-to-edge so sharpness holds sharpness. Circles of arranged finger-bones. A low wall, no longer than your arm, built and rebuilt and never extended. A hollow in the dust where hundreds of hands have smoothed the same spot over and over, not to make anything, but to make smoothness.

No houses. No fires — there is nothing to burn. Just marks. Faint scratches on glass where a tool was tested. A handprint in fine salt dust that has stayed perfect because no wind will ever erase it. Once, you find a twisted cord made of sinew, knotted and re-knotted by countless fingers, left draped over a stone like an offering.

Primitive, yes. Hands and stones. But persistent.

The sound is what tells you the rule of this place. It is mostly quiet, so quiet your own blood becomes loud. But if you hold your breath and stop shifting your weight, you hear it underneath: a vast, low, almost-subsonic hush. Not snoring. Not breathing together — they don't synchronize. It's the sound of millions of almost-still bodies radiating a little heat into air that cannot carry it away. Occasionally, a click. A single claw tapping glass, once. A sigh through nostrils. A stone settling a micron deeper with a tick you feel in your teeth. The dense mass drinks all echo. Shout, and it falls flat three meters out.

Nothing changes here. That is not poetry, that is physics.

The temperature is the same everywhere you walk — on the floor, two meters up where you climb a tilted plate, an hour in any direction. Warm. The air pressure never shifts. There are no currents, no weather fronts, although the vault is large enough to have weather. Your meteorologist brain keeps waiting for a breeze to form over that much distance, for heat to rise and fall. It doesn't. The system found equilibrium and stayed.

The young rock does not weather because there is no water or wind to weather it. The dead do not decay because there is no moisture for decay. The living barely live because they have learned how not to need to. Even your footprints — that one crunch — stay sharp-edged behind you. The dust does not resettle. Time does not pool or flow here; it just sits, warm and dark, with everyone in it.

If you stay long enough, you will feel the pull to sit down among them. They will make room, not by moving but by being soft. Shoulder to shoulder. Your warmth will join their warmth and the thermometers will not register a difference. Your lamp will dim and die and you will not replace it, because eyes are useless here and hands are better.

You will find a sharp flake that fits your palm. You will hold it. You will listen to the great quiet that is not empty but crowded, that is not cold but warm, that is not dead but paused.

And you will not get up.
