On the roof of the world, after the uplift, before the wearing-down.

This is a continent lifted too fast and too recently to have learned softness. Black basalt, grey andesite, pale rhyolite ash — all of it broken yesterday, geologically speaking. The ridges have not had time to slump. They stand in knives and fins and shattered staircases, frost-split and sharp enough to cut rope. Scree still has corners. Cliffs still show the fresh tear where they pulled apart. Valleys are not valleys yet but cracks between blocks, deep, straight-sided, full of water.

And it is high. High enough that the air is thin and clean and hurts a little to breathe, high enough that noon light comes in white and level and unfiltered — not bright, not dim, just ordinary daylight stretched thin over an extraordinary distance. You can see weather being born fifty miles away and dying before it reaches you. The plateau makes its own sky. Clouds form off its wet breath and never quite leave; they snag on the fins, tear, re-form in the same places every day.

Because it is wet. Not ocean-wet, not river-wet. Marsh-wet. Seep-wet. Every rock weeps. Water stands just under the skin of everything — press your boot into moss and water rises around the tread. Press your hand to a cliff and your palm comes away dripping. The air itself condenses. Cold metal sweats instantly. Cold stone sweats perpetually. In the mornings, and it is always a kind of morning here, everything drips, everything runs in a thousand small white threads down the sharp rock, into black tarns, into reed-choked mires that fill the flat bottoms between the fins. Waterfalls that should have carved gorges haven't had time yet; they just pour, loud, white, straight off a hard lip into a pool that never overflows and never empties.

Cold holds it all. Not deep polar cold. Working cold. The cold of standing water in late autumn, of breath fogging, of fingers going numb on railings. Frost feathers on the lee side of every pipe every night and melts every noon into the same drip, the same trickle. Nothing freezes solid. Nothing dries out. It is a post-rain landscape that never finishes being post-rain.

And despite the cold, despite the altitude, despite the rawness of the stone — it is green. Impossibly, continuously green.

Not forest. Too high, too cold, too young for trees. But a lushness at ankle height that goes on for thousands of miles. Cushion moss, sphagnum, liverwort in great black-green pelts, starry saxifrage, dwarf willow no taller than your thumb, lichens the color of rust and bone and sulfur. Where water stands, cotton-grass and sedge in dense shivering meadows. The green climbs up the weeping cliffs, colonizes the fresh scree before the scree has settled, swallows boulders whole and softens them without rounding them. If you lie down, it steams faintly around you with your heat, smelling of peat and iron and clean rot.

Rot without eaters. That is the first strangeness you notice, then forget, then remember with a chill.

There are no animals here. No birds to cross that enormous sky. No insects to rise off the marsh in clouds. No sheep, no goats, no rats in the towns, no gulls on the wires. No hum but the machines. No footprint but yours, filling slowly with water. The plants grow and die and layer into peat with no mouth to interrupt them. Spores drift in the billions and settle undisturbed. The green is deafening in its silence. You expect a croak, a buzz, a splash that is not water — and it never comes.

Because there are towns.

From a distance you would think them rocks, until you see the straightness. Scattered across the vastness at intervals so great the emptiness swallows all sense of pattern — a day's walk, then three days, then a week of nothing but moss and knives and water — a cluster of hard geometry rises: a mill-town, a factory ward, a settlement of stone barracks and steel sheds and brick stacks.

They are not ruins. They are inhabited, after a fashion, and they work. They were built to be cities in miniature: terraced housing in rows, a market square now full of settling tanks, chapels converted to transformer stations, streets that run straight to the edge of the plateau block and stop at air. Canals, leats, penstocks. Rail embankments on slender viaducts that run for a hundred miles across mire to the next lit cluster and carry low, endless trains of ore and peat and cut moss-brick.

Everything is industrial and everything is running. Boilers. Looms, or what were looms. Pulp presses. Electrolysis sheds humming behind frosted windows. Smokestacks breathe thick white steam that condenses instantly into the low cloud and falls again as drizzle on the moss a mile downwind. Turbines in the crack-valleys turn with the waterfalls. Cables on pylons march to the horizon, singing in the wind.

There are people — a few, maintenance crews, stokers, switch-watchers — but so few for so much mass and noise that the place feels empty even in its streets. Vast space between presences. You can stand in a cobbled square between five-story mills with every window lit warm yellow and hear only machines and water, and see no one. The next town's lights are a faint smear on the cloud base, too far to walk to before dark, if dark ever fully came. The hollow between is absolute: miles of dripping green under white sky, with only the far, constant throb in your sternum to remind you the world is occupied.

And the sound — you cannot understate it. This is a loud place.

Close up, it is the roar of water off hard rock, the crash of the mill-race, the shriek of steam valves, the low iron drone of dynamos that you feel in your teeth. Far out, it merges into a single chord: wind over fins + water over lips + current in wires + fire in boilers. It never stops. It never peaks. It never fades when the wind drops, because the wind never truly drops here; the plateau breathes in a fixed rhythm, west to east, cold off the heights, damp off the mires, always the same velocity through the same notches.

That is the law of this place, the deeper strangeness that holds all the contradictions together: nothing changes.

The cliffs weep but do not crumble. The waterfalls pour but do not cut back. The peat grows a millimeter a year on top and compresses a millimeter a year below. The stacks smoke the same smoke. The trains leave full and return empty on a schedule no one remembers writing, and the goods — ingots, bales, glass insulators, drums of black oil from rendered moss — pile in warehouses, are inventoried, are fed back into the furnaces. Production without progress. Industry without wear.

The mosses grow over running pipes and hot culverts and vibrating foundations without being killed and without stopping the machines; green felt creeps up the brick, over the sill, into the turbine hall where workers cut it back each Tuesday with flat spades, and by next Tuesday it is back to exactly the same line, as if trimmed by memory. Rust forms and stops. Frost forms and melts. Clouds form over the same three ridges every afternoon, rain on the same mire for an hour, dissolve.

Even the light does not really travel. Dawn and noon and dusk are slight variations on the same white-grey brightness, the sun a pale disc behind high ice that never clears and never thickens. Shadows shorten and lengthen a little, dutifully, and return.

If you stay long enough you begin to feel it in your own body — your breath fogging and clearing, fogging and clearing, your boots wet and never wetter, never dry. You do not get hungrier or less hungry. The cold keeps you alert without ever pushing you into shivering. You could walk for weeks across that lush, empty, ringing roof, from one humming island to the next, past weeping knives of new stone, through marshes that suck and release, under wires that sing one unchanging note, and always find the same water at the same level, the same green at the same height, the same lit windows burning for no arrival.

It is a world fresh-made and already in equilibrium. Young rock. Old patience. A city-system built for a multitude, tended by a handful, heard by no animals at all — just the planet keeping its engines idling, loud and wet and green in the cold thin air, waiting for erosion, or history, or hunger to begin.
