High up, where the air goes thin and bright enough to hurt, there is a valley that isn't a valley.

From the last switchback you can see it all at once — a town-size sprawl of level upon level, shelf upon shelf, filling what must once have been a cirque or a caldera between cold peaks. Nothing natural is left to see. The mountain is still there, you feel it under you in the way the whole mass leans and aches, but it has been completely encased. Plate over plate, deck over deck, riveted steel and poured concrete and whitewashed brick and glass, all the way down and all the way across. No rock. No soil. Even the slopes above are terraced in retaining walls so fresh the concrete is still pale, the edges still sharp enough to cut. This place is young. It has not been weathered smooth. Weld beads stand proud. Rebar ends bristle. Stair nosings haven't been worn round by feet yet.

The light is the first thing. At this elevation the sun doesn't filter, it hammers. The sky is a hard washed blue, the sun a white coin, and everything built throws it back at you — galvanized roofs, condensate tanks, miles of conduit. You squint even with your breath fogging. Because it is cool here, always cool. Midsummer noon feels like early spring elsewhere. The brightness has no warmth in it. Your skin prickles with cold while your eyes water from glare.

And it is crowded — not with people, but with *things*. Dense, near-touching, layered. Nothing stands alone. A smokestack rises and immediately it is braced by catwalks, strangled by pipes, bridged to the next stack by a mesh of cable trays and laundry lines and pigeon roosts. Buildings lean toward each other until their eaves almost kiss, leaving only a slit of sky. Below the main decks there are under-decks, and below those, service voids where you can hear — or rather feel — crawlspaces full of more pipes. Up close every surface is furred with add-ons: junction boxes, vents, insulators, eye-bolts, chains, nameplates, valves with their wheels painted red. The air itself is cluttered. Wires cross in every direction, sagging slightly with frost-melt. Conveyor gantries inch overhead. Even empty space has been built through.

This is an industrial place. You can tell by the scale of the breathing. Enormous slow engines turn somewhere deep inside, so large and so well-balanced and so patient that they make almost no sound — a low pressure in the sternum rather than a noise. Factories run here, whole terraces dedicated to them: long halls with sawtooth roofs of dirty glass, rolling mills, bottling lines, dynamos behind caged windows spitting faint blue corona. Electrified, all of it. The smell is ozone and hot oil and wet stone. But the motion is glacial. A gantry crane a hundred meters long creeps along its rails a few centimeters a minute. A belt carries ore or grain or crushed glass at walking pace, then slower. Pistons rise and fall like sleeping animals. Nothing hurries. Maintenance crews leave tools and go home for the week while a seal cures. Change happens here across seasons, not hours.

It should be loud. It isn't. That is the strangest mercy of the place. Sound falls short. Whether it's the thin air or the baffling maze of plates and wool insulation, or some other rule, noise dies within twenty paces. A hammer clank becomes a tap. A train of ore carts passes silently except for the tick of the rails cooling. Even the fauna — and there is a staggering amount of fauna — is muted. And so you notice them visually first: the sky boiling.

Gulls and alpine choughs and starlings in flocks so thick they make moving shade. Pigeons erupting from vent louvers in a gray applause you feel more than hear. Kestrels hanging motionless in the updraft off the heated halls. On the decks: feral cats sunning belly-up on transformer housings in the bright cold, marmots fat as sacks living in the expansion joints, rats running the pipe highways at dusk, mountain goats somehow up here, clattering along the outer retaining walls, eating paper. Moths the size of your palm crowd every lamp even at noon, because the lamps never turn off. Swifts nest in the weep holes. Spiders have strung the undersides of every walkway. Life has found the cracks and taken them entirely, though there is almost nothing green to support it. Vegetation is sparse to the point of rumor — a fringe of moss black-green in the drip lines, lichen like pale coins on north-facing concrete, a few tenacious weeds: bittercress and willowherb and a wiry grass, rooted in windblown dust where two plates meet, shivering in the cold. No trees. No fields. The animals do not eat plants. They eat scraps, and each other, and oil, and heat, and the endless condensation.

Because it is damp here, in a dry high way. No lake, no river you can put a boat on. Instead water appears and disappears. Morning brings a heavy dew that beads on every rail and wire until the whole dense mass glitters, then drips, then is gone by ten. Clouds boil up from below the rim and spill over, leaving everything slick for an hour. Afternoon sun draws steam off the factory roofs in thin veils. There are pockets: a sump courtyard where black water stands a foot deep and reflects the sky perfectly, full of drowned midges and drinking pigeons; a culvert that runs for three days after a storm and then is dry concrete again; gutters that sing briefly with melt; cisterns and drip barrels everywhere, half-full, rimed with ice in shadow even in summer. You learn to listen for drips more than flows. The whole town ticks with cooling metal and falling droplets. Workers leave cups out on ledges to catch it.

And then — the way the place does not hold together.

You will walk a straight service alley counting doors, turn, and find yourself one level higher than you started without having climbed. You will see the same blue water tower in two districts at once, and both are real enough to cast shadows. Shadows, in fact, often fall the wrong way here, leaning toward the sun in late afternoon, or pooling too dark under a catwalk that isn't that solid. Distances unlatch. The far side of the valley, which looked a fifteen-minute traverse, takes all morning because the middle stairs repeat — same oil stain, same dead pigeon, same chalk mark, three times — if you are not paying attention. If you *are* paying attention, you can sometimes step across. People here leave small offerings at the misaligned corners: a bolt, a feather, a twist of wire, to mark where you should not trust your eyes.

Rain, when it comes, sometimes falls up in the central court between the three tallest stacks, rising slowly from the warm grates to vanish into brightness. Lights buzz on in empty halls before you enter. A door you welded shut yesterday is open today, with fresh sharp edges, as if the building grew a new door overnight. Which, the older hands will tell you without surprise, it does. The place is still emerging. It hasn't settled on its shape. New rooms bud in the night, with uncured concrete and unpainted steel, smelling of mineral and cold. You learn not to store anything important in a wall less than a year old, because it might not be there in the morning, or might be twice as large.

The rules, as you absorb them, are simple:

Stay in the sun if you want to stay warm; stay in shadow if you want water.

Don't shout. Not because it's rude, but because loud sound comes back wrong — flattened and late, from a different direction.

Don't follow a cat onto a new deck. It knows where that deck will be tomorrow; you don't.

Mark your way with chalk, and don't trust chalk more than a day old.

Move slowly. Everything else does. The engines, the clouds welling up from below, the lichen eating the concrete a micron at a time, the goats picking their impossible traverses, the starling murmurations writing and erasing vast calligraphy over the roofs without a sound you can hear — all of it patient, all of it bright and cold and crowded and humming just below hearing.

At dusk the light doesn't fade so much as sharpen. The peaks around go pink, then blue. The valley-town turns on its million small amber work-lights and they reflect in every bead of condensation until the whole dense, built-through mass sparkles like something wet and alive. The air smells of iron and frost. Somewhere deep below your feet a huge piston completes a stroke it started at noon. A kettle of swifts drops into a vent to roost. Water ticks from wire to wire to deck. And far across, impossibly near, the duplicate water tower blinks on.
