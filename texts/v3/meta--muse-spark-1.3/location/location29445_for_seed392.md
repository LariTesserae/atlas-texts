It is noon here, and it has been noon for a very long time.

You come down into it, because there is no up to come from — the land is low, low as a held breath. A broad basin, a valley the size of a small country town and its fields, ringed on the far side by a pale haze that never burns off. From the rim causeway you can see all the way across it, and that is the first wrong thing: you should not be able to see that far in hot wet air, and you should not be able to see that everything, all the way to the haze, is made.

There is no ground here. Not soil, not bare rock, not sand. Someone fitted it all.

The floor of the basin is laid. Split palm and river cobble and sun-baked mudbrick and hand-plastered clay in overlapping patches, generation on generation, until the work met itself in the middle. Causeways on low arches over black water. Plazas of whitewashed lime that hurt to look at. Stepped mounds — you would call them hills if they weren't so clearly stacked by hands — rising twenty, thirty feet, their sides crisp as fresh carpentry. Cisterns like ponds, but rimmed, squared, each corner turned with a fitted stone. Even the mud is made mud: pounded clay and leaf compost carried up in baskets and tamped into troughs and gutters for planting.

The work is everywhere and it is new. That is the second wrong thing.

In this heat, in this wet, things should rot, slump, green over, wash out. Thatch should sag. Twine should fray away. Chisel marks should soften. They don't. Where a palm trunk was adzed to a point, the facets still shine pale and sharp. Where fiber was lashed around a joist, the cut ends stick out stiff and fresh, as if the knife just left. You can run your hand along a mudbrick wall and feel every straw fingerprint in the plaster, unmelted by the rains that must come every afternoon — because everything is damp. Water beads on everything. It weeps out of the vertical faces. It stands in the joints. It hangs in the air itself, condensing on your forearms though no cloud has passed.

It is hot. Not desert hot. Bath hot. The air is thick enough to drink, smelling of hot lime and crushed green and sun-warmed fiber. Sweat doesn't dry. It joins the general weeping. The bright is total. A white sun directly overhead, small and too close, bleaching color out of the whitewash until the plazas ring with light. There are shadows, but they are short, pooled directly under things like spilled oil, and they do not move. If you sit an hour — if an hour means anything when the light does not change — your shadow will not have crept. The glare stays pinned.

And yet it is lush. Impossibly lush for a place with no earth.

Green has taken the made world as its trellis. In every trough, in every gutter joint, in every cistern crack where leaf mold has gathered, it erupts. Broad taro leaves big as doors, leaning out over canals. Vines with heart-shaped leaves stitching up the sides of the stepped mounds, finding every peg-hole. Ferns in dense cuffs around the base of every post. Fig saplings rooted in mortar, splitting it with slow green wedges. Bananas and papaya in tended clumps where someone — long ago, yesterday — piled compost between bricks. The green is not a carpet. It is islands, sudden and extravagant. One courtyard will be a jungle, dripping, humming with life, leaves layered five deep, fruit rotting sweetly on fitted stone. The next courtyard, fifty paces down the same causeway, will be bare whitewash and empty, hot and clean and silent, nothing but heat shimmer.

Because the presences here are sparse. That is the rule of spacing. Much emptiness between things. Long empty causeways on their low arches with nothing on them but a basking lizard and your own footfalls. Vast empty plazas where your steps slap too loud and then stop too fast. Empty towers of woven palm, four stories tall, open to the wind, with no one in them, their ladders still pegged and fresh. Empty basins waiting for water that is already everywhere else. You walk a long time between gardens.

But where there is green, there is animal. Crowded, abundant, shoulder to shoulder.

Tree frogs the size of thumbnails plaster every damp wall in the garden courts, throat pouches pulsing, gold-eyed and emerald-backed, so many you cannot put a hand down without covering one — but they do not chorus. Once in a while one gives a single *pink* and then stops, as if the sound was cut with a knife. Clouds of dragonflies — sapphire, ember-orange — hang over the cisterns, wings blurring, never leaving the three feet of air they were born to. Herons, white and slate-gray, stand motionless in the shallows of the canals, necks folded, watching. Mudskippers and small black crabs work the weeping edges. Finches and sunbirds flick between banana leaves. In the hotter shadows, snakes, fat and slow, looped over warm bricks.

They are all here, and they are all quiet. Sound does not travel here the way it should. A heron taking off — huge wings, you expect a rush — makes only a soft towel-snap that dies three feet out. Your own footsteps on cobble go dull after two steps. The jungle courts should roar with insects in this heat. Instead there is a thin, high fizz at the edge of hearing, and under it, the occasional plink of condensed water falling from a leaf to a fitted stone. Plink. Long silence. Plink. The thatch and water and hot air eat everything else.

Water is near as skin. Lift any loose brick — and there are few loose bricks — and beneath it is damp, and beneath that, more brick, and water standing between. The canals do not flow. They are full to the brim with tea-black water that reflects the white sky perfectly and does not move, though if you watch long enough you see a slow upwelling in the middle, as if the basin is breathing up from below. The cisterns are the same level. The puddles on the plazas are the same level. It rains — you can smell it coming, a sudden iron coolness — and for a few minutes fat drops hammer the banana leaves and drum the thatch, and then it stops, and the level has not changed. Nothing drains. Nothing floods. The weeping rocks — the upright carved stones set at intersections to mark ways — drip steadily from their carved eyes and grooves, feeding small mossy basins at their feet, and the basins never overflow.

That is the equilibrium. Growth and rot in place. Evaporation and condensation in place. You see a papaya swollen and perfect, and beside it one collapsed to black slime feeding a cloud of flies, and you understand no argument is being won. The vine does not advance down the causeway year by year. The fig does not topple the wall. The heron does not empty the canal of fish. Things appear, persist, dissolve, in the same bright hour.

If you stay, you notice the other breakdowns, small at first.

Distances do not hold. That garden tower you walked toward for what felt like a long time is suddenly behind you, and you did not turn. Walk the straight causeway from the white plaza to the stepped mound and count your steps — walk it back and count again, and you will get a different number, though your stride has not changed.

Reflections lag. Lean over a cistern and your face arrives a half-second late in the black water, smiling when you are not.

And the made-ness goes down farther than it should. Children here — there are a few, sparse as everyone else, dark-eyed, barefoot, moving quietly with wooden buckets and stone scrapers — will tell you, without mystery, that there is no bottom. Their grandmothers' grandmothers laid brick, and below that was brick laid by older hands, and below that was wattle, and below that was something else fitted. If you dig, you find work. The world is work, all the way down.

They still work, with early tools. A polished stone adze. A wooden mallet. A fire-hardened digging stick. Coiled baskets. Bone needles for thatch. You will see an old woman on her knees re-plastering a wall that does not need it, smoothing the same fingerprint whorls that were already there, because that is what you do at noon. A boy replacing a lashing that is not worn, cutting the fiber with a flaked chert blade still sharp from yesterday that has lasted a hundred yesterdays. No metal sings. No engine hums. Everything is hand-liftable, hand-tied, hand-set. And everything is everywhere.

At night — there is no night. There is only this bright, this heat, this wet hanging air, this checkerboard of roaring green life and blinding empty made-space extending past the immediate in all directions until haze takes it.

You learn not to shout, because the air will not carry it. You learn to drink the water that collects in leaves, warm and sweet. You learn to walk slowly between the sparse gardens, through the great quiet white, while frogs watch you from walls that will still be sharp when your bones are fitted somewhere below as part of the floor.
