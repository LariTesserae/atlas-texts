It is not under the sky. It is under the weight of the sky many times over.

You are down where down stops meaning direction and starts meaning condition — three, four thousand meters of black water above you, pressing. The cold is not weather here. It is the medium itself, two degrees above freezing, thick, viscous with pressure, moving slow around your limbs like oil. There is no surface anywhere you can reach. There is only more water in every direction, and then, far below the idea of below, the floor.

The floor is old. Older than anything that still has edges.

This is a valley, though you would not know it at first in the dark. A broad, drowned valley, kilometers across, walled at a great distance by slopes so gradual and so smoothed they barely read as slopes. Nothing here is sharp. The basalt that once rose hot and jagged has had tens of millions of years to settle. Currents have licked it. Sediment has sifted down grain by grain, century by century, until every fracture is filled with pale abyssal clay. What remains is near-flat, gently undulating — long swells no higher than a sleeping animal, shallow runnels where the heavier water drains. An end-state landscape. It has finished becoming and now only endures.

You cannot see far. There is no light to see by. The last photons from the sun died two miles above you. What faint illumination exists is not light as you know it — it is absence interrupted, for a half-second, by life. A comb jelly tearing itself open in blue-green sparks and then gone. A photophore blinking on a fish that never comes close. Mostly, black that is not quite black, because your eyes invent a grainy gray to fill it. If you stay still long enough, the dark resolves into textures rather than shapes.

And the water is full. That is the first shock. You expect deep sea emptiness and instead find saturation. The water column is packed — not with fish, not with plants, but with presence. Marine snow falls without pause, a blizzard in slow motion: flakes of dead plankton, fecal pellets, molted skins, dust from continents you will never see again. The bottom water is turbid, loaded with fine silt that never fully settles because the current never fully stops. When you move your hand, you displace soup. When you breathe — if you could breathe here — you would breathe grit and cold and the faint iron taste of manganese.

The ground itself is crowded. From ankle to horizon, if horizon existed, the plain is paved. Nodule upon nodule upon nodule, black-brown manganese potatoes, each the size of a fist, a heart, a child's skull, fitted so tightly there is no mud showing between them. They click faintly when the current nudges them. They have grown a few millimeters per million years, accreting metal around a shark's tooth, a fragment of bone, and now they tile the valley floor in continuous armor. There is no room between presences. Stone touches stone touches stone for miles. You could walk for a day across that clattering pavement and never step on bare sediment.

Life, for all that fullness, is sparse. True plants cannot live where sun has never been. What passes for vegetation is a rumor — thin, dirty-white veils of bacterial mat stretched in the lee of nodules where a little methane seeps up from below. Stalked sponges here and there, a few centimeters tall, like gray reeds frozen mid-sway, their stalks encrusted with sediment. A crinoid lifting its feathered arms into the current and then folding them again. Acres of nothing between one and the next. The valley is not barren, but it is parsimonious. It gives only enough to persist.

Animals are rarer still. You can wait a long time. An hour of black stillness for a single event: a rattail glides just above the nodules, so slow it seems to be conserving the idea of movement, its long tail trailing, pausing to nose the silt. Tripod fish standing on their absurd stilt-fins, facing into the flow, waiting. A blind white isopod the size of your hand bulldozing through the snow-dust. They do not gather. They do not school. Each one is alone in its kilometer.

And yet — they use things.

You see it if you watch the crabs. Small, spindly spider crabs, bone-pale, almost invisible until they move. One has wedged a broken clam shell between two nodules to brace itself against the current while it feeds. Another picks up a sharp flake of chert — not made, just broken by pressure and time — holds it in two legs and scrapes at the bacterial mat, shearing it off to eat. The scratch marks remain on the nodule afterward, thin parallel lines. When the crab is done it drops the flake. It does not carry it. It does not build with it. No burrow, no nest, no wall, no artifact that lasts beyond the next storm. Only this momentary use, hand to mouth, stone as extension of claw. Primitive beyond primitive. No one here makes anything that stays.

Because nothing is allowed to stay. This is the rule of the valley and you learn it through your skin before your mind: despite the silence, this place is violent.

It is quiet — profoundly, crushingly quiet. Sound does not carry as sharp cracks but as low pressure. Your own blood is louder than the water. Sometimes a distant, infrasonic moan as a contour current shifts tons of water around the far ridge. Sometimes a faint hiss, like static, which is silt moving. But for the most part, muffled suspension. A held breath.

Under that quiet, turbulence. The bottom water is never still. Deep contour currents pour through the valley like invisible rivers, cold and heavy, accelerating over the low swells. Every few days or weeks a benthic storm arrives — no warning in the dark, only a quickening on your face. Suddenly the snow is no longer falling down but flying sideways. Visibility, already near zero, goes to zero. The silt lifts in sheets. Nodules shudder and roll. Sponge stalks bend flat. The bacterial veils tear and stream like rags. For hours the whole plain is in motion, creation and erasure at once: a carcass buried, a mat exposed, a flake-tool tumbled ten meters and lost, a new runnel carved and an old one filled.

Then it passes. The particles take days to settle again. The crabs emerge. The rattail returns. The pavement resettles with a slightly different pattern of clickings. The ancient floor, which looked so final, so flattened and done, has rearranged its skin without changing its bones.

That is how it holds: old enough to be smooth, deep enough to be dark and cold, crowded enough that every inch is occupied by water and stone and falling dust, and yet empty enough that a living thing is an event. No lights built, no roads, no ruins — the valley has never been touched by anything that intends to remain. Only the brief scrape of a shell held like a knife, then dropped, then covered by snow.

If you stay, you will feel the pressure equalize inside you until you no longer know where your body ends and the water begins. The cold will slow your thoughts to the pace of the tripod fish. You will watch the snow fall sideways in the dark and understand that turbulence does not need noise, fullness does not need abundance, and age does not mean peace — only a longer memory of storms.
