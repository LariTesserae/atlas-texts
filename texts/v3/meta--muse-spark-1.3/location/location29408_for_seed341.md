There is a place down where the land gives up.

Not a valley, exactly — the ground just gets tired of being ground and lies flat and low and lets the water table come up through it. If you walk in from any direction you walk downhill for a long time without noticing, through second-growth heat-forest, and then the trees thin and stop, as if cut with a knife, and you are on the lip of it: a shallow bowl, maybe a kilometer across, ringed by a levee that has long since gone soft and green.

Inside the bowl is the town. Though town is a generous word.

Twenty-some buildings, brick and concrete and sheet-iron, set far apart from each other in standing water, connected by pipe-bridges and duckboard and raised rail. From the lip you can see the whole of it at once. That is the size of it. A hamlet that thought it would be a city and then decided otherwise. You can walk its perimeter in half an hour if you don't stop for the steam.

Because it is hot here in a way that has nothing to do with sun. You almost never see the sun.

The air above the bowl is a lid. Part low cloud, part smoke, part vapor off the marsh and the cooling ponds and the open sluices, part transpiration from the huge leaves. It hangs yellow-grey-green and lets through light the color of late evening even at noon. The heat collects under it and stays. 38, 40 degrees, and saturated. Your shirt is wet in a minute, not with sweat exactly but with everything condensing on you. The iron handrails weep. The bricks weep. The undersides of the pipes drip constantly though it has not rained in days. Water is not so much falling as exhaled.

The buildings are old enough to have settled into themselves. This is not a ruin and not new. Corners rounded by a hundred years of wet air. Brick swollen and soft at the base, black with algae to waist height, then rust-red above. Corrugated roofs bellied and patched, orange with rust that blooms out in lace patterns like lichen. Concrete sluice walls worn smooth as river stone where the water has run. Copper and iron pipes, big as a man’s torso, run between buildings on rusted trestles, lagged in asbestos and vine. They tick and groan as they expand.

It is an industrial place, still. That is the first strange thing, because there are no people at the windows and no animals anywhere, but the machines are running.

Low in the center is the mill-race, or what was the mill-race: now a canal of hot green-black water moving fast between concrete banks, feeding three boiler houses and a dynamo hall with its windows blown out. Inside you can see the flywheels turning, slow and huge, throwing shadows. Pistons breathe. A drop-forge thumps on a timer no one remembers setting, THUMP — THUMP — THUMP, shaking water in puddles. A vent stack lets go every eleven minutes or so, a white roar that drowns everything for forty seconds and lays a fresh hot fog over the bowl so you cannot see ten meters. Then it settles. Hiss. Clank. The burr of a saw somewhere running on nothing. The gurgle and slap of water through gates. The 60-cycle hum of transformers on poles leaning at angles, their insulators furred with moss.

It is loud. You have to raise your voice even in the lulls. The sound bounces off the lid and the water and never leaves. Your chest feels it.

Between the buildings is mostly emptiness — that is the second strange thing for a factory town. Wide lots of shallow water and mud and reed and nothing built. A foundation slab with rails sticking out, drowned. A yard of stacked billets gone entirely to fern. The town was laid out for density and then thinned, or the water took back the streets. What remains stands isolated, each works its own island, with egrets of steam rising between.

And growing through all of it, over all of it, lush beyond reason: not weeds but enormous, confident tropical growth.

Banana clumps twenty feet tall around the boiler house, leaves torn to ribbons by the vent blasts, putting out new ones weekly. Elephant ear taro in the ditches, leaves big as doors, dripping. Banyans straddling a machine shop, roots gripping the walls like hands, sending down new prop roots into the hot runoff and loving it. Morning glory and bitter melon vine have taken the overhead wires and pipe runs as trellises and made a second, broken canopy, which is part of why the light is so dim — you walk for stretches under tunnels of leaf and cable, in green shadow, with water ticking down on your neck.

What roots here roots in metal. The canal banks bloom with orange iron bacteria. Copper-tolerant ferns feather out of mortar cracks. On the north settling pond, where the water is turquoise with dissolved salts and 50 degrees at the edge, a mat of water lettuce and water hyacinth thrives so thick you could almost walk it, steaming gently.

And nothing eats it. That is the wrongness you feel before you name it.

No birds. No herons in the marsh, no swallows under the eaves. No dogs. No rats on the duckboards. Most uncanny in this heat and wet: no mosquitoes, no flies, no drone of insects. The leaves are untorn by chewing, except by steam. Cobwebs with nothing in them sag and fill with condensate and break. It is a plant-only world, vegetable and mineral and machine. Whether the copper and the heat and the constant pressure-shock drove the animals out, or something in the vapor keeps them out, no one left a note. You notice your own breathing too loud in the gaps, and the absence under the machine roar — no call, no buzz, no splash of a frog. Just water against concrete, and iron against iron.

The rules here are not posted but you learn them fast:

Stay on the boards. The ground between looks solid with green but it is floating mat over hot mud that will take your boot.

Do not drink the drip. Everything drips, from every pipe and leaf, clear and tempting, but it tastes of pennies and hot stone and leaves a numbness on the tongue. The town cistern, raised and roofed by the dynamo hall, is the only sweet water.

When the stack begins to scream — a rising thin note about ten seconds before the release — turn your back, cover your ears, shut your mouth. The fog is not poisonous, exactly, but it scalds the lungs and blinds.

Do not stop the machines. Not that you could. Valves are frozen open, gauges long dead, belts self-tightening with vine. The few doors that still shut have been left open to let the heat out. If a flywheel throws a belt, step back. It will slap and find its own way home.

At night — though day and night are more a thinning of the brown light than any real change — the sodium lamps on the transformer poles still come on, buzzing orange through the fog, lighting empty water and huge leaves from below. Moths should batter them. None do. The light just burns, reflected double in the canal, until the lid pales again toward another dim morning.

From the levee lip, looking back in, it almost makes sense: a small, hot, wet engine for making vapor out of water and leaf out of vapor, settled long ago into its cycle, too loud and too empty and too green, running itself because no animal, human or otherwise, has told it to stop.
