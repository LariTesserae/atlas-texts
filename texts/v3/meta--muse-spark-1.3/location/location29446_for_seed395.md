It goes on until the curve of the world takes it.

You are standing low. Not in a valley — there is no high country to make a valley from — but on a plain so level and so wide that elevation has stopped meaning anything. The air sits heavy and warm over it, late-morning warm, the kind that settles on the forearms and the back of the neck and does not lift. The light is plain daylight, white-gold, no tint to it, falling straight down without drama.

At first you think it is empty because there is no water in it. No shine anywhere. No creek line, no pond, no mud, no river scar. The ground is dry all the way to the horizon, a raw mineral ground, pale rust and ash-grey, gritty underfoot. This land is new. You can tell by how sharp it still is. The pebbles have edges. The clods have not been rounded by rain. The dust has not yet become soil. It crumbles like fresh-baked brick, basalt and laterite just uplifted and cracked as it dried — a whole sea-floor lifted into air with no sea left in it. There are polygonal cracks big enough to put your boot in, and in the cracks, dust.

And then your eye adjusts, and you realize it is not empty at all. It is packed.

The green comes second, after the dryness. Not a forest green, not a wet green, but a dense, low, relentless lushness that covers everything that is not tower. A thigh-high scrub, fleshy-leaved and silver-backed, stem against stem against stem with no bare space between. Saltbush and ice-plant and resin-bush and things with no name, all interlocked. Their leaves are thick as a thumb pad, waxy, grey-green and olive and dusty gold, swollen with the night's fog. That is the only drink here. There is no ground water to tap. At dawn a white mist comes off the cooling plain for an hour and condenses on every hair and spine, and the whole plain drinks from the air and then seals itself shut for the day. By ten the leaves have turned their silver undersides out and gone still. From above it looks like a matted pelt, unbroken for miles, soft-looking until you push through it and feel how tough and dry-oiled the stems are.

Out of that pelt rises the city.

Not a human city. No cut stone, no metal, no wire, no glass. Nothing made with a tool at all — and still, unmistakably urban. Towers.

They start near your feet as knuckles of red mud, knee-high, and then further out, shoulder-high, and then further out, taller than you, and then far out, taller than houses, all of them the same burnt-orange clay, pocked and fluted, leaning very slightly with the prevailing wind. Termite mounds. Millions of them. The plain is zoned by them as far as you can walk in a day and further than you can see. Avenues run between them where the scrub has been cleared back to swept dust, wide enough for columns to pass. Side streets branch. Plazas open where the ground has been tamped flat around a great central mound with buttresses like a cathedral.

It is vast enough to make its own weather. Heat shimmers over the towers at noon and makes the farthest ones swim. A pale dust haze hangs permanently at the horizon, kicked up by a million feet. To the east a dark anvil cloud builds every afternoon, bruise-purple at the base, and dissolves every evening without ever raining. The wind that comes off it is warm and dry and smells of resin and hot clay.

There is no room between presences. That is the rule here. Every inch is claimed. On the scrub leaves sit scale insects like beads. Under the leaves run ants in black rivers along their cleared highways. In the dust between towers, spiders have pegged their trapdoors. Skinks flick in and out of cracks. Ground squirrels and mongooses and long-legged bustards work the avenues, dignified as commuters. Above, the sky is busy in a quiet way — kestrels hanging, shrikes darting from tower-top to tower-top to impale grasshoppers on thorn-bushes, weaver birds in loud colonies that from a distance look like fruit hanging on the few dead thorn trees, which themselves are so festooned with nests they seem thatched.

Abundant life, but you have to listen sideways to hear it. This is a quiet place. No roaring, no chorus. The light seems to absorb sound. What there is, is close and small: the dry papery rustle of scrub leaves rubbing as something passes underneath, the faint tick of grit falling down a mound face where workers are repairing, a soft *pfft* of dust as a lizard bolts, the almost inaudible crinkle of a foraging column moving through dry litter — thousands of mandibles clipping, but so synchronized it becomes a hush. Even the birds here do not sing out; they churr and click. Footfalls are muffled by dust and leaf-mat. If you stand still for five minutes, the plain goes back to a low, warm silence that feels pressurized, like a held breath.

And it is never still. That is the other rule: everything is under construction. This is an embryonic geology being built over in real time by biology before wind or water has had a chance to carve it. The towers are embryonic mountains. Some are fresh, dark and damp at the tip where this morning's mud is still being carried up grain by grain. Some are abandoned and crumbling, already colonized by succulents rooting in their flanks, by geckos laying eggs in their vents, by grasses capping them green. A mound lives for decades and then becomes garden. A garden is eaten flat and then becomes avenue. An avenue shifts ten feet to the left when a new queen founds.

Predator and prey move through it constantly, but without spectacle. A mongoose family trots single-file down a main street, noses down, turning over pebbles. A column of driver ants crosses the same street a minute later, carrying green leaf-crescents overhead like sails. A hornbill lands heavily on a tower, hammers, extracts, swallows, leaves. The termites seal the breach from inside before the dust settles. Repair crews boil out. Foragers reroute. The city heals without pause.

If you kneel and look, the scale inverts. The scrub becomes forest, the dust becomes boulders, the crack in the raw earth becomes a canyon with mossy walls — those fleshy leaves rooting impossibly in what looks like pure brick dust, splitting it with root acids, making the first whisper of soil that will, in a thousand years, soften this place. The sharpness will go. The towers will become hills. But now, now it is raw and new and already full.

By late afternoon the light lengthens and every tower throws a long blue shadow across the scrub, and the whole plain turns to stripe: orange clay, olive leaf, blue shadow, orange clay, olive leaf, blue shadow, to the end of sight. The air cools a degree. The leaves uncurl a fraction. Somewhere far off, too far to locate, something small moves a pebble, and it ticks down into a crack.

No water. No machines. Just warm light on new ground, crowded stem-tight and tower-tall with life making a metropolis out of spit and wax and hunger, quietly, endlessly, as far as the world goes.
