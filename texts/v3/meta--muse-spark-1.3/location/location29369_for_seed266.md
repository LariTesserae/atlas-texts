The Table

You feel it first in the lungs, before you see anything properly. The air is too thin to be warm, and yet it is warm — sun-hot on the skin, cool and short in the chest. Each breath you take doesn't quite finish. You have climbed miles above any sea, and the sea is gone anyway.

This is a continent lifted up and left to dry. A plateau so broad it makes its own weather, so high the lowlands around it are only a rumor of blue haze and poison pressure far below. Up here the world is flat in the way an ocean is flat, and cut through, in the way an ocean is never cut. Ancient water — gone for so long no one budgets for it — chewed great winding canyons and troughs and left them. Wind has been polishing them ever since. The result is not jagged. It is settled. Long, smooth yardangs like the backs of buried whales, shallow bowls a day's walk across, mesas with undercut skirts, salt pans cracked into perfect hexagons that go on until the heat-shimmer eats distance.

Everything is mineral. Bone-white salt and gypsum, rust-red sandstone, black basalt ribs where the old lava came up, pale dust that gets into everything. No green anywhere. Not a blade, not a leaf, not moss as you would recognize it. The ground itself has a skin in places — a dark, almost varnished crust of desert varnish and lithic bacteria, brown-black-orange, matte as leather. If you break it with your boot it smells faintly metallic. That is the closest thing to a field here, and everything eats it, one way or another.

Light is ordinary daylight, which is what makes it strange. A white-yellow sun at noon, clean shadows, colors true. But the air is so clear and so thin that distance lies. A town on a far scarp, a hundred kilometers off, stands razor-sharp at midday, every tower and cable-mast etched as if it were on the next ridge. You can see a dust-front coming for three days before it arrives: a low tan wall that thickens and thins, never seeming to get closer, then all at once it is on you. People here learn not to shout. Sound does not carry. The thin air swallows it.

It is mostly quiet, this vastness. Your own footsteps in the dust sound muffled and close, unreal. Your blood ticks in your ears when you climb. Now and then the stone ticks too — great slabs expanding in the heat, contracting after sunset with a soft rifle-crack that comes from everywhere and nowhere.

And it is hot, despite the height. Not furnace hot. Stored hot. The sun pours straight through the thin sky and the pale stone throws it back up at you. Midday the salt pans glare until your eyes water and then dry again immediately. There is no humidity to hold anything. Sweat vanishes before it beads. Lips crack within an hour if you are new. The old hands wear dust veils not for cold but for sun and salt.

The towns are sparse, and that is their logic. You can walk for two days between them across empty pan and see nothing moving but heat, and then come over a wind-ridge and find a whole settlement sunk into a trough out of the wind.

They are not large by lowland imagination, but they are complete — urban in the way a harbor is urban. Layered. At the bottom, the carved rock: rooms and cisterns and stairways cut a thousand years ago when the troughs were first sheltered in, their doorways rounded by wind to soft mouths. Built on top of that, and into it: blocky mud-brick and salt-brick houses, flat-roofed, blind-walled to the dust, painted with lime that blinds at noon. And grafted over all of that: the industrial layer. Sheet-iron sheds, kilns like squat bottles, water-condensers that never condense, only wait, rusting. Pipe runs on stone piers marching straight for the horizon to the next town, to the next mine. Cableways strung across canyons, their buckets moving so slowly you have to watch for a minute to be sure they move at all. A rail causeway, single track, ballasted with slag, running dead straight across a salt flat until it dissolves in shimmer. A train comes once a week, or once a month. No one is in a hurry.

For all of it runs slow. That is the rule here. Firing cycles in the smelters last eleven days. A kiln-master will tell you, without boasting, that his kiln has been warm for fourteen years. Roads crack because the Table itself breathes — salt and gypsum swelling with the day's heat, settling at night, heaving over decades by centimeters. So the newer buildings sit on rollers and timber sleds. The older ones have simply cracked and been re-pointed, cracked and been re-pointed, until the mortar is thicker than the brick. Things that break are not thrown away. There is nowhere to throw them. They are left where they fall and the town grows around them. Outside every settlement is a halo of dead machines: boiler husks, truck chassis with their tires long since stripped for seals, a walking excavator kneeling in the dust with its bucket down, as if grazing.

The factories are not loud. That surprises newcomers. You expect roar and clatter. Instead, at the edge of town, behind a wind-wall of stacked salt blocks, you hear a deep, felt-more-than-heard hum that sits in your teeth. The big low-speed engines turn day and night, turning mine fans and cable drums and grinding mills that chew basalt to powder and salt to brick. Electrified, yes — thick black cables sag between iron poles, humming faintly, feeding arc lamps that burn all night, too bright in the thin air. Smoke rises perfectly vertical in the morning, a black-brown column that only at two thousand meters begins to lean and spread into a man-made cirrus that never rains. There is nothing to rain. The weather here is dry weather: dust devils that wander the pans for hours, picking up gypsum crystals that fall back as dry snow that patters off your shoulders and vanishes, leaving no wet. Electric storms that build over the far scarp, flickering sheet-lightning from cloud to cloud with no thunder you can hear, only a prickling on the back of the neck and the smell of ozone.

And the animals. There should not be this many, in a place with no plants and no water. But there are. They are everywhere once you learn to be still.

The base of it is dust. Wind carries powdered crust, salt, iron, ash from the towns — a thin, constant soup. And on the sunward faces of stones grows that dark varnish, living rock-skin, sour and rich to those who can digest stone.

So: the sifters. Low, barrel-bodied lithovores the size of dogs and goats, armored in overlapping gypsum plates, moving in loose herds of twenty or fifty across the pans. They plow with their blunt snouts, rasping up varnish and salt, excreting neat pellets of clean crystal that glitter behind them like droppings of glass. They move very slowly, blinking with dusty eyelids, utterly indifferent. In the heat of afternoon they dig shallow scrapes and pile on top of each other, plates ticking as they cool.

Above them: the sails. At first you think they are kites, or trash. High up, circling without a single wingbeat for hours — translucent, manta-shaped gliders three meters across, skin stretched over cartilage so thin the sun shines through it amber. They are filter-feeders of the air, mouths agape, straining dust and furnace ash. At dusk they come lower, and you see the faint blue tracery along their wing-edges: static charge they use to sense each other and the coming storm fronts. They land only to lay eggs in warm slag heaps at the edge of town, where the ground stays warm all night.

Around heat and light: the furnace-moths. Not moths, really. Copper-winged, hand-sized, they swarm the kiln vents and arc lamps after dark in such numbers they sound like dry leaves shaken in a bag — one of the few loud sounds here. They feed on oil films, on skin flakes, on anything organic the humans shed. During the day they cling under eaves in overlapping scales, looking like rust.

And the small quick ones that live off humans without ever being tamed: pipe-rats with naked, heat-dumping tails that nest in cable insulation; cistern-lizards — though no cistern has held water in living memory — flat and pale, drinking nothing, making their water from the gypsum they chew, as white as the salt they hide on; carrion beetles big as thumbs that clean the sifter dead within a night.

All abundant. All patient. A sifter may live sixty years. A sail may ride the same thermal for a decade. They do not hurry, because nothing here blooms and fades fast. Even death is slow: corpses mummify where they fall, plates and wings intact for years, until wind and beetles undo them grain by grain.

The strange part, the part that stays with you, is how the built and the living have blurred. A dead excavator becomes a herd shelter; sifters rub their plates bright against its steel shins. Furnace-moths clog an air filter and the engine-men leave a lamp on all night just for them, because the pipe-rats come for the moths and the lizards come for the rats and the whole chain keeps the vents clear of dust. Towns feed the dust with ash, sails seed the slag with eggs, slag keeps the town warm. No water to waste, no plant to harvest — so they farm heat and wind and stone.

If you stay long enough you learn the manners:

Do not trust how close something looks. Walk to it.
Do not make loud noises and expect answer. The air will keep your voice for yourself.
Do not dig for water. You will find only salt that burns the mouth and gypsum that crumbles like dry cake.
Do tend the varnish. Do not scrape a stone clean unless you need to. It grows back slower than you live.
And when the dry snow patters down at noon, white crystals hissing on hot iron, stand still and let the sifters pass. They were here before the rails were laid, and they move at the speed this place actually runs at — the speed of stone learning to breathe.
