The High Shallows

You get up there out of breath, ears popping on the switchback road, and find not a peak but water. Mile after mile of it.

This is high country — high enough that your lungs work a little harder and the light comes in thinner and cleaner — but it is not sharp. Nothing up here is young enough to be sharp. The hills are long, whale-backed swells of old gneiss and granite, worn down over uncountable years until they are more like a memory of mountains. No cliffs, no needles. Just huge rounded domes, saddles, and shallow scoops, all of them drowned.

Because the water has taken the top of the world.

In the scoops are lochs, tarns, mires, fleets. In the saddles are bogs that quake when you step. On the domes themselves, where you expect dry heather, there is sheet water an inch deep running over peat and stone after rain, which is always. And above it all, low cloud sits most days right on the hilltops, so you are submerged twice: from below by the lakes, from above by the air itself. The air is wet to breathe. Clothes never quite dry. Every rock sweats. Every post drips. You walk in cloud-water even when it isn't raining, and when it is raining — wall-grey squalls that come over the swell without warning — you walk inside a cold, loud hiss.

It is cold. Not killing cold, working cold. The kind that keeps the water black and clear and makes your hands clumsy on rope. Your breath shows in the mornings even in summer. The water, if you put a hand in, steals heat fast. Fish like it that way.

The old stone shows where the peat is cut or the water is low: pale grey, pinkish, lichened orange and sage, rounded as river cobbles but the size of houses. Glacial erratics perch improbably on the swells, moss to their shoulders. There is no soil really, only peat — meters of black, waterlogged sponge built from ten thousand years of dead moss — and where it has slumped it exposes glossy black faces that smell of tannin and iron. Everything else is built on piles driven down through that sponge to the rock, or it floats.

And green. For how cold and high and wet it is, it is impossibly lush. Sphagnum in hummocks of lime and rust and acid yellow, cotton grass nodding white in every hollow, butterwort and sundew, dwarf birch and willow no higher than your knee but dense as a thicket. In the water itself, weed beds so thick you could almost walk on them — pondweed, milfoil, stonewort — rising and falling with the seiche, and mats of floating bog that detach and drift with their little trees and nests on them like islands. From a little distance the whole plateau looks like pasture. Up close it is a sponge, a mat, a raft of life layered three deep: moss over peat over water over weed over mud.

You hear it before you see the workings. This is not wilderness. It's a working water.

Put-put-put of single-cylinder diesels. The deeper thrum of the turbine sheds. Water going over the old rounded sills in a continuous white roar. Wind combing the lochs into chop. And over it, birds — always birds — screaming.

Because there is food here in obscene abundance. Char and trout roll in every calm, dark as shadows, rising to midges in clouds so dense they get in your mouth. Eels slide through the ditches. Frogs chorus from every pool in the thaw. Otters work the narrows at dawn, leaving fish heads on the jetties. Red deer stand knee-deep in the sedge, antlers hung with cotton grass, utterly unbothered. Hares, voles, shrews in the drier hummocks. And above: greylags and pintail and teal in rafts that blacken a bay when they lift, curlew and golden plover piping off the moor, gulls and terns following the boats for guts, ravens tumbling in the updraft off the turbine house roof, waiting for afterbirth in lambing week or offal in gutting week. Midges, caddis, dragonflies, diving beetles. The air and water are saturated, near-touching with presences.

People live thinly but busily on top of it. No towns. Scattered — a bothy here, a fish-gutting shed there, a concrete turbine house straddling a narrows, a corrugated-iron pump station with its chimney smoking peat-sweet, a pier of black creosoted piles with a winch and a fuel drum and a string of bare electric bulbs that burn all night in the mist. Causeways of tipped rock run like sutures from swell to swell, just proud of the water, with culverts where the lochs breathe through. Fish cages ride in the deeper old valleys — black rings of floats netted over, crowded with flashing backs, with a flat-bottomed workboat nosed up to each one, men in oilskins shouting over the engine. A smokehouse. A net loft. A tiny chapel with its graveyard on the one true knoll, stones leaning every way in the soft peat. Rusted cranes, abandoned drift-mines with their adits drowned to black mirrors, telegraph poles marching off into cloud with wire humming. Everything rusts up here, everything greens at the edges, everything has to be pumped or bailed or winched back from the water every spring.

At midday the light is ordinary — flat, bright grey-white that makes colors honest. When the cloud lifts for an hour you can see the scale of it: not one lake but a whole region of water, three lochs deep in every direction, the next shed's smoke two miles off, the next flotilla of boats like black seeds, the far swells fading blue-grey to the edge where, on a very clear day, you can see the lowlands far, far below as a different country. Then the cloud comes back down and the world shrinks to fifty yards of chop and reed and dripping wire.

Nothing here is stable. That's the rule. Weather turns in ten minutes from glass to white horses. Water levels rise a foot overnight and drown a causeway, drop a foot by evening when they open the turbines below, leaving boats heeled in mud and weed stinking in the sun. Peat banks calve off after rain and go drifting. A floating bog that was attached yesterday is fifty yards out today with ducks nesting on it. The boats are always moving — hauling nets, shifting cages, ferrying peat bricks, running a generator part to the far shed — jostling at the landings, engines idling, men waving you off or waving you in. It has the energy of a harbor and a farmyard and a storm at once. Loud, crowded, clattering, splashing.

If you stay, you learn the rules quickly because the place enforces them without malice:

Keep to the rock or the piles. The green that looks solidest will take your leg to the knee.

Keep an engine running or a bailer handy. Water reclaims everything it can reach, which is everything.

Keep your food covered from gulls and your fuel covered from rain, and you will get neither.

Do not trust calm. Calm is just the interval between squalls when the midges find you.

And do not look for dry ground to rest on. Rest on water — in a boat, on a jetty with your feet dangling, in the bothy with the stove going and the rain hammering the tin and the loch slapping the piles underneath. Let the cold settle out of you. Listen to the sheds thump and the sills roar and the birds quarrel and the halyards clank against the masts, all of it amplified and flattened by water and low cloud until the whole high plateau hums like a factory floor.

It is not strange. It is just old, high, wet, and busy — an ancient flattened range that refused to be a mountain anymore and became instead a cold, lush, shallow sea in the sky, where men with diesel and nets and electric light have learned, precariously and loudly, to live afloat.
