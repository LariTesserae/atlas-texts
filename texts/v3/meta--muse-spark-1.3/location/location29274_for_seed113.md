You go down to it by stairs that were not meant to be stairs.

The rim is street-level — curb, rail, a kiosk still selling something — and then the ground simply stops. A block, maybe two, hamlet-sized, has dropped straight down. Not long ago. The fracture is still sharp. You can see it in the walls: bands of roadbed and pipe and old foundation and then, below that, unprepared earth, black basalt and red cinder, snapped clean as a bone. No softening. No talus slope yet. Rebar hangs out of the upper concrete like whiskers, still bright where it tore, not yet rusted through. The place is young. It hasn't learned how to be a ruin.

Forty meters down is the floor. That is where the city is — was — will be.

It is warm down there. Noticeably warmer than the street you left. Geothermal warm, breath-warm. The air gets thicker with every switchback, until by the bottom your shirt sticks and your glasses fog and don't unfog. The heat comes up from below, from the exposed rock and from the machines, and the water comes down from above, and they meet in the middle as a permanent low cloud.

You hear it long before you see it clearly. A roar, but not one roar. Layered.

Water weeping out of the walls in a hundred places. Drip becoming trickle becoming sheet. Condensate ticking off every cold pipe. Pumps gargling in the culverts. And underneath it all, a deep electrical hum that you feel more in your molars than your ears, that rises and falls as something cycles.

Light barely makes it. At noon a single pale slab of daylight falls straight down the shaft and moves, slow as a sundial, across the sunken plaza. The rest of the time it's dusk. The walls are terraced with maintenance strips — amber work-lights left on years ago and never turned off, half-drowned in vine — and the vines themselves give off a faint green phosphor where they are wettest. Enough to see by, not enough to read by. Your eyes stay wide open down here.

The floor is not mud. It's the old roofscape, pressed down intact: a plaza of hexagonal pavers now tilted in three great slabs, a bus shelter still standing, its glass filmed with algae, a row of shopfronts with their upper floors sheared off, open to the sky like dollhouses. The city above bridged over the hole with impossible lightness — three filament spans, no thicker than your wrist, that should not be able to carry their own weight, let alone the service tram that crosses every eleven minutes without a driver. They don't vibrate. They don't sag. They just hold, humming.

And in the gaps between those few surviving structures: emptiness. That's the strangest part for somewhere so built. This is urban density turned inside out. All the infrastructure is in the walls — you can see the layers in section, conduits and culverts and cable trays stacked like strata, balconies and doors now opening onto sheer air, ladders that end mid-drop — but the center is hollow. Vast space between presences. You can stand in the middle of the plaza and shout and hear it come back three times off the fresh rock, uncluttered. A whole town's worth of construction holding open a mostly empty room the size of a cathedral nave.

Water owns that room. It is near the surface everywhere, under the surface everywhere. The pavers sweat. Put your palm flat and it comes away wet. In the low corner where the three slabs meet, there is a permanent black mirror of a pond, knee-deep, warm as bathwater, fed by wall-seeps and by rain that funnels down the shaft. Mangrove-like stilt roots come up out of it — not mangroves, exactly, but figs and ferns that have learned to live with wet feet. The water never quite drains, never quite floods. The old storm system, retrofitted with something newer, breathes it in and out. You can see the intakes pulsing: dark grates that inhale the pond down to mud twice a day and then let it sigh back up.

Everything is lush with no one to eat it.

Moss over the bus shelter in a velvet foot-thick mat. Maidenhair fern exploding from every crack in the concrete, fronds as long as your arm, dripping. Vines — kudzu-thick, aerial-rooted — climbing the sheer new cliffs, finding purchase in fractures no wider than a fingernail, hanging back down in green curtains that condense the cloud into constant rain beneath them. Fungi in white shelves along the warm pipes. The smell is chlorophyll and hot stone and ozone.

And no animals. None. That takes a minute to notice because the place is so loud and so green your brain keeps expecting pigeons, rats, mosquitoes, at least a fly. There are none. No birds nest on the ledges. No spiders in the ferns. No hum of insects under the roar of water. Leaves are uneaten, perfect, a little monstrous in their perfection. Spores and seeds just fall and clone where they land. The figs make small hard green knots that never ripen, never get carried. Even your own presence feels intrusive, mammalian, too loud in a different way.

The maintenance crews say it's the field. The same beyond-modern engineering that keeps the filament bridges up and the water in its slow tidal breathing puts out a low resonance that animal nervous systems can't stand for long. You feel it if you stay too many hours — a nausea behind the eyes, a sense that up is slightly to the left. Plants don't mind. Concrete doesn't mind. So the machines tend the plants, in a way: pruning arms on wall-rails snip the vines back from the intakes once a week, steam jets sterilize the pond edges, and otherwise let it grow.

Unusual things happen with water and light down here because of that field. Droplets falling from the vines sometimes slow, hang a half-second too long in the amber light, as if the air thickened for them. Condensation forms on the undersides of leaves in perfect grids. In the pond, your reflection arrives a beat late. No one has bothered to fix it. It's within tolerance.

The rules of the place, as you learn them by standing in it:

Stay on the high pavers. The pond breathes.
Don't drink the wall water — it's warm and mineral and will run straight through you.
If the hum stops, go up. It means the intakes are cycling and the air will go thin for a minute.
Don't bring animals down. Not even briefly.

It is small enough to walk across in ten minutes, walled on all sides by that fresh, weeping cliff. You can always see the boundary. You can always see the slit of real sky. And yet inside that boundary it has its own weather — cloud forming at chest height by mid-afternoon, burning off by evening into a warm drizzle that sizzles where it hits the hot exposed conduit along the east wall, sending up little ghosts of steam that join the cloud again.

An empty town at the bottom of a fresh wound, kept warm and wet and lit dimly by its own caretaker machines, growing furiously green over sharp concrete, loud with water, with no one in it to listen except you, briefly, before you climb back up into the dry noise of the street.
