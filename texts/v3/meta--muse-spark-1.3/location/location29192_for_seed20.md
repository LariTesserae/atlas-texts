On top there is a drowning.

Not a lake. A lake has a surface and a sky. This has neither. This is a small place — you can cross it in sixty breaths if the water lets you — a shallow saddle of fresh black rock pinched between two sharper teeth, high enough that the clouds live underneath it. From the rim you can see them: a slow white sea falling away in every direction. And above you, and around you, and in you, is the other sea.

It does not rain here. Rain implies drops and air between them. Here the air itself is water. A standing wall of it, warm as blood, hangs over the saddle in a cylinder perhaps three hundred paces across. You walk into it and your ears pop. Light gives up. Sound does not.

The rock is young. That is the first thing your feet tell you. It has not learned to be soft yet. The caldera floor is broken basalt and rope-lava still glazed, edges like broken bottles, vesicles hissing where the flood finds air pockets and pops them. No soil to speak of, only a slurry of ground glass and warm ash that clouds up around your ankles in black milk and then clears impossibly fast, sucked sideways. Pumice stones, light as bone, rattle around your calves, never settling. At the edges the young rim is still crumbling — you can watch it happen: a fin of rock shivers, sheds, drops into the cloud-sea below without a sound you can separate, and by morning there is a new fin, pushed up raw and steaming. Early-formation. The mountain is still deciding what shape to be.

And it is drowned. The whole saddle holds a skin of water a knee to a chest deep, tea-dark, bath-warm, though in pits it goes down further than it should. You test one with a stick — a warped shepherd's staff left by someone — and the stick goes down and down and does not hit. The water over the pits is a different color, a deep green-black that drinks the dim light. It smells of iron and hot stone and something green trying to live.

Because the light is dim. It should be noon, it should be bright at this height, but the column of water eats the sun. What reaches you is filtered, late-afternoon-underwater light, olive and grey, diffused to nowhere. No shadows. Things glow faintly at their edges instead — the wet rocks, your hands. Above, where the sky should be, there is only the churn: a low, boiling belly of cloud and spray lit from inside now and then by a bruised flicker that is not quite lightning, too slow, that hangs a second too long.

Warmth saves you. The water is  warm, the rain is warm, the rock under your feet is warm. Steam peels off everything in ribbons that are instantly torn away. You are soaked and you are not cold. Your skin prunes and steams at once. Breathing is drinking: each breath is half air, half mist, sweet and mineral on the tongue. You cough and it feels correct.

Then the sound. It never stops enough to be called background.

At the bottom there is a bass you feel in your molars — the whole mountain humming through water, a waterfall pouring off the saddle's lip on all sides at once into the cloud-sea below, a thousand throats swallowing. On top of that, the white hiss of the wall itself, the endless static of millions of gallons held upright. And inside that, details that should not be audible but are because everything is so loud your ears invent clarity: the clack of pumice teeth knocking, the glassy *tink* of fresh rock spalling off under water, a deep *whoom* at irregular intervals when a bubble of volcanic gas burps up through a pit and bursts, sending a ring expanding that does not dissipate but travels outward, climbs the wall of rain, and vanishes. Sometimes a crack like a rifle, close, that makes you duck — rock splitting open under your feet.

You learn fast not to shout. Shouting does nothing; the roar eats it before it leaves your mouth. And the place answers loudness with loudness: a wind gathers for a second after any sharp noise and throws a sheet of warm water sideways hard enough to stagger you.

Life is sparse here, spaced out like it is embarrassed to take up room. Vegetation: a few things that have figured out how to hold on. Black-green beards of algae stream from every leeward edge, flattened by current. In the shallows, mats of olive moss that inflate and deflate with the surge, breathing. Five, maybe six trees — if you can call them that — stunted, umbrella-crowned, trunks bent double away from the prevailing blast, bark swollen with stored water, roots like fists gripping cracks. Their leaves are waxy, cupped to shed, rattling constantly in the deluge with a sound like distant applause. Between them, much emptiness: open warm water over black glass, nothing, nothing, then one more moss island.

Animals are rarer still. You may not see any at all for an hour, and then it will happen all at once. A blind white fish, long as your forearm, flickers up from a pit, rolls in the shallows, vanishes before you can be sure. Tiny translucent shrimp cling to the algae beards, pulsing. Once, a bird — a storm-petrel, impossibly lost, high and bewildered — shears through the water-wall, screams, and is gone. It does not land. Nothing lands here for long. Under a rock ledge there is a crab the color of ash, holding perfectly still, letting the torrent pass over it. That is the whole census. Much space between presences. The emptiness is part of the ecology.

People were here, or something that stacked stones. The built things are faint, scattered, half-dissolved by water and rebirth. A low ring of basalt slabs near the center, enclosing nothing but deeper water — a sheepfold, a shrine, a failed attempt to mark safe ground. A cairn on the highest tooth, rebuilt and knocked down and rebuilt, stones slick, balanced impossibly against the wind. Three steps cut into the rim on the east side, leading down into white cloud and stopping mid-air where the rim fell away. Offerings wedged in cracks: a woven grass knot, a smooth river stone from impossibly far below, a strip of cloth that whips continuously and never tears. No tools, no metal, no wire. Only weight and balance and patience. Pre-tool prayers.

And then the wrongness, which is not occasional but structural.

The water does not obey. In the center of the saddle it gathers, mounds up a hand's breadth higher than the edges, though it should drain. Streams run *uphill* along hairline channels in the lava to feed it. Your reflection in a still second between surges does not match — you lift your hand a half-second after it does. Drips fall *up* from the surface back into the wall when the big *whoom* sounds, hanging for a moment like inverted rain.

Direction thins. Up-slope and down-slope swap if you stare too long at the churning wall. The deafening roar sometimes arrives *before* the surge that causes it, so you hear the future crash, brace, then feel it. If you walk the perimeter keeping the drop on your left, you return to start with the drop on your left still — as if the saddle has only one side. No one has mapped the pits because dropped stones do not make a plunk; they make a sound like a door closing far away, and sometimes, minutes later, the same stone rattles up around your ankles.

The rules, as far as you can learn them wading here with water in your mouth and thunder in your bones:

1. The water does not leave, though it pours off every edge. The level never drops, never rises. It is replenished from somewhere that is not sky.
2. The place is small and will stay small. If you try to leave by climbing the teeth, the wall thickens, the rock gets sharper, the roar pitches up until you turn back. The boundary is acoustic as much as physical.
3. Nothing rots. Leaves, cloth, algae — they are scoured clean, bleached, kept. The mountain is too young to know decay, too turbulent to allow stillness.
4. Everything that happens happens again, slightly louder. The surge, the burp, the rockfall. The place rehearses itself.

You stand chest-deep in a warm ocean perched on a sharp young knife of rock above the clouds, in twilight at midday, deafened, alone except for shrimp and one crab and the memory of whoever stacked those stones, while fresh earth is made and unmade under your feet and water stands up around you like a room with no roof.

It is terrible and it is, inexplicably, a shelter. The world below drowned or burned or simply continued — you can't remember which — but here, high and small and loud beyond thought, the drowning is held in one place, kept circling, and you can stand inside it and breathe water-air and be, for a little while, scoured clean yourself.
