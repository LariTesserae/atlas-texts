You come to it from above, because everything here is above it.

The upland is flat lichen tundra, wind-scoured, pebbles and dwarf birch no higher than your ankle. Then the ground gives. Not a slope so much as a cut — a long sunken trough a few miles long, a half-mile wide, dropped twenty or thirty feet below the plain as if a great thaw had softened the earth and let it sag. You descend by a raw peat stair, where the rim is still calving off in square blocks.

That freshness is everywhere down there. This is a young place. The banks are sharp, vertical in places, showing black peat over blue-grey till, roots still hanging white and unrotted where the willows were undercut last spring. Fallen slabs lie tipped in the shallows, not yet rounded. The water has only just found this shape and is still learning it.

At the bottom, there is almost no bottom. Only water, near-surface and at-surface. The basin holds a shallow, braided lake-mire — black water between moss, moss between water. Every footfall would be a splash if you could find a foot of empty ground to place it. You can't.

Because it is full. Packed.

From the rim, at first, it looks like vegetation — a deep green-gold carpet rolling to mist. Then your eye adjusts to the pattern, and you realize the pattern is streets.

It is a town. Not a human one. No woodcut, no axe-mark, no metal, no plastic, no right angle that wasn't made by teeth or bill.

Lodge upon lodge upon lodge. Beavers began it, muskrats infilled it, geese and ducks roofed it. In the center, where the water is knee-to-waist deep, the beaver lodges rise like thatched roundhouses, five, six feet tall, built of gnawed aspen and willow sticks plastered with cold mud. Each one is chewed and re-plastered until the sticks fuse. Between them run canals, straight as survey lines, kept dredged by nightly swimming — the avenues. Off the avenues branch narrower muskrat runs, and off those, the low domes of muskrat houses, thousands of them, the size of wash-baskets, woven from sedge and cattail thatch. And on every dry crown, every lodge-top and hummock, a bird has built: a goose nest of down-ringed sedge, a red-throated loon's rim of mud, a ptarmigan scrape lined with willow leaf, so close together that a sitting goose could stretch her neck and touch the next sitting goose.

Layered construction, three stories high though no story is more than a foot above water: underwater — the drowned lattice of old food caches and abandoned tunnels; waterline — the causeways of crossed sticks and sphagnum mats that hold like floating sidewalks; above — the lodges, nests, drying racks of sedge stems laid for bedding. Nothing wasted. Old nests become foundations for new nests. A collapsed lodge becomes a garden for cottongrass.

And all of it is held together by plants. This cold should be barren, but the hollow traps heat and water. It is lush beyond reason for the latitude. Sphagnum in hummocks soft as lungs, bottle-green and rust-red. Cotton grass nodding white. Dwarf willow grown tall here — chest-high thickets along the causeways, leaves slick with water. Marsh marigold, buckbean, sedges sharp enough to cut. Angelica gone leggy. Every stick of lodge-wood sprouts: willow wands stuck in mud for building have leafed out, so the houses themselves are green, growing houses, rooting into themselves.

The air is cold and wet at once. Four degrees, maybe five, high-summer for this place, but your breath still shows. The light is ordinary daylight, but filtered — a high, thin overcast that makes everything shadowless and bright, the black water silvered. The cold pools in the basin. As the warmer air off the tundra spills over the rim, it condenses. A low mist is always forming and unforming a foot above the pools, catching on willow leaf, beading, dripping. The peat banks weep. You can see water seeping out of the cut walls in dark lines, constant after rain. Everything drips, seeps, condenses. Your jacket is damp in minutes, not from rain but from the air itself touching you.

For how much life there is, it is strangely quiet.

There are thousands here. The water flicks with fish — sticklebacks in shoals so dense they darken the pools. Cloud of midges hangs over every hummock, and swallows — or rather the northern phalaropes and longspurs — work them without calling. A cow moose stands belly-deep in the far channel with her calf, pulling pondweed, chewing slowly. Fox tracks stitch the mud between lodges. On a mud bar, a dozen young geese, flightless in molt, preen shoulder to shoulder, silent. Even the beavers, usually slapping, move without slap; at this density, alarm would be constant alarm, so they have settled into truce and muffle.

Sound is swallowed by moss and water. What you hear, if you crouch and stay still: the tick of water dripping off a leaf into a pool. The soft suck as a muskrat surfaces. The faint squeak of wet sedge rubbing. The low, nasal snuffle of the moose calf. A ptarmigan cluck, once, then stop. Underneath, a constant faint rustle-chew — incisors on willow bark, bills pulling thatch. The town working on itself, quietly.

Because it is working. Nothing here is still. That is the rule of the hollow: build up or drown.

The water rises a finger's width after every rain, falls after a dry day. The mosses grow upward to escape it, a centimeter a year. The muskrats cut channels to drain their doors, the beavers dam the same channels to float their logs. A dam raises one street and floods another; the flooded lodge is abandoned, then re-colonized by nesting terns who like the new islet. A fox takes a goose egg here, so three geese there nest closer next year, packing tighter. A willow thicket is coppiced to stubs by beavers in a night, and by August it has flushed back denser.

Predator and prey live wall-to-wall. You see a short-eared owl quartering low over the sedge, unhurried, because voles are everywhere in the thatch — you can hear their tiny runways rustling under the lodges. A pike hangs motionless in a canal, gills fanning, waiting for sticklebacks to funnel past the narrows the muskrats maintain without knowing they maintain it for him.

No part is empty, no part is finished. If you stand long enough, your boots sink and sphagnum begins to close over the dent, green tendrils uncurling. If you left a pack here, in a week it would have a sedge sprouting from its seam, in a month a sandpiper nesting against it. The place does not permit vacancies.

From the center, you cannot see the rim for the willows and mist and lodge-roofs. The town extends past sight in every direction, repeating: water, moss, thatch-dome, stick-dome, down-ringed nest, canal, willow wall, water again. Only by climbing back out — up the weeping raw bank, hands black with peat — do you remember how shallowly it sits in the world: a wet, green, crowded city sunk in cold ground, built entirely without tools, holding itself above drowning for one more lush, silent, teeming season.
