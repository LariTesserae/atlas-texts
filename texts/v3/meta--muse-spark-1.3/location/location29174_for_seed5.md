You don't find it so much as step into it.

The moor is wide, flat, cold-white under a hard blue sky. Wind combs the short grass. Nothing rises. And then the ground simply gives — a lip of pale rock under your boot-toe, and below you a bowl.

Not a valley. A room.

Maybe forty paces across, oval, sunk ten meters straight into the lowland. Limestone gone soft with age, its edges no longer sharp or crumbling but settled, rounded like old teeth. Lichen has had centuries here: map-gray, sulfur-yellow, black script on every face. Nothing is still being carved. The hollow has arrived at the shape it intends to keep.

Light gets trapped here. Up on the moor the light is thin and cutting. Down in the bowl it piles up. The white stone throws it back and forth until the whole pit glows, painfully bright at noon, every wet surface glittering. You have to squint. Your breath comes out white and hangs, because for all the brightness the air down here is cold — cold that has pooled and stayed. Low air. It doesn't circulate. The wind shears straight across the mouth of the hollow and never drops in. Cold sinks and sits.

And in that cold, still brightness: a town.

It fills the bowl completely. That is the first shock. There is no ground left, no empty slope. From rim to floor, wall to wall, the hollow is occupied by building — small, low, fitted dry-stone cells, beehive huts and square pens and terraces, stacked two and three high, leaning on each other, roof becoming neighbor's floor becoming bridge becoming stair. Ladders of birch and bone lashed with sinew. Walkboards across alleys no wider than a shoulder. A cistern mouth here, a smoke-hole there, though no smoke ever rises. Stone on stone on stone, hand-set without mortar, chinked with peat and moss. Primitive work, but so much of it, and so tight you couldn't slide a hand between houses.

And over, through, between all that stone: green.

Not the sparse green of the moor above. Lush, indecent green for a cold place. Pillow moss, feather moss, sphagnum in the joints, ferns uncurling from every crevice, liverworts slick on the north faces, dwarf willow pruned to bonsai knots along the parapets. The roofs are not roofs, they are lawns. The walls are gardens. The town does not sit in vegetation; it is upholstered in it. Where a stone has fallen, green has already taken its job.

There is very little open water, for all that green. That is the second thing to learn. No stream runs here. The damp is interstitial. It beads. Every moss cushion sweats with condensation. Every overhang drips, slowly, at intervals so long you forget to wait for the next drop. In shaded corners, under slabs, black troughs hollowed from logs hold a finger's depth of clear seep — melt and breath and night-frost collected. Skims of ice form on them by morning and melt by noon and form again, without ever quite freezing solid or evaporating away. The stones themselves feel damp to the palm even when they look dry. Pockets of wet in a world that is otherwise stone and leaf. You could lick water off the walls if you needed to. Something has.

Everything living is packed in with the stone.

You notice movement only after stillness: the moss is crawling. Woodlice, pale and slow, turning under leaves. Spiders no bigger than a freckle working lines between fern fronds. Shrews flickering along the wall-tops. Above, around the rim, a churn of small white birds — redpolls, snow buntings — that drop down one by one to pick at seeds on the roof-lawns, and voles fat as fists moving openly between houses, unafraid. Moths. Beetles. A ptarmigan hen with a train of chicks crossing a bridge like a procession. Abundant, all of it, shoulder to shoulder with people and plants. No room left unclaimed. If you stand still, something will walk over your boot.

People too. They are there, you realize, because a shutter of woven willow slides back and eyes regard you. A hand, red with cold, trims moss with a flint flake. An old woman carries a bundle of lichen-cakes on her back up a ladder that sways almost imperceptibly. Children sit shoulder to shoulder on a terrace edge, legs dangling, watching you without calling out. There are many of them for so small a place — you can feel the density, elbows and knees, breath and bodies, the town humming with occupancy like a hive.

But there is no hum.

That is the strangeness that settles last. In a place this crowded and alive, there should be noise — voices, livestock, clatter, birdcall. Here: nothing carries. The moss eats it. The bowl shape holds it. The cold air deadens it. A ladder creaks, softly, like a joint. Water lets go from a ledge — *tick* — onto stone, and is gone. A bunting opens its beak on the rim and no song arrives, or arrives so thinned you think you imagined it. Even your own boots on stone sound wrapped in cloth. The inhabitants have leaned into it: they gesture. A flick of two fingers: move aside. Palm flat on chest: stay. They work stone with stone, and have learned to do it without striking sparks of sound. When they must speak, it is close to the ear, breath-warm, almost not a voice.

And nothing changes.

That is the rule here, though no one tells you. You see it after a while: a patch of moss on a roof clipped to exactly the shape of the stones beneath, new growth harvested daily to keep the line. A wall being rebuilt — a man lifts out a slipped block, packs fresh sphagnum behind it, sets the same block back in the same seat. A child carries water from one trough to another and back again, not to move it but to keep it from skinning over. The terraces don't expand. The population doesn't spill over the rim. The birds come and go but the same number seem always to be here. The ice forms and melts and forms. The light at noon today will be the light at noon tomorrow, reflected from the same white walls onto the same green.

You understand, standing with cold seeping through your soles and brightness aching in your eyes, that this is not a town that happened. It is a town that is being kept — exactly damp, exactly green, exactly full, exactly quiet — held in balance like water in a cupped hand.

If you climb back out, the wind hits your face again like a door opening. Sound rushes back. The moor resumes, vast and empty. Behind you the bowl closes. From five paces away you would not know there was a whole bright, crowded, mossy, silent city breathing cold air at your feet.
