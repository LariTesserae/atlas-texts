You come over the col with the sun in your eyes and drop into it all at once.

A bowl. A high hanging bowl, scooped out of old rock a long time ago and then left to settle into itself. The cliffs that rim it on three sides are not jagged. They are rounded, bellied, water-blackened — granite gone soft-shouldered with age, with potholes big enough to bathe in, with balanced boulders perched like eggs, with long vertical stains where water has run for ten thousand years and will run for ten thousand more. On the fourth side the ground falls away, a white lip and then nothing but air and far below, impossibly far below, a drowned blue sea of lower peaks. You could walk rim to rim in ten minutes if your lungs would let you. Up here the air is thin and viciously clear.

The light is the first violence. At this height there is nothing to filter it. It bounces off snowfields, off quartz veins, off a hundred shallow pools, off the pale undersides of wings, until it seems to come from everywhere at once. Shadows are not grey but a hard, mineral blue. Your eyes water and the tears freeze on your lashes. Everything glints. The rocks sweat glitter.

Because the rocks weep.

That is the second thing you learn, once your eyes adjust. The whole amphitheater is wet. Not a lake, not a river — a weeping. The cliff walls are seamed with seeps. Water beads out of stone that looks solid, gathers, runs in black threads, falls in bright needles two meters, ten meters, to the next ledge. Every ledge holds water. Shallow pans, rimmed with ice, linked by little spillways that never stop running. The ground underfoot is not ground but sponge: peat and shattered slate saturated to the surface, squelching, with your boot sinking half an inch and water welling up around it. Even in cold that should lock everything solid, it moves. It ticks. Drip, slip, trickle. Icicles fat as your arm grow beside open water. Mist breathes off the pans and instantly needles into frost on every edge — on lichen, on guy-ropes, on eyebrows.

It is very cold. The kind of cold that makes metal stick and breath come short and shallow. Exhaled air hangs, white, then falls as tiny crystals. Fingers go wooden in minutes inside mittens. The water should be ice and some of it is — a skin, a fringe, a clear lid you break with a knuckle — but underneath it runs, milky with rock flour, rusty in places, turquoise in others, smelling of stone and iron. Somewhere below, heat or salt or pressure keeps it alive. No one here can tell you which. They just bail it and channel it and drink it through cloth.

Almost nothing green can hold here. That is the sparseness that strikes you after the wet. A few starved cushions of saxifrage hunched into cracks, black-green. Beards of hair-lichen hanging off the lee sides, grey as old men. And in the seep-lines, smears of snow-algae, blood-red and sulphur-orange, spreading like spilled paint where the water is richest. No trees. No meadows. A stick of driftwood carried up from below is a treasure, saved for decades, passed down.

And yet it crawls, flutters, paddles, swarms with life.

This is a rookery before it is anything else. The cliffs are whitewashed with droppings. Ledges that from a distance look like snow resolve into bodies, shoulder to shoulder: cliff-doves, snow-auklets too fat to fly far, with translucent bills, with feet the pink of gums. Thousands. They shift and jostle, a feathered tide. When a section lifts, the air fills — a silent snowstorm that rises, wheels, and settles again on a different ledge. Below them, the water is alive: red ice-worms knotting in the pans, pale midges in clouds so dense they bend light, blind white loaches nosing in the milky pools. On the peat, herds of shaggy dwarf goats no higher than your knee, bred down over centuries, pick at lichen with yellow teeth. Long-tailed white voles run the walls like stitches. Everything eats everything else, or eats what the wind brings. The wind brings a surprising amount — updrafts plaster the wet bowl with insects killed by cold below, with seeds, with chaff. The doves eat the midges. The midges eat algae and drowned doves. The worms eat droppings. The goats eat lichen. The people eat eggs and birds and worms and goats, and their scraps feed the gulls and voles again. It is a closed, furious circle, crammed into two hundred meters.

Because people live here. Dense as the birds.

At first you mistake the town for more rock. The houses are dry-stone beehives, slate-roofed, low, stacked in terraces up every habitable ledge, wall sharing wall, roof becoming the floor for the house above. Ladders, knotted hide ropes, narrow stair-cracks worn to a shine connect them. Drying racks jut everywhere — fish, strips of goat, bird skins, lichen cakes — so close they touch. Cisterns cut into stone, spillways roofed with flat slabs to make streets a foot wide, bridges of two planks over black water. Smoke from dung fires hangs low, blue, unable to rise far in the thin air. Bone hooks, stone mauls, hand-looms, sinew nets: no forge, no wheel, almost no metal except for a few traded knife blades worn to slivers and carefully hafted in antler. Everything is made by hand and mended by hand and you can see it — the chipped edges, the lashings.

There are perhaps three hundred people in a space that should hold thirty, and they, like the birds, are always moving. This is the turbulent heart of it. The ground here is ice-cored moraine; it slumps overnight. A wall leaned yesterday leans more today. A pool that was there at dawn has drained by noon through a new crack, and a new pool has appeared under someone's floor. So they bail, they carry stone, they re-lay, they prop, they trade — a child runs past with a basket of worms, an old woman carries a slate up a ladder on her back, two men haul a goat out of a cistern, doves explode upward and resettle. The market is a thaw-pan plaza ankle-deep in icy water where people stand shoulder to shoulder exchanging eggs for sinew, down for salt, silence for silence. Hands flicker. It has the energy of an anthill kicked open, or a bazaar seen through glass.

For it is quiet. Unnaturally quiet for so much life.

Partly it is the snow and peat and thin air, which swallow sound. Partly it is custom. Sound carries painfully here — throats bleed in the cold if you shout — and so they don't. No calls, no songs, no hammering after sun-up; stone is set, not struck. What you hear, if you stand still and let your ears open, is small and close: the tick of drips from a thousand icicles, the soft shatter when one gives way, the low groan of settling rock underfoot, the papery whirr of wings passing inches from your head, the suck of boots in peat, the click of knucklebones in a game, your own blood in your ears. Even the goats bleat in whispers, hoarse little coughs. The doves make no coo at all — only the sound of their bodies landing, like soft hail.

And there is the strangeness, which you only notice after an hour, when the brightness starts to hurt.

Things thaw and freeze too fast. In full sun a pan will skin over with ice in the shadow of a passing cloud and lose it again thirty seconds later, a lid on and off, on and off. Your footprints fill with water and then with needle-ice that preserves the tread perfectly until the next seep erases it. The light itself seems to condense: motes hang in the air that are not dust but ice, and ice that is not falling but forming, mid-air, out of your breath and the weep of the rocks.

The old ones say words freeze here. That if you speak outside on a bright cold day your words turn to crystals and settle and are lost, and only come back at night when the hut warms and the walls drip and you hear the day's lost sentences ticking faintly off the stones. Whether or not you believe it, you find yourself speaking less. You gesture. You learn the hand for *egg* and *careful, thin ice* and *move, please*. At night, crammed shoulder to shoulder in a low stone hut with goats breathing under the bench and doves rustling in the thatch above — because the birds nest in the houses too, you cannot tell where thatch ends and feathers begin — you do hear it: a faint murmuring from the walls as they warm, like thaw, like memory. Drip, tick, sigh.

You do not stay long if you are wise. The bowl does not want you, exactly, but it does not push you out. It just continues — weeping, freezing, jostling, rebuilding itself around you while you stand there blinking in the glare. Water welling up around your boots. A dove landing on your shoulder because you are the nearest ledge.
