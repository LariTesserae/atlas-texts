The valley does not decide whether it is air or water.

You come down the last slag-slope and the rain is already a wall — not falling so much as standing, a warm grey curtain a mile thick that breathes. Past it, the lowland is drowned, but not like a lake. Like a lung full. The air itself is wet enough to drink. Your clothes are soaked in four breaths. Your skin never dries. Everything sweats and drips and condenses and drips again.

This is low country, flat as a pan, a former town-basin now under two to ten feet of bath-warm water. You can still read its shape: the grid of streets under the flood as darker green lines, the rooftops nosing up like flat stones. Elevation barely exists. A rise of three feet is a hill here, and on each hill there is something sharp and new.

Because nothing here is old. The rock is young — black basalt and poured slag still edged, still glassy where it broke. The flood tore up roads and left rebar bristling, unrusted at the tips, razor ferns already splitting around them. Concrete slabs stand on end where the water heaved them, their fractures white and unworn. Even the rust feels fresh, flaking off in bright orange peels that have not yet had time to soften. The world looks emerged last week.

And it is crowded. Not empty-flooded. Stuffed.

Warm shallow water to the horizon, and out of it, everywhere, growth.

Not trees as you know them. A drowned jungle of things that learned to live with iron. Giant taro leaves big as doors, waxy and black-green, shedding water in sheets. Mangrove-like stilts, but the stilts are copper pipe as often as wood. Kelp ribbons twenty feet long trailing in the current of the streets, tangling with fallen power lines. Bladderworts and water lettuce matting the surface so thick in places you could almost walk — until your foot goes through with a hot suck and the mat knits closed behind you. Figs strangling pump-houses. Vines as thick as your thigh climbing guy-wires and flowering with white trumpets that fill and spill with rainwater. Algae, velvet moss, liverwort slick on every vertical inch. Spores hang in the air like steam.

Layer on layer on layer. Underwater, more: root-braids, drowned hedges still growing downward, pale shoots reaching up for a sun they never quite get. The light never gets strong here. The cloud is permanent, storm-green, and the water filters what is left. Everything is lit as if through bottle glass — dim amber-green gloom at noon, firefly dark under the mats. The leaves shine wetly to catch it. Some of them glow faintly at the edges when the engines surge, as if lit from inside.

There are no animals. That is the first wrongness you feel before you name it.

No fish brush your calves, though the water is warm and full of rot to eat. No frogs. No dragonflies over the mats. No birds in the wire-tangle. Not even a mosquito whines in your ear. Lush beyond reason, hot, wet, dense — and utterly animal-empty. Leaves tear and fall and rot with no mouth to take them. Flowers open to nothing. Seed pods burst unheard — except you do hear them, because in the absence of everything else, the plants are deafeningly loud.

And the machines. That is the second wrongness.

Scattered — that is the word from above. From the slope it looks like almost nothing built remains: a chimney alone in a lagoon, a lock-keeper's hut with water to its windows, a billboard frame holding a mat of vines, a staircase descending into green water and stopping. A town unmade to parts.

Down inside, you understand it was never about buildings. It was about works.

Every half-acre, half-drowned, an engine still runs.

Diesel pump stations on concrete pads, their flywheels turning slow and black, sucking brown water in one side and vomiting it hot out the other for no reason you can see. Boiler houses with their doors blown off, fireboxes still lit orange underwater — impossible, but they burn — sending up columns of steam that punch holes in the rain. Transformer huts crackling blue, cables drooping into the flood and fizzing. A line of compressors along what was Main Street, chuffing in sequence. Pipes, pipes everywhere: eight-inch iron arteries running just under the surface, surfacing to leap over a roof, diving again, sweating, hammering with pressure. Valves turning by themselves. Gauges trembling.

No one tends them. The plants tend them, or the water does. Roots have grown through bearing housings and do not stop the shafts; they polish around them. A fig has swallowed a generator and hums with it. Electrified water shimmers. You feel it in your teeth before you hear the change in pitch.

Because the sound — god, the sound. It is physical.

Rain on ten thousand leaves, each leaf a drum. Water slapping water. The low diesel throb in your sternum, the high boiler scream, the clank-and-hiss of relief valves lifting all around the valley at random, steam whistles that start backward. And under it all, vegetal: the creak and pop of bamboo-fast growth, pods detonating, trunks splitting bark, lilies unfolding with a wet rip. Your ears fill. You have to open your mouth to equalize. You feel it in your jawbone. When a mat collapses somewhere out in the middle distance, it goes with a cannon-boom of trapped gas, and the echo does not decay right — it returns a half-second too early, from the wrong direction.

Nothing stays still long enough to map. This is a turbulent place, hyperactive, making and unmaking itself while you watch.

On your left, a new island of slag and root-mat pushes up, steaming, crowned in minutes with pale sprouts that darken as you stare. On your right, a pump-house that was there when you looked loses its wall to the vines — they swell, the mortar sighs, the whole corner slumps sideways into the water with a hot splash, and already the water lettuce is knitting over the hole. A pipe bursts fifty yards off and throws a white plume twenty feet high; for a moment the plume hangs, does not fall, breaks into slow cubes of water that drift sideways before remembering gravity and crashing down. The rain in one square acre suddenly falls up, hissing off the flood surface into the cloud, while the acre next to it doubles down.

Reality here is thin from being wet too long.

Reflections arrive before the thing. You will see the ripple of a falling leaf in a still pool a full breath before the leaf lets go above it. Shadows detach at the edges — a pipe's shadow lags three feet behind the pipe when the transformers surge. Depth lies. A wade that looks knee-deep drops to your chest without a lip or step, warm water closing over your ribs like soup, and the bottom under your boots is warm metal, vibrating. If you stand very still, you can feel the whole valley throb, irregular, like a heart with extra chambers.

It has rules, but not the ones you brought.

One: water is not separate. Rain, flood, sweat, sap, steam, condensate on a leaf, spray off a flywheel — it is all one circulating body, and you are briefly inside it. Breathing is just slower drinking.

Two: metal is a habitat, not an object. Iron does not resist the jungle; it participates. Pipes sweat, boilers breathe, current flows root to root as easily as wire to wire. Do not expect a machine to be dead because it is drowned. Expect it to be louder.

Three: growth is mandatory and fast. Anything still for more than an hour is grown over. Your footprints on a mudbar fill with seedlings while you stand looking back at them. If you set your pack down on a dry slab — rare, precious dry — come back in twenty minutes and tendrils will have tested its straps. Leave it an hour and you will need a knife.

The valley extends past seeing. In every direction the same drowned checker: green mat, black water alleys, steam columns, pipe-backs arching like whales, vine-strangled chimneys. Town-sized, region-sized, but you will never see its edge because the rain-wall eats distance after a quarter mile. Sounds come from farther than sight, relayed pipe to pipe. A whistle you hear may have blown an hour ago on the other side.

At dusk — though there is no real dusk, only a dimming of the dim — the electric huts strobe blue through the mist, and the glow-edges of the leaves brighten in answer, and the steam turns pink-grey, and the whole wet engine-jungle roars louder, if that is possible, as the cooling air tightens the water and the metal contracts with rifle-crack reports. The water around your legs, chest-warm all day, goes almost hot near the outflows, slick with oil and pollen and fallen petals — a thick broth no animal will ever sip.

You climb onto a rooftop to sleep, if sleeping is possible in the roar. The tar paper is warm, sharp aggregate still biting through your groundsheet, moss already finding the seams. Below you the street-river runs brown-green, carrying leaves, flowers, a spinning door, a whole intact window frame with vines for curtains, moving fast. A pump two roofs over hammers, misses, hammers again. The rain does not stop. It has never stopped. A new shoot, pale and blind, has already nosed up through a crack beside your hand, trembling in the vibration, opening its first leaf to the dim.

No frog sings for it. No hand will pick it. It does not care.
