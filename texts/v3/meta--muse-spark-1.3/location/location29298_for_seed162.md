It is high up and it is warm, which is the first wrong thing you notice.

You climb — or you must have climbed, because the air thins in your chest even as it stays hot on your skin — up onto a country that should be cold and bare and is instead sweating.

A plateau, but plateau is too small a word. A raised world. It goes on until the curve of it makes its own weather, and the weather never leaves. Above you, not sky but lid: a low, unbroken amber gloom, the color of tea and furnace-light, thick enough that the sun is only a rumor, a brighter bruise that moves. No shadows, or rather all shadow. The light comes from everywhere and nowhere, dim and diffuse and warm as breath. At noon it is dusk. At dusk it is dusk.

The ground under that lid is young. Violently young. This whole massif is fresh, still cooling from whatever thrust it up — ten thousand years is nothing here — and it shows in the sharpness of everything. Nothing has been softened yet. Ridges come up in knives and broken glass stairs, black basalt columns still hexagonal and unchipped, pahoehoe ropes frozen mid-flow, cinder cones with lips so crisp they could cut. You can put your palm on stone that is still warm from inside the planet. In places it is hot. Steam gets out through cracks in slow, deliberate exhalations, and the rock around those vents is stained industrial colors: sulfur yellow, copper green, iron rust-red. No river has had time to carve a valley. Water hasn't learned the way yet.

So water does not gather. That is the rule here: damp, never drowned.

There are no lakes, no long rivers. The vast dryness drinks everything. Rain comes, because the plateau makes its own clouds, but it comes wrong — a sudden, warm, hammering downpour at 3 a.m. that lasts eleven minutes and stops mid-drop, as if a valve shut. The water vanishes straight down into the porous lava, hissing. What remains is what matters: for hours afterward everything drips. Condensation beads on every frond. Dew pools gather in lava blisters and basalt bowls, in the cupped leaves of pitcher-ferns, in hoofprints that become temporary ponds. You walk and your boots are soaked in ten steps, though you haven't seen a stream in days. In the low pockets, in collapsed tubes and pits, there are hanging swamps — perched lenses of water held in moss, trembling, never quite flowing out. Frogs live their whole lives in a pool the size of a helmet. Then it evaporates and they aestivate, waiting for the next eleven minutes.

And on that sharp, hot, intermittently wet stone, life has gone lush and crowded and insane with productivity.

It is not forest as you know forest. It is upholstery. Velvet. Everything sharp is sheathed. Liverworts and mosses lay down inches of black-green sponge that swallows sound. Above that, tree-ferns twenty feet tall lean together, fronds interlaced until you cannot put a hand between them. Balloon-vines inflate with warm air in the morning and lift their photosynthetic sacs up into the dim to drink the diffuse light, then deflate at night with a soft sigh all across the scarp — thousands of them, sighing. Fungi the size of doors bracket the cliffs, pale and pulsing faintly with their own heat. Roots do not dig; there is nothing to dig in yet. They grip, they sprawl, they strangle the columns and drink the dew off them.

The air between the leaves is dense with presences. Not empty space with things in it — things with very little space between. Spores drift so thick the light shafts, dim as they are, become visible as columns. Insects in clouds that bend like a single animal. Birds, or things that do the work of birds, flicker everywhere, dark shapes against dark. This is a crowded country. Even the ground moves if you stare: pillbugs big as fists rolling basalt grit, velvet worms, heat-crabs with shells still soft from the molt clustered around a seep, drinking.

Nothing here was built. That is important to understand because at first you will think it was.

You will hear — or rather feel, because hearing is difficult here — a rhythm, low and regular, from under a ridge: *chuff... chuff... chuff...* You will push through ferns and find a fissure venting steam in perfect piston-bursts, the rock around its lip polished smooth by centuries of that breathing. Nearby, a line of cone-shell grazers, each the size of a boiler, are moving slowly along a mineral seep, scraping up sulfur and iron with radulas that spark faintly blue. Behind them, their dung is already being dismantled by a second line of smaller animals, and behind those, fungi. It is an assembly line. It is refining. Further on, a whole hillside hums — not heard so much as felt in the teeth — where a mat of mycelium is carrying charge, bioelectric potentials flickering like a power grid when a storm passes. Eel-things in the dew-pools discharge in sequences, lighting the water from within for a second. The forest distills. The herds grind and smelt and pump.

It is industrial without industry. No walls, no roads, no ruins, no straight lines at all. No one made the machines because the machines are the ecology. The great filter-feeders on the windward cliffs open ribbed mouths to the wet wind and exhale dry air and bricks of concentrated salt and metal, which they leave behind in geometric piles that look, until you touch them, like ingots. The bellows-birds — huge, slow, with throat sacs that glow dull orange — inhale cool damp air and exhale it hot over their nests of fermenting vegetation, keeping them at a constant kiln temperature. The piston-worms in the ash flats, ten feet long, drive their bodies through the ground in hydraulic strokes, aerating, turning, churning cinder into soil that did not exist a hundred years ago. Engines, factories, production at continental scale, but grown, not constructed. Untouched, even as it works night and day.

And because it works so hard and so fast, it is turbulent. Unstable. This place does not sit still.

You camp on a flat of new moss and wake to find a new ridge has shouldered up fifty yards away overnight, steaming, its ferns still smoking. A dew-pond you drank from yesterday is now a fumarole. A herd of mite-deer — thousands of them, knee-high, antlers like twigs, moving like a single spilled liquid — pours over a col in silence, reroutes around the new heat, and is gone by midday, leaving only crushed spores. Landslides happen constantly, but slowly, as if the mountain is shrugging in its sleep. Creation and destruction are not events here. They are weather.

It is mostly quiet. That is the strangest part, and the part that stays with you.

With all that movement — steam pistons, herds, collapses, storms forming and unforming inside the lid — you expect roar. There is almost none. The sponge-moss and the porous rock and the thick, warm, wet air eat sound. A cliff falling a mile away is a soft *whump* you feel in your sternum more than hear. The mite-deer run past and you hear only the faint tick of their hooves, like rain. Even your own voice falls flat after six feet, muffled, embarrassed. What sound there is, is close and small: water ticking off a frond into a cup-leaf, *plik... plik...*, the wet rip of a vine inflating, your own breathing too loud in your head, and underneath everything, if you lie still on the warm stone, a deep, almost infrasonic throb — the planet idling.

Stay long enough and the world starts to break, gently, without noise.

Scale does something here. Or heat does. Or youth. Distance stops behaving. You will see the same herd grazing on two adjacent ridges at once, and when you blink, only one ridge has them, and you cannot say which is the copy. You will walk toward a basalt spire all morning and find it is suddenly behind you, without having passed it. Storm cells are born out over the central flats, spin, rain their eleven minutes, and dissolve — and sometimes they rain upward, the drops rising back into the gloom. Time stutters in the hot spots: you step into a pocket of air over a vent and your sweat evaporates instantly and recondenses instantly, and for a second you are certain you have already stood here, exactly here, and left a footprint that is still warm.

The locals — animal, vegetable, mineral, the categories smear — don't seem to mind. A bellows-bird will stare through you with an eye like cooling glass and exhale, and in its hot breath you will briefly see the ridge as it will be tomorrow, sharper, steaming, already mossed over.

Rules, if you need them to walk safely:

Heat comes from below, not above. Trust stone more than sky. Do not trust stillness; stillness here is just very slow upheaval. Drink from dew, not from seeps — the seeps are working fluid, full of metal and charge. Make no loud noise; not because something will hear you, but because the place will swallow it and give nothing back, and that silence is lonelier than any predator. And if you see yourself ahead on the trail, in the dim amber, walking with your pack a little lighter — do not call out. Let them go. The plateau duplicates under stress, and it is under stress all the time.

At night — though night is only a slightly deeper dimming, a cooling of the amber to rust — the whole vast highland breathes out. Condensation runs down every surface. The balloon-vines sag. The piston-vents slow. The herds bed down in the sponge, thousands of warm bodies steaming faintly. The mycelial grid discharges softly, blue flickers running for miles under the moss like thoughts. You lie on warm, sharp, young stone under twenty feet of lush, dripping life, in a silence so complete you can hear blood in your ears, while all around you, quietly, turbulently, without any hands to guide it, the world manufactures itself anew.
