It is low. You notice that first, even before the damp — that the ground gives way and you are stepping down into it.

Not a valley. A made hollow. A shallow bowl sunk below the level of the marsh around it, ringed with a wall no higher than your chest, built of flat stones laid without mortar and black with age. From outside, in the fog, it looks like nothing; a hamlet that forgot to be a hamlet. No houses. No fire. Just the low ring, and inside it, a roof.

The roof is most of the place.

A dozen great posts, whole trunks stripped and adzed smooth long ago, lean inward at slightly different angles, holding up a sagging thatch of reed and bark and sod that never quite dries. It does not cover the whole bowl — it leaves a ragged oculus in the middle open to the grey sky — but it shadows three-quarters of it, so you come out of flat white daylight into a cool brown dim. Your eyes take a while.

Everything under your feet is made.

There is no soil here, no true ground. Where the footpath should have worn to mud, there is instead a pavement of split planks, hand-split, uneven, the adze-marks still visible under a skin of algae where they have not been polished by feet and by water. Between the planks are gutters, finger-deep troughs chiseled out of log, running in a slow spiral toward the center. Where planks give out, there are cobbles set on edge, and where cobbles give out, tamped clay mixed with crushed shell, pocked with shallow cups the size of a palm. Every cup holds water.

The air is wet without raining. That is the rule here. The stone sweats. The posts sweat. Water beads on the underside of the thatch, grows heavy, falls. The thatch itself does not so much shed water as condense it; even on a day without rain you can hear it, a constant ticking and plinking as the whole roof weeps into the gutters. The clay never dries. Your breath fogs, though it is not cold enough for frost — just cool enough that the damp sits on your skin and in your sleeves and does not leave.

It is weathered in a way new work never is. Nothing is sharp. The tops of the wall stones are dished where hands and rumps and feet have rested for generations. The planks are cupped in the middle from treading. The lashings that hold the cross-beams — rawhide and twisted withy — have been replaced so many times the posts wear grooves where the knots sat. It has been here long enough to settle into itself, to stop becoming a building and start being a weather.

And it is empty.

That is the strange part, at first. For something so small — you could walk across it in forty paces — and so thoroughly worked, there is almost nothing in it. Two upturned coracles, their hides cracked and hard as drums. A rack of poles for drying nets or fish, now bare, leaning. A stone quern overturned and half-sunk in clay. Vast space between one thing and the next. The air in the middle under the roof is just hollow, tall for its width like a chapel or a grove clearing, smelling of wet woodsmoke years old and rot and mineral water.

Then you hear them.

The loudness comes on you all at once, the way dimness did. Not human noise. Life.

Under the eaves, in the thatch itself, thousands of small dark bodies shift and rustle — swallows and swifts and wrens, and bats folded between them like dead leaves, waking and squabbling in the dim. They have unpicked the thatch from below for nesting, so the underside is pocked with holes, each rimmed with mud and feather and droppings. The chittering never stops; it layers, rises, breaks into a sudden whir of wings that circles the oculus, out into the fog and back in again, a vortex of flickering.

Below them: frogs. The gutters and cups are full of them, cool brown and green-black, throats pulsing. They call without pause, a low jug-o-rum and a higher metallic peep that cuts through the dripping. Between the cobbles, eels as thick as your wrist move in the permanent film of water, nosing the stone. On the bare drying rack, a heron stands perfectly still, and on the wall, three more, waiting. Every few minutes one strikes — a splash, a croak cut short, the wet slap of wings. A scatter of tiny silver fishbones grows whiter each season in the central hollow.

Almost no green. For all the water, vegetation has barely found a hold, because there is no earth to hold to. Only what can live on made things: a felt of moss on the north side of every post, liverwort in the chinks of the wall, a few ferns uncurling from a joint in the thatch where dust and droppings have made a thimble of soil. A thread of duckweed in the slowest gutter. That is all. Sparse, stunted, opportunistic, nibbled back as fast as it grows by beaks and teeth.

The light stays low. The fog takes the top off the day, the thatch takes the rest. What comes through the oculus falls in a single slanted column full of midges and down-feathers and drip-spray, and lands in the central sump — a round basin, waist-deep, stone-lined, hand-pecked, always full to the lip with black water that trembles with tadpoles and newts. Around it the planks are darkest, slickest. You walk carefully.

If you stand still long enough, you understand the workings. Water condenses, seeps, is channeled. Insects breed in the still cups. Frogs eat insects. Eels eat frogspawn. Herons eat frogs and eels. Swallows eat flies. Mites eat feathers. The droppings feed the moss and the algae that keep the planks slick and the water rich. The people who split these planks with stone adzes and lashed these beams are mostly gone — perhaps they come only at low water to lift a trap, to leave a fish head on the quern — but what they made keeps running without them, a small, loud, wet machine for making animals out of fog.

At dusk, or what passes for dusk when the day was never bright, the noise peaks. The bats drop from the thatch and stitch the hollow air. The posts creak as the cool tightens them. Somewhere a loose withy taps and taps against wood, keeping a rhythm under the frogs. The walls weep faster.

You are the only large mammal in a room built by small hands and now owned entirely by small hungers. The boundaries hold: wall, roof, water. Outside is marsh. Inside is this — hollow, handmade, dripping, teeming.
