You get there by ducking.

Above you there is ocean — real ocean, sunlit and shallow and cold — and a concrete breakwater holding it back. In the side of the breakwater, at low-tide line, there is a hatch the size of a dinner plate. You unhook it, you let the sea in, and you follow it down a throat of new concrete into a room that should not hold a town.

It does.

The room is nine strides long, four wide, three tall. You can touch both walls if you spread your arms. That is the whole of it. That is the whole country. And it is full.

Not full like a closet is full. Full like a downtown at rush hour is full. There is no empty water here. Every liter is claimed.

First, the water itself. You are submerged and you stay submerged — there is no surface inside, no air to rise to, only cool green water, 11 or 12 degrees, that presses on your ears with the weight of just a few meters. It is clear, strangely clear, lit like noon in a shallow bay, but it is thick with motion. Microbubbles rise sideways. Silt hangs and never settles. Currents braid. The vault breathes: twice a day the tide-engines in the ceiling inhale the sea through slots and exhale it through the floor, and the whole room becomes a slow, cold lung. When it turns over you have to hook a thumb into a cable-loop to stay put.

Light comes down in knives. Six sun-wells — simple-looking glass plugs in the ceiling, but if you stare too long you see they are doing something light shouldn't do, bending and holding it, combing it. They pour solid-looking columns of daylight into the water, full of drifting motes, and the columns do not waver even when the water moves. Inside the columns it is noon. Outside them it is bottle-green evening. The algae know this and lean for it.

The terrain is not floor, it is city-section. The original rock is still there underneath everything: young black basalt, glassy where the blasting cracked it last year, edges so sharp they will cut a glove. You can see it in the corners where the builders haven't yet covered it — fresh, unsoftened, no rounding, no patina. Everything else is built on it and into it and over it until there is no distinction.

Layered construction, wall to wall to wall. From floor to ceiling, from basalt to concrete lid, a scaffold of pressure-frames, printed terraces, pipe-bundles, mesh decks, lockers, lofts, gutters, grilles. Dwellings? They were dwellings. Or server-cabinets. Or both. The boxes are the size of shoeboxes, of microwaves, of coffins, stacked with no alley wider than your wrist. Cables as thick as your thigh run alongside capillaries thin as hair. Valves bloom like mushrooms. Ladders go nowhere except to another ladder. There is a street here, if you can call it that: a single kinked gap, maybe forty centimeters wide, that runs the length of the vault in a Z, and you have to swim it sideways, pulling yourself hand over hand, your tank scraping both sides. On both sides, windows — tiny portholes glowing amber — look back at you. Every surface has been used twice.

The materials are wrong in a familiar way. Concrete, yes, and stainless, and basalt, and rope grown fuzzy with biofilm. But also the other stuff: plates that weigh nothing until you push them and then weigh everything; membranes that hold their shape in water as if the water were air, forming dry pockets the size of fists where sparks crawl; paint that emits a low heat and a low tone; metal that heals around a bolt-hole if you watch for an hour. The sun-wells, the tide-lung, the sterility-hum — this is engineering that does not bother to explain itself. It just keeps working. A panel will iris open as you pass, not because it sees you, but because the water pressure changed three rooms away. A stair of light will fold out of a wall, hold for ninety seconds, then fold back into the wall.

It is loud. That is the first shock after the press of cold. Water in such a small, hard, crowded place has no room to be quiet. There is the low organ-thrum of the tide-lung, felt in the teeth. There is the constant champagne-hiss of the aerators and the sun-wells shedding excess photons as heat. There is clack and tick as shutters open and close by the hundreds to farm the light-columns — *click-click-click* like insects, but sharper, metallic. There is the groan of the whole scaffold flexing when a wave hits the breakwater outside. There is the sterilizer's whine, just at the edge of hearing, that makes your fillings ache. You hear it through your skull, through water, through suit. No muffling. No distance. Sound has nowhere to go.

And there is no animal life to soften it. None.

That is the strangest thing, and you notice it slowly. No fish dart through the street-gap. No crabs pick at the corners. No barnacles whiten the pipes, no mussels, no worms, no copepods in the light-columns. Even the motes are mineral, not plankton. The sterilizer sees to that — a lattice field that makes animal nerves misfire, that lyses larvae before they can settle. It tolerates you for an hour in a suit because you are large and temporary and sealed. Anything small and wet and trying to live as an animal dies here.

So what lives? Almost nothing green, and that almost nothing matters immensely.

Sparse: a beard of white thread-algae on the undersides of cables, shivering in current. A few pads of sulfur-green film on the basalt where the sun-wells miss. And one — only one — kelp. A single stalk, pale from low iron, that entered as a spore through the hatch and refused to die. It cannot grow up; there is no up left. So it has grown sideways, seventeen feet of ribbon winding through the street-gap, using the amber portholes as secondary suns, tying itself into bowlines around pipes to hold against the lung's exhale. It is the only soft vertical in a world of hard horizontals, and everything orients around it.

Everything else that moves is not alive, or not animal-alive. Maintenance motes — flakes of smart matter the size of fingernails — school and disperse, tightening a bolt here, scraping film there. A builder-arm, too large for the room, folds itself into eleven joints to pass through a gap six inches wide. Water does most of the work.

The rules here are simple and absolute, and you learn them with your body:

1. Everything stays wet. The dry-pockets are for machines, not for you. Do not try to breathe them.

2. Nothing larger than a fistful of open water can persist. If you stop moving, motes will begin to scaffold around you. Keep drifting.

3. Light must keep moving. The shutters farm it, the kelp chases it, the paint stores it. Do not block a column for long. The room will nudge you — a jet of cold, a shutter's clack — until you move.

4. Make no animal noise. The room is already loud enough to cover you. It prefers machine sound.

If you stay still long enough, hooked into a cable-loop in the middle of the street-gap, letting the cool take your fingers, you start to understand the scale. It is not small. It is hidden. From the outside, a valve box you could hug. From the inside, inexhaustible, because every box contains boxes, every pipe carries pipes, every surface has been subdivided until a shoebox dwelling has neighborhoods: a sleeping ledge, a heat-exchange shrine, a farm of light. The people who built this — and they were people, once; their handprints are still in the fresh concrete under the scaffold, small, hurried — built for a crowding that never came, or that came and left. Now the town maintains itself for no residents, growing crisper edges, adding another terrace over the basalt, polishing the sun-columns.

The kelp waves. The shutters click. The lung inhales. A wall of rain that is not rain — condensate forced out of the ceiling membrane in perfect beads that fall *up* with the exhale current — passes through a shaft of noon and for a second every crowded, packed, humming inch of the pocket-city flashes silver.

Then it goes back to green and loud and cold, and keeps building.
