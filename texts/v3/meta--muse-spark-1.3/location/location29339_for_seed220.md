It is below.

Not cut into a hillside, not in a valley — under. The rock closes over it. To get there you go down a throat that was once a river's work and is now a stairwell, a service incline, a throat lined with pipe. The air gets warmer as you go, degree by degree, until your shirt sticks and the stone under your hand is not cool anymore but the temperature of skin.

Then the incline ends, and there is no more down except falling.

The place opens all at once, and you feel it before you see anything — because there is almost nothing to see. A room. No, a vessel. A hollow smoothed to an egg-shape by water that has been gone for ten thousand years, long enough that the scallops and flutes in the basalt have stopped growing and just *are*, settled, balanced, polished by time and by a damp that never quite leaves. You could put a hamlet in it. You could put a small town in it, edge to edge, and still have air left over. From wall to wall is maybe four hundred paces. From the cap where you stand to the sump floor far below, twice that. You can feel the far wall as a pressure on your ears, not as a sight.

Because it is dark. Not night-dark. Buried-dark. The dark of a closed mouth. Your eyes do their frantic widening and find nothing to work with. After a while you make out faint differences: a rust-red smear where moss holds, a wet shine on steel, the slightly less-black of the void in the middle. No lamps. No windows. A long time ago someone decided that light was not worth it down here.

What holds instead is sound.

It is loud. Impossibly loud for how empty it is. You expect an empty place to be silent. This one roars, low, from everywhere. A chord, not a noise — three or four deep tones stacked so close they beat against each other, and over them a higher whine like a wet finger on glass, and over that the constant clatter and hiss of moving water and fans and feet. The floor under you trembles with it. Your sternum trembles with it. The sound has weight. It holds dust up in the air where your headlamp — if you turned one on, which you won't, there are rules — would show it hanging.

This is a city. That is the strange thing to understand in the dark. Your hands tell you before your eyes do: the rim where you stand is not rock. It is decking. Perforated steel, warm, vibrating faintly. Behind you, terraces climb the curve of the egg, narrow streets stacked ten, twelve deep, houses that are more like lockers, like niches, bolted straight into stone. Pipes run in bundles thick as old trees, sweating. Cables go over in catenaries, humming when you touch them. Stairs switchback. There are railings worn smooth by hands. There is infrastructure everywhere — water traps, heat exchangers ribbed like gills, vents breathing warm fog, signage embossed for fingers, not eyes. Organized. Layered. Unmistakably urban.

And empty.

That is the second strange thing. A town built for thousands holds dozens, at most. The volume is mostly air. The terraces are narrow and the center is nothing — a great hollow throat of warm air that rises and falls and rises again. Between one lit — not lit, *sounded* — presence and the next there are long gaps of black and hum where you walk alone and hear only your own boots and your own breath folded into the chord. Near-void, despite the density of building. The city is a shell around a lungful of air.

The middle is not entirely empty, though. It is where the beyond part lives.

Out in the black, suspended with nothing under them, hang the boroughs. Slabs. Platforms the size of rooms, of houses, made of a ceramic that isn't quite ceramic, pale gray, seamless. They do not hang from chains. The chains you see — thin filaments that glint when condensation beads on them — are tethers, not supports. They keep the slabs from drifting. What holds them up is the chord itself.

Stand on the rim and listen with your whole body and you begin to parse it: the city is singing to hold itself up. Low projectors set into the walls, round mouths crusted with mineral, pump standing waves into the egg until the air itself has shelves in it. The slabs sit in the troughs of that pressure. When the power shifts, they tilt, correct, breathe a few centimeters up and down. The loudness is not a byproduct. It is the foundation. Turn it off and the town's center falls three hundred meters into the sump.

So no one turns it off. The heat of the machines that make it keeps the whole egg warm as blood, 30, 32 degrees, and the warmth keeps the air wet. This is the damp: not a river, not rain as you know it. The warm updraft rises from the sump and the machines, hits the cooler caprock above, condenses, and falls again as interior weather. A perpetual, intermittent drizzle that exists only inside this one room. It beads on every pipe, runs in black trickles down the fluted walls, gathers in rim gutters that tick and gurgle, drops from tethers in fat, warm drops that never reach the bottom because they evaporate halfway, then condense again. In the acoustic nodes, where pressure is highest, the mist will sometimes just hang — a band of glittering droplets suspended at chest height, refusing to fall, shivering. Occasionally a node collapses and you get a true downpour for a minute, a sudden clattering rain on steel decks, steam hissing up off the exchangers. Then it passes.

Where it is wettest, life has taken hold, sparsely. No forests. No soil. Just the tenants of condensation: a rust-colored moss that grows in velvet patches on the lee side of pipes, pale as a lung; white root threads that dangle from cracks and drink fog; in the quieter corners, clusters of blind fern, translucent, the fronds no bigger than your thumb, glowing very faintly if you let your eyes starve long enough — not enough to see by, just enough to know they are there. They feed on mineral leach and heat and the steady rain of skin cells and oil from the decks. It is enough.

Animals are rarer still. You hear them more than see them. Pale moths, big as a palm, that navigate by the chord and are deafened if you clap. Things that click in the gutters — eyeless isopods the length of a finger, grazing moss. Down in the sump pools, where the fallen water collects in black, warm, mineral-bitter basins between heat-exchange fins, there are fish, white and finger-long, that feel vibration through their jaws. And people, if you count them as fauna. Few. You pass one on a stair and you both go quiet, listening for each other's footsteps to avoid collision. Maintenance crews with their throats trained to hear a projector going sour. A child once, humming deliberately off-key to hear the room correct her.

The rules of the place become obvious once you live in its sound:

Do not bring bright light. It kills the moss and blinds the fish and, worse, it marks you as someone who does not trust the chord. The city is navigated by echo — by the way your boot scuff returns a half-beat later off the far wall, by the different ring of deck versus rock, by the low warning flattening in the tone that means a slab is drifting close overhead. You learn to see with your ears or you learn to stay in your niche.

Do not make sharp noise. Shout and the whole egg answers, slap-back upon slap-back, and for a moment the slabs shiver. The old hands stuff soft caps on chair legs, wrap tools in cloth, speak in low vowels that join the chord instead of fighting it. The town adds its small domestic sounds — kettle whistle, hinge sigh, water tick — into the great tone like tributaries.

Do not go into the center without a tether. The air there will hold you, briefly, if you fall right into a node — there are stories — but it will also deaf you and turn you around until you do not know rim from sump. The center belongs to the suspended boroughs and the hanging rain and the moths.

And keep moving. The place is active, cycling. The fog thickens and thins on a roughly hourly breath. The projectors hand off load from one to another with a slow, audible changing of the guard, the pitch sliding a quarter-tone down and back up. The pipes knock as hot water slugs through. The moss spores when the drizzle peaks, releasing a smell like iron and bread, and the moths come out to eat it, and the isopods come out to eat the fallen moths, and the maintenance crews come out to clear the gutters before they clog. Predator and prey, weather and repair, all turning together.

If you stand long enough on the rim, sweating in the warm dark, your boots buzzing, the black pressing soft on your open eyes, you start to feel the unusualness that is not quite strangeness: your echo comes back a fraction too early, as if the far wall were a meter closer than geometry allows. It is the sound-lens, they say. The pressure in the middle is so high it makes sound travel faster there, bending the returns. The engineers who tuned this — who learned to pour foundations out of noise, to run heat through plates with no pipes, to grow walls that heal their own cracks with precipitated stone — left no manuals, only the hum. You do not need to understand it to live in it. You only need to listen, and sweat, and walk softly, and let the loud dark hold you up like it holds everything else.
