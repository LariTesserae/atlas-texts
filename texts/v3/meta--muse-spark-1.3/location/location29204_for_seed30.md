You come over the pass late, when the light is already going, and you drop into it without seeing it first.

The air changes before the view does. Down in the valleys it was thin and cold and clean. Here, one step over the basalt lip, it turns warm on your skin — bathhouse warm — and wet in your mouth. You have to breathe harder, not from the climb but from the thickness. The clouds are right on top of you, dragging their bellies across the rim.

Then the bowl opens.

It isn't big. You could walk across it in ten minutes if you could walk straight, which you can't. Two hundred paces, maybe three hundred, wall to wall. A shallow dish punched into the high shoulder of the mountain, ringed on three sides by a jagged collar of older peaks that keep the sun out. Above the collar, the real sky: deep indigo, a few early stars. Down inside, everything is pearl-grey dim, like evening that forgot to finish.

The ground is wrong. That's the first thing.

It hasn't decided to be ground yet.

Everywhere else on this range the rock is worn — rounded, lichen-spotted, stepped by old ice. Here it is raw. Black glass, froth, rope and pillow and shard. Lava that cooled last year or last decade, still with its cooling-cracks sharp enough to cut boots. Pahoehoe ropes frozen mid-twist. Mounds of cinder that slump when you look at them. There are no valleys. There are no streambeds. Water hasn't had time to carve anything. Instead the whole dish sweats.

Water is on everything and in everything. It doesn't rain so much as the air itself gives up. Mist presses in over the eastern rim every twenty minutes or so — you can watch it come, a slow grey tongue — and condenses on every cold edge. The black rocks weep. Sheets of seep run down the fresh faces without channels, braiding and unbraiding. In the hollows between pillows there are pools with no outlets, sky-colored, warm as blood, trembling with each drip from above. Your boots never dry. The rock under your soles is warm through the rubber. Steam lifts off in places where the ground is thinnest, and smells faintly of iron and sulfur and hot stone.

You think at first the standing stones are part of it. There are seven of them, scattered with no pattern across the bowl. Too far apart to be a circle, too deliberate to be accident.

They are not rocks.

Up close they are taller than you — three meters, four — slender, slightly leaning, like teeth coming in crooked. No seams, no bolts, no base. They just continue into the raw basalt as if they were grown there. Their surface is matte black that drinks the dim light, except when the mist is thickest, when faint lines of amber crawl up them from base to tip, slow as sap. They hum. Not loudly at first. You feel it in your sternum before you hear it: a low chord that never quite resolves, with a higher shimmer on top that makes your fillings ache.

They make the water. Or they call it. Around each needle the air is visibly wetter — a halo of droplets that hangs, unfallen. Some beads never fall at all. They rise, slowly, from the warm pools toward the needles, then stop midair and tremble, catching the grey light. If you put your hand out, the mist will condense on your arm hairs in seconds, run down in rivulets. The old guide-post at the rim, left by whoever was here before you — a collapsed weather tent, a solar panel gone milky, a cairn with a rusted instrument box — has a hand-painted sign: DRINK SECOND WATER ONLY. Meaning let it fall, let it sit, let the stone-cool do its work. Don't drink what hangs in the air.

Nothing much grows yet. There hasn't been time for soil. That's what makes the green so startling where it does happen: coin-sized cushions of moss in the cracks, acid-bright. Orange bacterial fans rimming the warmest pools. A few grass blades, single and brave, up through cinder. And a low, fleshy pioneer with translucent leaves that holds a bead of water in each leaf like an offering. Step carefully. Most of the bowl is empty black and silver water. The life is spaced out, each patch holding its own territory of emptiness around it.

Which is why the animals are such a shock. There should be nothing here to eat. But the bowl is loud with them.

It starts with the midges — clouds of them, hanging over each pool, glittering when they turn together. Then the frogs answer. Hundreds. Thousands. Little high-altitude tree frogs with throats like lit lanterns, copper and lemon, clinging to the glass ropes, calling so steadily the air vibrates. They were not born here. Nothing is born here yet. They climb up from the cloud forest a thousand meters below every wet season for the warmth and the water and the midges, and they stay until the mist thins.

In the pools, newts — black with vermilion bellies — flicker just under the surface, gorging on drowned midges. On the warm slabs, swarms of flightless crickets click and scrape. And above it all, birds. Swifts and swallows and something larger with a forked tail you can't name, circling the bowl endlessly in the dim, never landing, snatching midges and frogs in the same dive, their wingbeats and cries bouncing off the collar walls until you can't tell what's direct and what's echo. Sometimes a cry arrives a full second before the bird that made it banks into view. The needles hum underneath it all, and the drip — constant drip from stone to pool to stone — keeps a different time.

The rules here are simple and unbeautiful, because the place is still under construction:

The ground is still coming up. Survey stakes left by the last team — thin fiberglass rods — now lean at odd angles, lifted a finger's width each month. Put your palm flat on the cinder and you can feel it, very faintly: a tremor, a pressure upward.

The stone is still cooling. The warm spots move. A pool that was bath-warm in the morning can be cold by night, and a new steam-vent opens ten meters off, sighing.

Water has not learned downhill yet. Without channels, it sheets, it hangs, it rises toward the needles, it falls again. In the early morning, when the needles cycle low — the hum drops until you feel sick — all the suspended droplets fall at once in a soft, bowl-wide patter that startles the frogs silent for a breath. Then they start again, louder.

Don't build. That's what the collapsed tent tells you. Anything heavy sinks, slowly, into the soft between-pillow crust. The needles are the only permanent things, and no one built them to be permanent — they were seeded, or they seeded themselves.

Stay until dark fully comes and the place shows its strangest habit. The amber lines in the needles brighten. The suspended water around them catches that light and holds it. The whole small bowl glows faintly from within its own mist — seven dim lanterns and a thousand frog-throats and the reflected sky-pools — while the peaks above go black and the stars come hard and cold. The swifts keep flying, crying out in the dark. The air stays warm on your face. The rock ticks as it contracts.

It feels less like a valley and more like a mouth, or a womb. Something embryonic. Not eroded but extruded. Not a ruin but a beginning that happens to be loud, wet, crowded with visitors, lit from the inside, waiting for soil, waiting for rivers, waiting to learn what shape it will be when it grows up.
