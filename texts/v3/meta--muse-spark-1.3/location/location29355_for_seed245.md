It is not ground. Not really.

What you are standing on, as far as your feet can tell in the heat and the half-light, is floor. Laid. Made. To the horizon and past it, and under that, more floor.

Hand-sized bricks, hand-pressed. Every one of them still holding the print of a palm, a thumb dragged for keying the mortar. They were never fired right. They are dusky red and liver-brown and ash-grey, swollen with water, warm as fresh bread under your soles. You can feel them give, a millimeter, when you shift your weight. The mortar between them is not mortar — it is the same mud, wetter, squeezed up in little lips. Water stands in the seams.

There is no soil here. There never was. If you pried up a brick — and you could, they come up easy, sucking — you would find another brick below it, turned sideways. And below that another. Down as far as anyone has dug, which is far, it is only bricks, getting hotter and softer until at the bottom they are just warm paste. The whole continent is a thickening. A laying-down that never hit bottom.

This place is low. Not a valley low, a laid-flat low. Surveyors would weep here. For miles the floor holds level within a hand's breadth, and then it sags — a shallow pan where the water gathers in a skin, a slow dome where the bricks have humped up from heat below. There are no hills. There are only mistakes in flatness.

And it is vast. So vast it makes its own weather and then cannot get out from under it.

The light never gets stronger than this: a dim amber dusk, as if the day is stuck inside a cooling kiln. No sun shows, only a brighter smear in the steam where the sun ought to be. The air itself glows faintly from below in places, where cracks between districts vent heat. The sky is low, close enough to feel. It is not cloud, not smoke — it is exhalation. Hot water condensing out of hot air, hanging, dripping. Everything weeps here. The half-built walls weep. The scaffolds weep. The air will bead on your arms and run down without ever raining properly.

Heat first, then wetness. The air is  hot the way a laundry is hot, thick, hard to pull in. Your sweat does not evaporate. It joins the general damp. The bricks steam after the frequent almost-rains — those sudden, warm condensations that fall ten feet from a clear-ish sky and stop mid-air as steam again. The ground hisses softly when that happens, all around you, for acres.

It is scattered country. That is the strangest thing for a made place. You would expect density, a city. Instead: emptiness paved.

Far off — a mile, five miles, the distance smears — a beehive kiln squats, mud-plastered, mouth black and breathing white. Farther off, a scaffold forest: poles of black reed and pale, water-swollen wood lashed with rope, three, four stories high, holding up nothing. A ladder stands alone in the middle of nowhere, leaning against air that used to have a wall. A trough. A pile of unbaked bricks under a mat of woven river-grass, melting back into lumps at the edges. A wooden mallet. A basket. Hand tools, everywhere, and no hands.

Between these things: nothing but the wet shining floor going on until the steam takes it.

There are no plants. Not even weeds in the mortar. What looks from a distance like moss or lichen is mineral — salt whiskers and iron bloom pushing out of the damp bricks in pale rosettes, in rust-red fans. In the coolest seeps there is slag-glass that has grown like frost, thin black needles. It crunches underfoot and then softens again in the heat. Nothing photosynthesizes. Nothing needs to. The world is pre-life or post-life; the bricks have not decided which. Sometimes in the mortar joints you see a slick, oil-colored film tremble and spread a finger's width and stop, as if testing the idea of being alive, then giving up.

No animals. No insects. No birds to read the scale. Your own footprints fill slowly with brown water behind you and stay.

It is quiet. That is wrong, because it is moving all the time.

If you stand long enough and let your eyes unfocus, you see it: the floor breathes. A district a half-mile wide will sink an inch over an hour, water welling up to cover it in a mirror that reflects the dim amber, then settle. Somewhere out of sight, always, a soft, deep *thud-whump* — a scaffold giving up, a kiln wall slumping. No crash. The mud is too wet to crash. Just a tired collapse, swallowed by steam. And soon after, in another direction, a faint sucking, patting sound, as if great invisible hands were laying new bricks in the fog. In the morning — if there were morning — there will be a new low wall where there was flat yesterday, still shiny, fingerprints fresh.

No one builds it now. Or the building builds itself in memory of building.

And because it was never supposed to be this big — no hand-work was ever meant to be continental — reality here is thin at the seams.

Distance is unfinished. Walk toward a kiln and it stays the same size for an hour, then is suddenly beside you, larger inside than it looked. Turn around and your backtrail of filled footprints curves slightly left though you walked straight. Shadows fall toward the heat-glow, not away from the sun-smear. Sometimes rain condenses a foot above the floor and falls *up*, returning to the sky, drop by drop. If you find two bricks with the exact same fingerprint — and you will, if you look — they will be warm to exactly the same degree no matter how far apart they are. Put your ear to one and you hear faint patting in the other.

In places the floor has blistered. Great low domes, fifty paces across, where steam pressure has lifted whole sheets of brickwork. They are hollow and drum-soft. If you climb one, it rocks under you. Under the thin crust you can hear water moving, and something else moving, slow as paste. Do not break them. The one time you saw a broken blister — cracked open like an egg — it was full of perfectly laid miniature bricks, each the size of a fingernail, each with a fingerprint too small for any human hand, still steaming.

The rules here are not written, they are pressed in:

Everything has been touched. If it exists, a hand shaped it.

Nothing ever dries. Drying would mean finished, and nothing here is allowed to finish.

Water remembers level. It will find the same height across miles of floor, seeping, joining, holding. Wade through a pan and you are wading through the same held breath as a pan beyond the horizon.

Do not follow the patting sounds. They recede at the speed you approach. The builders — whatever they were, whenever they were — left only the habit of building.

The storm comes most afternoons, if you can call it storm. No wind, almost. Just the sky lowering until you are inside it, visibility dropping to twenty bricks, the air going white-hot and humming. Condensation runs down every pole in streams. The floor disappears under a half-inch of hot water that reflects nothing. The humming resolves, if you listen, into thousands of tiny ticks: bricks expanding, salt bursting, glass needles growing. Then the sky lifts its edge, a scaffold appears where it shouldn't, closer than it should be, dripping. The steam herds move off like slow animals, low bellies dragging on the floor.

At night — dimmer dim — the cracks vent brighter. Lines of dull orange open for miles, marking old work-days, the boundaries between one laying and the next. From a blister-top you can see the whole pattern: a planet tiled in vast, irregular work-squares, each slightly out of true with the last, stretching beyond seeing. Each square steaming faintly. Each empty.

You are the only animal heat in acres of cooling manufacture. Your breath joins the weeping. Your weight leaves four brief prints that fill and hold, like all the other prints, until the floor overturns them in its slow, turbulent, patient remaking — laying and unlaying, wetting and warming, never eroding because there has not yet been time to erode, only to be made badly and made again.

Primitive mud. Infinite hands. Hot water under everything.

That is the country.
