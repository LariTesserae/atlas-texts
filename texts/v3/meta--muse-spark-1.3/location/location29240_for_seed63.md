It is not big. You can hold the whole of it in one look, and then you spend the rest of the night trying to understand what you looked at.

Up on the high basalt — three thousand meters and change, where the air is thin enough that your heart works a little too hard on the walk in — the lava field levels and then gives up. A collapse. An oval mouth, maybe eighty meters long, fifty across, with a lip of rotten scoria and wind-polished pahoehoe. From a distance under the stars it is nothing. A darker dark. You only know it before you see it because the cold lifts.

Outside is high-desert night: cold that bites the knuckles, wind that finds every seam, stars so sharp they hurt. Inside is heat.

Not warm. Hot. A dry furnace-breath that climbs out of the hole and lays itself over your face. Your sweat dries before it beads. Your lips crack within minutes. The air coming up smells of hot stone and dust and something animal and ammoniac, but desiccated — old, baked, without rot because there is no water here to rot with.

There is no water here at all. No seep, no pool, no damp crack. The rock is thirsty basalt, red-black cinder, ash compacted to tuff, veined here and there with white efflorescence where nitrates bloom out of the stone like frost that will burn your tongue. If you put your hand in a crevice it comes back coated in fine, salt-dry powder. Even your breath feels stolen by the place.

And nothing green. Not a lichen, not a grass tuft at the rim. The heat and the traffic and the sheer dryness forbid it. What grows here is mineral: needle gypsum, crusts of halite, little puffballs of sulfur yellow around the deepest cracks where the mountain exhales. They crunch under boots like insect shells.

You lie on your belly at the rim and look down.

At first you think it is dark all the way down. Light has almost no purchase in the Pit. Starlight shears off at the overhang on the east wall. It touches the far rim, a thin blue line, and falls maybe ten meters before it is swallowed. Below that, your eyes adjust and adjust and find only texture.

Then the texture moves.

The walls are not walls. They are covered. Completely. From the lip down past where you can see, in overlapping shingles, in layers three and four deep in the hollows, are the Clingers — pale, eyeless, waxy crickets the size of the last joint of your thumb. Cream-colored in the starlight, pinkish where the deep heat catches them. No eyes, no wings, only long antennae always working, always tasting the hot dry air. They pack every ledge, every vertical face, every boulder on the talus slope. Where the rock bulges, they bulge with it. Where it cracks, they pour into the crack and fill it. There is no space between them and then there is another one underneath.

The floor, thirty meters down, is not floor. It is a slow gravel of shed skins, dry frass that never molds, broken antennae, wings brought back and dropped — a chitin sand two meters deep that shifts when you stare at it because things are moving through it. Pale snakes, almost translucent, swim it without sound. Centipedes as long as your forearm, rust-red, pick their way over the backs of the living carpet, and the carpet closes behind them without a gap.

This is an old arrangement. The rock is not raw. The talus cones have settled to their angle and stopped. The overhang has been undercut and rounded by a million years of wind and by ten thousand years of this heat, and now it holds. The ledges are worn smooth, not by water — there has never been enough — but by feet. By millions of feet, for generations beyond counting. The place is in balance with itself. It does not collapse. It endures.

And then the air moves, and you remember to look up.

They are coming back. The Returners. All day they are gone — you would never guess this crowded bowl is the source — scattered thin over fifty kilometers of dark sky, hawking high-altitude moths and wind-blown flies over the cold desert. At dusk they pour home. Not in a flock. In a river. Small, soot-dark bats, no bigger than a moth themselves, and with them sooty swifts that have learned to fly by heat, not sight. Thousands per minute funnel through the mouth, wings barely whispering, and vanish into side pockets and lava blisters in the upper walls that you took for shadow.

You don't hear them the way you should. That is the strangest part for how many bodies there are. The Pit should roar. It doesn't. It is mostly quiet. Profoundly, pressingly quiet. The living walls absorb sound the way snow does. Your own breathing is embarrassingly loud. What you do hear, when you hold still long enough, is small and close and dry:

a constant faint skitter, like rice poured on paper, as Clingers adjust;
a soft tick when a pebble loosened by a claw falls and never lands loudly in the deep dust;
a low sigh, once, as a thousand bats turn together in the dark and the warm air sighs with them;
sometimes a thin, high click at the edge of hearing, there and gone, their sonar brushing past you.

No chorus. No chirping — too dry for song, too hot to waste water on sound. They communicate in touch and heat. The whole bowl breathes together. Watch long enough and you see a wave cross the wall: a ripple of antennae lifting, settling, lifting, moving slow around the oval as the mass thermoregulates, opening and closing to vent the heat of their own bodies. The mountain gives heat from below; they give heat from within. Together they keep the Pit at blood-temperature while frost forms on the stones at your elbows.

People have been here. Not many, not now. Scattered, failed attempts. On the north rim three low windbreaks of stacked basalt, built by shepherds or guano-men, now half-fallen. A rusted tin box on a cairn with a rotted offering inside — corn husk, a blue bead. White painted marks, a circle and a line, flaked almost away. Halfway down the south chute, where starlight just reaches, the remains of a ladder: two rails, no rungs, wood bleached silver and snapped. Someone went down for nests or for shit to sell as fertilizer and found only dust that burned the lungs and a floor that moved, and climbed out and never came back. There are no wires, no sensors, no lights. Nothing to maintain a place like this. You either endure it on its terms or you leave.

The rules of the Pit are simple and non-negotiable:

Stay dry. There is nothing to drink and your body will try to give its water to the air. Don't waste it.

Don't expect to be seen. Eyes are useless currency here. Everything navigates by heat-shadow and touch. If you shine a light — don't — the near wall will not scatter. It will shiver and close, millions of backs turning at once, and the light will be eaten.

Don't expect silence to mean stillness. The quiet is full. Under it, eating is constant. The Returners bring the outside world home in their stomachs every night. They void it as dry pellets before dawn. The Clingers eat the pellets, the shed, each other. The snakes eat the Clingers. The centipedes eat everything slow enough. Nothing leaves except wings and dust on the updraft, and nothing green ever gets a foothold to start a different story.

You lie there until the cold on your back and the heat on your face split you in two, until the sky begins to thin in the east and the river of wings thins with it. Below you the packed, seething dark settles a fraction deeper into its cracks to wait out the day. The ripple passes one more time around the bowl, a shudder of pale bodies in the almost-light, and then stillness — if you can call a million held breaths still.

It is small. You could walk around it in ten minutes. And it is full to bursting, hot in the middle of cold, loud with life while making almost no sound, strange not because it breaks the world but because it has made a complete world without the things you thought a world needed: without water, without plants, without light.
