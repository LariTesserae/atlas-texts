High up, where the air should be thin and cold, it is warm instead. A broad saddle of a valley, cupped between blue-grey ridges that never quite come into focus, holds heat like a held breath.

At first you think it is forest. Then you think it is a city. Then you stop trying to decide, because here those are the same thing.

There is nothing here that was not grown.

The ground gives a little under your boots. Not soil. Not rock. A mat — dense, springy, thigh-thick in places — of interlaced roots and runners and fallen, fused leaves, all still alive. It is green-black and olive and pale new-green at the seams. If you kneel and part the moss-hair on its surface you see below it the layered thatch: old leaves become floor, become fiber, become floor again. Nowhere does your finger touch true dirt. Nowhere is there a stone that was not first wrapped, then infiltrated, then replaced from the inside by root. Everything load-bearing is living. The plaza you crossed this morning, wide enough to hold a town meeting, is a single leaf — a leaf the size of a plaza — still attached by a petiole thick as a bridge cable to a trunk half a kilometer away. It does not brown at the edges. It does not tear. When rain pools in its slight central dip, specialized grooves carry the water off to its drip-tip, where it falls in a perpetual curtain to a lower leaf, and then to a lower one.

For this is a sparse place, for all its greenness. That is the first strangeness. Total green, but not crowded.

There are perhaps nine of them. Perhaps eleven. It is hard to count because they are so large your idea of an individual tree breaks. The Great Ones stand far apart across the valley, each a mountain of wood in itself, trunks fluted and buttressed like cathedral walls, bark warm and damp to the touch, shedding water in sheets. Between them is open air — huge, humid, echoing emptiness — and below, the continuous living floor stretching to the horizon. You can stand in the middle of the inter-trunk savanna of air and see for miles in every direction: trunk, far trunk, farther trunk, leaf-roofs floating at different altitudes, nothing else. No underbrush. No thicket. The vegetation does not fill space by crowding it. It fills space by being enormous and few, and by making even emptiness out of itself. The railings along the pathways are living stems. The pathways themselves are fused aerial roots, flattened on top by centuries of soft footfall — though nothing here seems to wear down. The shelters, the cisterns, the stair-ramps that spiral up into the canopy, the bridges that sag impossibly far between two crowns without any cable or nail — all are outgrowths. No cut mark anywhere. No saw, no axe, no joint. Where a wall is needed, two leaves have grown together along their margins and lignified. Where a pipe is needed, a hollow vine has been coaxed to thicken. The whole valley is furnished, roomed, roofed, floored, guttered, and it was all exuded.

And it is wet. Always just-rained, about-to-rain, raining in miniature.

The air is saturated. Your skin never quite dries. Warm vapor condenses on every underside and gathers and falls. Weeping is the right word. The trunks weep. The great leaves weep from a thousand hydathodes along their edges. Even on a bright day with no cloud directly overhead, you walk through a fine, warm drip. Listen: it is loud. A ceaseless, layered percussion — plink on taut leaf-drum, splash into cup-pools, trickle along root-gutters, gurgle down inside the hollow trunks where you can press your ear to the bark and hear rivers falling inside the wood. High above, wind worries the canopy and the whole structure creaks and groans, deep timber sounds, slow as whales, as the giants shift almost imperceptibly to shed weight. Water is near the surface everywhere; press the mat-floor and water wells up around your fingers, clear and tannin-brown and smelling of green tea. In the low spots between root-ridges, shallow marshes have formed — not in soil depressions, but in leaf-basins, acres wide, ankle-deep, full of tadpole shrimp and floating ferns and mirrored sky.

The light is ordinary, and that is odd. Not the green gloom you would expect under such leaves, not dim. A normal, late-morning, warm-white light filters and re-filters through layer after layer of translucent green until it arrives at the floor dappled but full, gold-green and bright enough to read by, to warm your shoulders. And it does not change.

That is the second strangeness, the one you feel in your bones after a few hours: nothing changes here.

You notice first that your shadow has not moved. The sun — you can glimpse it occasionally through a tear in the high canopy, a white coin — stays at the same angle. Mid-morning, forever. The light never sharpens to noon, never slants to evening. The drips fall in the same rhythm from the same leaf-tips. The marsh water neither rises nor falls, though water pours into it constantly. The raw places at the far edge of the valley, where the living mat is still advancing over bare, newborn ridge-rock — pinkish, sharp, unweathered stone that looks poured yesterday, still bubbling faintly with mineral seeps — those raw edges never advance, never retreat. It is a world caught in the act of being made and then held there. Young geology, embryonic, soft at the margins where root-acids are still learning how to dissolve stone and replace it with fiber, but the lesson is paused. Growth equals decay to the exact leaf. Erosion begins and is healed in the same instant. A leaf you marked with a thumbnail yesterday — or was it an hour ago, or a week? time blurs — is still exactly as marked, neither healed nor wilted.

The ecologists would call it equilibrium, but that word is too small. It is stasis with a pulse.

Animals are rare. With so much plant, you expect insects, birds, monkeys — a riot. There is almost nothing. Once a day, perhaps, you will see one of the gliders: huge, slow, frog-like, translucent-skinned things that launch from a high branch and sail for half a kilometer on flaps of skin between elongated fingers, landing with a soft splat on another trunk to lick salt and algae from the bark. In the leaf-basins live tiny black frogs that never croak, only click. In the hollow trunks, something large breathes at night — or what would be night if night came — a slow in-out you can feel through the wood, but you never see it. The loudness here is not animal. It is hydraulic and vegetative: drip, run, creak, sigh, the pop of air bubbles in xylem as wide as your torso, the soft boom when a giant water-filled flower — some as big as houses, waxy white and pink, nodding on thick stalks in the open air between trunks — opens a valve and vents perfume and warm mist.

Smell everything: crushed leaf, warm sap like maple and blood, wet bark, ozone from the constant evaporation, the sweet rot-that-never-rots of the floor.

The rules of this place, as you learn them by walking:

1. Everything you stand on is alive. Step gently, not from morality but from architecture. The floor knows your weight and redistributes it.

2. Water does not leave. It cycles — leaf to air to leaf to trunk to leaf — but the valley drinks its own rain. No river exits through the ridges. What falls here, stays here, forever falling.

3. Nothing is built, and everything is made. If you need a bowl, you do not carve one. You find a young cup-leaf and sleep near it and breathe on it and over days it will cup deeper. If you need shelter, you wait under a leaf that is already lowering itself to become a roof. There are no tools because tools would imply dead material. There is no dead material.

4. Do not wait for change. The seedling at the raw edge will not be a tree tomorrow. The drip from the plaza-leaf will fall in the same place when you return. The light will be the same. You will be warmer, wetter, louder inside your own head from the constant sound, but the valley will not have moved.

It is a town-sized organism that decided to be a valley. A cathedral grown by blind, wet, patient giants who spaced themselves far apart so each could catch the sun, and then filled the space between with their own bodies thinned to floor and bridge and roof. Warm in the heights. Wet without river. Loud without crowd. Finished without ever having been started, and starting without ever finishing.
