You climb for days and it never gets steeper.

That is the first strangeness of this high country. You expect a mountain to declare itself — to sharpen, to thin, to go cold and stony. This one does the opposite. It rises and rises and then gives up on rising. It softens. The slopes lengthen until you can't tell slope from flat. The rocks lose their edges. The air stays warm.

You are very high up and you know it only by your lungs, by the way breath comes a little short, by the way clouds are not above you but around your knees. Below, somewhere far below that you will never see again, the lowlands must fall away. Up here there is only the Roof.

The Roof is not made of stone. It is made of living things on top of living things.

---

The ground here is a misunderstanding. There is stone somewhere underneath, the old men of stone, so ancient and tired they have slumped into low swells like sleeping animals. Erosion has had so long to work here that it has finished its work and retired. No cliffs. No scree. No sharpness anywhere. Even the boulders you find half-buried in the mat are soft — soapstone-soft, rotted through with rain and roots until you can gouge them with a thumbnail and they crumble into red-yellow clay that stains your hands for days.

But you will rarely touch stone. You walk on plants.

The substrate is a felt, meters deep, kilometers wide: moss upon peat upon humus upon moss again, springy, saturated, sighing under your weight and rebounding slowly after you pass. Your footprint fills with water within seconds. Everywhere water is near the surface, pressing up from below, condensing from above. Squeeze a handful of seemingly solid ground and it weeps.

Nothing here is single. A tree is not a tree but a neighborhood. Its trunk is furred in moss two hands thick, its bark is a garden of ferns and orchids and liverworts, its branches hold ponds in their crotches — small black-water pools cupped in bromeliads big as bathtubs, where tadpoles live their whole lives without ever touching the earth. Its roots are not in soil; they hang in the air, a curtain of red-brown hair drinking the mist. And on those roots grow other roots. On those leaves grow other leaves. If you peel back one layer you find another green, and under that another, until the idea of "ground" or "tree" dissolves and there is only mass, total and continuous.

They say — whoever *they* were, long ago — that you could walk from one edge of this plateau to the other, a journey of months, and never once step on anything dead. Everything underfoot breathes.

---

The light never gets bright.

The sun is a rumor. A paler smudge that moves somewhere behind the permanent low sky. The Roof makes its own weather, vast enough to do so: warm air rising from the sodden mat meets the cool high air and thickens into cloud that never lifts. So you live inside a cloud. Morning and noon and evening are variations of grey-green gloom, a dim aquarium light filtered through ten layers of canopy. There are no shadows here. Things do not cast; they glow faintly at the edges with wet.

And yet it is warm. A bathhouse warmth. Sweat does not evaporate; it beads and stays, mixing with mist on your skin. Your clothes, your hair, are always damp. Everything drips. Rocks weep. Leaves tip and let go of their gathered water in slow, deliberate drops. The air itself, if you stand still, will condense on your eyelashes.

Water does not flow here the way it does elsewhere. There are no rapids, no falls. The rivers are in no hurry — they have forgotten hurry over geological time. They spread instead of run: shallow, tea-dark sheets sliding inches a day through moss channels, pooling into marshes that are neither lake nor land. You hear water more than you see it: a seep, a suck, a plink from leaf to leaf to leaf, down a hundred green stairs.

Sometimes, in the stillest hollows, the mist does not fall at all. It hangs. Droplets suspend, glittering in the dim, neither rising nor settling, until a passing animal brushes them and they shatter.

---

It is quiet. That is what unnerves newcomers first, more than the green or the wet.

A forest this full should be loud. And it *is* full — dense beyond counting, layered beyond mapping. In any cubic breath of air there are presences: the moss-midges in their clouds, the glass frogs pulsing their throats without sound, the pale moths the size of your hand moving like ash. Above, things glide between crowns without flapping. Below, things rustle without breaking cover.

But the mat eats sound. Your footsteps make only a soft *chuff-chuff*. Your voice falls flat after a few paces, absorbed by wet felt and hanging hair-roots. Even the animals have learned quiet. The birds here seldom sing; instead they flash — throat sacs inflating with sudden color, blue to orange, a visual song in the gloom. The big amphibians in the crotch-pools communicate in ripples. The mammals — slow, shaggy, algae-furred climbers that hang for days in the same fork, and the smaller, blind, velvet-nosed rooters that tunnel through peat — move at a pace that makes no noise at all.

If you sit for an hour and hold your own breath, you will start to hear it: not silence, but smallness. Drip. Suck. The *tick* of a seed pod opening somewhere high. The long, low exhalation of wind moving through a valley a mile away, arriving late and tired. Once, very rarely, a deep boom from far off that you feel in your sternum more than hear — the sound of a giant emergent tree, centuries old, finally giving way and settling, slowly, into the mat, over the course of an afternoon.

Everything here happens slowly. Leaves take years to fall — they yellow at the edges, then brown spot by spot, then hang for another season before letting go, and then take a decade to rot, nursed by fungi. The rivers migrate by a hand's width in a lifetime. The hills, those old sleeping animals, breathe almost imperceptibly: in the wet season the whole Roof swells a finger's breadth higher as the sponge fills; in the drier weeks it sighs down again.

---

You will find houses here, after a fashion. Not built. Grown, or left.

Here and there in clearings where the mat domes a little higher, there are hollows like inverted nests, woven from living stems that have been coaxed and bent over generations — too regular to be accident, too seamless to be carpentry. No tools made them; no tools remain. Inside, the floor is dry moss, and the walls drip less than outside. Something made them to sleep in, to lay eggs in, to wait out the heaviest mists. Some are fresh, the stems still green and pliable at the bend. Some are old, collapsed, already being unmade by the forest that mothers them.

And rarer still: stones set upright. Three, four in a circle, waist-high, leaning inward like tired conspirators, bearded in lichen so thick their original faces are gone. No carving. No door. No roof. You cannot tell if they were altars or markers or simply a game played by something with time and hands, long ago, before it left or became something else. The forest does not care. It is already growing over them, root by root, softening them back into hill.

There is no metal here. No glass. No cut timber. If people ever brought tools to the Roof, the Roof has digested them.

The rule of this place, if you stay long enough to learn it, is this:

Nothing sharp survives. Water never leaves, it only pauses. Everything lives on something else living. And to endure, be slow, be soft, be quiet, and grow over whatever came before.

If you stand on the highest swell on a clear — clear for here, meaning you can see fifty paces — and look out, you will not see an edge. You will see green going to grey going to white, layer folding into layer, canopy into mist into canopy again, to the curve of the world. Weather is being born out there, a rainhead darkening a mile off, moving toward you at the speed of thought in a dream. You have time to sit down. You have time to wring out your shirt, to watch a frog pulse green-gold on a leaf, to listen to the mat breathe under you.

The rain will come. It always comes. It will not drum or lash. It will settle, like everything else, softly, endlessly, into the sponge of the world, and the world will take it without a sound.
