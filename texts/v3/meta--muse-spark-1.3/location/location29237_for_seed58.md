You find it by heat first, then by quiet.

From the flat pan outside — cracked clay, pale dust, nothing for a long way — there is a square cut in the earth, sharp-cornered as if made yesterday. Which, geologically, it was. The walls drop eighty meters straight down, fresh rock still showing drill stars and blasting feathers, basalt and sandstone not yet rounded by wind. The edges are too crisp. Rebar sticks out where a road was sheared in half. A guardrail ends in mid-air. Up here the light is white and brutal. Down there, it is something else.

The town is down there.

It does not sprawl. It cannot. It fills the bottom of the pit wall-to-wall, hamlet-sized, a single dense knot you could shout across. From the rim you can see the whole of it at once: a terracing of flat-roofed rooms, sheds, catwalks, cisterns, boiler houses, clinging and stacking and bridging until there is almost no empty air left. Corrugated tin, poured concrete gone pink with heat, mud-brick baked hard, black iron pipe, copper gone green. Everything touches everything else. Awnings over balconies over stairwells over engine beds. Ladders become roofs become gutters become walls. Cables hang in festoons thick as vines — power cable, guy-wire, condensate line, clothesline with nothing on it — layered five, six deep, sagging under their own weight.

Nothing reaches the top. The three stub chimneys, the derrick, the ventilator stack all stop ten meters short of the rim, exhaling their heat into the pit where it stays. This is why it is hot. At noon a single shaft of sun stands straight down for twenty minutes and then is gone, and for the rest of the day the bottom lives in its own blue-brown shade, lit amber by work lamps left burning around the clock. The metal handrails are too hot to hold bare. The air shimmers over the engine house roof even in shadow. You sweat before you start descending, and by the third switchback your shirt is stuck to you.

And it sweats too. That is the water here — not a river, not a well, but dampness that comes and goes.

Heat lifts what little water pools on the cracked sump floor into the air. Up against the upper walls and the underside of every shade-cloth and cold-water pipe, it beads. Condensation. Thousands of beads gathering, fattening, breaking. The whole town drips. Gutters tick. Buckets set under pipe elbows fill by the finger-width. Moss grows in a green-black line exactly where the drip falls and nowhere else. Twice in a summer, the story goes, a real rain comes across the pan, spills over the sharp rim in thin temporary waterfalls that hiss to steam halfway down, ponds for an hour in the low concrete basins, and is gone — pumped, evaporated, drunk by the stone. The rest of the year, life is lived off the sweat of walls.

It is very quiet for a place built of engines.

There is a power house at the lowest point, brick, with its doors always open, two big flywheels turning slow and steady inside. A pump house beside it, a machine shop, a tangle of condensers that look like church organs. They are all running. You can feel them through your boots more than hear them — a low sixty-cycle hum in the molars, a sigh when a pressure valve lifts and vents white for thirty seconds and then reseats itself. Otherwise: drip, tick, creak. The tick of tin expanding and contracting. The plink of water into metal. Your own footsteps too loud on the catwalk grating. Sound does not carry out of the pit; the fresh-cut walls swallow it. A dropped wrench rings once and stops.

What is strangest, after a day, is what is not there.

No pigeons on the ledges. No swallows over the shaft. No rats in the culverts, no lizards on the hot concrete, no flies around the sump pools despite the damp, no cockroaches under the boilers. Not even ants in a line along a pipe. A town this dense should crawl. This one doesn't. Whether it is the heat, the faint sour metal in the condensate, the constant low vibration, no one can say. The absence accumulates. Dead leaves — from what? — collect in corners and are not eaten. Dust settles undisturbed.

Because there is growth, just barely. Sparse, stubborn, arranged entirely around water and shade. Black crust-moss on the north-facing walls where the drip never quite dries. A rust-red lichen that seems to like hot iron. In the shadow of the cisterns, a clump of pipe-ferns, fronds pale and translucent, roots sunk into burlap wrapped around a leaking joint. Along one alley where two condensate lines sweat all day, a row of saltworts, stunted, gray-green, leaves thick as thumbs. They have learned to live without pollinators — they split, they spore, they send runners along a wet seam. They do not spread beyond the wet line by a centimeter. Step back and the stone is bare again, fresh and sharp and glittering with new fracture.

The place is young. You can tell. The rockfall blocks at the base still have knife edges. The concrete spall is still angular, not yet worn smooth by feet. Paint blisters but has not yet peeled away in long histories. Rust blooms bright orange, first generation, not yet brown. Even the town feels first generation — additions riveted onto additions without plan but with a strict local logic: keep the shade, keep the drip, keep the air moving along the floor.

That moving air is the rule here. Everyone who lives — and someone does live, laundry strung under a catwalk, a kettle on a waste-heat plate, a chair dragged to where a crack in the buildings funnels a breath — everyone learns the three rules fast:

Stay low. Hot air pools, but the machines push a slow, cooler current along the sump floor if you don't block it.

Don't touch bare metal after ten. Wood handle, rag, glove.

Harvest, don't take. You put your cup under the communal drip lines, you don't tap the line. The water that keeps the moss alive keeps you alive.

In the late afternoon, when the amber lamps look brightest against the blue shade, the whole pit does something you feel before you see: as the desert above cools a degree, the dense hot air below sighs upward all at once, carrying dust and steam in a slow column that almost — almost — reaches the rim before it collapses back down. For a minute the hanging cables sway, the tin ticks in chorus, the beads of condensation tremble and fall together. A small rain inside the earth. Then it settles. The flywheels turn. The pumps thump. The walls sweat again, drop by drop, in the dim, dense, airless town at the bottom of the fresh wound, busy and nearly silent, with no other living thing to witness it but the ferns and whoever is tending the kettle.
