The first thing is the air, how little of it there is.

You come up over the ridge — hands on cold, lichen-rough rock, lungs working harder than the slope warrants — and the world drops away on all sides. That's the elevation. Not a mountain so much as the idea of up, carried too far. Serrated white and grey teeth to every horizon, and below, a blue depth that has no bottom, only haze. The sky is not above you here. It is around you. Huge. Almost nothing in it.

And then, scooped out of the lee of the summit tor like a spoon taken from stone, the hollow.

It is small enough to hold in one look. Sixty, seventy strides across, bounded on three sides by a broken wall of dark rock and open on the fourth to the void. You could walk its rim in ten minutes if the air let you. That smallness is what makes it possible at all. The ridge takes the worst of the wind and curls it over, the way a hand curls over a candle. Inside that curl, something has been allowed to settle.

The rock itself is old. Not shattered-new, not sliding. Weathered into rounded shoulders and long, settled slabs, blackened with patina and split by frost long ago into forms that have since stopped moving. The cirque has found its shape and kept it. Talus locked with moss. Old snow patched in the high shadow, not a glacier, just a stubborn remnant that feeds everything below it drop by drop.

Because everything weeps here.

It rained an hour ago — one of those fast, cold mountain showers that comes up from nothing and leaves as quickly — and the place is still draining it, and draining the melt besides. Water is not in a river here. There is not enough flat for that. It is in film and thread. It beads on the undersides of overhangs and falls in slow, bright ticks. It seeps sideways out of the rock face in dark damp tongues where the moss is black-green and saturated. It moves underfoot. The whole floor of the hollow is a low sponge of cushion plants and peat, and each step sinks half an inch and sighs water up around your boot. Near the center, where the ground dips, it gathers into a tarn no bigger than a room — clear, black, sky-colored, rimmed with sedge and cotton-bright heads of alpine cottongrass. You can see the bottom: grit, a few fallen petals, the skeletons of midges.

And it is lush. Absurdly lush for this high. That is the shelter and the water and the third thing: dung. Life has manured this bowl for centuries. The low mat is dense — moss campion in tight green domes, starry saxifrage white against wet black stone, dwarf willow no higher than your ankle but spreading wide, rosettes of houseleek gone red at the edges from cold sun. In the dampest seam there are ferns, stunted but true ferns, unfurling close to the rock where the seep never dries. Everything grows sideways, pressed down by wind and snow, but it grows thick. If you kneel you smell it: peat and green crush and cold mineral water.

The light is ordinary daylight, but up here ordinary feels scoured. Sun white-gold on the wet, shadow blue-black under the tor. No haze in the hollow itself, only below and above. Condensation is still coming off the rocks as they warm — a faint breathing-off of mist that rises ankle-high and is torn away the instant it lifts above the rim.

There is almost nothing built, and what is, is barely built. A low, collapsed dry-stone windbreak on the eastern edge, three courses high, cupped to shelter something the size of a sitting person. A cairn of flat slates stacked without mortar, topped with a bleached stick wrapped in faded cloth now rotted to threads — prayer flag or trail mark, impossible to tell. A few smaller cairns along the rim, waymarkers for a path that no longer needs marking. A shallow overhang has a soot-blackened back wall and a ring of stones for a fire long dead, with a split bird bone and a flake of chipped chert still in the ash. Hand work. No metal. Someone came up here light, stayed briefly, and left stone arranged to remember wind by.

No one is here now. In human terms it is empty.

In every other term it is crowded and loud.

The air over the tarn shimmers — not with heat, but with insects. Midges and black flies and copper-bright mountain bees working the brief flower glut, so many that their hum is a constant wire in the air. Pikas whistle from the talus, short, indignant cuts of sound, darting with mouthfuls of green to their haypiles under the slabs. A family of marmots has taken the sunniest rubble; you hear them before you see them, a rolling chuck and the scrabble of claws. And the birds own the void.

Choughs, red-beaked and screaming, ride the updraft off the open side, tumbling and righting themselves inches from rock. Alpine swifts cut straight through the hollow at impossible speed, their scythe cries echoing. They nest in the tor cracks above the seep. Every few minutes one whole section of the hollow erupts — pikas diving, bees lifting — as a shadow crosses: a golden eagle circling out over the emptiness, riding with almost no wingbeat, watching.

So the sound never stops, and the bowl makes it more. That is the small strangeness here, the unusual rule: the hollow holds sound a half-second too long. The back wall is curved just so, wet rock faced with moss, and it throws everything back at you layered. A pika's whistle comes, and then itself again, fainter. Dripping water ticks and ticks again from a different direction. Wind goes over the top with a low organ roar — the loudest thing, a continuous tearing rush — but inside, under it, you hear the small amplified wet chorus: drip, hum, cheep, rustle, your own breath loud in your ears from the thin air.

The rule of this place is: move slowly, stay low, drink only where it runs clear off rock, not where it stands. The weather will turn twice before afternoon. Clouds are already building again over the next peak, white towers going grey at the base. Temperature hangs in that cool knife-edge — sun hot on the back of your neck when the wind drops, chill immediately wicking up from the wet ground through your knees, your breath just visible if you exhale toward shade. Everything living here is working fast against the short season. The eagle is hunting. The pikas are cutting. The flowers are open all at once, white and yellow and violent pink, because there may be only three weeks.

If you stand at the open edge and look out, there is nothing between you and the far ranges but bright, hollow air. Vast space. Near-void. The bees, the birds, the moss, the tarn — all of this vivid, wet, noisy life — exists as a single green coin balanced on the edge of that emptiness, kept alive because the rock cupped just enough water and broke just enough wind, and life, once it found it, refused to leave.
