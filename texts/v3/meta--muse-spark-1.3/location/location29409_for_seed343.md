On first sight it is empty.

You come up out of the lowlands through a day of switchbacks and then there is no more up. Just a rim of pale stone and then a flatness so wide your eyes keep trying to find something to hold. The High Moss — no one who lives up here calls it that, but that is what it looks like from the edge — runs to a horizon that shivers in warm air. Extreme altitude and you expect cold, snow, a bite. Instead the light is ordinary noonday light, gold-white, and the air is warm against your skin, thin enough that your breath comes a little short on the climb, thin enough that sound carries too far.

It looks near-void. A table of weathered rock with nothing on it. A few low humps. Distance without measure.

Then your boot sinks half an inch and you understand that what you took for rock is not rock.

The whole plateau is covered. Totally. There is no exposed soil, no bare scree up here except at the very rim where the wind has gnawed it back. From cliff-edge to far blue edge, the ground is plant. Not forest, not meadow — substrate. A felted, interwoven skin many feet thick, made of thousands of things grown into one another: cushion mosses the color of old copper, grey-green lichens in cartwheel rosettes as wide as tables, dwarf creepers with waxy leaves, hollow reed-grass that grows sideways instead of up, bladderworts swollen with air. You can peel it back with your hands — the herders do, for fiber — and it comes up in layers like thatch, like cloth, like meat. Underneath: ancient stone ground down to flour.

This is a very old place. The rock beneath, when you find it in a cutbank or a footwell, is not jagged. It is primordial in the way of things that have finished becoming. Granite and limestone dissolved and rounded and re-settled over uncountable rains until all edges are gone. Boulders like sleeping animals half-buried in green felt. Shallow pans where nothing drains. The plateau was once mountains; now the mountains have agreed to be a table. Even the undulations are soft — long swells and settles, ten feet from trough to crest over half a mile, as if the land breathed once and then held it.

What makes it strange, faintly wrong in a way you feel in your soles before you understand it, is that it is hollow.

The ancient erosion did not only flatten. It honeycombed. Water, over deep time, found the limestone seams and ate them out, leaving caverns and throats and vertical shafts fifty, a hundred feet down, now bridged over by root and moss. The living mat is a roof. Walk, and in places your step returns a drum-sound. Tap with a herding staff and you hear emptiness boom back. In other places the skin has torn — breathing holes, the locals call them — round mouths three to twenty feet across where the mat curls back on itself like a lip, showing dark wet shafts below. Cold air sighs out of them at noon. Warm air is sucked in at dusk. The whole plateau respirates.

And because it is hollow, it holds water without showing it.

There is no lake up here, no river. On the scale of pocket-damp. Mornings come in white. Condensation pours off the high air at night and the moss drinks until by dawn every leaf is beaded, every hollow reed drips, every low pan holds a temporary mirror an inch deep that will be gone by ten. An occasional rain — brief, warm, slouching across the flat in grey veils — hammers the felt to saturation for an hour and then vanishes down the breathing holes in gurgles and waterfalls you can hear echoing far below. If you are thirsty, you do not look for streams. You squeeze moss. You lift the skirts of the big cup-lichens where they keep a shot-glass of clear water each. You listen for the drip-shafts. Life here is not built around abundance of water but around its capture and delay.

By midmorning the mirrors evaporate and the light turns ordinary again and then — the sound begins.

You had thought it empty because you were listening with lowland ears. Up here density is low if you count trunks and bodies and stones. It is overwhelming if you count wings.

The air fills. First a thin whine, then a layering: midges in columns like smoke over every breathing hole, thrips and jewel-flies glittering, grasshoppers the length of your thumb launching from the reed-grass with a click that multiplies until the ground itself seems to crackle. Then the birds that eat them — not dozens, thousands — swifts and swallows and whiteback kites pouring up out of the shafts where they roost in the dark, circling in tornadoes so dense they shadow the moss. Then higher, the grazers: skeins of long-necked geese and whistling ducks moving between pans, landing with a thunder of water even where there is almost no water. At ground level, under the mat edge, things rustle constantly: burrowing cavies, blind mole-rats fat on roots, snakes patterned exactly like lichen.

And it is loud. Deafening at peak. Not city-loud, but living-loud, total. The hollow reeds are instruments. Wind moving across the plateau — and wind always moves up here — plays across their cut tips and broken stems, a million tiny flutes slightly out of tune, a reedy wheeze underneath everything. On top of that the insect chorus, a high electric sawing that gets into your teeth, and the wingbeat roar when a flock wheels low, and the odd deep boom when a slab of mat settles over a void. You have to shout to be heard ten feet away at noon. The old herders wear ear-plugs of rolled felt. At night it drops, but never to quiet; the shafts tick and drip and something down in the dark chirps endlessly.

The plants are architecture because they have to be. Nothing else to build with, and no need for much. Scattered across the openness — so far apart they emphasize the emptiness — are human marks, all hand-made, all low.

A sheepfold: a ring of stacked flat stones, waist-high, with a gate of woven living reed still growing. A lookout: four poles of bundled hollow stem lashed with fiber, topped with a platform of moss-turf where a boy sits all day watching flocks of dun, short-wooled high sheep that graze the mat without breaking it, knowing by foot where it is safe. A cairn at a trail fork: ancient stones rounded like eggs, each tied with a strip of cloth that snaps in wind. A hut, only a few across the whole region: half-sunk, walls of cut turf blocks, roof of living moss that you have to trim with a bone knife so it does not seal the smoke hole. Inside: a fire pit, a loom of bone and wood, baskets, a hand-axe of chipped stone with a polished edge, coils of fiber drying. No metal except a rare copper awl traded up from below, treasured, re-sharpened to a needle.

The tech is hands. Everything is cut, twisted, knotted, felted, stacked. Ladders of knotted fiber go down into the upper shafts to collect guano and cold water and the blind white crickets the size of mice that the children roast. Nets of fine thread strung between reed poles to harvest midges by the sackful for cakes. Bone whistles to call sheep back from dangerous thin-mat areas — though half the time the whistle is lost under the general roar.

The rule of the world up here is simple and does not forgive forgetting:

Stay on the thick mat. Test with the staff. The green that looks most lush over a shaft is the thinnest, fed by the updraft damp.

Drink morning. By afternoon the cups are empty and the shafts are the only water, and going down costs time.

Make noise to be found, be silent to find. Predators listen. The striped shaft-fox hunts by the vibration of your steps through the felt. The great mottled harrier hunts by the silence between insect waves. The sheep know to freeze when the chorus dips.

Give back weight. Cut turf only where the mat is three layers deep, and lay a stone in the hole so the wind does not start a tear. A tear, left, widens by yards in a season, and a whole pasture can slump whistling into the dark.

At dusk the light stays normal a long time — clear, level, ambering — and then drops fast. The warm air cools quickly. Fog pours up out of the breathing holes in slow columns, smelling of wet stone, resin, crushed green, guano, humus a thousand years deep. The flocks pour back down into the earth to roost, screaming, wing on wing, until the sky empties as suddenly as it filled. The reeds go quiet as dew weights them. For an hour the plateau is what it pretended to be in the first glance: vast, hollow, empty, a living skin stretched over ancient bone, holding its breath under ordinary stars, waiting for morning condensation to begin the deafening day again.
