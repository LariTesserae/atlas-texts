You come over the lip late in the day, when the light is already failing, and you find it all at once because there is nowhere else for it to be.

A small cirque, no wider than a hamlet, cupped on three sides by fresh rock. The walls are too young to have learned softness. They are plucked and broken, black-grey, sharp as poured glass where the last ice let go. No scree slopes yet, no smoothing — just raw blocks tipped at angles, their edges still bright where they split. Above them, the ridge cuts a thin line against a low, milk-blue sky. The sun never clears it here. It just drags around behind the stone and leaves this place in a permanent blue dusk.

The air is thin and cold enough to ache. Your breath stays. Every inhale feels a little unfinished.

And at the bottom of the bowl, packed rim to rim between the cliffs, is the town that is not a town.

At first you think it is moss. Then you think it is houses covered in moss. Then, as your eyes adjust to the dim, you understand it is only plants, but behaving like houses.

Everything is living and everything is full. There is no soil to see, no bare stone on the floor, no gap. Sphagnum and liverwort and dwarf willow and cushion saxifrage have knitted into a single, saturated mat a meter thick, and out of that mat they have raised themselves into walls and domes and corridors. It is dense beyond walking-around: a layered construction, street over roof over root.

You have to crouch to read it. The "streets" are narrow runnels, willow stems bent low and grafted by wind into arches, their tiny polished leaves overlapping like shingles. Between them rise hummocks the size of huts, perfect round moss domes, tight and springy, each crowned with a cushion plant pulled taut as a roof-skin. Their sides weep. Water is everywhere here, not as a lake or a stream but as nearness — seeping out of the cliffs in black threads, beading under every leaf, pooling in every footprint before you even lift your boot. The whole borough glistens. In the cold, half of it is ice: needle ice lifting the moss in silver combs, drip icicles under every arch, frost feathers growing out of the living walls.

Nothing here has been cut. That is the first rule you feel. No blade, no timber, no dead wood, no device of any kind. What looks like thatch is still growing. What looks like a gutter is a living groove in a liverwort bank, guiding meltwater from roof to runnel to a central marsh-eye that never freezes quite solid. What looks like a plaza is just a place where three drainage ways meet and the mosses have grown low and flat to let water pass, forming a springy green floor that gives under your weight and sighs water up around your boots.

The second rule is that water decides. Every passage slopes a finger's width toward damp. If you follow any tunnel — and you must go on hands and knees, the willow vaults are only shoulder-high — it will lead you downhill to wetter and wetter growth, until the stems open and you are looking at black water cupped in moss, sky reflected dim. Then the passage branches back uphill to drier cushions. It is infrastructure, unmistakably: circulation, drainage, load-bearing. Only it was never built. It was allowed.

Light does not reach straight. It filters through cloud and cliff-shadow and then through leaves, so even at midday it is the color of deep water. The greens are almost black. The reds of the sphagnum tips glow like embers when you get close. Condensation hangs in the little streets as a low mist that never lifts, because the plants breathe and the cold catches it. Droplets form on every hair and stem and fall with soft, irregular ticks.

It is very quiet. The moss eats sound. Your own steps are muffled to a rubbery compression. What you hear, when you stay still long enough for the place to forget you, is water work: seep-ticking off rock, a faint gurgle under the mat where an unseen runnel moves, the occasional sharp *pink* of ice fracturing as a dome settles in frost-heave. Once in a long while a low creak as willow wood, thick as a thumb but decades old, adjusts to snow weight. Wind passes over the top of the cirque and does not come down.

Almost nothing moves in it that is not plant. That is the strangest, most town-like thing — it is fully occupied and empty at once. No birds nest here; too low, too wet. You might see a single white ptarmigan track stitched across a roof, gone by morning melt. If you part the moss with cold fingers you will find mites, springtails, a beetle the size of a seed, all slow with cold. They are tenants, not citizens. The citizens are the plants themselves, creeping at glacial pace but never stopping: this week a shoot inches across a doorway and narrows it, next thaw a cushion swells and closes an alley and a new one opens two feet over where water found a faster line. It is active, restless in a plant way. Freeze by night, lift and crack; thaw by pallid day, slump and flow and knit again.

Stay until your knees are wet through and your fingers numb, and the pattern resolves: lower town of drowned mosses around the marsh-eye, middle town of willow tunnels and leaf-shingled lanes, upper town of dry lichen-crusted cushions leaning against the weeping cliffs where the rock sweats even in frost. Layer on layer, no space wasted, a hamlet you could cross in forty steps if you could walk straight, which you cannot.

From the ridge again, looking back, it does not look built at all. It looks like what it is — a dense, wet, cold green knot, holding warmth it does not have, holding shape against young stone. A place that learned the shape of shelter without ever learning tools.
