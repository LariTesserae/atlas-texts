You come up through the hatch because there is no other way in.

A vertical steel ladder, cold enough to ache through gloves, up sixty rungs inside a concrete throat, and then a square of grey light and the hatch coaming at knee height. You duck, shoulder through, and stand up too fast and hit your hardhat on a pipe.

This is the place. All of it. There is nowhere else.

A room, maybe five paces long by four wide, low-ceilinged, buried high in the works. Not on the ground. *High.* You felt it in your ears on the climb. Through the vent slit on the east wall you can see nothing but falling air and a far, far valley floor with a river like a wire — firs, a road switchbacking, cloud shadow moving fast. The wind up here finds the slit and whistles thin, and then is torn away by what's inside.

Everything is made. Floor, walls, ceiling, doorless doorways, even the drain. There is no rock showing, no dirt. Poured concrete with the grain of the formwork still in it, sharp arrises where the forms were stripped not long ago. Checker-plate steel over a trench. Galvanized pipe racks, conduit, junction boxes. Paint still glossy on the big valve wheel where hands haven't worn it. Fresh weld beads like caterpillars on the new bypass line, and where the concrete was cored last spring the edge is still knife-sharp, dust not yet rounded. It is a young room. New, and already wet.

Water is everywhere, and it should not be, and it always is.

It weeps out of the uphill wall — not a leak, exactly, the mountain pushing through. The concrete sweats. Beads gather on the ceiling pipes, on the underside of cable tray, on the caged bulbs, and fall. A constant ticking rain indoors. The floor never dries. A shallow sheet, a millimeter deep, moves always toward the trench drain along the west side, wrinkling under the vibration, carrying oil in rainbow skins toward the sump grate. In the trench itself a proper stream runs, clear and furiously cold, two inches deep over steel, talking under the roar.

Outside the slit, the air is condensing. Cloud is in the room half the day — it comes sideways, piles against the intakes, forces its way in and beads on everything. Your jacket darkens at the shoulders in a minute. Your breath shows. The metal tastes of cold when you breathe near it.

Dim, even at noon. Two bulkhead lights with wire guards, one dead, one brown-yellow and flickering with the voltage sag whenever the No.2 pump kicks. A red EXIT-type lamp over the hatch that is always on. The corners stay black. The big pipes throw shadows that move because the light filament is vibrating. You learn to work by feel: hand along the cold handrail, boot finding the raised edge of the plate by sole.

And the sound. You were warned, but warning does not prepare the body.

Beneath your feet, three feet below the plate, the penstock goes past — a two-meter steel main carrying the whole upper reservoir down to the turbines. You are standing astride a river in a pipe. The roar comes up through the steel and through the concrete and through the soles of your boots and into your sternum. On top of it the whine of the sump pumps, the 60-cycle snarl of the transformer cabinet in the corner, the rattle of a loose chain on its hook that never stops shaking. It is not loud like a concert is loud. It is pressure. You feel your sinuses buzz. You shout to the man next to you and see your own voice vanish — lip-reading only. When the spill valve cycles every eleven minutes, the pitch drops half an octave and the floor kicks, and water in the trench jumps, and the weeping from the wall increases for a time, as if the mountain flinched.

Nothing lives here. That is the first strange normal thing. No mice, no pigeons in the vent despite the opening, no spiders in the tray, no flies around the sump. Too cold, too loud, too wet, too high. The vibration alone would drive anything with a heartbeat out. What grows does so without permission and without company.

Sparse, scattered, low to the crack: a smear of black-green algae in the mortar line where the wall meets floor, always damp. A cushion of moss, no bigger than a thumbprint, in the lee of a conduit clamp where drip is steady but not scouring. In the trench, where light from the slit strikes for an hour a day, a film of orange bacteria on submerged steel — rust-eaters, thriving on the fresh metal. No soil. They root in concrete pore-water and condensation. You could count the living patches on two hands and have fingers left.

The machines are spaced out as if the room is holding its breath. Northeast: the bypass riser and its big red wheel. Southeast: the grey transformer cabinet, humming, warm to the touch on one side — the only warm thing in the room — with DANGER 4160V stenciled and a log sheet in a plastic sleeve gone milky with moisture. Northwest: the duty sump pump on its plinth, cycling on float, coughing, spitting water up and out to the spill. Southwest: nothing but a pallet with oil-absorbent pads, a bucket, a coiled hose. Much empty air between them. You can stretch your arms and touch nothing. The emptiness is part of the function — clearance, swing radius, splash zone.

It is always doing something. Float rises, contactor slams with an audible *clack* you feel more than hear, pump roars higher than the roar, trench level drops an inch, pump stops, water rises again. Cloud pushes in, visibility to three feet, then clears. Drip, run, evaporate on the warm cabinet, condense again on the cold pipe. A cycle every few minutes, a cycle every eleven minutes, a cycle every day when the sun hits the slit.

Rules, learned fast:

Keep gloves on. Everything is cold enough to stick and vibrate enough to blister.

Don't set anything flat on the floor. It will be wet in thirty seconds.

Tap the gauge before you read it; the needle sticks from vibration.

When the floornote changes — when the deep roar stutters — step back from the trench and hold the rail. The surge is normal. The surge is still a surge.

Log your time. Sign, wipe the pen, sign again because the first ink ran.

You will leave the way you came, down the ladder into the mountain's humming dark, ears ringing in the sudden quiet that is not quiet at all, but the memory of pressure. Behind you the room will keep weeping, dripping, pumping, humming to no audience, high and hidden and fully made, a small steel-and-concrete lung breathing cloud and reservoir water, too loud for anything to live in but the moss in the crack.
