At first you think it's just high. Very high.

The air gives it away before the eyes do — thin to the point of not being there, cold going in like a needle, coming out damp. Your breath doesn't plume and vanish. It hangs a second too long, as if the cold is too tired to disperse it. That is the whole valley: too high and too tired to finish anything.

It's a basin, really. An open cirque the size of a small township folded between ridges, not sharp ridges, not young Alps-knife ridges, but old, weathered, settled-down mountains. Long-existed rock. Granite and schist gone soft-shouldered from eons of ice and wind, scree slopes sorted by frost into neat polygons and stripes, as if a giant hand had raked them and then left. Tors stand around the rim like rotten molars — grey, lichen-spotted, wind-rounded. Nothing here is becoming. Everything has already become and decided to stay.

The light never fully arrives. It is a permanent blue-grey dusk, though there is no night to come and no day that left. The sun is a paler smudge behind cloud that does not move. You keep waiting for your eyes to adjust and they never do. Distances stay uncertain. The far wall of the cirque looks an hour away and also looks painted right behind the near boulders. The sky directly overhead is a little darker than it should be at this hour, bruise-violet, with stars sweating faintly through though the ground is still visible. At this elevation you are almost out of atmosphere. There isn't enough air to scatter light properly, nor enough to breathe properly.

Cold is the rule. Not killing blizzard cold, but deep, seeping, bone-cold. The stones sweat with it. Every leeward face is beaded with condensation that never quite becomes a drip. You touch a boulder and your glove comes away wet. In the hollows between stones there are pockets of water — small black tarns, no bigger than a table, perfectly still, perfectly reflective, but reflecting wrong. They reflect the ridge opposite, not the sky above. One long string of them threads where a stream should be: a riverine ghost. Damp moss and black mud connect tarn to tarn, saturated, squelching faintly underfoot, but no water flows. There is no flow. There is the *shape* of flow without the event.

Vegetation is almost an afterthought. In the damp seams only: crust lichens in rust-orange and sulphur-pale green plastered tight to rock, and low cushions of something dark and springy that holds meltwater like a sponge. A few tufts of straw-colored grass, flattened, frozen mid-bend. No trees. No shrubs higher than your ankle. Between these damp freckles are acres of nothing — bare frost-shattered pavement, sand, hollow wind.

And it is hollow. Empty in a way lowlands never are. Sound takes too long to die because there is nothing to stop it, and also nothing to make it. You can see a hundred meters in any direction and count on one hand the living things. That is the density of the place: near-void, vast space between presences.

What presences there are:

Rare fauna. You won't see them at first. Then — a flicker at the edge of sight. A bird the color of dirty snow, ptarmigan-sized but wrong-legged, perched on a tor and not moving. It does not fly. It watches with the patience of something that knows time doesn't pass here. Further on, a herd-trail of cloven prints leads from one tarn to another and simply stops in the middle of bare rock, as if the makers lifted off. Under one overhang, a coil of shed white fur, stiff with frost, unrotted. Nothing rots here. Nothing grows to replace it.

And the built things. For there are built things, though no one built them with tools.

Low dry-stone windbreaks, waist-high, horseshoe-shaped, open always to the east though the wind — if there is wind — comes from everywhere. Inside each is a flat slab, swept clean. Cairns, three or four stones high, marking nothing: not a summit, not a path, because paths don't hold. A circle of stones set flush in the mud around one of the larger tarns, like a hearth with no ash. A single beehive hut, half-collapsed, made of unmortared slabs leaning inward, no door, no roof hole, too small to sleep in unless you fold yourself double. No metal. No wood. No cloth. No wire or cut mark or right angle. Only stone placed on stone by hands or something like hands, long ago, and left to weather into the general weathering.

You understand slowly that the loudness is not weather. That is the first breakdown.

Because it is loud. Very loud. From the moment you crest the rim into the basin, there is a roar — a low organ-pipe hum from the rock itself overlaid with a high, thin whistling that seems to come from just behind your ears. Like standing beside a waterfall you cannot see, or inside a struck bell. You raise your voice without meaning to. You feel it in your sternum. And yet —

Nothing moves.

The cloud smudge does not scud. The grass tufts do not tremble. The surface of the tarns does not ripple under that roar. The condensation beads do not run. Your own jacket does not flap. The sound is detached from motion. It is simply *on*, at fixed volume, from everywhere and nowhere, equilibrium noise. You clap and the echo returns too fast, and too loud, and slightly *before* you finish clapping, as if the valley anticipated it. You stop walking to listen and the roar does not change. You walk for an hour toward the far wall and the roar does not change and the far wall does not get closer.

That is the second breakdown, and the one you live inside: nothing changes here.

This is not quiet stasis. It is loud stasis. Frozen-equilibrium. The water is damp but never wets further, never dries. The light is dim but never darkens or brightens. The cold never deepens into storm, never eases into thaw. Occasional rain — you feel it as a prickling on your face, tiny ice-needles hanging in the air around you — but it never falls, never lands. It just suspends, glittering in the grey light, and stays.

If you stay long enough you notice the other rules, the weirder ones underneath:

1. Distance is negotiable. Two cairns that look ten paces apart take a hundred paces to walk between, and when you look back they are still ten paces apart.

2. Up is thin. At the rim, your pack weighs nothing for a step, then twice nothing the next. Small jumps hang. Pebbles you kick fall too slowly, then stop falling a finger's width above ground and have to be pushed down.

3. Duplication. The tors repeat. You swear you've passed that same split boulder with the orange lichen shaped like a hand three times, from slightly different angles, though you walked straight.

4. The tarns remember. If you kneel by one, after a while a faint secondary roar rises from its black surface, tuned just under the main roar, and in it you can hear — footsteps, breathing, stone set on stone — sounds that must have made the windbreaks and the hut, still circulating, unable to decay.

No one lives here now. Nothing could make a living at this sparse margin, on lichen and condensation and thin air. But the place is not abandoned. Abandoned implies something left and time moved on. Here, time did not move on. Whoever stacked those stones is still somehow stacked into the sound, into the damp that never evaporates, into the dim light that never resolves.

You sit in one of the horseshoes out of an old instinct for shelter, though there is no wind to break. The stone is cold through your trousers, wet through your trousers. The roar fills your skull until you can't tell if it's outside or in your blood. Across the open basin, the white bird — or another identical white bird — sits on another tor, equally still. Tarns glint like dropped pieces of night sky. Condensation gathers on every surface, bright, unfalling.

You do not get warmer. You do not get colder. You just become, for a while, part of the established balance: stone, damp, lichen, roar.
