It is not a cave. A cave suggests you could enter it. This you could only find by kneeling.

On a cool heath, where the wind keeps the grass short and the soil is thin over shattered rock, someone has lifted — or the frost has lifted — a single flat slab of pale limestone about the size of a hand. Under it is the place.

A pocket. No wider than a soup bowl, no deeper than the first joint of your thumb. Below the surface of the world by only centimeters, but completely removed from it. The walls are raw in a way rock almost never is. Not weathered, not rounded, not stained. Fresh-broken, sugar-grained, glittering with un-dulled crystal points, edges still razor-sharp. The geology here hasn't decided what it is yet. It has no patina, no soil skin, no lichen crust except in flakes. It feels just-born, embryonic, as if the ground inhaled once and left a bubble that never popped.

And yet it is bright. Unreasonably bright.

A crooked fissure in the slab above — no thicker than a root — lets in a shaft of daylight, and the pocket holds it and amplifies it. The pale walls bounce it back and forth until there is nowhere shadow can hide. At noon it is white in there, surgical white, brighter than the overcast field around it. You have to squint to look in. The light does not flicker when clouds pass. It does not lean in the afternoon. It stays. It has the quality of light trapped in snow, or in a quartz crystal: diffused, sourceless, everywhere at once.

The air inside is cool. Noticeably cooler than your face. A breath that smells of wet stone and iron and something animal-sweet, like crushed grasshoppers. Damp, but not wet. There is no pool, no stream. Water exists here only as persistence: a bead along every crystal, a fog on every surface, a dark damp halo in the stone flour at the bottom that never spreads and never dries. Each droplet is beaded and hangs. If you watch — and you will watch for a long time because the brightness holds you — none of them fall. Condensation forms on the underside of the slab and stays there, fat and trembling, reflecting the white light back in miniature suns, but never heavy enough to drop.

It is packed. That is the first shock after the light.

There is no empty space in the pocket. From rim to floor, wall to wall, the surfaces are occupied edge to edge with bodies. Not scattered. Saturated.

A shivering living pavement of springtails, grey-white as ash, shoulder to shoulder so their furred flanks touch. Between them, amber mites moving with slow deliberate legs. Pale, blind woodlice, curled and uncurled, packed like beans in a jar, antennae overlapping. Cave-black beetle larvae, translucent at the joints. Thread worms knotted in the damp flour. Pseudoscorpions no bigger than sesame seeds, claws raised, riding on the backs of larger animals because there is nowhere else to stand. In each hanging droplet, if you bring your eye impossibly close, a wheel of rotifers turning.

They are abundant beyond what this thimble of space should feed. And they are loud. That is the second shock.

Put your ear to the rim and the pocket roars. For something you could cover with your palm, it makes a sound like a summer field at midnight. A high, dry, continuous rasping — ten thousand tiny legs on crystal, chitin on chitin, mandibles scraping — plus a glassy ticking, stridulation that never pauses for breath, plus the weird amplified ping of the chamber itself, which rings like a tiny cathedral. The sound has no direction. It presses up out of the hole and seems too large for its makers. You feel it in your teeth.

But nothing changes.

That is the strangeness, and it takes a minute to notice. At first you think they are seething, swarming. Then you realize no one is going anywhere. A woodlouse lifts a leg and does not put it down. A springtail tenses to jump and does not jump. The sound goes on and on without rising or falling, a held chord. The mites vibrate in place. The water beads do not grow or shrink. Even the dust motes in the light shaft hang motionless, lit and suspended.

It is equilibrium so perfect it feels like a pause. No gradient from wet to dry, warm to cold, crowded to empty. No succession. No decay. The few plants understand this. Vegetation here is sparse to the point of apology: three or four bleached root hairs stabbing down through the fissure from the grass above, tipped with a single clear exudate drop that never falls; a flake or two of powder-lichen, sage-green, clinging to the brightest part of the wall; a thread of moss protonema like green frost in one corner and nowhere else. They do not grow. They do not die. They hold.

What has been built here was not built by hands.

There are structures, scattered and low, made entirely of body and earth because there is nothing else. A trio of mud turrets, no taller than a grain of rice, chimneys for some vanished or still-present larva, their rims polished by endless circling. A bridge of silk, a dozen lines guyed from wall to wall, beaded with condensation, thrumming faintly with the general noise — you can see the springtails walking under it, not over it, because there is no room above. A hollowed-out seed husk, dragged down who knows how, its interior licked clean and now used as a sounding drum; a beetle sits wedged inside it, pulsing. Shed skins, perfectly intact, standing upright in the crowd like empty houses still occupied by the shape of air. Frass pellets stacked with deliberate care into a low wall around the dampest hollow. No tool marks. No metal, no plastic, no cut. Only secretion and accretion.

The rules of the pocket reveal themselves slowly:

Light does not leave. Once it enters, the walls keep passing it back and forth.

Moisture does not wet. It coats everything and saturates nothing.

Bodies do not thin. There is always exactly enough pressure of flank against flank to keep everyone upright.

Sound does not decay. The chamber feeds a little of its own echo back into itself, so the rasp sustains without effort.

Time does not pass. Or rather, it passes everywhere else — your knees ache from kneeling, the wind moves the grass against your neck — but not in there. The pocket is a held breath under the field. Cool, blinding, deafening, crowded, unfinished rock holding a crowd that never arrives and never leaves.

If you lower the slab back, you hear the sound cut off with a soft click, like a lid on a music box. The grass resumes. The coolness stays on your fingertips for a while, and the afterimage of that white light stays behind your eyes, a small sun that was burning, inexplicably, a finger's depth below the earth.
