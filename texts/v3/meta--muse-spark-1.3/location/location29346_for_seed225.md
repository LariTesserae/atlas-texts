You find it by almost falling in.

The plain goes on — flat, sun-bleached, thin grass hissing — and then simply stops. A lip. A missing place.

The hollow is not large. You can stand on its eastern rim and throw a stone to the western rim if you throw well. A single held breath of country, maybe forty strides across, maybe fifteen deep. Small enough to hold in your eye all at once, and to know its boundaries: a rough oval of pale rock with the sky set hard on top of it like a lid left ajar.

You go down because there is no other way to see it. The sides step down in broken shelves, not cliffs. Limestone, old sea-bed lifted and then dissolved. Everything here is weathered into patience. No sharp new break, no raw scar. The rock is cream and honey and pigeon-grey, pocked, fluted, licked smooth in places by water that is no longer here. Loose slabs lean where they fell a thousand years ago and have not moved since. Dust has settled into every pocket. It is a place that finished becoming itself a long time ago and now only maintains.

The light is the first thing that changes as you descend. Up on the plain it is bright. Down inside it is *brighter*. The walls lean back and throw the sun at each other. The pale stone throws it back again. At midday there is no shadow to hide in, only a thin dark line under each overhang, and the air itself seems lit from within, white-gold and trembling. You squint. Your skin feels the warmth coming from all sides at once — not hot, not desert-fierce, but a steady stored warmth. The rock holds yesterday's afternoon and this morning's and gives it back against your calves, your palms.

And yet, for all its openness, it is empty. Profoundly empty. You expect a pit to be cluttered, but this one is mostly air. A great hollow volume of warmed air with almost nothing in it. A ledge here, a boulder there, vast intervals between. Sound falls through it and disappears. Your footstep clicks too loud and then is gone.

It looks dry. It is dry — except it isn't, quite.

Follow the dark line. About two-thirds of the way down, circling almost the whole bowl like a tide mark, the limestone darkens to umber. This is the damp seam. It does not run. It does not pool. In the heat of the afternoon you would say it is just stain. But crouch close, put your cheek near the stone in the shade of an overhang, and you feel it: cool breath. Condensation. The pit breathes at night when the plain cools, and the cold air sinks and gets trapped here below the surface, and dew beads out of nothing onto the stone. By dawn the undersides of ledges are strung with drops that never fall. They shrink backward into the rock as the sun climbs, wicked into moss and root and tiny black lichens.

No stream. No pond, except after a rare hard rain when for an hour a tea-colored sheet stands in the lowest dish and then is gone, sucked down. Life here lives on that intermittent kiss — beading, seeping, evaporating. If you know where to look, in a north-facing crevice, there is a pocket the size of a cupped hand where water stays a day longer, dark and tannic, with a skin of dust on it.

Where the damp is, the green is. And only there.

Vegetation is sparse to the point of deliberation. No carpet, no thicket. A dozen things, each alone. A dwarf fig wedged in a crack, its roots flattened into white ropes that crawl ten feet along the seam to find the next weep. Tufts of needle grass that have bleached almost white. A brittle shrub with tiny lacquered leaves that click faintly in the updraft. Cushions of moss like green felt in the deepest shade, no bigger than your thumb. Each plant is an island, with yards of bare warm stone between. They do not compete. They wait.

It is quiet. Mostly.

At first you think nothing lives here because you cannot hear it. No chorus, no rustle. Just the high thin whine of air over the rim, and your own breathing, and far off the tick-tick-tick of expanding stone.

Then you stay still, and the hollow fills.

A wall lizard flattens itself on a sun-slab, throat pulsing. Another, smaller, darts for a crevice. High above, swifts cut the bright column of air — five, seven, eleven of them — never landing, screaming once, very faintly, like a needle dragged on glass, and then silent again as they tilt. They live elsewhere but hunt here, because the hollow funnels insects. And insects there are, in astonishing number for so empty a place: mason bees working the fig flowers, blue-black wasps prospecting holes, ants in a slow black river along the seam, pillbugs shrunk under stones where it is still cool to the touch.

The strangeness comes on slowly, the way heat shimmer does.

Things hang here that should not. The swifts, for instance — at noon they stop flapping entirely and simply circle, wings locked, riding the warm air rising out of the bowl. They go round and round without effort, as if suspended from the light itself. And the light itself is wrong. Down on the floor looking up, the rim seems to glow, a corona of reflected brightness, so the sky inside the oval looks deeper blue than the sky outside, a well of blue.

And the stones ring. Not metaphorically. Many of the fallen slabs on the floor are undercut, hollowed by solution from beneath until they rest on three points like inverted bowls. Step near them, or tap with a knuckle, and they give a low, ceramic *tong* that hangs for a second in the still air before the emptiness swallows it. Each has a slightly different note. The floor is an instrument no one plays.

No one has built anything here. No wall, no cairn, no hearth, no cut mark. Not even a footpath that lasts — dust covers it by evening. And yet there are tools.

You find the first anvil by accident, in the lee of the largest fallen block. A flat-topped stone, knee-high, its top pitted with small round dents. Around it in a scatter: empty snail shells, all broken at the same whorl, all white-toothed tiny. Perched on the edge, a twig, stripped, one end frayed. While you watch, a crow — iridescent, wary — drops down from the rim, lands, picks up the twig in its beak, fishes into a crevice you cannot see into, extracts something pale and wriggling, and eats. It wipes its beak on the stone and leaves. A few feet away, another station: a smooth pebble, and beside it a neat pile of spiny seed pods, husked. Lizard tracks in the dust. Ant columns diverging.

No architecture. No storage. Just this: use. A stone held, a stick shaped by beak and claw, a problem solved and abandoned. Hand-work without hands. The hollow is full of intelligences that do not build, only borrow for a moment and set down again. If you did not know to look, you would call it untouched. It is untouched, in the human sense. In another sense it is a workshop used daily, tidied by wind.

Nothing hurries. That is the rule.

Water does not flow, it beads and retreats. Plants do not grow, they persist. The fig puts on perhaps a finger-length a year. The calcite drapery under the biggest overhang — thin as eggshell, translucent amber when you lift it to the light — grows at the pace of dew. A drip forms, hangs for an hour, never falls, shrinks. Ten thousand such almost-drips make a millimeter.

Even the animals obey slowness. The lizards bask for hours. The bees work one flower for a whole afternoon. The crow returns to the same anvil day after day, year after year. The swifts circle.

By late afternoon the western wall throws a single tongue of shade across the floor. The air cools by a degree, then another. The damp seam darkens again as condensation begins, invisible at first. The ringing stones cool and tick. The light, still bright on the eastern rim, turns the color of honey, and for a few minutes the whole bowl glows as if lit from inside the rock.

You climb out with dust on your hands and the taste of limestone on your tongue, and behind you the hollow empties itself again — vast space between a fig and a grass tuft and a bee and a lizard and a crow's twig — holding its damp, its warmth, its brightness, waiting out the night to bead again at dawn.
