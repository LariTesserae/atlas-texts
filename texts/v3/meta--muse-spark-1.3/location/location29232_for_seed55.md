It is not under the water. It is inside it.

Far above — so far that it is a rumor more than a sky — there is a surface. Ice, perhaps, or just winter that never lifts. The light that gets down here has given up most of itself on the way. What arrives is not day. It is a direction: a faint, cold greening a hundred meters up, thinning to black in every other direction. You learn distance here not by sight but by pressure, by cold on exposed skin, by how long your breath takes to come back.

The bottom, if you can call it a bottom, goes on farther than weather. The sea holds a continent that was made flat, and kept flat.

Everything under your hands is made.

There is no rock here that was not cut. No sand that was not crushed from something cut. As far as a slow drift can carry you — days, if you had days of air — the floor is fitted stone. Enormous slabs, each longer than a man is tall, laid edge to edge in courses that step down, and down, and down. Courtyards the size of fields. Stairs twenty, forty, a hundred treads wide, descending a hand's breadth per tread into darkness. Long straight canals, now just slightly deeper troughs of still water, running true for kilometers until they blur. Low walls at hip height, everywhere, dividing and dividing again, the plan of a place that housed more hands than you can imagine.

It was made with almost nothing. You can see that when you get close, and down here, you are always close — visibility dies after six or seven meters in the cold haze.

The faces of the stones are pocked with the cup-marks of pounders. In the joints, where the silt has washed out, you can see the short, chattering strokes of copper chisels. Holes drilled for rope and wooden pegs that swelled to split the block. No cranes. No iron. No saws. Just weight, and water, and time, and more people than stones. Ramps of rubble still lean against the sides of the drowned platforms, where sledges were dragged. In places a wooden pole still stands, wedged upright in a socket — black oak, cold-preserved, bearded with growth, not rotted because rot here is slow, almost thoughtful.

And that is because nothing has finished falling apart, and nothing will soon. This is old beyond ruin. Erosion has stopped arguing. Corners that were once sharp are now round as knuckles. The great blocks have settled into each other, tilted two degrees, five degrees, found their final lean and stopped. Mortar — lime and crushed tile, pinkish when you scrape it — has softened to a kind of clay that oozes but holds. Silt lies over everything in a thick felt, a meter deep in the courtyards, ankle-deep on the high causeways where the slow current combs it clean. If you fan it away, there is not earth beneath. There is another floor. Tile. Brick set on edge in herringbone. Another floor beneath that. The makers paved their world many times over, and the sea has paved it again with its own dust.

Out of every crack grows the forest.

In the absence of anything to eat it, it has become lush beyond sense. There are no fish here. No crabs picking at holdfasts, no urchins, no seals, no gulls. Not even shells. Only the empty white pits on the stones where barnacles once gripped, long since dissolved. No eyes watch you. No thing darts away.

So the vegetation has taken everything and kept it.

Black kelp, dark red-brown and olive so dark it reads as black, rises from the joints between slabs in dense curtains. Fronds thirty meters long trail horizontally in the slack, layer upon layer, ceiling over the sunken streets. Where the current is a little stronger, on the lips of the canals, they stream like hair. Where it is still, they pile, rotting only very slowly in the cold, forming warm-less peat-banks of half-living frond.

Lower down, where even kelp gives up, the stones are furred. Velvet mats of encrusting algae, wine-dark and bottle-green, so thick you can push a finger into them to the knuckle before you touch stone. Tufts of feather-weed, pale and translucent in your lamp — if you bring a lamp — trembling although there is no surge you can feel. Fields of low, fleshy fingers, swaying a degree to one side and back over the course of an hour as the great, slow tide turns over the continent.

It is dense in a way land forests never are. Water holds everything up. You do not walk here. You push through. A frond brushes your cheek, another your knees, another closes behind your tank. Spores and shed skin and fine mineral wool hang in the water, never settling, never clearing. Near-touching, everywhere. Stone, then an inch of water, then leaf, then leaf, then leaf, then stone again above you where a lintel still spans between walls and the kelp hangs down from it like a drowned thatch. Rooms still roofed, if you care to squeeze in, full to the doorway with slow green-black growth.

The cold is total. Not the sharp cold of air, but a seeping, even, ancient cold — one or two degrees above freezing, always. It makes your fingers stupid within minutes. It preserves. A woven basket, left on a step, is still a basket under its coat of algae; you can lift the rim and it holds. A coil of rope, turned to iron-hard fiber. A sandal print filled with hardened silt in a brickyard the size of a province, one print after another, going nowhere you can follow before the forest closes over.

Sound does not travel as it should, or it travels too well and finds nothing to carry. Mostly, quiet. Your own breathing, too loud. The faint creak of stipe against stipe as the water masses lean. Occasionally, from far off and impossible to place, a deep soft thud — a slab, somewhere out in the dark miles, settling another millimeter after a thousand years. A sigh of silt slumping off a terrace edge and falling, for a long time, through black water. No clicks. No songs. No scrape of claws or fins. The quiet of a place where the animals left, or never arrived, and the plants were left alone to be patient.

Because everything here is patient. That is the rule.

Light will not increase. Warmth will not come. The current will turn twice a day, moving at the pace of a sleeping breath, bringing dissolved stone-dust and leaf-tea from one endless ward of the city to the next. The kelp will lengthen a cell, then another. The stones will soften a grain, then another. Where a wall finally gives — and walls do, after ten thousand years — it does not crash. It exhales, leans, comes to rest against its neighbor, and within a season its fallen face is already furred over, already stitched down by holdfasts strong as hands.

If you rise a little, kicking gently so as not to stir the silt that will hang for hours if you do, and look out over the edge of a great platform, you see it repeated until dark takes it: platform, canal, wall, courtyard, stair, forest, forest, forest, all flattened by age and water into one low, vast, made plain.

Not a ruin in the sea. A sea that happens to have floors. A world built entirely by hand, with stone hammers and fiber ropes, at a scale so large it made its own slow weather — and then flooded, or sank, or was left — and went on, cold and dark and green-black and quiet, growing over itself forever without anything left to watch but the growth itself.
