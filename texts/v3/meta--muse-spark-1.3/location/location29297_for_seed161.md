On top of the world there is a bowl.

You do not see it until you are already on the rim, lungs working thin, legs burning from the long switchback up through scree and dead wind. The mountain below you is enormous and empty — pale flanks falling away for thousands of feet into a sea of cloud that never lifts. And then, under your boots, the ground simply stops being a summit and becomes an interior.

A round pit, no wider than a good shout will carry. A collapsed throat in the oldest rock anywhere. The stone here is not sharp. Nothing has been sharp here for a very, very long time. The limestone lip is rounded like a worn molar, pocked and honeycombed and smoothed by rains that stopped mattering a million years ago. Everything has settled into its final shape. The slopes do not slide. The boulders do not fall. They sit, half-buried in their own dust, lichen-crusted, patient.

Inside, it is dimmer than it should be at noon. The walls are thirty, forty feet high all around — a broken circle — and they cut the sky to a coin. Direct sun only falls straight down for an hour or so. The rest of the day the light is borrowed: bounced off pale rock, filtered through leaves, the color of late evening even when the cloud-sea below is burning white. Your eyes adjust slowly. Then you see how full it is.

There is no water here. That is the first rule of the place. No spring, no pool, no stream, no seep. The floor is dry dust and dry humus that sucks at your sandals and puffs up with every step. Rain, when it comes once or twice a year, hisses on the stone and is gone within the hour, swallowed down through cracks too deep to follow. The throats of those cracks are dark and smell of cool mineral. Nothing comes back up.

And yet it is green. Impossibly green for a dry stone cup in the sky.

Because the green does not drink from the ground. It drinks from the air.

Every night the cloud-sea rises and the bowl fills to the brim with fog. You cannot see it if you sleep below, but the people who live here will tell you: it comes in silent, over the lip, and settles. By dawn every leaf is beaded. The whole bowl drips without a single puddle ever forming. Beard-moss hangs in curtains from fig limbs and wrings itself out. Rosette succulents with thick, waxy tongues cup the dew in their crowns. Elephant-ear taro-like leaves, dusty on top and velvet underneath, tilt to pour their load down stem-channels to roots wedged in rock seams. Vines stitch everything to everything else.

It is a hanging garden with no gardener, and into it a village has been packed so tight you cannot tell where thicket ends and house begins.

Small — you can walk rim to rim in four minutes if no goat is in the way, and there is always a goat in the way. But dense. Layered. There is no empty angle.

Terraces of dry-stacked stone, each stone fitted by hand and re-fitted for a hundred generations, step down the inner walls in irregular rings. On the terraces: flat-roofed huts of the same pale stone, roofed with thatch of dry fog-grass and held with rope. Between huts: alleys no wider than shoulders, roofed over with poles and mats and more vines so they become tunnels. Under the houses: root cellars and bat roosts and storage pits lined with grass. Above the houses: drying racks, pigeon lofts, small shrines of stacked bone. Ladders everywhere — notched logs, knotted ropes — because up and down matters more than side to side here.

Everything is hand-made. You can see the hands in it. Quern stones worn into saddles from grinding millet and amaranth. Wooden looms clacking on porches. Bone awls, flint flakes, hammerstones left on a wall to be used by whoever passes. Fire-blackened cooking pits with three stones for a pot. No metal, except a few precious traded copper knives kept wrapped and oiled. No wheels — there is nowhere to roll. Baskets do the work, and backs.

And the smell is warm. That surprises you after the cold climb. The bowl holds heat. Stone gives back the day all night, bodies and cookfires and composting leaf-litter give more, the wind passes overhead and does not dip in. The air is close, dusty-sweet, smelling of crushed greens and smoke and dry dung and the faint ammonia bite of guano. It coats the throat. You will be thirsty within the hour and there will be nothing to drink except what they offer: a chew of succulent stem, sour and astringent, or a cloth wrung from last night's dew-catchers — a mouthful, never a cup.

They are not alone in here.

The bowl is loud with life because life cannot leave. Swifts and swallows cut endless circuits under the lip, screaming, nesting in thousands in the wall-pocks. At dusk they are replaced by fruit bats that pour out of the deep cracks, leathery wings thudding close over the thatch to work the figs. Pigeons coo from every loft and wild doves answer from the branches. Insects never stop: cicada-like trillers in the heat of the brief noon, crickets after, beetles clicking against stone at night. Lizards rustle in the thatch. Hyrax — fat, grey, whistling rock-rabbits — sun on the walls and scold intruders. And the domestic: chickens underfoot, guinea-pigs squeaking in wicker pens, a herd of wiry black goats, ribs showing but stubborn, pruning the lower leaves, bells clanking.

People add to it. Someone is always grinding, chopping dry wood, mending thatch, calling a child, coughing in the dust, singing low while they weave. Sound does not escape. It hits the curved walls and comes back, so the whole hamlet hums inside itself like a hive in a jar. From the rim above, you would hear it before you see it: a low roar of wings and voices and bells rising out of solid rock.

Nothing here happens fast.

Lichen grows a millimeter in your lifetime. A fig will split a terrace wall over two hundred years and the masons will simply build around the root, then, eventually, out of the root. The dew-cloths are laid out every dusk exactly as grandmothers laid them. The guano is scraped from the same ledges, the millet sown in the same dust-enriched pockets, the dead carried up the same stair-cut in the rim and laid on the outer scree for the sky. Children learn which crack whistles before fog comes, which vine holds weight, which goat bites.

If you stay a day you will learn the rules without being told: do not waste a wring of cloth. Do not cut a living limb when deadfall will burn. Do not shout after dark when the bats are navigating. Do not leave the bowl unless you must — the world below is vast and falling and cold, and there is nothing up here but this one small, crowded, green, dry, dim, warm cup holding on against the sky.
