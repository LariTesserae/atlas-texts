You come down into it at dusk that never fully lifts, though it is midday.

The basin is low and warm and wide — a shallow bowl of land a day's walk across, rimmed by higher dark that you can see only as a smudge when the mist thins. The ground underfoot is wrong-young. Not soil. Not yet. It is fresh black glass, rope and shard, a lava plain or something that pretends to be lava plain, cooled so recently its edges still cut. The plates haven't had time to soften. Every step crunches on needles. Between the slabs there are cracks a hand wide where heat still sighs out, sweet and metallic, and in those cracks, impossibly, green is already forcing its way up.

You expect barrenness from new stone. You get the opposite.

This place is lush with a violence that feels personal. Not forest as you know forest — trunk and crown and shade — but a hanging lushness, an air-lushness. Because there is almost nothing here to hold onto, life has learned to hold onto nothing.

Look up.

The city — for it is a city, organized, intentional — does not crowd. That is the first disorientation. You have been taught that urban means dense. Here urban means spaced. Towers rise out of the black plain at half-kilometer intervals, regular as plantings, each one a kilometer or more tall, vanishing upward into low cloud. Each one impossibly thin. From a distance you think they are smoke. Coming closer you see they are hollow.

They are not built, they are extruded. Sharp fins and lattices, unweathered, still bearing the burr of their making. No rust, no rounding, no pigeon wear. The material is neither metal nor stone — a pearlescent dark that drinks light and returns it a half-second late, so that when you move your head the tower shimmers after you. You can see stars of brighter infrastructure inside them when the skin goes translucent: stairwells that spiral with no stairs, elevator shafts where capsules of air simply rise, rooms stacked like honeycomb but ninety percent void. Whole floors are just tensioned air. If you knock, it rings far longer than it should, as if the sound has farther to travel than the size suggests.

No one lives in them. Or no one you would call people. The builders — whatever seeded this place a season or a year ago — made it vastly larger than its occupants. Vast space between presences. You can shout in the plaza between two towers and the echo has time to get lonely. The streets, if they are streets, are just polished seams in the basalt where the stone was fused flat, wide enough for processions that never come.

But empty does not mean lifeless. Quite the opposite. The emptiness is full of mouths.

Because the towers are cold. A few degrees colder than the warm wet air — that is their trick, or their byproduct. The warm air of the basin kisses their skins and gives up its water. All day and night, condensation beads and runs and gathers and falls. Not rain. Never quite rain. A perpetual sweating. Rivulets braid down the fins and drip from every ledge, and where they drip, life hangs.

From every tower, from every cable strung impossibly thin between towers, vegetation pours downward and outward without ever touching ground. Long curtains of moss, black-green and copper, ten meters, forty meters long, swaying in the heat convection. Epiphytes on epiphytes: orchids big as dinner plates, translucent, veined with light they have stored; ferns that unfurl overnight and collapse by afternoon; vines that do not climb but dangle and interweave in midair to form nets. They drink the tower-sweat. They have no need for soil. The air itself is soil here — warm, damp, rich with spores and shed skin and droppings. You breathe and taste chlorophyll and iron.

Between towers the air is a meadow turned vertical.

And in that meadow, animals. In abundance that should be impossible for such young stone.

At first you hear them before you parse them. The sound is the dominant fact. It is deafening — not loud like a machine, but loud like weather. A continuous roar at the edge of pain made of a million small sounds layered until they become one pressure on the chest. Clicks, saws, whistles, throbs. Frog-throats the size of fists in the hanging moss pumping out bass notes you feel in your molars. Cicada-things — though they are not cicadas, they are six-winged and glassy — stuttering in such numbers that their chorus becomes a metallic sheen over everything. Flocks of moth-bats, pale gray, hand-wide, moving in ribbons between towers, their wings making a dry rain sound. Higher up, things you only glimpse: kite-hunters with three-meter wingspans riding the warm updraft, screaming thinly as they fold and drop through the moth ribbons. Down in the cracks, quick lizard-metal scuttles, copper flanks flashing.

And underneath it all, the city itself hums. A low, almost subsonic throb from deep in the basalt — the engines that still maintain this place. You feel it in your ankles more than hear it. Every few minutes the hum steps up a quarter-tone and all the hanging curtains tremble, and a fresh sheet of condensation bursts outward from the towers as if they exhaled. Then the animals go briefly silent in shock — one stunned heartbeat of absolute quiet so complete it hurts — and then roar back twice as loud.

The light never gets bright. The towers, together, are dimming the sky. Whether by design or accident you can't tell. Above the tops there is a frayed ceiling of cloud and vine-spore and something else — a faint hexagonal shimmer, like a sunshade made of force rather than matter, filtering the sun down to amber dusk. The sun itself is a diffuse smear, directionless. Shadows are soft or nonexistent. Colors come forward instead: the wet green of moss, the bone-white of orchid flesh, the warning orange of frog eyes, the bruised violet of the towers in half-light. Your eyes adjust and then keep adjusting, never quite finding noon.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. There are no rivers here, no lakes. The damp lives on surfaces. Your shirt is damp within minutes, not from rain but from the air condensing on you as it does on the towers. Your skin beads. Leaves drip steadily from their tips — plip-plip-plip onto black glass, where the drops sit for a moment, perfect lenses reflecting dim amber, then wick away into cracks. In hollows of fused stone there are pockets: clear shallow pans a meter wide where tower-drip has collected, and in each pan a whole world — tadpole-things flickering, mosquito larvae writhing, a miniature floating garden of duckweed and fallen petals. Step a few meters away and the stone is dry and hot and sharp again. Damp is not a climate here, it is an event that follows the towers around.

The strangeness accumulates if you stand still.

Drops sometimes fall up. Near the base of the larger towers, where the maintenance field is strongest, mist that should settle instead rises in slow spirals, defying. Dust hangs motionless in columns where gravity seems to have thinned. You throw a pebble and it falls too slowly, as if considering. The towers themselves occasionally glitch — you will be watching one and its upper third will stutter, become double, become transparent to a sky that is not this sky, then resolve, with a soft chime that is immediately swallowed by the biological roar. Birds that fly through that stuttered zone come out reversed, flying backward for a few wingbeats before correcting, unperturbed.

The city repairs itself while you watch. A fin broken by a kite-hunter's impact crawls back over an hour, extruding fresh pearlescent edge, sharp as a razor. Tiny bright motes — too small, too purposeful to be dust — swarm the wound, weaving. If you hold out your hand, they will land and taste your sweat and leave, uninterested.

Rules emerge, not posted but obvious if you want to live until morning:

Stay on the fused seams. The fresh stone will open your boots and your feet.

Don't drink from the ground pans after the towers exhale — that water is cleaning fluid for a metabolism you don't share. Drink from leaves, from the drip-tips before they fall. The moss filters.

Make no shelter under the curtains. Hunters hunt by shadow, and the curtains are full of eyes. Sleep in the hollow of a tower base where the field keeps insects back, even though the hum will enter your dreams and make them architectural.

And listen. Because despite the deafening, there are patterns. When the frog bass drops a third, rain-that-isn't-rain is about to sheet off the east towers. When the moth-bats go silent in a moving patch, a kite is inside that silence. When the city hum steps up, cover your water. It is about to get wetter.

You walk on, between immensities. Towers recede in every direction until mist takes them, each draped in its green veil, each sweating, each ringing with life. The ground remains raw and new beneath, the sky remains dim above, the air remains thick and warm and loud enough to drown thought. It is a town, a valley of a town, but built for giants who never arrived, tended by machines that do not sleep, and already — hungrily, brilliantly — claimed by small, wet, screaming life that does not care who built it.

It does not need you. It barely notices you. It sweats, it sings, it eats itself and grows.
