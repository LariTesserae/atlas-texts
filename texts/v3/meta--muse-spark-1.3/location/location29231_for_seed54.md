It is high before it is anything else.

The air thins out on the way up and never comes back. You climb for hours through black basalt switchbacks and your lungs go shallow, your breath comes out and does not dissipate — it hangs, it thickens, it joins the air. At about four thousand meters the road simply stops climbing and opens. A valley. Not a peak, a held place. A wide, shallow bowl ringed by unsoftened ridges, and in it, a city that you hear long before you can make out.

There is no light to see it by. The sky is a low, closed darkness, starless with cloud and vapor, a polar night that has lasted longer than anyone has bothered to count. No moon. No dawn coming. Your eyes keep waiting to adjust and they never quite do.

What you learn, standing at the rim with sleet needling your cheeks, is that the dark here is not empty. It is full.

The ground under your boots is wrong in the way new things are wrong. This whole caldera is raw. Ten years, fifty years — geologically nothing. The rock has not learned to be landscape yet. No soil. No rounding. No river valleys, no soft slopes. Just black volcanic glass, ropey pahoehoe frozen mid-flow, pressure ridges sharp enough to cut gloves, fracture faces still glossy where they split last winter. It crunches and rings when you walk. There is nowhere flat that water has made flat; water has not had time.

And yet water is everywhere.

It weeps. That is the only word. The stone weeps. Because below the glass there is heat — a shallow magma tongue, or the city's buried engines, no one distinguishes anymore — and above there is killing cold, twenty below, thirty below, cold that makes metal groan. Between the two, ice forms and unforms continuously. Meltwater cannot sink through the glass, cannot run off in proper streams because no channels exist yet, so it sits just under a skin of ice, it presses up in seeps, it beads on every vertical face. The air itself is saturated. Each exhalation, each vent, condenses instantly into fog that never lifts, only drifts, knee-high, chest-high, pooling in the raw hollows to make marshes where there should be no marshes.

A high cold marsh on fresh glass. Black water, grey ice-needles, brine that refuses to freeze because it is so salted with silica and sulfur. Your boots go through with a crack and then suck. Slush, scum-ice, warm mud that steams for three seconds before the cold kills the steam.

Nothing green grows here. Nothing has had time, and nothing would survive the dark anyway. No trees, no grass, no moss to soften an edge. Only mineral. If you crouch with a headlamp off — and you learn to keep it off — you can feel with your fingers what your eyes miss: thin, slick biofilms on the wettest stones, orange and rust-red and milky white, bacterial, fungal at most. They are not vegetation. They do not photosynthesize. They eat stone and heat and sulfur breath. They slick the causeways and make footing treacherous.

The city is built directly onto this weeping glass without apology.

From the rim in darkness you would think it is a geological feature, another pressure ridge. Then the wind shifts the fog and you realize the ridge is terraced. Windowless, lightless terraces stepping down toward the marsh-center in dense, layered rings. Towers like organ pipes, low domes, causeways on arches, cisterns, walls within walls. Settlement-scale, valley-scale — you cannot take it in at once. It extends past hearing. Block after block of close-fitted stone and grown basalt and something else, something that is not quarried but coaxed: walls that look poured, then frozen, with a faint vitreous sheen, seamless around corners. No lights in windows because there are no windows. No streetlamps. No neon. The city does not use light.

It uses heat and sound. That is the post-modern part, the part you feel in your teeth before you understand.

Between the buildings stand the pillars — tall, slightly leaning, dull black, humming. You feel them as pressure in your sternum when you pass. They hold warmth in localized envelopes, they bend the fall of sleet, they make dropped stones land a hand's-width to the left. Gravity lattice, resonance anchors, thermal capillaries; the engineers who maintain them no longer explain them in physics you would recognize. The buildings grow a millimeter a year around them, self-healing cracks with fresh mineral deposition. The infrastructure is dense to the point of no gaps: pipes over pipes, walkways under walkways, heat-exchangers breathing out warm wet air that instantly becomes fog. Everything drips. Everything condenses. The whole urban mass exhales.

And on every surface, the fauna.

You thought the ridges were dark stone. They move.

There are so many of them that density stops being a number and becomes a texture. The pale ones first: moth-bats, if you need a name, blind, white-furred, fist-sized, with broad moth wings and bat ears, roosting in overlapping shingles on every lee wall, on every pillar, under every arch, packed shoulder to shoulder, layered three and four deep, chittering in sleep. When one shifts, ten thousand shift. Then the lift — without command, a section of wall detaches into air, a living sheet a hundred meters wide, and the air pressure changes.

Above them, higher, the long-necked screamers circle the thermal columns, never landing, black against black, riding the warm updrafts from the cisterns. Below, in the marsh-water, the skaters and gulpers: flat segmented things that dimple the brine, ice-worms as thick as your arm turning over in the warm mud, colonies of clicking shrimp in the culverts so dense the water crackles.

They do not eat plants because there are no plants. They eat each other, they eat the biofilm, they eat the sulfur and the heat itself, they eat the city's waste — the sloughed skin of biofilters, the condensate rich with minerals, the dead that fall daily by the thousands and are cleared nightly by other thousands. It is a complete ecology built on warmth in the dark, and it is overwhelming not in variety but in mass. The weight of bodies. The smell of bodies: ammonia, wet fur, hot stone, iodine.

Which is why it is so loud.

There is no silence to be found. The city drones at the low end — the pillars at 40 hertz, a tone you feel in your molars, slow and patient and unceasing. Water ticks and patters everywhere, ten million drips from arches and eaves and glass fangs. And over that, the animal noise: wingbeat like canvas tearing continuously, high echolocation clicks that merge into a frying hiss, the screamers' long descending whistles, the deep croak of the gulpers in the marshes. You have to shout to be heard two feet away. You learn, quickly, to stop trying to talk and to navigate by sound-shadow, as they do.

Nothing here happens fast, despite the noise. That is the rule you have to internalize or you will go mad waiting for change.

The ice on the marsh inches. The towers accrete. The roosts shift downslope over seasons as one wall becomes too fouled and another, freshly grown, becomes preferable. The pale clouds of wings that blacken what little grey there is in the sky move with a slowness that looks like weather, not decision. Even the cold is slow — not a snap freeze but a patient, total permeation.

Rules of this place, as you learn them by living a day:

Keep your light off. Light blinds the roost and brings the screamers low.

Stay warm by staying close. The envelopes between pillars are life; the spaces beyond are death in an hour.

Do not step where it is slick-orange. That film is alive and it is also someone's food and someone's farm.

Do not expect the ground to hold its shape. It is still being born. New cracks steam. Old causeways sink an inch a year into warm mud.

Listen for the change in drone before you look for a change in weather. The pillars sing lower when a fog-bank is coming thick, higher when the ice is about to heave.

By the second night your eyes have given up on seeing and your other senses have taken over: the press of warm air on your face meaning shelter, the sudden quiet under an arch meaning a dense roost overhead holding its breath, the mineral tang on your tongue meaning you are near open water.

You stand in the center marsh, boots sunk to the ankle in warm black slush, walls rising on all sides into fog and dark, white wings moving ceaselessly over you like snow that will never land, the whole basin humming in your bones. Cold gnawing at any exposed skin. Water seeping up through new stone. No leaf, no blade, no green thing anywhere in the valley — and life so thick you have to push through it.

The mountain is still making itself. The city is still making itself. The dark holds them both, wet and loud and crowded, and does not hurry.
