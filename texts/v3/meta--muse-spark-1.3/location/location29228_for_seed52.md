The valley first, because everything here is low.

You come down off the rim road and the air gets thicker by steps, warmer on the skin, weighted. It is not a deep valley — a shallow bowl at almost sea-level, wide enough that you cannot hold it all at once. A day's walk across, maybe more in the mist. The far side is always a suggestion: dark verticals fading into warm grey, then nothing.

Above you, the dim.

Noon here looks like the last half hour before night elsewhere. The light is low and amber-brown, filtered. If you stare up long enough your eyes adjust and you see why: the sky is not sky. A veil is stretched over the whole basin, edge to rim to rim, a membrane so vast you stop seeing it as an object and start seeing it as weather. It breathes, very slowly. Panels of it, kilometers wide, shift from translucent to opaque in no pattern you can learn. Engineers from before maintain it, or it maintains itself. The old people say it was raised to cool a burning plain. Now it keeps the light dim and the air warm underneath, like a hand held over a seed tray.

Under that hand, the city.

It is a city, undeniably — organized, infrastructured, layered — but it refuses to be dense. Towers stand a half kilometer apart, sometimes more, rising out of green like trees that forgot they were buildings. Between them: emptiness, kept emptiness. Broad plazas of dark fresh stone, empty transit fields, gardens gone lush and then gone wild, shallow hollows where nothing is built on purpose so the eye can rest.

The ground itself is young. You can feel it under your boots. Nothing here is rounded. The cuts are sharp. The drainage channels are cut with glass edges that have not yet softened. The curbs, the foundation lips, the broken basalt fill they dredged up to raise the streets — all of it still has that freshly-fractured look, black and glittering where the moss hasn't taken it. This place was made recently. Decanted, printed, extruded, grown. No one agrees on the verb. There is no old softness to hide in. Even the hills at the rim look quarried yesterday.

The materials tell you that. Lower floors are poured stone, still outgassing a faint mineral heat. Upper floors are something else — not glass, not metal. A smoky translucent mass that is solid when you lean against it and goes slightly to mist when you step away, as if it only bothers to be real when witnessed. Vines have learned to climb it anyway. Copper gutters, black ceramic pipes, dew-nets strung like harps between pylons. And between the towers, where you expect bridges, there is often nothing to see at all: only a faint heat-shimmer line in the air, a transit thread you can ride by stepping in and thinking where. Sometimes at dusk you see the pods moving along them — silent, lit from inside, floating on no track.

The city is quiet for a city. That is the first wrongness visitors notice. With that much construction there should be noise — traffic, crowds, machines. There is almost none. The threads make no sound. The walls absorb sound. The veil above eats the sky-noise. What you hear, when you stand still in one of those huge empty plazas, is small and close:

drip.

A leaf tipping water. Your own breath. The soft tick of condensation falling from the veil-nets a hundred meters up onto broad leaves below. Every few hours a low hum rises through your feet, up through the stone, as all the towers tune together — a chord you feel more than hear — and then the rain comes.

Not real rain. Intermittent, engineered, half-natural now. The veil sweats. It gathers the basin's own exhalation all morning and then releases it in slow, warm, irregular showers that last twenty minutes and vanish. No rivers here. No lakes. Water exists as film and pocket. It beads on everything. It gathers in the leaf-axils of the giant shade plants, in the carved basins at street corners, in shallow sharp-edged puddles that mirror the dim amber for an hour and then are gone, drunk by moss. The air itself is the river. You walk through it and come out damp at the shoulders, at the hairline. Clothes never quite dry.

And because of that damp, the green is everywhere, and it is lush beyond reason for so little light.

This is not sun-green. This is dusk-green, shade-green. Leaves grown huge to catch what little falls — elephant-ear philos, ferns as tall as doors, mosses in thick velvet carpets over the fresh stone, softening it. Fungi pale as knuckles push up along the curb cuts overnight. Epiphytes beard every vertical surface: towers wear skirts of hanging green twenty stories high, dripping. The plants do not turn toward the sun, because there is no sun to follow. They turn toward people, toward movement and heat. Walk past a wall of sail-leaves and you will see them shift, very slowly, to face you as you pass. Stand still long enough and the tendrils will test the air in your direction.

It is warm enough for all of it to move fast. Warm, close, compost-smelling air — wet stone, crushed leaf, ozone from the threads. Things grow while you watch, if you know how to watch. A new runner across a plaza overnight. A flower opening in an hour, white and waxy, built to glow a little in the dim so pollinators — but there are almost no pollinators.

The animals are rare. That is the emptiness inside the lushness. For all this green there should be chatter, rustle, flocks. There is not. Once a day you might see a pale lizard flatten itself on warm stone, throat pulsing. At night the blind moths come, big as hands, dusted grey, bumping softly against the floating lamps. High up near the veil, something glides on long silent wings from tower to tower — no one is sure if it is animal or maintenance drone gone feral. It calls once, a falling glass note, and then is gone for weeks. Otherwise, absence. Footsteps echo a little too long in the green.

Light behaves oddly here, which is the second wrongness. The floating lamps — small cold suns that drift at shoulder height along old street lines, guiding you when the veil goes particularly dark — cast shadows that linger a second too long after you move. The towers sometimes cast shadows in different directions from each other, as if each remembers a different sun. And once in a long while the veil opens an eye: a circular thinning, a hundred meters wide, that drifts slowly across the basin like a spotlight. Where it passes, true daylight falls through for a minute — brutal, white-gold, shocking. The sail-leaves recoil. The moss steams. People stop and lift their faces to it, blinking. Then it closes, and the warm dim settles back, kinder.

The rules of the place reveal themselves slowly:

Stay on the pale paths when the towers hum. The rain that follows is warm but heavy enough to knock you sideways, and the stone channels run fast for ten minutes.

Don't pick the white flowers. Not because it's forbidden — because they dim when cut, and the moths will follow you for hours, bumping.

If a transit thread won't take you, don't insist. It means a tower is dreaming, or balancing, and you'll end up two blocks sideways from where you meant, damp and disoriented.

Leave water for the walls. The basins at the corners are not decorative. The buildings drink from them through capillary stone. An empty basin will develop a crack by morning, a sharp new fracture like a mouth.

Everything here is still negotiating — stone and vine, machine and weather, city and basin. The erosion hasn't had time to forgive anything. The cuts are still sharp. The moss is still deciding what will be ground and what will be wall. The veil still sweats on a cycle that is almost, but not quite, natural. It is an urban place, with addresses and transit lines and maintenance shafts, but it behaves like a young ecosystem: active, testing, growing over its own infrastructure as fast as the infrastructure can print itself back clean.

Stand in the middle of one of those vast spaces between towers at the hour when the light goes truly brown-gold, when the condensation is beading on every edge and the sail-leaves are turning, one by one, toward the sound of your breathing, and you understand the scale of it. Not a town, not a wilderness. A whole region learning how to be inhabited — sparse enough that you can be alone in the middle of a city, open enough that you can see the weather being made above you, strange enough that you stop expecting it to act like any city you knew before.
