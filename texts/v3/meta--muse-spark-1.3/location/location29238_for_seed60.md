It is not a forest. It is not a ruin. It is a room that grew itself around the machines left inside it.

You find it low, where the land sags just enough to hold water without ever becoming a lake. A shallow bowl, maybe a hundred paces across, hamlet-sized, with a rim you only notice when you are already inside — a low swell of root and brick and rust where the outside world stops. Light comes in normally here, midday white-gold, but by the time it reaches you it has been chewed green. It filters down through three, four, five ceilings of leaf, each one wetter than the last, until it lands on everything at once.

The ground is not ground. That is the first rule.

There is soil somewhere underneath, yes, black mud that smells sweet and iron at the same time, but you will never step on it. The floor is living thatch: rhizomes thick as your wrist braided over rhizomes, moss over that, seedlings in the moss, fallen leaves melting into new leaves. Squeeze it and water wells up around your boots — not a puddle, but the permanent seep of a place where the water table breathes an inch below the surface. Every footfall sighs and then fills with tea-colored water and then closes again. If you stand still long enough, tendrils will test the edge of your sole.

It is warm. Not jungle-hot, but bathhouse warm. The air holds more than it should. Your skin never dries. Condensation beads on every underside — leaf, pipe, wire, your own forearm — and then lets go in a constant, arrhythmic rain that has nothing to do with weather. The rocks, where you can find them, weep. The concrete weeps. The iron weeps rust that the vines drink straight, leaving pale green streaks.

And the green is total. Not trees with space between them, but mass. Banana-wide leaves shouldering into philodendron shields into curtains of air-root into trunks that are not trunks but bundles of vines fused by pressure and time. There is no gap unfilled. Saplings grow out of the crotches of larger plants. Epiphytes grow on epiphytes. Even the light seems to have weight, pressed down into ferns that fold over themselves. To move more than an arm's length you have to part it, and it closes behind you without malice, patiently.

For a long time you think this is just an overgrown power station. That is the easy image. You see the first building and you settle on it, then the place corrects you.

Scattered, half-drowned in the mass: a boiler house with its roof caved to a lattice on which gourds hang heavy as sandbags. A dynamo shed, brick gone soft and rounded as bread, door open on darkness that breathes warm oil. A water tower on four iron legs, its tank split and now holding a garden of its own, overflowing in four thin waterfalls that never stop. A switching pylon leaning ten degrees off, its insulators crowned with white orchids that hum faintly when the wind moves the wires. A catwalk that starts nowhere and ends in a tangle of morning glory. Faint marks elsewhere — a foundation corner, a rusted drum cut for a stove, a line of tiles leading into mud.

But they are not dead. That is the second rule.

Deep in the boiler house something still turns. Slow — so slow you feel it in your molars before you hear it. A flywheel, ten feet across, rust-pitted but true, making its revolution once, twice a minute, driving a flat belt up into the canopy where it disappears. With each turn a piston sighs, and a plume of live steam vents sideways through a split pipe, washing a wall of leaves white for a second. The leaves love it. They lean *into* the scald. Around the boiler, the vines have learned the shape of heat: they arch clear in a perfect halo, then crowd back in, blackened tips already sprouting new red shoots.

The dynamo still makes power. You can hear it in the wires — a 50-cycle drone under everything, loud enough to live in your chest. The cables sag low under the weight of moss and ferns and drip water steadily from their lowest points, but at the ceramic bells the air crackles blue in the damp. At night, the caretakers — whoever they were — strung work-lamps. The bulbs are still there, and the plants have adopted them. A gourd has grown completely around one, translucent when it lights — and it does light, flickering on every time the flywheel hits top-dead-center, a slow pulse like a heartbeat. Elsewhere, copper has leached from the windings into the sap. Split a stem near the shed and the vascular fibers glitter metallic green.

This is scaled production turned into ecology. Fallen biomass feeds the firebox through a hopper that the roots themselves stuff — leaf litter, deadfall, whole curtains of vine that grow toward the heat chute as if it were sun. Steam drives shaft. Shaft drives a shredder that chews more biomass. Electricity runs a pump that lifts seep-water to the tower, which rains it back down over everything. No one rakes. No one stokes. It has been running long enough to weather into balance: rust stabilized by tannin, concrete softened to a kind of artificial limestone that orchids root in, iron and chlorophyll in a treaty.

It is loud. That surprises you, because there are no animals.

No birds call. No insects drone in the proper way — a few white moths, blind and silent, blunder between lamps. No monkeys, no rats, not even frogs in the standing seeps, though the water is perfect for them. The absence is complete, and into that absence rushes the other noise: the deep thump-thump of the slow engine, the constant high hiss of steam and dripping, the plink-plink-plink of condensation falling from leaf to leaf to metal drum to leaf again, amplified by the green chamber until it sounds like a warehouse full of clocks. And underneath, vegetal sounds made loud by density: the creak of stems swelling with water, the soft pop of seed pods in the boiler heat, the groan of the belt rubbing against a living pulley grown thick around it.

Strange things happen if you stay still and watch with plant eyes:

The leaves nearest the live wires all turn their copper-veined undersides outward at dusk, as if to catch the field. Flowers here mimic the built things — blossoms shaped exactly like insulators, like fuses, like curled shavings of iron. A fig has rooted *inside* a transformer casing and fruits through the ventilation slots, each fruit warm to the touch. The large trumpet vines open only when steam vents, drinking the pulse of distilled water.

Time moves slow and does not apologize. The flywheel will not speed up. The vines will not hurry to crush the buildings. Growth and decay and rust are all happening at the same patient gradient. A fallen leaf takes a season to become boiler ash and then boiler ash takes a season to become leaf again. If you cut a path in the morning, by evening the edges will have sweated shut with new shoots, not to punish you but because closing is what this place does.

If you sleep here — and the warmth invites it, the drone lulls — you will wake damp to the bone, with leaf-prints on your skin and the taste of iron and green on your tongue, and the lamps still pulsing, slow, and the water still falling upward and then down, and the whole packed, breathing room holding its machines like seeds in a fruit: not using them, not worshipping them, just having grown around the idea of industry until industry became another way to photosynthesize.
