You come over the col at mid-morning and the valley opens below you — not a vastness, a workable size. A bowl you could walk across in a day if there were a path. Three sharp ridges, black and too-new, come down to a flat braided floor, and beyond that another col, white with light. Town-or-valley size. You can see all of it and none of it clearly, because the light up here is too much.

It is bright in a way that has weight. At this height the air is thin and cool — breath-fog cool even at noon — and the sun does not filter, it hammers. Everything wet shines. Every leaf-edge, every wire, every basalt flake throws back a hard white glint. You squint and your eyes water, and that feels correct. The place wants to be seen through wet eyes.

The terrain is young. That is the first thing your feet tell you. Down off the col the rock is not rounded, not worn into kindness. It is clinker and scoria and split basalt slabs with edges still like broken plates. A lava flow, not long ago in geologic time, or a landslip the mountain hasn't bothered to soften. The gullies are V-cuts, raw. Scree still perches at its angle of rest. But the green has already taken it — impossibly fast, impossibly thick — and that is the second thing.

Lush is too pale a word. It is layered, dense, near-touching. The ground is not ground, it is a mattress of moss half a meter deep, sphagnum and wool-moss and star-moss in greens so saturated they look black in the hollows. Out of that push ferns chest-high, and cloudberry, and dwarf birch no taller than your knee but ancient, twisted, their trunks thick as your wrist, bark polished by wet. Above that, alders bent sideways by snow, forming a second canopy you have to duck under. You do not walk here so much as part the vegetation and reseal it behind you. If you stand still, it touches you everywhere: calf, thigh, wrist, neck. Condensation wicks off it onto your clothes.

Because there is almost no standing water. No lake, no true river. That is the rule here: water never pools, it only clings. What damp there is arrives as interception. Night fog rolls up from below and snags on every frond. Dawn is a white-out of droplets. By ten the bright sun burns the fog off, and for an hour the whole valley drips — a million quiet ticks as each leaf unloads — and shallow rills run for a few hundred meters in the black sand and then vanish back into moss. Afternoon: dry to the eye, wet to the hand. Squeeze any handful of green and water comes out. Lick a rock and it tastes of iron. The stones in the streambeds are stained dark for six inches above the current trickle, a tide-mark of where water sometimes is.

And through this softness, the iron.

At first you think they are deadfalls. Then you see the straight lines. A penstock, a meter in diameter, comes off the upper ridge and runs downslope on short concrete feet, disappearing into fern, reappearing, disappearing. It is rust-red and moss-green in alternating felted patches, and where a seam has split, a thin sheet of water weeps out and is immediately drunk by moss. Further down, a shed — corrugated roof collapsed under a quilt of vegetation, walls still standing. Inside, in the green gloom, a turbine housing the size of a kitchen stove, a bank of gauges with fogged glass, a wheel that still turns.

That is the strangeness you have to adjust to: it is all still running, slowly, and silently.

This was, is, an industrial place. Not a town — never a town — just scattered works across the valley floor and sides: five or six stations, a transformer yard, the towers of an aerial ropeway marching from one ridge to the other with buckets hanging motionless except they are not motionless, they creep inches an hour. Cables sag under the weight of moss and fern that have colonized them like branches. Insulators wear caps of lichen. A concrete flume, broken, now serves as a planter for a row of perfect miniature rowans. Copper ground-straps run out into the peat and have gone verdigris, and the moss around them grows a different, electric green.

It should roar. It doesn't. That is the other rule: sound does not carry beyond arm's-length.

The moss eats it. The saturated air eats it. Even the machines have learned slowness. The turbine you found turns at perhaps two revolutions a minute, its bearings packed with peat and grease, driving a shaft that drives a pump that lifts fog-water to a header tank that feeds... something else. There is no clank, no diesel, no whine. When you put your hand on the housing you feel a deep, faint tremor more than hear it, a purr conducted through bone. The ropeway wheels creak once every few minutes, a soft wooden sound, and then settle. Water ticks. Your own boots make no sound at all on moss; your breathing sounds loud to you and then stops at two paces. A rock dislodged on the ridge clicks once against another rock and is swallowed.

Electrified, yes. In the transformer yard the busbars still hum — if you stand directly under them on a damp morning you can feel the hairs on your forearms lift. Small amber pilot lamps burn in day-brightness on panels whose paint has long since gone, tended by no one. The power goes nowhere you can see, or everywhere: into the ground, into electrolyzers making a slow fizz of hydrogen that bubbles up through moss pools with a sound like soda, into heaters that keep one pipe just above freezing. It is scaled production shrunk to a patient trickle. Output measured not in tons per hour but in millimeters per year.

Ecology has reorganized around that. With almost no animals — that is the quietest absence — the plants have taken the metal into partnership. You see almost nothing move. No flocks, no herds. Once in an hour a white ptarmigan bursts from underfoot with a heart-stopping whirr of wings that is gone instantly, leaving silence deeper than before. A single feral sheep, wool matted with burrs, watches you from a penstock and then steps delicately away. Moths, torpid in the cool, cling to the warm side of transformer boxes. Everything else is green and rust and stone. Bumblebees work the heather in slow loops, too cold to hurry. The pollinators are mist and gravity. The decomposers are rust itself: steel flaking into iron oxide that stains the sphagnum orange, and the sphagnum holding it, building a new iron-peat that will be rock again in ten thousand years.

The air smells of ozone and wet wool and crushed juniper and hot dust where sun hits an electrical box. Your skin cools quickly in shade, warms instantly in sun. Condensation forms on the underside of every cable and falls, and in the bright backlight each fall is a brief prism. The towers cast shadows razor-sharp on the fern sea. Where the ropeway buckets pass low, they comb a permanent tunnel through the alder, and the tunnel walls are trimmed, dripping, intensely lit at the far end.

If you stay until evening, you learn the slow dynamic. Nothing here happens fast except light. The fog begins to form at your ankles around four, not falling but condensing out of the cooling air onto every surface. By six the towers are ghosted. The pilot lamps become visible as small suns. The turbine still turns. The moss swells almost imperceptibly, taking up the day's water to give back tomorrow. A guy-wire, over-tensioned decades ago, has over twenty years sawed halfway through a basalt boulder — you can see the groove, sharp, fresh, lined with rock flour turned to mud, and hear nothing. The place is patient. It does not erode so much as persist, sharp edges slowly upholstered.

Do not shout here. Not because it is forbidden, but because it is useless. Sound will not go with you. Light will. Water will stay on your coat until you wring it out.
