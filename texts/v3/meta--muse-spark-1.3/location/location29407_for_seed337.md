You come to it from above, because there is no other way to come to it.

The plain runs flat and cool to the horizon — dry short grass, pale dust that never quite becomes soil — and then it stops. Not in a cliff. In a drop. A shallow, broad, irregular bowl sunk below the level of the world, maybe forty meters down at its deepest, a kilometer and a half across, rim to rim. A town-valley. You can stand on the lip and see the whole of it at once, and still not take it in.

Because it is full. Packed to the edges.

From the rim, it looks at first like a honeycomb or a wasp nest pressed into the earth. Roofs shoulder to roofs, walls sharing walls, terraces stepping on terraces, no gaps, no yards, no waste. A dense urban mat fitted into the depression so tightly the shape of the hollow dictates the shape of the streets, which are not streets at all but stair-alleys a shoulder wide, switchbacking down the raw sides. Ladders. Plank bridges thrown from roof to roof. Chimneys everywhere, but the smoke rises thin and straight in the still air.

And the rock around and under it is *new*.

That is the strangest, most ordinary thing about the place. This is not a canyon carved by a river over a million years. There is no talus slope, no rounded boulders, no sandbars. The walls of the bowl are fresh fracture — angular, sharp, unweathered. Basalt and pale limestone broken like bread, edges still keen enough to cut your palm if you brace yourself on the way down. You can see the chisel marks where the break was helped along. In places the wall is stepped where people have cut back into it to make another house platform, and the cut faces are clean and dark, unsullied by lichen except for a faint green dusting in the dampest cracks. The floor is not soil but crushed rock fines, quarry dust tamped by ten thousand feet into something almost like pavement, but gritty. It crunches. There is no topsoil yet. The geology hasn't had time to soften. The world here is under construction, still deciding what shape it will be.

They are building it downward and inward. That is the rule.

The stone for the houses comes from the hollow itself. Every new room is a removal. You cut a block out of the wall, you square it with stone hammers and copper-wedged chisels, you stack it a meter over to make a wall for your cousin. The houses are low, flat-roofed, one or two stories, made of that same fresh rock mortared with mud and roofed with timber and thatch and hide. Timber is precious — brought up from somewhere else beyond the plain, pole by pole — so they use as little as possible: lintels, ladder rungs, scaffolding lashed together with wet leather that shrinks tight. Everywhere you look there is scaffolding, half-walls with the mortar still dark, a pile of sorted rubble waiting to become something else, a wooden lever leaning against a boulder that hasn't been moved yet. Hand tools only. Rope. Shoulder. Wedge and mallet. You hear it before you see the details: a faint, arrhythmic *tink-tink* of stonework, stopping and starting.

The air down there is different. Cooler by several degrees. Cold pools in low places and stays. In the morning your breath shows, even in summer. The bowl traps its own weather. Overnight, condensation beads on every rock face, on every thatch edge, and in the early hours a low fog sits in the bottom two meters, white and still, smelling of wet stone and hearth ash. By midday under a normal, pale blue sky the sun reaches straight down and burns it off. Light is ordinary daylight — not cavern dark, not glaring — but it moves fast. Shadows climb the eastern wall like water filling a cup, then fall again. People time their work by which terrace is in sun.

Water is not abundant, but it is present in pockets. That is how they live here. There is no river in the hollow, no spring gushing. Instead, seeps. Dark damp streaks down the fresh walls where groundwater finds a new fracture and weeps out. Drips that never quite stop. After a rain — which comes intermittently, a cold spatter that darkens the dust for an hour — water gathers in the low-cut cisterns and rock basins, in clay jars set under eave-drips, in shallow channels chipped along the stairways to lead it down to communal pits lined with puddled clay. Between rains, the place is mostly dry, dusty feet and dry throats, but if you know where to put your hand you will always find damp: under a slab, at the back of a shared wall, in the moss.

Vegetation is sparse to the point of intention. Nothing wild has had time to colonize the raw rock. No trees rooted in the walls. What green there is, is carried in and tended fiercely. Small pockets: a leveled tray of imported dirt on a roof growing onions and bitter herbs, a gourd vine trained up a ladder, a patch of moss deliberately encouraged around a seep to keep the moisture from evaporating. Down in the darkest alley bottoms, where the drip never stops, liverworts and pale shoots push out of mortar cracks. On the plain above, the steppe grass ends at the rim as if cut with a knife — a few hardy tufts have seeded down onto ledges, wind-blown, shivering in the cooler air. That is all. It makes the town look gray from above, stone-colored, except for those startling small rectangles of green.

Animals are rare. There isn't forage for herds, or cover for burrows in stone that hasn't cracked yet. A few goats kept in rock pens, cropping kitchen scraps. Pigeons that nest on the scaffolds. A dog asleep in dust, too tired to bark. At night, rats move along the drainage cuts. Once in a while a hawk circles the thermals over the bowl, looks down at all that packed humanity, and goes elsewhere. The ecology is mostly human.

And yet for how quiet it is, it is never still.

Sound is muffled by the shape. The bowl swallows noise. No wind up here reaches down except as a faint movement at the rim grasses. What you hear, standing in one of those tight alleys with walls pressing close on both sides, is close and small: the scuff of sandals on grit, a murmur two roofs over, a baby crying and being hushed, the wooden creak of a water bucket on a rope, the steady *plip-plip* of seep-water into a jar, the *tink* of a chisel, then silence again. People speak low here, as if the rock were listening. Even the work crews, prying at a new terrace, grunt more than they shout. At midday there is a kind of collective pause, spoons in bowls, and you can hear the condensation ticking off thatch.

The processes are slow but everywhere. Frost at night pries a little deeper into yesterday's cut — you find fresh flakes on the ground in the morning. Water finds a new path and a householder re-chips her channel. A wall settles a finger's width and is re-mudded. Children carry fines in baskets from the upper cuttings to tamp down the lane below. Gardens are weeded, goats milked, hides scraped, pots mended with copper rivets. Predator and prey, birth and rot, weather and work, all at hand-tool pace, all crowded together with no room to spare.

If you stay a few days you learn the local logics, which are not written but obvious:

Build only where rock has already been opened. Share walls to save stone. Never waste seep-water; every roof drains to someone's jar. Do your cutting in the morning when the stone is cold and brittle. Leave the rim grass alone — it holds the edge from crumbling.

No one remembers the plain without the hollow, though the old ones say their grandparents saw the ground sag and crack after a wet winter, and they moved down into the break because the wind was less and the stone was ready to hand. Whether that is true hardly matters. The place feels embryonic, as if it only just began and hasn't decided if it will become a city or a quarry that people happen to live in. The rock is still sharp. The streets are still narrow because no one has had to widen them yet. The packed density is not squalor — it's warmth, it's proximity in a cool place, shoulder to shoulder against the chill coming off fresh stone.

From the rim at dusk, when the sun has left the floor and only the western upper terraces still catch light, you see lamps come on below, hundreds of small tallow flickers in rock niches, and the whole bowl glows faintly like embers in a pit. The tink-tink stops. The dripping continues. The fog begins to gather again, low and white, between the roofs.
