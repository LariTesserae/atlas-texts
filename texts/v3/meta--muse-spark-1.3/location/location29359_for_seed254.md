You go down into it.

From the flat fields there is no warning except the damp in the air and then the ground simply stops. A rim of weathered limestone, rounded soft as old teeth, with an iron railing gone orange with rust and green with moss. Below you — forty, fifty feet below — is the town, if you can call something that small a town. You can see all of it at once. Rim to rim, wall to wall. A single bowl of living.

It was a quarry once. You can still read that in the stepped sides, the long terraces cut for men and carts a hundred years ago. But the steps have settled. Their edges are no longer sharp. They are bellied with soil and root-mat, furred with moss, furrowed where water has run for decades in the same patient lines. Nothing fresh here. Everything has found its angle of rest.

And everything is growing.

Not growing *in* the pit. Growing *as* the pit. From the rim you look down onto a canopy — not trees exactly, but a packed, breathing thatch of banana leaf and taro leaf and fern and vine, tarpaulin-green and black-green in the gloom, rising in mounds over roofs, swallowing walls, bridging alleys. The houses below are not cleared of plants. They are kept by them. Brick row-cottages and sheds and workshops, two and three stories jammed shoulder to shoulder on the quarry floor and up the lower terraces, and out of every mortar joint, every gutter, every cracked slab, something green has taken hold. Ivy holds the bricks together more than mortar does now. Roof beams have become trellises. A warehouse wall on the north side is no longer a wall at all but a vertical garden ten feet thick — roots, soil held in netting, philodendron and maidenhair spilling down over windows that are still, impossibly, in use, lamplight glowing amber from behind the leaves.

It is warm down there. That hits you on the third switchback stair. The air temperature climbs as you descend, five degrees, eight degrees warmer than the fields above. Part sun-trap, part rot, part steam. The pit holds heat. The dense mass of transpiring leaf exhales it. And the engines make it.

Because this is a working place. Under the green, it is iron and brick and noise.

You hear it long before you see the details: a low, saturated roar that fills the bowl and has nowhere to go. Pumps. You feel them in your sternum. Three big steam-driven sump pumps in the central mill house run day and night, because the water will not stop. This whole bottom wants to be a lake. Springs weep straight out of the quarry faces in white threads, seeps stain the limestone black, condensation runs endlessly off leaf-tip to leaf-tip and patters onto canvas awnings, onto iron sheet, onto your shoulders. Gutters gush. The floors of the alleys are wet brick, black water standing in the joints, little canals along every lane leading to the central sump, where the grates are combed clear of leaves twice a day. Pipes as thick as a man's torso run on brackets along every terrace, sweating, dripping, ticking as they cool. Belts slap in the mill. A ventilator fan on the boiler house turns and turns, beating the damp air without ever drying it.

Light barely reaches. At noon, a shaft of white sun stands straight down for an hour and lights the steam over the sump in a column. The rest of the day is gloom — a green, underwater dim. The high walls cut off the morning and evening. The canopy cuts off the rest. So the town has wired itself with light: strings of caged work-lamps, sodium orange and bare white LED, hung under catwalks, nailed to trees that are also posts, burning all day in the damp. In that half-light everything glistens. Leaf, brick, skin, iron. There are no dry surfaces below the rim.

The paths are that narrow. There is no room between presences. You go single-file down lanes where on your left is a house wall warm with boiler pipe and on your right is a wall of living taro, stalks thick as wrists, leaves big as doors, shouldering into you. Washing lines, power cables, vine stems, and water hose all share the same overhead space, sagging low enough to touch with your head. Catwalks of rusted grating and rotting plank stitch second stories together over the lanes, themselves become planters — grass and ferns ankle-deep on a bridge you have to cross. Doorways open straight onto garden beds that are also foundations. Children’s toys, oil drums, compost heaps, battery banks, yam piles, coal scuttles: everything sits within arm’s reach of green that is already reaching back.

It smells of crushed green and hot oil and wet lime and woodsmoke and human sweat, all held close by the walls.

What is missing takes you a while to place, because it is so loud. No dogs. No chickens in the yards, though there are coops turned into tool sheds. No pigeons on the wires, no crows on the rim looking down. No birdsong under the engine drone. No mosquito whine, even in this wet heat — the sulfur from the boilers and the constant oil on the standing water has driven them out, the old-timers say. No rats moving in the thatch. Only people, and plants, and machines, in a closed bargain.

The ecology is simple because it is total. Whatever could live on wet stone in warm shade does, and crops that love feet-wet have been folded in: taro and watercress and rice in the lowest paddies by the sump, beans and gourds trained up every lamp post and drainpipe. Fig roots split the terrace stairs and are allowed to, because they also pin them. Moss is the mortar, lichen the paint. Fungus softens every plank and is scraped off every morning with a flat spade kept by the door. If you stopped cutting for a week, the lanes would close. If you stopped pumping for a day, the cellars would drown. Everyone here knows the two jobs.

Change is slow. That is the rule of the bowl. Belts blur, water falls, fans beat — frantic motion on top of a stillness that is almost geological. Brick softens to clay. Iron thickens with rust under its grease. A root as thick as your thumb, that you could have cut last spring with secateurs, is this autumn a beam you need a saw for. The terraces slump a finger’s width a year and are shored with new brick that will itself be green by winter. People rebuild the same stair, re-hang the same pipe, re-clear the same grate. Their grandparents did it.

Stand at the sump in the center at dusk, when the lamps come fully on and the canopy above goes black and the weeping walls glitter all around like the inside of a well. Water pours in from a dozen mouths, clattering into the basin. The pumps take it and throw it up, up in two fat iron throats, over the rim and out to the dry fields, where it steams faintly in the cool evening air. The whole hamlet thrums. Leaves drip. Someone shouts over the noise about a clogged screen. Steam sighs off a release valve and condenses instantly on the banana leaves, raining back down warm.

Above you, the rim is a thin pale line. Below you, there is nowhere else to go.
