You expect cold up here. That's the first wrong thing.

You have climbed out of the blue — three thousand meters of switchback through pine and then dwarf pine and then nothing — and the air should be thin and cutting. It is thin. It is not cutting. It opens at the rim like an oven door.

At first you think it's a crater. It is not, quite. It is too small and too new for that. A shallow saucer, maybe a hundred and fifty paces across, blasted or slumped or grown into the top of the mountain, and ringed all around by a lip of fresh rock. Beyond the lip, the world falls away on every side into hazy heat. Far below, clouds are still trying to form and failing. Up here, there is only the bowl and the sun standing straight over it.

The rock is young. You can tell with your hands before your eyes catch up. Nothing is rounded. The basalt — black glass where it broke clean, rust-red where the iron is already bleeding out — comes up in fins and shards and ropy coils that still show the direction they flowed. Edges that would cut a boot. Channels only a finger deep where last week's rain tried to find a way out and didn't. The ground rings faintly underfoot, like thin ice over something hollow and warm. In places it *is* warm. Put your palm flat on the dark slab at noon and it comes away stinging. The mountain hasn't finished cooling.

And yet it weeps.

That's the second wrong thing, and the one you don't get used to. In this heat, at this height, there should be no water. But the stone sweats. The whole saucer sweats. Seeps line every inward-facing wall in dark vertical tongues. Pumice hollows hold pools so still and clear they are only visible as a thickening of light. The air itself is wet to breathe — thin, hot soup that condenses on your upper lip, on your eyelashes, on the underside of every leaf. In the morning, the guidebooks would say, there is steam. By midday it has burned off into a trembling clarity, and by three the bowl will make its own weather.

For now: wet black rock, white mineral mustaches where the water has evaporated and left its salt and silica behind, and green. Furious green, but not everywhere.

The green grows in islands. That's the rule here. Between islands is emptiness — ten, twenty meters of naked glass and gravel where nothing lives, where the sun bounces back up at you from below as well as above. And then, where a crack holds a little soil, where a seep keeps the stone permanently damp, life has gone insane.

A clump of tree-ferns no taller than you, but fronds as wide as doors, waxy to shed heat, roots wrapped white around the rock like knuckles. Mosses in cushions so thick you sink to the wrist, electric green, steaming faintly. Pitcher plants squat low, their pitchers half-full of rain and digesting flies, rims beaded with nectar and condensation both. Orchids with leaves like blown glass, translucent to let light through without cooking. Everything is drinking at once. If you crouch and look close you can see the water moving — beads running along hairs, along veins, being pulled up out of the stone.

Nothing connects the islands. Each one is its own damp world, its own argument that life is possible, surrounded by black desert. You learn quickly to step only on the black. The green is too busy to be stepped on.

There are buildings, if you can call them that. Not really. At first you miss them entirely because they don't interrupt the green; they interrupt the empty.

Three ribs, tall as a house but thin as a wrist, lean over the center of the saucer at slightly impossible angles. Not stone. Not metal you know. When the sun hits them they go transparent at the edges, showing interference colors — oil-on-water purples and greens — and when cloud passes they go dark again. They are beaded always with water, droplets that grow but never quite fall, that hang until they vanish. Beneath them, half-sunk in gravel, five or six low discs, like millstones, warm to the touch on one side and shockingly cold on the other, though no shadow explains it. A cairn of balanced slabs near the eastern seep has been threaded with a filament, black on black, that you only see when it is wet and catches light.

No one lives here. Someone was here. A boot-print melted — not pressed, melted — a centimeter deep into glass that must have still been soft. A low ring wall, knee-high, following no defensive logic, just marking where it is safe to sleep above the seep line. A lone doorway frame standing by itself near the largest pool, holding no door, framing only ferns and sky.

But the ribs and discs are still working. That's clear. Stand under them at noon and the air is a degree cooler on your skin. The sweat on your arms pricks and lifts. The faint, high static you thought was your own blood resolves into the faint ticking of water being pulled out of air, molecule by molecule, and fed into stone. It is technology that doesn't bother looking like technology. No seams, no lights, no noise. Just a place that stays wet when by all rights it should have baked dry a decade ago, when the lava was still warm. Someone, something, seeded a spring here and left the tap running.

The animals know. Which is to say they come rarely, and leave quickly.

There should be insects screaming in this heat, in this green. There are not. The bowl is mostly quiet — unnaturally so for a jungle in miniature. What you hear, when you stop panting, is water counting itself: *plink* from seep to pool, *plink* from leaf tip to moss, a long *tick... tick... tick* as the black slab expands and contracts. Your own footfall too loud on glass. Every ten minutes or so, a glasswing bee, huge and almost invisible, crosses the empty between islands in a straight line and is gone. In the largest pool, if you are patient and do not cast shadow, white frogs surface — blind-looking, translucent-bellied, throats pulsing — to gulp air and sink again. Once a day a hawk comes up from below, circles the rim without entering, as if the air inside were too thick to fly in, and tilts away.

And then the strangeness that isn't the ribs.

Mid-afternoon, as the heat peaks, the droplets stop falling. You notice it first on your arm: a bead of sweat lifts, hesitates, hangs a millimeter off the skin before deciding to fall. Over the pools, motes and midges hang motionless. Your shadow, when you move quickly, lags a half-beat behind you, as if light itself were wading. The ribs go very dark.

Then the bowl breathes out.

It starts at the center — a white wisp that wasn't there, that condenses out of nothing. Within minutes the whole saucer holds a cloud, its own private, hamlet-sized cloud, bounded perfectly by the lip. Outside the rim: hard blue sky and the far valley glinting. Inside: white, dripping, cool, visibility twenty meters. It rains, briefly and vertically and only here, drumming on fern and glass, filling the pitchers to overflowing, turning every filament and moss hair into a rosary. The frogs sing — one startled, ecstatic chorus — and then stop.

Twenty minutes later it is gone. Burned from above. The stone steams. The pools are full again. The ribs are beaded again. The air smells of hot iron and crushed green and ozone.

That is the cycle. Every day. Boil, sweat, cloud, rain, steam. The young rock cracks a little more each night as it cools; the moss takes the new crack by morning; the discs keep the water coming; the islands grow a millimeter outward into the black.

You can walk across the whole place in ten minutes, rim to rim. You could see it all in an hour. But you will stay longer, because the rules are simple and absolute and you learn them with your body: drink only from the running seep, not the still pool where the frogs live. Rest in the empty, not in the green. Do not touch the ribs at three o'clock when they are making rain — your hand comes away numb for an hour, smelling of metal. And be quiet. The place is doing delicate work, holding water up here where water should not be, and loudness feels like it might spill it.

When you finally climb back out over the lip, your shirt is soaked though you never swam. Below you the mountain drops into heat-haze. Above you the sun is still white and normal and merciless. Behind you, in the saucer you cannot see anymore, water is already beading again on black glass, waiting to become cloud.
