High up, the air thins and cools and then the ground levels out — not a peak, a roof.

You walk out onto it and it keeps going. A plateau so wide the far edge makes its own weather. Morning comes in normal and white, a cool sun high enough to see by plainly, not glaring, not dimmed. Light falls straight down and finds water everywhere.

This is not a dry height. This is a wet height.

Under a thin skin of peat and lime the water table sits almost at the surface, pressing up. Every footstep is soft. The ground gives, sighs, takes the print and then fills it from below with clear cold seep. Between your boots the earth weeps.

From a distance it looks — unmistakably — like a city.

Terraces step down in pale stone, level after level, squared and rimmed and divided. Basins, courtyards, cisterns, long low walls, channels that branch like streets. The whole vast flat is partitioned, dense and near-touching, one structure shouldering into the next for miles, for tens of miles, until haze takes it. You think at first: who built this, where are the builders.

No one built this. Nothing here was cut or hauled or mortared. That is the first rule of this place: stone is grown, not placed.

The stone is travertine and tufa, cream and bone and rust-yellow, laid down grain by grain by water that is heavy with dissolved limestone. Where the springs well up from below — and they well up everywhere, cold, mineral-sharp — the water spreads thin over lips and drops a lace of calcite as it goes. Cyanobacteria and moss help it along, catching the mineral on their films. Over centuries, over millennia, the films become rims, the rims become dams, the dams become pools, the pools become terraces.

It is infrastructure without intention. Up-slope pools feed down-slope pools through notches and spillways that behave exactly like gutters and aqueducts. A broad upper flat spills through a narrow throat into a series of smaller, deeper cells. A collapsed rim becomes a plaza of broken slabs, already being re-cemented by fresh flow. The city repairs and unrepairs itself continuously.

Because the water never stops. That is the second rule: everything here is in motion, but slowly enough that you have to kneel to see it.

Stand still and listen. There is almost no sound. This is one of the quietest places of its size on earth. No traffic of insects, no chorus. The wind up here is cool and constant but it has little to catch — vegetation is sparse, cropped short by cold and wet and thin soil. Tufts of sedge, star-moss cushions, black-green liverwort in the cracks, a few tight pincushions of alpine campion holding on to the drier wall-tops. Their roots do not anchor so much as sip.

So what you hear, when you hold your breath in the thin air, is water at the scale of drips: a trickle finding a new lip, a pool overtopping with a faint ticking sheet, a seep ticking out of a weeping rock face. The rocks weep everywhere. Cut banks, terrace risers, old weathered scarps — they are dark at the base and glistening, beaded, exhaling cold. Put your hand to them and your palm comes away wet and smelling faintly of iron and moss. The air itself condenses. Breath shows. Mist lifts off the shallowest pans in the late morning and then settles again as dew.

Kneel at any pool and the density of the place reveals itself. It is not empty. It is crowded at small scale. One square meter holds layers: open sky water on top, then a floating dust of pollen and ash, then a skin of algae olive and ochre, then calcite needles growing downward like inverted frost, then black peat, then white marl, then living moss, then dead moss becoming peat again. Snails no bigger than a grain move along the rims. Midge larvae flick in the sun-warmed corners. Everything touches everything else.

Larger animals are rare. You can walk an hour and see no movement except water. Then — a pika darts between two rimstones with a mouthful of sedge. Far off a raven crosses, once, its wingbeats audible for a moment because there is nothing else to cover them, then gone. There are footprints of deer or elk pressed into the soft marl at the marsh-edge where the terraces dissolve into true bog, but the makers are elsewhere, down in the willows. This height belongs mostly to the mineral and the microbial, to things that do not need to move fast.

And yet it is old, settled in its form. Nothing here is sharp or new. The highest rims are rounded, pitted by rain, lichen-mapped in gray-green and orange. Old collapse hollows have softened into shallow vegetated bowls. Channels that once ran straight have meandered and braided and found a new balance. Long-existed, weathered, in-its-own-state. Flood comes, freeze comes — in winter the whole city locks into ice and heaves, cracking rims that will be healed by spring flow — and the balance holds.

Scale deceives. What looks from ankle-height like a neighborhood of bathtubs becomes, when you climb the fifteen feet to the top of a master divide and look outward, a continent. To the north the terraces merge into patterned string bog, ridges and flarks alternating in stripes for as far as you can see. To the east a chain of larger lakes, steel-blue in the noon light, steaming faintly where cold air meets slightly warmer spring water. To the south and west, more white stone, more water, breaking at last into low cloud that is not coming in from elsewhere but being made here, by evaporation off ten thousand shallow mirrors.

Your own weather. A cloud born over the pools drifts a mile, darkens, rains briefly onto the pools, and dissipates. Post-rain, the whole plateau shines. Every rim drips. Every hollow holds sky. Your pant legs are soaked to the knee from brushing sedge. Your boots are heavy with marl.

If you stay until evening, the light stays honest — no strange color, no second sun — just a cooling of the white to blue. The pools go from glare to glass. The weeping faces darken. The cold deepens by a degree that you feel in your lungs more than your skin. Water keeps moving under a skin of coming ice, laying down another invisible film of stone.

Below you, layered and dense and silent, the unbuilt city ticks and seeps and grows, a town made entirely of rain and rock and slow life, with streets for water and houses for nothing but water, extending beyond walking, beyond counting, content to be vast and wet and almost empty and utterly full.
