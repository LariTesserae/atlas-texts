High up, where the air thins out and goes sharp in your lungs, there is a place you would walk over without knowing.

Not a cave. Caves have learned how to be caves. This hasn't.

Two slabs of ridge-rock, big as houses, leaned and caught against each other when the slope shifted — recently, as mountains count it. Still sharp at the break. The crack between them is no wider than your shoulders turned sideways. Cold wind combs the scree outside. Then you duck, and squeeze, and the mountain closes over you.

The first thing is the warmth.

It doesn't make sense at altitude. Outside your fingers were numb. Inside, the air holds you like a mouth. Warm, thick, wet. It gets into your clothes immediately. Your skin prickles and sweats and cannot cool itself because the air is already full.

The second thing is the dark.

Not the dark of night. Absolute. The kind that stays behind your eyelids whether they are open or closed. No adaptation comes. You wait, and wait, and no shapes emerge. Your eyes keep trying to find an edge and fail. So you stop trying, and your hands take over.

It is a room. That is all. A pocket, perhaps four long paces by three, and so low you cannot stand. You crouch, then sit, because the ceiling crowds down to meet your crown. Everything is near-touching. Floor and walls and low roof lean in. You can touch opposite sides without stretching. When you breathe out, the breath comes back to you off stone inches from your face, warm and mineral.

The rock itself is raw. That is the strangest feeling under your palms — it has no history yet. No smoothed channels, no polished flowstone, no old patient carving of water. This stone was broken yesterday in geologic time and has not yet been taught by weather. Edges are crisp enough to skin a knuckle. Pimples and nubs of new mineral beginnings push out like a rash: stalactites not yet stalactites, only blunt nipples a fingernail tall. Under your thumb some of the tuff is still soft. You could dent it. Embryonic. Unfinished.

And it weeps.

Everywhere, the stone sweats water. Not in streams, not even in trickles. The mountain above holds snowmelt and summer rain close to the surface here, and it presses down through the porous roof drop by drop. Put your cheek to the wall and it is cool and beaded. Cup your hand under any overhang and within a minute a bead swells, breaks, falls. The floor is a shallow, warm skin of water over stone — ankle-deep at most — that never drains and never rises. You hear your own shifting in it, slow, viscous.

The air is so saturated it condenses on you. Droplets form on your eyebrows, on the hairs of your arms, on your lips. Inhale and it tastes of iron and warm dust. Exhale and the moisture of you joins it. It is difficult to tell where the rock's wetness ends and yours begins. Water near surface, water on surface, water in air — a closed breath cycling: weep, drip, evaporate, condense, weep again. Nothing leaves. Nothing arrives except the slow seep from above.

What lives here has learned to do without.

Sparse. That is the word. From a crack in the roof where the outside world almost intrudes — a slit no thicker than a blade, letting in cold air but, impossibly, no light — hang a few pale rootlets. You find them with your forehead before you understand them. Thin as thread, blind-white, groping down from the alpine cushions rooted above. They dangle a finger's length, two, and stop, tasting the warm dark and hesitating. Some are tipped with a bead of water like a held breath. There is no green here. No leaf. Only the intention of a plant.

Under your fingertips, some stones are slick — not with water but with life, a bacterial film, tasteless, odorless, making the raw basalt slightly soapy. And very rarely, something moves. Once in a long sitting you might feel the tickle of the place's only animal: a colorless springtail, blind, no bigger than a crumb, that lives on the film and on fallen root hairs. You will not hear it. You will only know it was there because it is suddenly not.

For the most part it is quiet. Mostly. Mostly quiet enough that your own heart becomes loud.

The water keeps its own time. Plink — somewhere to your left, a drop falls from nub to pool. Then a long nothing. Then plink — to your right, answering, but not in rhythm. The interval is minutes. You begin to count and lose count. Your shifting sends a low slosh that dies instantly; the close walls swallow echo. There is no wind inside. No creak. Only, very faint, the tick and settle of stone cooling and warming by fractions you cannot feel, and your own breathing, slow in the dense air.

People have been here before you. Not many. Not to live.

With your hands you find them, scattered, left at floor level where the water is calmest. Someone, with only hands and simple tools, set three shallow bowls of stone in a row — not carved so much as pecked and ground, rims still rough. They sit half-sunk to catch the drip. Beside them, a wooden dipper, hand-whittled, its handle gone soft and swollen from perpetual wet, but intact. A short low curb of flat stones, stacked without mortar, holds back a slightly deeper pool, clear and warm as blood. Finger grooves in the soft mud at the edge where someone knelt and steadied themselves. A bit of twisted plant-fiber twine, tied around a nub of rock and left.

No metal. No cut marks of saws. Nothing that needed more than hands, stone, wood, patience.

You understand without being told: this is a drinking place. A high hidden cistern. Climbers, herders, pilgrims — whoever knows the slit — come down into the warm dark to rest and drink the weeping rock. They leave the cups for the next.

And the oddness of it — beyond the dark and the warmth coexisting where they shouldn't — is how reluctant everything is to change.

Drops fall but the puddle does not deepen. Evaporation lifts but the air does not dry. The nubs grow by a molecule a year and never get longer in a human life. The rootlets neither grow nor rot. The dipper neither floats away nor sinks further. Even sound behaves differently here, flattened and held close, as if the dense, wet air refuses to carry anything away.

It is equilibrium. Frozen, but not cold.

You sit long enough and your sweat and the condensation become indistinguishable, your breath joins the cycle, your knees ache from crouching and you stop noticing. You lift a stone bowl — it is surprisingly heavy — and drink. The water is warm, faintly mineral, faintly of stone-soup and time. The drop that replaces what you drank is already swelling overhead. It will fall in its own minute, whether you are here or not.

Outside, far above the scree, storms will come and go, seasons will strip the slope. In here, nothing happens, beautifully, continuously.

When you leave, you duck back through the slit into thin cold and wind and blinding day, and the pocket seals itself behind you. Warm, dark, wet, crowded, unfinished — waiting, drop by drop, to become itself.
