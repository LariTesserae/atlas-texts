You come to the rim and the ground simply gives out.

Not a cliff, not a valley — a Pit. A great stepped bowl sunk below the level of the fields, below the level of the sea that you can smell but cannot see, a town-sized absence cut straight down through topsoil and clay and sand into blue-grey raw earth. From up here you can take in the whole of it at once: the far wall a mile and a half off, hazed, the bottom lost in low cloud that never lifts. It is open country, but inverted. A country with a sky made of steam.

The air is warm. Not hot-season hot, but bathwater warm, close, breathed-in-and-out too many times. Your shirt sticks in the first minute. Sweat doesn't evaporate. Everything sweats here — the rocks sweat, the pipes sweat, the underside of every handrail beads and drips.

Because this is a wet place that was never meant to be wet. They dug down past the water and the water is coming back.

Stand a while and you hear it before you see it: a roar that fills your skull from the molars up. Not one sound but a saturated, packed-together sound with no gaps in it. The spillways where the groundwater rivers pour off the upper benches in white sheets. The pump stations on the middle benches hammering day and night, diesel and electric both, their exhausts shuddering. The high-tension hum from the cable trays strung down the haul road. And underneath, holding it all up like a bass note, the frog chorus from the bottom marshes and the screaming of birds — tens of thousands of them — bounced and re-bounced off raw walls until you can't tell echo from source. You have to lean in and shout into someone's ear to be heard three feet away. Earplugs hang on strings at every stairwell in little dispensers, bright orange, used ones trodden into the mud by the thousand.

Light never quite arrives. Noon here looks like an hour after sunset elsewhere. Part of it is depth — the sun has to clear the rim and by the time it does the Pit has already filled its lungs with its own weather. Warm water over warm mud under warm air makes a perpetual low mist that hangs at the third bench down and never burns off, only thins and thickens. The work lights are always on: strings of sodium-yellow and work-white along the ramps, in the pump houses, on the tall masts at the sump. They make halos, not pools. Everything is seen through a veil — wet, dim, yellow-grey.

Go down. There is only down.

The road spirals, wide enough for the big trucks that no longer run, now potholed and streaming. The geology on either side is embarrassingly new. This is ground that was buried for ten thousand years until last year. There has been no time to weather it, to soften it, to learn how to be a hillside. The cut faces are still sharp. You can see the teeth-marks of the buckets in the clay, the clean shear planes in the peat, the bright, unoxidized sand that is already starting to rill and slump where water finds it. Little embryonic gullies, finger-deep, start at every seep and stop a few feet later in a fan of silt. It is a landscape under construction, and the construction is water undoing digging.

Water is everywhere and it is all moving, slowly. It weeps. That is the word the pump men use. The walls weep. At every contact between sand and clay a dark damp line appears, then beads, then joins into trickles, then into falls. The upper benches drip from their lips in curtains. Puddles never dry; they skin over with oil-rainbow or iron-red. The ditches alongside the road run rust-orange and clear-green, warm to the touch. In the low spots the road itself is a shallow ford. Your boots are wet by the second switchback and stay wet.

Nothing much grows. For a place so wet and so warm you expect jungle, but there has been no time for soil, and what fines wash down are sour with pyrite and diesel. Vegetation is a rumor. Algal mats, dark green-black, slick the stagnant pans. A few pioneer reeds, thin as wire, stick up in clumps along the ditch edges, yellowed. On one south-facing bench someone threw straw and seed and a sparse fuzz of grass holds on, shivering in the vibration. Otherwise: mud, wet rock, rust.

What lives here instead is animals. In abundance, packed in, shoulder to shoulder because the Pit, for all its rawness, is safe and warm and wet and full of food.

The ledges are white with them. Egrets, herons, gulls, swallows — every narrow bench that stays dry for a week is fully occupied, nests jammed against nests, droppings whitewashing the fresh clay. When a pump kicks to a higher gear the whole wall lifts for a moment, a sheet of wings, and the scream rises a notch until even the machines are drowned. The air itself is crowded. At bench-level in the warm dim, the gnats and midges and mosquitoes hang in pillars so dense you breathe them, and the swallows and bats cut through them ceaselessly. They roost under every eave, every conveyor gallery, every pipe bridge — packed brown chattering masses.

Below, at the bottom, is the marsh that is winning. The final floor was supposed to be dry, a sump with crisscross drains to the main pump house. It isn't. The drains couldn't keep up, or the men stopped trying so hard. Now there are shallow warm lakes, knee to waist deep, spreading bench to bench, connected by slow overtoppings. The water is tea-colored, warm as blood, and it moves so slowly you have to stake a stick to see it move at all. But it moves. Millimeter by millimeter it rises around the sandbag walls, around the legs of the idle dragline left like a drowned dinosaur, around the concrete plinths.

And it is alive. Wade — everyone wades — and the water erupts. Frogs by the ten thousand, green and brown and gold, plopping off every hummock. Tadpoles blacken the shallows in writhing carpets so thick your boot leaves a pale print that fills in seconds. Water beetles, dragonflies big as sparrows, leeches. In the deeper channels, carp and catfish, fat on midges, roll slow and unbothered by the roar above. At dusk — which is only a slightly darker dim — the rails and crakes call from the reeds, and the bitterns boom low under the engine drone.

People are here, but thinly, scattered. This is not a city. It's an industrial holding action. A pump house of corrugated steel and concrete block on every second bench, each with its own grumble, its heat shimmer, its slick of oil on the puddle outside, its coiled hoses and stacked sandbags and a single bare bulb burning inside. A transformer yard humming behind chain-link, insulators weeping. A row of site cabins on stilts near the bottom, doors swollen, windows lit, laundry strung and never drying. Catwalks of rusted grating spanning the drains. Pipe runs as thick as a man's torso marching up the walls on wooden trestles, sweating, ticking as they cool, leaking at the flanges in hot jets that make their own rainbows in the work lights. A small concrete batch plant abandoned mid-pour. A fuel tank farm with floating booms around it just in case. The marks of living: boot prints, cigarette butts, cable ties, a shrine of plastic flowers wired to a handrail where someone fell.

The rule of the place is simple and everyone knows it without being told: keep the machines running or drown, and shout to be heard, and watch where you put your feet. Nothing is stable. The fresh cut slumps without warning in a slow slur of mud. The water finds a new path overnight. Rust eats a handrail from the inside. Subsidence cracks the concrete in hairlines that weep lime. Change here is not dramatic — no floods, no collapses that make the news. It is patient, gradual, weak-gradient change. A pump wears. A bench settles six inches in a year. The marsh takes another yard. The iron stains spread a little farther down the wall. The Pit is slowly, slowly becoming a lake with engines in it, and the engines are slowly, slowly becoming reefs for swallows and frogs.

Stay until your ears ring and your clothes are soaked through and you stop trying to wipe your face. From the bottom, looking up, the rim is a thin, bright, impossible line — the real world, dry and loud in a different way. Here below, the walls rise in wet steps all around you, dripping, steaming, crowded with wings and noise, lit dimly like an aquarium at night. Warm rain begins — or maybe it is only condensation falling from the pipes overhead. No one looks up. The pumps hammer on. The water keeps weeping out of the new earth, and the frogs sing under it, deafening, and the Pit breathes in its own warm breath and breathes it out again.
