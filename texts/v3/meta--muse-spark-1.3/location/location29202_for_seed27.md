On the high flat, there is no water. Not hidden water. Not underground water. None.

The world here wore itself out a long time ago. This was mountains once. You can tell by the way the stone remembers being tall — great rounded backs of basalt and pale granite rising and falling for kilometers, all sanded down to the patience of old molars. No peaks, no cliffs, only long swells and shallow dust-basins, ventifacts polished black by a wind that never learned to stop. Elevation does not mean steepness here. It means thinness. The air is cool and threadbare, cold in the mouth, smelling of mineral dust and iodine and dry leaf. Your breath shows. Your ears pop when the wind changes. The horizon is too far away and too sharp, and above it the sky never quite gets to day.

The light hangs at permanent dusk. A small, white-worn sun sits low and sideways, never climbing, never setting, giving less light than its size promises. Everything is seen in blue-grey half-tone, edges softened. Shadows are long and never move. In that dim, the land looks empty at first.

Then your boot crunches, and you realize you have not set foot on ground at all.

It is all plant.

From ridge to basin to the far smudge where the town lights blink, the plateau is upholstered in a single living substrate, meters thick: the Mat. Not green. Not soft. A dry, cool-climate lichen-moss built for a world without rain — ash-grey and rust-black and pale sulfur-yellow in patches, felty and papery and brittle, ankle-deep and knee-deep and in hollows waist-deep. It does not bleed sap when you tear it. It snaps. Inside is hollow tubing, dead air and dust, a lattice that drinks stone and static and light. No roots in soil, because there is no soil left to root in. It etches the rock directly, secreting acid finer than vinegar, loosening silica and iron one grain at a time and building its body out of it. Glass-hair, stone-flesh. If you put your ear close you can hear it tick as it cools.

This is the forest, but flattened. Upright things are rare — that is the sparsity of this place. For every hundred meters of crisp, crackling Mat, there is one Tower-Stalk: a dry candelabra, twenty or thirty meters tall, jointed like bamboo, bark peeling in vast translucent sheets that the wind worries loose and sends skittering. They are alive, utterly alive, though they look dead. Their crowns splay into flat tables of filament, combing the wind for dust to eat. They are spaced like watchmen, far apart, so that the valley feels both full and empty — full underfoot, empty at eye level. Between them the wind has uninterrupted run.

And the wind is loud. That is the first law here.

It comes off the high peneplain without water to soften it, and it hits a billion dry filaments and makes them sing. A low roar first, then a rattle, then a high papery hiss that rises and falls like surf but sharper, drier. The Tower-Stalks groan and creak on their fibrous hinges. Their loose bark clatters. Under that, always, the deep organ-hum of the town's Tuning Forks — black glass pylons set among the stalks at irregular intervals, two hundred meters tall, too thin to be standing, vibrating at a frequency you feel in your molars. They are not quite architecture and not quite machine. They hold the dust out of the lungs of the town. They tune the Mat, they say. They keep the stalks from walking.

The town is there because the plants allow it. Call it Kettlehollow, though it is neither kettle nor hollow — a sparse, spread-out urbanism for a valley-sized organism. No roads. No foundations. People do not build on the Mat; they build with it and under it. Chambers are coaxed and hollowed, walls grown thick and then cured to leather-hardness, lined with sintered stone where heat is needed. From above you see only low domes, vents breathing dust, ropes and filament-bridges strung stalk-to-stalk, laundry lines of lichen-paper, and the Forks blinking amber in the dim. From inside it is layered, warm with body heat and machine heat: nurseries for Mat grafts, workshops where chitin is laminated, galleries where children learn to listen for rot. The town extends past what you can see, a dozen such clusters in neighboring swells, connected by trodden troughs in the living carpet that heal behind a caravan if you wait long enough.

This is post-water engineering. Nothing here pumps, pipes, or dams, because there is nothing to pump. Instead there are looms that weave charge: lattices that pull moisture—not water, never water—out of air as ion drift and feed it as faint current to the Mat's roots. There are gravity-seams, places where a dropped stone falls sideways for a second before remembering down, left over from when the first settlers tried to anchor the plateau against the wind and overcorrected. Dust hangs in those seams permanently, glittering in the low light, never settling. At night — though it is always almost night — you can see the shepherd-machines moving: low, crab-like, silent except for their fans, combing the Mat, singing to it in ultrasound to tell it where to thicken and where to thin.

Abundant life, all dry-adapted. Nothing drinks.

The grazers come in the blue hour, which is every hour. Herds of bellows-goats — barrel-chested, six-legged, hides like overlapping shingles — move through the troughs, cropping the Mat with horny beaks, their flanks pumping bellows that filter dust for organics. Where they crop, the Mat exhales a puff of spores like flour and regrows within days. Above them ride the sails: flocks of paperwing mites, each the size of your hand, then the size of a coat, then the size of a door as the wind lifts them, translucent grey membranes stretched over twig bodies, clicking and whistling as they tack. And the borers and weavers below: cask-beetles big as fists that hollow stalk-fall and ferment dust in their guts, choirs of needle-birds that nest in the Forks and imitate the hum, adding their own frantic descant until the whole valley rings.

It is never quiet. Even when the wind drops — and it does, suddenly, leaving your ears ringing — the Mat settles with a thousand tiny cracks, the goats cough dust, the beetles tick, the Forks hold their note. Then the pressure changes, the Tower-Stalk filaments lift like hair on an arm, and the roar returns from far off, rolling toward you across kilometers of living crisp.

The rules are simple and non-negotiable, and every child will recite them if you ask:

One, do not bring water. Do not spill, do not cache, do not weep more than you can help. Water rots the Mat from the inside and wakes the black mold that eats both plant and lung. Offworlders have their water taken at the rim and are given dust-tea instead, salty and warm.

Two, do not make open flame. Everything underfoot is tinder that never got wet. Heat lives only in stone boxes and glass.

Three, stay tethered when the filaments rise. The wind that feeds the forest will take you just as easily, and the Mat, though soft, will not catch you so much as record your passage — a long bruise-trough that will remain for weeks, until it decides to forgive you.

Stand still long enough and the strangeness stops feeling strange. That the ground breathes. That the trees are mostly air. That a town can be a pruning, not a construction. That life can be total without a single drop to it, a green that gave up green for grey and learned to eat rock and sky. The cool gets into your knees. The dim gets into your eyes until you can see the faint bioluminescence along the Mat's underside where you lift an edge — cold mint sparks, there and gone. Somewhere two stalks rub and scream faintly. Somewhere a Fork shifts pitch and the dust-seam above the valley shivers.

You are very high up, on very old stone, in a forest that is also a field that is also a floor, in a city that hums to keep from blowing away, under a sun that never bothers to rise. And it crackles, everywhere, under every step, alive.
