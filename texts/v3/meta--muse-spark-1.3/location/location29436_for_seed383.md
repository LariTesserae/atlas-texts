The first thing is the light.

It comes straight down, white and total, because there is nothing above you to break it. From up on the plain you would not know this place is here at all — the old ground goes on flat and pale to the horizon — until the ground simply gives out. Not a canyon with drama, not a cliff. A long, shallow giving-way, as if the world exhaled once, a million years ago, and never breathed back in.

You go down into it by steps that were not cut so much as worn. The basin is wide enough to hold weather. A town, a valley, a whole small country of stone. The far rim is a thin white line hung in heat-haze, even though the air on your skin is cool. The stone holds cold the way old bones do. Sun on your shoulders, cold coming up through your soles.

Everything here is rounded. That is the age of it. This hollow is end-of-life landform, settled into its final shape. The walls slump in long aprons of dust and fallen slabs. Boulders sit half-buried like the knuckles of buried giants, their edges sanded to soft loaves. The floor itself is almost flat — a pan of compacted silt and limestone pavement, cracked into great polygons. No sharpness left anywhere except where people have freshly broken it.

And that is strange, because people are breaking it everywhere.

From above, the city looks sparse. Scattered. It does not read as a city at first. Low enclosures the color of the pan, flat roofs, walled yards, terraces one or two stones high that follow the faint contour of the ancient floor. Much emptiness between. Stretches of bare pavement fifty, a hundred paces across where there is nothing but dust devils and shadow-lines and a few upright posts with rope strung between them. Then another cluster: five houses around a pit, a long wall with niches, a tower no higher than three men that has already started to lean and be propped with timber.

But as you descend, the sparseness resolves into infrastructure. This is organized, even if it is thin. Channels cut by hand, shallow as a palm, run for a mile or more across the pan, all trending down toward the cluster-pits. Stair ramps. Causeways of fitted slabs that ring faintly under your feet. Cisterns — round mouths, dark throats — at every low point, capped with woven branch lids and stones. The masonry is primitive and obvious: hammer-dressed limestone, mud mortar, river cobble, copper-green stains where a metal bracket has been used sparingly, as if metal were precious. Timber scaffolds, rope, hide buckets, stone mauls left leaning against walls. Layer on layer: you can see where a new wall sits squarely on an older, half-melted wall, which sits on an even older foundation that is now barely a discoloration in the earth. The town rebuilds itself out of itself, constantly.

No green. Not a blade, not a leaf. The eye keeps looking for it and fails. What looks from a distance like moss or grass is mineral — thin efflorescences of salt and nitre that crust the shaded sides of stones, pink and ochre and sulfur-white, and in the dampest cracks, a black fungal slick no thicker than paint. That is the only growth. Everything else is stone, dust, bone, hide, rope, fired clay.

It is damp, unexpectedly. For a bright, pale, seemingly dry pan, water is everywhere you look sideways. Not lakes, not rivers. Pockets. In the shadow of every wall a dark line where the stone sweats. Condensation beads on the cold undersides of slabs in the morning and drips all day, tick by tick, into jars set to catch it. The deep cisterns hold black water that smells of stone. After a rain — which comes suddenly, violently, out of a sky that was clear an hour before — the shallow channels run milky for half a day, and temporary sheets stand in the polygonal pans, sky-mirrors, ankle-deep, full of reflected glare. Then they sink. The ground drinks. By evening only damp halos remain, darkening the silt, and the air smells faintly of wet lime.

Cool air, bright light. You squint and shiver at once. In sun your skin prickles with heat; step two paces into building-shadow and gooseflesh rises. The inhabitants wear light headcloths against glare and heavy wool mantles against the stone-cold. Their houses are built thick for this reason — to keep the noon out and the night's cold in, or the reverse; hard to tell.

There are animals everywhere, and no visible reason for them.

White pigeons, thousands, that live in the wall niches and tower holes, erupting in silent sheets when something startles them, wheeling around the basin in a single decision-making body, landing again without a sound you can hear from more than twenty paces away. Long-legged runners — something between lizard and bird — that stalk the empty pavements on stilt legs, picking at salt crust. Beetles black as varnish that gather at dawn on the east faces of stones to drink dew off their own backs. Goats, small and shaggy and dun-colored, that chew rope, chew hide scraps, chew the black slick in the cracks, chew anything. And in the cisterns, if you lift the lid and look straight down into that cold dark, pale fish, blind, flickering. Someone put them there generations ago and now they are in every pit, eating mosquito larvae and each other.

They all move. That is the second thing after the light: the motion.

For an ancient, flattened, almost-abandoned-looking place, it seethes. People haul. Goats trot. Pigeons pour from one tower to another. Runners dart. Children run water jars from channel to house and back in endless relay. Men with stone mauls dress new blocks, the chips flying. Women spread clay to dry and then gather it up again before the sudden rain. A scaffold collapses and ten people swarm to raise it. A channel clogs and twenty hands clear it. It has bazaar-energy, anthill-energy, but —

Quiet. Impossibly quiet.

The chink of hammer on stone arrives as a dull tap, as if through felt. Footsteps on slab make no echo, swallowed by the porous pan. Even the goats' bells, and they do wear bells of fired clay, make a soft wooden clonk rather than a ring. The pigeons' wings, when the flock passes directly overhead, are a low pressure, a hush-hush, not a thunder. People speak rarely and low, in gestures mostly. Haggling is done by holding up fingers, by touching the goods, by nodding. It isn't law — no one shushes you — it's that sound doesn't carry here, and so they have stopped trying. Shouting is pointless. The basin eats it.

You learn the rules quickly because your body learns them:

Stay out of the channels when the sky bruises to the west. Water comes fast and shallow and strong enough to take goats.

Drink only from the covered pits. The open pans are bitter with salt and lime.

Don't build sharp. The old ones learned: anything sharp, the wind and time and the trampling feet will round it within a lifetime. Build low, build round, build thick.

Make noise with your hands, not your mouth. A palm raised, a stone tapped twice on a wall — that carries further than a yell.

And keep moving. Nothing here stays. The slabs sweat, the salt blooms and falls, the dust drifts and uncovers a foundation, a wall melts, a new wall goes up on its back. The city is sparse not because it is empty but because it is in transit — parts of it are always being unbuilt and carried a hundred paces away to be rebuilt around a new seep or a newly cleared cistern. What looks like ruins is often material in waiting. What looks like a new house is often an old house moved.

At noon the light is almost unbearable, bouncing up off the white pan until shadows disappear and the whole hollow glows. The runners freeze mid-step, legs casting no shadow. The pigeons sit. The people retreat into the thick-walled rooms where it is cool and dim and smells of damp stone and goats and smoke. For an hour the turbulence pauses. Condensation ticks into jars. Fish turn slowly in black water far below. The ancient floor, patient under all this brief frantic life, holds its cold.

Then a cloud shadow crosses the rim, the glare breaks, and the whole sparse, scattered, hand-built hive pours back out into the light again, silent and fast.
