The town is in a hole.

Not a valley, not a canyon — a hole. Someone cut it straight down, a rectangle out of the flat hot country, about as long as three football fields and half as wide, and about four stories deep. From the rim you can see the whole of it at once, edge to edge. That is the size of the world here. The sky is a lid.

The walls have not learned how to be cliffs yet. They are raw. You can still see the teeth-marks of the excavators in the yellow marl and clay, long horizontal gouges, and where the cut exposed a lens of gravel it spills a little, patiently, grain by grain. No gullies, no talus slopes, no smoothed hollows — erosion hasn't really started. This geology is embryonic, under construction. In places the wall is shored with shotcrete and rock bolts, in places it is just earth, damp-dark at the bottom and sun-baked pale at the top. Rebar bristles out where a foundation was started and then stopped.

Because the hole went too deep. It found water.

Not a lake, not a river. Seep. The lower six feet of the walls weep constantly. Clear water beads out of the marl, runs down in black streaks, gathers in a foot-wide rill that runs all the way around the base of the walls like a moat. The floor itself is close to the water table — you can feel it give under your boot, a softness under gravel and broken concrete. After the pumps run, the low middle stays marshy.

It is hot down here in a way that has nothing to do with the sun. Up on the rim it is hot — white light, thorn scrub, heat shimmer. Down in the hole it is hotter, and wet. The air gets trapped. The diesel pump house and the two generators on the north side dump their waste heat and never stop. The walls radiate back what they absorbed at noon. The water evaporates and then has nowhere to go, so it condenses again on every cool pipe and culvert and rains back down, a permanent post-rain. Your shirt sticks in a minute. Your glasses fog. Concrete sweats. Steel sweats. Rock sweats. The smell is hot oil and hot mud and ammonia and algae cooking on concrete.

Light barely reaches. The rim is blinding, but by the time that light gets down here it is used up, bounced and browned by dust. What lights the town is work-light: a string of caged amber bulbs on sagging wires, a sodium flood over the pump house yard that never turns off, a few blue-white LEDs in doorways, the flicker of a welding rig when someone is patching pipe. Pools of orange, long shadows, and between them, stretches of near-dark where the damp swallows everything. At noon, a single shaft gets in and you can see the dust and midges turning in it, and the thousand white streaks of bird lime on the east wall.

Because the people built, but sparsely. This is, technically, urban. There are streets — or, the ghosts of streets: poured slabs, some tilted and cracked by the heave of wet clay, laid out in a grid that was meant for more. There is infrastructure: a twelve-inch main on concrete piers running the length of the south wall, dripping at every flange, catwalks, ladders bolted to the earth, a transformer on a pole that goes nowhere, corrugated sheds and cinderblock rooms with tar-paper roofs and swamp coolers chugging on top. A chapel with a painted door. A shop with bars and warm soda. Layered construction — pipe over pipe over cable.

But there are maybe thirty structures in a space platted for three hundred. Much space between presences. Empty pads with weeds pushing through expansion joints. A street that leads to a wall and stops. Clotheslines strung between a house and nothing, anchored to a rock bolt. The town feels both crowded with intent and abandoned in fact, a city drawn at 10% opacity. You can shout across it and be heard, but you have to walk a long hot wet minute to get to your neighbor.

Almost nothing green survives. A tamarisk by the sump pond, salt-burned and leaning, its roots in oily water. Mats of dark green algae on the standing puddles that skin over and break like ice. Liverworts and pale moss in a fringe where the wall weeps most, and in the cracks of the slabs, bleached crabgrass and a bitter weed with dusty leaves. White mold furs the north side of anything wooden. That is the vegetation: opportunistic, flat, low.

The animals own it.

First you hear the pigeons. Thousands of them have taken the excavator gouges and the weep-holes and the bolt-holes in the walls for nesting, a vertical favela of birds. At any hour a few hundred are in the air, circling the rectangle, their wings slapping in the trapped air, loud as laundry. Their cooing, amplified by the walls, is a constant low roar with sudden flurries when a hawk passes over the rim and they all lift at once.

Under that: frogs. The rill and the sump pond at the low corner — rust-colored, warm as bathwater, filmed with oil rainbows — seethe with toads and small mottled frogs that call day and night, a metallic trill that never quite stops. Mosquitoes and midges cloud every light, and swallows cut through them at dusk, screaming.

And rats. At night, when the generators throttle down and the amber bulbs buzz, they come out along the pipe runs, along the edges of slabs, dozens, hundreds, moving low and unhurried, not afraid because there is nowhere to chase them to. The feral cats watch from the roofs and do not bother. Cockroaches the size of thumbs work the damp under the houses. Bats drop from under the catwalks at last light and stitch the air above the water.

It is loud. Not city-loud, pit-loud. The pump house — a brick box with its door always open, black inside — thumps on a slow cycle: *chug-chug-chug ... chug-chug-chug*, patient as a heart, moving seep-water up to a ditch on the surface that steams in the sun. The big exhaust fans on the generators drone. Water ticks and drips everywhere, a thousand small impacts that echo off the walls and come back as a general wet hiss. And over it all, wings and croaks and the clank of someone dropping a wrench on a catwalk, amplified.

Nothing here happens fast, except the animals. The pumps lift and fall. Rust creeps up the corrugated walls millimeter by millimeter, orange bleeding into gray. The floor subsides a finger's width a year, cracking slabs a little more, tipping a doorway a little more out of square. The water rises in the hot season, falls a little when they run both pumps, rises again. Men move slow in the heat, conserving themselves, sitting in shade until the metal cools enough to touch with gloves. Laundry dries stiff with minerals and stays damp underneath.

The rules are simple and everyone knows them:

Stay below the high-water stain on the walls. That line, dark as tea, is where you do not store anything you love.

Do not stop the pumps. If both stop for a day, the streets become creeks. For two days, the floors become ponds. The old-timers point to a watermark inside the chapel, waist-high.

Make noise when you walk at night, so the rats move aside. Leave the tamarisk alone — it's the only shade that isn't tin.

Look up sometimes. The rectangle of sky is small, but it tells weather. If clouds stack over the rim and the air gets even heavier, the walls will slump somewhere in a wet sheet, and you will hear it before you see it — a soft *whumpf* — raw geology still deciding what shape it wants to be.

It is not a strange place. No one will tell you a legend about it. It is just a town that was meant to be a foundation for a factory, that hit water and heat, that kept digging anyway until the walls were too high to climb without ladders, and then stayed, because the pumps worked and the rent was free and the birds were already there.
