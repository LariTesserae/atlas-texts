It is not a valley. It is not a mountain. It is a room on top of a mountain.

Outside, the air is too thin to hold heat and the world falls away in grey scree and cloud-sea, four thousand meters down to wherever the ground decided to be. Inside, it is warm.

You come up the service stair — the rock is long gone, cased over in riveted iron and concrete, then in tile, then in rust — and the pressure door sighs open, and the heat takes you by the throat. Wet, green, oily heat. A greenhouse heat, an engine-room heat. And the noise.

The place is small. That is the first disorientation. You could walk it end to end in three minutes if there was a straight way to walk. It is one chamber, about the size of a cathedral nave stood on end, a hamlet squeezed into a vertical tin can. A cylinder, sixty paces across, a hundred and more tall, capped with a louvered crown. Everything you can touch is made. Floor plates, wall plates, ceiling struts, gantries, pipes, chain, ceramic, copper, glass. There is no outcrop, no soil, no native stone showing anywhere. Even the bolts have been painted over so many times they have soft shoulders.

And for all that, it is almost entirely empty.

That is the second disorientation. The walls are near and the center is far. The middle of the cylinder is nothing — a held breath of warm air, floor to crown, uncrossed by any walkway. You stand on the narrow perimeter gantry and look across a hollow that your eyes insist must be larger than the building that contains it. Presences cling to the edges and leave the volume alone. Presences are few and far between. A hanging chain here. A lamp cage there. A slow fan. Between them, gulfs of lit, humming air.

Because the walls are alive.

Lush is too dry a word. The interior skin of this machine is a vertical jungle. Not planted — accreted. Mosses an inch thick over the cold-water pipes, liverworts black-green in the seams, ferns unfurling from bolt-holes, figs with pale strangler roots following the vertical cable runs down twenty meters to find a drip, orchids clamped to warm ducts where the metal sweats. Vines have learned the shape of industry and repeat it: they run horizontal along conduit, vertical along downspouts, diagonal along bracing, until from the empty center the whole cylinder looks upholstered in green geometry. Leaves press against mesh, spill over railings, dangle. Flowers the color of warning lights. Where a panel fell away decades ago, roots have knitted a replacement.

There is no river here. There is no rain, properly. This high, the clouds do not rain so much as pass through. Twice a day the crown louvers open and the cloud comes inside — a white, blind, cold animal — and then the machines breathe and the walls drink. That is all the water this place will ever get, and it is enough: damp, intermittent, miserly and constant. Condensation beads on every leaf, every pipe, every hair and feather. It purls along the undersides of handrails, gathers at the low point of every chain link, drops. The floor is cambered to a central drain that never sees a torrent, only a perpetual tic-tic-tic of drips from a hundred heights arriving late. The air tastes of pennies and chlorophyll. Your shirt sticks to you in a minute. Your glasses fog, clear, fog again. In the drip pans, mosquito larvae flick. In the shadow of the sump, a pocket of standing water no bigger than a bathtub holds algae, newts, a drowned wrench turned to reef.

It is warm because the engines never stop. That is the industrial pact that keeps the garden from freezing to death in the night up here.

Down at ankle level, around the base ring, are the low buildings-within-the-building: six compressor houses, squat, green with verdigris, humming. Copper bus-bars run up the walls like vines made before vines, crackling faintly with normal daylight power — no sparks, no apocalypse, just electrified work. A great screw, thirty meters of rust-darkened steel, rises through the green along the east arc, turning so slowly you have to watch a painted mark to believe it moves at all. One revolution a day, lifting mist. Ventilator fans the size of houses stir once a minute and settle. Everything is worn smooth. Handwheels polished to a mirror by ten thousand hands that were not hands. Paint gone to powder. Edges rounded. Rust and root have come to an agreement after a very long time and no longer fight; the iron feeds the fig, the fig shelters the iron. Settled-form. Weathered into balance.

Light is ordinary. That is strange given everything else. No cathedral gloom, no forge glare. Flat, white mountain noon comes sideways through the louver slats and the grime-frosted portholes, diffused by cloud and leaf into a working light. You can read a gauge. You can see dust motes and spores and moth scales hanging in it.

And the animals. There are no people here — not now, perhaps not for decades — but it is not uninhabited. It is crowded, if you know how to look.

Cloud-moths in their thousands, pale as steam, plastered under leaves by day, rising at louver-opening to drink the fog. Soot-sparrows and white-collared swifts that never leave the cylinder, nesting in junction boxes, screaming as they ride the warm updraft round and round the void, never crossing it. They orbit. Everything orbits. Copper lizards bask on the warm ducts. Roaches the size of thumbs work the grease traps. In the sump, rats. And the macaques — a troop of short-coated high-altitude macaques, oil-dark on their fingertips — who have inherited maintenance.

They do it slowly, without hurry, as a rite. An old female checks the drip pans with the back of her hand, tips one a finger's width to change the flow. A juvenile rides the great screw's housing up and down, picking moss from the inspection port. Another turns a handwheel a quarter-turn, listens, turns it back. They are not imitating humans. This is their ecology now: to tend. They nap on warm transformers, groom each other on catwalks draped with flowering vine, watch you with total indifference.

The noise is deafening. There is no other word that holds. It is not loud like a crash; it is loud like a place. The low fundamental of the compressors in your molars, the shriek of wind knifing the louvers above, the rattle of a loose chain somewhere beating time against a duct, the hiss of steam bleed, the amplified tic of water, the screech of a hundred swifts bounced off tile and iron until direction fails. You feel it in your sternum. You have to put your mouth to someone's ear to be heard, and there is no one. Earplugs would be sensible and useless; the building sings through bone.

And yet nothing happens fast. This is the slowest factory in the world. Leaves lengthen by millimeters a week. The screw lifts fog a bucket at a time. Verdigris creeps. A fig root thickens around a pipe over a decade and gently, patiently crushes it, and the water finds a new path and a new garden starts six meters to the left. The macaques repeat circuits their grandmothers learned. Change here is seasonal, decadal. The roar suggests emergency; the reality is patience.

There are rules, and you learn them by watching the swifts.

One: do not cross the center. Nothing does. Moths circle it. Swifts circle it. Seeds, fluff, dust — all take the long way around, as if the hollow repels them or as if the sound cancels there into a pressure they dislike. From the gantry you can see it: a column of clearer air, faintly shimmering, where the din thins just enough to hear your own blood. Throw a leaf into it and the updraft will carry it up and out to the side. It stays empty. The garden presses in but never fills.

Two: water belongs to leaves first, metal second. Condensation forms heaviest not on cold steel but on living undersides, as if the plants have learned to coax it. The machines drink secondhand, from leaf-drip.

Three: everything made will be grown over, and everything grown will be maintained. No part is allowed to die completely, rust to collapse or vine to rot. The macaques prune. The sparrows eat the moths. The moths pollinate the orchids on the ducts. The ducts warm the orchids. The compressors warm the air that lifts the swifts that scream in the deafening nave of this small, high, hollow, total garden — and the whole warm, wet, roaring room hangs above the clouds, turning very slowly, going nowhere at all.
