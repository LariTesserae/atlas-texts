You come down off the levee road and into it all at once.

From up top it just looked like a green plug in the flat — a low smudge of trees and steam in the middle of the wet fields. Then the path drops six, eight feet, no more than that, below the waterline of the paddies and the canal, and you are inside the bowl.

It is a hamlet. That is the size of it. You can stand at the old boiler house in the center and see its edges in every direction: the ring dike with its blacktop gone soft, the curtain of cane and willow and second-growth poplar that closes over it, the water standing silver beyond. Forty-odd roofs, maybe fifty if you count lean-tos. A mill and a pump house and houses and sheds all shouldering each other until there is no between left. Everything touches everything else.

This low it is warm in a way that stays. Not hot noon, but a held warmth. The air is thick with water that never quite becomes rain. It sweats out of the brick. It beads on the underside of every pipe and leaf and wire and then falls. The ground underfoot gives a little when you step off the plank. Dig down a handspan anywhere — behind the washhouse, under the office steps — and you hit water, brown and smelling faintly of iron and roots.

They have to pump to live here. That is the rule the place was built around and never got out from under.

Day and night the big low-pressure engines in the pump house turn. You hear them before you see anything: a deep, patient thump-thump, thump-thump, and over it the whine of the belts and the dynamo, and over that the constant white rush of water being lifted. The tailrace vomits a smooth glassy sheet up and over the dike into the high canal, always the same volume, always the same sound. The men here listen for a change the way you would listen for a heart skipping.

Because if the pumps stopped the bowl would fill in a week to the knees, in a month to the windowsills. Everyone knows it. So the town is built around keeping them fed — coal barge in the wet season, rice-husk and pressed cane trash in the dry, a tangle of conveyors, ash carts, oil cans, spare gaskets hung on nails. The mill and the power are the same building, really. Cane comes in on flat punts through the south sluice, is crushed, bagasse burned, juice boiled down in open pans. Sugar and power and drainage all one slow loop.

And over all of it, through all of it: green.

Not green as decoration. Green as substance. The first thing you learn walking the lanes is that there is no surface here that is not alive on top. The brick of the mill went black-green a generation ago and never went back. Touch it and your palm comes away velvety with moss and soot. The corrugated roofs have an inch of humus on them now; grass grows there, and on the grass, seedlings. Every spring the company sends boys up with scythes to mow the roofs so they don't cave in. The walls of the workers' cottages to the north are not walls anymore so much as clipped hedges — privet and fig trained flat between posts, trimmed square every month with shears, doors and windows cut out like holes in a topiary. Inside is brick, but you wouldn't know it until you are inside.

Elephant ear crowds the alley so tight you turn sideways. Water hyacinth chokes the sump pits and the ditches, glossy and purple-flowered, and has to be forked out every morning and thrown steaming onto the compost heaps. Kudzu runs the wires. Morning glory runs the kudzu. Strangler fig has taken the old chimney that cracked in '58 and now holds it upright — roots like rebar down the flues, crown exploding thirty feet over the yard, dropping aerial roots that the children swing on at lunch break. Even the light poles sprout: fern in every bolt-hole, grass tufted on the crossarms.

Under normal midday light — white-yellow, filtered through haze and leaf and boiler smoke — it all looks settled. Not raw or ruined. Weathered into itself. The settling cracks in the concrete sluices have been settled for decades, now lined with maidenhair fern. The wooden pilings under the walkways have gone soft at the waterline but hold; they have held for forty years and will hold for forty more if the teredo don't get ambitious. The paths are laid brick herringbone, sunk and wavy, with purslane and pennywort growing up between. Nothing collapses here suddenly. Things sag, thicken, root, rust in place.

The layering is what makes it dense. You look up a single alley and you count six systems in ten feet: the ground ditch running tea-colored, the plank walk over it, the low steam pipe sweating beside the walk, the clothesline above the pipe, the power line above that sagging under trumpet vine, the roof gutter above that overflowing into a rain chain of cut cans that drips and drips and never stops dripping. Houses have added lean-tos onto lean-tos until their original shapes are unreadable: a kitchen of woven cane matting, a wash-shed of salvaged boiler plate, a sleeping loft with walls of living bamboo that still leafs out in April. People move through by ducking, sidestepping leaves, lifting trapdoors in vines.

It is loud, for how slow it is. Nothing hurries, but nothing is quiet. The engines thump. The belts slap. The cane crusher groans and chews at intervals. Water falls everywhere in small voices — from eaves, from leaves after no rain, from the weep holes in the dike, from the condensate pipes that spit every few seconds onto moss pillows. The leaves themselves make noise when the warm air moves, a big wet rustle, and the sugar pans bubble low and constant. You have to raise your voice to be heard across the yard, and then your voice comes back flattened by green.

What you don't hear is animals. That takes a while to notice. For all this plant mass there is almost nothing moving in it. No chorusing frogs, oddly, though the water is everywhere — the oil film and the heat and the constant draw-down see to that. A white egret stands for hours on the sluice gate, motionless as a valve. A one-eared cat sleeps on the warm dynamo housing. Mosquitoes, yes, and white cabbage moths around the vegetable plots, and the black mill beetles that live in the bagasse dust. Once in a while a moorhen picks through the hyacinth. Otherwise the place belongs to people and plants and machines, in that order of patience.

If you stay till evening, when the shift changes and the air cools one degree toward bearable, you see how the rules work. A woman comes out with a hoe and clears the same twenty feet of ditch she cleared yesterday, throwing the weed onto the same heap. A boy climbs the transformer pole with a machete to cut the vine back from the insulators before it arcs. An old man checks the sump gauge, taps it, marks the slate. No one talks about fighting the green or beating the water. You don't beat them. You trim, you lift, you burn, you pump. You keep the balance where it has sat for sixty years, low and warm and wet, humming.

Later the lights come on — bare bulbs strung under the fig canopy, humming amber through moths — and steam from the pans and transpiration from a million leaves rise together and make a low cloud that sits in the bowl and will not leave until morning. From the levee road above, anyone passing would see only that: a shallow cup of light and mist, smelling of crushed cane and hot oil and wet earth, throbbing faintly, holding its small, crowded, living shape just below the water.
