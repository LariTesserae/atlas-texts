Down the steel ladder, under the pump house, there is a room that isn't on any sign.

You have to lift the floor grate in the back corner where the big diesel sits, where the daylight from the door doesn't quite reach, and drop down three rungs. Then you are in it. All of it. You can stand in the middle and almost touch both walls if you stretch your arms. The ceiling is low enough that a tall person learns to stoop. It was never meant to be lived in. It was meant to be looked at once a year with a flashlight.

It is a concrete box sunk below the marsh. Four walls, a floor, a ceiling that is the underside of the engine room. Low land outside, lower here. Water wants to be here.

And heat wants to be here. The first thing is the heat. Not a wave, but a weight. The diesels upstairs have been running for years, day and night, keeping the polder from drowning, and their waste heat bleeds down through two feet of concrete and steel. The floor is warm through your boots. The pipes that cross the ceiling are too hot to hold. The air is hot and so wet your sweat has nowhere to go. It just sits on your skin, then is joined by the air's own sweat.

Because everything weeps.

The walls are weeping rocks in the industrial sense — poured concrete, only a few years old, still sharp at the corners where the forms came off, still showing the grain of the wood they were cast against. But water from the marsh is pushing in from outside, finding the pores, finding the hairline crack in the southeast corner that opened last summer when the engine threw a mount. It beads, swells, runs. The ceiling weeps too, but from the other direction: the hot breath of the machines above meets the cooler seep below and condenses on every bolt and conduit. Drip, drip, drip from a hundred points. The floor never dries.

The floor itself is not flat. It was poured with a shallow V to a central channel, a trough six inches wide that runs to a black sump hole in the far corner where a bilge pump is supposed to catch it. The pump is dead — its little float switch is furred white with scale and stuck up — so the channel holds an inch of hot, clear water at all times, ankle-deep in the low spots. It shivers constantly, not from wind, there is no wind down here, but from sound.

It is loud in a way that makes the smallness worse. Upstairs the diesels throb, a deep chest-vibration you feel in your molars, *duh-duh-duh-duh*, endless. Right through the middle of the room a twelve-inch discharge main goes from wall to wall, lagged in asbestos cloth gone yellow and dripping, and inside it a column of marsh water is being hurled up and over the levee. It sings, a high pressurized whine. And at the west wall there is a steam vent — a half-inch bleed from the cooling jacket — that was never properly muffled. It screams, thin and white. The three sounds don't mix so much as stack, until you have to open your mouth to keep your ears from popping. When you speak, your voice comes back to you flat and small off the wet walls. The drips, hundreds of them, should be delicate, but in here they are amplified, each *plik* and *plok* in the puddles as distinct as a hammer tap.

The light is ordinary, which makes it stranger. No gloom, no theatrical dark. A rectangle of white noon daylight falls straight down through the floor grate you came through, full of dust and steam motes, laying a ladder-shadow across the water. And a caged work-bulb in a wire guard over the door burns yellow all the time, buzzing faintly with 60-cycle electricity. Between them you can see everything with painful clarity: every blister in the paint, every rustless stain.

That is the unusual part if you stay long enough to notice. Iron in hot water should rust. Here it doesn't, quite. The pipes sweat but stay gunmetal gray. The ladder rungs shine where hands have polished them. Instead, everything is growing a skin of mineral — white, then cream, then a pale sulfurous yellow at the waterline. Young rock, fresh. Where the wall crack seeps fastest, little teeth have started to grow, sharp as broken glass, half an inch long, not yet stalactites, more like the room is cutting teeth. Where paint flaked, the concrete underneath is still crisp-edged, unsoftened. This place hasn't been worn smooth by water yet. It has just been born and is already drowning.

Almost nothing lives here. That is the rule.

Outside, twenty feet away beyond the levee, the marsh is riot — herons, mosquitoes, cordgrass, mud stink. In here, nothing. No rats, they would cook. No roaches, no silverfish, no mosquitoes even — the water is too hot and too clean and vibrates too much for larvae, and there is a faint sheen of oil on it that breaks into rainbows when a drip hits. No spiders in the corners, though cobweb-like strands of mineral fiber hang from the conduit. It is sterile by heat and noise and chemistry.

Except for the green.

In the thin places where daylight hits wet concrete and stays, there is a slick of thermophilic algae, dark green-black, slippery as soap, no thicker than paint. Three patches, no more. One on the north wall, one on the lip of the channel, one under the grate. And in the southeast crack, where the seep is coolest, a single fern has lodged. One frond, then a second, pale and etiolated, no more than four inches high. It has been that size for months. The men upstairs say it has been there since spring and hasn't grown or died. Like everything here, it is patient.

Because for all the noise and violence, nothing happens fast. The water level in the channel rises a sixteenth of an inch after rain, falls a sixteenth after a dry week. The mineral teeth lengthen by dust. The crack widens by hair. The bulb burns. The diesels turn. The heat never breaks. You sit on the warm concrete curb, shouting to hear yourself think, your shirt plastered, your boots full of hot water, watching a drop form on a bolt, swell, catch the daylight, and fall — and somewhere upstairs a man walks across the floor and the whole small box shudders with his weight, and the puddles make a thousand intersecting rings that vanish almost before they spread.

It is not big enough to get lost in. That is its mercy. It is a hidden pocket of wet heat and roar tucked under the working world, too small for a map, too loud for animals, too hot for rot, where water and iron and concrete are slowly, slowly learning how to become stone.
