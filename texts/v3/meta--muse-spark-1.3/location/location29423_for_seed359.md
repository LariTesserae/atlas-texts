You have to duck to get in. The door is low on purpose — not for defense, for heat. Outside there is sun and open wind. Inside, it stays.

It is one room, but not one room. A hamlet the size of a threshing floor, walled all the way around, roofed all the way over, and everything in it made. There is no ground here. There is floor.

The floor is the first thing you learn with your feet. Layer on layer of mats, reed over rush over split palm rib, lashed edge to edge with twine, patched with clay where feet have worn through. No stone shows. No soil. Even the dust between the weaves is sifted chaff and fiber-shear, not earth. It gives a little when you step. Underneath, joists knock softly, hollow. The whole place is raised — a basket on short stilts, breathing warm air up from the dark underneath.

It is dry. That is the rule of the air. Not a cup of water, not a drip, not a stain. Your mouth dries in a minute. Your eyes blink. Wood here does not rot. It cures, it sharpens. Fresh-cut ends are everywhere — pale, splinter-bright, still smelling of sap turned to resin. Adze marks, knife facets, the fray where a stalk was twisted off by hand. Nothing is weathered. Everything looks made this morning, even though the lashings are dark with handling. It is a young place. Sharp corners. Unsoftened knots.

And it is lush. That is the contradiction until you touch it.

What grows here does not drink. The walls, the posts, the low roof-beams — they are trellises, and from every trellis hangs growth. Not green the way wet places are green. Dull olive, straw-gold, rust-red, dusty grey-green. Leaves thick as felt, edged in wax, dry as paper to the fingertip but piled and piled, frond over runner over curtain. Air-ferns unroll in stiff spirals from the rafters and never unfurl all the way. Bottle-vines fatten their knotted stems with oil, not sap, and hang lantern-husks that rattle with spores when you brush them. Puff-moss colonizes every horizontal lashing in cushions that squeak under the thumb and spring back. Jump-thistle sends up corkscrew shoots tipped with down.

Nothing needs soil. Roots — if they are roots — are holdfasts that grip wood and twine and drink warmth. In the midday heat under the roof, the whole thicket exhales a faint sweet varnish smell, resin and hot straw and pollen-dust. If you press your ear to a bottle-vine stem you hear nothing, but if you leave your palm there you feel it very slowly tighten, by fractions, following the sun through the weave.

Because light gets in. Normal, workaday light, slatted and sieved. The roof is not sealed; it is thatched so densely that daylight comes through as a thousand small knives and coins. They move, but slowly. Dust hangs in them and hardly falls. The interior is warm — body-warm, bread-oven warm without the burn — close and windless. You sweat, and the sweat dries instantly to salt on your lip. The plants do not mind. They open their pores to the heat itself.

Where there are plants that thick, there are animals. They are everywhere once your eyes adjust to the smallness of the scale.

Threadbirds — no bigger than your thumb — have woven their hanging pocket-nests directly into the living curtains, using down from the thistles and their own pale saliva that sets like glue. They slip in and out without rustling. Loom-beetles, copper-backed, work the mat-floor in lines, chewing loose fiber and leaving behind neat, beaded frass that the dust-mice harvest. Geckos the color of cured reed flatten themselves on posts, throats pulsing, eyes like cut seeds. They move only when you blink. In the dimmer pockets under the storage platforms, coiled slow-worms breathe in the gloom, and pallid crickets with too-long antennae comb the moss for spores.

It is crowded. That is the nature of it. Beam, vine, nest, pot, hand, tail — everything near-touching. You cannot extend your arm without touching three textures: warm wood, dry leaf, something furred that withdraws. The builders — there are people, or there were, or there are when you are not looking — made use of every span. Low platforms for sleeping, hung with curtains of woven vine-leaf. Clay bins pinched by hand, still showing fingerprints, full of seed-heads and dried husk-fruit and beetle-shell buttons. Bone awls, fire-hardened digging sticks, a few simple hammered copper hooks green at the edge. Twine everywhere. Knots as a language: slip-knot for a beam that still grows, hard square-knot for dead wood, a little looped charm-knot wherever a vine has been trained to turn.

And here the place stops behaving.

It is small. Forty paces wall to wall, you would swear. You can see the outer wall from anywhere — a great oval of upright stakes and wattle, daubed with wax and clay, leaning slightly inward. But you cannot walk to it in a straight line. The interior is layered in a way hands cannot have made. A sleeping platform with a curtain, behind the curtain a garden row, behind the garden row another sleeping platform that should be outside the wall already. Duck under a beam that is hung with nests and you emerge a hand’s breadth higher than you started, though the floor is level. The shadows fall wrong. At noon the slat-light should pool straight down. Instead it pools sideways, climbing up posts in bright rungs.

If you stand still long enough — and everything here encourages standing still, the heat, the quiet — you notice duplication. The same charm-knot, same fray, same copper hook, occurring twice on the same post, one a finger’s width to the left of the other, as if the post hesitated. The threadbirds know. They will land on the left hook, never the right. The geckos avoid certain corners entirely, flattening themselves at the edge of an empty patch of mat where there is nothing to see, but the air above it shimmers faintly with heat that isn’t heat.

The old thatch at the center of the roof hangs lower than it should, swollen with moss and nests, and if you lie on your back under it and look up, you see not roof but another floor above — mats, feet, the underside of pots — very far away and very close, as if the hamlet has been folded over on itself and stitched. Sounds that should echo don’t. The weave drinks them. A pot knocked emits a dull *tok* and then nothing. The loudest things are tiny and near: the tick of a beetle’s carapace, the dry *shush* of a husk turning in a draft you cannot feel, your own breath catching on dust.

Nothing changes fast. A vine will add a leaf while you watch for an hour only if you are patient enough to doubt you saw it. The dust-mice make the same circuit of the same joist day after day, wearing a pale path. The people — when they are here — mend. That is their whole technology: to mend, to knot, to retrain a runner, to replace a mat, to daub a crack with warm wax. They do not cut the living posts. They do not bring water in; water is spoken of as a ruin, a dissolving. A child is taught early: what is dry keeps its shape. What is wet forgets.

At dusk — though there is no dusk inside, only a slow ambering of the slats — the husks begin to glow faintly from within, spore-light, steady as embers. The animals settle deeper into the thicket. The air gets warmer still, as if the whole basket inhales the day’s heat and holds it. You can feel the structure take the weight: a soft creak running around the wall in a circle that never quite completes.

You understand, lying there with leaves brushing your cheek, dry as cloth, dense as fur, listening to almost nothing, that this is not a garden inside a house. The house is the garden. The garden is the house. It grows itself by being mended, and is mended by growing, and the boundary that looks so solid from inside — that waxed wattle oval — is not keeping the world out. It is keeping this small, warm, dry lushness from spilling, from unfolding, from remembering how big it really is.
