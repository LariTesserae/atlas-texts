It is high. That is the first fact, before shape or color.

You come up out of the thick air for a long time — a climb that takes days, though there is no trail, only a rising — until your lungs go shallow and your thoughts go shallow with them, and the light thins out and does not come back. This is not a peak. It is worse than a peak. It is a high, broad, tilted table of the world left unfinished, a valley the size of a small country cupped between ranges that are still being born.

The air here is cool in the way deep stone is cool. Not winter. A permanent late-evening chill that sits on the back of the neck and does not lift. Your breath shows, a brief white flag, and then is taken instantly by the dryness. Everything is dry. There has never been water here. No riverbeds, even dry ones. No lake rings. No rain-gullies. No dew. The ground does not know the shape water makes. It is raw geology, new rock, black and iron-red and ash-pale, broken in clean, unworn edges. The mountains on either side are too sharp. They look knapped, like flint just struck. Nothing has softened them yet. Talus has barely begun to fall. The scree slopes are short, abortive. The cliffs end in fresh fractures that glitter where they caught the last, dim sun a hundred years ago and have not dulled since.

Light does not really happen here. It leaks. A low, bruised, violet-grey luminance that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere, as if the sky were a lid with the lamp left on behind it. There are no hard shadows. There is no noon. The sun is a rumor below the eastern rim — a paler smear that never clears the teeth. You can see a long way, past the immediate, across the whole open bowl of the plateau, three or four ridges deep, to where distance turns to static, but you cannot see clearly. Edges smudge if you stare.

And it is loud. Impossibly, deafeningly loud for a place so empty.

Not wind-loud, not storm-loud. A low, continuous organ-tone that lives in your molars and sternum. The mountain humming to itself as it grows. Stone under tension, a note so deep you feel it more in your knees than hear it with your ears, and over it a higher tearing, like a vast sheet of metal being slowly ripped a mile overhead, unending. You have to press your palms to your ears and still hear it inside the bone. If you shout to the person next to you, your voice is shredded instantly, taken. People here learn to speak with their hands, with elbows, with close foreheads. The sound never changes quickly. It is patient. Hour to hour it is the same, and yet if you camp for a week you will realize the pitch has shifted a quarter-tone lower, as the range beneath you rises another hair's breadth. This is a slow place. Everything takes a long duration. The rocks creep upward. The light does not cycle. The roar does not crest or break.

In this dry, cold, ringing height, there should be nothing living. Instead: lushness.

Not everywhere — that is the strangeness. The plateau is mostly emptiness, great stretches of bare, ringing mineral pavement where your boots squeak and the dust hangs in the air for minutes because the air is so thin and dry it cannot pull it down. And then, scattered, low and dense and tenderly mounded, gardens that have no business being alive.

They are not green as lowland green. They are deeper, bluer, almost black-green, with flashes of amber and blood-orange. Cushion mosses thick as mattresses, piled a foot, two feet high. Trumpet lichens the size of your fist, open bells turned all to the sky. Broad, papery leaves veined like hands, overlapping into thatch, into roofs. Fronds that unroll and unroll and never quite brown. If you press your face close — and you have to, to hear anything but the roar, to see in the dim — you see they are furred, dusted with fine silica hair, dry to the touch. Crisp. They rustle like paper. No sap. No wetness when you crush them. They do not drink. There is nothing to drink.

What they drink is the sound.

That is the rule of this place, and once you see it you cannot unsee it. The lushest mounds grow where the resonance pools, in hollows and at the foot of sheer walls where the tone doubles back on itself. Their bells are tuned. The big amber trumpets shiver visibly with the bass note; you can put a fingertip to a leaf and feel it buzzing so fast it blurs. The plants lean *into* loudness, not light. Where a rock overhang creates a little acoustic shadow, there is bare stone. Where two ridges focus the hum, there is a jungle in miniature, thigh-high, tangled, exuberant, leaves split by vibration into fringes to catch more of it. When the pitch shifts, over years, the gardens migrate, dying back on one side to papery skeletons that do not rot — nothing rots without water and without animals — and swelling on the other. The dead thatch remains, pale and dry and still shaped like life, so each living clump wears a halo of its own ghosts.

There are no animals. That absence is complete and eerie and, after a while, deafening in itself. No flies above the leaves. No worms beneath. No birds to be torn by the wind that isn't wind. No footprints but your own and those, older, of whoever came before. The leaves are uneaten, perfect, torn only by sound and their own growth. It makes the lushness feel un-witnessed, like a performance with no audience, until you arrive and your boots are obscenely loud.

People have been here. Sparsely. That is the other impossibility that the place holds without effort.

Scattered across the open bowl, a day's walk apart, sometimes two, are structures. Low beehive huts of dry-stacked black slab, no mortar, no wood — there is no wood to burn — each just large enough to curl inside, door facing away from the focus of the tone. Cairns, waist-high, then man-high, built stone by stone to mark the safe paths between gardens. Circles of upright shards where someone sat. Offerings: a knapped blade of volcanic glass left on a flat rock, a coil of woven leaf-fiber rope, a cup made from a giant trumpet-lichen, hardened and lacquered with something — not water-based — to hold dust, or seeds, or sound itself.

The tech is hand-tech. You can see it in the chipping. Everything is pecked, ground, tied, wedged. Hides scraped and sewn with gut, though what animal gave hide here is a question you learn not to ask aloud when the rock is listening. Perhaps they brought them up from below and never took them down. Perhaps they stay. A few smudges of charcoal on a sheltered wall: handprints, tally marks, a wavy line that might be the tone made visible.

No one is in the huts now, or if they are, they are very still and very accustomed to not being found. But the gardens nearest the huts are tended — fringed leaves trimmed with those glass knives, thatch cleared, bells thinned so they do not shatter each other with their own ringing. Someone harvests the dry lush, weaves it, eats it perhaps. It crunches like frost without cold.

And then the weirdness, which is not an accent here but the foundation. The breakdown is total if you stay long enough for your thin-blooded brain to stop insisting.

Directions do not hold. Uphill is not always up; on the western pavement, if you walk with your eyes half-closed against the dim, you will feel yourself descending while your breathing labors harder, as if the slope disagrees with itself. Distances do not add. A cairn you left an hour ago will be ahead of you again, not behind, but slightly to the left and paler, as if you are walking through drafts of the same place. Your own echo arrives before you shout — a flat, bone-conducted pre-echo that makes newcomers gag. Shadows, such as they are in this shadowless light, fall upward off the moss mounds, leaning toward the pale smear in the sky, trembling.

Worst, or best: the sky has seams. If you lie on your back on a moss mattress — which is soft, shockingly soft, and vibrates your spine into numbness — and stare up, you will see them. Long, straight faults in the dim where the grey is a fraction darker, crossing at angles no geology allows. Along those faults, sometimes, the deafening tone catches, skips like a needle, and for three heartbeats there is something else underneath: a silence so absolute it feels wet. And in that silence the gardens hold their breath, the bells stop shivering, and you understand that this whole high valley is not on top of the world at all, but inside a held note, an instrument still being carved, pre-erosion, pre-river, pre-animal, pre-rain. A place still deciding what a place is.

Then the seam passes. The roar floods back into your teeth. The leaves resume their trembling. The cold settles back onto your neck. A dust devil of dry mineral powder, without any wind you can feel, unscrews itself a hundred yards off and walks slowly, over minutes, across empty pavement from one garden to another, as if carrying a message too slow to ever arrive.

You do not stay long on the extreme height. You cannot. Your nose bleeds dry blood that crusts instantly to black dust. Your thoughts fray. But when you go down, if down is still a direction that will have you, you take with you a plug of cottony moss in your pocket, still buzzing faintly hours later, and the knowledge of the rule:

Here, water never came. Life learned to be lush on noise. Stone learned to sing before it learned to fall apart. And people, long ago, with only their hands and stacked rock and woven bells, learned to live in the loudest quiet there is, tending gardens that hear them coming long before they are seen.
