On top there is no mountain anymore. There is only the Work.

You climb for three days through dry switchbacks of shattered shale to reach it, lungs working thin air, and when you finally pull yourself over the last rise you expect a summit — rock, sky, drop. Instead your hands come down flat on fitted stone.

The whole top has been taken. Leveled, paved, walled, roofed over in places with slabs. Nothing natural shows anywhere. Even the cliffs at the edge have been faced with dry-laid blocks to make them sheer, so the place ends all at once in a straight cut: pavement, low parapet, and then a thousand feet of empty warm air.

It is small. Eighty of your own sidestepping paces from east parapet to west parapet, sixty north to south, and you cannot walk it straight. That is the first thing the altitude and the heat do not prepare you for — the packedness. There is no room between things. The pavement itself is only the floor. On it, shoulder to shoulder with no alleys, stand the makings: low altars of stacked slate, beehive huts no taller than your chest, clay stupas coil-built and thumb-smoothed, cairns, offering platforms, hollow towers, basins that never held water. Hundreds of them. They touch. They lean on each other like teeth. To move you have to turn sideways and slide, hand on warm stone, foot finding only more stone — a lintel here, a capstone there. There is nowhere to set a pack down that is not somebody's roof.

Everything was made by hands, and only hands. You can see it because nothing has been allowed to weather away completely, and nothing has ever been replaced with anything better. The tool marks are still in the soft rock under the polish: peck-marks from hammerstones, shallow grooves where cordage was dragged back and forth to saw, the print of four fingers wiped across wet clay three thousand years ago and then fired in a dung fire that left the clay pink and black in patches. No cut metal angles. No sawn timber — there is no timber this high and dry, there never was. Simple copper chisels at most, mostly stone on stone. Roof beams, where they exist, are long slabs of sandstone carried up on backs, spanning four feet because that is as far as stone will span without breaking.

It is all deeply, unreasonably old, and worn to its final shape. The wind up here carries no water, only grit, and for uncounted time it has worried at the Work. Corners are gone. Everything that was square is now round-shouldered. Doorways to the little huts are mouths smoothed to ovals you must crawl through. The pavement flags dip in the middle where feet and knees polished them, and the joints between flags are tight with blown dust cemented by heat. Nothing is collapsing and nothing is sharp. The place has settled into the shape it intends to keep. Erosion has done all it can do to handmade things, and handmade things have endured it.

There is no water here. Not a seep, not a cistern stain, not a lichen cup. Your lips crack within an hour. The air is warm — not hot noon-desert hot, but kiln-warm, stone-warm, a stored warmth that comes up through the soles of your boots and stays on your skin even though the light is dim. The sun never gets through. The sky over the summit is always the color of smoked glass, a low amber-brown gloom, as if you are under a lid. At high noon the sun is only a paler coin in the haze, casting no real shadow, just a darkening under each cairn. Maybe it is dust forever suspended. Maybe it is the altitude itself. The light does not change. Morning does not come.

And there is no life to use even that. No grass in the joints, no moss on the north sides, no lichen. No flies. No birds cross over — you notice their absence after a while, how the sky beyond the parapet is empty. No goats, no people now, though people must have lived jammed here once to build so close. The only smell is warm dust and fired clay.

What fills the place, what owns it, is sound.

From below, on the climb, you heard it as wind. Up here, inside the Work, you understand it is made.

Every second hollow tower, every third cairn, every upright slab with a hole pecked through it is a flute. The builders set them facing the prevailing west wind, which at this height never stops and never varies. It funnels up the faced cliff, hits the parapet, and is forced through hundreds of throats of stone and baked clay — some as wide as your leg, some no wider than a finger. Each gives one note. Together they give a chord so loud and so dense it stops being music.

It is deafening. Not gusty, not howling. A solid, continuous pressure. It sits on your sternum. It makes your teeth ache in sympathy. With your mouth open you can feel the different pipes beating against each other — a low organ drone from the big towers that you feel in your knees, a middle wail from the cairns, a needle-high scream from the little clay whistles wedged between huts that seems to come from inside your own ears. Put your hands over your ears and it gets louder, conducted through bone. Shout and you cannot hear yourself. The stones themselves hum under your palms.

And it never changes. That is the strangest part, the part that makes visitors sit down after a few minutes with their backs against a warm stupa and stare. Wind should gust. It does not. The tone you hear on arrival is the tone an hour later, a day later — the old ones who stayed a week said a year later. Same volume, same pitch, same beating. The dust in the air hangs motionless in the dim light even as the roar goes on, as if motion and sound have become uncoupled. Your shirt does not flap. The heat does not rise or fall. The light does not dim further toward evening. The place is in equilibrium, holding its breath at the top of its roar.

If you stay long enough you stop trying to listen for variation and you begin to feel the rules: do not add, do not remove. There is no space to add. There is no loose stone that is not part of the tuning. To lift a single capstone would open a gap and silence a pipe and the whole chord would be marred, and there is nowhere to put the stone down anyway. To bring water would crack the clay. To bring a plant would choke a throat. So you crawl, sideways, between the warm, humming, hand-smoothed shapes, in dim amber light, in air that vibrates and never cools, on a tiny crowded island of pure making held high above a world you can no longer hear, and after a while your own pulse seems to tune itself to the drone, and you understand why they built it so packed and so small: so that there would be no room left for anything else to happen.
