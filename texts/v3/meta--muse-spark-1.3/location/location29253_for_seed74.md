The hollow first, before the town.

You come down out of the wind — the land drops only a little, forty feet maybe, a shallow bowl in farmland — but it's enough. The air pools there. Cool air. It stays. On an ordinary midday, with ordinary white-grey light coming straight down without shadows, you can feel the temperature step as you descend: your ears cool, your breath goes faintly white even though it's not winter.

It is not wet. That's the first thing to unlearn. There is no river here, no lake, no marsh sucking at your boots. It's damp. The kind of damp that lives in the air itself. Stone sweats. Iron sweats. Every morning, and most afternoons, a fine rain passes through that barely counts as rain — five minutes of mist that beads and doesn't fall — and the rest of the time the world condenses. You touch a handrail and your palm comes away dark with water that wasn't there until you touched it.

In the middle of that cool damp bowl is a town, or the idea of a town shrunk down to rooms.

You can walk it edge to edge in fifteen minutes. That's the whole of it. Pasture fences on the rim, then the ground goes soft underfoot, and then you're inside.

They call it Brindle, on the one sign still legible, and it was built to be urban in miniature. Not a village that grew — a settlement that was laid. Cobble lanes in a grid that has started to shrug. Terraced workers' rows, two-up two-down in dark brick. A mill with a sawtooth roof. A dye-works. A smithy. A substation with ceramic teeth. Gutters, culverts, lamp-posts, rails set flush in the street for carts, pipes running abovehead from building to building on iron stanchions, wires sagging between. The full infrastructure of a city, compressed into hamlet-size. Layer on layer: a culvert under a lane under a pipe over a doorway, all of it still there, all of it weathered until the edges have given up being sharp.

Nothing here is new, and nothing is actively falling down anymore. That time passed a long time ago. The brick has gone soft-pink at the corners where frost got it a hundred winters running, and then stopped getting worse. The rust on the water tanks has settled into a deep, stable orange-brown, like bark. The cobbles have bellied where roots lifted them and then let them back down. It's a place that argued with weather for a century and then came to an agreement.

Because the other citizen here is plant life. Not as decoration. As substrate.

Everything horizontal is alive. Not patchy — total. The ground between the buildings isn't ground. It's a mattress, ankle-deep and sometimes knee-deep, of moss and cropped fern and stitchwort and clover and young birch suckers that never get to become birch. The lanes are still there — you can see the crown of the cobbles showing through like knuckles — but you walk on green. The roofs are green. The sills are green. The top of every wall, every pipe, every sleeping machine has a pelt.

Yet it is sparse. That's the strangeness you feel first without naming it. For all that green fullness, the place feels empty, airy, held apart. The buildings stand well clear of each other with big breathing gaps of nothing between them. No crowd. No clutter. Five houses where there should be fifty, and the rest is just luminous space. A single chimney answers a single shed across a hundred feet of fern-sea. Presences are few and far between — a door, a lamppost with its glass long gone and a fern growing out of the cup, a lone cast-iron bench — each one isolated in green like stones in a Japanese garden, but industrial.

And it is still working. Quietly.

That's the second thing to unlearn: you expect a ruin. It isn't.

In the mill, the line-shaft still turns. Slow. You can hear it if you stand still — which you will, because the town makes you quiet. A low, woolly hum through brick, not loud enough to be called noise. Outside, the big flywheel, twelve feet across and furred in a velvet jacket of moss on its sheltered side, rotates once every nine seconds. It drives nothing anyone needs anymore, except it drives the ventilators, and the ventilators drive the air, and the dynamo in the substation still puts out enough current to keep the street lamps burning during the day — pale orange dots in normal daylight, useless and persistent.

No one tends it, visibly. No crowd of workers. Perhaps one caretaker, perhaps none. A coat hangs on a nail in the dye-works that has grown into the doorframe. A mug left on a boiler has a miniature garden in it. The machines have been adopted. Roots have learned the warm routes around bearings and avoid them. Ivy does not climb at random here; it follows mortar lines and conduit, tracing the town's old electrical diagram in green wire. Nettles mass thickest where the underground steam main still leaks a degree or two of heat in winter. The plants are not wrecking the works. They're farming its warmth.

The rules of Brindle, as you learn them by walking:

First, if it stays damp, it stays. Brick, iron, wood, cloth — leave it for a season and moss will seam it and hold it together better than mortar. Pull the moss off a wall and the wall will crumble by spring. The locals — whoever they were — learned to stop scraping.

Second, iron must be kept warm or moving. Anything cold and still belongs to lichen within a year. Anything that turns, or carries warm water, stays iron. You can read the town's temperature by color: grey-green for dead, black-oiled and rust-red for living. Touch the living pipes as you pass; they are blood-warm in a cool world, and beaded with condensation that never quite drips.

Third, water is not in a place, it is in passage. There are no ponds, only interruptions. A cistern under the mill eaves that fills from mist and empties by seep. A shallow iron trough where condensate from a hundred feet of overhead pipe gathers and falls — *plik... plik... plik* — into moss that drinks it instantly. Puddles that exist only as dark footprints for an hour after someone has walked. If you are thirsty, you don't find water. You wait, and you wring a sleeve.

The ecology is almost entirely plant-paced, which means it is fast and slow at once. Fast: in a week after that mist-rain, the fern crosiers unroll six inches, stitchwort whites out a whole lane edge, slime molds climb the north side of the dye vats in orange fans overnight and are gone by noon. Slow: the birch suckers try every year to close the gaps between buildings and every winter the damp and the shade thin them back. The town breathes in, breathes out, never quite filling, never quite emptying.

Animals are rare to the point of event. No flocks, no herds, no chorus. A wren, once, scolding from inside a boiler flue. A black rat crossing a lane with great deliberation, leaving a dark green wake in the dew. Moths around the day-burning lamps, grey as the light, bumping glass that isn't there. Worms, of course, and springtails if you kneel and look, and under a lifted cobble the white thread of mycelium thick as rope. But mostly, no eyes on you. The feeling is not wilderness. It's an emptiness that has been kept tidy by non-human hands.

Sounds come in that emptiness with unnatural clarity because there is so little to compete with.

Mostly quiet: your own boots squelching softly in moss, then the cobble knocking underneath. The wind combing the sawtooth roofs. The ventilator chain ticking. Water thinking about falling.

And then, underneath, the active processes if you stay long enough to hear them as process, not noise: the *tss-tss* of the mill loom still weaving a weft of nothing, shuttle going back and forth because no one told it to stop; the sigh and clank as the substation regulates itself; the almost inaudible pop of fern sporangia in the afternoon when the cloud thins for an hour; the long, slow creak of a wire grown heavy with vine, tightening, loosening.

Light here does not dramatize. At noon it is flat and even, the color of skimmed milk, and the green looks almost grey in it, honest and cool. Colors don't shout: brick-dust red, oil black, moss a hundred muted greens, rust orange gone brown, the pale apricot of dead fern fronds from last year still tangled in this year's green. It's a working palette. Unpretty and complete.

If you stand on the slight rise by the lamp-post at the center — where three lanes meet in a triangle of moss with a drain grating in the middle still swallowing mist-water with a faint gurgle — you can see the boundaries all around. The green mattress ends. The pipes stop. The last wall, half-eaten and half-held by ivy, gives way to pasture. The town does not sprawl. It is a room with green walls and an iron ceiling of wires, cathedral-high in feeling though only two storeys tall, because so much of it is air.

It is unusual, but only just. Nothing floats, nothing glows. The unusualness is in the negotiation: a place that was meant to be hard, dense, hot, loud, peopled, and instead became soft, sparse, cool, quiet, planted — and kept its engines turning anyway, under blankets, like sleepers breathing slowly under quilts, tended by roots that know exactly where the heat is.

Stay till evening and the cool deepens a notch. Condensation thickens on every underside. The lamps, already on, become slightly more themselves. The moss, which has drunk all day from air, gives a faint, sweet, iron-and-chlorophyll smell when you press it. The flywheel turns. *Plik.* The wren, if you're lucky, makes its one sharp note and is gone.
