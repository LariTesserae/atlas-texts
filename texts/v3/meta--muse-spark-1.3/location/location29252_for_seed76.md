It is noon and it is cold.

That is the first rule here. The light is absolute — white, direct, unfiltered — and it does not warm you. You stand very high, kilometers above any sea, on a plain so broad the curve of the world shows in it, and the air is too thin to hold heat. Your breath makes a small cloud that is torn away instantly. The sun is a hard coin in a dark blue zenith. At the edges of the sky, below the level of your eyes, there is a flat, endless whiteness: the cloud deck. You are above weather. And yet this place makes its own.

The ground is pale. Not sand, not quite rock — a dry mineral skin, chalk and pumice and salt pressed and cracked into polygons the size of houses. Between the plates, the seams are dark with wind-compacted dust. Every stone is pitted on one side, polished on the other, shaped by a wind that has blown the same way for ten thousand years. There are no rivers. There have never been rivers here in the memory of anything living. No lakes, no springs, no seep. If you kneel and press your palm to the ground, it pulls the moisture from you. The earth tastes of salt and iron and glass. It rings faintly if you tap it.

And yet it is lush.

That is the second rule, and it takes a while to see because the lushness is spaced out. For a long time you think the plain is empty — white to the horizon, shivering in heat-glare that is not heat. Then your eyes adjust and you pick out the green.

They sit far apart, hundreds of paces between them, each one alone in its polygon like an island. Great domes, low cushions, shaggy nests three, four, six meters across and waist-high. From a distance they look like moss heaps, soft and overgrown. Up close they are dense, layered, impossibly leafy — tens of thousands of shoots packed tight, outer leaves silvered with hair, inner leaves deep teal and verdigris and oil-green, warm to the touch even when the air is cold. Lush is the only word. You could bury your arms in them to the elbow and not find the bottom.

Nothing here uses water. The leaves are sealed. Break one and it does not bleed water; it bleeds scent — turpentine, lemon-resin, a clear gold oil that does not evaporate and does not freeze. The hairs on the leaves are hollow glass. Each one catches light and pipes it inward, and catches wind-borne dust and holds it. The plants eat dust. They eat sunlight and night-cold and the mineral breath of the plain. At night, when temperatures drop below freezing, their salts sweat just enough to pull a molecule or two of thin vapor from the air, then seal shut again by dawn. They have no roots as you understand roots. They have anchors, glassy threads that fuse directly into the chalk and dissolve it slowly, trading acid for phosphorus, for iron. They are half plant, half stone. If you try to pull one up, it will not come. It is part of the polygon.

Between the cushions, lesser forms: flat rosettes of black-green blades laid flush to the ground to escape wind, fields of brittle lace — coral-like lattices a hand high that shatter if stepped on and regrow over decades — and the tall ones, very rare, the sails. A single stalk, two meters, topped with a fan of translucent amber membrane that never moves with the wind but against it, turning edge-on with a slow creak you feel more than hear. They orient to dust flow, not to sun. All the young cushions lean downwind of the old ones, catching what sloughs off.

The land itself is old and settled. This is not a young sharp mountain but a high, worn table, its valleys broad and U-shaped and dry, its mesas soft-shouldered. Yardangs — long whaleback ridges carved by wind — run in parallel fleets for kilometers, and in their lee the dust has piled into ripples hard as wood. In the hollows, salt has heaved up into honeycomb pans that crunch underfoot into powder. Nothing is collapsing, nothing is new. The erosion has finished its work and now maintains it. A stone balanced on another stone will be there for your lifetime and your grandchildren's. The shapes are in balance with the wind that made them.

Because the plain is so vast it breathes. By mid-afternoon the light, still bright, slants and the ground gives back the little heat it took, and columns of warm air rise, invisible until they pick up dust and become wandering pillars, slow dust-devils that travel for miles between the cushions, combing them. The cushions exhale then — you can see it if you lie still — a faint shimmer above each dome as they open ten thousand stomata at once and release spores, oil-coated, buoyant. The spores rise and join and make a high haze, a thin gold veil that for an hour dims the sun and then precipitates nothing. No rain. Just a falling of tiny lights back to the polygons. The plants make their own weather, and the weather is dry.

Animals are rare enough that you doubt them. For days you will see only the wind-tracks, a single line of three-toed prints going straight from one cushion to the next and stopping, as if the maker vanished. Then in the late light you will see it: a grazer, far off, the color of chalk, tall and narrow as a folded umbrella, moving with absurd slowness between domes. It does not drink. It buries its head in a cushion and sucks oil, and the cushion closes around the wound within minutes. It looks at you without fear because fear is expensive here. Later, higher up, something else — a manta of dust and membrane riding a thermal, mouth open, filtering spores, utterly silent. They do not call. Sound would be wasted in this thin air.

For it is quiet. That is the third rule. The wind makes almost no noise because there is nothing to flap or rustle; the cushion hairs take the wind and break it into a high, faint whistle at the edge of hearing. Your boots on pumice are the loudest thing for miles: a dry crunch, then nothing. If you hold your breath you can hear the sails creak as they turn, and very faintly, if you press your ear to a large dome at dusk, a ticking as its inner leaves fold against the coming cold. Otherwise, silence, piled deep between the green islands.

Stay long enough and the weirdness settles in, not dramatic, just wrong at the edges. Shadows here have a blue-green fringe that lingers a second after you move. The dust sometimes hangs motionless in the air for minutes, suspended by static, each grain lit. The stones migrate — not rolled by wind but lifted on needles of salt ice on cold mornings, inching a finger's width each year away from the cushions, as if repelled. And the cushions themselves: they are warm inside. Put your hand deep in one on a freezing night and it is like an animal, summer-warm, humming with slow chemistry, digesting stone and light into oil.

No one built this. No wall, no cairn, no path. The spacing is not made, it is negotiated — each dome poisons the ground just enough around itself that another cannot root too close, and so the whole continent is a loose grid of solitudes, lush and empty, lush and empty, to the horizon and beyond. You could walk for a month and not cross it. The light would stay bright, the air cool, the ground dry underfoot, the green domes recurring like breaths.

Learn to live as they do: seal yourself at noon, open at dusk, drink light, eat dust, keep your water — if you brought any — hidden deep inside you, oil-thick and precious, never to be spilled on this ground that would drink it and give nothing back.
