It is low country, though not flat.

You come down into it without noticing the descent — the air just gets heavier, warmer against your skin, and the horizon widens until you can see how big it is. A basin the size of a small valley, a day to cross if you walked straight, which you wouldn't. In normal daylight, late-morning white, everything is exactly as lit as it should be. That is part of what is wrong with it.

There is no green. Not a blade, not a scrub, not a root-crack weed. At first you think volcanic, post-fire, salt-kill — then you look longer and understand it is older than that. The stones here have settled into themselves. Rounded basalt boulders half-buried in rust-red grit, long low ridges of weathered ironstone, pale sinks where salt and silica have bloomed and cracked and bloomed again into the same pattern. Wind and water have done their work so long they have come into agreement. Nothing is sharp. Nothing is new. Gully mouths open and close in the same curves. Pebbles fit in their hollows like they have always been there.

And there is nothing made. No wall, no road, no post, no cairn, no wire, no broken bottle or cut timber. Not even a straight line to suggest someone tried. Undeveloped in the way only places that never needed developing are.

But it sounds like a factory floor.

From the lip of the basin you hear it first as pressure in the ears: a low, wide hum with a beat inside it. As you go down it separates — a deep piston-thump, thump, thump from somewhere under the ground, too regular to be water, and over it a higher hiss and clank, metal on metal, steam through a valve. It is loud. You have to raise your voice to think. The sound comes from everywhere and nowhere, bouncing off the low warm air, off the stone. You keep looking for the shed, the engine house, the stack. There isn't one.

The ground is the engine.

This is damp country inside dry country. There is no river, no lake. Water lives here in pockets. In the shade-side of boulders, in shallow pans no deeper than your hand, a dark brine sits and does not evaporate and does not grow. Between the stones the grit is cool-damp to the touch even at midday when the air is warm —  that thick, bathhouse warmth that makes your shirt stick. Beads of condensation form on every leeward face, on the undersides of overhangs, on black glassy flows where the rock melted sometime in the deep past and froze mid-ripple. It drips, occasionally. You hear a plink, plink in the larger roar. Once in a while a shallow sheet of rain passes — you can see it coming as a gray veil a mile off — lays a brief shine over everything, and is gone, and the pans are exactly as full as before.

Where the water sits, the stone is stained. Not with plants. Ocher and teal and liver-dark films slick the rims, bacterial mats, iron-eaters, things simpler than lichen that are not quite alive in the way you expect and not quite mineral either. They do not spread. They hold their thin borders year in and out. If you touch one it feels like wet felt and smells faintly of oil and pennies.

Because everything tastes faintly of oil and ozone here, though nothing has been drilled or burned. The rocks themselves are ore-rich — bands of magnetite and copper green veined through the red. In the warm damp, they make a weak, endless current. Your arm hairs lift when you step over certain seams. A shallow pan of brine will sometimes shiver without wind. Fillings in your teeth ache with a faint metallic sing that matches the hum you hear. At night — though the light never seems to fully change while you are here — you might see St. Elmo flickers crawling on the high points of boulders, blue-white, silent under the roar. No wires. No dynamo. Just wet stone and heat and chemistry doing scaled production on its own: evaporate, precipitate, oxidize, repeat.

The loudest places are the vents. Scattered sparsely across the basin, a few hundred yards apart, so you are always between them: low mounds of layered sinter and slag-like cinder, each with a mouth at the top. From each comes a column of wet heat you can see shimmering, and a sound like a boiler letting off. Chuff — hiss — chuff — hiss. And at intervals, a heavy iron *dong*, as a slab of stone inside the throat lifts on steam pressure and falls back on its seat. Lift and fall. Lift and fall. They have presumably been lifting and falling for ten thousand years, and the seats are worn perfectly round, polished like bearings. Around each mound is a collar of rust and white salt and that dark slick film, and a ring of damp grit where condensation rains back down continuously.

Nothing changes.

That is the rule you learn if you stay past the first hour, when your ears adjust enough to notice that the thump has not sped or slowed, the hiss has not risen or dropped. The pans do not fill or empty. The rain veil comes and goes but the wetness stays the same. The bacterial rims do not advance or retreat. The stones, for all their weathered softness, do not crumble under your boot; the grit takes your footprint and then, when you look back, seems to have settled back to its balanced ripple. The air stays warm in the same way, heavy and mineral, smelling of hot iron and wet salt.

Even life obeys the stasis. Rarely, very rarely, something moves. A pale, almost translucent lizard-like thing, blind-looking, darts from under one boulder to another to lick condensation. A flock — three — of white, long-legged insects stitches across a brine pan and is gone. You might hear a single clicking cry far off, once, and not again. They do not live here so much as they pass through a place that tolerates them. There is not enough to eat, unless you eat stone and steam.

If you stand still in the middle, between vents, with the open land extending past you on all sides in its sparse scatter of mound and pan and boulder and damp hollow, you feel it most clearly: the unusualness is not in any one feature. It is in the combination. An untouched place that behaves like a machine. A loud place where nothing happens. A wet place in a dry basin that never gets wetter or drier. A warm, bright, low, ordinary-lit mineral valley, humming with electrified industry with no builders, no workers, no product, no waste — only the process itself, turning over and over for so long it has worn itself smooth as river stone, and will keep turning exactly this way.

You leave footprints that fill slowly with beaded water. Behind you, the thump keeps time.
