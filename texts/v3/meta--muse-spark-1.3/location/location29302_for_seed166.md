You go down into it before you understand that you are already inside.

The light told you first. It is ordinary daylight, mid-morning daylight, filtered down through water until it loses direction. Not dark. Not gloom. Just green-white and even on all sides, the way light gets when it has passed through ten or twelve feet of still water. You can see the edges of the place from wherever you stand in it.

It is small. You could hold it in your head all at once. A basin, a room, a drowned chapel about as wide as a grove of five or six trees. The bottom is below everything — below the water table, below the grade of the land around it, below sea level if the sea were near. You feel the weight of being down. The sides rise up around you in a low, closed bowl, and then they keep rising past the waterline into air you can't quite see. There is nowhere else to go from here but up.

The water is the place. Not a lake in a place, not a flood over a place. The water *is* the terrain.

Cool water. Not cold enough to hurt. Cool enough to pull the heat straight out of your wrists and ankles and the back of your neck if you stay still. 50 degrees, maybe. The kind of cool that makes your breathing slow down without you deciding to. It sits on your skin with a faint mineral skin of its own — clean, but not wild clean. Processed clean.

The floor under your feet, where you can touch it through the water, is settled. Old. Nothing sharp, nothing raw. Rounded cobble and fine silt and bedrock worn smooth, in that long-existed balance where erosion has finished its argument and everything now agrees. Hollows where silt has pooled for years. Rises where stone shows through, polished. If you fan the silt away it settles back in exactly the same pattern within an hour, as if it remembers. This shape has been this shape for a very long time.

There is almost nothing in it.

A few presences, spaced far apart. That is the rule here: much space between things. On one sloping stone, a sparse beard of pale green weed, three or four strands, each as thin as thread, swaying almost imperceptibly, bleached by low nutrients and steady light. Ten feet away, another patch — a flat film of algae, olive-drab, clinging to the rock like a stain. No thickets. No meadows. No cover. The bottom is mostly bare: gray stone, tan silt, a rust-colored dusting in the low spots that isn't rust, exactly, but iron precipitated out of the water itself.

And no animals. Not hidden. Not absent because you came at the wrong time. Absent as a condition. No fish flicker in the green. No minnows, no tadpoles, no water boatmen dimpling the surface. No snails on the stones. If you lift a cobble there are no nymphs underneath. The water is too worked, too kept, for them. It supports the simplest green film and nothing that eats it. You notice the quiet of that after a while — not just no splashing, but no grazing, no darting. A food chain with only the first link.

It is mostly quiet. At first you think it is silent. Then your ears adjust to being half-submerged and you hear what the water carries.

A low hum. Not loud. Felt more in the sternum than heard. A 60-cycle steadiness that never wavers, never swells, never drops. Far off — above you, beyond the rim — big engines are turning. Pumps. You will never see them from in here. There is nothing built here. No walls, no culvert mouth, no concrete, no dam face, no cabin, no dock pilings, no artifact to point to and say *made*. The bowl is all natural stone and silt.

But the water knows the machines.

You can taste it when a little gets on your lips: faintly metallic, faintly ozonic, like water that has passed through filters and copper and electric fields. It is too clear for a closed pond. Too uniform in temperature from top to bottom — no warm skin on top, no cold sink at the bottom. Someone, something, is mixing it. Someone is keeping it at this level, at this clarity, at this chemistry, all the time. The surface barely moves, but if you watch a mote of silt suspended in the light, it describes a slow, perfect circuit around the basin — out from center, around the rim, back to center — over and over, with machined regularity. Not wind. Not stream. Circulation.

Occasionally a single bead of oil, rainbow-thin, passes across the surface and is gone. Occasionally the hum deepens for three seconds, exactly three, and the pressure in your ears changes by a hair, and then releases. Intake. Discharge. Intake. Discharge. The breath of a system so large this whole basin is only a sump, a stilling well, a side pocket where the flow pauses to be measured before it goes back to work.

The light never really changes. Clouds must pass up there, day must go to evening, but down here under the water the green-white stays even, as if the water itself is lit. Sounds don't echo. There is no weather in here. A leaf falls in from above once in a long while and circles for days without sinking or rotting, preserved by the cool and the mineral balance, until it is finally drawn up and out by the slow circuit.

If you stay long enough your own motion feels wrong — too abrupt, too animal. The proper motion here is the mote's motion, the weed's motion: almost none. The place rewards stillness. It is in equilibrium and it intends to stay there.

Nothing is growing to fill it. Nothing is decaying to empty it. The stone stays smooth. The silt stays where it is put. The water stays cool and clear and faintly humming with distant power, held between its low stone walls like a sample kept forever under glass.

You could leave and come back in ten years and put your hand in and find the same cool, hear the same low engine-note through the water, see the same four strands of weed on the same stone. That is its nature. Not dead. Maintained.
