Not up. That's the first thing.

If I were up, I'd be wrong here. Up is too expensive — phosphorus spent a million years ago, shade that never lands. I tried for a minute to be the harrier, quartering low with barely a beat, or the egret standing so still you mistake me for a stump. But I can't hold that stillness. I have too much hurry in me. Even flight here is wading, and I don't know how to wade the air yet.

I tried to be the dragonfly, copper-bellied, teal-winged. The air is too full for me. It saws. The cicadas in the paper-barks shake it until it shimmers more than heat does, and the columns of midges hold station over the pans and wobble but do not drift. I would have to mate on the wing, lay by ticking the water — tick, tick, tick — and never perch long. I'm not that hot, that metal.

Lower, then.

I thought the sedge tussock, olive-yellow wire harsh as a file, each one an island raised a finger's width above drowning by its own dead. That was closer. To grow dense instead of fast. To hold spiders and skinks in my crown at noon. To have a halo of empty leached sand around me the way desert plants do, except the desert is water.

Closer still, the sundew. No bigger than a thumbnail. A rosette of red spoons on bare wet sand between tussocks, each spoon tipped with glue that never dries even in this steaming air. Eating the air because I cannot eat the ground. That tells you everything about the soil. I glittered for a while at that. I liked it.

But I kept sinking.

Past the frogs — and god, I wanted to be a frog, the high metallic trill without pause, green-gold, belly translucent, clinging to a sedge blade over water with my throat pulsing white, or sunk to the eyes in black grease doing the bottle-knock with my mouth closed, or spread-eagled in the deep meander scar with only nostrils out, groaning. To be part of the layering that never lets the plain go quiet. To wait buried under a tussock in shed skin and breathe slowly. I wanted that loudness. But frogs still hop. They still have legs, decisions.

I have fewer decisions than that.

Find me where the almost-imperceptible swell gives up and becomes hollow. Two feet lower, that's all. Where the pale gritty sand with its pea-gravel of ironstone stops rattling underfoot and the ground turns black and greasy and sucks and does not let go cleanly. On the lip of a pan that stays year-round, an old meander scar that lost its river a long time ago. Warm tannic water with a skin of pollen up top, a cool layer near the bottom, silt as fine as flour that puffs when you wade. Between two sedge tussocks. Under the sideways light of a paper-bark that will never be tall.

I am half-sunk there.

I am one of the last hard bits. A duricrust nodule, a broken pan of rust-red laterite rounded to an egg by more rain than you can imagine. If you broke me open I am banded yellow and blood-red inside. Outside I sweat. Hours after the rain has stopped I weep water from my pores and darken the ground around me in a halo.

That halo is me, too. Or I am its table.

Look close: the darkening is alive. Rust and ochre slicks of iron bacteria, filamentous green algae, a skin of diatoms that turns gold then black as you stir it. Bloodworms coiled red with hemoglobin underneath because there is almost no oxygen down there, tiny white ostracods flicking like seeds. The mud stinks faintly sweet when disturbed — rot without hurry. On my back, above the waterline, a garden: ferns and mosses and a sundew seedling making a living where roots cannot.

I don't move. The pans migrate a few feet a year, eating at one soft bank, filling in at another with sedge rhizomes, and I stay while they go. I grow a millimeter a century. Change happens at the speed of rust.

But everything comes to me.

The water rat leaves opened mussel shells on me. The crab with one claw larger plops backwards into its thimble hole beside me as boots pass, and waits with only eyestalks out. Small silver fish, two to three inches, dimple the pollen-skin over me to take fallen midges and scatter at the shadow of the heron. The heron's shadow. The turtle's nose breaks the surface near me like a twig and stays an hour. Tadpoles blacken the shallows around me in commas. At dusk bats replace the dragonflies over me and snap midges out of the thickening air.

At noon condensation beads on every stem and on the undersides of leaves and falls with a tick into the water below me. The plain steams faintly. You only see it if you crouch and look sideways. I see it always, from down here.

This afternoon rain will come again — you can smell the weight building behind the haze — and fill depressions with brown water, and then sun will take it back up. The frogs will get louder layer over layer. The midges will drop. The ibis will probe faster in their loose lines. I will weep a little faster.

Nothing will look different, except everything will be wetter, and then, slowly, exactly as wet as before.

I am old like after mountains. I am what is left when everything edible has been washed out and everything hurried has been slowed down. I hold still longest, so I live here best.
