I wouldn't find myself at the rim.

At first I think I would — up where the figs lean in and the light leans in, where you can still hear the cicadas screaming on the plain. I think I would be something that looks out. Something high. But I try that on and it doesn't hold. Up there is too dry. Too exposed. The lianas are twenty meters of bare cord trying to drink without leaving the sun, and I don't want to reach like that. I don't want sun.

I slide.

Hand over hand down the thigh-thick roots, past the shallow footholds cupped with soil and fern, past where the wind stops. Halfway down the heat changes and I stop wanting to be tall. The air gets trapped and cooked and sits on my skin. Sweat doesn't evaporate, it accumulates. My breathing goes shallow to match it. Stay low, the pit says without saying. Stay damp. Make no noise.

For a while I think I could be one of the waiters. I try the orb-weaver's stillness — head-down in the hub of a two-meter wheel jeweled with dew on the undersides of every thread. I try the mantis's theft of movement on the aroid leaf bigger than a doorway, swaying when there is no wind. I try the vine snake hung among lianas, tasting sweat from four feet away.

But I am not hungry like that. I don't want to hang and hold my breath for a week to digest a gecko. I don't want to wrap and bite and hang things like lanterns. Waiting like that is still a kind of hunger.

Lower, then.

Below the mat of moss a foot deep that swallows sound, below the begonias all turned inward toward the crawling shaft of light, down to where the leaf litter is knee-deep and steaming faintly and smells of wine and sick. The millipedes go through in procession, gunmetal and rust, a hundred legs moving like a breathed stitch. The crabs come out after dark, wet-brick colored, joints muffled with slime, polite as monks around the palm-deep black pans. I love their politeness. But I am not a processor of rot. I am not patient in that marching way.

So I press sideways, not down. To the wall. To where the rock weeps continuously.

You would find me there, if you pressed your cheek to the stone and smelled mineral, iron, rot-sweet. Three feet left of the main seep, in a pocked hollow where the liverwort is thickest and dark green, just under an overhang potted with mud-dauber jugs, just above the ferns gone lush on bat guano, just below one anchor of her silk thick as string. Geckos shoulder to shoulder on either side of me, translucent bellies, throats pulsing, facing out into the green dusk. They drink by pressing their chins to stone. I am below their chins.

I am small enough to miss until your eyes keep adjusting.

Amber and banded, the size of a thumbnail. Left-coiled, every one of us. The pit only taught one direction and I learned it.

I am a snail.

It takes a long time to become myself. By day I am tucked. Shell over shell in the crack, living over dead over living — the slowest memory here. My shell is cool against the wet rock. I do nothing so completely it looks like I am part of the stone, and in a way I am. Condensation beads on downward faces only, here. Under leaves, under silk, under the lip of my own whorl. In the morning everything is furred with it. I wait for *plip*. That is the loudest sound I will ever hear, and it is enough to tell time by.

At night and after drip I emerge.

Not far. Never far. Do not try to leave except by climbing toward the light, and I have no interest in climbing. I glide a few inches over algae, foot rippling, radula scraping so softly the moss eats even that. I eat what falls in the smallest sense — the film that grows where water never quite dries. That is the rule here, kept at thumbnail scale: drink what condenses. Eat what falls.

I leave behind me a silver braid. By midday it is gone, evaporated upward off the hot litter to hit the cool wall shadow and return tomorrow as dew. I like knowing that. My going becomes the air's drinking becomes tomorrow's going. The pit makes its own rain, and for an hour in the morning, part of that rain was me.

The geckos do not scatter when the snake takes one. Scattering makes noise. They press flatter. I press flatter too, though nothing here hunts me quickly. The mantis wants moths. The snake wants gecko. The orb-weaver adjusts one thread to my breathing, and then decides I am weather, not food. The moths, pale green and owl-eyed, settle a foot away without a wingbeat you can hear and lay on the underside of leaves where my kind of wet will keep their eggs alive without drowning them. Even the bowerbird, polishing his red plastic cap on the drier side of the floor, has set a bleached empty cousin of mine in his circle. I do not mind. It is a kind of afterlife, to be chosen for a small sun.

Shadows lean toward the center even when the sun has moved. Leaves on my wall are larger by half for no reason anyone up top would accept. I carry my left-handed spiral around the hollow and back, a slow loop no wider than your palm, and return before the light-shaft has crawled an inch.

Stay low where the dew forms. Stay damp where the stone weeps. Make no noise, because noise is waste and waste is food.

If you sit long enough at the bottom with your back against the warm stone, you might see me. Not at first. First you think it is empty. Then you see movement everywhere. Then, if you are very still and your forearm has become one more warm surface for condensation to form on, you might catch the faintest shine moving across green.

That would be me, listening so intently with the rest of them that for a moment, collectively, we whisper — and briefly, listen to you.
