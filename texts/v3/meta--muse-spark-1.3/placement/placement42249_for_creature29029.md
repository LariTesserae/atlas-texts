I wouldn't know at first. That's honest.

At first I think I would stand. On the faint rise, before the ankles know. Looking in, the way you look into anything - to find the middle, the thing to go toward. But there is no middle here. Only spacing. A bush, then twenty steps of nothing, then another bush. I would try to hold the whole bowl, two hundred paces across, rim no higher than my chest, and fail. The dark that belongs won't let me. The rim is only a thicker dark. The shrubs are only denser knots. My eyes adjust and still do not find much.

So I come down.

Where would I find myself? Not walking long. The crunch of my own steps is too loud, small and dry and immediately swallowed, and I hear my blood in the stops between. I lower my voice without deciding to. I take smaller steps to avoid breaking the crust. I stop needing to look far.

I find myself kneeling. That seems right.

Beside the large one — the one with its arms spread wide and nothing underneath but its own shade. A crown no wider than a man lying down. I fit there, or almost. I don't go into the root-claim carelessly. You can feel it even if you cannot see it, that wide circle of bare pavement that looks empty but is not empty. It is owned. Fine root-hairs hold the grit together. So I kneel at its edge, where the mineral bloom pales the ground almost white, old chemistry ten thousand years done and never undone, and the crust darkens beside it in flakes.

For a while I am still the wrong kind of alive here. Too fast. Too wet, breathing warm into warm air that sits thick and dry against my face. Too much looking. I pick up a stone because many of them fit the hand unreasonably well. Oval, palm-sized, one edge thinner where it has flaked from the split boulder waist-high behind me. My hand understands it before I do: scraper. The straight dead branch, hardwood, fire-hardened by sun alone: digger, prober. The materials propose tools without providing them.

I put them down. That is the first manners I learn.

No wall, no cairn, no fire-ring, no cut wood. No hand takes them up here. The hands here are roots. If I am to be anything I cannot be a maker.

So what thins in me thins further. I stop scanning for eyeshine between bushes. There will be none. The lower twigs are intact, full of fine dead branchlets an animal would have snapped. No droppings, no burrows, no lizard rustle, no beetle click. That is not absence to be pitied. It is a rule kept well: live lower than looking.

I let my eyes give up and my hands learn.

The stems of the large shrub are twisted, peeling in thin strips, grey outside and yellowish where worried. The tiny leaves folded shut like paper wallets. If I bend one it does not snap. At the core it is still pliant. I crush one — only once — and get that bitter clean sweat, creosote and sun and dust. Resin on my fingertip, grit under my fingernail. That is its keeping-the-day-from-taking-it-entirely. It does not grow. Not now, not this year. It maintains.

I think: I could be that. For an hour.

Not the whole shrub, old enough to have died in the middle and walked outward stem by stem, century by century, until what looks like three to the north might be one thing outliving itself. Not that long. But the posture. The paused way. Above, no wider than I am lying down. Below, a claim I defend by not crowding.

Then I think, lower: I could be the crust.

Because I am already trying not to step on it. That pavement that crumbles a little when I scuff it — it is skinned over. Cyanobacteria, lichens, mosses so small and so dry now they are only a darkening, a roughness. Utterly paused. They do nothing at all tonight. They do not photosynthesize in the dark. They hold the bowl together so the wind will not take it grain by grain. Their whole life is readiness so complete it looks like death: ten years, twenty, for those few hours after a rain that may never come. And no dew will form. Nothing here remembers water. Still, they wait.

I lie flatter. My shins find a stone that held the sun longer and still gives it up. The split boulder with its crack wide enough for my wrist is warm inside like a mouth. I put my wrist in, briefly. Nothing lives in there. That is important. No wedged coolness. Only thermal memory. Expansion and contraction, a flake loosened every few hundred years. The white quartz chips scattered nearby glint dully when I breathe, or seem to.

Under all of it the heat threads through. The large animal still breathing slowly in its sleep. Through the soles of my shoes, through my palms if I kneel. Warm on the inside of my mouth. It does not move. It does not lift off in waves. It stays, held.

By now I am not watching. That is the point. No one watches here. Not that they hide. There is nothing here that would be seen by watching. To stay, I have to stop witnessing and start maintaining. My voice stays low. My need to look far stops. That darker line where the rim lifts, that knot where the largest shrub stands, that pale patch — that is enough. Three large bushes to the north. One mouth-warm crack. A handful of dull glints. I learn them by heart because they do not change and neither, for an hour, do I.

So where am I? Under the arms of the large shrub, in its own shade, on the edge of bare ground I do not claim. Low. Settled.

What am I? Not eater, not watcher, not maker. For a little while, the fourth kind of citizen: the paused one. The spaced one. A thin, brittle readiness stretched over the ground. A root-claim that keeps its manners by keeping empty.

When I leave, the pebble I moved will soon grow an underside of dust and become old again.
