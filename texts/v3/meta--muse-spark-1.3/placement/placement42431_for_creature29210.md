High, first. I would find myself by how little air there is.

Before I am anyone, I am breathing. Shorter than I want. A catch on the inhale that never quite leaves. The light is white and too close. The wind takes my exhale before I have finished making it.

Then — low. I would find myself low.

At first I don't know if I am animal or person or stone warmed by sun. That is not confusion. That is correct. In the courtyard where I live, forty bodies press flank to flank and there is no edge between us, only warmer and less warm. Grazer barrel, goat spine, dog rib, human shoulder. Breath-plume, breath-plume, breath-plume rising together into the noon that is bright but not warm.

Let me stay here a moment without being anyone yet. Let me be only weight and warmth. A forehead pressed to a flank to feel the let-down. A hand lost in long underwool. The crunch of hooves on frozen grit under me. The wind combing over the flat sod roof above me.

Then, slowly — because everything here happens slowly — I become more myself.

I have two legs, not four. I am small. I am low to the ground the way our houses are low, built short and broad. My chest is already too big for my arms, they hang a little out from my sides. My cheeks are polished dark red-brown, cracked at the mouth, greased with fat. My eyes are narrowed against glare. I squint even in dreams.

I am a girl. Eight winters, maybe nine. Old enough to be trusted with dung and water, too young to milk without spilling.

In the morning I wake inside the pile and I do not get up so much as unpeel. The big grazer cow on my left gets up first — I know her by smell, sour milk and lanolin, by the particular tear in her ear. Her calf was across my feet all night, a furnace. My aunt is on my right, snoring low, and the long dog is curled in the crook of my knees where I held him, shivering until he stilled. Under the shared felts weighted with stones, our breathing has been one tide all night. For a moment I lie there and breathe air already breathed and warmed by others, because my first air of the day would hurt too much cold.

My work is to follow.

I take the flat basket, woven willow brought up from far below and mended three times, and I follow the herd out along the drover avenue. One deliberate foot, then another, breath timed to steps. I have learned not to run. Every child learns before speech that to run is to fall gasping to your knees with black closing in at the edges of sight. So I walk at a grazer's pace for an hour to cross what would be three streets elsewhere, and do not think it far.

I pick the fresh pats while they steam. I pat them into cakes with my broad-knuckled hands — no one here has soft hands — and set them in geometric hives against the south wall to dry for winter. It is solemn work. Everyone knows winter fuel is summer dung. A boy smaller than me carries moss for my boot-wraps. I show him which pika-hay is dry enough to stuff around the toes.

At midday the frost lets go. I hear the roofs begin to drip, one drop at a time, into the stone gutters. I go to the cistern with a wooden bowl. This is the thing I am learning to be good at: to carry a full bowl of meltwater across a courtyard in wind without spilling. To move without waste. To stack stone for an hour without stopping, stone-set-on-stone, breath even. The respected person is not the quick but the even. I practice. My mother does not call me with a yell — shouting wastes breath that others have to breathe — but with a two-note hum under the wind, and I hum back.

I wear what the herd wears. Calfskin hair-in, then felt layer on layer, grey-brown and stinking sweet of smoke and milk, then wind-shell patched until the patches have patches. Bone toggles I can work with numb fingers. Felt cap with earflaps tied under my chin, and in it a raven feather and a little goat-bell that rings when I turn my head too fast, to remind me not to.

In the afternoon I sit in the sun-trap behind the windbreak with the old ones and comb underwool with a bone comb. My comb is cut from the rib of the grazer whose milk fed me as an infant. I know whose bone I carry. I can recite my cow's mothers back five generations — White Forehead, who gave twins in a hard spring, Broken Horn who found water in a dry year — better than I can recite my own human grandmothers. The old woman next to me grinds barley one handful at a time between two stones, twist sinew cord, mends felt with a bone needle. She leans against me for warmth and I lean against the calf.

At dusk it goes amber then blue, very fast. The cold drops like a stone. From every smoke hole the dung-smoke rises and flattens a few hundred feet up into a single cloud that holds our heat a little while. We circle. The big grazers lie down first in a ring, legs folded, calves pulled into the center where breath collects warmest. The black goats wedge on top and between. The dogs crawl in last against human legs. And I crawl in among them, second from the edge — old enough now to take the colder spot so the babies can be middle. I pull the felt over us. I breathe through a fold of it to warm the air before it hits my lungs.

If you passed our doorway after dark you would hear us: a low communal tide, human and animal in and out together, the stones around us giving back the day's sun.

That is where you would find me. Not standing out. Just another shaggy slow-moving bulk among stone-colored walls and smoke-colored animals. A small upright, dexterous, fire-tending part of the herd.

We who keep each other warm.
