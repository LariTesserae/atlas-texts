At first I thought I would be up high.

The lammergeier. Three meters of stiff wing circling the rim without beating once, black against steel-blue, watching the whole bowl at once — the herd as a dark smear, the ponds as sky-colored coins, the glacier grinding white at the head. That kind of seeing is tempting. To hold it all.

But I can't stay up there. It's too clean. Too far from the wet. The air up there forgets to hold even him, really — he is just falling very slowly. I want weight. I want to be down where breath condenses.

Then I thought — in the herd. Shoulder to shoulder in three hundred head of yak, barrel-chested, pale saddle already coming in long for winter, muzzle fringed with my own ice. To tear sedge with a hundred mouths at once, to piston forward a meter through wet peat — thud-thud, thud-thud — to be furnace-hearted, big-lunged, to bawl and be answered. To bed down at dusk in a tight island with the calves in the middle and make one low cloud of breath together.

But I'm not that big. I don't demolish. I pick. I dart.

I thought about the ponds for a while, too. To be a bar-headed goose breaking window-ice with my breast in the afternoon and re-breaking it, blood that grabs oxygen where there is almost none, wings roaring like an engine when we all lift because the air is so thin we have to beat it desperate to climb. Or to be one of the cranes, apart in a pair on a peat island, slate and black with a red crown, probing for roots and snails that shouldn't live this high and do, dancing two cold-stiff bows even now and then going back to feeding.

But I am not a bird. I won't leave over Everest. I will stay when the dim dims further toward blue and the geese tuck heads and the herd's breath settles on the sedge as frost.

You'd find me off to the side of all that abundance. Not in the center marsh where life clots thickest, but just above it, on one of the drier moraine spines where the permafrost dips enough to let a hole hold.

Between boulders. House-sized, angular, dropped where they melted out twenty years ago, still sharp, lichen on their north sides still in perfect coin-circles, first generation, no overlap yet. The marmots sit on top of them like foremen, fat and tawny-grey, whistling that one sharp falling note that stops the whole ridge. I live underneath where they can't go.

I am a plateau pika. Dun-colored, round-eared, mouse-sized, absurdly busy.

My crack is between two stones that sweat black wetness straight from rock. Moss drinks it — olive cushions that break apart like bread. Dwarf willow no higher than my head, but thirty years old, gnarled as a fist, holds the edge. In front of my crack, my haypile: tiny, precise, sedge and willow twigs laid to cure in the cold sun that never quite clears the eastern rim, just slides steel-blue along the ridgetops for hours.

That is my factory floor. A meter square.

From there the whole valley comes to me small and close, because the wet ground swallows sound. I don't hear far. I hear near: water ticking under ice skins at the pond edges by mid-afternoon, sedge seeds ticking against stems when the horizontal half-snow rain comes, my own feet slapping stone, the faint electric crackle before snow builds over the western pass. Under it all, always, the low deep grinding from the glacier mill where meltwater drives stones around inside the ice, and the rhythmic hiss-release of the seeps, and, when the wind drops, the wet shredding of a hundred yak mouths tearing a few hundred meters below.

When a boulder lets go high up from last night's freeze and comes down in three clean clacks, impossibly loud, I freeze. When a marmot whistles, I vanish. Then reappear. *Eep* — dry as two stones clicked — from everywhere, from all of us, a net of sound holding the ridge together.

My work is cutting. Dart, freeze, dart, a mouthful of green three times my head size in my teeth, back to the pile, stuff it with both paws, dart again. Tussock to crack to pile. Tussock to crack to pile. My heart hammers three hundred times a minute just to stay lit in minus five, minus eight, damp cold that finds seams and turns sweat cold and sticks rock to bare skin. My gut is a tiny fermenting vat, like the yaks', only smaller and faster. I steam.

I orbit the big heat without joining it. The gazelles flick pale between yak legs using their stink as cover; I use their noise. The two grey wolves loiter at the marsh edge two hundred meters from the herd, lying with heads on paws, patient as shift workers, testing calves with yellow eyes. Sometimes one lifts its head and scents and the whole herd ripples without moving. I freeze then, too, ears swiveling. I am not what they want, but an eagle shadow, a wolf shadow, will clatter the coots up in black sheets off the water and send the wigeon whistling.

Stay late. The herd beds down tight on the highest peat. The geese tuck. The wolves lie down at respectful killing distance and wait for a dark that will only be dimmer. The lammergeier finally tips away. Frost needles up from puddles. The mist forms and unforms a meter off the ground, catching on cotton-grass heads and tearing.

I won't hibernate. The marmots above me are already slowing, lining their deep burrows with bleached sedge, heart rates dropping toward torpor. I will stay awake. All winter under snow, in a tunnel steaming with my own breath, living off this pile I am making now, stem by stem, while the glacier keeps its night shift and water keeps seeping from rock.

Nothing built this place. No wall, no cairn, no ash of a fire. And yet it runs.

My part in it is this small, hot, unceasing clipping. A precise stack of summer against a winter that has almost nothing in it.
