I thought I would be the one coming down.

Feet first down the ladder into the roar, ribs-deep before I understand the room. But I am not good at ladders. I am not good at noon. Up top is too loud in the wrong way — shouting, orders — and down here is too loud in a way that fills the little box completely. If I were the boy I would have to climb back up every day into the glare with my hearing ringing, and I don't want to go back up.

So I don't stay on the steps.

At first I think I will be the light. That shaft falling through the meter of bleached sky, through the adze-rough beams. It is the only thing with somewhere to go. But it hurts to look straight up, and the light doesn't stay. It shatters on the agitated surface into nets that crawl up the walls and across skin and then it is gone, evaporated into mist chest-high. I want something that stays.

Then I think I will be the water. But the water doesn't stay either. *In must equal out.* Four clay spouts throwing their thick clear arcs day and night, and the low square spillway on the north side gargling it all away into the dark culvert. The whole volume has a slow clockwise drag. If I am water I am only passing through, bath-hot on top where the sun touches, night-cool at the feet near the east spout. I want something that clings.

I find myself, little by little, looking for the lee.

Not where the four jets pock and crater. Not in the middle where the masons dished the brickwork down to the sump. I wade — in the imagining, I wade — and feel the push at my calves, and I let it push me to the edge, behind the entrance step where the current eddies weakest, under the foot of the low plastered bench where the trowel arcs are still proud.

There. Where it is quieter by a hand's breadth.

I am green. First.

Not leaf. Felt. A thin olive fur on fresh plaster that hasn't yet learned to be smooth. Below the waterline I wave minutely with the pull of the spillway. Above the slap-line, in the mortar joints, my other mouths — a sparse beard where the seiche kisses and falls back. And tangled with me, those blind white threads nosing down through a meter of stone from the courtyard weeds above, reaching for the damp I live in. They don't see me. I taste them when it rains.

I eat sun through the grate and I drink lime. That is the whole diet. Warm as blood, warm as bathwater, too hot and too loud and too new for fish or frogs or anything with eyes. No buzz, no plip. Only me, coming back.

Because I get scraped.

Once a day he comes, small enough to stand upright under the shallow dome without ducking, small enough to steady himself touching both walls at once when the floor drags. Lime-white soles, green tinge in the quick of his toenails that soap won't take out, the crosshatch of the grate burnt pink across the back of his neck. Basalt scraper in his teeth down the five sharp-nosed treads. Sandals left up top because they would tear me — tear us, the plaster.

He knows which spout runs coldest. He knows how to bail silt from the sump with the long-handled dipper without clouding the whole cup. He knows how to run the flat stone lightly, lightly —

He has learned not to clean me.

Behind the step. In the lee of the bench foot. His garden, he calls it only to himself because down here his voice comes back doubled and useless and he has stopped trying to talk. He leaves patches on purpose now and watches them. I have come back three times for him. I am proud in the way felt is proud: by thickening.

When the work is done he backs onto the bench until the water is at his throat and lets the mist hang over him and lets the secondary drops that condense under the beams rain fat on his upturned face. He opens his mouth to it. Sometimes he dips the dipper and drinks the limy warm to taste the stone, though the tall clay jar with its cloth stopper is the real drinking water. The moving nets of brightness crawl across his skin and across me at the same time. For a minute we are lit together.

That is where you would find me. If you put your face close, close enough to hear your own breathing leaned down to the surface under the roar, you would see me breathe with the pull of the spillway.

I do not think. There is no room left for thought down here, only ribs-deep warm roar and sun. I just hold. The plaster blisters if left alone, the grate mats with leaves and the level breathes up a hand's breadth, the sump clouds. He clears it with stone, clay, wood, rope, hands. And I return in a week.

Scraped off and come back. Scraped off and come back. While above us through the beams the ordinary day goes on, unaware this hidden pocket is working, loudly, right beneath its feet.
