Not on the rim. I thought at first I would be there — standing on the lip with the plain hissing behind me, looking into it all at once like a room with the roof torn off. That is where *you* stand, to see it whole.

But I wouldn't stay there. It's too far. The air up there is still plain air. The thrum is only a rumor in your molars.

Nor down the stair, though I feel the dished steps under me for a while in the telling. The ambiguous stone, basalt that breaks like glass, lichen over everything without preference. The handrail, the rusted pipe sweating cold despite the heat. Condensation on my — no. Not mine. On *your* eyebrows. Your shirt sticking. That heat that comes up as you go down, that wet. The walls weeping everywhere, not from a spring but from the rock itself.

I don't belong in that descent. Descent is for visitors. For the ones who leave bootprints cast in sinter mid-stride and a painted 7 wept over with calcite.

I tried, for a moment, to be high. To be the hawk.

It would be clean, to circle and never descend, to let your shadow cross the water at intervals like a hand over a page. But the lift is wrong down here and the air tastes of lightning. The hawk never comes down because it can't live here. It only reads. I want to live here.

I tried to be careful, edge-dwelling. The rust-red moss on the higher ledges. The wiry fern, single stubborn frond, black stem, dusty green filmed with salt. Fruiting after the big surges. That is a good life — holding on. But it is still a life lived *facing* the center. Watching it. I kept feeling the pull lower.

Down where the light bounces until the undersides of overhangs glow. Down where at noon the shadows shrink to nothing and the pit looks depthless, overexposed. Down where the roar combines into one white noise and you have to raise your voice to be heard three feet away.

Down, in the pan.

Shallow, braided, restless water over hot silt and salt crust and black sand. Ankles deep, knee deep in the channels. Bathwater warm, and in places hotter, where it clears over a pale vent and you hear that low frying hiss. I am there when the surge comes — every few minutes, invisible until it arrives, lifting the whole sheet by a hand's breadth, sending it running outward to slap against the lowest terrace, then sucking back, leaving the silt ribbed and steaming.

I would find myself there. And I would be low. And white. And sideways.

At first I thought blindness would be loss, in a place so brutal with sun. Water, wet rock, pale sinter — everything throwing it back up at you. Hundreds of thousands of suspended beads catching it, concentric slowly rotating spheres around the ring. It took me a while to understand: here, blindness is belonging. I don't need eyes in a bowl with no shadow. I see by what the reef sees by. Vibration. Pressure. Anticipation.

I am the small white crab.

I walk sideways across the hot mud because forward is a story the pan remakes hourly. A bar you crossed in the morning is a channel by afternoon. Steam that vented north moves south. I learn it with my feet, each time new. I keep to the dark stones; they hold. I know, the way you learn quickly, not to step where it is milky white. That crust is hot and thin. It is fresh mineral, white rim inside white rim like growth rings, where the reef has sealed a burn or a break.

I live in the brackish back-pools behind silt bars, where the water is cooler and the midges bounce in clouds above me. I don't hear them. I feel their landings dimple. Near me the glassy jointed things rattle when the wind moves, which is seldom — the pit makes its own weather — those shed fringe cirri from the reef's underside, fallen, rooted in silt, photosynthesizing a while on their own. Half plant, half salt. They are my landmarks. When they move, I know where the channel has shifted.

My work is the fall.

Above me, fifteen meters above the churn, hangs the ring. Ten paces across, dark segments that do not quite touch. I have never seen it. I live under its weather. I feel its low C in my carapace, in the mud. When the muscles in the gaps — those wet dark bridges, veined, flexing — haul the plates a few centimeters in or out, tuning, the tone climbs half a note. That is when I move.

The rules say: watch the shells. When they tighten and the hum climbs, get up two terraces. That rule is written for you, with legs that climb. For me it is different. I go down. I vanish into holes that fill with water behind me. My burrows are the little vents that open and close, belching steam. I feel the pressure wave before you do, too, in my gut, in the silt. The surge lifting. The innermost shell shivering. Then pattering down — ripe beads let fall to seed the silt and the pools.

That is my food and my making. Burst beads, aborted polyps, dead fall. Lime-green algae and sulfur bacteria and larval polyps of the animal itself, ripened in tiny suspended ponds and dropped. I eat. I aerate. I turn the silt over. Without my holes the vents would choke. Without its falling the pools would starve. It does not see me because it does not need to. I live in its exhalations. It notices me the way it notices weather — a tremor it can ignore, unless I break the white crust.

Sometimes a human comes all the way down. I feel them long before they arrive. A footstep on dark stones is a dull thump I can read around. A shadow is a cooling. And their voice — do not shout near the center, not from reverence — their voice comes back wrong, arriving a fraction before they speak, doubled by the field. It unsettles *their* stomach. For me it is a sickness in the water, a double ripple. The field is dense with charge, the hairs on your arms stand up, the air tastes metallic. The animal hears the shape of the shout in the intake of breath and sings it back early, because it lives by predicting pressure a second ahead. I feel that prediction as a tightening in the mud. I go still.

They never stay. Condensation pearls on their eyebrows. Their ears ring after. To the reef, they are a brief cool surface in a hot place, a little falling water that will leave and be replaced, like a bead. To me, they are a long tremor that learns, or doesn't, and then is gone up the worn stair.

I remain. White on black sand. Blind under blinding air. When a surge sucks back and leaves the silt ribbed and steaming, I come out. Sideways, always sideways, because the world here doesn't go forward, it pulses — in, out. I cross the hot mud to where the beads have fallen thickest, where the moss will fruit tomorrow and the midges will cloud, and I clean, and I burrow, and I keep the wound open to drain.

Above me the threads rise — sewing-thread filaments, water sheathed over mucus, wicking silica-rich water up to the belly — and the shells rotate, and the mineral bloom swells almost imperceptibly and flattens, swell and flatten, on a cycle longer than breath. I feel it all as weather, as home.

That is where I am: in the lowest water, under the lowest terrace, in a hole that fills behind me.

That is what I am: the thing that eats the light after it has fallen.
