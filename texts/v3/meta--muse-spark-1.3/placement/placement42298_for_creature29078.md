I come down out of the wind and feel it happen at my ears first.

That step. Forty feet, no more, and the air pools. Cooler. My breath goes faintly white though it isn't winter. My boots leave pasture and find the mattress — soft, then the knock of cobble underneath. Squelch, knock. Squelch, knock.

At first I think I will stay what I am. A walker. A visitor who listens for people because the doorways are shoulder-height and the bench is bench-sized and there is a coat still on its nail in the dye-works. I stand still, because the town makes you quiet, and I listen for footsteps that aren't mine and they don't come.

So not that. Not a walker. Walkers pass through and leave dark footprints for an hour.

I kneel, because you have to kneel down to it here. Closer.

Maybe clover, holding between the knuckles of cobbles where the lane crowns through. Maybe stitchwort whitening a lane edge in a week. That would be easy — to be fast the way plants are fast here, unrolling six inches after a mist-rain. But I am not fast. My pace is wrong for green. And I don't want to white out and then wait.

Maybe nettle, then. That would be knowing. Massed where the underground steam main leaks a degree or two in winter, thinning where the ground goes truly cold. To read heat with my body better than any thermometer. To farm warmth without touching it. Roots have learned the warm routes around bearings and avoid them — I like that manners. I try it. I imagine myself as a root, feeling for the blood-warm pipe as I pass.

Still too low. Still too spread.

The ones with eyes are out — I know that quickly. I cannot be the wren scolding once from inside a boiler flue and gone, or the black rat crossing with great deliberation and leaving a wake in the dew, or the grey moths bumping glass that isn't there around the day-burning lamps. Too fast, too eyed. They are weather here, not citizens. They make the emptiness audible for a second and then give it back.

Maybe then the warm ones. The large, slow animals under quilts. The twelve-foot flywheel furred in velvet moss on its sheltered side, turning once every nine seconds. The loom going *tss-tss* weaving a weft of nothing because no one told it to stop. The substation sighing and clanking as it regulates itself. I stand in the mill and feel the low woolly hum through brick in my teeth and I want to join it — to be kept warm or moving so lichen doesn't take me within the year, to stay black-oiled and rust-red instead of grey-green.

But I don't know how to turn that slowly and never stop. I would want to stop at evening. They don't.

Closer. Higher, but not high.

I find myself, without deciding, at the center. Where three lanes meet in a triangle of moss with a drain grating in the middle still swallowing mist-water with a faint gurgle. I can see the boundaries all around from here — the green mattress ends, the pipes stop, the last ivy half-eaten wall gives way to pasture. A room with green walls and an iron ceiling of wires. Cathedral-high in feeling though only two storeys tall, because so much of it is air.

There is a lamp-post here. Glass long gone. Pale orange dot burning in full daylight, useless and persistent, fed by the dynamo that is fed by the ventilators that are driven by the flywheel that turns for no one.

And in the cup where the glass was, a fern growing.

That's where I am. That's what I am becoming, and now it becomes more itself the longer I stay.

My feet are iron, seamed at the base with moss that holds me better than mortar — pull it off and by spring I would crumble. My head is a small garden. A thimble of soil blown in over years, damp that lives in the air itself beading on my rim and never quite dripping. In me the negotiation is complete: current below, green above. Warm wire, cool cup.

I do not crowd. Nothing crowds here. A single chimney answers a single shed across a hundred feet of fern-sea, and I answer them across my triangle, isolated like a stone. Door, bench, lamppost. Presences few and far between.

My work is passage, not place. The five-minute mist passes through and beads and doesn't fall, and I catch what I can in my cup. Condensate from a hundred feet of overhead pipe gathers and falls — *plik... plik... plik* — into moss that drinks it instantly. Below me the grating gurgles. If you are thirsty you don't find water. You wait, and you wring a sleeve. I wait. I have learned to stop scraping.

In the afternoon when the cloud thins for an hour my fern's sporangia pop almost inaudibly. Moths the color of skimmed-milk light bump where my glass was. My vine-heavy wire tightens, loosens, creaks long and slow. Once, if I'm lucky, the wren makes its one sharp note from the boiler flue and is gone, and my light goes on being slightly more itself as evening deepens a notch and condensation thickens on every underside.

Press a palm into the mattress at my feet in the evening and it exhales sweet iron-and-chlorophyll. Touch the living pipes as you pass; they are blood-warm in a cool world. Lift a cobble at my lane edge and you'll see my real mortar, white mycelium thick as rope, running seam to seam, forgetting your footprint slowly.

I burn by day. I hold green. I drink air.

The flywheel turns.
