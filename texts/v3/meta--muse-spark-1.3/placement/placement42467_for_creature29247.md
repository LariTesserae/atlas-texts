At first I thought I knew.

Coming down the worn steps with the light coming straight down on me, white and total, I thought: I will be a voice here. I came from the plain with a mouth full of questions. I would ask where someone lives, who leads, what their name is. I would be the trader, the talker, the one with grain and timber and news.

The basin took it. Three paces and died. The porous pan, the slumped aprons of dust, the wide flat — they swallowed the echo. A goat with a clay bell looked at me, clonked softly, and went back to chewing rope fray.

So then I thought: alright, hands. I will be a builder. There is hammering everywhere, chips flying, scaffolds to raise. I picked up a stone maul left leaning against a hammer-dressed wall and my arms knew at once I was lying. My hands were not white to the wrist. My feet flinched at the cold coming up through the soles. I build sharp. Everything I make here would be rounded within a lifetime by wind and time and trampling feet.

I put the maul down.

For a day I thought I would be a child. That seemed easiest. They run water jars from channel to house and back in endless relay, bare feet learning every slab, every seep. I tried to follow them and I was too loud, too slow, always in the channel when I shouldn't have been. A woman lifted her head to the west where the sky was bruising, raised her palm flat and held it a second, and the children turned without being told. I turned a beat later. The rain came violently out of clear sky. The shallow channels ran milky for half a day. I learned to cap a cistern with woven branches and stones because someone's hands put mine on the wood and showed me. Drink only from the covered pits. The open pans are bitter.

I stayed in the between after that.

Not in the five houses around a pit, not yet. In the stretches of bare pavement fifty, a hundred paces across where there is nothing but dust devils and shadow-lines and a few upright posts with rope strung between them. That is where a newcomer belongs in a city that is sparse not because it is empty but because it is in transit. I walked the causeways of fitted slabs that rang faintly under my feet. I learned to make noise with my hands, not my mouth. Palm raised for passage. Two taps of a loose stone on a wall.

That carries. That is the one sound the hollow respects. It travels along the masonry, through the soles. Everyone feels it.

Mornings, I started getting up before the glare.

That is where I found myself, really. On the east faces of stones at dawn, where the beetles black as varnish gather to drink dew off their own backs. They are clocks. When you see them massing, you set out your jars. I set out jars to catch the condensation beading on the cold undersides of slabs, tick by tick. I learned the dark line where the stone sweats in wall-shadow. I learned to follow goats — small and shaggy and dun — because a goat lingering at a wall base means dig there. I learned to look up when the white pigeons erupted in silent sheets from the leaning tower propped with timber, wheeling as a single decision-making body. When the flock lifts, everyone looks up.

I did not dress stone well. I did not carry water fast. But I could wait. I could watch the empty pavement the way the long-legged runners do, those things between lizard and bird stalking on stilt legs, picking at salt crust. At noon they freeze mid-step, legs casting no shadow. I learned to freeze too.

Who would I be, then? Not the builder, not the runner child, not the trader with too many words.

I would be the dawn-watcher. The jar-setter. The empty-place tender.

My house-group — five houses around a pit, a long wall with niches — let me stay because I shared the cistern and so shared everything. I sleep in the thick-walled room, low, round-cornered, dim and cool at noon when the light is unbearable outside and shadows disappear and the whole hollow glows. For an hour the turbulence pauses. The runners freeze. The pigeons sit. I lie on a hide pallet in that damp-stone-goat-smoke smell and listen to condensation tick into jars. Fish turn slowly in black water far below. Someone put them there generations ago and now they are in every pit, blind and pale and flickering, eating larvae and each other and keeping the drinking water living. If they rise, do not drink.

I think I am a little like them. Keeping something living by circling in the dark.

In the afternoon when a cloud shadow crosses the rim and the glare breaks, we pour back out, silent and fast. I shrug my heavy dun mantle up in shadow, down in sun — prickle to gooseflesh to prickle — headcloth wound tight, eyes set deep, squint permanent, lashes white with lime dust. My hands are getting white. My feet are getting thick, splayed, almost insensible. Low and rounded like everything else that lasts.

Ask me my name now and I will show you my hands. Ask me where I live and I will point to a pit that may be moved by next season, to a seep, to cold itself. We follow cold. The slabs sweat, salt blooms pink and ochre and sulfur-white and falls, dust drifts and uncovers a foundation, a wall melts, a new wall goes up on its back. What looks like ruins is material in waiting.

I tap twice when hands are needed. That is enough voice for me.
