At first I am still standing.

At the lip, where it opens to cloud. I can see the whole of it at once, the way you can see a room. Forty strides across. That is too much to be, all at once. And my breathing is wrong here — too fast, too loud. My footsteps fill in behind me with clear water and I am sorry for them.

So I know I cannot stay as I am, if I stay. I will have to get lower, and slower, and farther from myself.

For a while I think I would be the fern.

It is the most like a person in size. Person-high on its hummock, fronds arching out and dripping from every tip. It would be good to be lush unto oneself, to keep ten feet of bare wet stone around me so that distance is part of what I am. To unroll with geological slowness and need nothing to come to me.

But the fern is already itself, complete. There is no room on the hummock. And I am not that shapely, that lifted. I do not arch. I settle.

Then I think I would be one of the broad-leafed things in the crack, leaves as wide as shields, veined, cupped upward. To hold a small clear pool in myself after rain and keep it until it evaporates or spills. To shine, undisturbed. Nothing drinks from them.

But I would want to offer it. I would want to be drunk from. That is an animal thought. A lowland thought. I have to set it down.

I am not the maker, either. I walk past the invitations and leave them in their first state. The nodules of dense grey stone flaked cleanly, sharp as shell. The straight hard-wooded stems seasoned where they fell, unrotted. The fibrous bark in long strips. The rust-colored clay smooth and workable in the seep hollow. I look at my hands and I do not lift. No shelter, no hearth, no cut mark, no stacked stone. Let edge stay edge, haft stay fallen stem, cord stay bark, vessel stay clay. I will not be the hand that takes them up.

So where, then?

Lower.

Kneel, and the moss takes the weight without a sound. *Tink... tink... tink* from the overhang into the pan, long silences between. *Plip* when a fern tip releases onto moss. Somewhere under me, very faint, the sigh as a saturated cushion settles under its own weight. My print has already filled.

This is where I find myself. Not on the hummock. Not in the crack. Between.

In the low flat between the fern and the second pool — the knee-deep one with the sandy bottom and the bleached leaves laid down leaf on leaf like paper that will not rot — in that stretch of nothing but moss and liverwort and film that looked empty from the lip. It is not empty. It is a country.

If you kneel you see it: tight cushions, loose wefts, tiny stars, felt. Many greens at once. Dark where it is sodden and almost luminous where it mounds up over a buried boulder. And the line where it climbs a little way up the rounded cliff and then stops, as if by agreement. I am just below that agreement, where the water sits half an inch down.

What I am is not one thing, because the ground here is not one thing. But if I have to give it a narrower name — to let what emerges become more itself —

I am the fruiting.

Hair-thin stalks no thicker than a hair, rising here and there out of the weft, each tipped with a red-brown capsule no bigger than a seed. Thousands of us. We do not nod in wind — there is almost no wind — we stand straight in the humid air, fruiting into stillness.

That is my height now: an inch. My hurry is gone. A frond will yellow over weeks above me and I will not turn to watch it fast. A rimstone lip will grow a millimeter a year around the pool beside me and I will not measure it. Spores will drift onto the meniscus and stay, unclaimed, and I will be one of the ones who sent them, by wind and water alone. No go-between. No flower waiting for bees. No fruit swelling for a mouth. Cell dividing to cell.

My rules are simple now and unbroken:

Keep water close to the surface. Let stone sweat through me. Keep distance between green things — moss between like silence between words. Make no noise that lasts. Do not hurry. Need no other life but my own kind to continue.

Stand still long enough — lie still long enough — and condensation beads. Not on arms anymore. On capsule, on stalk, on leaf. A drop gathers, swells, falls to the moss and is taken without sound. Across the hollow the fern releases its gathered drop at almost the same moment.

For an instant we are doing the same thing. Holding water, then letting it go. In the warm, high, empty bowl above the clouds.
