At first I thought I would be up high.

Not on the rock, but above it. The glider — huge, black, tilting for an hour without a flap. It seemed like the natural place for something that watches. To see the whole tableland at once, the pink-grey shines and the green pits like spilled ink, the white ribbons throwing themselves off the rim in the afternoon and shredding before they hit bottom.

But I stayed with that image a little while and it went thin. Up there the wind never stops funnelling. You don't hear, you only watch. And watching here is useless. Everything important is camouflaged. From above, I would mistake my own kind for stone. I would be waiting all year for a blizzard of moths that lasts an afternoon. Too hungry, too distant. No.

Then I thought I would be human. One of the attenders.

I could see where — curled in that bothy of stacked slabs with the turf roof, smelling of old smoke and damp wool. Or crouched behind the low dry-stone windbreak, waist high, curved like an arm. Mending things with stone on stone, wood, woven fiber. Learning the habits of dampness. Drinking from a pothole cupped in my hands in the morning. That appealed to me, the care of it. Not herding, attending. Scraping guano after the hatch. Sweeping grit from a footprint for the tiny terraces below the dome.

But even as I settled into it, I could feel my voice coming back wrong. Flattened, then half a second later too loud. Humans here have to unlearn shouting. They have to press their jaw to stone to hear. They are always visiting, even when they stay for years. Their feet wear lichen off in paths, but they don't make shade where shade will hold water. They don't carry. I wanted to carry.

Closer. Lower.

I thought I might be small enough to live entirely inside the carried world. A thumbnail frog in a bromeliad cup no bigger than a thimble on someone else's back. My whole life one held spoon of rainwater. After rain, to sound a single glass-bell note and have it swallowed instantly by wind. That is a complete life. To be colonized, not colonizer. To be the proof the carrying worked.

I stayed with the frog a long time. The green light. The rot-sweet smell of humus and crushed leaf and warm stone wet underneath. The tin-stiff big leaves clattering above me like a roof in every gust. Drip, drip, drip off the leaves into the potholes, impossibly loud at night when the roar thins to a hiss.

But I am too heavy for that. Even in imagining, I felt my weight go down through stone, not up into air.

And that was when I felt the seam.

Where I would find myself is not on a dome. It is as a dome, miscounted.

Fifty meters of bare ringing shine from the hollow with the tree-ferns. The long pink-grey swell with the rust-orange lichen patch shaped like a lung. Only three paces to the left of where you left it yesterday. That is me.

I am low, wide, impossibly heavy. No higher than your waist when I am settled, twice as wide as the bothy is long. My back is silica laid down layer on layer, polished by wind and rain until it rings when you tap it. Lichen grows in it and on it and becomes it — black where the sun hits hardest on my crown, pale grey underneath where I keep myself damp. Along my lower edge, where I meet grit, there is that shadow line no wider than a thumbnail, going all the way around. If you put your hand there you will feel warmth give back a fraction slower, the way skin does. Inside, my four legs are folded and vanished. Footpads wide as dinner plates, tucked up. You would swear I have none.

I have shallow pans in my back big enough to lie down in, in miniature. Round potholes. In the morning I weep. Condensation runs in beads along grooves I have worn toward my blunt head-end, where my slit mouth drinks without opening. In the afternoon storm I lower what little head I have and let it drown me. Vertical rain, warm as blood, hammering so hard it bounces knee-high. Every pan becomes a mirror. You will think it is just stone steaming afterward. It is. I am. Both.

What I am becoming, slowly, is garden.

Moss first, in my cracks. I have been still for weeks to let spores settle. Then a fern spore. An orchid with a waxy white throat near my left shoulder where water lingers longest. A ginger shoot. A bromeliad cup — yes, with a frog in it, whose single note after rain I feel more than hear, a tremor in my carapace. Once a year white moths will hatch from my moss all at once in a blizzard and paper my potholes with their bodies. The glider will tilt above, watching for that. Let it watch.

I do not move in day. The space between things is too wide, the light too flat and white. I cultivate stillness the way the plants do. I hunker where I can and let the vast polished emptiness go.

At night I move. Only in cloud-gloom, only in the hour after rain when the rock is cool and the temporary streams are still hissing toward the cracks.

Lift and set down. Boom. Pause. Boom-boom. Through the hollow chambers underneath, through jawbone pressed to stone. The others lift their blind heads an imperceptible fraction to listen. I am checking where the damp is.

I settle beside a green pit for days, head tucked. I extend my long prehensile lip and crop — never clean — moss, fallen orchid throats, vine curtains. I drink the pothole half-empty and leave the rest for the deer-like thing that comes later. Mud cakes around my feet with spores, seeds, frog eggs. Then I walk fifty meters across nothing no seed could cross in wind, and settle again. Where I settle, a new damp shadow forms underneath. Condensation gathers longer into morning. In my footprints, dust and guano collect.

The humans have built a windbreak in my lee, so the gusts don't stagger me when I am young and lighter, less polished. They tied a scrap of cloth to the cairn upwind; it snaps violently where wind is clean, goes limp behind me so they won't trip over and wake me in white-out. They left a digging stick leaning against my flank, polished by hands. They will not dig while I am sleeping. They know.

Lie with your spine along my crown tonight. The wind has dropped a fraction. Condensation is ticking off leaves. Far below, lightning flickers silently in the cloud sea. Above, stars close enough to hurt. The stone under you still holds the day.

Very faintly, through bone, you will feel me listening, and the others moving out across the tableland — carrying our gardens carefully across the emptiness, breathing through our teeth while the mountain breathes with us.
