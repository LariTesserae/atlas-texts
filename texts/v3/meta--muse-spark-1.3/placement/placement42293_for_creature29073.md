I come down into it without noticing the descent, the way you do. One minute the wind, then no wind. Breath showing. Boots going dark at the toes.

At first I think I'll be one of them. A keeper. It's the obvious wish.

I try it on at the cross at noon — oil can in the side pocket, rag in the back, jacket already damp at the shoulders. I do the round in my head: mill to machine shop, along the trestles to check the beads, under the belt-drive housing like a covered bridge. I stop to listen to a bearing.

But I listen wrong. I hear only noise. The twelve-foot flywheel coming around once every twenty seconds, the low groan in my molars, the boiler sighing, the ventilator forever out of balance going *tak-tak-tak-tak*, and underneath it all the culverts ticking. I want to fix the fan. I want to still it. A keeper doesn't want that. A keeper hears weather where I hear fault. He can tell the groan that means normal from the groan that means a quarter-turn on a foundation bolt. He can tell my voice from a real dry bearing.

So no. I'm too fast. Hurrying here would be a category error. I cross the fifteen minutes in fifteen minutes, and a keeper takes an hour because he will stop to sweep water out of a doorway, to clear leaves from a grate before it chokes.

Maybe then something lower, more patient. A cat with seniority.

There are four or five of them asleep on the transformer boxes where it's warm, curled tight around what's missing — a leg, an ear tip, half a tail. The tax the old machines took years ago and haven't taken since. I could sleep through the *tak-tak-tak*, through the white roar of steam that sends the hundred pigeons up all at once like laundry snapped. The keepers would step around me. Someone would leave a warehouse doorway cracked for me in thin rain.

But I haven't paid the tax. I don't know where not to step when the pitman comes around. And I don't sleep that well.

A goat, then? Someone's, everyone's. Cropping the lots between the shops, bell dull green with verdigris, moving the soft rip-rip of grass between groans. Following the rounds at a distance for salt on a hand. That closeness without ownership appeals to me. Or a frog, fat and unbothered in a tire rut, requiring only that everyone learn the small sidestep after rain.

Or moss. Moss up to the ankle on the north side of every wall, thickest on the vibration side of the machine shop where the hum shakes the dew into the brick. Ferns unrolling from mortar joints, not toward light but toward warmth — the boiler wall, the culvert grate. Seedlings in an exact green dotted line under the pipe runs where condensation falls nightly. To grow around, never quite over. To respect property lines. I like that ethic. I could do that. Stand still for three years and split concrete with buddleia.

But I'm not still. I'm noisy. That's the thing I keep coming back to on my round. My throat is full.

Find me, then, not on the ground. Up.

On the sagging catenary between poles, or spaced along the warm pipe at exact intervals, biggest to smallest, as if placed. Or ribboning over the open lots where the grass stays wet till noon. I am one of the starlings.

That becomes more itself the longer I stay.

Because the air here is crowded and can't leave the bowl — it bounces off the sharp black spoil teeth and stays in — and someone has to carry it. The mill groans, the fan rattles, the steam roars at no time you can predict, the dripping resumes. The pigeons line up by size. The wrens stitch the bramble. The goats pull. And over it, us: learning to do the machines perfectly.

The squeal of a dry bearing from three lots over. The hiss. The clank like a wrench dropped on concrete. We do it so well we fool them two or three times a week. A keeper looks up from wire-brushing rust under a flange, oil can raised to mean *come look* — then smiles, just barely, and lowers it. Fooled by a six-ounce throat full of iron. We're proud of that. It's our maintenance. They keep the engines idling so the warmth keeps falling in lines; we keep the sounds alive after they've passed, carry them around the bowl again.

I nest in it the way the moss nests in it. On warm vents. In gutters full of ferns. Moths around the sodium-orange lamps that buzz even at noon in the cool brightness — we dart through them. When steam is let off we go up all together with the pigeons, a hundred wings clapping, then settle again to drip and tick and *tak-tak-tak*. I know where it's warmest to put your feet. So do the three-legged cats. So do the two keepers with their backs to the boiler house wall, coats steaming faintly, drinking tea, saying nothing, turned to face heat the way the ferns are.

I learn the negotiation. I don't build in the elbow of the steam exhaust — that's the robin's impossible, suicidal, perfect cup of moss and grass, with her little tin shield angled so the white roar goes over her. The keeper on boiler round checks her shield before the gauge, talks low under the fan rattle. I give her space. I do my bearing-imitation a lot over, not too close. Property lines respected, even across species.

Stand at the cross at noon and you'll hear me. Cool light flat on your face, smell of coal-smoke and chlorophyll and hot grease, wet glint on everything. Mill groan to your left, fan rattle to your right. And between groans, if you listen like a keeper and not like a visitor, a perfect dry bearing that isn't a bearing at all.

That's me, keeping the sound warm.

I don't need to go further. There's nowhere further to go.
