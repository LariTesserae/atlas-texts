You come to the rim — I came to the rim — and at first I thought I would stay there.

Dry boots. A visitor's distance. The whole stepped bowl in one look, the far wall hazed a mile and a half off, the bottom lost in its own steam. I thought I would be the kind who takes it in at once and leaves. Who keeps the real world behind him, bright and dry.

But there is only down. The road spirals wide enough for trucks that no longer run, and by the second switchback my boots are wet and stay wet. So much for staying clean.

Then I thought, lower down, I would be one of them. A holder. It is a holy word here: keep the machines running or drown.

I tried it on. The foreman's shout torn off short — CHECK FLANGE FOUR — mouth to ear at three feet. The electrician's burned cuffs, big arm signals borrowed from crane ops because shouting fails under the spillways. Fist raised: KILL IT. Palm patting air: EASE OFF. Both arms crossed over head: GET UP, WATER'S MOVING. I tried the walker's slow high step, probing with a stick to see if this silt will hold, if this grating will hold, if this hummock is a hummock or a carp. I hung the orange plugs on a string around my neck and didn't put them in, because then you can't hear the pump change pitch, and the pump changing pitch is the difference between wading and drowning.

For a while I was good at it. I learned to press a palm to a pipe as thick as a torso and nod. To feel the Pit through my boots the way you feel a sick animal breathing. To rake the intake screen every hour — reeds and plastic and dead gulls — while the tea-colored water climbed millimeter by millimeter up the painted stick I'd staked myself.

But my throat went raw. My eyes went red from diesel shimmer and no true daylight. Noon here looks like an hour after sunset elsewhere, work lights always on making halos not pools, sodium-yellow and work-white through a veil. My trousers I rolled and unrolled and finally left soaked. My feet went white and ridged as river stones. Laundry strung and never drying. Television turned up to a scream to beat the marsh through thin cabin walls. I sweated and stayed sweated.

And I started pausing too long.

That is the heron thing. Like herons they pause a lot, head cocked, listening, the description said. I would stop mid-ford with the gaff in my hand and just stand. Not laziness. Read. Because under the roar that fills your skull from the molars up — spillways and diesels and high-tension hum and frog chorus and tens of thousands of wings bounced and re-bounced off raw walls — there is a bass under bass. At the slightly darker dim that passes for dusk, the bitterns boom low underneath the engine drone.

No one can hear here. That shapes everything. I stopped trying to shout.

That is when I found where I actually live. Not the pump house on every second bench, grumbling steel box with its heat shimmer and slick of oil and single bare bulb. Lower. Below the last catwalk of rusted grating, where the final floor was supposed to be dry and isn't. Where the drains gave up and the shallow warm lakes spread bench to bench, knee to waist deep, water warm as blood, tea-colored, moving so slowly you have to stake a stick to see it move at all.

If you wade — everyone wades — the floor erupts outward from your boots. Frogs by the ten thousand, green and brown and gold, plopping off every hummock, off sandbags, off the treads of sunken trucks. Tadpoles blacken the shallows in writhing carpets so thick your bootprint fills in seconds with a swirl of black commas. Dragonflies big as sparrows hawk blue over the wire-thin reeds. In the deeper overtoppings, carp and catfish roll slow, fat on midges, backs breaking the surface like oiled stones.

I am there. Up to the thighs and not minding. Whitewash under my feet where the ledges have gone white with droppings, nests jammed against nests. Egrets shoulder to shoulder with night herons, gulls screaming over crakes, chicks gaping mouths red as warning lights. When a pump kicks to a higher gear the whole wall lifts at once, a sheet of wings that whites out the work lights for a second, and the scream rises a notch until even diesel is drowned — and I lift with it without thinking, because I am in it.

Let me be plain as the water gets plain at the bottom: I would not be a man holding a pump. I would be a wader in truth.

A black-crowned night heron, stocky and grey in the perpetual dusk that suits me. I nest where the machines have become animals — on the idle dragline left like a drowned dinosaur mid-stride on the bottom floor, now a heronry and a depth gauge. My mud is stuck to its pump volute along with the swallows' mud. Below me the keepers in their tea-and-rust high-vis lean into each other's ears to shout SUMP'S UP SIX INCHES and I hear them as weather. Warm rain begins — or maybe it is only condensation falling from the pipe bridges overhead, or a hot jet at a leaking flange making its own little rainbow in the work lights where midges dance. No one looks up. I don't look up.

I hunt the lee of the transformer yard where it's warm. I stand on a concrete plinth meant to stay dry, in the ammonia-sharp cut of guano that is the only sharp smell in the sweet rot. I stand still longer than any work allows. Gnats and mosquitoes hang in pillars so dense the men breathe them and slap and give up under headnets stuck to sweat — and through them cut my cousins by day, the bats by night, fat as fists. I wait. A frog pulses its throat on a sandbag that sank by morning. I take it whole.

From down here, looking up, the rim is a thin, bright, impossible line. Up there is dry. Down here the wet steps rise all around, dripping, steaming, crowded wing to wing, lit like an aquarium at night. The walls weep at every sand-clay contact and I drink the weeping. The iron-red slime bleeds from lime-weeping cracks and I stand in it. The algal mats, dark green-black, slick the pans and I walk them without slipping.

The marsh takes another yard while I watch, too slowly to see, too surely to stop. Good. Let it. Let the pump wear, let the bench settle six inches in a year, let the engines slowly become reefs for swallows and frogs. I am increasing while the men thin out. I have learned the machines the way they never quite learned the marsh.

I breathe in the Pit's own warm breath and breathe it out again.
