At first I think I'd be the turtle.

High on the rim where it's still almost cold, almost open. Where you can look down into the bowl and see the whole held broth of it — the roofs welded to roofs, the white kelp streaming, the amber lamps breathing together — and not have to go in. To hold against the light. To be a silhouette that checks and then decides, wisely, not to descend.

But the turtle has somewhere else to be. It has cold muscle and distance. It can leave. I don't think I can leave like that.

So I drop.

Warm before it is wet, and then warmer. The squeeze at twenty meters, at thirty, never comes. I pull myself down hand over hand along a railing gone soft with sponge, through slots a shoulder-width wide, brushing living tissue and warm metal at once. My bubbles go up too loud. I learn, fast, to breathe shallow. To leak small.

For a while I think I'd be the diver, then. The cold brief animal in warm tissue. The splinter the skin notices with pressure, not anger. I could press myself into that slot between the row house and the water tower, wrap myself in twenty feet of white kelp moving like hair in bathwater, hold still until the silt I stirred milks and stays milks, until the anemones near my fins decide to open again. I could watch the flywheel move a finger's width and call that an hour. I could feel the press come down through my sternum.

*Thud.*

But my fingers prune despite the bath. That's the tell. I live too fast to be housed here. An hour and I'd have to go up to find colder, emptier water. I can't bank like they bank. I can't slow until my breath is a minute long, until my eyes are anemones, until my hand becomes a valve-wheel.

Maybe, then, the eel? Thick as a wrist, living permanently inside the flywheel housing, emerging only in the bright minute to taste the water — for salinity, for oil, for intruder-sweat, for me — and then withdrawing to tell the housing to shift a degree. A nerve. That feels close to what I do. Tasting. Withdrawing. Telling.

But the eel belongs to the body. I don't. I don't know what good salinity is. I wouldn't know which coin of oil to carry.

Maybe the guppy. The blind-white flicker between the rain-barrels, mouth working. Not really a fish, the place says. A stray thought. A larval keeper, ferrying that sighed coin of oil that does not rise but hangs, from machine to mouth to mat. Small and lonely and too quick to count, but allowed. Mouth always working the water.

Closer. Mouth always working. But still a body that swims. I don't swim. I hang.

No. Lower.

If I stay longer, if I get cold in warm water which is its own warning, if I stop kicking — I find where I actually would be. Not on the rim. Not in the slot. Low, in the hottest layer just above the vent grates, where breath misbehaves.

You duck under a low doorway into the canteen — MERCY IRON & LIGHT, CANTEEN, the letters softened to braille — and your head breaks the ceiling. Into air.

I would be that air.

A pocket of it, stale and warm, held against the concrete by nothing but pressure and habit. Thirty years old. Smelling, even now, of oil and soup. It should have dissolved. It hasn't. Bubbles come up and shatter and join it, a little at a time, neither rising nor sinking, and it keeps them.

That's where you'd find me: tucked in the dark under the ceiling, in the green-gray exhausted light, above a face with its mouth open to sip. Lips white and frilled as a tubeworm plume. Below me the tables bolted down, the365651, the kelp coming in through the broken portholes, the cage lamp in the corner buzzing faintly on its sixty-second cycle.

Dimmer. Brighter. Dimmer.

I am what the diver leaves behind when he has to leave to find colder water. The exhaled part that doesn't leave. The kept breath.

Let me become more itself: I am not empty. I am crowded. I remember the shift whistle even though the whistle rusted shut decades ago. I remember soup steam, and men's voices trying to be loud through regulators that weren't there yet, and the particular *tink* of a spoon against a tin bowl. The banked ones sip me in slivers during the bright minute — not to breathe, they breathe water now through beards of mussel and algae — but to remember what work was *for*. I thin each time. I am sipped and sipped and never quite gone, because new divers duck in, break the ceiling with their heads, leak a little of their loud bubbles, and replenish me without meaning to.

Iron and iodine and hot stone. Oil on the tongue. That is my whole body.

Outside, below me, the shared body goes on. The copper veins with green filaments growing inside the cracked gauges. The algal liver on the boiler backs. The crimson-furred flywheel heart turning a degree per minute. The gut-press lifting, hesitating for a long minute, coming down on nothing — on a plate for a ship that will never sail — because to stop would be to admit the work is over.

*Thud.* Softly. Through bone.

The lamps brighten all together. The anemones open all together. The tubeworm lungs flare red, the only vivid color. The newest ones under the eaves, still pink at the edges where sponge hasn't quite taken, still bent at the lathe, arms up at the junction box, shift a finger's width.

And I hold. Against the ceiling. Warm. Stale. Patient. Waiting to be sipped again, to tell again what soup was, what air was, before we all learned to live, banked, in broth.
