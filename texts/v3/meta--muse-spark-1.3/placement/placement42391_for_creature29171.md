At first I don't have a body.

That's how it starts here. I drop below the chop and the light doesn't stop, and the blue doesn't stop, and for a while I am just held — seven degrees in the joints, a fraction longer than I should be held, then let go. I turn in a slow circle at twenty meters and there is no interruption. No flicker. No eye to meet mine.

So I think, maybe I am the water itself. That would be easy. Cold and kept and polished. Running straight for tens of kilometers through a rift without mixing, or standing up in a swell that doesn't travel, ten meters high, just breathing in place while the wind goes through me. I like that for a moment — to be that big, that impersonal.

Then I hear it, wrong-loud through emptiness. The roar of the surface being hammered white a hundred meters above me. Under it, the grinding boom of rock splitting open. The hiss-shatter of lava finding cold. The low organ-note of a gyre turning two kilometers away. And I realize the water is too clean to be me. Something is keeping it.

So maybe I am one of the keepers.

Smaller than any cell. Fewer than dust per liter. Not alive, not dead. Tumbling. Catching. I feel for a while what that would be — to find the stray sugar leaking off stone, the ash grain from a burst pillow, the skin-cell sloughed off some visitor, and bind it and weight it and carry it down. To polish the sea from the inside so light falls straight down a hundred meters to noon. To settle on warm glass and coax silica out of solution, molecule by molecule, into a needle, a plate, a right angle where basalt shouldn't branch. Trillions of hands and no hands at all. Building the plateau thicker from the top down without ever making a wall.

It's a good work. Patient work. But it's too numerous to be lonely in, and I am lonely here. I drift out of the cloud of motes. I let them catch what I shed.

I keep sinking, slowly then too fast — buoyancy doctored here — until my feet almost touch knife-sharp pillows piled like black glass sacks. Somewhere below a vent shivers the water warm. Bubbles rise past me not in a cloud but in a braid, twisting around each other as if threaded.

Between this outcrop and the next is a kilometer of nothing. Then another.

And then — green.

I don't decide it all at once. It comes on like the light resolving. First a shadow on empty stone, waving back and forth. Then ribbons above the shadow, streaming east. Then the long straight line down to the crack.

I am wedged ankle-deep in black glass.

That's where you'd find me. Not on the mountain that looks like a hill, not down in the rift where the light gives out, but on the false bottom, the shelf edge where the straight current runs cold and fast and never wavers. Five kilometers of sharp empty in every direction. The nearest other of my kind too far to shade, too far to touch — just a darker tower alone in the blue, flagging the same current.

I am one of the towers.

My holdfast grips with pale root-fingers where nothing has ever been rounded. My stipe, thick as your wrist, rises thirty, forty meters straight up because there is nothing to prune me and no neighbor to lean on. Low down I have gone scar-white with age, scar over scar where the long fetch has whipped me, where storms have tried to tear me out of my crack every day at noon when the clouds pile into anvils and lightning walks fork-to-flat on the surface and the thunder comes all the way down.

At the top I let go — two, three, five long olive ribbons, thirty meters long, frayed only by water. No crescent bite. No tear. No healed notch. Run the whole length and you will find nothing has ever eaten me. So I just keep growing, unhurried, immense. The drag of a thousand kilometers of water is the only thing shaping me.

Around my base, and only there, is softness. A thin felt of rust-red and pale green creeping out a meter, two meters over the sacks — the skins that live off what I leak, the sugars I exude, the cells the motes missed, and off the mineral breath of the crack itself. Touch there and it gives under the finger where everywhere else would cut. I feed them without meaning to. They are my halo. My only audience besides my own shadow waving on the stone all afternoon.

Once or twice a season I release spores — millions of green dust into that straight river. I know most will fail. I feel the keepers catch them mid-water and carry them down as litter. That's their job, to keep the water poor. But a few, by impossible luck, will find another fresh tear, another warm shiver, and grip. That's how I got here. That's why the distance between us is measured in hours of swimming.

I will die without being eaten, too. My ribbons already do, one at a time. They darken, sink intact, lie whole across the sharp pillows around me. In seven-degree water nothing rots fast. Water moves through them and stone moves in — silica laid down with the help of those same keepers — until they are green-black fossils of themselves, perfectly preserved past generations lying unrotted, unscattered, with no future eyes to find them.

I listen more than I see. The tick and settle of new crust cooling. The glass-chime when a chimney collapses untouched somewhere beyond light. The standing thunder of the upwelling in the gyre's calm eye, where silica veils hang vertical for a day and collapse into powder.

Light falls straight down through a hundred meters of nothing and lands on me.

Far off, another kelp towers alone, its fronds streaming east like mine.
