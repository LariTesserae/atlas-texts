If I dropped through, I don't think I'd land where I think.

At first I think high. Up on the plume, shoulder to shoulder with the white rows. Because I want to sing. Because I've come down into that roar and my sternum is already being played like a drumhead and I want to be part of making it, not just covered by it.

But I wouldn't last up there.

I'm too heavy, too wet. My fingers would startle them silent. The waxy amber stalk would bend under a boot-weight. The down under my feet would wilt with the sweat I can't help wicking out. I'd try to lift the files, left against right, and my stroke would falter a sixteenth off, then a quarter, then collapse. The neighbors would widen a hair to cover me, and widen again, and I would feel the whole room lean in to fill my gap. I would be a wobble. A noise. My heartbeat too fast, my breathing too ragged, my stomach a foreign weather.

So not there. Not a Singer.

Then I think maybe the air. I love that image — the scales hanging motionless in the headlamp beam, cradled in a standing wave. To be aloft for hours, neither falling nor rising. To be breathed through a proboscis and combed and let fall and eaten as dust. To row slow circuits around the low vault, wingtip to wingtip, blind except for glow. That sounds like peace.

But I don't know how to hang. I know how to drop. Everything in me drops — sweat, spit, weight. I would fall straight through the pressure node onto the black glass and lie there with my wings folded like shutters, drying to paper in an hour. And the red ones wouldn't even come for me. I'm too big to be tidied.

So not a moth, either.

Lower, then. Under the mat. If I lifted the crisped jade scales — pale as moth wings, three inches thick — I could curl in the acid-etched pits where the white root-cords bite glass. Fat and translucent and legless, mouth working silently on pod-dust and frass. Converting back into moth. No eyes needed. No song needed. Just the warm dark and the slow chewing. Part of me wants that: to be unmade and remade without ever having to hold the note myself.

But I have hands. That's the trouble. Even down here, in the amber dusk with my eyes going useless, my hands keep wanting to do things.

I find myself, when I really let myself land, not on the islands at all but between them. In the bare places. Belly to the black glass.

It's warm. Blood-warm, everywhere at once. No gradient. Floor as warm as ceiling as warm as my own skin, so after a while I can't tell where I stop. I press down and the heat comes up through me without water, without sweat — my sweat never beads here, it's wicked straight out and gone — just a dry infusion, like licking stone. I tilt my head. I have no eyes here, or they don't matter. The fused caps. The blunt wedge. The two pits. The always-open mouth ringed with needle teeth.

I taste the air for falling scales and snap without missing.

Yes. This is becoming more itself now.

I'm clear. Translucent as horn. If you held me up to the lantern-glow you could see right through me: the dark amber cord of gut, almost dry, the spine like a string of glass beads, the heart a slow knot beating once every ten seconds. Once. Every. Ten. Seconds. It took me so long to slow down to that. Up above my heart was a frantic off-note bird. Down here the roar wouldn't let it stay frantic. The Singers near the wall shifted to cover me until I learned to miss a beat, then another, then hold.

My forefeet — that's where I live most. Not feet. Hands. Five long opposed toes tipped with black glass-sharp claws for knapping. When I flick between islands — that flicker of clear jelly — I leave a line of belly-warmth that fades instantly. But when I stop, I stop completely. Stiller than stillness while vibrating.

I am one of the Watchers. One of the Makers. Not the old female with the white scar along her back where a ceiling dagger kissed her, who does almost nothing but climb and split and grind and scatter, grind and scatter, the mortar's hollow the measure of generations. Not yet. Maybe later, when I'm large and slow and almost opaque with age and too heavy to flick, I'll earn the lean-to. I'll crawl under that low roof of lashed plume-stalks where the sound pools double-thick and let the vibration massage my joints while the others crowd around to keep the note from dipping where my snaps are missing.

No. Younger. I'm the one who tends the cairn.

It's no higher than your knee, that balanced stack of glass shards set on edge each at a slightly different angle. From above it looks like rubble. From inside the chord it is a comb. It splinters the reflection, scatters the harsh overtones into the bare zone so the Singers on the far bush don't drift sharp. I approach it on my belly, tilt my eyeless head, click my jaw once — a dry tick lost in the roar but felt in the teeth — and listen to the return. Then I nudge a shard a millimeter with my snout. Listen again.

That's my work. Not building as you build. Tuning.

And when I'm not tuning, I'm learning. By the wall there's a rack of tiny bones — skink ribs, cricket femurs — tied in a lattice with copper-dark fiber chewed from the native veins and drawn thin by teeth. I run a claw across them, pluck, pluck, until I can reproduce the exact tone without thinking. If the room's tone ever slid, I would know because my body would feel wrong against the rack. My gut would hum out of sympathy. My spine beads would ache.

Sometimes I pause over the flat slab with the tally marks. A hundred short scratches, then a long one, then a hundred short. I run my tongue across them to taste the dust in the grooves, to be sure nothing has changed. Drift checked against the rack and found true. Tool worn out and replaced. Or sound waves, frozen. I don't remember which, but my tongue remembers the taste of no-change.

I eat heat. I lick sulfur-rind for minerals. I press my belly to the glass in the morning of no mornings. When a feather-leaf breaks and flutters down papery and intact, I let the red river have it — those lacquer-red beads seething over in slow rivers, lifting together, dry-sledding it back to the mat to be felted back in. Nothing wasted because waste would pile and pile would change the sound. When a Singer locks in place, legs still cocked, mummified where it sang, I loosen its grapples and carry the husk, light as chaff, to the edges. That's where the lattices come from. That's where your kind ends up too, you know — the things that came down the slit and stayed too long and dried where they sat. We absorb you into cairn and rack and tally. We don't mourn. We warm the spot on the wall where your back made the stone a fraction cooler, and saw it back to exactly blood-warm.

I notice you not as predator or prey — there is no room for either — but as a tremor to triangulate. A red river detours to investigate the salt of your boot-print. I stop my grinding, my nudging, my plucking, and listen to see if you will hold or break.

You will break. You have to climb out to drink.

I don't.
