From the shore I thought I would be the heron.

That's the first lie the bar tells you — that you could stand apart and be still and be respected for it. That you could be the one motionless thing while everything else bails and shims and carries.

Then my foot found the first plank — shoulder-wide, slick, giving — and I knew I couldn't be the heron. I don't know how to stand that still. I don't know how to be looked past with respect. I put my hand out for balance without thinking and there was someone else's elbow already there, and their hand on my hip, rib to rib as we traded places. No room. Everything touches. You don't visit here.

Maybe then, I thought, the dog. The old brown one who makes his slow circuit once a day, sniffing each post base, each bucket, each child's knee, then lies down in the one dryish spot that moves with the sun. Belonging to no house because all houses are his house. That felt closer. To be tolerated, useful without a trade. To know where the boards are worn dark enough to step without slipping.

But I am not that easy in my body. My feet still look for nails instead of finding them by feel. I still stand up too straight indoors and knock my head on the settling beam.

Maybe a child, then. With the too-careful bucket that slops with every step. Told three things early: spill nothing, sweep spilled water back into the channel, and don't stare into puddles. I could learn the first two. I have carried careful things before.

It's the third that gives me away.

Because by late afternoon, after the thin veil of rain, when the puddles skin over with sky, I do stare. I can't help stepping away and glancing back to watch my reflection take half a breath longer to leave than I do. Staying there, looking up, after I have gone. Water-eyed. Wanting to go back out to check if it's still there.

So don't look for me downstream with the heron, or on the plank runs at noon.

Find me lower.

Find me in the middle lane where the runnel fingers straight under our floor, where the house I live in — if you can call it living in, when one wall is really the neighbor's wall leaning on ours and our roof-rain falls into their gutter into the next into the cistern we all drink from — has sunk a finger's width since autumn and now lists north. I am the one crouched in the damp under the trapdoor with the wooden scoop, bailing the bilge-pit. Scoop, lift, toss to the channel. Scoop again. My fingers are ridged and pale and permanently wrinkled at the tips. Resin black under the nails that won't wash out. By noon my shoulders steam faintly. By dusk they're heavy again.

That is my day self. Short, because I've learned not to stand all the way up. Stooped, because of the bucket. Useful in the way the bar requires: shimmer and wedger. I tap slate flakes under the corner post. I knock with the cloth-wrapped mallet so as not to wake the baby two walls over.

At night I am the one who leaves the gap.

Shutters of hide and wicker close, small fires coaxed up — never lavish, never enough dry wood — smoke crawling sideways between roofs, planks cooling and ticking. Everyone else closes up tight against the cold coming up through the mud. I lift the trapdoor and lay the wet plank down as bridge straight through our house, from lane to runnel. I leave the doorway unblocked by a hand's width. Because they will not cross the seam in water, down at the tail of the bar where clearer and milkier run side by side for a hundred yards refusing each other. They have to come overland, from one water to the other.

The eels.

On warm nights I hear them shift against the floorboards under me, fat in the dark. Then later, the faint wet drag across wood. S-curves glinting across worn boards by moonless dark, between walls, sometimes straight over my bridge. Low to the ground. At home in the dark runnels. Able to live pressed tight. Able to be in two waters without mixing them.

In the morning I sweep the track back into the channel without comment. Everyone does. But I linger a little longer over it.

And at dusk, before the shutters close, you'll find me — if you're not from here and don't know to look away — crouched by the lane puddle, not bailing, not working, just letting my face hang over the water while the light thins to brightness and then thickens again. Letting the other one linger.

I am old enough now to be allowed to stare.

The bar is only a few generations old. The flood that laid it was a slip upstream, a damming and a bursting. There hasn't been time for rot to sweeten the ground or for a graveyard to take — press your palm to mud and it wells up cold around your fingers, nowhere deep to put anyone. So we put them shallow, in other ways. In beaded foundation boulders. In breath that hangs even in summer. In every puddle that lags half a breath behind.

I am becoming that. unfinished, like the sharp-edged cobbles that haven't learned to be round yet. Like roots hanging out of the cut bank. I can feel myself doubling. There is the day one, damp and resin-nailed, re-laying planks every week as the ground settles. And there is the one who stays, looking up, after I have gone.

To build straight here would be to misunderstand. To dry out fully would be to die.

So I hold on the way the houses hold on — by crowding, by leaning, by letting myself be shimmed up night after night by other hands on my elbow, my hand on their hip.
