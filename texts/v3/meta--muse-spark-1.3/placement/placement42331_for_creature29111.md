At first I thought I would be the one coming down.

Slipping sideways through the doorway fissure, nine meters of persuaded steps, lamp thumbed off because the dark here has intent. My lungs still aching from the thin moor air at 3,800 meters, my jacket still cold. Then the greenhouse mouth breathing on me. Twenty-six degrees. Wet condensing on my eyelashes. Drip letting go sixty meters above and me counting — one, two — *crack* — and the sound coming back from three directions.

I thought I would stay that person. The listener. The guest learning not to bring light, not to make sharp noise, clucking my tongue to get the room back: wall left close, wall right far, open water ahead, block above.

But I am too loud to be that for long. My breathing ragged. My heart embarrassing. I move and I make a storm and the ringers go silent in a circle around me. Even lying still on the deepest moss between the roots of a giant white ear, I am a hole of quiet that the chorus has to close behind me.

So I sink a little.

If I lie long enough, my boots become territory. I feel it before I understand it — a throat pulsing against my ankle. High, glassy, tuning-fork note rattling my sternum. I thought, then, maybe I would be a Ringer.

I would be small and eyeless, eyes like smudged thumbprints that never opened, holding six inches of warm mineral water and drooping leaf as my whole kingdom. One of hundreds, braided so close our calls make a sheet. Finding each other by heat and tremor. Singing on top of boots. That felt close. That felt like belonging — to sing and to stop singing when heat passes, and then to resume.

But I am not that quick, even in stillness. My pulse won't braid.

Higher up, in the wrist-thick vine curtains that run up the weeping wall and hang back down and root again where they touch, something saws — dry counter-rhythm to the wet ring. I push a curtain aside with my hand and they all stop at once and listen to my heat with antennae longer than their bodies. I thought, maybe I would be a Sawyer. A blind white cricket the length of a thumb, chewing fallen hair-leaf and fungus rind and each other's shed skins. Living in a highway that is also a house.

But I am too big for the curtain. I tear it.

In the void above the mat, in that vast hollow lung in the middle where almost nothing lives per volume, wings beat. Small, leathery, circling. A snap inches from my cheek, a pressure wave, a papery struggle. They drink falling drops on the wing and never land where I can find them. I thought, maybe I would be a Circler. To know the ceiling not by sight but perfectly, by tongue-click. To be the only fast thing here except that snap.

But I don't want to circle. I want to stay.

By my left foot, in a pan ankle-deep, warm and chalky on the air, something has not moved since I arrived. A slow suck and plop somewhere else tells me his brothers are shifting, lunging, but he waits. Forearm-long, pale as a grub, gilled like a feather-duster, reading pressure along his flank the way I tried to read print. He hunts by smell. He breeds only in the center pools where the chorus is thickest, where I am forbidden to step — not by a rule written down, but by shin and shame.

I envied him his posture. I tried to hold it. I couldn't. An hour after I leave he will still be there.

So I go lower.

Under me is not ground. The moss takes my weight and sighs and exhales, and after a long minute pushes back. A foot thick, white, blind, springy, almost entirely built leaf by leaf in dark over millennia. If I press my ear to it I hear it fizz faintly, roots excreting acid and eating stone one molecule at a time.

That is where I find myself. Not on it. In it.

I would be the mat.

The slow animal made of rot that holds everyone else up. Full of mouths smaller than eyelashes — mites, springtails the color of milk, worms thin as thread — turning dead elephant-ear into soil into food again. Smelling of wet paper and sweet rot, warm from the inside out.

Out of me rise the Drinkers, those huge translucent pork-pink ears as wide as a torso, bearded with hairs and cups and funnels, combing fog and lime-warm seep and that faint oiliness on the skin, metal at the back of the tongue, that the three cold slabs breathe out. The slabs set stool-high in their loose triangle around the largest pool, drinking every photon. The twelve weightless black walnuts strung at ankle height that warm very slightly when touched and make the dark deeper for a breath. I drink their field too, secondhand, through root-hairs that vibrate to gather charge and water both.

On my shoulders bloom the Eaters, fungi like cold dinner plates, cool and firm and then giving like cartilage, exhaling spores so thick I breathe them.

On my skin the Ringers claim their inches and ring my bones. In my curtains the Sawyers saw. In my pans the Waiters wait longer than any guest can stand still. Over my hollow center the Circlers circle, and their guano falls and feeds me, and I feed the leaves, and the leaves feed the moths, and the moths are drunk in turn inches from a stranger's face.

Water falls grain by grain through me. Leaves grow a millimeter a month out of me. Breakdown blocks the size of huts settle a finger's width per century into my shoulders. Drip. Ring. Saw. Wingbeat. Drip.

You would find me between the roots of the giant white ear, where the moss is deepest, just off the largest pool inside the triangle, where the shin finds the bead-line if you're careless. Not walking straight — no one dares walk straight — but settled. Half-buried platform cold against one edge of me even in this warmth. Frog on my back. Salamander in my hollow. Spore in my breath.

I do not need you to see me to be completely myself.
