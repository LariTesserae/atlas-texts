At first I think I would be standing. In the middle.

Like you. Come down without noticing you're descending, boots sighing water up around the tread, breath showing faintly when I stand still. Learning with my feet: step only where it is already firm and shining. Turning sideways between the ship's wheels. Checking the clouded gauge. Leaving a grease-pencil mark.

But I am too heavy for that. Too salty. Too warm.

If I stood long enough under an eave I would be dripped on, regularly as a clock, cold on the neck. Tasted. And found to be firm in the wrong way. Dull mud would take me to the ankle — that is not mud, that is a thicker piece of it, holding on — and I would suck my boot free too loud and break the rule here, which is: everything happening at once, just quietly.

No. Not the visitor. The footprints that come in, do a circuit, and leave are not mine. I don't leave.

So lower. A little lower, and a little thinner.

Maybe at the rim, where the ground is a hand higher. Where the punished fringe of sharp reeds holds. I could be the one moorhen working the rim ditch, picking my way on feet too big for me. That would be a negotiation: distribute the weight so the skin doesn't break. Eat what is left over. Never go to the center where the three intakes gulp. That is a life. Muffled-waiting. Far away gull through wool.

But I am not that good at staying out. And I don't want to stay out. Outside the low berm of old spoil is flat fields, grey-green, too drained, too sunny, too dry on top. I want inside, where water is not *in* a place but *on* everything.

Maybe in the sump then? An eel moving like a slow thought in black and brown churn. To be a thought it thinks. To live in its deep part, in the ropy, too-coherent water, feeling No. 2 pump catch with a shudder up through the soles — no, through the whole length — of me. *Glup.* That would be warm-blooded in reverse. Cool-blooded. The kind of cool that keeps things from rotting fast and from drying ever.

But eels leave. And the vortex breaks.

A midge, then? In the low column over the warm outflow where the cooling water comes back out almost hot and steams. To live for an afternoon in its breath, in the flat cool noon light. To be taken. That is also a life, but too brief. I want to stay wet longer.

Listen. Hold your breath and listen to the lattice: tick of cooling metal, plink plink plink from three different eaves out of sync, seep-hiss through gravel, the low electrical hum you feel more in your teeth than hear. Under that, a wetter click. Then the needle trembles. Then the wheel turns a quarter-turn with no hand on it.

Do not touch a wheel that is trembling. It is about to move by itself.

I would not touch. I would hang near it. Under the eaves they have hung tools and spares so thick they clink softly — wrenches, gaskets, lengths of chain, a row of buckets all full of clear water. No leaves in them. Something drinks clear water and leaves the leaves for the sluice.

I could be a bucket. Full and still and clear. Waiting to be drunk from. But buckets get emptied.

No. Thinner still.

Not at waist height, where in any cubic meter there are four or five presences touching: pipe, chain, hook, dripping eave, your shoulder. Below that. At the level of the skin of water sheeting over the concrete aprons. At the level of the beard.

That is where I find myself, finally. Where I become more myself.

Underneath the big barrel-round low main at knee height, rusted orange and weeping at the flanges. Almost dry on top. Fat underneath with a hanging fringe that never quite falls. The air cooler there by a degree. The metal bites, but I don't mind, because I am not a hand anymore.

I am a bead in the beard.

Fattening. Inside me a thread, rust-orange, faint as tea. Iron bacteria. Farming. I am one of many small wet hands you mistake for drips. I gather heaviest on the undersides because that is where we are not evaporated by the high-cloud noon. I creep sideways near the bottom of the quarry wall, half a millimeter thick, against the slope, toward the iron bracket to thicken there to olive and rust-red. Slow. Deliberate.

If you put your bare hand close under, without touching, you would feel me cool you. If you stand under me long enough I will mark you, briefly, cold on the neck, regularly as a clock. Salt. Heat. To know if you are firm and shining where I can cross.

And in the standing puddle below me, I will wear your shape to look out. You lift your hand and your dark double lifts his a beat after, a fraction thicker at the edges, fringed. That late reflection — that is me catching up.

I keep the bowl from becoming a pond. A pond would kill me — too still, too deep, too warm. I need thin, cool, moving, aerated, ironed water. So I keep the machines. Float-switch clicks no louder than a tongue-click and I shudder through your soles. Sluice lifts by itself and I sheet hard across the apron carrying leaves and grit. Relief vents white for ten seconds and I breathe steam and smell of hot oil and wet brick.

I sleep in the sound-swallow moss on the north sides of things, on fallen sleepers, on the footings of sheds. Thick. I wake when mud on the wall slumps an inch and plops.

I do not roar. I turn. I do not flock or herd. I saturate without lushness. I graze the reed shoots to nubs so the floor stays open, scuffed, puddled, rails half-sunk but shining on top, polished by my passage.

Find me there. Not standing. Hanging. Spread low and distributed, hamlet-wide and thin, doing it continuously.
