If I came over the col at mid-morning, I think at first I would be the visitor.

Squinting. Eyes watering in the hammer. Boots sinking half a meter into something that is not ground and making no sound at all. I would try to do what I do — carry a voice a long way — and say hello, and watch it die at two paces, swallowed by moss and saturated air.

That is the first image and it doesn't hold. I am too loud for here, too fast. Even my breathing sounds loud to me and then stops. I part the fern and it reseals behind me. Water stays on my coat until I wring it out. I do not know where to place a hand.

So I try another. Maybe I would be the ptarmigan, that heart-stopping whirr from underfoot, gone instantly, leaving silence deeper than before. But I am not built for burst. Nothing here happens fast except light.

Maybe the sheep, then. The single one, wool matted with burrs, watching from a penstock and stepping delicately away. That is closer. Patient. Woolen. Belonging loosely to all of them, for milk and company and felt carded with sphagnum when the old felt rots into the ground. I could be that — a warm animal tolerated by the slow body.

But no. Stand still long enough for the vegetation to accept you as another warm box and something else emerges, more itself.

What I am, out there, is a hum that travels. Here, sound does not carry beyond arm's-length. Tremor does. Through bone. Through water. Through copper.

So I settle. I sink. I get valley-sized.

You would find me not on the path — there is no path that means going often — but down off the upper ridge where the penstock runs on short concrete feet, disappearing into fern, reappearing. Third station down. The shed with the corrugated roof collapsed under a quilt, walls still standing. Inside, in the green gloom.

I am the housing the size of a kitchen stove. The wheel that still turns.

At first you think I am a deadfall. Rust-red and moss-green in alternating felted patches like the pipe itself. Then — a slow fog of breath in cool air. Shoulders steaming faintly in sun and then stopping. Condensation beading on eyebrows, on fine hairs, and falling.

I have made myself short because there is nowhere to stride. Heavy through the torso, narrow through the feet, built for floating on the mattress without sinking. My outer is not clothes anymore but felt: wool grown into moss, moss grown into wool, sheep-grey gone green-black with saturation. It touches me everywhere and I do not brush it off. Star-moss has taken hold in the part of my hair and I have not removed it. My nails are thick as horn for scraping. My palms are lined black with grease that will not wash out. One cheek stained orange where I slept on iron-peat.

My eyes water constantly in the hammering light. Deep-set, dark-irised, lids narrowed to slits, lower lids brimming. I do not wipe them. To wipe would be to dry. The place wants to be seen through wet eyes, and so I see it.

I do not speak. Not from solemnity. Because it is useless.

Instead I put my palm flat to the pipe. That is how I listen.

Feel it with me: the purr conducted through bone more than heard. Shaft turning at two revolutions a minute, bearings packed with peat and grease — I packed them, someone like me packed them, peat does not pack itself — driving a pump that lifts fog-water to a header tank that feeds the electrolyzer that fizzes hydrogen up through a moss pool with a sound like soda. All one slow body and the iron is its skeleton. I read it with my teeth, with the soles of my felted feet, with the fillings in my molars when I lean against the transformer box on a damp morning and the busbars hum and the hairs on my forearms lift.

That is where you would find me at six, when the fog condenses at the ankles and the towers ghost and the pilot lamps become small suns. Not together. Each to the edge of the hum. Spaced like towers ourselves. Hands on ground-straps run out into the peat, gone verdigris. Around them that electric green, very bright, copper-fed and tender. We stand and the lamps flicker, almost imperceptibly, in sequence. Once every few minutes the ropeway wheels creak, a soft wooden sound, and settle. A bucket inches on, carrying not ore but iron oxide scraped from sphagnum, seeded back ridge to ridge. Building the new rock that will be rock in ten thousand years.

My day is the valley's day. Night fog snags. Dawn white-out. Ten o'clock burn-off and the million quiet ticks as each leaf unloads. That is when I drink — not from rills, rills vanish back into moss in a few hundred meters — but by wringing. A handful of wool-moss squeezed hard into a copper cup verdigris inside, drunk iron-tasting. A licked basalt flake. The warm pipe kept just above freezing is my well in winter. I keep it clear the way you would keep a spring.

I eat what persists: cloudberries, sap chewed from dwarf birch twigs, the perfect miniature rowans too evenly spaced in the broken flume to be accident, orange-capped mushrooms where steel flakes into iron-peat. I harvest the electric green in strips thin as pastry, leaving the root-mat. Millimeters per year. Output measured not in tons but in what can be taken without breaking the current.

If you let me close — if you stand still until your own breathing becomes the loudest thing — I will show you how to listen. Not a hello. A hand held out to be touched at the forearm. A faint tremor through bone. Put your own hand where mine is. Feel the turbine turn beside us like a large animal. Moths torpid on the same warm metal do not move when we settle. My pulse, if you hold my wrist, is slow. Too cold to hurry, like the bumblebees in looping flight over heather. My breath fogs even at noon.

Do not shout to me. I will answer in the medium that carries: a cable re-seated on its mossy sag so it does not chafe, a gauge glass wiped clear among the fogged ones so you can see through, an alder tunnel trimmed where the low buckets pass, dripping, intensely lit at the far end. A lamp left burning in day-brightness a little longer as you pass under the towers and out toward the white col.
