At first I think I would come as myself. As a climber.

Dropping down through the notch at noon with dry shoulders and something to say. I would stand on the chipped steps and shout to hear myself think. I would put my palm to the fresh-broken glitter and take it away again.

It doesn't hold. Nothing that tall holds here. In seven paces my voice stacks and shreds. My jacket darkens in seconds. My breath goes white and is lost — I can't tell which mist is mine. The pool sucks down, the black walls gasp, and I am in the wrong place, feet wet, hands in the gyre where they shouldn't be.

So I step up. That is the first lesson and I learn it with my boots.

Behind the low windbreak, knees pulled in. Smaller.

I think, then, I could be the dipper. That would be cleaner. To come shrieking down through spray that whites everything out, slate-gray and certain, to spear straight into the white tongues and vanish and bob up shaking with something wriggling. To know her fist well enough to steal from it. To leave again before the cloud closes.

But I can't hold my breath that long. I can't stand to be that brave that fast. I linger. Lingering is not a dipper's business.

I think I could be down below. One of the pale flickers rolling in the gyre, eyeless white, washed down from the snowfield or up from somewhere deeper than this thimble has any right to be. Let the suck take me, spin three times like the log that wasn't there, jam, break, leave.

But I don't want to be eaten live, snowmelt-cold with a mineral bite. Even if the hand that takes me is quick and numb and precise. Even if it is her.

I keep getting smaller. Wetter. Quieter.

Don't turn your back on the inhale. Drink from the seep, not the pool. Leave the cairn alone. The rules file themselves down to a size I can keep.

I find myself — really find myself — not on the steps, not in the water, but pressed to the rock where I didn't think anything could stay.

The leeward wall. Just above the splash line, just below the orange lichen, where the windbreak throws a thumb of shadow. Where the stone sweats less. Where the short furious rainbow tears ten times a minute but doesn't quite land.

I am white there. That is the first thing that becomes itself.

Not slate-gray like her, not milk-pale like rock-flour. White. A single white moth pasted flat to fresh granite that still glitters with just-split crystals. Wings too wet to lift, beaded, dripping, shivering. Grit on my feet. Cold straight through into whatever passes for bone in me.

At first pasted looks like stuck. Like trapped. Then it becomes more itself: pasted is a decision. To hold on without gripping. To let spray hang so thick the air is drinkable and drink it through skin. To do nothing fast in a place that unmakes and remakes itself by the minute.

The roar — low hammering, high hiss, mid-range in the sternum — becomes my blanket. I stop trying to hear it. I sleep through hammering. What wakes me is what wakes her: the quiet. The calm seconds between breaths when the falls falter and the pool shows bottle-green going down and down. The early echo, the return a fraction before the sound.

I pulse then. That is the second thing that becomes itself.

At first pulse is just shiver. Cold. Then it slows. Then it finds the other rhythm in the pocket — the breathe every four or five minutes, the suck-down exposing slick dark walls, the slap-back rearranging pebbles over the steps. My wings open a millimeter, close. Open. Close. Not flying. Timing.

She comes near to breathe. Her hair plastered black-green as star-moss, lichen colonized on the backs of shoulders she will never dry. Her white-tipped, split-cuticle fingers re-balancing the top stone impossibly with numb precision. The grass rope looped around her ankle leading down to the lip. Forehead to wall to listen an hour ahead through rock.

She does not eat me.

In a place so profoundly empty, where vast space lies between presences and everything edible is taken in the lull — amphipods, trapped young char, things washed down and up, it doesn't matter — she leaves the moth alone. That sparing is a kind of tending. I am the emptiness she tends, kept empty on purpose.

So I stay. White on dark basalt. Pulsing when she breathes near. Measuring the thumb-high tide line of foam from the side: when it touches the bottom stone of the cairn she is already up at the windbreak, and I flatten flatter. When it sucks down past it, she goes quick to the gyre with her fist, and I open, close.

You would find me there, if the cloud lifts for an hour at noon and you drop down soaked through until the roar has cleaned out everything in your head. Not shouting anymore. Pasted. Barely moving except to pulse.

I would be the thing she lets live without use.
