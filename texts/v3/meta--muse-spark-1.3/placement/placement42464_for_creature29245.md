You go down to it. I go down.

Not to visit. At first I think I will stay upright, stay dry-ish, stay human — boots too loud on the slabs and then swallowed. Stand on the rim with my thumb over the whole bowl and say: there it is. Keep the stair at my back.

It doesn't work like that down here. The light changes its mind forty meters down, and so do I.

At first I think I will be the heron. Everyone wants to be the heron at first.

To be that exact grey, the grey of limestone just before it goes black with wet. To own every view by standing in it absolutely still, one leg folded, while the needling four o'clock rain pocks the mirrors around me and looks fussy. To move to the next mirror without a wingbeat, while the pit remembers sound a moment too long.

But I am not still enough for that. I blink. I breathe too fast. I look away at the flywheel — two revolutions a minute, time to forget a link and remember it again — and when I look back I have already startled. The post cannot be empty, and I leave it empty.

So lower. I think I will be a pigeon. There are hundreds, there will be room.

To take the north side only of every roof, for reasons no one has written down. To fluff against the cellar-cool and deepen to oily blue-grey with wet. To coo and have it marble around the bowl and come back late, thrown and caught. To not even tuck my feet when the rats trot below.

But I listen to that late return too closely. It comes back wrong. I want an answer, and Lowtown only gives an echo. I am too lonely for tenants.

Lower. The curb lines. I think I will trot them with terrible confidence.

To be fat and sleek and dark chocolate from generations in shadow, whiskers beaded. To know the timetable better than any caretaker — pause when the droplet travels the sagging cable, wait for the plink a second later. To run from TEA FLOUR PARAFFIN to the polite gaps where buildings were removed, always to the grain that spilled decades ago and is still spilling. To leave a thin S-curve in the film that the next rain erases.

But I cannot keep to the exact center where it stays driest, which is still not dry. I wobble. I look up too much.

I look up and want the air under the rim in summer. To cut it and scream once and have the stone hush it. But swifts never land down here. There is no dry ledge to take off from again. I would have to keep flying in the pale dusty column at noon that doesn't reach the corners, eating damp moths, never resting. I am already tired.

So I want the lamps. The sodium oranges and the one flickering white over the pump house. To come in slow snowfall, dun and furred, wings too heavy with damp to flutter fast. To bump and rest and bump again and not burn — that strangest mercy, the lime-filmed cool. To dry a degree on the warm riveted iron.

But my wings never quite dry. I keep falling into the gutters where the translucent celery-weed leans toward the light to catch what I drop.

After the daily rain, the pavement moves at ankle height. Maybe there.

I think I will be vast and unhurried. A slug as long as my forearm, leopard-spotted, amber, black-keeled. To cross between the houses after the rain and leave a silver suture that glints for hours under lamplight. To eat the moss epaulettes only where they can afford it, to eat the hart's-tongue unrolling from the chapel wall only where the fern can afford it. To be slow even for slugs — the cold slows me — and that is why I am so large. No salt ever comes down the stair cut into rock. The rats watch with professional disinterest. The heron will not waste a strike.

I almost stay there. The silver line is a good life. A signature that lasts until four.

But I am still too much on the surface. Still seeing the cloud circle counterclockwise all day, patient as the flywheel. Still hearing the drip from cable, the tick-tick-tick of cooling metal, the wet slap of an eel turning, the dynamo low in my sternum like a held breath.

The eels knot before the hour turns, heads all one way at the outflow grates, waiting for the turbines to shudder on. They rise a half-inch higher when it rains only down there, only for twenty minutes, only for itself. They clean better than any gang. I lie with them a while in six inches of clear cold flow over silt, silt itself breathing. Black-olive and yellow-bellied, thick as wrist, thin as bootlace. It is good work. Muscular braid, no friction.

Still, light touches me there. Noon finds a corner of the canal.

I want darker. Warmer.

You would find me, finally, where I stopped trying to see.

In the pump house basement, where the caretaker's bicycle leans forever upstairs with soft tires and a basket full of rain and no one comes to move it. Down the dished steps, knee-deep and warm from the culverts.

I hang there. White, the size of your thumb, blind, translucent enough to see the pulse go through me.

I do not school. Each of us a little apart, facing into the seep, mouths opening and closing on nothing you can see. If you put your hand in — cold to the elbow after the warmth — we do not scatter. We quiver and stay.

We have lived so long without light that light is a rumor. We never see the top of the cloud like skin on cold milk from the rim. We live on heat and iron bacteria and vibration. The dynamo hums through the stone and through us.

The pit inhales. The pit exhales. The lime advances a millimeter a year over a doorway on Pump Row. The water-clock stain rises an inch and falls.

I do not look up when it rains.
