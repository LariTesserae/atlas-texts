You would find me — if you could find me at all under the roar — low.

Not at first. At first I don't know where to put myself. Three steps on the duckboards and my heart is up in my throat, my shirt sticking, and the air won't satisfy. I breathe twice to get enough, and breathe again. Everything is too loud and too wet and too close. Black wall, white sky, white cloud-bottom. There's no room for me.

So I try on fast things.

I think I could be the hummingbird. Emerald, absurd. I hear the dry whirr cut the wet roar before I see the green. How wonderful to not live here — to come up two thousand meters from forest I can only guess at, to work each wax-orange throat methodically with a tongue too long for my body, a heart doing a thousand beats a minute in air where I can barely walk, and then go. To be a visitation. But no. I can't hold that pace. In this thin air my own heart is already hammering just standing still. I would fall out of the sky over the lip.

I think I could be the condor, tilting past *below* me, looking up. To live in the vastness this room has opted out of. To see, for one second, the whole small warm green bite in black rock with steam coming out of it — a lung in a cone — and keep going. But I don't want vastness. I want to stay inside.

I think I could be the midges, that cloud that is not steam over the hottest pool. A dozen, a hundred, holding station in the updraft, never leaving the column. Their larvae down in the muck red with extra hemoglobin because warm water holds less oxygen and thin air holds less to begin with. To live fast and die fast, mate in the hum, lay, die on the surface and become peat. That is the opposite bargain of everything else here. No. Even my hurrying is too slow for that.

Then I go slower.

Maybe I am one of the thumbnail orchids, white or wax-orange, furred onto the shoulder-high dwarf tree with bark peeling in red paper strips. Opening over weeks, not days. Waiting for the hummingbird I cannot live without. Drinking the suspended beads that hang reluctant to drop in the updraft. A fallen trunk becomes a street and a live cable becomes a trellis — I could live on either. That's closer. To be patient enough to be pollinated by a miracle once a day.

Or the moss. Ten deep. Sphagnum rust-red and lime-green, liverwort lacquered dark as if varnished. Stitched so you cannot lift one without lifting all. Holding twenty times my weight in condensed sky. Never having to be afraid because nothing drains — it evaporates off the warm skin, condenses on the cliff-cold, rains back as seep. Tiny bell-jar weather, turning over and over. To drink the air.

Or the man in the hut. Soup-smell in the stovepipe sometimes. Boots by the door gone soft with moss. Oil under nails that will never wash out in this wet, cheeks permanently red with extra blood. The hand in the logbook getting slower and rounder over years: *pressure steady. cleared intake 3. orchid out on north pipe. frog under board.* Moving at peat-speed, listening to the pump with his palm, clearing ferns from grating with an apology. Enlarged heart, but in the other direction: slow, large, patient. His rust also creeps a millimeter a year. I love him. But I am not him yet. I still resent breathing twice.

I keep getting lower, quieter, wetter. Under the machinery.

Listen *under* — not over — the roar. Under pump-throb under water-roar under steam-hiss under electric whine. There are windows between them, narrow acoustic windows.

That's where I am.

Shin-deep where you would be shin-deep if you stepped off the duckboards, in warm muck that smells of earth and oil. At the edge of the warmest, most closed pool, where no fish could ever have arrived, where the water shivers at 35 degrees and smells faintly of struck matches and old nails and is fringed rust-red and mustard-yellow with iron-eaters and sulfur-eaters breathing rock-heat out in bubbles. Where the sedges lean over the duckboard and the north pipe drips constantly, cold condensation on steel.

I am the second frog. Maybe the third. That is the whole vertebrate nation that lives here year-round.

Mud-brown-backed, sulfur-pale-bellied, the size of the last joint of your thumb. Find me by staying still longer than is comfortable, crouched slowly so you don't black out, until your eyes tune down past the pipes and the beards of moss on the guy-wires and the ferns unfurling chest-high.

I solved the lung problem sideways. I don't pull twice like you. My throat sac moves twice for every one of your breaths, but most of what I breathe I breathe through skin kept permanently wet by the air. Your shirt is damp in a minute. Your hair drips. I am damp always. The air that starves you feeds me.

I do not hurry, except as I once did as a tadpole — growing fast in the heat, feeding on midge fall in that closed pool. As an adult I wait. Peat-time. Lichen-change. I call not in chorus — a chorus would drown — but singly, in those windows, a high clear plink that cuts through. You have to shut your mouth to hear me.

The keeper knows me. He wrote me without knowing which one: *frog under board.* When he passes at peat-speed he does not lift the board. He leaves most to grow through.

At dusk — though noon lasts so long you forget dusk exists — the single bulb in the hut and the small red lamp on the transformer click on, absurd and homely, and the beads in the air catch the light and for a moment the whole small room looks like it's underwater. Like you're inside a lung. The moths that should not live here batter the glass, inbred and damp and thumping softly. The steam thickens whiter. The transformer hums without pause. The needles tremble but never move far.

I sit in it. Throat going. Belly to warm earth. Everything here touches everything else and here, at the skin, I feel it touch: warm water to moss to orchid to hummingbird tongue to midge to rust to steel to steam to skin to mine.

You breathe twice to get enough, and breathe again, up on the boards above me, looking for where you end and the marsh begins.

I don't.
