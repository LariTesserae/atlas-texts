At first I don't know where I am in it.

Because it's flat first. My body understands that before I do — that there is nowhere to hide my height. I keep trying to stand the way I stand elsewhere, upright to see further, and there is no further. Only more flat. Grey eats the distance. Weather comes for an hour before it reaches me. My footsteps are too loud.

So I crouch without deciding to. The place teaches it.

I try, for a while, to be many things.

For a breath I am up in that shrieking weather, one of the slate waders with a ballooned white throat, *krr-krr-krr*, because the noise is so total it seems easier to be the one making it than the one enduring it. I feel it in the sternum, the slabs humming underneath the inch of water. But I can't hold it. My throat is too small for ten thousand voices.

For another breath I am lower — down in the sheet, flat-backed and copper, clicking claw on stone. That is simpler. Graze the orange mat, turn all at once with the whole bank flashing, scuttle for cover when the shadow passes, clean the bones by morning. I like the honesty of that. But I can't stay there either. My hands keep wanting to pick things up, to rearrange.

And I do. That's how I find myself.

Not in the middle of the boil where the shallows teem and the midges bend the light and the black divers sit on their single speckled eggs. I can't work there yet, I am too clumsy and too loud. The adults go silent and signal — flat palm for *wait for the sound to pass*, two fingers walking for *high stone path*, fist for *pothole* — and I don't know the language in my tendons yet. I shout at the wrong time and the colony lifts and takes my voice with it.

So I am out in the between. The long half-mile of nothing but glistening rock and sky and sheet-water winking. The place the stranger files under uninhabited. The plain breathing between heartbeats.

That's where I would find myself. On the high stone, where it's a shade lighter — that's higher by an inch. Never in the mirror-pools. My feet learning, slowly, to stay dry if they can.

Because someone has to keep the sentences readable out here.

I become the one who stacks.

Not the old woman at the weir — though I watch her until my knees ache from kneeling beside her, hood up, utterly still. She reads the orange where the mat is thickest and most wrinkled, lifts the sinew trap, shakes three pale fish flipping, slits them with greenstone, saves the guts for the bone hooks in the deeper potholes. Blood into water. Midges settle. Nothing wasted. I am not her. My hands are still too slow, my cuticles not yet stained that permanent rust-orange from wringing and lifting all day in iron water.

Not one of the children either — though I envy them. Only children can afford to be loud here, running flapping with hide cloaks spread to panic the waders off a patch of eggs, screaming back at the colonies, feeling the wave build in the sternum and holding breath until it breaks, then speaking a whole sentence clear in the three seconds of relative quiet. I am still learning to hold my breath that long. I still try to cover my ears when the ground ahead darkens and lifts. I am still learning to open my mouth instead. To drop to one knee, head tucked, and let the pressure pass through me.

No. I am the one who walks the empty stretch after them and before them.

My feet — in sealskin moccasins slipped off when no one is looking to let my toes splay wide and grip — barely whisper on the quartzite where boots would ring. My cloak is mud-grey like everyone else's, otter and fox and wader skin scraped thin and rubbed over and over with crab fat and fish oil until water beads. Underneath, feather-in bird skin for warmth that breathes. I let my elbows get wet. I keep my core dry. I have stopped trying to stay dry the way I meant it elsewhere.

In my lap, as I go, flat slabs. I lift a fallen arm of a weir stone by stone — fifty paces an arm converging to a gap no wider than a basket — and set it two inches to the left where the rill narrows. I raise a cairn an inch where subsidence took it. I turn the boats? No, that's later, with everyone, before we leave. For now: I find the stack topped with a white shell knocked off by the pale gull-thing, and I put another one up. Crab carapace bleached white, facing the way you should walk. *Safe here, knee-deep there, eggs that way when the tide drains.*

A child who cannot yet read water can read shells. A stranger like me, who cannot yet read either, can learn by replacing them.

At dusk that never comes — just that perpetual pearl-grey late-afternoon, sun a paler smear — I am back in the shallow hollow behind the windbreak no higher than my shin, hides stretched over driftwood. I sort bone hooks in piles with the others. Re-knot. Re-rub cloaks with fat. Eat what the mat makes: boiled white fish, roasted crab split and steaming, a speckled egg taken one per hollow so the divers stay, and that sour orange paste scraped from the thickest mats and fermented in seal stomach, iron and rot and salt on my fingertip to keep my gut alive. Drink stone-water scooped from a seep where it runs clearest off granite. Laugh in short barks timed to the quiet troughs.

And when I go into the beehive bothy — drystone, roof of whalebone and precious driftwood from a forested coast I will never see from here — to lay down the greenstone chopper to cache under a slab, to touch the pile of bone hooks and the strips of hide drying stiff in the damp, I touch the nail.

Everyone has one. Hammered into the lintel, rusted almost to nothing. Iron does not belong here. Everything else is chipped, ground, lashed. So iron must be for luck. The grandmothers say it holds the roof down when the wings become weather.

I know it's nonsense and I touch it anyway, going in and out. Because I am also a thing that does not belong here, learning how to be low. Learning how to add a few careful stones and let water and animals do the rest.

Walk a little further with them, and the ground will darken again and lift, shrieking, into the dim air. I will already be kneeling.
