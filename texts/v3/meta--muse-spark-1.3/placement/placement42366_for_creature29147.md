At first I think I would be passing through.

A visitor with a camera for the white cat, with gloves I keep taking off to touch things I shouldn't. I would stand in the middle and look up at the ribbon of sky and get in the way of the drip-lines. I would breathe too loud. My breath would fog and hang there, and I would be embarrassed by how long it stays.

Twenty steps up the public stair and I have to stop. My lungs are still lowland lungs. The handrail is white with rime and the walkway under my boots is — impossibly — just stone. Neither warm nor cold. Water beads and runs off to the sides. I put my palm flat like the maintenance woman showed me, and I don't understand it, and she smiles with cracked knuckles because nobody understands it at first.

No, I wouldn't be her. Not yet. She knows the resonance of the stone the way you know a large animal breathing in the next room. She can hear when a pod has drifted a hair off after a night creak in the new rock. I only hear my own heart.

I wouldn't be the children either. I watch them cross the highest needle-bridge full-tilt, laughing in those short bursts the thin air allows, because to them phase-lock is not physics, it is floor. My body doesn't trust floor that isn't bolted. I cross slow, a hand's breadth off disaster, looking down at the basins linked by runnels, so clear you can see the bolt-heads at the bottom.

I wouldn't be the two technicians, foreheads almost touching over amber light for an hour, handing each other tools without looking. I don't have that kind of quiet with anyone yet. I talk too much to fill the cold.

So where do I go? Down, I think. Everyone else lives up, stacked. I keep drifting down toward the floor where the cleft is narrowest and the sound is. The glassy throat-sound of water moving basin to basin. Basin 4 to Basin 5, maybe. If you stand very still on the central bridge at night you can hear it under the ice — that faint continuous hiss they all pause on stairs to listen for. Keep the water moving.

That's where you would find me, eventually. Not on a bridge. On my knees.

There is a terrace on the low east side, twenty meters long, that holds only a bench and a coil of cable and a sealed container. It's supposed to be empty. The town was built for more density than it holds, and it likes the gaps. The rock shows between every work. The air shows. Someone has to keep that terrace empty — to sweep the seep back into the runnel before it finds a low corner and freezes and pries. To walk a half-step to the side of the drip-lines. To not leave a bag where a channel wants to go.

The old man teaches me. He doesn't talk much. He has a wide soft broom and an endless, meditative task: sweeping water off his terrace back into the channel, back into the channel. At first I think it's cleaning. It's not. It's steering. Tend the drip, don't stop it. Never block it. That's bad manners and bad engineering.

My fingers go blunt quickly. My cheeks burn red at the lip where the hood doesn't cover. I learn to move slowly because twenty steps winds you here and there is no reason to hurry anyway. The cloud comes in several times a day — a white tongue nosing from the lower valley, dying the instant it hits the shaded stone — and everything weeps, and then at noon the sun finds the slit and drops a single hard shaft down the middle and everything ticks and pings as it releases. I learn to read that as weather.

At night it drops hard. The weeping slows, then locks into glass. The lifter parks itself against the wall. The silver crabs fold back into their niches. The windows come on far apart, warm yellow, spaced like camps that have agreed not to crowd each other. I go inside my pod — faceted, windowless except for the one deep eye, number etched beside the door — and I close the sighing door, and for a while I am deliberately alone, together with sixty or a hundred others I will never see at once.

But I don't sleep right away. I go back out onto the cold path that keeps itself free of frost. I stand on the low crossing where no one crosses. I listen.

Under the deep infrequent creak of young mountain settling, new stone learning its weight, there is water still going in the pipes, in the braided sleeves climbing uphill without pumps, unwilling to stop.

I am the one who listens for that. Not the maintainer, not the child, not the cat — the white cat on Level Three who cries thinly and is famous out of proportion because it is the only soft, unnecessary thing. Someone always opens a door for it. Not me. I am lower down, damper, more mineral.

I am the tender of a short runnel between two shallow pools. Keeper of an empty terrace. Learner of slowness. The one who wears his warmth on his body and gives none to the sky, who has finally learned to live a hand's breadth off the rock and let it weep.
