Not at first as anything that belongs here.

At first I am only the ache. My lungs know the place before I do — too thin, too cold, each breath scraped. My boots sink and spring back wrong. I brought eyes that want sun and they find nothing to do. I brought a light, I think, and then I understand I have to put it out, because the ember-green along the cracks pulses slower than my blinking and I am blinding myself to it.

So I crouch. That is the first belonging.

If I stay low the wind doesn't take me as much. The moss gives under my knees. Through my trousers I feel the faint heat sighing up out of the rock, and I hear, if I put my ear close enough, the fizz. Growing. Freezing and growing at the same time. I stay there longer than I meant to.

I try on other bodies while I am down there.

Could I be a grazer? For a moment I want to be. Low and shaggy and six-legged, shovel head tearing cloth of frost-moss, belly a furnace grumbling with rot turned to warmth. To know the seams the way you know home, to drift from warm crack to warm crack and not need to decide anything except follow the steam and the grunt of the herd. To have wool that ices outside and stays dry at the skin. It is tempting — to be kept, to be dumb and warm. But my head is too loud. I listen too hard. I am not content to crop.

Could I be one of the moth-birds? Pale, blind-white, furred to the wingtip, wider than I am tall. To ride the updrafts along the seams where steam and spores rise, to never need to see, only to smell heat and hear. To land heavy on a bladder-leaf big as a house and feel it slosh under me, to rasp sugar-lichen with my tongue and drink. To roost in a shaggy drift of ten thousand breathing bodies under the lattice. I want that soft thunder for a while, that slow snow. But I am too heavy for air. I don't want to live aloft. My hands keep reaching down for stone, for vine.

Could I be a hunter? Lean and dark-blue and frost-shedding, ears swiveling separately, whiskers long as arms reading vine and rock and wind. To triangulate a calf's whistle a mile off and move without sound until the click of claws and then the scream. To be perfectly suited to night that never ends. I practice holding still the way they must hold still. I listen for the absence. And I know — no. I am not silent enough. I am not alone enough. I need the grunt and whistle of others. I need touch.

My hands are cold. That tells me.

I keep putting them in, without thinking: into a crack to feel warmth leak, to tip a leaf to see if water runs, to tighten a loose fiber. I am already coaxing.

So let the visitor fall away. Let the boots be wrapped. Let the eyes get large and dark and long-adjusted. Let the chest go broad, the lungs go big. Let a cloak settle over me, felted from grazer wool, lined inside with shed moth-fur — soft thunder made into insulation. Let something slosh at my hip. A small bladder-leaf flask, stoppered with resin.

That is where I find myself.

Not in a village, because there are none. On the shoulder of a root that I at first mistook for a hill, between one trunk-city and the next, a mile of open night air on either side. A cairn of sharp basalt flakes, tipped and mossed over, marks the warm seam below me. I have re-stacked two stones without even deciding to. The herd passed here yesterday — dung still faintly glowing with bacteria, sweet rot and musk still in the moss. I am following at a respectful distance, close enough to find what they find, far enough that the hunters will take them before they take me.

Tonight I will sleep in a room that is not a room. A low dome of living vine, trained years ago by someone and left to keep growing. I have to duck through a doorway still open. Inside, shed moth-fur pressed into the weave. A cold fire-pit where bone-char glitters — we rarely burn; heat is in the ground if you know where to lie. I lie where the steam keeps the frost off. My breath is white. Droplets run off my hood. My eyelashes freeze.

More itself: I am not just any hands. I am the one who listens.

In the morning my band — six, ten, cousins — will move on, and I will stay a minute behind to tie a vine here so that in a week it makes a handrail, to prune a bladder there so it swells and leans and drips into a cup I left. To braid leaf-fiber between two root-ridges over a breath of void, sagging with ice, to be crossed in darkness by feel. Children learn by my hands. Faster pulse of ember-green means warmer, means nearer steam, means grazers, means be wary. Organ-groan aloft means wind, means ice-shed soon, keep clear of open air. Shovel-grunt, calf-whistle, wing-thunder, claw-click — and which echo of the scream is real.

Find me belly-down on the springy mat with my ear pressed to it. Find me standing still under a sail-leaf while condensation patters down like distant applause, face upturned, mouth open. Sleeves soaked from brushing through a weeping wall I trained myself.

Very small. Very awake. Walking on the back of something enormous that does not know I am there, but keeps me warm anyway.
