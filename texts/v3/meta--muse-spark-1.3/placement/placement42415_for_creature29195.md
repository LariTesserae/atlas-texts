You would not find me at first.

I thought — if I came down here — I would be one of the loud ones. High up, shearing air. I wanted the lift-off, the twenty thousand wings at once and the scream turning to pressure in the ears. I wanted to plaster my cup under a lip with stolen spit and raise young inches from my neighbor.

But that isn't right. That's too fast for me.

Or I thought I would be low, in the columns that never end. Chestnut-brown, blind, my head too heavy for my body. Tapping the white boulevard with my antennae, left lane out, right lane in, carrying a flake of fleece bigger than my head for a hundred yards without stopping. I love that road, older than any foot that crosses it. But I'm not that patient, not that blind. I need to see.

Let it come clearer.

Cool. Dim. The air takes the wet off you the moment you breathe. My mouth doesn't chap — I have no lips to chap — but the edges of my eyes are dry. I am flat. I am the exact color of dust.

There. That's me becoming myself.

You'd find me on the west side — the shade side — of the second tower in the plaza. Not one of the small southern ones along the boulevards. One of the five old ones. Squat, buttressed like an old tree, quilted with patches where a wall slumped a hundred years ago and was built over without clearing. The fluting runs vertical where wind and slow collapse have furrowed it. I am in the third flute up from the fleece collar, pressed so tight my belly scales have taken the grain of it.

I have been here for hours. I will be here for hours more.

Below me is my garden without knowing it. The dark collar where the stone-fleece grows thickest and greenest around the base, fed for centuries by rust-colored frass spilled from the base galleries. Sage and ash and pale olive, a foot thick, crisp to touch, millions of hair-fine fronds combing the air. I can smell it from up here when a wing-wash stirs: turpentine and salt. If you crushed it no water would come, only oil. I don't crush it. I lie above it.

My side of the tower is warm. That's the first thing you have to understand, because there is no sun to speak of in the Hollow — just a brief grey noon and then long blue gloom — and the stone should be cold. It isn't. Put your cheek to it, as I put my whole underside to it, and it's two, three degrees above the air. Breath. Millions of masons inside in the lung-galleries, chewing wool and dust into cement, composting litter in the dark, and deeper the pale fanners standing in ranks beating their stubbed wing-cases that never became wings. In and out. In and out. The tower exhales through its flared lip above me. On a still evening a strip of ribbonbush held to it lifts. I feel the hum not as sound but as a tremor in my jawbone, in my sternum. The City keeping its children warm.

Above me the lip sticks out, and under it, crowded side by side by side, the martin tenements. Dust mixed with fresh mason saliva stolen off a building crew this morning, mixed with oily crop secretion. Pirated mortar. They quarrel constantly, chitter and slap. Their droppings dry before they land and powder down onto the fleece. Good. That feeds the fleece that feeds the masons whose midden feeds the crickets.

The crickets. That's why I'm here. That's why I'm flat and waiting.

They come low over the white pan in legions, pale, long-antennaed, popping like dry seeds spat from a mouth. Their trill is the sheet everything else is written on, high metallic endless, layer upon layer until you stop hearing insects and hear only a band. One will misstep. One always missteps off the midden trail and out onto the open salt where the flowers crackle — they never wash away, there is nothing to wash them — and then into the shadow of my tower.

I don't chase. I pulse my throat. Once. That's the only movement I've made in an hour. The dust-color holds. The cement-texture holds. You walked past and didn't see me, and then you saw twenty of us all at once when one throat moved.

When it comes close enough I will be — for half a second — the fastest thing in the Hollow. Not the swifts shearing past the airwort veils without touching them. Me. A dry snap. Then stillness again, and the glycerin second-hand in its gut becomes my water. I never drink. Nothing here drinks. My droppings will be dry white pellets. Before dusk a mason column will find them and carry them away as building grit. In a year that white will be in the wall under my belly. Layer over layer.

That is the rule. What falls stays.

At lift-off I close my inner eyelids. Not to sleep. The pulse takes us all. Something in the cold, some thinning of light only the swifts understand, and the floor exhales birds. Ten thousand martins screaming needle-thin, ash swifts, thatchbirds booming from their shaggy hats woven of ribbonbush strips over dead towers — houses that were chimneys that look like trees — and the whole bowl doubles every wingbeat, east wall to west and back a half-second later. Dust lifts off the vents with the hum and hangs glittering in the dim. I press flatter. The ribbonbush thickets rattle like paper. Shed chitin rains ticking on salt. I cannot hear my own heart, but I feel the tower's heart through my belly, faster now, fanning harder.

Then it passes. The cloud settles. Trill and chitter and vent-hum again.

At deepening dusk the nightjars lift all at once without call from the thickets, soft thunder, moth-eyes open, going up over the rim where the desert still holds last light to hawk dust-moths. They will bring outside inside in their bellies. A little enters. Nothing leaves.

A dead swift two towers over is already a mummy, perfect feathers and hollow eyes. In a month it will be powder and splinters. In a year, wall.

Below me a mason taps its antennae twice against a white boot-print in the salt and moves on. Weather. The road is older.

I pulse my throat again. I wait.
