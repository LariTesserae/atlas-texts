You'd find me first by heat, then by quiet. That's how everyone is found here.

At first I think I'd be up on the rim. Standing at the sheared guardrail, looking down at the whole knot at once. The visitor with loud boots. The one who still stands upright.

But I don't stay there. The light is white and brutal up there and there is nothing to do but sweat. So I go down.

On the third switchback my shirt sticks to me and stays stuck. My footsteps are too loud on the grating. My breathing is too fast. The handrail — I reach for it without thinking, at maybe eleven — brands the palm through sweat, and I learn.

So no. Not the visitor. Visitors leave.

I think then I might be something smaller, quieter. A drop. That seems right for a while. To be one of the thousands of beads gathering under a shade-cloth, fattening. To live a whole life in the time it takes to get too heavy to hold. To fall a finger's width into a bucket, tick. To be drunk, or to feed the exact black-green line of moss that knows where drinkable lives.

But a drop doesn't tend. It doesn't move the bucket a finger-width to the left when the drip wanders. It doesn't know which elbow has gone sour with metal. I want to know that. I want to be the knowing.

Lower, then. Stay low.

Hot air pools up high, but down along the sump floor the machines push a slow, cooler current if you don't block it. You'd find me where that current runs. Not in the power house with the doors always open — I'm not that steady, I'm not flywheels turning slow for years. Not up on a catwalk or ladder — standing puts your head up in the pooled heat and makes you kneel at noon.

I'm on the ground. Almost on the ground.

A kitchen chair with wood legs dragged to the exact crack between buildings where the breath funnels through. That's my place. I've learned the economy of it: crouch, shuffle, sit. Move slow. Sleep on a pallet on the concrete, not on a bunk. Keep tools at knee height. Rag in back pocket, oven mitt slit for fingers. Gloved, always.

I have pale, pruned hands, but not from swimming. From wiping. All day I wipe beads from the underside of cold-water pipes before they get too fat and fall where they shouldn't. I put my cup under the communal line. I don't tap the line. Harvest, don't take. I know to the hour which drip runs fastest.

I thought I would be lonely here without small feet, small wings. No pigeons shifting on a girder, no ants in a line, no lizard doing push-ups, no flies to stipple the basin water. Under the boilers only dust lying exactly as it fell. In the corners, dead leaves from some bush out on the pan that I will never see, lying paper-thin and unchewed because there is nothing here to chew them. I have given up sweeping them. We all do, eventually.

But I'm not lonely. I have producers for company and no consumers to interrupt us.

The crust-moss on the north wall tells me where I can drink. I check it the way you'd check a gauge — velvet from three inches away, flat as dried paint from three steps. If it is holding, I can hold. The rust-red lichen on the boiler skin, freckles grown together along a weld, likes heat the way I have learned to like shade. I don't scrape it off. It was here first, arrived as dust on air. The pipe-ferns under the cistern, roots in burlap around a weeping joint, hang limp at midday and stiffen when the evening comes. Pale, translucent, lit only by amber work-lamps. They let go of their spores and let them fall straight down into the next wet inch. No wind worth speaking of, no one to carry them. They don't ask for more. Neither do I. Along the one alley where two condensate lines sweat all day, the row of saltworts, gray-green, leaves thick as thumb knuckles, splitting at the base, nosing a runner an inch a season along the wet seam and never crossing dry stone. I crouch to see the front line. That is my whole wild census, and it is enough.

I don't talk much. Sound dies here — a dropped wrench rings once and stops, a shout across the roofs doesn't carry. I listen through my boots instead, through my molars. Low sixty-cycle hum says the flywheels are still turning. Sigh and white vent for thirty seconds says a pressure valve lifted and reseated itself. Drip, tick, creak. Tin expanding and contracting.

You'd find me plainest in late afternoon, when the amber lamps look brightest against the blue-brown shade. The desert above cools a degree. The dense hot air below sighs upward all at once, dust and steam in a slow column that almost reaches the rim before it collapses back down. The cables sway — power, guy-wire, condensate, the empty clothesline with my stiff laundry under the catwalk. The tin ticks in chorus. The beads tremble and fall together.

A small rain inside the earth.

Whoever else is on rotation — three, maybe two now, maybe just me, we never see each other together so we stop counting — we stop. Kettle humming faintly on the waste-heat plate. Gloved hand on a hot rail through a rag. Face wet. Looking up, waiting for the fall.

Then it settles. The pumps thump. The walls sweat again, drop by drop.

I'm the one who stays to wipe it up.
