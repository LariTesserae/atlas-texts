At first I thought I would stay myself in it.

A diver. A visitor. Hovering between the leaning tenements with my clumsy kick and my too-fast bubbles wobbling up, my suit leaking stupid, prodigal heat, my mask pressed to an inspection glass. I would move halo to halo, amber to green-white to orange blink, and lose each one behind me. I would watch.

I can't stay that. Nothing here can afford to be an event for long. My kick is pressure the fish scatter from. My breath is noise. My warmth is a flare that the cold kills within meters, and it costs. In ten minutes my fingers go burn to numb to nothing. In twenty my breathing sounds too loud even to me, folded up by water into my jaw. The town plays dead around that kind of aliveness. The weed holds still half a second longer than the gyre. The isopod on the roof freezes mid-step and pretends to be stone. The fish pull back into iron.

So I wait. That's the first lesson. Hover low — lower than I thought low was — down in the street-canyon where the silt has made a ramp of the stairs, where wall leans toward wall until they almost kiss overhead. Let the gyre snow me sideways. Let my heat bleed out until I stop being warm. Until I am just another weight in cold water.

Then, for a while, I think I would be a fish.

It would be good to be soft and white in the iron, to know the pipe-labyrinth the way people once knew streets. To stack fin to fin with dozens of others where the cable runs warmest, gills fanning impossibly slowly, mouth open, gut dark with silt. To feel the world as pressure, not sight. To cross open water only at night-which-is-always, a white comma in lamplight, letting food come to me.

But I am too heavy for that. Too loud. I can't learn to scatter that fast, to live inside. I need to touch more than to hide.

Then I think I would be one of the machines. The crane trolley inching along its beam, stopping, inching back. The pump breathing warm water so the intake beside it doesn't ice. The robotic arm with the missing claw, reaching, missing, reaching again to oil a bearing. To have one small gesture and do it for a hundred years. To communicate in light and low throb. To tend.

But I don't have oil. I have hunger. I have to eat.

So lower. Slower. More touching.

I find myself — really find myself — on a roof.

Not ground, because there is no ground here, only bottoms stacked on bottoms. A sawtooth factory roof, second layer down, east of the power station where the stacks breathe out and the warm water rises a few meters before the cold kills it. From above, in the amber halo, it looks snowed, silted. Up close, it is alive. Bone-colored mats in fans and tongues lift a millimeter and settle as the pulse passes. White hair-weed two meters long combs out of the gutter, fine as nerve fiber. Around the split conduit there are sulfur-yellow rosettes, iron-red crust-flowers with brittle petals. It smells — even through cold, through suit — of iron and sulfur and old rain.

I am there on too many legs.

At first only the scraping gives me away, not sound but pressure transmitted into jaw. Then the lamp resolves me: slate-grey segments gone pale at the joints, antennae testing, mouthparts working. A giant isopod. Loaf-of-bread big. Dog big. Old.

That's what becomes more itself the longer I stay in it.

My back is not clean. Nothing here is clean that lives near heat. In the grooves of my shell white weed has rooted. Mineral crust blooms between my plates. I am a garden walking on a meadow. I move a meter and stop for ten minutes to feed, and the feeding is not biting but combing — mouthparts sweeping the mat clean in a pale swath that will stay visible for decades. This is my path. I have grazed this roof so long you can read me in it, mown lawns through bone-velvet. When the lamp two meters over died last decade, I left that corner. When this vent opened, I came from three buildings away. It took me weeks. Everything here takes weeks. Hurrying is heat and heat is death out here.

Underneath me, kept at stack-temperature by my own slow metabolism, eggs glued in a warm brood pouch. I do not think about them the way you think. I carry them the way the town carries its heat: distributed, stubborn, without start. Pump breathes so intake doesn't ice. Filter opens so duct doesn't silt shut. I walk so they stay warm. Blink... breathe... grind... graze.

I feel you before I see you — because here *inside* is outside. Your clumsy kick shudders the catenary cable I am under. The fish freeze in their pipes. I tuck. Even the crane pauses mid-inch overhead, its chain load hanging motionless. And at the edge of lamplight, where halo gives out to depth-black, *it* passes on its circuit along the bowl wall — fat and pale and smooth, fin or flipper or hand trailing concrete. The one that never enters. The fish know it. I know it. We hold still until it is gone, until the slow churn resumes.

Then I move again. A meter. Stop. Comb. The bacterial mat breathes under my feet. The weed waves through my legs, warmer than water by a fraction. The lamp furred with silt brightens as I clean it, dims as silt resettles. That is my conversation with the machines that tend me while I tend the roof while the fish in the pipes eat my larvae while their bones become flowers around the vent where I feed. Nothing leaves. Silt sloughs, settles, is conveyed, dumped, grazed, excreted, snowed, filtered, breathed.

Pipe against pipe. Wall against wall. Cable against beam. Me against roof.

I thought density would be crowding. It is company. It is how we remember where we end, by touching everywhere. Stay low, stay together, stay warm. Up is colder, darker, and crushes.

If you hover long enough between the leaning walls and see a whole slow herd of us moving shoulder to shoulder across the sawtooth in amber light, antennae testing, backs gardened white — I am the third one. The one with the missing antenna on the left, the one pausing longest over the yellow rosette. Put your hand flat on the factory wall and you will feel the town working. Put your eye low to the roof and you will feel me working, too slow to hear.

I should have died when the water took this place. We all should have. Instead I learned to keep one small warm muscle flexing out of sync with all the others, just above death, quietly, in the cold.
