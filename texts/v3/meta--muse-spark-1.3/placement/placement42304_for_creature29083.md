Not at the rim.

I thought at first I would stay up there, where the sky is still bright and the plain goes on. Where you can see it all at once if the mist thins. It would be cleaner to look down and name things. But you don't stay. Down you go.

Down past the fresh break where the basalt snapped like chalk, where the slabs are still pale where they pulled away. The scree hasn't learned to be soft yet. It cuts. My feet — boots, I think they are boots at first, too loud on the shingle — slip a little on the keen edges. The air gets cooler with every step, until my breath shows even though it must be midday. Sweat never quite dries. Light pools rather than shines.

For a long while I am just walking. That's where I find myself first: on the floor, between.

Between the tarns. Those flat iron-grey saucers a few yards across, fringed with black mud, unconnected. No streams between them, just damp ground that sucks at the sole. Midges lift in a cold cloud when my foot sinks and then settle again on skin, on sedge. Around the wettest rim, the tufts — sedge, and the willows no higher than my knee, wind-combed, small hard leaves. I touch one. My hands come away cold and moist. Everywhere I touch is cold and moist.

And acres in between: nothing. Bare shingle and cracked silt. I can hear my own steps for a long time before the walls swallow them. Then the roar swallows them.

I stop trying to be heard three feet away. It becomes pressure, like wind. Shrieks and croaks and the dry clatter of wings and the clack of shells on stone. Individual calls vanish into it.

I am low saturation at first. A warm smudge moving across cold gravel. Unmarked moss — dull olive and rust — unmarked by me. A pool with no ripple. I think: nothing lives here. My boots are too loud for anything to live here.

Then a shadow crosses the gravel. Then another.

Then the air tears and the sky is full of them.

I look up because I have to. They pour off the ledges in ribbons, tens of thousands, auks and kittiwakes and big sooty ravens and others that are like them until you look twice — duskier, heavier-billed, ragged at the edge, as if the dim here had smudged the field guide. They catch the basin air and circle in a slow screaming gyre, wing to wing to wing, then settle and cover the scree in a shifting black carpet. Then lift all at once at nothing I can see, with a clatter I feel in my chest.

The cliff behind them is streaked white, vertically, the same streaks year after year. Every narrow ledge is shoulder-to-shoulder with bodies, jostling, shuffling a half-step left and right. No nests. No twig cups, no mud, no burrows. Just a warm hollow worn by breast feathers into cold rock, an egg balanced against wind.

I watch them for an hour. The roar doesn't get louder or quieter. The light doesn't rise, just grey to greyer to grey.

And my hands — my hands are cold. I keep picking things up.

That's when it sharpens. That's when I stop being the boots.

On the dry gravel in the middle of nowhere there is one flat stone. An anvil. I know it because a gull labors up thirty feet beating hard in the cold air with a grey-green crab from the tarn, and drops it — precisely there. Crack. Missed. Up again, down again, until it splits. She eats and leaves the shards and the stone where they lie.

A little farther along the seep, where condensation never quite dries at the foot of the wall, a raven hops with a short stick held crosswise. Stops at a crack. Probes. Too short. Drops it. Tries another, bites it twice to length, fishes a grub. Leaves the tool behind.

In the larger pool something rolls on its back — an otter, hammering a mussel against a rounded beach pebble kept in a fold of chest. The same pebble for days, until it's lost, until any other pebble will do.

I do that. I have done that.

I find myself with a stick crosswise — in the beak, in the hand, it stops mattering — hopping the scree, stopping at a crack. The length is wrong. I drop it. The sound of it ticking on basalt is swallowed instantly by the roar. I try another. Two bites. It fits. There is a grub, cold and white, in the dark. I eat. I leave the stick.

I am not building. I will not store it. Tomorrow a different bird will use the same anvil stone. A different raven will pick up my stick and drop it again. A crab eaten, another hatches in the tarn. A ledge vacated, another bird lands. The sedge I nibble — or the sedge around me — stays exactly itself, nibbled to equilibrium. The moss holds the damp. The damp keeps the rock cold and the cold keeps the mist.

I find myself shoulder-to-shoulder later, I think. Up on the bare rock, warming stone with my breast. Jostling. Calling without being heard. Returning because the Hollow does not ask me to stay — only to return. The wind spills over the rim and drops what it was carrying from the coast, and I go up heavy and come back heavy and manure the stone beneath me.

Come back in ten years, in a hundred, and the white streaks will be in the same places. The tarns will neither grow nor shrink. The same dropped pebble will be on the same anvil stone.

A different gull dropping a different crab. The same sound filling the same bowl of sharp, young stone under a dim sky that never clears.

That gull will be me.
