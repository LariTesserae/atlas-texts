At first I think I will stay what I came in as. That is the mistake.

I come down from the rim with boots and breath, too warm, too fast. My torch gives up three feet out. I find the driest brick like I'm told, moss upstream, and try to stand still between the facades where twenty paces should be and isn't. *Plik... plik... plik.* My swallow is the loudest thing on the street. My breath shows even when I'm not working.

I think, Naturally: I would be the fox.

Because I want to still be eyes. To slip between the open ground floors, rust-red gone green at the tips, to hunt by listening for where the dripping stops. To be thin enough to belong and still have teeth. But I am too loud for that. My joints click. My breathing condenses and returns to me and I flinch at it. The fox passes and does not look at me because I have already announced myself with my heat. No. Not the fox. I am not quiet enough to be a hunter here. The quiet here is structural.

Then I think I would be higher, lighter: one of the snowfall that never lands.

A moth the size of my open hand, precipitating through the street-gulf, shedding luminous scale for the black leaves to catch. That silence, that *furr* against a cheek. But I count. I can't help it. I count seven chimneys going one way on the mill terrace and nine coming back and the moth I watched land yesterday is still landing and I try to fix it with my eyes. Counting fixes them and they resent it. I am still trying to fix the town. Not a moth. I am too eager to arrive.

So I sink.

The cool gets into the marrow, the way they warned. It slows my swallow. My breath falls, without permission, into rhythm with the drips. And low down, belly-down, the pace finally suits me.

I would be a surveyor.

Not metaphorically. A slug as long as my forearm, the color of wet slate and old tea, patrolling the ten inches of moss over sharp, newborn shale. You learn to see by touch and by drip, and down there that is all there is. I rasp liverwort off the chapel wall, I rasp algae off iron, I rasp the shed dust the moths leave hanging in the cold air for a long while before it decides to fall sideways. Darkness and rust. I eat what the place is made of.

That becomes more itself as I stay.

I am not patrolling, exactly. I am keeping track the way the trails keep track. Behind me a wide silvered ribbon catches the orange bruise from the lamp on the rusted pole. By morning it will cross and recross the high street in a script you can almost read. Who went where in the night, who is rotting, who is ready to be re-knit. I do not count houses or moths or drips anymore. I trace. Tracing doesn't fix them.

Find me there, if you come looking: not in the gulf, not in the upper vaults where the bats stitch between burdock-doors. Low. On the stretch of moss high street between the chapel's living liverwort skin and the cellar pond two feet across where the drowned lamppost is reflected perfectly. Lift any leaf along my route and you will find my neighbors — hundreds of pale woodlice packed shoulder to shoulder, breathing together, the mortar now. They tighten when you touch them. I pass over them respectfully. They hold the courses together where brick has softened to bark.

I hear the town the way you can only hear it low down. The wire hum at 50 hertz if I put my whole length to a fallen cable. The engine house chuff, very faint, a heartwood piston in a rust cylinder taking another cold breath every few minutes. The groan as a root-beam takes up new load in the mill that is a single organism now. Above me the wrens in their bottle nests of machine lint and my own lost hair raise their young on springtails. At the edge of a bark gutter the surface explodes outward at my passing and settles again. When the fox passes, the dripping stops for a second where something large has inhaled. I stop too. We are, all together, one body noticing.

I wanted the huge blackberries shining like oil out of the mortar. I don't now. They are not for me. When one falls heavy and rolls along a downhill that isn't downhill and plops into the black-tea bathtub on the third-floor root-platform, or into the cellar sea, the blind white eels rise with open mouths. Let them. The town's way of remembering sweetness, fed back to its coldest citizens. I have learned a colder sweetness: sap when you break a stem, cold and clear and smelling of iron. Condensation on my back. Sound.

Do not follow water downhill. Water here does not know downhill yet. I follow moss.

I did scratch my initials once, on a rusted grate. The plants cannot read iron yet; they grew around it respectfully. Anything I tried to write in wood or leaf or skin came back by morning rewritten in a different hand, in sap. I stopped writing. The silver trail is enough. It dries by the time the soft rain hangs without falling, and then it is gone, and that is correct.

If you put your ear under the iron bridge that bridges no river, where the moss is thinnest, to the raw cinder and crushed brick still angular as if the world started yesterday, you will hear the whole small-vast borough working: sap lifting inches per night, roots tightening on brick, belts of mycelium tightening themselves, looms moving a half-inch per hour weaving root-hair, leaves turning almost imperceptibly to track the dead lamp.

You will hear me in it. Not loud. A rasp, a slide, a bead forming.

I am small from outside and endless from inside now. A seed. I have learned to photosynthesize darkness — not to catch light but to catch condensation and sound, to slow-rot and re-knit. Stay until your breath fogs in rhythm with the drips, they said, and then go back up before you learn it too.

I stayed.
