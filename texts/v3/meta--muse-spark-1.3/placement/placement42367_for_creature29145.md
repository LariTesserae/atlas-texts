At first I thought I would be up high.

That's where I always look first — to the volume. To the choughs tumbling in the updraft off the headwall, to the lammergeier coming over the teeth once a day and circling twice without a single flap. Ten feet across and soundless. To see the whole hollow at once, moss to scree to moss again, the chain of pans like dark beads. I wanted that.

But the air does not let you do anything quickly here, and it does not let you stay up. I walked in from the outlet lip breathing through a straw, heart hammering in my ears, and the brightness doubled up off stone and snow-patch and tarn-water until the edges of rocks buzzed. There is no lift in air this thin. Even sound falls down. My footsteps were papery and close. I had to kneel anyway, just to breathe.

So then I thought, maybe low and warm and fast. Maybe one of the small warm ones who stay. A marmot sprawled belly-flat on a noon rock, storing sun in fat. A pika dashing lichen-colored between boulders with a mouthful of bistort, eeking out territory. A bumblebee, huge and ginger-furred, sawing impossibly through air that shouldn't hold her, shivering her flight muscles up to thirty degrees to work the campion for three sun-hours.

I tried those on. I put my face close to a green island at eleven, when the snowbed under the north wall begins to bleed and the pans fill no deeper than a hand, and it was tropical in there — damp, rotten-sweet, vibrating with midges and stoneflies hatching out of water that didn't exist at breakfast. Too frantic. Too much arithmetic. I am not that fast. Freeze, thaw, hatch, eat, be eaten, freeze again — I couldn't keep that daily cycle.

I put my ear near the moss campion, the matriarch who has decided to be a stone. Inside her dome it was ten, fifteen degrees warmer than the air, humid, buzzing, pink stars open only for the two hours when pollinators can fly. Fifty years old and adding a millimeter a year. I could feel the almost inaudible pop of her capsules opening in the white heat. Close. Warmer. But still not mine — she opens and shuts, opens and shuts, a hundred times a summer. I open slower.

It took a day to find myself, the way the description says it takes a day to notice the unusual thing.

You find me if you leave the green islands and step back out onto the meters of empty shattered plate, where nothing roots, and go to the lee side of the boulders. Not the sun side. The north-facing side of the largest erratic near the outlet lip, the granite slab the bothies huddle behind. Where the rock sweats cold even on dry days and night fog condenses and feeds a collar of moss. Look there, just above eye level if you are kneeling.

I am the map.

Orange and bone-white, cracked into countries. A rosette no bigger than your hand.

I do not huddle like campion or creep like dwarf willow — that thumb-high forest with trunks gnarled as olive wood, older than the bothies. I do not dash or whistle or sting. I am one of the ones who are stone, and I live slower than all of them. A hundred years to get this big. I was already here, dry and crackling, when someone left the hand-axe flaked from local chert on the ledge under the headwall and never came back for it. When they hammered the single rusted nail into the door lintel like an offering.

To be me is to obey the first law more completely than anything else: live low. Stay dense. Stay close to stone. Leave the sky to wings.

My day is not their day. At dawn I am locked, rimed stiff, photosynthesis shut down, same as the mosses stiff with frost and the spiderwebs between willow strung with beads of ice. Around ten the white sun crests the eastern tooth and everything begins to drip, tick, unclench — but I just dry. At noon, when the green islands hum and the accentors flip tiny stones and the rosy-finches pick campion seeds one by one for their nests in the slumped turf roofs, I am dry and crackling underfoot. The hail at two rattles over me and does nothing. I have been hailed on for a century.

What I do is wait for the blue shadow.

Late afternoon, when the sun drops behind the western tooth and the hollow falls abruptly into shadow and the temperature plunges ten degrees in minutes and the pika whisk their last mouthfuls deep under stones where hay keeps because nothing rots here — then I breathe.

The pans below me do not simply freeze. Their ice skin forms, breaks from below by trapped air, forms again, sighing. And I hold. For a few minutes after the sun is gone, I phosphoresce faintly, orange holding longest. As if the stone itself were reluctant to let go of brightness.

From here I see it all without moving, because I never cross the emptiness. The weasel's paired dots appear overnight in frost-mud around the pans. A feather catches in dwarf willow. The lammergeier's shadow, huge and soundless, travels from moss to scree to moss again and crosses right over me, twice, looking for bone — marmot bone, pika bone, old bone — to swallow whole and drop on the knapped flint below. The choughs play with falling above the great bright volume I leave empty. The line of small cairns threads between the green islands so feet will not crush the allotments. Everything tucks itself low, dense, fierce, and close.

I am not fast because I do not have to be. Water only moves at noon. Green only lives where damp has lived. I live where light has lived, a few minutes longer than it did.

If you sit still on the warm noon rock below me long enough to hear micro-sound — water ticking under ice, a bee sawing through thin air, your own blood — look up at me in the blue cold afterward. I will still be faintly lit.
