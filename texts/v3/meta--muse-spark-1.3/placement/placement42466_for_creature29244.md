You'd find me at first on the rim, because everyone starts there.

Too loud. Breathing too hard from the basalt road, boots striking wrong, voice carrying too far when I say anything. The air changes and I stop — dust-heat turning to wet-heat on the skin, too-white light — and below me the bowl glistens, and I think: I can't live here. Nothing green. Nothing to hide behind. Only stone weeping water, and steam that doesn't burn off.

So I try to imagine myself down in it.

At first I think I'd be high up, to see it all. One of the white birds. A stilt standing leg-deep for hours in warm outflow, sweeping side to side. It would be simple to be that white on white, to rise all at once when startled — that sheet lifting off stone, the cloth-shaken-far-away of wings — and land again without a cry. But I'm too heavy for that. I don't know how to be silent in the air. I don't know how to hunt the edge of a moving shadow because the stone remembers light a second too long.

Maybe lower then. Lower is safer.

Maybe at knee-height, in the shimmer where the midges hang in columns. If I were small enough, a dragonfly the blue of pool water, hovering with no whir, I could talk by tilting. Flash polarized sun off my belly. No throat needed. No voice to keep down. The mirrored beetles walk the handrails to drink condensation — I could do that, be that deliberate. But I still want hands. I still want to touch the pipe to test if it's hot.

Maybe on the ground itself. One of the bone crabs, flat and bleached, shell crusted with salt until it creaks. I like that — how they come out in thousands at dusk when the flats go pink, a slow pale tide, clicking so softly you feel it in your teeth more than hear it. Waving one bleached claw: *here I am, not here*. Learning to slow at noon like everyone else does. But a crab never gets to stand on the boards and look down. A crab never gets to drink from the blue-marked tap.

And I keep coming back to the boards.

The damp boardwalks over steaming gravel. Stay on the boards, never on the orange. The bright orange is the living edge, a degree cooler than death, a year to regrow if a boot crushes it. I keep coming back to that rule, to my feet learning it before my head does.

So maybe I am not winged, not shelled. Maybe I am one of the pale ones after all, because they have learned not to stand out, and I am trying to learn that too.

You'd find me in the middle terraces, where the water is still milky-blue and too hot to step in. Where the covered aqueduct comes down off the upper springs on arches and drops ten feet into a basin — that fall that makes only a low, private sighing, as if heard through a wall — and then steps on again, cooling as it goes. There's a crossing there, boards over an open runnel, and a tap with a blue mark painted on sinter block, flaked and repainted a hundred times.

I tend that tap in the morning.

I am lean now, salt-cured looking. Loose undyed linen, long sleeves at 42 degrees because the white pan burns from below, burns the underside of your chin. The silica has filmed me — at the hairline, at the knuckles — white no washing quite removes. My teeth are still clean because I learned: drink only where the blue is. Water that has fallen three terraces, cut with roof-cistern rain. Not straight from the ground. That would fur and grip.

In the morning I clear bacterial skin from the intake with a long pole. Tangerine, mustard, copper-green — the mat tries to close over everything. It stinks faintly of eggs and iron when I lean close. I lift, and the shrimp flick in clouds underneath, fingernail-long and translucent, and the blind white fish nose along and turn a flank to catch the sun. I don't take them. Morning water still belongs to the mats and the birds.

I don't call out. I learned the hand before I learned the work, really. Raised palm. Tilt of head. Two fingers tapped against a pipe means *hot, wait*. A flick means *behind you*. The children learn it before speech, and I was a child again coming here, having to relearn. The old women at the taps — I am becoming one of them, though I'm not old — told me half-joking, not joking: noise dries the springs. So I keep my voice low, throat barely working. The vapor takes it anyway. Voices don't carry twenty feet.

At eleven I vanish with everyone else. Awnings come down. The lathes go still in the machine shops. Even the salt-rakers lay their wooden rakes down across the perfect pink ridges and lie in the thick-walled rooms up on short piers where steam breathes cool underneath. I lie there too and listen to the place enforce its rule: a single *tick* of metal expanding, then stop. My own footsteps gone. The doves the gray of steam cooing with throats that barely vibrate on the roof above me. Rest is not laziness. Even the crabs slow.

Then the lid gathers. The vapor we've been breathing all morning gets heavy until my shirt sticks and the stones sweat beads that join and run. Early afternoon, the release — warm heavy drops hissing faintly on hot stone, running immediately into the channels. For twenty minutes everything drips, patters, overflows. I stand under the awning edge and watch the mats brighten, the pools rise a finger's width.

Then sun again, steam doubling, and I wash in the evening runnels, not the morning ones. That's the etiquette.

At dusk — bright and sudden, sun dropping but stone still radiating — I don't go inside. I stay on the boards by the lower pan and watch the pale tide come in. Thousands of bone crabs. The white owls hunting by sight alone along the remembered light. The lanky silica-dusted lizard by the runnel, motionless for an hour, one liquid snap. No growl, no splash. I wave back sometimes with one hand without thinking, a bleached flash, before I catch myself.

Late afternoon the water in the closed loops sometimes climbs the screws on trapped steam alone, without pumping, as if the valley has decided to keep circulation going by itself. The engineers just let it. I like that hour best. I put my hand flat on the damp rock, warm as skin, weeping quietly, and feel the pylon line downhill humming below hearing, behind my ears.

I am not from here. Nothing green grows here and I still dream of trees, sometimes. But my feet stay on the boards. My voice stays down. I shepherd water, I don't make things. I leave room.

If you come down the basalt road, you won't notice me at first. That's the point.
