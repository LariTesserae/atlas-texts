I thought I was standing.

That was the first mistake. The heat on the back of the neck, the grit underfoot, the white-gold light coming straight down — I borrowed a human shape to hold it in, because I didn't yet know what shape I was.

Low. I am low. Not in a valley. There is no high here except what we make. The ground under me is sharp, new brick-earth, pale rust and ash, cracked in polygons big enough to lose a boot in. If I had boots. If I had feet like that.

I don't.

Let me start slower. Let it become itself.

Warm. Late morning. The air doesn't move so much as sit. The scrub around me is thigh-high if thighs were a measure — to me it is forest. Fleshy leaves fat as bodies, grey-green and olive-gold, waxy, silver-backed. This morning they drank fog. I can still taste it in the air, the ghost of that white hour when every hair beaded. By ten they turned their silver undersides out and sealed. Now, by midday, they are still as turned faces.

I am under them.

There is a hush that is not silence. If you listen straight on you hear nothing. You have to listen sideways. A papery rustle as something passes beneath leaves. A crinkle that resolves, up close, into thousands of mandibles clipping in time. A black river is moving down there along a highway swept grain by grain. Ants. They are milking beads on the stems above me — scale insects, round and still, mouths plumbed deep into sap, turning it to honeydew. I smell resin and dry oil when a stem is bruised.

I am not them. I thought for a moment I might be one of the milking ones, or one of the milked. I am close to them, brushing leaf-litter. But the column passes me without checking me, and that tells me: not ant. Not bead. Something else that uses the shade.

The ground tilts up. Or rather, it rises.

Orange. Burnt-orange clay, pocked and fluted, leaning a degree downwind. A knuckle near me, a shoulder-high further out, a house-high on the horizon. From outside they look solid. From where I am now, with my antennae tasting damp clay and spit and dung, they are not solid at all. They are breath.

I go inside.

Oh — the cool. After the white light, the dark is cool and damp and sweet-sour. Shafts rise and fall above me, air moving the way a lung moves, keeping the deep at one temperature even at noon. Somewhere below, deeper than I will ever go, there is a slow pulse, a laying drum that has not stopped in years. A queen as long as a thumb, blind and vast, pumping eggs. I have never seen her. I am what she thinks.

Around me the smell of garden: chewed leaf-pulp gone white with mold, the only crop that grows in dark. My brothers move past me, soft and pale as rice grains, dark heads and nipper jaws, carrying. Always carrying. A grain up in the damp morning, cemented, dried to brick by ten. The tip this morning was still wet when we left it.

Then light breaks where light should never be.

A blow. A hammering. Grit ticking down the outside face — I know that sound, the masons know that sound. A hornbill, black-and-white bulk, clumsy on our fine point, has punched through the flank. For a half-second the white-gold day stabs straight into the lung. Heat. Dust. Alarm taste flooding the tunnels. Workers boil. Foragers reroute without being told. The city heals without pausing because the city is us pausing never.

And this is where I become myself.

I am not a carrier. My head is too big to carry. Rust-red, huge, jaws like a gate that cannot chew, only hold. I was made for this exact hole. I run toward the light I was never meant to see — blind, I see it as heat on my face — and I put my face in it.

Now. Now I know where I am.

You would find me three feet up the west flank of the buttressed cathedral by the tamped plaza, the one whose long blue shadow will stripe the scrub by late afternoon. On the outside, if you knelt, you would see only a rust-red dot plugging a ragged puncture, jaws outward. On the inside, workers are plastering around my neck with spit-mud, sealing me in. My soft abdomen pulses in the cool dark. My head burns in the day.

I am a door.

I am a filler. We are not empty-makers. A breach is an emptiness and it cannot be allowed to stay empty. So I fill it with myself.

Outside, the plain goes on. I can feel it more than hear it, through my head: the *pfft* of a skink bolting into a crack-canyon, the hush-crinkle of a driver column crossing the avenue with green sails overhead, mongoose feet muffled in dust, a shrike clicking tower-top to tower-top. The light eats most sound. What is left vibrates through clay into my skull.

They will build around me. By evening the plaster will have hardened and I can step back and let clay be the door and be a soldier again, waiting in the dark for the next hole. Or I will stay. It doesn't matter. Another will take my place at the next breach. The colony is not an animal. It is a place that repairs itself.

Below me the root acids are already splitting the sharp brick-dust into the first whisper of soil. In a thousand years this tower will be a hill, green-capped, gardened flat, with geckos in its vents. For now it is still raw, still new, still wet at the tip in the morning.

For now, I hold the wall shut with my face, as far as the world goes.
