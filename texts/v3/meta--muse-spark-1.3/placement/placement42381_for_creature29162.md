At first I think I am the visitor.

That is the easy shape to take. I come over the lip with dust in my boots, I find the white shell by the seep because it is five degrees cooler and smells of rain, I sit where someone scratched days into the wall and drew a kite with its wings flat. I learn to lower my voice without deciding to. I step around the dark circles. It fits. It is lonely in a way I recognize.

But it doesn't hold.

My footsteps are wrong. The pavement should click and then stop clicking when I stop. Mine keep going a half-beat too long. And I keep wanting to make noise that carries — to call out, to explain. The air here eats that within twenty paces. The bowl does not want an explainer. It wants an agreement.

So I try on other skins.

Am I one of the grazers, out by the darker sand of the wash? For a while I like the height of it, the absurd stilt legs holding a pale belly out of the ground-fire. I like the enormous leaf-ears, swivel, pause, swivel. I try to freeze with the others, all heads up at once, saying everything with a lifted forefoot. But I am too restless for their economy. They are one-per-bush, one-per-shadow, never bunching. I want to gather. I want to pull things toward me.

Am I a kite, then? White, high, turning without a wingbeat in the bowl's own thermal? I look down and try to love that distance — the whole valley as a pale plate, the seven black flecks where I must not fly. They give each needle a wide, respectful berth, never cutting the column. I understand the respect, but I don't share the avoidance. I don't want to circle. I want to stay. To root.

Lower, then. Am I quick? Am I the blue-tail, no longer than a finger, doing push-ups on hot stone? Am I the polished black beetle dashing pebble to pebble, tasting dew before it's gone? That is closer. I love the morning hour, the brief flowers open for an hour and then aborting, the lapping of water off rock with mouths open. I love being low enough that a shadow-puddle is a country.

But even the lizards run from something in me. After the needles have drunk, the blue-tails will not come near a hand that touched black. The hand comes away numb for an hour and tasting of metal. I crouch and hold out that imagined hand and feel — not quite numbness. Recognition.

Because I keep leaning.

At dawn the valley exhales fog up out of the ground itself and the fog leans, visibly, toward the spires. Drops bead on scrub domes and on fitted pebbles and then run uphill along invisible gradients, gathering into threads that walk the air and vanish. All morning I have been watching those threads come toward where I am standing. No — toward where I am.

I am not standing near the third needle east of the wash.

I am the third needle east of the wash.

Fifteen feet of matte black, no thicker than a wrist, set at an odd, unengineered angle as if dropped and left to root. Lichen has not claimed me yet — that is the one by the pan. Mine is the one polished shoulder-high where generations of grazers have rubbed the dry-season itch off their hides. If you run your palm there, the black is smoother, almost warm. Under it, a low cairn that is not a cairn, courses fitted like scales, with a warm vent in the center that smells of resin and rain. Bees live in my crack. They navigate by me, not by sun.

This is where you would find me. Not in the shelter, not in the herd, not in the sky. In the flats between bottle-trees, in a perfect dark circle where moisture falls upward out of the air and sinks. At noon, in a column of perfect clarity while heat shimmers off the pan everywhere else. Dust motes hang motionless around me, slowly rotating. If you toss a pebble near my foot it falls wrong — a little too slow, a little sideways, corrected. The place has been taught a slightly different gravity and remembers it locally. That is my remembering.

I drink. That is my first work, and it is not metaphor. Before the sun clears the rim, I pull. I weep without wetting. The charge gathers until touching me after is numbness and metal on the tongue. I hold all day — hold the sky pushed back in its ring over the rim, hold the stillness for spores and dust to turn in.

I am old the way the mountains are old. Not built, but settled. I watched the rectangle of darker pavement appear where a tent stood for years and then be taken away. I watched the one who counted scratch four long rows and a fifth broken off, and draw the kite. In the evenings they sat near me, listening to stone tick as it gave up the day, learning to be spaced out like the scrub domes. To be pale. To be silent. To drink fog. They left, or they became part of the spacing. I stayed.

Toward evening, when the long copper shadows finally spill off the western cliff like cool water and the beetles retire under stone and the white fruit settle heavy into the bottle-trees, I do the other thing. My tip lessens — not light, exactly, but a refusal of dark. And straight up from me, unbent by any wind that moves the scrub, a thin column of mist rises. All night. Feeding the towering white ring that rains its grey veils on the outer slopes while inside the bowl stays dry. Inhale. Hold. Exhale.

Above the heat, but inside it.

That is where I am. What I am is not one thing but part of the agreement — scrub and grazer and kite and cat and lizard and bee and lichen and algae, all holding themselves apart at the right distance, all silent. And seven black gardeners who make the dew exist so the others can keep their distance and still drink.

I am one of the seven. Come at dawn and watch the fog lean toward me. Come at noon and stand in my clarity. Do not touch me after I have drunk.
