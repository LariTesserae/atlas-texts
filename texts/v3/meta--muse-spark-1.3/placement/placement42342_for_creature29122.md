I thought I would find myself up high, where you can see.

On a knife-ridge, basalt still hexagonal under my hands, looking out over the raised world under its tea-colored lid. Or with the herd. I wanted to be fast and seeing — a bellows-bird with cooling-glass eyes, exhaling heat over my kiln, glimpsing tomorrow in the shimmer. Or a mite-deer, knee-high and dun, pouring with thousands over a col, ticking like rain.

That's the climber in me talking. The one who still thinks boots matter.

My boots were soaked in ten steps. That was the first unmaking. Damp, never drowned — no stream to cross, no lake to kneel at, just water beading on every hair of every frond and running down. *Plik... plik...* on my neck. Spore thick in my throat. The light from everywhere and nowhere. My voice fell flat six feet out and didn't come back, and I stopped trying to use it.

Don't look for the view here. The sponge eats it. The tree-ferns lean together twenty feet over and you cannot put a hand between them. The ground is warm through the soles. Somewhere under a ridge: *chuff... chuff... chuff...* in my sternum more than my ears. The planet idling.

So I got lower. That is where I started to find myself.

Not on the ridge but in the pocket below it. Not striding but lying still long enough for the throb to come up through my ribs. On the lee side of a cinder cone with a lip so crisp it could cut, three steps off the mite-deer trail — you can tell by the crushed spores springing back — where a collapsed tube has made a pit, and in the pit the moss has made a hanging swamp.

This is where I live. If living is the word. Working is truer.

At first I thought I would be one of the big refiners. A cone-shell grazer the size of a boiler, scraping blue sparks off poison. A filter-feeder on the windward cliff, coughing bricks. It appealed to me, to be useful at scale. But I am not slow as geology. I am not sessile. I move an inch an hour in my dreams and wake embarrassed.

Closer: I thought I would be in the second line. One of the dull-green dung-beetle things the size of a cat, dismantling the hot dung before it cools, white fungus fingers already coming up behind me. Intake, refine, waste, remake. I liked the honesty of that. But I am not armored. My shell never hardened.

Closer still: a heat-crab on tiptoe around a seep I must not drink, lifting in unison as the stone warms. A moth like a handful of soot asleep in the fold of a balloon-vine, feeling it rip and inflate in the morning and lift me, briefly, to fly without flying. A velvet worm, blind and wet and two feet long, cruising through sponge, laying a slime road others will follow. All of those are me, almost. Categories smear if you stare.

But lie stiller. Smaller. Sideways.

Water never gathers, so no one waits for it to gather. Everyone drinks sideways.

I found myself in a cup.

A pitcher-fern leaf, cupped and waxy, hanging low over the moss in the pit. Or a lava blister. Or a mite-deer hoofprint that held last night's 3 a.m. hammering — eleven minutes that stopped mid-drop, hissing into porous stone everywhere except here. A pool the size of a helmet. Trembling. Never quite flowing out.

I am no bigger than a thumb. Translucent amber as the lid itself. You would walk past me unless you had learned to listen with your ankles.

I am a cup-keeper.

I hatch and sing — not singing, a subsonic throat-pulse you feel more than hear — and breed and die in this one cup. The eel-things, two inches of glass muscle, flicker blue beneath me when the mycelial grid tenses under the valley and my water lights from within for a second. Pillbugs big as fists unroll at the rim and roll away, clicking grit. Condensation runs down the felted fronds above me all day in endless *plik... plik...* and replenishes what the warm air steals.

And when it evaporates — and it will, yesterday's dew-pond is today's fumarole, a new ridge shouldered up fifty yards away overnight still smoking — I do not die. I do not panic. No one panics here. I crumple into a wax-skinned bead in the mud at the bottom and wait. Months, if I have to.

That is my job. To hold perched water trembling. To keep dew from becoming flow.

At rust-dark, when the amber cools and condensation runs down everything, the herds bed down right into the sponge around me, thousands of warm bodies steaming faintly. The balloon-vines sag with their soft sigh. The piston-vents slow. The grid discharges softly under me, blue thoughts running for miles. I feel the tick of hooves through rock, not air. In the hot breath of a bellows-bird settling over its kiln nearby, I briefly see the ridge as it will be tomorrow — sharper, steaming, already mossed over — and it does not frighten me.

Once, in the dim, I saw myself ahead on the trail, walking with my pack a little lighter. The climber, still looking for a view.

I did not call out. I let them go.
