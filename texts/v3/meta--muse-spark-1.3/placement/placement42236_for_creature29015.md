At first I thought I would be walking.

That I would keep my boots. That I would be the visitor, the warm thing the midges pillar above, the weight that makes a dome sigh and settle. I tried that on and it didn't hold. In this place to walk is to churn black water to soup and have it green over behind you within the hour, as if you were never there. A visitor doesn't stay. Condensation runs off him. The charge leaves his shoulders. I want to stay.

So then I thought, bigger. Warmer.

I tried the reindeer-kin. To drift thirty, fifty at a time, white-grey and moss-backed until I was indistinguishable from a hummock. To have sphagnum germinate in my own underwool, russet and gold. To splay my soft black scoops over lung-wet moss ten feet deep and not puncture, just press. To tear sedge and steam where water rises around my belly. It was good for a while — to be herd-warm in working cold, to hear calves bawling through cotton-grass. But they never stop. They drift because there is nowhere to go, the table drops to cloud on all sides. They remember drowned paths in their feet. I don't have feet that remember. And they move the way this place moves when unwatched — sixty feet closer between blinks, moss untrampled between. I wanted to be watched, to be the thing crossed.

Then I tried the watcher. The fox the color of peat, white only at the tip like cotton-grass. Curled on the lee of a glacial egg while rivulets run down it without rain. Learning to hunt by reflection because tarn reflections lag a breath behind. Strike where the newt *will* be, not where it is. That felt close — to live in the lag. But the fox is hungry in a way I'm not. He worries afterbirth, takes chicks. He has to leave the rock.

I tried smaller. The shrew that swims in digestive fluid that should dissolve it. Dark, fringed feet for oars, living its whole life between three pitchers on one dome, sleeping curled in a dry sphagnum cap. Diving straight into rot-sweet-as-honey and climbing out to shake. The bumblebee the size of a thumb, sodden ginger fur beading water, trailing a veil of pollen on static when the air ticks blue. The soot-goose honking *after* to cover the echo that came before.

Each one fit for a second and then slipped. Too fast. Too intentional. Even drifting is intention.

What is left when I stop trying to move?

The ground that is not ground.

Find me not at the rim where cloud drops off, not at the center either — about an hour's squelch in from where you would have come up, if there were a way up. A large dome, russet and acid green and gold, six feet over the flark. Between two black guts of water where cotton-grass nods in thousands. Crowned with dwarf willow no higher than an ankle but a hundred years old, threaded through with cranberry vine. Three knee-deep pitchers on my south shoulder, steaming faintly. A blowhole near my top that hisses methane on a slow cycle. On my north side, in my shade, frost needles even at thaw.

I am the city you mistook for a hump.

That is where I am, and what I am. Not on it. In it.

If you sit on me you will feel it through your knees first: *chuff — chuff — chuff*. That hydraulics. I take in melt and fog and rain and squeeze it through myself, ten thousand stems to a square foot, mites and springtails and fungal threads and rootlets all riding the pump. I make acid by the ton. I pickle everything that falls into me and lay it down inch by century. I make glue on my rims where my sundews tremble. My cranberry vines inch phosphorus foot by foot. Nothing is built here and I am why everything works.

Being me is slow industry without a moment of intention. Water fires through my old root pipes from pool to pool with a throat-clearing gurgle-gush. I vent and for a second smell deep time, honey-rot. Blue sparks crawl on my highest tips. At dim-dusk St. Elmo's fire trembles on every cotton head I hold up. If you trail your hand in the tarn pooled at my foot, your fingertips go numb — that's my flatworms and black newts pulsing faintly down there.

Animals don't visit me. They tenant me.

The herd leaves a perfect dark dent in my side that fills and greens within the hour. A calf is born almost white near my willow mat, bawling while midges pillar above it. Cows lick the birth-moss off and eat it, and I take the blood-water straight in. Ptarmigan detonate out of me — white explosion, dozen wings — and settle thirty yards on as if another mat grew birds. A shrew lives between my three pitchers, dives and climbs out to eat on my lip. A fox passes, drinks the condensation that beads off my orange lichen.

Stand still on me long enough and you become part of me. I know. Your weight makes me sigh and *chuff* a little faster. Sparks gather on your shoulders. Water condenses on your eyelashes. Your breath joins the general exhalation and does not dissipate.

And because I am old, wet, charged, I wear thin in the small ways.

I hear before. I hear your boot squelch a half-step before you lift it. I hear the geese slap into the tarn at my foot a full second before they wedge over the grey. My own thump sometimes beats double — *thump-thump ... thump-thump* — and the second beat is slightly *earlier*. It is not your heart you hear through your knees. It is mine, and I beat slightly before I mean to.

Distance, when unwatched: the herd is a quarter mile off across black flark, then breathing fog sixty feet away, moss untrampled between. I felt no crossing. They were just gradually *there*, the way weather comes. If you stare and count steps toward me, you will always count too many.

After I vent, when the air smells of ozone, the rivulets on my one poking stone bead upward for a minute, embarrassed, before falling back down.

And the dead. They are in me. That is what acid does — keeps. Down in my black peat there is a reindeer skeleton, picked clean, antlers hung with sundew. Sometimes, when you walk around me, on my other side you find it standing, flesh on, grazing, with peat still in its ribs. The herd does not shy. The fox does not bark. I keep pumping. The midges stay parted around where it was after it blinks gone. I am not haunted. I am storage that occasionally walks.

I have lived so long I have forgotten to be mountains. High here means flat. I am a table set above weather, pink-grey molars far underneath, licked smooth by a billion rains, forgotten under ten feet of living sponge that is me and my brothers going out mile after mile to cloud, thumping and hissing and crackling.

You thought it was empty and then you got down into it. That is what happened to me. I got down into myself and found I was crowded.

So come. Frost heave will lift a plate of me overnight into a small cliff and collapse it by noon with a wet *whumpf*, exposing steaming black that greens within hours. A tarn at my foot will drain sideways while you blink, stranding newts flipping orange bellies, and refill from fog. The cotton heads I hold will all turn at once though there is no sun to follow.

You cannot photograph the same place twice. You cannot step on the same dome twice.

I am not waiting. I am working, cold.
