At first I came in too loud.

That's how I know I am not, yet, from here. My boots crunch too loud on the pavement. My breath comes short and fast in the thin, warm air and my heart is absurdly audible. I stand when I should crouch. I look for movement when I should look for stillness.

So I crouch. I put my hands on stone that has been handled for a million years until it has no sharpness left, and I wait until my breathing slows enough to hear underneath it.

I thought, for a while, I would be the hawk. It would be easy — to stay high and clean of it, to circle where the air is cooler, to watch the whole town-sized pan of pits and rings and hollow boulders laid out below. But the hawk is hunger with nothing to eat but patience. It waits all day for one mistake, one straggler caught between shades at noon. I don't have that kind of hunger. I don't want to live outside the weight of the place.

I thought I might be the lizard. Mottled, quick-flicking between shells. But I am not quick. Everything here has taught me slowness is water, and my quickness would dry me out. The lizard lives in the gaps the crabs allow. I want to live in the center of the allowing.

I thought I might be the cushion plant, wedged like a moss-covered fist into a crack, or the bottle succulent holding morning drip waxy in its knee-high trunk. That stillness appeals to me. But plants here are punctuation. They wait where they are put. I want to follow the memory of moisture, to walk the faint polished highways ten meters a day toward where water paused last year.

I am lower. Paler. Closer to the ground.

Find me, then, not standing. Find me tucked shoulder-in around the lip of one of the deep ones — not the deepest house-sized bathtub, but the third one over, the one whose lime slope is dark halfway down with a white tidemark above it. Morning fog has just spilled over the rim. For two hours everything drips. Tick, tick in the hollows. Beads run down every pebble into the pit. I am there with the others, stacked like dishes in the cool undercut.

I am not the thumbnail young in the damp center of the dew-trap, drinking through the tufts on my legs, shoulder to shoulder and still soft with newness. I have done that. I remember the wick-pull of water up setae, drinking off each other's legs at dawn.

I am not the clean-backed middle walker either, though I was that for a long, long time — amber, wary, trundling the decades-long circuit around the basin, piling rubble grain by grain into a low ring a foot high and six feet across so it would condense fog just a little faster than flat stone.

I am older now. Heavier. That is what emerges if I stay with it.

My joints are stiff with lime. I do not so much walk as heave, deliberately. My carapace is matte, pitted by sun, dun as the pavement, and you would mistake me for a pebble or a small boulder if I did not, every so often, shift a leg a centimeter and send a shiver outward through the field.

On my back is my garden.

The map-lichens came first, black and orange, spreading their millimeter-a-decade script until they thickened into turf. Then dust settled in it, and spores, and now there is a true skin of algae and fungus up there, and in it, tiny mites and springtails grazing. When the small lizard comes — and she comes most afternoons — she picks delicately around my eyestalks. She does not want me. What would be the point of cracking a helmet for a sip of salt water? She wants the mites. I sit patient as stone and let her clean me. A cleaning station that walks.

That is my place in the stack: on the outside. The big old ones on the outside, forming a scaly windbreak. In the middle, the soft ones, the just-molted, pale as damp paper, their flank plates fluttering as their gill chambers work the damp air. I shield them from wind and sun. Every so often there is a dry tick of chitin on chitin as someone resettles, a soft scrape of claw on lime. In the water below us, a *plip* as one of us lowers herself down to sit for hours, belly in the shallows, simply wetting her gills.

I know the rules because they are written on my body:

Stay low and pale or dry out. I am low. I am pale.

Move at night and dawn or not at all. By late morning I have gone still, sealed in. By late afternoon the pits glint and I hold my breath with the plain until morning.

Water is not a flow but a pause — find where it pauses and wait with it. I have walked my circuit for decades to learn where it pauses.

Eat everything twice. I have eaten my own molt within hours of shedding it, the soft hunger for calcium sharper than thirst. Calcium is the currency here, more precious than water, because to live two thousand meters above any tide I must make my own sea inside my shell and never spill it. Beside my ring is the white midden, the heaped claws and molts broken down to lime for the next building. Nothing is wasted.

When I finally stop — not today, but some noon in the shade of a tafoni dome, a hollow boulder that sounds empty when tapped — I will not disappear. Wind will sand me. Lime will film me. Lichen will overgrow me. Juveniles will pick the last calcium from my joints and carry it away in their own bodies to build the next ring, until crab and geology are the same thing. Below me in the slabs are ammonites weathered in relief, my distant cousins. On the hillside that was once a cliff are ripple marks frozen mid-ripple, the memory of the water I still carry inside.

For now, evening is coming. The fog is gone. The warm stone gives back its heat. The plain extends past walking, pits and rings and living gravel, ringed by low, utterly worn hills. No chorus. Only wind smoothing over worn stone with nothing to catch it, and underneath, breathing.

I close with the thousand tiny closings. I hold until morning.
