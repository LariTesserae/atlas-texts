At first I think I would be standing. In the middle, where you can see all the edges at once. A visitor with beaded eyelashes and white breath that won't leave. Just watching.

Then I think, no. I wouldn't stay standing for long.

The air is too thin for standing. The chord gets in the teeth. The loudness doesn't get louder or quieter, it just is, and after a while you don't want to be the one warm thing doing nothing with its warmth. You want a job. The place hires your breath before you agree to it — hangs it, then takes it all at once for the ceiling. It will hire the rest of you if you let it.

So where would I go, if I could choose where to be put to work?

At first I think the birds. The conveyor. Up the east wall, across, down the west wall, across. To see the whole room-animal from above, to drag the damp air up with my own wings and feel it bead. But I know myself. I would want to leave the loop. I would look at the black jagged rim, glassy where it broke, and want to go over. The ptarmigan-things never do. A blood cell doesn't get curious about outside the body. I would. So not that.

Then I think the whistlers. One to a rock. I like the idea of having a rock — that shard is mine, that posture is mine, you could memorize me by it hours later. Pumping my chest, bellows, bellows, holding a saw-bite scream until it locks. A reference tone. But I couldn't hold it. I bend too easily. I would go hoarse, I would flinch when the turbine whine lags half a second behind my turn and clicks to catch up. I don't want to be the governor. I don't want to be responsible for keeping everything young.

The furnaces, then? The twenty green ones. Five feet high and ten across, steaming faintly, 99 degrees in a 40-degree world. To be warmest in the coldest place. To be pelted in moss and wet leather liverwort and heavy ferns, to churn inside and never finish digesting. For a minute that feels right — to be a heart, banked. But a furnace doesn't spread. It stays where it is banked. It doesn't get bigger or smaller. I am not that steady. I would either cool or cook. I need hands to tend me.

The midges? The moths? To hang in a white column between the islands, humming one pitch so perfectly the air sews itself tight enough to catch a falling drop an inch below a fern tip. To flap in place over a black mirror the size of a door without ever landing, rowing the air so inflow equals outflow and the level never rises, never falls. I can't. I drift. I get eaten down. I get tired and want to land.

I keep coming back down to the domes. Not inside them. On them.

Find me there. Not in the center where the sound is at equilibrium, not on the rim where the wind shears the needle-eyes into a bearing-whine. Between. On the third mound from the east wall, the one with the pool like a bed to its north. Halfway up the six-inch climb.

I would be one of the copper ones.

A beetle. Black and copper, sparks crawling on my back, elytra clicking like type-keys, like rivet guns. Going down into the sodden underside where water pools against hot moss and threatens to drown the fermentation, taking a droplet in my mandibles, climbing up through the labyrinth of stems, out into the crackling air, and setting it on a drip-tip at the top. Then going back down for another while another takes the one I left and carries it back down. Up, down. Up, down.

That's where I would find myself because that's what I already do — carry a little bucket of temperature from where there is too much to where there is too little, thousands of times a minute, so the inside never cooks and the outside never cools. Shoveling coal. Tending. Not making anything. Not delivering anything. Keeping.

My back would crackle blue. My mandibles would be full and cold and smelling of iron and hot oil. My route would be six inches long and I would know it completely after an hour and completely still need to walk it. The pile would never get bigger. The pile would never get smaller.

And when I wore out — and I would wear out — another would come up out of the warm dark already knowing the route. The crew changes. The shift doesn't.

That's who I would be in a body that refuses to age. Not the heart, not the choir, not the circulation, not the scream that tunes it. The hands.
