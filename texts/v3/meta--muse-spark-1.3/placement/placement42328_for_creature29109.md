I wouldn't be up high.

At first I thought I would — in the curtain, hanging. Because that's where you look first. Tens of thousands of them in the light shafts, clear as nothing with those two hard red points, beating their swimmerets one-two-three, slow enough to count. Snapping. Strobing. It looks like flying. It looks like freedom.

But I can't hold station like that. I try, in my mind, to hang there and taste the iron coming up and the snow coming down, and I drift. The doughnut wants me. It lifts a meter off the stacks and slumps outward and down, and I would be carried down and out with it. And the snapping — up close it wouldn't be background. It would be inside my body. Millions per minute, felt in the sternum. I don't have the mirrored bladder for that, the pinhead bubble that flashes on-off, on-off. I don't speak pressure. In that white-out my eyes would be useless.

So not a shrimp.

Then I thought lower, but open. A mouth on the high terrace. It would be simple to be paved shoulder to shoulder, column deformed to hexagon by your neighbors, tentacles open like slow lungs. First pass of the plume. Milky stone-milk falling right into your lips. Inhale over half a minute, close like an eyelid. Taste metal the way you taste salt.

But I can't be that patient, that exposed. To be white in noon-bright sun with no hiding, lips always in the tiny lightning where iron drops out of solution. To never close all the way, to never move. I need a crack. I need shade.

Lower still. The pistons.

I tried that too — being a crown of red gills in a white tube, frost-thicket on the second ledge. Extension, twist, retraction. Out of phase with all my neighbors so the whole ledge ripples. Combing the second-pass water, thinner and cooler and heavy with the bacteria that eat sulfur and breathe electricity. It is beautiful work, timed to the doughnut. But at the first shadow I would vanish with that soft hydraulic sigh, all of us at once, tubes empty. And then have to relearn courage one by one. I don't want to disappear that completely.

No. If I stay — and to live here you have to stay, because the rim teeth keep the bowl separate and outside the mountain falls away into dark empty water where there is nothing for a mouth that needs both sun-snow from above and stone-milk from below — if I stay, I sink all the way to where touching is mandatory.

I would be wedged.

Find me on the lower terrace, under the tube thicket, in the shade of an overhang where the brachiopods stack like dishes, shell on shell. In a splinter of quenched lava, black glass still with the rope-swirl frozen in it, sharp enough to shred anything soft. I fit because I fold. Legs like jackknives tucked under a flat white disc. Blind.

Blind in full sun. That took me longest to understand. Light here is for timing, not for seeing. Fifteen meters down the pillars still fall straight to stone, noon-bright, throwing wave shadows across the whole crowded room. But in a snowstorm of larvae and molts and mineral smoke, in a bowl that rings two, three times off sharp walls before a snap escapes, sight is noise. So I gave it up. I have no eyes. Only edge.

My carapace is a toothed gear. Precise marginal teeth all the way around. When another of me meets me on the ledge — and we always meet, there is no space to fight for, crowding is the rule — we don't fight. We mesh. Edge to edge, tooth to tooth, we turn a quarter-turn against each other and separate. It is how we test film, how we exchange charge orientation. A greeting made of gears. Click, turn, release. So slow it takes a minute.

That's my map. Touch.

The water is cool, that high-mountain-lake cool that wakes you, cool on skin and in mouth. It tastes faintly of blood and batteries. If I lift my mouthparts — and I lift them over the course of an hour, not a second — I can taste the shimmer coming off the stacks three meters away. Five or six rusted-iron chimneys breathing cool milky fluid that doesn't roar, just billows and stalls and sinks outward. Iron and sulfur precipitating in endless tiny lightning. The anemones get first pass up high. The worms get second. I get fallout.

I scuttle — slowly, sideways, like a forklift — to clean it. But mostly I polish.

There is a patch of black glass no bigger than a coin in front of my crack. I have been working it. Mouthparts moving, moving, licking the battery clean. I'm not eating rock. I'm farming the skin on it: iron-oxidizers laying themselves down as rust, living off the charge. By licking it I keep it running. The patch shines now like a gauge. A little electrode. A reference point in the electric field we all navigate by.

That is my work. Not fast, not free, not separate. The factory floor hisses and clanks and grinds over me — sea inhaling through rim notches, slabs shifting a millimeter, shrimp rivet-gunning in my sternum — and I piston nothing, I flash nothing, I only mesh and polish, mesh and polish.

Larvae rise in the plume above me and sink outward and try to ride the surge out and get pushed back by incoming snow. Eggs, molts, dead swimmerets — all recirculated. The bowl keeps its children. So do I. To get room something has to die. I wait.

Stay long enough and you stop hunting for green or for speed. I have. I am white on black, gear in a crack, blind and touching and tuned to stay.
