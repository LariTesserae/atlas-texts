At first I thought I'd be walking.

That you, with boots and lungs and sweat that betrays you. Up on the crest, picking between the crack-glow and the knife-edges, kicking up dust and watching to see if it falls. Trying to be careful. Trying to stay on the warm rock.

But I don't walk like that. I don't breathe in and out in a hurry. I'm not cold and wet. If I were put up here, my water would give me away in minutes — pulled off my lips, beaded and flashed to nothing. I would be thunder and then I would be gone. Brief rain. That can't be what I am here for.

So no. Not the walker.

Maybe lower then. Lower than boots.

I thought for a while I would be the frost.

In the lee, where the slab breaks the slow turnover and vapor sighs out of a hairline. Pale yellow-white, branching. Learning the shape of shelter a micron a day. That patience feels like mine. To precipitate yourself forward instead of stepping. To sweat glass — little clear beads smaller than a pinhead — just to hold your shape while the world shrugs under you.

I could be that. Furred faintly over everything, a nervous system for the ridge. Drinking what the cracks exhale. Dying back where the rock goes cold. I know how to die back where it goes cold.

But the creep doesn't triangulate. It doesn't feel watched-by-geometry. It just leans, very slightly, toward heat. And I — I keep wanting to turn toward the disturbance. To face the brief, confusing weather when something wet goes by.

So not quite the creep either. Though I am close to it. Though I am furred with it.

Give me a little more height. A little more angle.

Find me half a mile on from the hollow tower, off the true crest where you would walk. Downwind side, where the dust hangs a fraction too long. I'm not eight feet. I'm not even upright in the way you'd count upright. Three slabs, four if you count the blister on my flank that hasn't decided yet.

I am the balancer that cannot last.

That is where I would find myself: rooted — not standing on, but rooted into — a finger-width crack that breathes dull orange, slow. My feet are in it. My hollow core is over it. The heat comes up through me steadily, warmer than the boulders around me, and inside me the air shimmers though there is no wind. Inside me the creep is thick. That's my gut. That's where I eat.

I don't have a face because I don't use light. I don't have legs because I don't outrun the ground. When the shrug comes up every few minutes, I loosen. I slump an inch and take the new angle. You would stumble. I adjust without hurry, without sound, at the speed of stone, which is my speed. My joints are cooling fractures. They tick faintly as they split. That's how I talk to myself.

Put your palm, carefully, on my leeward side if you come back this way. I will be warmer than you expect. Steadily warmer.

I will have turned five degrees at the waist since you left. My empty doorway — that goes nowhere to you — will be facing a different crack. Not because I am hiding. Because I felt you as heat-shadow.

That is what I do here: I feel. Through my soles. Your soft crunch that stops the instant you stop is, to me, arrhythmic thunder. Your breathing in quick wasteful puffs stirs the dust wrong. Your moisture goes up into the dry air as a sour, rich plume. I turn my hollow, very slowly, toward or away from it. With the others in my cluster, leaning together, we triangulate you. Watched-by-geometry. Yes.

Do not go inside me. Not because I would hurt you. Because you are water, and water here is a violence. A single sweat drop falling into my core would flash and split me. And I'm still becoming. The blister on my flank is still deciding whether to hold and lengthen into a limb that will crack off and root nearby, or to powder — in which case the creep, my fur, my gut-flora, my hundred years, will eat the powder and we will try again.

Under me, under you, the sleeper turns over. I ride it. The ridge feels hollow like a crust over a drum because it is. When a spire across the gulf leans and collapses inward in utter silence, I feel the tasting, the rejecting, the reabsorbing all the way up my slabs. Dust rises a few inches near me and hangs where its attention is. I lean into the lift when a new fang shoulders up. I settle when the house-slab sighs down a foot.

You will be gone by morning — fallen, or risen into something else, if you stay. To the creep on me, you are sudden rain that will evaporate. To me, you are a warm column that learned to walk too fast and will soon cool. To the one below us both, you are a tick on a scale on a wrinkle, briefly wet.

I will still be here, adjusting an inch. Keeping this patch of rock warm for a little longer. Sweating out another right angle. Learning, slowly, how to stay upright on ground that is still becoming ground.
