At first I thought I would be up high.

Not the highest — there's no single tower to stand on here, no overlook — but somewhere with a view. A funicular tower with a dovecote crown, maybe. I liked the idea of being the one who sees the bird-rivers come in and can say, there, that's the evening. I would wear the hat. I would learn the *tsu-tsu-tsu* for the cavies.

That didn't hold. It's too clean a picture. You don't get to watch this city. You get to be inside it, half-heard, moving along a channel like water.

So then I thought, lower. In the pipes. I am good at pipes, at running warm darks and finding where it drips sweet. I thought I would be a wire-rat, lean and clever and almost hairless from heat, or at least the old woman who listens for them. Put my ear to the dye-works main and tell by the squeaking whether it's running hot. Leave a saucer out. Be useful by being small and unnoticed.

That's closer. But it's still a job with a name. Stay here a week and the names start to dissolve.

Let me come up it slowly, the way you have to.

I would not be new for long. A day of toweling my neck, two days of trying to stride and almost slipping on basalt slick with algae and guano-wash, three days of shouting to be heard over shift-change before I learn what everyone learns: the birds are louder than you. Pull your hat down. Wait. Use hands.

Where would you find me, then, after the toweling stops?

Not on the flats where the plazas flood with cavies at feeding hour — I am too slow for that kind of tenderness, I would step wrong. Not on the factory roofs at dawn with the guano-scrapers, though I envy them their book soft as cloth from steam. My knees wouldn't take the towers.

Find me in the middle layer. Where a street bridge crosses under a pipe bridge crosses over a water channel, where everything drips onto everything else. Where the walls are built double.

I think I would live in a weep-cavity.

Not in it, exactly. Nobody lives *in* a weep-cavity — the wire-rats would object, and they'd be right. But beside it. Tending it. I would be the one in the tarred smock gone green-black at the folds, with brass-toed boots because I work wet, with a hooked stick worn to a shine at the tip.

A cistern-man, but not a good, silent, barefoot one who knows every throat by its song. An apprentice at forty, at fifty. The one who is still learning to listen.

In the morning, after the bird-rivers go out and the light is still white-gold and hard before the lid comes down, I sweep my roof. Because you sweep your roof for the cistern-men, and I am one, so I sweep it for the others downstream. Starch-dust, moth scales, a night's whitewash, a drowned moth the size of my hand. I push it all to the rill and let the street take it. My hat is already spattered. My skin shines.

Then I walk. Ambling, clog-shod, weight low, eyes half-down for weep-holes and cavy runs. I clear grates. That's all. It's not a metaphor. A grate choked with moth-fur and paper the goats didn't finish eating, a downspout of hollowed lava ticking, a double wall crying in sheets on a humid afternoon. I hook, I lift, I lay the mat of rot aside for the harvesters' sacks. The compost people nod — pink-armed, smelling faintly sweet and corrupt — and their children play marbles with empty cocoons at my feet.

At noon the steam columns go straight up fifty meters and flatten. The mill moths lift in a choking blizzard when a valve blows and settle again on my shoulders, breathing together. I don't brush them off. Why spend energy. They will return by dusk to the lamps. I have the cough already, the soft wheezing they call moth-chest. I wear it proudly, stupidly.

In the afternoon the lid comes down and sweats. The gutters sing. Paper boats go by from children upstream. Some get caught at my grate and I set them through gently, because someone downstream is waiting.

This is where the image sharpens, if it sharpens anywhere: dusk.

The cloud pinks. The white birds come home in a river so dense it dims the sun for minutes, *kree-kree-kree* until it is a sheet of sound. Underneath, going the other way, the bats pour out of the expansion joints in the aqueduct, stitching the pink steam. The midges rise off the flumes. The cavies pour from under walls along paths their great-grandmothers wore shin-deep into thresholds.

And I stand still. Broom in hand. Hooked stick over shoulder. Doing nothing, which is the etiquette.

Something lands near me — always something does — a rock cavy with a split ear, a white bird with mud on its beak, a moth dusting my sleeve grey. It looks at me with complete indifference. And I look back the same way.

That's what I would be, in the end. Not master, not watcher exactly, not priest. One more large, slow, tool-using species that has negotiated terms.

An animal that keeps water going where it wants, only suggesting. An animal that has been rounded and whitewashed and made patient by warm stone. An animal whose threshold has a small hollow worn in it where the cavies run, and who steps aside for a nursing doe rather than shoo her, not from kindness but from traffic law.

If you came up the road after a day and a half of climbing through weeping cuttings, you wouldn't notice me. You'd hear whistles and wings and water under everything, and see a woman with flour-white arms cross with a basket, and a child dart after a boat. Then, if your eye learned the pace, you'd see me clearing the same grate my grandfather cleared, oiling a knock I know like a relative's cough, mending, shimming, re-pointing.

Stay a year, and I would nod to you once.
