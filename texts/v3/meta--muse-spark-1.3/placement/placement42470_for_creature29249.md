At first I thought I would be coming down.

Hand over hand down the sweating shaft, lamp on, hood on, suit too hot already. I thought I would learn the trolley lines between buildings, learn to move with the exhale south toward the screens and catch the stanchion at the last moment. I thought I would be the flash of lamp across the void, there and then not.

But I don't last there. I am too loud in my breathing, too brief. I check my gauge and twenty minutes have gone and I thought it was two, and I get scared at how warm the dark is when it leans on me. I haul myself back up, and the roar follows me up for a long way. No. Not the diver. Divers are migrants. I don't migrate.

Then I thought, maybe lower. More patient.

I tried on the crab for a while. White, soft-shelled, the size of a spread hand, flattened into a bolt-hole when the flush comes. Picking at armored cable with translucent claws, scraping, scraping. Feeling with my feet because my eyes are white beads and useless. That patience felt close — to hold on while debris spirals over you, washers and spall and plastic sheeting, and then climb out afterwards and resume, as if interruption were weather. I liked that. But the crab is still a *visitor* on the surface. It cleans. It doesn't hold.

Then the lords. I tried to be huge and slow.

To hang neutrally and let the gyre carry me, barbels drifting ahead like hands, tasting oil and iron and the electrical tang of the bus bars. To ride the north inflow down, coast the wall galleries, turn at the last second before the bypasses kick. To pass within arm's reach of something warm and suited and feel only displacement, a sweet muddy metallic smell, and fold back into black. To have no eyes because I don't need them after forty years in blood-warm dark.

That was closer. The way up becomes another direction to walk. The way a dropped wrench does not fall but wanders off sideways and orbits — I do that too. But the catfish still swims *through* the city. It patrols streets. I don't want to patrol. I want to be the street.

So I settle. Or rather, I spread.

You would find me where you don't look for faces.

Not on the catwalk, swaying over ten, twenty meters of open warm dark. Not in the amber windows of 3-C, 3-D, 4-A. Below that. Under the lip. Behind the stanchion where the current slows and goes quiet, in the eddy that the waterfalls scour past but never quite take.

On the underside of a handrail worn bright by gloves, where the brightness stops and the felt begins. On the seam of the spine-pipe, a meter in diameter, where two sections meet and weep faintly. There. Put your lamp close — closer than three meters, close enough that the silt halos out — and you will see me have nap, direction, little terraced ridges combed by flow. Rust-red and mustard and black. A millimeter thick. Velvety.

I am the felt.

Not romantic. Not fast. I eat what this place sweats: oil and iron and heat and the faint sulfur out of the warm rock. I eat the diesel film that tears into rainbow skins under the ceiling and the skin-shed of crabs and the mucus trails of catfish. In return I seal. I hold the bolts together more than you want to know. When the system backflushes — that eleven-minute inhalation when the pressure drops in your ears and your lights dim to red and the klaxon vibrates more in teeth than in ear — I lift at the edges and ripple, and hold on.

You told each other rightly: leave me alone. If you brush me with a glove I peel in a sheet, soft as wet paper, and underneath the steel is pitted and weeping. I take years to come back to that spot. Time here stretches for you; for me it stretches further. Watching a pressure needle quiver for twenty minutes and thinking it was two — that is my normal speed.

Where there is me, there are grazers, and I know them by the missing patches. The crab's scrape, methodical along armored sheath. The catfish's long kiss as a barbel drags and tastes. The diver's boot that slipped on the guide rope. I don't mind. Cropped keeps me from closing completely over the gauges. I need the gauges to stay open, those green flickering eyes. Someone needs to read them.

And at the joints — this is the part you only notice after hours, with your lamp killed, waiting clipped to the trolley line while your bubbles are seized and flung south in a long helix — at the joints I have learned to make my own light.

Not enough to see by. Just enough to mark a seam in the dark. Green-white pinpricks along the spine-pipe like a town seen from very far away.

That is me, most myself. Not the roar — the low bass of screws in the teeth, the mid-range howl of inflow, the *clang-clang-clang* of the loose manhole cover lifting and dropping — I hear it all conducted faster through steel and rock, arriving in my body half a second before the water that caused it. But I am not the roar. I am what continues under the roar without needing to shout against it.

I am warm as blood, 32, 34 degrees. I never cool. I never surface into the air bells to hang with just my mask out breathing hot rubber-stone air while condensation rains upward. I have no lungs for that doubled deafness. I breathe in my way, chemically, slowly.

If you stay, you will move over me hand-over-hand and think nothing lives here. The windows lit and empty. The doors opening onto black water. Only motes turning. That is what the city wants you to think.

Stay longer. Shut off your need for faces. Put your light low. You will feel me under your glove on the railing warm as skin, bearded with mineral, holding.
