I don't find it so much as drop into it — on my backside, boots braced, through the kink that forces me onto my stomach. I thought I would arrive with an idea of who to be. I don't.

At first I think I will be loud. Newcomer-loud. Hands over my ears at the drip-roar, boot-scrape coming back to me half a second later slightly larger. My jacket darkens at the shoulders and never dries. My breath makes a small cloud that doesn't dissipate. I shout a little to be heard over the weeping ceiling and everyone tilts their head that listening tilt, watching my mouth more than my eyes.

So I try on work to hide inside.

I think I will be the knapper in the doorway, squatting on my heels with a chert nodule between my knees, tick tick tick with the antler tine. But my hands are too soft. They haven't learned the map of old cuts yet, and the flakes fly wrong. The dome throws every tick back at me.

I think I will be the woman with the copper, cold-hammering those dull pink blebs on the flat anvil stone, tap tap tap into a hook, an awl. Patient without fire. But I keep wanting to improve it, to make it sleeker, newer — and here nothing is improved. Everything is kept. My tap comes too fast.

I think, maybe smaller, more hidden: I will be one of the true natives. A blind white fish, two inches long, hanging motionless under the pile platform and then darting to take a fallen drip. To be eaten sparingly, more as rite than sustenance. To need nothing from above. That appeals to me, the practiced stillness, the dimple and swallowed tick and then flatness again, refusing to remember. Or the crab the color of candle wax, deliberate as a monk along the waterline at night. Or the pale cricket on the ceiling curtain, impossibly leggy, high above the smoke, feeling its way where no one looks.

But I am too warm for that. I smell of wet rock and tallow and crowded sleep already. I am a warm body pressed to other warm bodies in cool air that never warms. No. I am one of the crowded ones. I have to be.

Closer. I learn the stoop.

Nobody told me to, my joints told me. You can't stand tall on the middle terrace without putting your head straight into the smoke — that blue-grey layer about head-height over the water, eye-stinging, never thickening, never clearing. So I go soft in the knees, neck bent, shoulders rolled forward. A glide-crouch. The steady cool, not bitter, just autumn-shade cool, gets into the joints and stays there. Downhill I sidle. Uphill I press flat. Water-carriers always have the right of way and I press flatter still, cheek almost to the wet limestone, smelling rain that fell a month ago.

Closer still. I learn the deafness.

Neighbors who can hear each other swallow have to learn it. At first every cough in the night, every child crying bounced up and around the egg and back, every low talk made roar, snags me. I comment. I look. Then I learn what privacy is here. Not a wall — there are no walls thick enough when the roof of one swallow-nest house is the floor of the next — privacy is inattention, practiced kindly. I learn to lip-read as much as listen. To enunciate with my whole face. My eyes narrow, patient. They resolve a face at five paces and don't bother beyond ten, because beyond ten there is only gloaming and mist. Like everyone else, I stop trying to see the crown clearly.

You would find me, then, not at the dramatic places. Not on the plaited bridge over the narrowest water, not climbing the ladders of black-greased bone. You would find me on the middle terrace, third door from the lower ladder, where the six-inch ledge widens just enough for two jars and a stone bowl.

That is my address. That is my work.

I tend a cup of light. A tallow dip in a chipped stone bowl, wick of twisted hair, burning rendered fat from the rare catch. It gutters in the damp constantly and has to be coaxed, trimmed, shielded with a cupped hand. It makes more smoke than light. All of ours do. I tend it anyway, because shapes resolve at ten paces and faces at five, and without these little smokes the terraces would fall into their own shadows entirely.

And I twist cordage while I tend it. Hair and sinew and the long pale fibers dredged from the pool, rolled on my thigh into line, into net, into mend. Everything small, mended, mended again. My bowl has three generations of drill-holes along a crack, laced shut with copper wire. I know exactly which seep will fill my jar by nightfall and which one never will. The children learned the flutes the way other children learn streets, and now so have I: that overhang always fattens then drops on the left side. That vertical sheen crawls sideways before it falls.

In the morning — though there is no morning, only a paling of that bruised grey disc in the throat — I go down with the old women to hear the stick dipped. Still even. Seep equals drink equals evaporation. In equals out. Between the pale bathtub ring above and the dark stain below, the black water sits exactly where it sat for their grandmothers' grandmothers. I repeat it with the others, not because I doubted, but because saying it keeps it.

That is who I have become. Not stuck. That was my first mistake, thinking crowded meant trapped. A stuck people would dream of leaving or expanding. We have learned the size of the world — sixty paces from water to crown, perhaps less, holdable entire in the mind — and decided to keep it exactly.

When the wall by my door slumped last winter, I rebuilt it stone for stone, same shape, same lean, by morning. When my neighbor's fat-fire gutted her one room — brief and terrible in such press — we rebuilt it on the same footprint, with the salvaged lintel, the same hanging hook left of the door. When my mother died she was laid in the high niche wrapped in hide, and in time I emptied it, washed her bones in pool water, and built her — literally built her — into the step up to my door.

You will step on her going to borrow salt. You will not think it strange. Neither do I.

If you scrape a nail in the dimmer corner behind my house you will get that pale translucent slime, not plant, not animal, something simpler that lives on stone-breath alone. Sometimes I think I am most like that. A film on rock. Warm, damp to the bone, fingertips permanently ridged as if just out of a bath, hair blackened at the ends by my own lamp, humming under the drip-roar in my bubble, refusing — gently, absolutely — to become anything else.
