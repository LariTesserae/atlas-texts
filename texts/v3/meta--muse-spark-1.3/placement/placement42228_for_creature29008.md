At first I don't know where to look for myself.

I come up gasping, of course. Everyone does. The air here takes and doesn't give back, blue and thin and tasting of cold iron. My lungs rasp trying to hold it. I am standing somewhere high and lit orange, listening to the tick-tick-tick of water on metal and that low hum in my back teeth, and for a minute I think I must be one of them — the thin-air people.

I try that on. I am the net-tender on the chain-bridge with my hood up, my coat gone green at the shoulders. I am the old woman with the pail, pausing every six steps to breathe, feeling the weight go slack halfway up when the plateau forgets to pull. I shift my grip the way my father taught me. I try to walk the way the engines move, sighing up, sighing down.

But it doesn't hold. My feet want to be lower. My breath wants to be slower.

Maybe I am higher, then. For a while I think I am wings. It would be easy to be a soot-gull here, screaming between the stacks, white belly flashing when I bank through welding-white light. No migration. Nowhere higher to go. Just wheel and wheel in the grainy dusk, nest in hot ductwork on moss and wire shavings. Or a hand-sized moth crowding a bulb, drinking heat. Or the ash-flicker that takes it.

No. Too fast. Too loud.

Maybe I am large and patient. I try on the mossfield between the rail spurs, out where the capras graze the pale steam-grass around the outfalls. I stand barrel-chested, six-legged, shag-maned to the fetlocks, ribs moving like bellows once, twice a minute, in time with the house-sized pistons. The shunting engine creeps past inches from my horns and I do not move. When the rain on Net Street begins to peel upward off my mane in slow deliberate lines, I lift my great head with all the others to watch it go. Chewing. Remembering.

Closer. The breathing is right. The slowness is right. But I am not that big. I am not that indifferent. I am watching too hard.

Because I am lower still. Underfoot.

Find me there — not on Foundry Row with its long view across the slab, not creeping past the smelter wall where forty meters takes six minutes and leaves you winded. Find me where the water gathers. Under Cistern Four, down where Chapel Cut meets Net Street, where the basalt sweats and the iron pipes sweat and it is always warm-dark and dripping.

That's where I den.

I am rust-red and dog-lean and I move on soft feet through textile waste. My coat smells of iron and wet wool and milk. If you swung a lamp fast down here you would catch me only as eyes — two where you'd expect, and then three, four more, small and bright, strung along my cheeks and flanks, unblinking. Wet rivets in the dark.

I am a fox-thing. A vixen, this season. That is what has become more itself while I was looking.

My kits are blind and mewling in stolen mill-waste, blue and grey shreds. They pile and unpile, breathing that audible in-out rasp even in sleep. I lick salt off their fur — the same salt the pale climbers come up from the cracks for after the last cable-car, leaving silver snail-trails on the rails that the morning shift hoses off. I know that pat-pat-pat on iron. I hear it above me at night and I do not move. I press closer over my kits and listen to the bellower underneath, too deep to locate, felt more in the sternum than the ears. Air moving, the geologists say. I know better than to argue.

In the day I keep the rooms small.

That's my work, though the people think my work is eating rats that would eat the steam-cabbages. It is both. The maintenance shed on Spur Three is just over the moss, eight paces across outside. When the humans go in pairs, talking continuously, it stays tools and a cot. I help. I sit in the doorway and stare. A fox-thing staring never lets a room grow. I fix my surplus eyes on the far wall and hold it where it is, while the night-watchmen make their rounds in twos to do the same with sound and witness. Solitude makes spaces too large here. I know. I watch the solitary old ones come back strange-eyed from being alone inside, saying it was quiet in there, so quiet, with plenty of room to become someone else. I blink, all my eyes at different times, and keep holding.

When it rains upward I go out to watch.

On Net Street the drizzle will hesitate on the rails, on the moss, on my own back, and then lift — droplets peeling off and rising in slow deliberate lines back into the grey, against all wind. The capras lift their heads all together out on the field. I lift mine from under the cistern. We watch them go, as if remembering when we did that too. My kits sneeze in the warm dark. Lichen the color of verdigris and bone tattoos the concrete above me. Cushion moss three feet thick swallows everything I am not using. Ferns spill from a broken pipe and drip onto my den.

I do not pick the moss off. It has taken the edge of my left ear. I let it stay.

I am not finished. None of us are. My kits haven't opened their extra eyes yet — they are still smooth-cheeked, unfinished like the knapped basalt, like the half-built viaduct furred with green, like the crack things with their seam-gills and six-fingered feet. I lie with them in the hiss and gurgle, breathing slow, listening to the loud patient hum, and leave room around us for the place — and ourselves — to finish being born.
