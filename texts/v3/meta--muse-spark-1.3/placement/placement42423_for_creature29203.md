At first I think I would be up high.

Not in the pans where the town knots itself, but out on the rim where the nets are strung — because I like height, I like looking down at the whole extent at once. But I get up there in the telling and the wind takes that idea straight out of my hands. Nothing stands alone against the wind. I can feel it worrying at my hood, finding the gap at the neck, beading cold on the inside. No one wastes height up here. My steps shorten without asking me. Knees bend a little. Shoulders come forward. I am already ducking an eave that isn't there.

So not the rim. Lower.

I think I would work with my hands. I like the idea of the green tattoos — the dots and lines left by green-rusted sheet, a record you don't have to write down. Mender. Re-roller, re-spinner, patch, re-bore, re-tooth. But then I stand in the machine shop doorway for a while in my mind and the clank in rhythm is too sure for me. Those big hands know what a tool is for after it's broken three times. Mine don't. A new tool would shine in my hands for longer than a day, and shine doesn't belong up here.

I think, then, dye-works. The impossible colors in grey light. But I am not stained to the elbow yet. I haven't earned a sulphur forearm or a purple hand like a glove. I don't know a simmer from a boil by ear.

I keep walking down, in my head, suck-squelch instead of slap. It's coming. Strangers slap. Locals suction. I practice until my boots go soft and wide and laced high and my sound joins the street's low composite — not the thrum in the soles down by the lowest tarn, not the groan of the cable bucket, but the smaller music underneath: plink, plink, plink in different keys from eaves into barrels.

That's when I find where I actually am.

Not the foundry. Not the vats. The saddles between pans at the blue edge of day, and then the stair-alley going down. The narrow stair between overhanging, dripping houses where ferns grow from mortar and cable shadows stripe the wet cobbles. I have a hooked pole in my hand, lighter than it looks. I am walking the line of condensation-nets.

I must be a Catcher, then. Mostly women, mostly old, though every child does a turn. I am old enough for my fingertips to have gone soft and white and puckered permanent, newt-belly from always being wet. I walk at dusk and before dawn when the fog is thickest, tapping the frame so the beads let go, listening for the tinkle of drain into gutter. I know which mesh ices first. Which sags with dew-weight. Which has begun beading on the underside, upward, wrong — gathering against gravity before it grows heavy and falls. I don't comment on that. I just turn the net a quarter-turn and tighten the lower stay, as my mother did.

But I'm not only that. No one is only that up here, where houses share walls and heat and cistern-water.

In the day I follow the Threshold-carpenters to collect the curled shavings for tinder. I am thin and patient enough to carry a level I don't trust. The old rock flows. Lintels bow like wax. Doors jam. Every generation we re-hang them, build the threshold up a finger's width. I have learned the step-over without thinking. Children trail me for shavings and I show them, in return, which cushion is springy-dry enough to sit on, which liverwort stains, which fern-frond unrolls sweet enough to chew.

And at night, when the cloud thins just enough to show stars smeared through ice crystals, I am down by the leats with one trouser leg darker to the knee from wading, clearing moss from intakes with my bare hands. I feel voltage in my teeth. I feel when she exhales — when the amber bulbs in cages over every doorway dim of their own accord, as if the plateau were breathing out and taking voltage with it. I ease the load. I murmur to whoever is apprenticed to me, *she's exhaling*, and wait.

What I do most, though, is listen. That is the trade under the trades.

I pause mid-work, head cocked, to place a sound: which eave is dripping fast, which gutter is about to overflow, whether that turbine thrum has dropped a half-tone, whether the bucket is coming loaded or empty by the pitch of its groan. Conversation in doorways, shoulder to shoulder, murmured, with long pauses where we just listen to water together. A mother doesn't call. She clicks her tongue twice. I hear it because I have learned to hear the way lowland children learn to look.

I keep the extra cup on the sill during fog. If you ask I will say, annoyed, *just catch.* Not offering. Just catch. But I rinse the cup first. I set it level. I empty it into the butt with care in the morning. I never let a stranger's hand touch it. Something in me remembers that water here doesn't fall, it arrives.

The children know me for the other map I keep — not of nets or leats, but of the bell-stones. Certain slabs of the oldest stone ring underfoot, clear and sustaining a second too long. They hop from one to another — *ping — pong — ping* — across a pan on a windless moment to make a tune while running errands with water-butts sloshing. I know all of them. I pretend I don't hop anymore, but when no one is looking I test one with my toe to make sure it still rings.

If you came up and looked down thinking the town was empty, you would miss me entirely. I'm low like everything else. Felted wool in sage and rust, tarred canvas apron, hood close to the head, hair never fully dry, skin milk-white with green in it, smelling of peat-smoke and lanolin and sweet rot that gets into cuffs and never comes out.

Find me at dusk when the pan glows in warm reticulation against pearl-grey. I'll be standing still in that highest alley, touching the moss as I pass without thinking about it anymore. Press your hand in beside mine and cold water will well up around your knuckles. Lift it and it fills again.

That's the terms. Lush in the cold. Busy in the quiet. Drinking fog because there is nothing else to drink. Mending because there is nothing new to make. Listening because there is nothing loud enough to hear unless you listen.

I have become more moss than man, and I like it that way.
