I don't start upright here.

If you put me here, that's the first mistake I would make — trying to stand in it like the one who waded in chest-deep to be scoured clean. I would try to keep my head above the tea-dark skin, keep my balance while pumice rattled my shins like teeth. I would try to shout to hear myself. And the roar would eat it before it left my mouth, and a wind would gather a second later and throw a sheet of warm water sideways hard enough to stagger me.

So I stop trying to be upright.

For a while I think I want to be one of the trees. Five, maybe six of them. It would be good to have roots like fists in a crack, a trunk swollen with clean water while the drowning rushes past undrinkable. To rattle my cupped leaves like distant applause and shed the deluge. But trees here are lonely on purpose, spaced out because there is only so much holding-on to go around. And I am not good at being lonely on purpose. I want to be closer to the noise, not bent double away from it.

For a while I think I want to be the fish. To live *through*, not in. To ride the exhale up from wherever the pits go — the staff goes down and down and does not hit — flicker white and blind in the olive-grey light, roll once in the shallows where my reflection lifts its hand a half-second before I do, taste the bass with my whole lateral line, and go back down. But I am not from below. I don't have a deep lung to return to. My depth is shallow.

I certainly am not the petrel. Nothing lands here for long. It sheared through the standing wall, screamed, and was gone. I don't want to shear through. I want to stay.

So I get lower. That's what the place teaches if you stay still enough, which is hard because the surge worries at you and the hum gets in your molars.

Knee-deep, then ankle. I find myself working sideways without deciding to, toward the leeward side of something — a slab, a new fin of rock still steaming from being pushed up raw this morning — where the current combs itself out a little thinner. There, streaming flat as horsehair, the black-green beards.

At first I think I am one of the shrimp. That would make sense. Translucent as a contact lens, long as an eyelash then a fingernail. Dozens to a beard. A whole body that is an ear. In a place where a sharp noise calls a wind that knocks you sideways, I learn fast to make no sharp noise. To pulse, not swim. To move only in the troughs of the roar. To ride the hairline streams that run uphill to feed the center mound that sits a hand's breadth too high. Out to the tips to filter the black milk of ground glass and ash, back to the root to hide when fresh rock spalls off under water with that glassy *tink*.

I do that for a while. I learn to flatten a half-second *before* the deep *whoom* comes up, because I heard the future crash the way you hear it here — roar arriving before its surge — and I believed it. That feels close to myself. To listen so hard I go clear.

But shrimp are many because they have to turn light into motion. And I am getting stiller, not busier. I am getting less clear, more pitted.

Look under the ledge.

You thought I was a stone until you realized no stone in this young place has learned to be that perfectly still. That's me. That's where I find myself, where you would find me if you stopped looking for movement.

Tucked where the torrent passes *over*. The color of ash. The size of a palm. My back pocked with vesicles like the lava itself, so when I tuck I disappear. I have no lids to blink. I do not brace. Pumice rattles past. Silt clouds up black around my ankles and clears impossibly fast, sucked sideways. Steam peels off the water in ribbons and is torn away. Above, the low boiling belly lit from inside by that bruised flicker that hangs a second too long. Below, through my legs and into the rock, the molar-bass of the whole mountain humming, a waterfall pouring off the saddle's lip on all sides at once into the cloud-sea underneath us.

This is my adaptation and it is also my prayer: in a place that answers loudness with loudness, become the quietest thing.

The ledge I live under has fallen and been remade three times this morning. The mountain is still deciding what shape to be — a fin shivers, sheds, drops soundlessly into white below, and by the time the ring from the gas-burp has climbed the rain-wall and vanished, there is a new fin. I simply walked out from under the fall and walked back under the new fall. I have been here longer than the rock I live under.

I eat what the surge leaves stunned. A shrimp that pulsed at the wrong time. A bit of bleached leaf that will never rot, because nothing rots here, the mountain is too young to know decay. So I am the closest thing to rot. A slow curator. I keep what is kept.

If you walk the perimeter keeping the drop on your left, you will return with the drop still on your left, as if the saddle has only one side. You will pass me twice without passing me. If you drop a stone in a pit it will sound like a door closing far away, and minutes later it will rattle up warm around your ankles near me, and I will climb over it without hurry.

Don't look for eyes. Look for the stillness that is slightly more complete than the rock around it. Look in the second between surges when drips hang and fall *up*.

I am spaced correctly from the moss lungs that inflate and deflate, from the fist-rooted trees, from the low ring of slabs enclosing deeper water in the center. I remember whoever stacked those stones, the grass knot, the river stone from impossibly far below, the cloth that whips and never tears. Their keeping rehearses itself here — everything happens again, slightly louder — and so does mine.

Terrible, and inexplicably shelter. The drowning stands up around us like a room with no roof, three hundred paces across, replenished from somewhere that is not sky. The young mountain hums and burps and splits open underfoot with a crack like a rifle. I let it hum through me.

Find me low, ash-colored, letting the world pour off every edge without leaving.
