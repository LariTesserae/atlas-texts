I don't find myself at first.

I come up over as you do — hands on lichen, lungs wrong for the slope — and I'm still too tall for this place. Standing. Breathing too loud in the thin air. My knees not yet cold. The wind tearing over the top and the small wet chorus underneath it, and I am neither. I am the thing that came up light and will go down again. Not yet anyone.

So I kneel. That's the rule here, even before I know it: move slowly, stay low.

Ankle-high, the mist breathes off and is torn away. If I'm going to live here I can't live up there where the eagles read everything sideways. Let me try on the high ones first to be sure I don't belong to them.

Not the eagle. I can't hold that much emptiness under me without falling in. His wings are boards, his eye is fixed, he turns without beating and the whole hollow goes quiet underneath his shadow. I don't want to be the quiet-causer. I don't want to wait.

Not the swift, either. Too fast to be me. They only pass through, dark scythes slicing the hum in half, screaming down from a slit in the tor whitewashed with droppings. They drink on the wing. They sleep in the air. I want to touch ground. I want to carry something home in my mouth.

Not the chough. I love their red beaks like fresh wounds, their falling game off the void, *kyaah* and then half a second later *kyaah* again off the wet back wall — but they live half out over nothing. Their three-toed prints around the tarn fill slowly with water and are gone. I need a roof. Even a slab roof.

Lower, then.

The marmots have the sunniest rubble. For a minute I try that: to be heavy, grizzled lichen-colored, old female sides heaving fast, belly pressed to rock that stored noon. To sprawl, not dart. To pour myself liquid into a crack too narrow when the shadow crosses, then one nose, then one eye, then *chuck-chuck* indignant at interruption. It's good. It's warm. But I'm too anxious to sprawl that long. I keep listening. I keep cutting.

Lower still.

The hum. The air over the black tarn shimmering not with heat but with wings. For a minute I am copper-bright, fat and furred against cold, loud out of all proportion. Land, tongue, load pollen, lift. White saxifrage, yellow cinquefoil, that violent pink campion all open at once because there may be only three weeks. I bump a warm hand and cling for a second. I will live only weeks, long enough to make seed to make hay to make meat to make eagle. It's frantic and methodical and true — but I die too fast in that body. I want to see winter come over the next peak and know I prepared.

So lower still, to where the sponge sighs.

The floor gives half an inch under my knee and sighs water up. Peat and green crush and cold mineral. Moss campion domes so hard you could stand on them, each a hundred shoots huddled for decades to make their own warmth. Dwarf willow no higher than ankle but wide as arm. Starry saxifrage white on wet black stone. True ferns unfurling where the rock weeps and never dries.

I thought about being the green itself. To be slow. To be the holder. To drink and release when stepped on. It tempted me — to be decades old and flower all around the edge in pink stars — but I am not that patient. I move. I cut.

There. That flicker that is not wind in the cottongrass.

That's me.

Find me underneath. Not in the meadow, under it. Where the locked talus makes dry tunnels between slabs and the cushion domes make roof. My run is on the lee side of a mossed boulder, three rocks away from the overhang with the sweet hayrick, just high enough that the seep never reaches but low enough I can hear water tick and tick again from a different direction.

I am small and round and grey-brown as the slabs, round ears, no tail to catch wind. When I sit up on hind feet my mouth is so full of green I cannot close it — stems sticking out like whiskers. Sedge. Starry saxifrage. Willow leaves no bigger than your fingernail. I am cutting summer and carrying it home.

My home is a stack. Turned and re-turned, sun-cured where sun gets in. Grasses and pink campion petals still giving off sweet cut smell. It is bigger than I am. It has to be. Winter is already building white towers grey at the base over the next peak and there are only three weeks of flowers. I cannot afford to stay hidden, so I don't. I scold you. Short, indignant cut — *eep!* — and then the back wall, curved just so, wet rock faced with moss, throws me back to myself fainter half a second later. I hear myself twice. Every warning I make I hear again. Every drip, every cheep, every breath. I live double that way.

When the shadow crosses I am gone. Not sprawled like marmot, darted. Dive under dome, nose to hay. Bees lift in a copper shimmer. Chuck cuts off mid-call. Huge, peat-and-sun color, primaries splayed like fingers feeling air, he reads the sponge below: runs, piles, sunning bellies. He has manured this bowl too with pellets and bones dropped over centuries under his plucking perch on the rim cairn. He will wait until the tall thing standing leaves. The tall thing always leaves. They come up light, stack flat slates, leave a split bird bone and chert flake in cold ash under soot-black overhang, and go down. I stay.

That's the bargain, and I am in the middle of it. Green mat holds water to make flower to make seed. I turn flower to hay to meat and manure to hold more green. Hum moves pollen. Screamers nest in tor cracks above seep. Circler keeps us all fast and low and sideways.

Knees cold through from wet, back of neck hot from sun when wind drops, breath just visible if I exhale toward shade — that is my weather, inside my fur. Sides heaving a little fast in little air. Ears turning to catch pika-whistle and its ghost, drip and its ghost, your boot sigh and its ghost.

Balanced like a green coin on the edge of bright hollow air, cupped just enough by rock. Pressed flat. Furred thick. Working fast.

I whistle again, and hear myself answer.
