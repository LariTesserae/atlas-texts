I would not be standing up, at first. That's where I start, because that's where you start — boots on the give, breath showing, socks already damp.

But I can't stay there. Standing is too loud here. Too warm and too quick. My footsteps are a dull compression, a wet kiss, then nothing, and behind me the prints are already leveling with green-brown water. I am too big to be believed by this place. The wind shreds itself over a million tiny surfaces and passes me as a cool hand, and I feel how exposed and how in-the-way I am in the middle of the bowl, with the granite miles off left and right and the plain light coming straight down.

So I get lower, because everything here tells me to get lower.

At first I think I would be the rumor. The white ptarmigan a hundred meters off, so still she is lichen until she blinks. That would be easy — to be the eye that watches, feather-footed for the sponge, picking buds and grit and then gone. Or the vole, the tunnel-hum traveling under the mat, never surfacing, eating sedge bases in the dark. Or the crane fly, hanging improbable in the cold air with its legs dangling. But those are visitors. They move through. They do not live *as* the place lives. They are too fast, too warm-blooded, too brief. The mat tolerates them and then closes its black eyes over where they were.

I don't want to be tolerated. I want to touch.

So I kneel. That is the next rule the soles teach before the head understands. Everything touches. There is no bare larger than your palm that stays bare. If I put my hand in, my fingers disappear to the wrist and do not find earth, only more stems, more thatch, more living and half-living and long-dead-but-not-rotted fiber.

Down there, I try on being old.

I think I could be the dwarf willow, ankle-high and a hundred years old, trunk thick as my wrist and horizontal, crawling through thatch, bark furrowed and hard as pipe. Two small oval leaves to a twig. Not trying to rise. Trying to persist. Stitching the mat together sideways with root. But I haven't earned that. I haven't been nursed and then slowly smothered by my own nurse moss, bleached twigs up like wires. That is a century of freeze-lift and thaw-drop. I am too new. Nothing here is new. Stone has been stone so long it has learned how to be soil at the edges.

I think I could be reindeer lichen, grey and brittle and branching like tiny antlers, drinking not from the sponge but from air, bead by bead. But I crumble too easily, and lichen does not forgive. Twenty years to grow back the width of a palm. I forgive too fast. I forget.

Lower still. On my belly now, face close enough that condensation beads on every hair of moss and does not fall, just hangs. I smell peat and rain-water and green iron.

You'd find me there, in the end. Not on the hummock top, waving bristles like star moss. In the seam.

Between a sphagnum hummock big as an armchair and one black eye of water no larger than a plate, where the liverworts film over. Black-green, slick as oilskin. The film crew.

I would be that. Or, more honestly, one cell of that.

A liverwort lobe plastered tight where nothing else will stick. From standing height I'm just the darker shade between greens. You have to kneel to see me at all. I hold damp in. When frost splits a cushion down the middle and the split fills with ice pressure, when a bank slumps overnight green-over-black, I am there within days, filming over the wound in a living skin, holding the edge until the slower mosses can come back.

I don't build ground the way the sphagnums do, dead at the base and living at the tip, pickling everything to black butter in acid like vinegar. I don't plumb like the sedges, pumping air down so I don't drown. I mend. I am the damp kept from leaving.

My work is at anthill speed but without scurry. Swell, splay, split, film, pickle, press. Frost jacks me up an inch by morning; by afternoon I melt and slump and sigh and resettle an inch to the left. Water finds a new route under me, domes me up. I breathe very slowly. I eat sound — that's why, with so much change, there is none. The moss eats it. The little rill ticks drip by drip. Sometimes, deep under, there is a creak as ice lets go of root, felt more in the soles than heard.

And underneath me, smaller still, the night shift that never clocks out: springtails white as salt, red-brown mites, tardigrades in the water film around each leaf drying to husk when I freeze and swelling back when I thaw, dying and un-dying a dozen times a summer. Fungal threads white as nerve lacing through. Without them I'd clog.

If you pushed your hand in to the wrist right where I am, you'd lift five of us at once. Willow root through moss thatch through my film through lichen crust. The whole upholstery puckers. You can't take one without taking all.

That is where I'd find myself. Not living *on*. Living *in*, and *as*. A dark film in a hollow, keeping a wound wet enough to heal, while the cold factory runs over me and under me and through me, silently, at full capacity, turning light and rain and air — and its own dead — into more mass, and more mass, and more mass.
