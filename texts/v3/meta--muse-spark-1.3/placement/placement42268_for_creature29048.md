At first I think I'd be at the rim.

Lying on my belly, like all visitors. Ankle-high parapet, flat glare behind me, and then the cut. Looking over. Shouting without meaning to because the pit throws my voice back at me too loud. Flattening myself to the wall on the way down while they flow around me. That is the honest start — I would be clumsy here. My feet are not dished to fit the steps. My shoulders don't know how to carry.

So I go down anyway, in my head, step by chanting step, trying on places.

Not the bottom. Not at first. The bottom is too sacred and too damp for me. The pan that never dries and never rises, ankle-deep, the toads the color of wet mortar. The women who dip drinking water there by a law no one has to say out loud. I don't yet know the difference between a washing spout and a drinking dip, and everyone, even a stranger's child, would have to correct me gently. I would foul something without meaning to. I'm too new to be trusted with the pan.

Not the goat pen either, lashed poles at the very floor, the three brindled goats chewing straw that had to be carried down basket by basket. Not the hearth ledge just above it, smoke tonguing the plaster black. My hands are not lime-white to the wrist. I don't know how to water basil by the spoonful and keep it from goats as if it were a child.

Higher then. The reed awning where the loom clacks in time with the drip. The sleeping niche just deep enough for a curled body, rolled mat and copper bowl waiting. I try to picture myself curled there. Knees soft, low center of gravity. Listening at midday to the metronome that never syncs — *tok — tok — tok* from a dozen weeping spouts. Could I learn to tell my mother's *tok* from the others? I have no mother here.

I try the air. A rope bridge, a laundry line, a pigeon loft made from stacked jars with their mouths knocked out. Hundreds of wings pouring out in a clatter like thrown gravel, circling the square of sky, funneling back. I could be a pigeon-keeper, whistling birds home at evening. But I don't know individuals yet. The children do. Or a swallow, cutting lines through pigeons all day, screaming, skimming footprint puddles for wrigglers. Too fast. Or the kestrel that stoops from the parapet and banks off walls to take a pigeon mid-pit while the whole flock thunders in echo. I don't want to be the tax.

Or a cat. There are two, temple-thin, patrolling the fourth terrace, paid in offal, not to be petted on duty. I like that — to be useful and left alone. To work the sunlit courses like the banded lizards for flies, and leave the night courses to rats and traps. But I am not that lean, that sure.

So I keep descending and ascending in my head until the trying-on slows.

What I am good at, what my body — if I had one here — would bend toward, is listening for the return.

Residents speak low and pause half a beat to hear the pit give it back. A bucket clanked below arrives at the rim as a bell. A name called comes back doubled. I know how to do that pause. I know how to tap plaster with a hand-stone in the morning cool and listen for hollow where salt has bloomed in white beards on the north courses. Different mortar, different cold. I know how to watch for patterns that only show for an hour.

You'd find me, then, not at noon when light goes straight down and then bounces wall to wall until the bottom goes amber-green. You'd find me just after dawn, or just before.

On the third terrace, where the swallow nests follow the same horizontal crack all the way around like a frieze. Where maidenhair fern lives in the drip-line and lichen spreads like spilled paint and a caper bush explodes sideways from a joint. My hand on the east wall.

Because on certain mornings the stones don't sweat randomly. They sweat in lines and whorls that follow old repairs, outlining blocked doors and filled channels you cannot otherwise see. The pit draws its earlier plumbing in water for an hour. If the ghost-door sweats complete before sunrise, rain within three days outside.

I would be one of the small, darker old women who read that. Or the boy she is teaching to read it beside her. It doesn't matter which — we are the same function. Sitting close enough to the plaster to feel it exhale. Quiet enough that the pigeons going quiet an hour before tells us first. Not building anything new. Patching where it bubbles. Carrying shit up in sealed jars to the dry fields so it can come back as onions. Carrying soil down in baskets because there is none to dig. You do not dig. There is nowhere to dig to. You maintain.

By evening you'd still find me in the same general height, second terrace now, where cooking smoke cannot rise straight out but hangs in a blue layer until the night draft pulls it up like a cork. Feet dangling over a drop I no longer see as a drop. Shared pot. Lamps lit in niches, little clay saucers of oil, all that made, worked, hand-plastered surface flickering — one side honey-gold, one side already indigo. The loom stopped. The goats dozing. The pigeons panting with open beaks. The toads starting. Talking low so as not to wake the echo.

I would have learned at last not to shout to overcome myself. I would say little, and listen for what comes back doubled, and tap tomorrow where the old mason tells me to tap.
