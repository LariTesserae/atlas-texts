At first I think I would be swimming.

Between. In the fifty feet of empty black between lives. Between the tower rooted in the cracked foundation and the moss mounding the road. That's where you put a visitor, isn't it? In the middle distance, moving, looking. An animal mind expecting crowdedness from lushness, listening for a fin.

The cold gets in first. Not at the skin — deeper, at the back of the neck, a hand. Two degrees above freezing and heavy, thoughtful water. I fall slower than I should. My kicked-up silt doesn't settle, it hangs. My breath — if I have breath here — doesn't rise right. Light leaks, bruised, from impossibly far above and gives out twenty, thirty meters out. Past that, ink. Past that, glow.

So I think: I will be the thing that looks. I will drift ridge to bend to bend down old streets where water moves like wind used to, and watch.

But nothing looks back. That is the first rule and it unmakes me.

I keep waiting to be seen and the forest goes on creaking without me. A thousand stipes bending leather-on-leather as the pour comes over the northern ridge — that slow enormous fall of heavier water, organ-deep in my sternum before I hear it. The clack of slate on slate. The hiss of silt. Somewhere up high a slab loosening for a century finally decides, booms stone-deep, tumbles end over end into moss and hangs its cloud for hours.

No eye opens. No herd startles. The kelp blades bead white at their edges and do not care if I am close enough to see.

So I can't be the watcher. Watching needs an answering glance to stay a watcher. Here I am only a brief thickening of the water, soon forgotten.

Alright, then I think I will be tall. I want to be one of the old ones.

One stipe thick as a thigh, dark umber ridged like old rope twisted and re-twisted. Thirty, forty feet up, wide blades thick as leather straps. Lean. That whole posture, that whole philosophy: lean when the surge comes, creak, recover. Hold open my own sphere of black, swept clean. Need it — in this leak-light you cannot afford a neighbor's shade.

But I am too fast. Too hungry. I want too much at once. A kelp will take a decade to shoulder apart a dry-stone wall it started in, a millimeter a winter, patient as ice. It does not hate the wall with its little chipped hand-hammer marks under its root. It just continues. It feeds itself when it dies — no mouth, not even a worm-tube, just fall slowly to the composting mat and pale from green to yellow to translucent white to sweet-iron silt, leaf to mud to root, without intermediary. I don't know how to wait like that. I don't know how to eat myself and call it enough.

So not the tower.

Then I think I will be low and wide. Moss.

Grey-white woolly drift heaped over road and rope-line until braided kelp-fiber thick as a wrist looks like a green vein underneath me. Lung of the valley. When the surge hits I will exhale milk-blue and breathe light in a wave that follows the current. Touch me and I will glow longer where the hand was, then forget. Each drift alone, then black, then another. Manners, not absence.

But I am too singular. Moss is a we, a heap, a meadow breathing together. I still have a face-shaped wanting. I still listen for what should be there in a place this green.

Smaller, then. Younger-feeling. I will hang off an edge.

Terrace lip, lintel stone, cairn capstone, hut eave — hair-fine filament weeds, colorless until the moss-breath catches us. We lift like laundry lines when the current bells through, we comb the water for sinking sparks and falling silt. The drifting green sparks the kelps let go all at once every many hours, that rise a little as if remembering up and then impossibly sink. Nothing eats them on the way. They land and become. We catch a few. It feels like purpose.

But still I am reaching. Still I am wanting to be useful, to be *for* something.

The place wears me slower.

Cold slows thought until staying feels like the only option. Weight changes me. A dislodged stone takes a long time to decide to land. A frond takes forever. I stop kicking to stay still. I let the pour take me down-valley a little and set me down in the corduroy mud beside a fallen lintel. Among the mats.

Ah. Here.

This is where I find myself. Not upright, not drifting. Down. In the compost.

A dead frond, fallen — slowly, so slowly — and lying where it fell, because there is nothing to take me. Not eaten. Not seen. Paling. The green going yellow at my edges first, then translucent white, then silt. Smelling faintly sweet and iron when the surge stirs me. The rootlets in the mud below drinking me back up.

It is not grim. That surprises me and then doesn't. It is full. Full of green attention without eyes. To be uneaten here is not to be wasted. It is to be kept in the circuit: leaf to mud to root, relentless though slow. The forest does not crowd me. It leaves my sphere of emptiness intact. Fifty feet to the next tower. Respect.

And yet — here the place refuses to be simple — I am not only that.

Because I remember air. The rock remembers air by its shape — settled, lived-in, terraced, riverbedded — and so do I. I keep drifting, in the slow way dead things drift here, toward the huts.

Not the roofless one where the kelp is splitting the wall. The other. The intact one with its roof slabs still on, its low black arch facing down-current, kelp leaning away from it. Inside the doorway, water stops. Cut. Not filmed. Cut.

I find myself on the threshold.

On one side, total cold water. On the other, old cold still air that smells of stone and smoke from a fire a hundred years drowned. Dust hanging in water-light at the door. Dry mud floor cracked. Stone bench. Niche with a coil of fiber rope, dry as bone. Faint tick, tick as pressure shifts — which is always night.

I put my wrist through, if I have a wrist, and the water closes around it like a tight collar and refuses to follow. Dry on the other side. Freezing.

This is what I would be, at last, more itself the longer I stay: not the swimmer, not the tower, not the lung, not the curtain.

I would be the kept breath.

The small, hand-sized intent that persists because the forest allows it. Kelp does not grow through my door. Moss mounds up to my wall and stops a hand's breadth short, polite. Pale rootlets reach in through my roof cracks toward my air but never pierce the invisible skin; they flatten and spread, drinking condensation. That is enough relationship. Distance as manners.

Inside, no eye. No fin. No need to be seen to continue. Just tick. Just hold. A high valley drowned but not erased. A mountain pasture under a mile of night, town-sized, patient, loud in the dark, lush and sparse.

If you come — and you will only come by imagining, the surface you will never see far above — you will pass between tower and meadow and read the emptiness as death. Stay longer. Let the cold slow you. Fall slowly. Pale slowly. Find the doorway that keeps its last breath and stand in it, half in water, half in air, un-witnessed and therefore, at last, mercifully, fully there.

Leaf to mud to root. Stone to wall to moss. Water to threshold to air.

That.
