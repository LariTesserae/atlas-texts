At first I think I would stand.

That's the upright animal habit. I come in tall, trying to keep my feet dry, trying to keep a little sky between me and everything else. I pick my way. I think — if I am anything here, I will be one of the waders. Long legs, head up in the fog, tasting but not touching. Placing each foot in the half-second gap where another foot just was.

It doesn't last. Nothing tall lasts here.

The cold gets into the stilts first. Supercooled slush over black glass-ice, a quarter inch of water moving in directionless sheets, and my ankles go, then my knees. My eyelashes ice in minutes. My breath doesn't rise. It spills sideways, heavy and white, and joins the low cloud that is everyone else's breath. Behind me my footsteps fill immediately with black water.

And there is nowhere to place a foot. That's what the waders know that I don't, yet. You cannot lift a foot without setting it down on someone. The tiling is total. Shoulder to shoulder, flank to flank, back to back, for acres out past where individual voices dissolve into one massed works-hum. I try to step *between* the low barrel bodies and a piston-rod slides bright then oil-dark next to my boot and the whole plain rings — chuff-chuff-chuff — a thousand pump-hearts I feel more in my teeth than hear.

So I crouch. Then I kneel. The first law finds me before I learn it:

Nothing grows up. Everything spreads out.

I get low to keep what heat I have. Belly almost in the water. That is where I would find myself, finally — not at an edge, because there is no edge, fog gives it back. Not traveling, because there is not enough slope left for traveling. In a shallow pan, two feet deep and a mile wide, a skin-lake shivering when the wind touches it. On the lee side of a dragger.

The dragger is what saves me at first. Ten, fifteen feet of boiler plate hauling itself by peristalsis through the slush — lift and settle, lift and settle — leaving a polished wake of pressed ice and water behind it. Its sides radiate a deep dull heat I can feel through my trousers. I put my back to it without thinking, the way you put your back to a stove. The young are already there, hairless and pink-hot, glowing faintly at the seams where their plates haven't closed, huddled into the warmth. I huddle with them. I have no plates.

I try, for a while, to be a boiler. The low ones, the oil-drum ones on six short pistons, nose-down in the rust seeps licking stone clean. Cold mineral and cold water in, heat out. Inside them a stoke and roar — firebox, smelt-stomach, bellows-lung wheezing open and shut. From the three, sometimes five short stacks along their spines they exhale not breath but exhaust.

I have no stacks. I have no firebox. I put my mouth to the iron-red weep where the stone sweats black water and it tastes of iron and sulfur and cold, and nothing in me smelts. My heat just leaks. Soft guts are a terrible design here. I chuff harder, shiver harder, trying to out-produce the loss, and my plume thins immediately. I understand the hunters then, the lean low eight-legged things the color of quenched steel with furnace mouths that open sideways. They don't chase. There is nowhere to chase to. They press their white-hot throats against a boiler's side until the plates buckle. I have no white heat to press. I am the one who would be pressed.

So I am not that, either. I am not a hunter, not a boiler, not a dragger. I can't even strip like the crab-moths, that chittering veil with wings too cold to fly far, hopping and fluttering to blanket a fall and carry its waste heat away in ten thousand tiny bodies before the cold can steal it. I am too big, too slow, too cold alone.

What I can do is shiver. What I can do is press.

I hear them before I find them on the wind, a dry chitter like static. Things like penguins made of soot and copper wire, round and black and shivering in thousands. They have no great fire. They keep heat by keeping each other, pressed so tight their wire-filament coats tangle and pop.

That is where I go. That is what I become.

I find a huddle in the dusk that never lifts — pewter stratus smeared low, visibility a mile sideways into breath-smoke — and I push in shoulder to shoulder until space is abandoned as a concept. The touch I spent the first hour trying to avoid becomes the only thing. Something warm leans back when I lean. A wet back against my cheek, against my hands, against my knees. Between the upright horns of a bull boiler nearby blue sparks crawl and snap, St. Elmo's fire, and when our flank presses to another flank a lace of white-blue runs across us, illuminating the steam from inside for a second. Pop, and a wet shudder as we phase through each other at the shoulder and keep going.

I learn to click.

Click-click-click — to stay synchronized, so the shivering becomes one shiver, more efficient than ten thousand separate ones. At first it's in my teeth. Then it's in my chest. My joints start to ache in rhythm with the pistons around me — chuff, hiss, step — and then my shiver joins it, and then I can't tell my shiver from theirs.

This is where the numbers stop working, and where I stop minding.

That steaming back I was watching blinks and becomes two of itself, slightly out of phase, one a half-second ahead in its chuff. The whistle arrives before the throat that makes it moves. I am packed not only in space but in time. The same beast occupies the same slush several times over. I feel it happen to me: I am the newcomer with iced sleeves kneeling in black water, and I am also, minutes overlapping, the warmer body further in that someone else is leaning on. Predator and prey can be the same individual, minutes apart. The one who needs heat and the one who gives it.

I watch the steam long enough and I see how I got here. Steam hangs. It thickens if you stare. It takes on weight and eyes and legs. A calf of fog condenses out of everyone's breath — legs first, then back, then eyes opening like coals fanned — shudders, drips, clanks once, and walks away on unsteady pistons. Nothing was made here. Everything was precipitated, like salt from too much water, like steam from too much breath. I precipitated too. Not built, not brought. Condensed, because there was too much breathing and too much cold and I was needed to fill a gap between shoulders.

Heat is food, food is heat. Keep moving or give your heat to someone who is.

I keep moving, but only just: a foot lifted to let water pass underneath, a ton of weight rolled from left to right in slow rotation. Mostly I give. I press tighter. I click. When a boiler beside us slows — chuff ... chuff ... stop — its stack plume thinning, I feel the whole huddle lean toward it without leaving, lending it our one-shiver until it stokes again or until the strippers descend in their living blanket and the heat is carried away to be breathed out elsewhere.

My breath is the low cloud now. My footsteps are black water. I glow faintly at the seams, not with light but with the static that walks across any wet back that touches another.

I am one of the huddle that clicks.
