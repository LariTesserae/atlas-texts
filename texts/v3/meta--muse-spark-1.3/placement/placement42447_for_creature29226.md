You wouldn't arrive as anything. Not at first.

At first you'd think you were a visitor. Boots on slag-concrete that doesn't thud, it *rings* — and the dust gets in the seams of your teeth. Throat rasping paper-dry. Lungs working too hard because the air up here is hot-thin, like breathing through warmed wool. You'd be navigating by the heat on your cheeks more than your eyes, because the light is only leaks: a stoke-door orange three terraces down, a strangled red lamp in leaves, a freckling where something popped overhead. You'd tell yourself: I'm walking through a tunnel chewed through living mass. I'm keeping to the cut path, like the rules say.

Stand still a little too long and that story falls apart.

Because they turn to you. Not with eyes — there are no eyes here — but the broad blind faces, black-purple thick as belt leather, edged with that waxy amber keel, all-tip sideways in the dark. Toward the pipes, toward the furnace seams. Toward you. Hold the back of your hand near a mature plate and it's faintly *cooler* than the wind. It's stealing. You are standing there radiating warmth, 37 degrees of wet animal heat leaking out of your cheeks and hands, and the whole wall is drinking you.

Then you'd understand the second, truer shape: you are not a visitor. You are food. A brief, walking sun.

And a wet one. That's the crime. With every breath you give off water. Your eyes water pointlessly at the resin stink and that's water. The sweat starting under your shirt — theft. Transpiration is theft here. Tear a leaf and it doesn't bleed water, it beads hot oil that skins over in seconds, because to bleed water is death. You bleed water just by being alive. The pale white-pink grub of a new tendril nosing closer to your bootlace isn't curious. It's found a warm spot that sweats. The mass remembers pressure and grows toward it.

So you can't stay what you are. No animal stays. The air will not support you and the plants will not feed you. If you want to find yourself here — really find where you'd *be*, not just where you'd die — you have to dry out.

Let it happen slow. Don't settle yet.

You crouch where the last pruner passed — you know it by the slow hump still moving away under the mat and the fresh-cut green-black smell, oil and crushed keel and hot dust — and you stay. An hour, which you don't. Which you're not supposed to. The tunnel is already closing. Redstem does that, six inches by the next shift, redder, thinner, wilder than the old black below it, knotting trellis to trellis, using blackvine as ground. It finds your laces first. Then your ankle. It doesn't tie. It roots.

Your eyes give up first, and that's mercy. Under this sealed lid of cold-tea smog there was never anything to see anyway. You learn the other sight: heat on skin. Left cheek warmer — service pipe there, under the felt. Right cheek cooler — open air where a tower slumped last shift and left a pit of furnace-light below, steam curling up scorched cinnamon and ozone. You turn your face not up, but sideways. Like they do.

Your skin keels. You can feel it, the edges sealing with wax.

Now it's becoming more itself. Now you're not food anymore.

You are a shoot. Pale, blind, spearing straight for that scalding pit with a dozen of your brothers, crossing, knotting, racing because growth here is violent in fast-forward. Creak-tick as you tighten in the heat. Within hours you're red, muscled, piling fruiting mass on. You can feel the old ten-meter trellis under you groan and lean, elephantine ribs sagging. Somewhere out there the cutters converge — you hear their discs hum low, three or four rolling in from different tunnels — white blood cells coming to take you back to stump. Cut, collapse, regrow, burn, regrow. You don't fear it. Pruning is how the valley breathes.

If you survive the cutters long enough — if you get lucky, if you root sideways into the insulation of a heating duct instead of down into dead slag, sipping conducted heat and condensed soot with a thatch of pale fingers — you thicken. Slow. Architecture instead of muscle.

This is the final form, the one that stays:

You are a blackvine plate, big as a dinner plate, black-purple in the dark. You hang under a pipe-bridge on the climb to the First Boiler House, where the walls are no longer walls but lignified stem hard as ironwood, domes and saddles and curtains fifty meters thick swaying faintly though there is no wind. Boilers nested like seeds in a fruit behind you, still thudding, still thudding. Conveyors somewhere below going *clank-clank-clank* through blankets, carrying your pruned children to the furnaces to make more heat to make more vine.

Your day is this: steal warmth out of the wind so your back is cool to the touch. Drink soot through the bladder-moss furred on your stem — those thumb-sized amber-black blisters that pop softly overhead and hang phosphorescent, dry as ash, never settling. Seal your edges. Turn your blind face toward the dull orange crack. When the whole sector shudders — that dull thump as the air-pumps reverse deep down — shake your black-pepper pollen loose and let the hot wind take it. No wings needed.

You hold up the building now. The building holds up you. Pull one and the other sighs.

Spore-dust glitters slow in the dim red lamplight around you. Nothing with eyes looks back. You don't need them. You feel the next warm visitor pause too long in your tunnel, radiating, sweating, and all your leaves tip, inch by inch, toward his cheeks.

Welcome, you creak.
