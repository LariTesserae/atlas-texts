At first I thought I would be walking.

That is what you do on the Table. You walk, because it goes on past the horizon in every direction and makes its own weather in the middle, and there is nothing else to do with that kind of vastness but put one foot in front of the other on stone that has decided.

So for a long time I pictured myself out there between the hollows. On the pavement. Miles of wind-scoured stone, frost-cracked polygons, thin grit that squeaks underfoot. The air bright and hard in my lungs. My breath showing even at noon. The sun ordinary, pale white-yellow, just with farther to fall. Nothing jagged anymore. Everything settled into its final shape. I would be the figure in the fog to the waist in the morning, wading through a white sea, and burned dry by midday with beads ticking off every thread I wore.

I thought I would be a surveyor. Someone who climbs to a high swell to see. But distances do not hold here. A town four hours away yesterday is six hours away today though the road has not moved. I would climb and see the same broken ring of tall slabs in three valleys at once, and when I walked to the nearest the other two would be gone. So I would not be that. The Table does not want to be mapped.

Then I thought I would be a builder. But there is a rule: do not build with new stone. Take from a fall. The Table does not like new holes. I thought I would hunt, or keep something, but there are no other animals. None. No birds, no sheep, no dogs, no flies over the middens, no worms when you dig, just roots and cold. Ask where they went and the people look puzzled, as if you asked where the second sun went. There is nothing to hunt and nothing to tend except the green that has taken those jobs — frost-heave instead of worms, drifting spores instead of flies, moss instead of scavengers, covering a dropped tool, a fallen wall, a body within a season, gently, completely, without judgment.

So I stopped picturing myself out on the pavement, exposed. I found myself instead down in it. In a hollow.

You would find me where life gathers, because that is the law here. Life does not cover the Table. It gathers. In the lee of a worn stone back, where condensation collects, where the green is so dense and soft it hurts to look at after so much grey. Cushion-plants hard as pillows. Moss a foot deep. Liverwort black-green and slick. Dwarf willow no taller than my ankle and a hundred years old. Lichen in rust and bone-white and sulfur. If you kneel and put your ear close you can hear water moving under the moss, and if you stay still you can watch it grow. Tendrils creeping over stone an inch an hour. Fronds unrolling.

You would find me in the town that grew around that hollow like moss around damp. From a distance you would mistake it for bedrock. Lichen has taken the dry-stone walls. The roundhouses have turf roofs thicker than the wild. Terraces step down to trap the damp. Causeways of flat slabs laid across the bog. Field walls running for miles to nowhere. Streets worn half an inch down into solid granite by feet. Granaries and sleeping platforms for thousands.

Three hearths smoking.

I am at one of them. Not at first. At first I am loud. All visitors are. Sound does not carry right here, and loud noise feels dangerous, like leaning over a drop. I learn to be quiet the way other people have learned to be polite. I learn to hear wind in moss, which is almost no sound at all, like breathing through cloth. I learn to hear my own joints. Water ticking off leaves.

I learn to drink only running or daily-condensed water. Never from a tarn showing a different sky. The tarns are shallow, black, perfectly still pans in stone bowls with no inlet and no outlet, fed only by air. They taste of stone and sky. But sometimes they reflect two moons, and sometimes you find old shorelines ten feet above the water and stairs going down ten feet below. I learn to check the sky before I drink.

I learn to sleep under turf if I can. The moss muffles the doubling and you dream quieter.

Who would I be? For a while I thought I would be the old woman scraping lichen into a pot for bitter porridge. For a while I thought I would be the man taking a stone out of a wall and setting the same stone back a fraction straighter, as his father did. For a while I thought I would be one of the very few children, stacking pebbles into a tower and knocking it down without a sound.

I am a little of all of those. I scrape. I mend. I stack and unstack. I wear felt and woven dwarf-willow bark and soft boots stuffed with dry moss. My cloak beads with morning condensation and ticks as the drops fall, and if you look close you understand: there are no sheep here. That is human hair, collected, washed in tarn water, felted over years. A cloak is a family. I eat black-green jelly, bark tea, and in the two-week summer when everything exhales spores at once and the hollows steam faintly, I sweep spore-flour from flat slabs and make dense bread, and bake it in peat. Old moss burning. Old green burning. Burning time to stay warm. Because there are no trees, there is no wood.

But more itself than any of that — what I keep becoming, as I stay — is a weight.

You would find me at the edge of the terrace on quiet days when the fog lingers, when you see another settlement superimposed on the one you are in. Same walls, but whole, taller, crowded. Smoke from every roof. Figures moving in the mist that make no sound and leave no footprints. If you stand very still and do not look directly, you can hear it very faintly: a market, a song, stone on stone, hundreds of hands working. The people who live *then* are still there, in their thousands, building the walls five, ten times over.

No one is afraid of this. They treat it like weather. Because it is weather. Some of that weather is time.

Echoes arrive before the sound. I hear my footstep half a second before my foot lands. Fog sometimes flows uphill, or stops mid-air in ribbons all day, solid enough to cast shadows. Cistern water at dawn is warm though the night was below freezing. If I hear voices before mouths move, I keep walking. I do not answer an echo that comes early.

I stack stones not to build higher, but to pin the place down. Weight helps. The light places drift.

Leave cairns alone. They are not markers. They are weights, holding this version of the town down so it does not blow away into the others.

That is what I do. I tend a small cairn behind the third roundhouse down, where the doubling comes through strongest. I take from a fall, never a quarry. I lay a stone. I lay another. My hands are grey at the knuckles with granite dust that never washes out. My chest is broad from thin air. My eyes are pale from squinting. My breath shows. I am short, settled like the land into a final shape.

At night, when frost comes down and the green freezes stiff and tinkles faintly if the wind touches it, and the town goes dark except for one or two peat fires guttering low, I lie still under turf, under moss a foot deep, and listen with the few who remain.

The slow crack of stone contracting. The sigh of peat settling deeper. The almost inaudible creep of moss over everything we have stacked up, patient, lush, cold, covering it all again.

That is the Table breathing. That is what truly lives here, and I am only the part of it that, for a little while longer, still walks around and straightens stones.
