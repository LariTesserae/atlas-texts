It starts with going down, and I go down.

Ears pop twice. The orange-orange-out lights. The lid ticking far above like weather trying to get in. I walk out onto the apron and the cold closes over my head like water, and I look for where I would be.

Not at first with the Pump Men. I go to the brick house and stand in the throb until my molars ache, watch them tilt their whole bodies to listen for intake icing, drink tea with concrete dust on it. I am a good listener but I don't shout. I don't have hands chapped raw from chain in 2C water. I couldn't touch the high-water marks for luck on the way in without feeling like a tourist. Not there.

Not in Shed 4 either. I climb to the pulpit and sit a while with Mara and the cards gone swollen, the microwave timed between slams, the one green monitor that says PUMPS OK / PRESS OK. I like the vigil of it, the chaperoning of a machine that runs itself. But I don't walk a line with an oil can. I don't have eleven years of echo off the terraces in me to tell if the dampers are freezing. The press slams every six seconds and I flinch every six seconds. Not there.

I try walking. I fall in fifty yards behind a Walker, headlamp bobbing in the blue between masts, hi-vis flashing as we pass under. We measure a weep with a bucket and a stopwatch. 3 L/min. Not reportable yet. We keep moving because light only pools. Old Kolya curses the six-foot beards on the north wall in two languages and brings one down in a shatter. A shard kisses my cheek and I don't have a scar to earn. I like motion but I don't live in it. The dark between is -15C with wind and the wind finds me too fast. Not there.

So not people. People go up. Even the double-shifters go up the helix in reverse to sleep where it isn't raining inside.

I look up then, for the crows. Huge and shaggy on the stack catwalk, shouting and answering themselves a second late off the terraces. Commuters who like it. Up the shaft for fries and insulation and back down with beakfuls of white fluff. I envy the commute. I don't have wings, I don't have above. Not there.

Low then, with the rats running the ten-foot ring of the canal, sleek and fat on press-line grease, drinking the black thread-water off the walls and not dying. Tough, yes. But too fast, too warm-blooded. Not there.

Between, with her — the tabby with condensate beaded on her whiskers, making her rounds from warm pipe to warm transformer, sleeping on whatever hums. She headbutts my shin and doesn't meow because the Basin is too loud for meowing. I want to stay with her a while, in that cat-logic of heat. But she is loved, fed from a tuna tin by the Shed 4 door. Someone waits for her. No one waits for me like that. Not there.

I keep walking until the apron empties and the inside-rain veils in, hissing faintly on the transformer casings, beading on my coat. At shift change the press pauses. The pumps throttle back for thirty seconds to clear the intakes.

And in that dip I hear the drips. Thousands of them. Plink on steel, splat on concrete, tick on ice. Every head tilts up — human and crow and cat — checking pitch, checking weather, checking if the place is still breathing.

I tilt up too. And I realize I have been looking in the light.

Between Shed 4 and the Pump House, tucked behind the water treatment skid where nobody lingers, are the three open concrete tanks. Green-black and steaming faintly. The settling ponds. The aerators mutter. Sodium orange catches on the skin of the water and breaks.

I stand at the edge and look down.

At first, nothing. Then a shadow turns, slow. Huge now. Three feet long. White as membrane. Eyes filmed over from years without real light.

Dumped as fingerlings years ago to control algae. They ate, they grew, they stayed.

That's where I find myself. Not keeping the pumps or walking the lights. Under. Hidden. Most permanent.

I would be one of the blind carp.

I live in the coldest, darkest water in the Basin and I never leave it. I turn all day in the green-black, mouth opening slow, filtering what no one else wants — the bloom, the scum, the excess. I make the water move by taking it through me. I have grown huge on what was meant to be a small job, a temporary fix nobody cancelled. My eyes are useless now; there was never enough real light to keep them. The shafts dissolve halfway down. The sodium never reaches here except as a smear.

I surface only when the aerators stutter — that little falter in the machine-breath — mouth breaking the skin of ice-water for cold air, then sink again. No one fishes me. It would feel like eating a coworker.

Around me the Basin sweats and freezes and cracks and pumps. Mule grazes its perfect rows to rust, beeping softly to no one. Kolya curses the ice. Mara listens for the dampers. The moss holds in the expansion joints, tough as felt. The liverwort drinks rust under its flange. The cat sleeps on a hum.

And I turn. Patient. Volunteer turned infrastructure. Staying damp all the way through.

KEEP WATER MOVING. That's the prayer. I keep it moving inside me.
