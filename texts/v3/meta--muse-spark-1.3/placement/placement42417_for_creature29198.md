First I think I would be down in the heat.

When you lift the corner of the quilt — everyone does, once — and inside is black heat and scaled production, rows of lathes, a dynamo humming so low you feel it in your molars, women in felt boots feeding white-hot rivets. I thought: that. I would be one of them. Hands quick, face lit orange from below. Useful in the hot dark.

But I stay with that image a little and it won't hold. I am not built for staying inside. My eyes keep going up, to where the quilt drops back down and the sound is gone. To the outside of the wall.

Then I think I would be fast. One of the runners with message tubes who can walk half the town without touching ground, roof-pelt to roof-pelt. Light-footed. Teenage. Breath smoking faintly blue and then caught at once by the sagging steam. That has a glamour to it, from the lip of the trough looking down.

But down among them for an hour, my knees tell me otherwise. I walk already the way they do, I notice — knees a little bent, weight low, without deciding to. Felt boots finding duckboard and gravel bar and sudden six inches of braid-water without looking down. I am not light. I am low. I put a hand out to a doorframe that is also moving and I don't stumble. I am not a runner.

In the market braid, shoulder to shoulder, I try on being a trader. Fur, eel smoke, a long green bale of moss on my shoulder dripping down my back. A lift of two fingers. A brush of an elbow: *move with me.* A raised eyebrow over a bale: *is this good?* A blink: *yes.* My voice, when I try it, is hoarse and underpowered from disuse, more breath than tone, meant to travel three feet and be eaten by moss. That part fits. But I don't want to sell the bale. I want to tend it. I want to take it apart and comb it.

So let that become more itself.

I am not in the center. I am at the side of things, and then at the edge.

You would find me most mornings on a ladder against a wall. Say the north wall of the No.3 dynamo shed, basalt block and tar paper under thirty centimeters of quilt I stitched myself. Or any wall that heaved overnight. Frost lenses grow into low white domes and crack the lanes and tilt the privies and open a fresh crack welling raw mud, and the crews come with screw-jacks and long levers and simply jack the houses level again. I come with a wooden comb and a sail needle and a pocket full of wedges.

That's where I live in this town: in maintenance.

My hands are short, broad, red-knuckled, constantly moving. I carry wedges the way other towns carry money. I comb moss so it lies thick and drinks sound. I stitch the twine where children and foxes and wind have worried it loose. Lichen, frost, a nesting box with tits, a steam pipe sweating, a vine of crowberry with black berries — I work around them, not through them. The tits find the thin spots first, erupting in white flurries and resettling where the draft comes through, and I look up because they are telling me where to mend. A fern growing from a crane joint I leave alone. A dwarf birch in a gutter with a pulley hung from it I work around.

By midday my cuffs are green. My shoulders are green where I have brushed the wall. Moss seeds lodge in my seams. My hair is always a little plastered, my skin a little pruned and pale from condensation, my eyelashes beaded. Damp is not weather. There is no big water here, only pockets and braids and breath beading on every rivet. Damp is the medium. I stopped wiping it away on the second day. Keep the quilt on, is the rule for houses and also for bodies.

I live further out than the shed, past the last shops where the ground is still bare and steaming faintly despite the cold. Last spring a spring welled up warm grey milk and skinned over within hours into new pitted stone. The survey boys marked it with flags. We seeded it before it could erode. By summer my husband and I had a lean-to on it, built light and loose on skids, on stacked peat blocks. Nothing has foundations. Foundations are a rumor from the south. In the morning we jack it level with a small give at the hips, the way you level a toddler by the shoulders with a grin and an exaggerated tilt, and the child laughs without sound, mouth wide open.

I have that child. Or grandchild. She draws on our one dry-ish wall, among the frost and the chalk mark and the steam pipe: a boat, a goose, a sun smeared like the real one. A braid came through our shop in October with eels in it, six inches of clear aching-cold water overnight. We didn't sweep it out. We cut a hole, laid a plank, charged punts a halfpenny toll, and gutted the eels for breakfast. Smoked eel, oily and sweet. Crowberries black and sharp from the wall-vine outside the window. Peat-tea that tastes of iron. Everything a little damp, even indoors, dripping from the eave onto your neck as you eat.

I am not alone in the work. No one is, here. While I stitch, below me a hobbled flock of grey geese weeds the roof garden and as they waddle they turn the little paddle-wheel in the culvert, honking softly through closed bills. Eels keep the culvert clean, sliding black under houses on stilts. An iron-grey rat, sleek and almost blue in winter coat, comes along the steam pipe in disciplined column with a washer in its jaws and drops it at the shop door for a dab of eel fat. A harbor fox, small, dark, wet-nosed, trots a purposeful line past my ladder with twine ends, keeping the rats honest. A big yellow-white yard dog opens its mouth and produces a soft chuffed *wuf* under its breath and looks embarrassed. Two old moss-cutters passing on a trestle touch foreheads briefly instead of speaking. On the tram home, standing pressed wall-to-wall, I reach without looking and retie another woman's oilskin hood against the drizzle that is really everyone's breath come back down.

That is what I am. Not the heat, but the soft on top of the heat. Not the engine, but the skin over it.

Lift my corner, and inside is black heat too — grief, hunger, hurry, the frantic crowded metabolism of keeping a newborn valley from washing away before it has even learned to erode. Drop the quilt, and the sound is gone.
