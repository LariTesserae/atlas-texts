At first I think I'd be anywhere. That's the trouble with coming over the rim.

I'd come over the way you do, ears popping, lungs coming up short, expecting cold because of the height and getting it wrong at the ankles first — the ground breathing up through my shoes. I'd stand there too long looking down at the bowl, at the terraces stepping down into steam you can't see through, at all those sodium halos burning in amber dusk for nobody. I'd tell myself I'm just passing through. An observer. Margin-life with boots on.

So I'd start up high, where it's almost bearable. The housing grid. Three- and four-story tenements, laundry lines still strung, shutters half-open. I'd do what a visitor does: knock on an open door, say hello too loud into a room with a chair and a kettle and a bed made, and hear my own voice come back off wet concrete. I'd sit on the bed because it looks inviting and then get up again immediately because of the pressure — that hollow that sits in the ears inside buildings here, like being too deep underwater. No one sleeps there. I'd learn that first.

I thought I might be the tram person. There's a tram stopped mid-street, pantograph still up, windows fogged from the inside. I wipe a circle and it fogs back while I'm looking at it. Someone breathed there not long ago. Or I did. I'd sit in the driver's seat for a while and listen to the drip ticking off the awnings outside, the plink in the cisterns, my own thin breathing. I'd think: I could live here, in transit, never arriving. But the seats are metal. After the siren — and there will be a siren, low and spreadeagled across the bowl — I understand why my thighs are branded through my pants. Don't sit on metal after the siren. I stand up fast. I don't sit there again.

So I drift. That's what the empty does to you. You walk a whole boulevard and you're the only footfall, slapping back too loud, and you start walking quieter without deciding to. You start moving when the city moves. A pump house shudders awake, groans water uphill through a fat insulated main, and under that groan there's room for your feet. Louvers breathe. A rooftop vent lifts with a soft *whoosh* and dumps white that falls as hot rain. That's when you cross. You learn it the way an animal learns it, between cycles, in short naps curled not in beds but in the lee of cisterns, in pump houses where it ticks.

I would find myself, I think, where the water is born.

Up on the upper roads, where it weeps straight out of the cut rock in long dark tongues. Where every cool pipe and cable sweats condensation and rains it back down in a localized drizzle you hear before you see. The city built half-pipes bolted to walls and aqueducts on stubby legs to catch exactly this, and they clog. They clog with salt cauliflower and fallen moth wings and sheets of that rust-colored gutter-film peeling up when it dries.

I'd stop to clear one with a stick because my hands need something to do while the pump is running. I'd clear another the next day with a hooked pole I find left against a culvert. On the third day I'd turn a valve a quarter-turn back from screaming because no one else is there to do it and the pipe is shaking itself loose. Just coaxing. No one could run this place. You just coax it.

That's how I'd become — slowly, without a decision — one of them. One of the thirty, spaced a tram-route apart.

Not down in the furnace heat. I couldn't take the core. Up high, on the weep circuit. A range of stairs and cisterns and shaded walls where the sulfur blooms white and yellow, fragile as frost. I'd learn to scrape only the white heads, never the yellow, and boil them with handfuls of lamp-moths. At dimming, when the pilot flames come out as a constellation, the moths get so thick around the work-lamps they cast huge slow shadows, and I'd do what the tenders do — walk in with my mouth and shirt open. Protein that tastes of struck matches. It would disgust me the first night and sustain me the second.

I'd stop washing the varnish off. That living slick in the gutters, not plant not fungus, orange-brown in the creases of elbows and behind ears where sweat never dries — it keeps the metals from raising blisters. I'd let it stay. Salt would bloom at my hairline, white on my eyelashes. My teeth would go faintly blue at the roots from living inside the transformer hum. I'd learn to press my jaw to a cool pipe to listen for whose hum is cycling where, so I keep my distance. Vast space between any two living presences. I heard someone coming a half-mile off once, by the slapping of bare feet in hot runoff stopping when the pump stopped, and I stepped sideways into steam before I even thought about it. Like the heron does.

Oh, the heron. The featherless-looking gray wader in the outflow marsh where the water finally spreads and cools into black mud rimmed with white crust. There's only ever one, maybe two in a day. I would go there when my circuit allows, ankle-deep in warm muck, and stand statue-still for an hour the way it stands, waiting for heat-ticks to come to the salt on my legs to pick them off. I learned that from watching. Or it learned it from me. It doesn't matter.

You'd find me at venting hour under the same awning on the lower plaza. When the vents all close in the stillest heat and thousands of droplets hang at chest height, neither rising nor falling, catching lamplight for a full minute — the city inhaling. I'd stand at the edge with my arms held a little out from my sides, head bowed, mouth open. The only water clean enough to drink straight, condensed from air before rock touches it. Manna, twice a day. My double shadow would pool at my feet like spilled water, lagging a half-step, paler at the edges. Everyone gets it after a while. Accumulated steam. A personal cloud.

In the mornings I'd press my palms to the film in my gutters and read the night's heat in its thickness. Same organism up here as down by the furnaces, continuous for miles through canals and weeps. It tastes what I sweat. I feed it piss and moth husks. It keeps my pipes from corroding through. In a way I'm never alone.

And at night I'd walk with a taper, relighting my small constellation of pilots on walls and stacks so the gas doesn't pool, outlining my stairs and cistern rims so the place stays legible from the rim for whoever comes next over it, ears popping, expecting cold.

If you stay until the dim dims further, don't try to follow me. You'll hear the *tink-tink-tink* as cooling metal — that's me saying *pump about to run, lift your feet* — or three taps for *get under*. Take cover. Listen under the hum in your teeth for bare feet stopping and starting with the pumps. For a second the steam will part across the plaza and you'll see a tall thinned gray figure, salt on the eyelashes, moth-dust on the shoulders, mouth open to the settling air.

Then the echo will take its time crossing the bowl, and I'll be gone back into the grid, back into the vast space between, where I live, and tend, and sweat, and hold.
