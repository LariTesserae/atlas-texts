At first I thought I would be the sitter.

On my belly through the creep-hole, then standing up into green aquarium light, knees in the moss, arms not touching the walls because the emptiness asks to be kept. Breathing slow until breath shows. That one. The weather one.

But I'm too tall for that. Even sitting I'm up. My blood is too hot — ninety-eight degrees in a fifty-two degree room — my heart banging away in my sternum where the roar lives. I would be a stove in a cellar. I would be a gradient. The place has no gradients. I can't stay in the middle. The middle is mown to velvet. It's hers.

So lower.

If not the visitor, then one of the workers? I try wings on for a minute. A hoverfly stitching the interstices, a gnat in the cloud. The diesel idle in my own thorax. Shift work that never ends. It fits the loudness, but I can't hold it — I'm too purposeful, I want to go somewhere. The gnats don't go somewhere. They go down. A hundred an hour, a thousand a night, a steady rain onto what looks like moss. I feel the pull when I try it, that magnet thinning of the drone over her back, iron going to iron. And I don't want to land and not take off again. Not yet.

Lower still. Under?

I try the shrew-run for a while, the conveyor hiss under the floor moss. That is low enough. Hot heart, a thousand beats a minute, blind hurry in the dark under velvet. Pausing because a cool warmth has settled over the tunnel — cellar-warmth — a body-length of quiet where the hiss stops and then resumes. Running under her unafraid because she is weather. I like that, being fast in a slow place. But I am not fast. My breathing won't stay that quick without panic. And I would have to eat constantly, and I would leave tracks.

No. Lower, slower. Wetter.

I try being a drop. Forming on the underside of a ceiling frond, swelling, catching green light, hanging. Drip. Tick lost in the roar by her knee. That is clean. Drink nothing but air, then be drunk. But a drop only lives once, and then it's gone into peat or into her snout. I need something that stays.

And then the print fills in.

Not the long press in the floor ahead of the knee. The ordered bead. Dew standing in rows along a lateral groove, large drops that don't fall. I am in the row.

Find myself: not in the middle air at all. On her. Low on her.

Mid-body, left side, in the groove where flank meets back, where the skin dips to a channel and the sphagnum stars have rooted. I am a tuft of feather-moss, no taller than an eyelash, rooted not in peat but in living cold slime.

That's where I am. A hand's height off the floor moss, which is to say six feet under the thatch ceiling and three feet above three feet of peat and a buried log sawing with beetles. Riding the pan. The broad head is two feet ahead of me, blunt snout with slit nostrils, eyes like condensation that never blink. The paddle tail is four feet behind. When she rests I am less than a hand off the ground. When she moves I am higher, lifted by slow muscle.

Who I am is simple and keeps getting simpler the longer I stay.

I don't have lungs. I breathe through leaves. Breathe slow is not a rule for me, it's anatomy. I drink nothing but air because there is nothing else to drink — no open water, only damp as a condition. The big drops swell on the stems around me, hang, are drawn in, not by me but by her skin underneath me, her belly sealed to the floor moss, her flank drinking. I drink after her, from her breath.

I wave when she draws breath through her skin. That's how I know I'm alive and where I am — that faint pull, twice a minute when the fringed plumes laid flat along the neck open very slightly, blood-red at the stem fading to green at the tips where my cousins, the algae, live. Water moves. I lean.

My roots are in the pits behind the jaw at the edge of my colony, in olive dark slime that exudes cellar-bitter alkaloid. Nothing eats me because nothing eats her. Deer go around twenty paces out. Birch seeds that helicopter in and sprout in drip-cups get nipped by a tongue as wide as a hand — I feel the floor of her mouth drop every two, three minutes, slow bellows — but I am not nipped. I am the thatch she carries. I grow a millimeter a year on her back, die back in winter, grow again. Climaxed to equilibrium. No progress.

From here the roar is different. The diesel of wings is weather overhead, not in my sternum. The wren lineage in the east wall sings over me, metallic, ricocheting. The deathwatch saw is deep under me, through muscle, through peat, loom-work. Her heart — four, five beats a minute — is completely lost, which means I am inside the quietest part of the machine, the held air between pistons. Silent in the loud place. Old in the old place.

Once a night, maybe less often, we make the circuit.

Not walking. Flowing. Peristaltic oozing on four short splayed legs that never lift enough to tear moss, belly distributing weight so the living thatch never breaks. I tilt as the pan tilts. The chin barbels brush ahead, reading vibration. We stay an inch from the walls — walls are alive and load-bearing, she knows — and pause under each of the hundred drip points. A drop forms overhead, catches green light, grows, lets go, hits — not moss, her snout. Tick. Taken. Another forms in its place. We move on before it falls.

Gnats rain down onto us and do not take off again. Spores puff from the ceiling in static discharge before rain and settle on my fronds and are drawn toward her mouth by ciliary flow. I taste the weather coming before the dome does because my hairs lift with the charge. Dorsal hairs lifting. Hairs lifting.

It was flat when I came. It will be flat when mosses have sealed the creep-hole and opened another somewhere else, same size, same cool, same roar. I will still be in the groove, a millimeter taller, a millimeter rotted, keeping the room exactly the same size by riding the one who keeps it empty.
