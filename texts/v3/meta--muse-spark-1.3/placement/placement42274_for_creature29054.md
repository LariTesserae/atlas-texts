At first I'm still just standing there.

In the middle of the four-lane service road where you can see half a mile either way and see no one. Shirt already stuck to my back. Metal too hot to touch. The sky huge and low, that hot-milk white, sun a dull coin behind it. Everything dripping long after the rain — gutters pouring, rust-water bleeding in orange-black tongues down the corrugated walls, BAY 7 — NO PARKING — KEEP CLEAR chalked to ghosts.

I hear the pump to the south first. Put-put-put. Felt more than heard. And then plink from an awning into a puddle, hiss off a hot roof, my own breathing too loud in the wet air.

Then the clicking. And I can't un-hear it.

If I stay here I'm going to have to be something. A person doesn't stay. No one stays. So I try them on, one at a time.

I think I would be high. One of the kestrels hanging up there on the heat coming off the concrete, wings blurred, tail fanned. To see the whole spread — the tank farm, the cold stacks, the laydown yards turned to shallow lakes, the rail spur disappearing into standing water. But it's too exposed up there. Too much sun, too much work to stay still. I'd have to beat and beat just to not move.

So then low. One of the three dogs working the far road, yellow, black-muzzled, nose down. Scattering rats and then giving it up because it's too hot to chase. Lying panting in the two-foot shade of a tank wall. I can feel that pant, flanks heaving, ears still up. But it's too open out there on the four-lane. Too much waiting in the open.

I think closer, inside. Up on a beam in the dark volume of a warehouse with hundreds of pigeons, blue-grey and dun, cooing low, dust sifting down in dim shafts. Whitewash of droppings below over oil stains. That soft roof of living sound. Tuck your head and doze through midday steam. But it's crowded. Too many bodies ticking and shuffling.

Lower still. Along the base of the wall where the rats have worn their runways — along pipes, along siding, culvert to dumpster to pallet stack. Brown, slick-backed, whiskers bright with water. Flattened now in shade with mouths open, panting, not going far. Drinking only from drips, from an awning edge, from a tank seam, never from the oily ground film. I can feel how to run that way, pour and stop and pour. But it's too hurried. Too much hunger, too much scurry.

The crabs are even more hurried. I stand still and they go around my boots, thousands of them, fiddlers waving that huge white-ochre claw, shore crabs flat and quick, clicking claw on concrete, shell on shell. Picking at the iridescent skin on the puddles, eating drowned midges and gull shit. They must keep moving because they dry from the top down. I don't want to dry from the top down.

The cats are close. Very close. I can feel that one — thin, brindled, belly low, dashing shadow to shadow between pallets, amber eyes huge in the dimness. Knowing the rat runways better than the rats. Licking condensation off the underside of a rotted truck. Kittens somewhere back in the dark in rotted pallet fiber, blind and mewling. But even she has to dash. Even she pants.

I keep walking, without deciding to, toward where the salt comes back in. Toward the canal edge. The lots get softer. My boots — when I still had boots — sink to the ankle in black mud that smells of diesel and rot and salt. The air shivers with columns of midges over stagnant water, flies blue-green over a dead pigeon in a ditch. Gulls stand one-legged in two inches of water. Egrets high-step through the flooded yard, neck coiled and then thrown like a spear.

And on the wall, motionless as a shut valve: the herons.

That is where I find myself. That is what I become, as the heat settles and settles and doesn't lift.

On the canal wall at the far edge of the last shed, where concrete meets seep. My feet — grey, scaled, cold-blooded — grip barnacle and mussel crust, that black band that opens and closes with the tide, filtering what it brings. Below me the water holds sky. Above me the hiss. Behind me the whole ticking town of meat eating meat. In front of me the marsh exhaling salt and sulfur and warm mud, and beyond that the haze that is the sea.

I am blue-grey. Old. Neck folded back into my shoulders so I am just a hump, a valve, a shape the heat can't find. I have removed myself from the heat entirely. Not like the dogs panting, not like the gulls standing on one leg, not like the pigeons tucking heads. Removed.

I don't move. An hour. Longer. A crab side-steps too far from the rut. A midge column drifts low. I do not waste movement — that is the rule here, normal as any tideflat: stay out of midday, drink from the sky not the ground, don't waste. I take my water from rain as it falls and from the bodies I spear. I strike only once, sudden, neck uncoiling, throat working it down whole, and then I am still again.

Steam rises off the road behind me in thin ribbons and is gone. Water ticks off metal. Pigeons murmur in the dark sheds. Rats pour along the walls. The cats watch. The kestrels hang. And I stand, and stand, until the light dims and the evening rain comes again.
