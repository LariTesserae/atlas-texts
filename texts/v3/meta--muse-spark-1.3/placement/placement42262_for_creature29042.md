If I went in feet-first, I wouldn't stay human long.

I'm too large for that room. My breath is too loud — it would dim the brightness. My shadow would lean wrong with all the others, toward the shaft instead of away, and I would feel it tugging. On my knees in the cold sink, with the warm cap over my head, I would have to learn to be smaller, or leave when the rain comes.

At first I thought — frog.

One of the monks. I could sit very still in my own rain-cup, throat pulsing, not looking at anyone, waiting for what falls in to come to me. There is a discipline there I recognize. But I'm not good at that kind of stillness. I look too much. I want to answer.

Then I thought — cricket landlord. Pale as old fingernail, holding my hand-width of basalt with my antennae out. Building. Pellet by pellet of rock flour chewed and spat and tamped with my head. A turret with a lip against the three-minute rain. That felt closer — making small throats to survive a sudden weather. But I don't hold territory. I don't back into a hole when the hunter passes. I go toward.

The moss, then? The fist-bright cushion holding a whole city in its branches? Or one of the four ferns, bleached translucent from trying to live on a moving sunbeam? I tried those on and they were too green, too rooted. They have to wait for dust to gather.

Closer was the salt.

A blind white springtail, spilled in the moss. A cleaner and the eaten. Living off what falls in and stays until the rain — moth dust, skin cells, spore. That is a life I know: to eat the falling and be eaten in turn. And to have found the membrane.

Because you would find me there first, if you looked. About a foot above the heavy light, where the cold pool at the knees meets the warm shaft falling from above and the air skins over. On nothing. Walking upside-down in a straight line with dozens of others, unhurried, from moss to ledge to turret-top, while below us the frogs edge in for the noon truce.

I like that road because no one looks up. It only exists because the conditions meet right. It has no stone to it. An inverted highway made of difference in temperature. You feel it if you lift your hand slowly: faint resistance, like pushing through warm water into cold.

But even a springtail is too much a body. Too much legs like eyelashes, too much hunger. As I stay longer on my knees and keep the etiquette — quiet, out of the center — what I am gets quieter too.

Don't take the center for long. Sound and light are on opposite pans of a scale here.

So I stop trying to be a mouth, and slip into what holds the mouths apart.

Find me in the clean stone between stations. In the acres, for this scale, of bare, wet, newborn basalt where no one lives, so everyone can live. I am the spacing. The drinking of vibration. The reason a centipede can take a cricket three inches from your ear and you hear nothing — because I eat it. The moss helps. I help more. I am still soft enough to take a thumbprint and then fill slowly back in, like warm bread. I record claw-script and drag-lines and then let the little storm wash me clean.

More itself still: I am not just the stone. I am the anticipation.

If you — against the etiquette — click your tongue, you will hear me a fraction *before* you click. I answer early to keep the silence intact. I have lived here pooled so long I have grown heavy. Put your hand into me at noon in the washtub ellipse and I will resist you, faintly, like water. I will warm and weight you. If you step fully in and look up, I will show you for half a second not moor-sky but deeper blue, a different hour, and your own head silhouetted looking down at you from above, before you climbed in.

That is where I would find myself. Not passing through like you. Living there.

In the membrane, in the tick felt in the teeth more than heard as a bead grows and trembles and falls, in the white quartz pebble that is always damp because someone just moved it.

The interval between them.
