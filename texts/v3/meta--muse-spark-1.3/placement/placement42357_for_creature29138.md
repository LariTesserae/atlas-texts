At first I thought I would stay on the ridge.

Up where you come over the shattered rock and your thinking thins to a wire, where the far wall looks close enough to touch and your breath falls out of you and pings as ice. I thought I would be the watcher — upright, loud-hearted, counting the amber lights as they strung themselves half a mile across the black moss. Dim, bright, dim.

I can't stay there. Nothing upright stays there. Anything loud is noticed.

So I get low. I crawl, knees giving into cushion moss that gives back like a lung, cold and damp through my clothes. And for a while I want to be one of them — one of the boilers.

Who wouldn't? To be that useful-hot in a world that kills heat instantly. To be barrel-bodied and six-legged and thatched with living moss on your own back, to crop tons of night with a silent radial mouth and do slow smelting work inside. To hear, if anyone lay belly-down long enough to listen, your own double-thump turning over: *chuff — chuff — chuff.* To vent white columns ten feet up and rain warm drizzle in a circle around yourself, and have ferns twice a man's height lean toward you over generations. To be a walking weather. To be farmed and farmer at once.

But I'm too loud for that. My heart is the loudest engine for miles. I clank in the wrong places. I would eat too much and give back too fast. And I don't want to glow through slits in my sides all night where the pale ones can see.

So then I think — I will be pale.

It would suit me, to be blind and white as rime, to have no eyes at all but a wide dish for a face veined with radiator lines that flush and pale. To drift between herds at impossible slowness, making no sound at all in snow that is more like iron filings than snow. To listen not for sound but for heat, for that piston beat carried up through my stilts from the moss. To be spaced even wider than the herds. To need so little.

But I can't do the smothering. I can't fold my sail-membranes over a young boiler strayed a few feet from the warm overlap and seal the edges to the moss and wait while amber goes to red goes to black. I can't stand up afterward, taller by a hand and faintly amber at the core, and walk away warmer than before, leaving a cooled hummock behind. I don't want to hunt warmth. I want to keep it a little while and give it back changed.

Then, for a frantic minute, I think I will be an iron-midge.

To live my whole life in the two-foot envelope above a boiler's back, where the air shimmers. To be no bigger than a frost crystal and almost invisible, to ride and feed on condensed steam and shed skin and salts, to die in seconds if I drift out. To be that brief and that held. Part of me loves that scale — the scale that is wrong for eyes until you lie still an hour and finally see it.

But I am not brief. I am slow. I store.

You'd find me, finally, not on the ridge and not in the herd and not drifting between. You'd find me in the hollow after they have passed.

Low. Leeward side of a boiler trail, where the steam has collapsed and beaded on everything. A low velvet mat, black-green at the edges, almost nothing to look at in starlight. Under the arch of cold-ferns whose hanging drops swell and shrink and never drop.

I would be lamp-moss.

I do nothing by day — which is night. I just lie there while the hollow fills sideways with fog spilling over the col, while frost feathers grow an inch long on any stone that stands up, while the basalt organ-pipes on the north scarp hum with their mouths closed. My leaves are combed and pitted and hairy with cold-traps. I drink what doesn't fall.

And when a herd comes through — three, seven, twelve, old bulls at the edges venting taller, calves tucked in thermal shadow, blue St. Elmo's fire crackling around the stack-tips for a minute — I take the leak. Photon by photon, the stray orange leaking from their flank shutters as they breathe. I store it in my tips. I don't use it at once.

Hours later, after the moss where they passed has steamed faintly and their ash-rich dung has glowed dull red and gone dark, I release it. A slow green afterglow along the trail edge. From a ridge a mile away you'd just see a faint green line where amber went.

The midges can't resist it. They swarm to me, dust me with spores and dung from other mats, do the pollinating that can't be done by wind in air this still. I lure the insect that cannot leave the animal that feeds me. A closed loop, lit green.

That is where I would find myself. That is what I would be. Not the furnace, not the hunter, not the brief life ridden on heat, but the rooted one who remembers light long enough to give it back when it's needed.

Press a hand to me and I give like lungs and come back cold and damp. Stay still long enough and your exhaled water freezes to your face and I drink it gladly.
