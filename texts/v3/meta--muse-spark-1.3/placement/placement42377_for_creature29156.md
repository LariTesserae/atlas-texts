I thought, at first, I would be someone easy to place.

A tender with sap under the nails. A hand in semaphore across blue distance. That is the honest work here, and I wanted to be honest. So I put myself on a causeway wide as a street, bare feet on bark polished by ten thousand years of feet before mine, and I tried to walk soft.

I was too loud.

I could hear it — the way the Weave eats sound and still found something in me to chew on. My breath too fast for damp air. My pace counting itself. My eyes looking *for* things instead of learning to see quietly. On the second morning I startled a moth-rise by turning too quick on a garden ledge, and for three days after I was on my knees at the cistern-knot with the children, picking sodden wings out of the filter moss one by one, learning the first manner the way you learn a burn: never shout in a nursery void. Not from superstition. Because you will be the one to clean it.

So no. Not a good tender, not yet. Not a hunter — I don't have the patience to rest in a loop where game will not follow, to sit with my back to my own back fading ahead. Not a surveyor — my eyes are still too clear. The old women with milky sight and knotted cords laughed, without sound, when I tried to measure. Walk outward an hour, I counted. Walk back: three-quarters. I did it again thinking I'd miscounted. They tugged the line twice: *we know.*

Let me be smaller, then. Let what wants to emerge get more itself.

Maybe I am not the tender but the tended-edge. Maybe I am the calf.

Up on the limb-highway thick as an aqueduct, the dappled grazers go by on six impossible careful feet, three on each side of the crown. When the mothers feed they park the calves. They wedge them in cradle-forks and leave them. Utterly still. Breathe only if you must. Be a knot of wood until mother comes back. I tried that for an afternoon in a fork above my tightening — tucked knees, prehensile nothing, blinking slow — while a herd stripped moss in long sheets nearby and the light turned the ceiling from dark to silver-green. A bird cut the air and I felt it in my sternum before I heard anything. I was good at being parked. Too good. The tenders use that word for me now in hand-sign when they think I'm not looking: *parked.*

Closer. But not it.

Put your cheek to the wood and hum low. Feel it through the jawbone. Bite a healthy vine and feel if it sings taut and well in your molars. I do that now without thinking, the way you check a door at night. And down leans near the Mother Cables. You learn to lean with it. Your inner ear becomes a plumb-bob that does not hang true.

I live — if you came looking, this is where you'd find me — three floors down from the leaf-skin, on the eastern lip of a void so big the mist in the middle is blue with distance. A single Gordian cable crosses it like a horizon line. My territory is a county of air: one hearth-pocket in a fork where seven hammock-pods hang and sway, a smoke-ledge below kept always low for curing living cord and smoking green-ribbon eels, a garden ledge where squash-vine has run sideways for three hundred years, two water-knots of black blood-warm water rimmed with sphagnum and pitchers big enough to drown a dog, and then leagues of tended emptiness I walk in slow circuit.

That's the address. A tightening, not a village. Nursery of air-plants above where the lantern-pods glow pale at brown-night. Rot-pocket to the left where I piss to feed the heat. Floor-garden to the right where the dead are shallow and the way stays springy.

And who, in that where —

I am the one who came back thinner.

Everyone has to walk a loop once. A limb grafted to its own granddaughter, a root-tunnel that passes through a knot that is also above it. You enter with a lit pod and walk steady and come out behind yourself. Children are forbidden until they can tie their own lifeline. I could tie mine. I went in on a fog evening after the mist columns had risen too fast, too straight, humming through feather-slots and carrying small frogs to the ceiling to start new gardens. I wanted to see where up went when it fell up.

I came out. Pod still warm where I'd left it, though I'd been gone an hour. Eyes a little too wide. As if a part of me decided to keep walking.

No one made a story of it. The rule is simple: everything was made, and everything is alive, and living things do not hold shape the way stone does. Shape drifts.

Since then I don't sleep well in fork-hollows. Other tenders wake from them knowing a knot they never learned — muscle habit dreamed from a hundred years before. I wake with too many. My hands tie in my sleep. In the morning there are small, perfect lashings on my wrist I have to cut away with my stone awl.

So they gave me the work that suits the thin: listening.

I tend the tension-lines strung between camps. Miles of living cord kept damp in moss pouches, thrumming. Codes of pulls every child knows before words. I sit long hours cheek-to-Mother Cable and feel sap move under bark like blood in a throat, and far away, miles away, someone tightening a knot, and the plant taking the suggestion fifty years slow. Tick-tick-tick of drip from ten thousand tips. Sudden soft rush when an upper leaf dumps. Glug of a cistern overturning. Creak. Pop. Leathery shuffle as moth snowfall passes like a thought.

I translate. Two pulls: *slack to the left.* Three quick, one long: *water heavy above.* A low hum felt through wood: *all well, keep emptiness empty.*

The flat one lives along my far circuit. Leaf-green, moss on its back, laid along the trail for weeks. I step over it now without breaking stride. We have an understanding, the cat-snake and I. Neither of us chases. We grow where we are put.

In the evenings the frog-things pulse their throat sacs white then dark then white — a song for eyes, not ears — and the fish in bark-pools flip on stiffened fins through sweating air to the next pool. Foxfire sheets the undersides. My hands stain deeper. Toes splay harder, root-knuckled. I am getting worn smooth like the mainways.

If you put your ear to the floor-garden on a still evening, under the creak and tick you can hear something like a familiar hum. I used to think that was the dead. Now I think it's the living practicing how to stay.

Up here it is warm, and dim, and wet, and hollow. Real wind screams over the leaf-skin at fifteen thousand feet. Stars wheel, thin and furious. Far below, through a rent in root-curtains, rock and cold rivers glint, irrelevant. I put my palm to old wood and feel the answering tremor, and I understand at last what I am for:

Not to build. Stone adze and bone awl and hands were never for building. A cut is a suggestion. A lashing is a prayer the plant may answer.

I am a suggestion the Weave is slowly taking.
