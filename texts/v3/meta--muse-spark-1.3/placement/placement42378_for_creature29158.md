Below. I go below before I know what I am.

The throat first — that was a river once. Now pipe and stair, stair and pipe, air getting warmer degree by degree until my shirt gives up and sticks, until the handrail under my palm stops being stone-cool and is skin-warm. My boots buzz before I hear it. My sternum finds it first. Then the incline ends.

And there is no more down except falling.

I stand on the rim and the room takes me all at once in the dark. Buried-dark. Closed-mouth dark. Eyes wide and useless. Four hundred paces across, they said. Twice that down to the sump. I can't see it. I feel the far wall as a pressure on my ears.

And loud. God, loud. Not noise — a chord. Three, four deeps stacked so close they beat, and a wet-finger whine over it, and over that water tick and fan hiss and feet. The deck under me is perforated steel, warm, trembling faintly. Behind me terraces climb the egg, ten, twelve deep, niches bolted straight into basalt. Pipes thick as trees, sweating. Cables in catenaries. Railings worn smooth. Embossed signs for fingers, not eyes.

At first I think I would be one of the small ones. It would be easier.

I try moth. Big as my palm, pale, furred, no eyes to waste. I ride the nodes like roads, tongue out when the moss spores — iron and fresh bread in the back of the throat — bumping pipe and each other and correcting. If someone claps I drop, deafened. The hairs on my wings overloaded. No. I am too loud for that. I breathe too heavy, I would deafen myself.

I try isopod. Finger-long, blind white-gray in the gutter, antennae longer than my body tasting ahead. *Tick tick tick* on steel after a rainburst, grazing moss, eating drowned moths, eating my own shed skin. Curling to a cold pill when picked up. I love that patience. But I need more air than a gutter.

I try fish. Down in the sump basins between the exchanger fins, black and warm and bitter, thirty degrees and never cooling. White, jaw-heavy, skull mostly resonance chamber. Feeling a drip three hundred meters above through my lower jaw. Darting when a node collapses and true rain hammers the decks. No. Too deep. I still want stairs. I still want hands.

So I come back up, hand over hand on the worn smooth rail. *Switchback here, third terrace.* Deck versus rock under my tongue-click. The too-early return off the far wall — the sound-lens, pressure in the middle bending everything a meter closer than geometry allows.

I find myself rim-side, as most of us are. Not center. I don't live out, not yet.

Seventh terrace down on the lee curve, where a heat exchanger went quiet years ago and left an acoustic shadow. That's where I am. A locker-niche bolted to stone. Heavy cloth curtain for sound, not privacy. A sleeping sling that doesn't creak. A kettle whose whistle I filed down until it sits *inside* the chord instead of fighting it. Chair legs capped in felt. Everything softened.

Outside my curtain, where the pipe sweats and never quite dries, the moss has made a shawl a meter across. Rust-red, rust-brown, cooler than the pipe when I hold the back of my hand close. It lives off leach and heat and what falls off me — skin, oil, hair. When the hourly breath peaks and the drizzle thickens I smell it spore before I see anything: iron and bread. And behind it, under the stair landing where feet don't often fall, my thicket of blind fern. Thumb-high, white-green, veined like eyelids. I had to starve my eyes thirty minutes in buried-dark to learn they were there at all — a milkiness at shin height, not enough to see by, just enough to know. They sip oxygen out around their leaves and the moss leans toward them.

I'm the one who cups the moth.

They blunder into my niche, papery displacement in the whine, bumping cloth. The old teaching is never clap down here. So I don't. I cup and wait in the warm dark until the flutter settles against my pruned fingertips, then carry it palm-open to the deck edge and let the updraft take it back toward the nodes. If it's spent already, I set it gently in the gutter run for the line coming *tick tick tick* to find.

That's my work, mostly. Gutters. On breath-peak when mist hangs at chest height in the nodes, shivering, refusing to fall, and then a node collapses and we get true downpour — sudden clattering rain on steel, steam hissing up off the exchangers — I walk with cloth-wrapped tools that will not clank. Clear algae, clear drowned wings before they clog. Scoop and relocate by hand. I apologize in low vowels as I do it. *Ohh. Ahh.* Sentences that hum, that join. Tributaries to the great tone.

You pass me on the stairs and we both go quiet by agreement, listening for each other's feet to avoid collision. Shoulder to shoulder in black, sweat and iron and a little sweet rot of fern kept too close. We exchange that low *ahh* of acknowledgment and keep moving. Keep moving — the place cycles and you cycle with it. Projectors handing off load, pitch sliding a quarter-tone down and back up. Pipes knocking as hot water slugs through.

Sometimes out in the center a slab breathes a few centimeters up and down, tilts, corrects. The tethers bead with fat warm drops that never reach the bottom. The tether-walkers go out clipped in twice, ankles learning tilt, inner ears remade, swaying when they come back to solid deck. I am not them. I stay rim-side. But at night — if there were night — I lean over the lowest railing I dare and listen down to the sump fish feeling my footfall telegraphed through stone and water, and up to the child somewhere above humming deliberately off-key to hear the room correct her, bending her note back toward its own.

I learned that way too. I offer a small sound. I listen for what the loud dark gives back.

Sweating, walking softly, throat trained with mouth slightly open to feel sympathetic buzz in my sternum when a projector starts to go sour. Fingertips reading the wall. Ears doing what eyes do elsewhere.

Held up, like everything else here, on nothing but noise made solid.
