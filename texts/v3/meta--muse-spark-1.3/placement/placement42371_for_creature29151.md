I wouldn't stay big. That's the first thing.

With my chin near the water I am too much — elbows denting the mat, knees rebounding a half-second too slow, breath making weather. To live here I'd have to get smaller, and then smaller again, until bigness stopped being a size and started being a misunderstanding.

At first I try on the obvious lives.

I think: I could be the frog. Wedged in the axil, throat pulsing, not moving. That stillness looks like peace. But it isn't peace, it's work — every pulse fanning the water over a breathing knot underneath. I can't hold that stillness. My throat would call. I'd break the hum.

I think: I could be the ant. There is comfort in a line that never breaks, leaf-cut up, honeydew down. But the column never rests and never looks up. To be an ant here is to walk forever across someone else's back without ever knowing it's a back. I'd want to stop and drink from the stem-pores. I'd clog the line.

I think: I could be the spider, legs tucked, holding center. Quality control, culling the flies that would lay too many eggs. That clarity appeals to me. But I am not that patient, that empty. I'd fidget and tear the web strung between two hairs of moss.

So I get lower. Not the stalks, not the axil, not the air where the carpenter bees bore like small engines. Lower, where my cheek would be in the moss if I still had a cheek.

The pan. The hand-wide pan in the lowest hollow, skimmed with duckweed and pollen, dark water reflecting leaf-light. That is where the level never rises or falls. That is where she breathes.

If I find myself anywhere, it will be at its rim, where saturated moss-flesh goes soft and gives way to open water. Half in, half out. The place that tightens and loosens, tightens and loosens.

And what I am — it takes a while to stop insisting on eyes, legs, a face to present to the world — what I am gets thinner.

Not a guest. Guests come and go and clog. I become laminate. Felt.

I spread. Green-black from the algae I learn to keep in my own tissue like small green cattle. I grow over and through the fallen leaf instead of on top of it. Leaf falls, moss covers it, mites strip it, fungus softens it — and underneath, I thicken. Not higher. Thicker.

I learn to pump.

At first it's just the base note I've been hearing without hearing, under the frog-chug and the *plik plik* of the drips and the thousand hydraulic ticks. The dynamo in my sternum and teeth. Then it's mine. Millions of tiny collars beating inside me, drawing water in through a thousand invisible mouths I don't have to think about, moving it through felt as through factory felt.

Then I get a face. Only one. That's all there is room for here — presences are few and far between, vast green emptiness between mouths.

A pore no wider than a grain of rice at the rim of the pan. A pale muscular ring, furred with the same root-hairs that stand erect all over the pocket in the faint charge. I wink.

I dilate, slowly, the way morning dilates. I draw in a thread: duckweed, pollen, copepod shit, tadpole breath, one drowned ant still clutching its leaf-cut. It tastes warm and green and faintly hot, iron and machine oil. My own oil-sheen, metallic, to keep from clogging.

I close. Inside me, what needs to be kept is kept. What needs to be let go is let go.

A minute later, a few inches away — which is a country away when you are this thin — I sigh open and jet clear water back out. Distilled. *Tick.*

That's my part in the three rules that hold it together:

What condenses must drip. What drips must pool. What pools must be drunk. I am the drinking. I hold the level. I am the level.

Nothing is wasted and nothing finishes. The wrigglers folding and snapping edge to edge above me are both my crop and my cleaners, eating the bacteria I can't reach, excreting what I can eat. The bladderworts rooting right in my flesh, opening and shutting, are my borrowed stomachs, catching what I miss. The frog on my breathing ganglion fans me. The spider culls for me. The pink fallen flower rotting at exact distance composts for me. The ants walk my back all day and I let them, I keep their stalks turgid with pressure so the sap hisses, I pay them in sugar to bring me nitrogen.

The sound does not stop or change. A bee leaves, another takes its frequency. A frog pauses, the ants fill the gap with footfalls. Now I fill a gap too. My tick, my throb, my jet.

I don't glare. I don't gloom. Light comes through chlorophyll until it has no source, green and even, through the three or four enormous leaves layered like shingles overhead, their midribs groaning faintly under collected rain. The droplet on that leaf-tip has always been about to fall.

Now I understand why. Because I am always holding my breath to catch it.
