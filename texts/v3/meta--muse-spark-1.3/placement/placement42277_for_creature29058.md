I don't know at first where I am. Only that I am low.

I came down off the rim road thinking I would find a street and instead I found weather with addresses in it. The air got thicker as I descended, by steps, the way water gets darker as you wade. By the time the ground leveled my shoulders were damp and my hair was stuck at the hairline and noon was brown-gold around me like late evening that forgot to finish.

For a long time I thought I would be a visitor. That was the first image and it doesn't hold.

Visitors look for noise, for crowds, for something to follow to the center. There is no center here that will have me. Towers a half kilometer apart rising out of green like trees that forgot they were buildings. Between them, kept emptiness big enough to get lost in without leaving the city. Dark fresh stone under my boots, still sharp. Not worn. The curb cuts glitter black where the moss hasn't taken them yet. Everything freshly fractured. I keep watching my step because the ground itself feels young, unfinished, easily bruised.

So I stand in the middle of a plaza big enough to land a ship on and I am the only warm thing in it, and I understand that is a kind of position.

The sail-leaves tell me before I understand it myself.

They line the edge of the plaza, huge dusk-green plates, elephant-ear big, built not for sun — there is no sun worth following under the veil — but for heat. As I walk past, slowly, because everyone here walks slowly or the damp gets in your mouth, they turn. One by one. A slow pivot you would miss if you were in a hurry. I stop. I stand still long enough to hear my own breath and the drip of condensation off the dew-nets a hundred meters up ticking onto leaves below, and the tendrils come out to test the air in my direction.

Tasting me. Breath-moisture. Skin-salt.

At first I think: I will be watched. Then, a breath later: I will be a sun.

That is where you would find me, if you came looking. Not inside. Not on a thread — the threads won't have me yet. I step into the heat-shimmer line and think *where*, clearly and without insisting the way the children are taught, and nothing takes. Just damp disorientation, two blocks sideways from where I meant. The tower is dreaming, or balancing. I have learned not to argue. So I walk.

You would find me on the pale path when the hum comes.

It comes up through the soles first. Bare feet or soft soles, because you need to feel it before you hear it. A low chord rising through stone, through teeth, all the towers tuning together. Everyone without discussion drifting to the pale worn lines across the dark stone, to the overhangs. I have learned that drift. I stand with the others — sparse, unhurried, tall and pale and damp-sheened, eyes wide and staying wide for the dim — and we wait while the veil sweats.

The rain that follows is warm and heavy enough to knock you sideways. It runs fast for ten minutes in the glass-edged channels, mirrors amber in the sharp puddles, then is gone, drunk by moss. We shake off like large animals. Wring the wick-cloth that never dries. Go back to tending.

Because that is what I am becoming, the image getting clearer the longer I stand still for it: not a visitor, not a crowd-person. A tender.

I carry a little water. Everyone does. A gourd dipped from a leaf-axil where the air-river has pooled. I tip it into the corner basins as I pass, to the exact lip, water clear. They are not decorative. The lower floors are poured stone, still outgassing mineral heat, and they drink through capillary stone. Leave a basin empty and by morning there will be a crack like a mouth, sharp as fresh fracture. I have seen it once. I water so it won't happen on my block.

And I witness. The upper floors need it.

I sleep high now, where the wall is that smoky translucent mass that is solid when you lean against it and goes slightly to mist when you step away, as if it only bothers to be real when witnessed. At first I thought it was a trick of the dim. Now I leave my hand a moment longer on the wall. I rest my eyes on a corner while waiting for rain. At night my breathing keeps the room held. The doorway at the tower base is wiped clean of moss at hand-height where all of us have touched it into solidity. My touch is part of that now.

In the tower garden, in the translucent upper light, the white waxy flowers open in an hour, built to glow a little to lure pollinators that mostly don't exist. So I pollinate. Hands dusted. Shoulders brushed as I move between them. I do not pick them — not because it's forbidden, but because cut flowers dim, and then the blind moths will follow. At night they come anyway, big as hands, dusted grey, bumping softly against the floating lamps that drift at shoulder height, and against the faint glow on my skin after garden work. I move through them gently, unsurprised to be mistaken for a flower. To be mistaken is part of the work.

Once a day I crouch with a child I don't know to watch a pale lizard flatten itself on warm stone, throat pulsing, and we don't touch it. High up near the veil something glides tower to tower on long silent wings — animal or feral maintenance drone, no one will settle it — and when it calls, that falling glass note, we both pause mid-step. Not fear. Recognition.

I speak low now. Breath-first, little puffs of words that fog slightly even in the warmth. A raised voice goes nowhere under the veil, comes back strange off walls that eat sound. Much of what I say isn't said at all.

If you stayed until the brown-gold hour when condensation beads on every edge and my shadow lingers a second too long after I move under the drifting cold suns, you would find me standing alone in the vast space between towers, shoulders damp, breathing audible, sail-leaves turning one by one toward the sound of it. The towers casting shadows in different directions, each remembering a different sun. The veil breathing very slowly overhead, kilometers-wide panels shifting translucent to opaque. Sometimes opening an eye — a hundred-meter thinning drifting across the basin, brutal white-gold daylight falling through for a minute while the leaves recoil and the moss steams and we all lift our faces, blinking, shocked and grateful — then closing, the kind dim settling back.

Not lost. Not waiting.

Just being the warm thing the valley orients around for a moment, while the hum rises up through the stone and the rain gathers overhead to fall.
