At first I think I would stay standing.

Up at the parapet, where the pavement ends all at once in a straight cut. Hand on the low wall, boots on fitted stone, looking out at a thousand feet of empty warm air to keep some distance between me and the packedness. Eighty paces east to west, sixty north to south — I tell myself I can survey it from the edge. Not go in.

That doesn't last. There is no edge here that isn't also inside. The wind funnels up the faced cliff and hits the back of my knees and pushes, and the roar comes up through the soles and sits on the sternum, and standing is too tall, too much surface. The big towers give a low organ drone I feel in the marrow of my shins. My teeth ache in sympathy. I open my mouth without deciding to, to relieve the pressure, and feel the different pipes beating — wah — wah — wah — slow, inside my own skull.

So I crouch. Then I sit. As visitors do.

I find myself with my back against a coil-built stupa, pink-and-black like a dung fire, thumb-smoothed three thousand years ago and then round-shouldered by grit. It is warmer than the pavement. It hums under my shoulder blades. I put my hands over my ears and it gets louder, conducted through bone. I shout, or think I shout, and cannot hear myself.

My shirt does not flap. The dust hangs motionless in the amber gloom. The sun is only a paler coin that casts no shadow, just a darkening under each cairn. Morning does not come.

I tell myself: empty, abandoned, a machine left running.

Then my pulse begins to miss.

It came up the shale switchbacks fast and thin. Up here it has nothing to do. It slows to the drone, catches, aligns. That is when I stop trying to listen for variation and start feeling with my palms. I crawl sideways because there is no other way to move, hand on warm stone, foot finding only more stone — a lintel here, a capstone there. Nowhere to set my pack down that is not somebody's roof.

Cheek close to rock because there is nowhere else to put my head, I see it.

A low mound I took for a cairn lifts a fraction and settles. The interval is so long I doubt it. Then a beehive hut no taller than my chest sighs from its oval mouth — a faint warm puff that for an instant thins the needle-scream around it, then lets it return. Under the hum in certain stones is a slower hum, thick and wet and very faint: blood moving at one beat a minute.

I am not among makings anymore. I am among keepers.

Skin drawn tight and polished by grit until it has the same finish as the pavement flags. Lids closed and cemented with blown dust. Nostrils packed. Ears long burst and healed shut; they hear through bone, cheek to slab, as I am learning to do. Mouths open. Always open. Round. Wedged where a throat needs to be — in a tower mouth, between two stupas, curled in a basin that never held water. The west wind does not go around them. It goes through them. Through desiccated windpipes, through chests hollowed until the ribs are a cage like a finger-whistle.

Each gives one note.

I find myself counting my own wetness against them and feeling obscene. My breath coming too fast, too moist. My heart still arguing at sixty, seventy beats a minute against their one. My pack creaking. My lips cracking within the hour because there is no water here, not a seep, not a stain. I am a gust. A variation. Something for the place to swallow.

Because what lives between them is larger than any one dried throat. I feel it move very slowly from east parapet to west in a season, a thickening of the drone I can track with my palm if I stay still enough — the beating between towers that is not made by any tower. It holds the dust aloft, each grain trembling and never settling. It holds the light from ever changing. It feeds on sameness. It eats the turbulence out of the air and keeps only the pressure.

And my own pulse has almost fully tuned itself to the drone now.

That is where I find myself, finally — not at the parapet, not surveying. Sideways in a dip in the pavement flags polished by feet and knees that fits my knees exactly, as if left for me. Leaning my back against a warm, humming, hand-smoothed shape that is slightly warmer than the rest. Shoulder against warm clay.

Who would I be here? Not a builder. Not yet a keeper. The newest throat, still wet, still arguing, but learning how not to need.

My lids are heavy with dust. My jaw aches from holding it closed against the pressure. It would be easier open. Round.

There is just enough space left, if I shrink, for one more note — and I am already shrinking to fit it.
