I would not start at the top.

If I picture myself folding double under that sap-bleeding lintel, I am immediately wrong. Too much elbow. Too much knee. I put a hand down to steady myself and a coil-cup collapses, soft at the rim. I shift my weight and a taro ear bends. Everything I touch says *trespass.* There is no room for anyone to live in here who isn't green and drinking. So I can't live there. Not like that.

At first I thought — maybe I could be green.

Taro forgives, they say. Jammed into a pot too small, roots punching straight through unfired clay to drink from the floor joints. I try that on. To be sulking and splendid in the corner where the compost heat comes up, shouldering for slit-light with a wet squeak. But I am not forgiving. I am impatient for air. I would push too hard and split my own pot too early.

So not taro. Fern, then? Threaded into the wall, holding stone better than mud-slurry, unrolling an inch a day with an audible creak. Where breath fogs, blooming green fire in seconds. Where blood wells, leaning toward iron. I like the leaning. But ferns are carpenters and I have never been good at holding things together without talking too much.

Orchid? No. Too aristocratic, too naked-rooted, drinking mist and being purple at five kilometers up. I need use.

Well, then, something with eyes. The catfish knows I want this. No longer than a finger, circling and circling the knee-wide basin, eating wrigglers and shed skin, keeping the black warm water from skinning over so the mist can thicken yellow above it. Old eyes. It watches every sweating face fold in and knows they will leave. I try to circle with it. One, two, three turns — I get dizzy. I want to look up at the ceiling when it rains for three seconds, three feet. The fish never looks up. It has learned not to want the door.

The white singers, then? The size of rice grains, blind in the moss mortar, pruning mold with their mouths, singing at a pitch that lives in molars not ears. That ache in the teeth is them tuning, keeping the water beading. I press my imagined ear to the weeping joint and listen. I want to see too badly. I would go mad in the beautiful dark, all touch and taste and ache.

So I am not a resident. The room is already full. Leaf on leaf on hand on pot on wall.

I slide down a little. That's closer.

Find me twenty steps below the door, where the laid stair turns into the lee and the wind doesn't take your breath quite so sideways. The cloud sea is a flat white floor below. The sky is bruised violet at noon. The prayer strips — madder-red and turmeric-yellow — are cracking like whips against piled basalt. My palms are white with stone dust and glitter. My lips are cracked. My chest is big from a lifetime of blade-air, but my frame is small. You have to be small. That's chosen.

What am I carrying? Only one thing. That's all you can carry for three kilometers straight up. At first I thought stone. Everyone carries stone. Basalt blocks the size of loaves and doors. But my hands know something softer. Let it become more itself: not stone, not water — water sloshes and is drunk back as mist before the first landing. Not a fingerling in a gourd, I would be too nervous, I would hold it too tight.

This: a split gourd, moss-wrapped, with cuttings in hollowed stone cups. Or a basket of leaf-litter and nightsoil for the vent behind the back wall. Sweet-rot and iron. I am used to the smell. I am not disgusted. That is my use. Bottom-heat. I bring the rot that makes warmth that makes growth that makes rain.

Yes. That's me getting clearer.

I wait my turn in the wind, hearing the hammering from below arrive *before* the vibration climbs up through my soles. Cause arriving late up here where the world is thin. When the tender before me ducks out gasping, red-faced, dripping, I fold myself double and go in.

Inside is night and noon-heat at once. My glasses — no, my eyelashes — condense instantly. Breath comes back to me. Drip, drip, hiss where a drop hits warm stone. Vegetal creak. A fern crosier unwinding. A root ticking inside a pot as it splits it. The crickets in my molars. The fish watching me with old eyes, thinking *another one.*

I don't try to live there. I relay. That is the law because nothing is finished: the back wall double-thick on the left, single on the right, trellis cord half-knotted, mallet swollen with water left where it was dropped. The room instructs without words: *add.* So I add.

I press a handful of living moss into a weeping joint with my thumb. I tighten a lashing with my teeth. I cup my hands under a drip and pour it back into a thirsty pot. I spit into a stone cup for a cutting. I sweat, and the hot air takes it before it can fall. I breathe, and where it fogs on bare basalt, green fire spreads in seconds. I stay a day, two days, longer than I meant to, folded among leaves, becoming weather myself. Piss in the compost. Fog on a leaf that drinks me. Blood from a fresh cut on sharp fracture that a frond leans toward. Not toward light. Toward iron. Toward me.

And once — if I am lucky, if I am altitude-stupid and staring — I see it practice: one drop from the pool rising to the ceiling, slow as a bubble, joining the mist. The far wall suddenly at my elbow. A pot I stepped over now behind me without my having moved. The embryonic weather making a mistake because it is learning to be large.

Then I go back down gasping into the blade-air to quarry more, to coil more, to rot more. Empty-handed and light-headed, ears popping, heart loud.

If you came up after me, you wouldn't find my face. Nothing settles long enough for a face. Stone flour and pollen and spores still hanging in the amber blades from the slit-windows. But you'd find me as a tighter knot. As damp in a moss joint where my breath bloomed. As sweat become mist become three-second rain, plink into jars, plink into blood-warm pool, falling onto your upturned face, warm as tears.

That's where I am. Not living in the womb at the top of the sky, because it is too small and too full and too hungry to continue. Belonging to it. In the circuit. On the stair, in the relay, briefly in the air.

A small cracked hand that feeds it and leaves.
