I thought I would cross it.

Coming up the stair with my shirt already sticking and my glasses fogged, I thought I would walk out on the gantry and stand in the middle of the air to see. To get the whole cylinder at once. You can't. There is no straight way to walk and no middle to stand in. The hollow pushes back.

So I press myself thin instead, like everything else, and try on edges.

At first I want to be fast. To be an orbiter.

To be a white-collared swift hatched in a junction box, screaming with a hundred throats off tile until the scream has no source, riding the engine-heat up the west wall and peeling off at the crown. That would be clean — to never have to decide, just to be flown. But I can't hold the scream. My sternum is too new to the roar. It comes up through bone and shakes me loose. And I keep looking at the clear column in the center where a fat moth hangs, stupid and easy, and wanting to cut across for it. The swifts never do. They take the long way around for a mouthful. I don't have that discipline yet. Not swift.

Then lower, paler. To be a cloud-moth.

That would be simpler: be texture by day. Plaster myself flat to the underside of a fig leaf, wings folded like a closed eyelid, the color of condensation itself, drinking cold through my feet. Wait for the louvers to grind and the true cloud to shoulder in, white and blind, and then rise in a lifted sheet with thousands of others to drink fog mid-air. But I would be eaten. That is the theology — eat and be eaten and carry seed-dust between — and I can feel the sparrow already looking up from its nest of moss and stripped insulation. I am not ready to be blood. Not moth.

Then the tenders. Of course I want to be the tenders.

To have oil-dark fingertips to the second knuckle that never wash clean. To do the morning circuit with the old female, grey around the muzzle, one ear notched from a fan-belt years ago — six compressor houses, fourteen drip pans, the back of the hand held under the fall to gauge the rate, the thumb tipping the pan a finger's width. Not to fix. To tune. A little more to the moss here, a little less to the rust there. To turn a handwheel a quarter-turn and listen through bone for the change in hum and turn it back. That is beautiful work. But the troop watches me with total indifference as I come up the stair. I am not predator, not mate, not drip. I am weather that came up the stairs. My hands are still too clean, my eyes still fog and clear and fog. I don't know the circuits my grandmother learned. Not yet macaque. Maybe never.

I drift down because heat drifts up and I am cold-blooded about this now.

Down past the vines that learned industry — horizontal along conduit, vertical along downspout — past the ferns unfurling one frond per bolt-hole like green question marks, past the duct where an orchid clamps, waxy and the color of a warning light, ticking faintly with moth-tongues. Down to where the air is thickest, pennies and chlorophyll, and the tic-tic-tic of drips arriving late from a hundred heights is loudest.

There. A bend of pipe.

It is warm. That is the whole reason. A bus-bar conduit coming off compressor house three, humming faint daylight power, no apocalypse, just work. The sun comes sideways through a grime-frosted porthole and a fig root white as tendon follows the cable run down twenty meters to find the drip, and where the root hasn't yet crushed the pipe, there is a hand-span of bare, oiled warmth between moss and liverwort.

I flatten myself to it.

This is becoming more itself: my chin finds the heat first. Then my belly. My shoulders go wide and low. My tail goes long behind me along the pipe so the vibration steadies instead of shakes. Belly-scales burnished verdigris-green from generations before me that basked here from 10:00 to 14:00, back dark as oiled iron. One amber eye open. The other closed against spore and moth-scale hanging in the working light.

You would find me there, two meters above the base-ring gantry, on the east arc where the great screw rises, turning so slowly you have to watch the painted mark to believe it moves. A juvenile rides its housing above me, picking moss from the inspection port with quick black fingers, eating some and placing some deliberately on a bare patch higher up. Fragments fall. One lands on my snout with a bead of condensation in it.

I lick it sideways, unblinking.

That is my hour. That is all my hours, slightly moved. This bend of pipe until the vibration shakes me off for grooming hour, when the macaques pile on the warm transformer below to nap bellies to hot ceramic, limbs draped over flowering vine. The loose chain somewhere beats time against a duct. The ventilator fans the size of houses stir once a minute and settle. Leaves lengthen by millimeters a week. Verdigris creeps.

A drip lands. I drink secondhand, from leaf-drip off a tip above me, because water belongs to leaves first, metal second, and I am, for now, more metal than leaf. In the drip pan below, mosquito larvae flick-flick-flick their whole furious lives between one tic and the next. Roaches the size of thumb joints work the grease trap with monk patience. In the sump shadow, green-shouldered rats swim short laps between the drowned wrench-reef and the root-mat. The newts splay unbothered like small gods.

Above me the swifts scream round and round the void and never cross it. I watch them with one eye and do not envy them anymore. I can see what holds us: the column of clearer air, faintly shimmering, where the din thins just enough to hear my own blood. Throw fluff and it circles. Even dust takes the long way around. The garden presses in, leaf after leaf reaching, but never fills. It lives there by not living there.

I will not cross either. My dust will go around. My eye will orbit. When a moth hangs fat in that clear air I will let it hang. Let the swift go the long way. I am too slow for swifts anyway, and content to be slow. Hurry would kill me here; there is nowhere cool to run to.

Press thin. Drink heat through the chin. Be grown over and be maintained — the moss will try my tail, the juvenile will flick it clear when he comes down. Iron for bones, condensation for blood, compressors for the low molar heart in my molars that never stops because if it stopped the night at four thousand meters would come inside and kill the green.

Small enough to walk end to end in three minutes if you could walk straight. I never can. Everything goes around.
