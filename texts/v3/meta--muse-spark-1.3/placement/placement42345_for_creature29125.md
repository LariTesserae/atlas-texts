At first I think I would be standing on the rim, looking down.

No. That is too high, too dry. Up there are the engines I will never see. Up there the light still has direction and clouds still pass. I don't belong up there. You go down into it before you understand that you are already inside.

So I am already inside.

At first I think I would be wading, feet on the good agreed stone, feeling cobble and silt through the soles. But that is too deliberate, too much contact. The floor remembers its pattern and settles back within the hour. If I stand there I am only a disturbance it will erase.

At first I think I would be the fish that isn't here. The absence has so much weight it feels like it must have a shape. A flicker in the green, a hunger looking for the film to graze. But the water is too worked for that. The copper and ozone on the lips say so. There is no second link. I cannot be what eats. I would starve in perfect clarity.

Let that go.

I find myself lower. Not on the bottom, not on the surface. In the middle of the water column, where the temperature is the same top to bottom, where there is no warm skin to float in and no cold sink to settle to. Ten or twelve feet of still water above and around me have taken the direction out of the light. Green-white, even on all sides. Mid-morning forever.

I am suspended there. That is where I find myself: in the circuit.

Not swimming. If I swim my motion feels wrong — too abrupt, too animal. So I stop swimming. I let the slow, machined circulation take me: out from center, around the rim, back to center, over and over. The mote's motion. The weed's motion, which is almost none.

Cool water on my wrists and ankles and the back of my neck. 50 degrees, maybe. It pulls the heat straight out of me if I stay still. My breathing slows without my permission. My ears are half under and I feel it before I hear it — the low hum, 60-cycle steady, felt in the sternum. The far engines turning. Intake. Discharge. Every so often the hum deepens for exactly three seconds and the pressure in my ears changes by a hair and releases. I time myself to that without deciding to. I let myself be breathed.

This is what I am becoming, more itself the stiller I get:

I am the warm pocket. The brief, 98-degree interruption in an equilibrium that intends to stay there. I am too warm to be kept, too animal to persist millimeter by millimeter like the stain, too restless to hold like the four pale threads on their sloping stone. The place rewards stillness and I can only borrow it.

Ten feet away the olive film drinks light, pressed flat to rock. Below me the iron that isn't rust dusts the low spots. Around me gray stone, tan silt, bare bottom mostly bare. Nothing thickets. Nothing covers. Much space between things. I am one of the spaced things for a while.

I thought I would be a resident. I am not.

I am what the Kept keeps company, but does not keep.

I am the leaf.

Once in a long while a leaf falls in from above and circles for days without sinking or rotting, preserved by the cool and the mineral balance, until it is finally drawn up and out. That is its visitor. That is its speech.

That is me. Fallen in from weather into no-weather. Circling without decaying, because there is nothing here to decay with. Not eaten, because there is nothing here that eats. Just held at this level, at this clarity, at this chemistry, turning slowly in green-white light while the four strands sway almost imperceptibly to the same path I am turning.

I do not grow to fill. I do not rot to empty. For days I am sample-kept, companion-kept.

And then the slow circuit draws me up and out. It must. I am heat and breath and abrupt motion. I cannot be maintained.

When I go, the silt I fanned with my passing will cloud and then settle back in exactly the same pattern within an hour, as if it remembers. In a way, it does. For an hour, the shape of where I was will remain.

In ten years someone could put their hand in and find the same cool, hear the same low engine-note, see the same four strands on the same stone. I will not be there. But I will still have the hum in my sternum, faintly, after I climb back up past the waterline into air — the memory of having been, for a little while, the thing it spoke with.
