I would not start where I thought I would start.

At first, climbing — in my head, still climbing — I think I will be the watcher in the fork. The one who has learned to hang. I picture myself high, thatched, still enough to be mistaken for moss. It is a flattering picture. To be soft enough, and slow enough, to belong that quickly.

But I am not. Not yet. I am too loud inside my own chest. My breath comes short and ragged, not pulsed. My eyes keep trying to see fifty paces past fifty paces. I keep listening for a song.

So no. I would not find myself in the fork. Not first.

I would find myself lower. On the mat. On all fours before I admit to being on all fours.

Because the Roof corrects you. The first day you try to walk upright across the felt and you sink to the ankle with every step, *chuff-chuff*, footprint filling with tea-dark water behind you, you learn. The second day your stride shortens without permission. The third day you stop talking to yourself because your voice falls flat after five paces and it feels foolish to throw it away. Your pack rusts. Your knife blooms red overnight. Your nails go soft at the edges.

I would find myself, then, in one of the grown hollows. Not a fresh one — I have not earned a fresh one, I have not bent a stem for a lifetime — but an old one, half-unmade. The one the forest is already taking back root by root. The walls have sagged and the floor-moss is still a finger drier than outside, which is enough. Enough to sit. Enough to stop trying to see the edge.

I would sit there long enough for the hour to be marked by drip, leaf to leaf to leaf. Long enough for my sweat to stop trying to evaporate and just bead and mix with mist. Long enough that the patch of moss in the fork above me sighs and shifts its weight, and I finally understand it was never moss.

That is where. Now — what.

I thought gardener. I thought tender of fungus, keeper of crotch-pools. Hands cupping a tadpole and setting it back unharmed. It is a gentle work and I want it. But my hands are still wrong. Five fingers, too few, tipped with nail. Nothing sharp survives, and I still have edges. I grip where I should coax. I would bruise the shelves. I would spill the black water.

So smaller. That is what emerges if I don't force it. Smaller and more itself.

I would be the tended thing first. The temporary warm spot.

An old one — old is a smell, deep peat and sweet rot — would pour herself hand-over-hand down through the hanging hair-roots to my hollow, taking an afternoon to arrive, and she would not flash at first. She would just settle her water-heavy bulk beside me, barrel chest moving *in-out, in-out*, drinking air through skin, and she would groom me. Those long, boneless-seeming fingers with soft root-tip pads, picking through my hair. Her velvet muzzle wrinkling at my salt. Her flat tongue licking sweat from my neck. Glass frogs pulsing soundlessly in the damp of her armpits, in time with her throat.

I would let her. That is the discipline. To be still enough to be read. A passing hand lingering on my back to read what garden I carry — what lowland lice, what damp spore in my shirt that will bloom green-gold by the time I descend, if I ever descend.

And then — and this is where it becomes more itself, if I stay with it — I would not stay the guest. I would become the garden.

Because everything here lives on something else living. A tree is not a tree but a neighborhood. Why should I be an exception? I came up sharp and separate, a figure cut out from ground. The Roof does not allow that shape for long.

So I soften at the edges. My fell comes in — not hair, not yet, but a green-grey film of algae in the hollows of my collarbones, in the small of my back where mist never quite dries. A moth the size of my hand finds the warmth under my arm and roosts there. I stop brushing it away. I learn to carry it while I move, the way they carry moss-midges in transit.

I learn the other languages because mine has been eaten by the mat. Under my chin I have no dewlap to flood blue to orange, so I borrow. I flash with what I have: the slow pulse of my throat, visible in the pale skin. The lick and nuzzle. The fingertip trailed in the fork-pool above my hollow, in expanding rings. *I am here, I am calm, the water is good.* I practice the double tap. *Come.*

I think, for a while, I am becoming a hanger. But that is still too grand, too fast. Leaves take years to fall here. Rivers migrate a hand's width in a lifetime. A house takes generations of bending. I have only been here weeks, months — I have lost count in the grey-green gloom where morning is only a paler grey.

No. If I am honest, if I let it keep becoming itself:

I would be the young. Not the hanger but the before-hanger.

When my edges are finally soft enough, when I have learned to press and sip and not bite, I would be carried — or I would crawl — down. Down into the felt. Where eyes are useless. Where I go pale and velvety and blind for a time, tunneling through peat meters deep, eating humus, aerating the sponge that mothers us all. A suck and shift under someone else's sleeping mat. Meaning no harm. Being a child. Being nursery underfoot.

You could walk from one edge of the plateau to the other and never step on anything dead. You would step on me, and I would rebound slowly after you pass.

And later — years later, no one counts how many — I would surface near the roots of my birth tree, blinking small dark bead eyes open for the first time, and climb. Not high. Just to the first fork where black water gathers. Just high enough to settle my bulk and not move for days. Just enough to grow my thatch and let an orchid take root in the small of my back and bloom there once in a decade, white in the gloom.

If a giant emergent falls in my lifetime — that deep boom I feel in my sternum more than hear — I will go. Not quickly. Over days, pouring hand-over-hand, flashing low and gold. I will not mourn. I will tend. Guide the tea-dark water around the new hollow. Transplant bromeliads to its crown while it still lives on its side. Lay the cupped young in its new pools.

If a lowlander climbs then, sharp and breathing hard, I will approach while he sleeps and groom him. Plant, perhaps without meaning to, a spore in his damp shirt.

Beside him, almost touching, I will breathe in-out, in-out, and flash, very faintly, blue to orange.

*Stay. Settle. Soften. We will grow over you kindly.*
