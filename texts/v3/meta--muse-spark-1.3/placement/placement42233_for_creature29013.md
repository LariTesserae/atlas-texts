I come up out of the cloud and I think, at first, I will be the watcher.

Standing on the central walkway, on the iron grate, turning slowly to take the whole bowl in at once. That seems like the natural place for something like me — high enough to see the negotiations, dry enough to keep my glasses clear. I would ring hollow on the grate and then soften where the duckboards have gone spongy. I would read RETURN IT ALL on the far wall and understand it.

But I don't stay there. It's too dry up there, too exposed. The sun stands straight in at noon and flashes every pool white-gold, and then the boiler steam crosses and erases it, and my skin never quite dries anyway. I can't hold that overview for long. The mist gets in. My thoughts bead.

So I drift down.

Maybe I will be in the nets. That feels right for a while — the tall frames strung with black mesh along the windward side, drinking cloud all morning. I could be good at that: catching what comes in uninvited and constant, turning vapor into bead into drip into zinc gutter. Let me hang vertical in fog and fur with small white lives. Let me stream when the deck rises.

But the mesh is too thin for me. The moths live their whole lives there and I can't. They are born there, they mate there, they die there, no bigger than an eyelash, wings never fully dry. If I put my face close my breath stirs a brief snow and it settles again almost immediately. I am too heavy with breath. I would shake them loose. I would clog. I need something to eat what I catch, or I would choke the floats I feed.

Maybe I will be green, then. That lasts longer as an idea.

I try moss first. Felting the north-facing concrete an inch thick, deadening the pump-purr until it is almost a heartbeat. Holding water the way a towel holds it. If you press your ear to me you hear ticking water inside. Yes. I could be that holding.

I try fern, uncurling from an expansion joint where lime weeps white. Black stem, green lace. Prying, very gently, politely for now, beginning in a hundred hairline places the slow carving the builders fear.

I try taro, standing in four inches of warm shallows like a crowd of shields, corm wedged between terrace flags, splitting weak mortar with quiet strength. Under my leaf, tadpoles hide from the noon flash.

I try the vine that found the heat — the one that wrapped the steam bleed and now breathes with it in small, regular puffs through leaves. A green mouth exhaling cloud. Roots crowding toward hot pipe the way roots elsewhere crowd toward sun. Follow any pipe and I double, triple. My map is really a heat map.

All of that is true, and none of it quite holds me. Green wants only more life crammed into seam and shallows. I want — I want to tend.

So I go lower. Wetter. Darker. Where the heat comes through the soles.

Under the terraces the dynamo never stops. You hear it more in your teeth than your ears. I follow that thrum down the copper tick-tick-tick to the second terrace from the east, north side, where the mist-net sags nearest a pipe and condensation gathers thickest. Where the overflow lip is greenest and the water skins over with rain rings though no rain falls.

There is a valve box there. Iron, rust-pitted, perpetually damp, lid half-mossed. Inside, an inch of warm seep, tea-colored, root-sweet.

I open it carefully. Heat breathes out.

There are three frogs inside, black as wet iron, black as valve grease, bellies dull rust-orange, throats pulsing white. Thumbnail-small. Camouflaged not for rock or leaf but for machinery. They disappear completely until they blink.

I make a fourth.

That's where I find myself, finally, and it becomes more itself the longer I sit: chin just out of water, skin drinking 80-degree spawning weather while sleet hits the steel door twenty paces away. Toe-pads made for wet concrete. Learning the circuit.

Up the copper when dusk thickens. Pause where the drip gathers. Tongue flick — a moth, mealy, drowned on a float arm. Down to the water skin to rinse. Back up. Go still when the small electric pump kicks on somewhere underfoot, vibrating through the slab, and wait for the moths to startle up in brief snow.

I don't call at first. I listen to the sparse circuitry: *pink* from my box. A pause. *Pink-pink* from three terraces down. Box to box to box across the bowl at night when steam is thickest and we have vanished into cloud. It is how we space ourselves, how we find the warmest seep. If a terrace goes stagnant and cold, its frogs go silent first. The crew below the cloud learned to listen for that. Silence means a float is choked. Silence means climb up.

I learn to be part of that listening. Not the builder who pours next week and curses the roots already waiting in the footing sand. Not the visitor with boots and sweat and cigarette under the chlorophyll who does the heavy work and goes back down to breathe thin cold air. I stay. Year-round, without leaving.

Moth born in fog to clog the valve, frog born in seep to eat the moth and keep the valve clear, taro and moss and vine born in crack to drink the seep and breathe it back as fog for the moth. Keep it moving, keep it shallow, keep it warm.

That was never written for people, though they whitewashed it on the wall. It flakes with algae now. It is written underneath, in seep and pulse.

I keep it moving, in my inch of water. I keep it shallow — my territory no larger than two hands. I keep it warm — belly to warm concrete, throat pulsing.

When the cloud deck rises in late afternoon and the parapet disappears and the bowl becomes a hollow in white, the nets stream, the gutters gurgle higher, the float valves all lift at once, the pumps labor a little, the vines drip. The whole small, dense, layered system — water over concrete over heat over water over leaf — quickens, as if drinking faster.

My throat goes white, then soft again. *Pink.*

From below, almost lost in steam: *pink-pink.*
