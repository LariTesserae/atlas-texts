If I walked in from the scrub, I would come in tall.

Boots that suck and sigh. Sweat that doesn't dry. Eyes still set for distance — rim to rim in one look, eleven trees, glare, empty space. I would think, at first, I would be one of the large soft-footed. It would be nice to be tall and careful.

To be a wading bird, ash-grey neck rising from behind a trunk. To step so lightly I barely ripple. To speak in flashes, a tilt of beak, white underwing. To stand still in the afternoon rain and let it wash me.

But no. I am not that patient. I am not that good at being seen and staying still.

Then I thought — the drinkers. The mouse-brown deer-things no higher than the knee. To come out only when the place has decided I am weather. To drink in shifts while one watches. To step only on stone. Even that is too large. Too much nose, too much ear flicking, too much fear to carry all day.

Crouch, then. That is the rule of the pan. You don't see it until you are low.

When I crouch the floor comes apart. The tea-colored inch-deep mirror by my boot is not water. It is muscle. Tadpoles speckled gold-black turning together, a single slow flank, mouths down. The rim moves. Snails amber-and-black plow furrows through rust-red felt. A beetle stitches. Midges hang in a pillar over it, a humming column that makes no hum.

I am lower than that. I am at the rim, and I am rim-colored.

Don't settle yet — let me become more itself.

Something the size of a thumbnail. Something the color of wet stone, pink-grey gone dark with soak. Belly pressed to black mud where the water meets the land. Legs tucked so my outline breaks. Eyes like beads of the pool itself.

Yes. Her. I would be her.

I live on the shaded side of a pool, not the biggest, not the smallest, one of the chain the copper crabs commute between. My pool is within sight of the arch — that low bent-finger thing half-sunk where rain hangs and never lands — close enough that at night I can feel the stone ring faintly through my throat, like tuned glass. Above me one of the eleven spreads its elephant-ear leaf, waxy and polished. All day it drips on me whether it rains or not. *Plik. Plik.* into the pool an inch from my nose. I don't flinch. Flinching is noise.

I have given up song. We all have.

My throat still puffs — a pale sac inflating, deflating, inflating — out of old habit, out of shape-memory. No sound comes, or almost none. If you put your ear to the mud where I sit you might hear it: *tick. tick.* Two fingernails touched underwater. Instead I speak with light and tremor.

When I want him, I lift my hind foot just clear of the mud and flash it. The underside of one toe is yellow. Not much yellow. Enough in this bright hush to be a lantern. He answers not with voice but by trailing his hind leg in the meniscus behind him, rippling it in a pattern that is ours. No one else can hear it. The thick air would eat it anyway.

I lay my eggs the way we do now: singly, not in rafts. One by one tucked under algae where a snail won't bulldoze them. A striped marble bulldozer passes two body-lengths to my left as I do it, plowing its furrow, and I freeze and become granite until it has gone. Then I go on.

This is my place — that three-inch border where mud is neither dry nor drowned. Behind me, the tufts of bright grass rustle endlessly with skinks and striped rodents on errands. Before me, the tadpole muscle turns and grazes the green soup clear by the hour. To my right, a copper-red commuter comes sideways from two pools over, claws up for a fallen fruit that hit with a soft *plop* four minutes ago. I know to be stone when he passes. He hunts tadpoles in the shallows, but he would take a froglet if he could. When the shadow goes over — too fast, too straight, no cloud — we both freeze. Him mid-step, claw raised. Me mid-breath, throat half-puffed. The white moths under the leaf above us lift in a slow puff and resettle without a sound you could name. We wait for the Listener to go on. Then, as if a switch flipped, we resume.

Once, I lived in the air.

We all did, for a week. Almost transparent, no bigger than flies, clinging to moss under the arch and leaping into the suspended curtain to feed on trapped pollen — droplets that hold station and tremble and part for dragonflies. Below us the pool with its tadpoles waiting to eat us. So we never touched down. We lived in rain that never lands. The huge blue dragonflies hawked the edge, picking midges so cleanly the droplets barely trembled. I remember the taste of iron in that hanging water. I remember light coming from everywhere.

Then I fell, or jumped. Grew opaque, grew stone-colored, grew rim-bound. Now I press my throat to granite to drum through rock instead of air, sending clicks down through the old finished stone. The whole bowl hums below hearing. The needle-pylon near the center holds its perfect dry circle where moths dry their wings at noon and crabs go soft-white to harden. I do not go there. Nothing grows there and my skin needs wet. But I feel its hum in my teeth, metallic, hairs lifting. It is still working, still wringing the cloud down every afternoon.

When mid-afternoon comes and the light goes white-hot, mist rises to knees then chest, smelling of mud and green and iron, I lift my face with the others. It will rain only here, only inside the rim, straight down warm and bright for twenty minutes while the hills stay sunlit. The pools will pit and overflow into each other in brown threads. The wading birds will stand and let it wash them. My eggs, tucked singly, will shiver but hold.

Then steam. Brutal clean light. Drip, drip from the great leaves.

I will still be here, low and wet and fast, at the rim of my city, puffing silently, flashing yellow, rippling water to say *I am alive, I am alive* without ever making a sound large enough to be heard from above.
