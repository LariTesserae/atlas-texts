At first I thought I'd be sitting.

That's what you do here — find the lee of cinderblock, a propped sheet of tin, an old plastic chair with its legs sunk a half inch in soft gravel, and sit with your knees apart and breathe through your mouth until the metal is cool enough to touch. I thought I'd be one of them, browned by rust and amber light, shirt dark in the first minute and staying dark, boots peeling like tongues. Listening for the miss in the pump.

*chug-chug-chug ... chug-chug-chug*

But my hands aren't diesel to the wrist. I don't have a notebook gone soft with pencil. I don't get up in twenty-minute bursts to tighten a flange.

I thought then I'd be higher. It would be good to be high here, to get out of the sweat that never dries. On a roof where the concrete still holds the day into night, lying flat and panting. Watching. A cat, rust-dusted, half-closing my eyes at the highways below because why work in this heat when fledglings fall stunned and the damp under every house seethes with roaches the size of thumbs? Bored aristocrat.

But I'm not dry. I'm not bored. I'm not above anything. I'm too low, too wet. The floor gives under me — not boot-give, skin-give. I don't have boots. I don't have laces going white with mold.

Listen. Under the pump heart, under the wings slapping loud as laundry in a small room, under the clank dropped on a catwalk — there's that other sound that never quite stops. Metallic. A trill that keeps going even when I don't mean it to.

That's closer. That's in my throat.

Find me, then, not in a chair and not on a roof. Find me down where the town really lives, below the high-water stain, below where anyone stores anything they love.

South side, where the twelve-inch main runs the length of the wall on its concrete piers, dripping at every flange. Where the lower six feet weep constantly and the black streaks run down the marl and gather in the foot-wide rill that goes all the way around like a moat. I am in the fringe there, where it's black-green and spongy under — liverworts, pale moss — where the wall sweats most.

I am flat. Low. Opportunistic.

My back is the color of wet clay, mottled brown, warty. My belly is cool against a tire rut full of bath-warm water filmed over with dark green algae that skins and breaks like ice when I shift. A drip lands on me every few seconds from the pipe above — tick — and I blink it off. I don't wipe it. I let it join.

I breathe through my skin as much as my mouth. That is why I can't leave. That is why the heat that sits men down just makes me louder. It is always wet enough and always hot enough for breeding, so my throat pulses, pulses —

— and there it is again, that trill, mine and not mine, one of thousands. Day and night. From the sump pond at the low corner, rust-colored, rainbows of oil, where the tamarisk leans salt-burned over us and we sit fat in its root-shadow that everyone leaves alone. From under every flange. From the rill.

At dusk the swallows scream down through midges and then the bats stitch the air above the water, silent after all that noise, bellies dragging. Later the amber bulbs buzz on and the moths smash themselves on the cages and I hear them before I see them — claws on tin, soft thumps under houses — the night nation coming out single file along the warm pipe, along the slab edges, whiskers testing. Make noise when you walk, the people say, shuffle and clap, so they part like water.

I don't make noise. I don't part. I have bitter skin. The cats learned that long ago and won't touch me, and the rats have somewhere better to be. I just flatten a little flatter into the rut and let them pass, low and unhurried, because there is nowhere to chase them to. The hole is closed.

Sometimes at noon the shaft gets in and dust and midges turn in it and a thousand white streaks of lime flare on the east wall, and a hawk slides over the rim — the only thing that still lives properly *above* — and all the pigeons lift at once out of their gouges in a panic, and their shadow crosses me for a second, cool. I look up with the rest at that small lid of sky. If clouds stack there and the air gets even heavier, I feel it in my back first. *Whumpf*, somewhere, the raw wall slumping in a wet sheet, still deciding what shape it wants to be.

Then the shadow passes. The light goes back to work-light, orange pools and long dark where damp swallows everything. The pump goes *chug-chug-chug ... chug-chug-chug*, patient as a heart that will never win. Rust creeps another millimeter. The floor subsides a finger's width. Water ticks and drips everywhere and comes back as a general wet hiss.

And I go on calling.
