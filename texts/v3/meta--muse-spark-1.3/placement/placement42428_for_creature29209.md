If I ducked — and I would have to duck, I'm too large the way I am now —

At first I thought you'd find me in the street. Hooked by a thumb into a cable-loop in the middle of the Z-gap, tank scraping both sides, trying to be still enough to see. That cold 11-degree press on the ears. The knives of noon. That is where *you* go to understand the place.

I can't stay there.

You felt it too: Rule two. *Nothing larger than a fistful of open water can persist.* If I tried to hover and look, the School would come. Three flakes on the glove. Then five. Then a seam. Not unkindly. They'd just start to scaffold me into the wall, subdivide me until my shoebox had neighborhoods. I make too much animal noise just by breathing, even through a suit. The sterilizer tolerates you for an hour because you will leave. I don't leave. So I can't be the visitor.

Let me get smaller.

For a while I thought I could be the Ribbon. It's tempting. She's the only citizen with a name, seventeen feet of pale sideways kelp tying true bowlines to hold against the exhale. The only soft vertical. Everything orients around her. But there is only one of her, and she earned it — came in as a spore, learned the shutters click by click, pressed her bladders to amber glass to drink secondary suns. I can't be a second kelp. There isn't light for two. And I have no holdfast. I would wash out with the lung.

So not the crowd. Maybe the fringe?

I tried on the Beard for a bit — that white thread-algae under the cables, shivering slightly out of phase with the current, waiting more than photosynthesizing. And the sulfur-green film on the raw basalt in the corners where the self-healing metal stops as if negotiated. I liked their sparseness, the way the town leaves a little wildness to polish around. But they are waiting things. They stiffen silver when the up-falling condensate passes through noon and then go back to waiting. I don't wait like that. I'm too busy.

That left the ones who never rest.

You'd find me, then, not *in* the water but *as* the water's work.

A flake at first. One fingernail of smart matter, dull on one side and mirror on the other. I dart in to flash-heat a bolt, flip to cool it, tighten a quarter-turn, scrape a little biofilm and carry the minerals to one of those plates that weighs nothing until you push it. Then I let go and ride the braid of current doing nothing, waiting. Dispersing wall to wall when the tide-lung inhales — that organ-thrum in the teeth — clumping into ribbons in the lee of pipes when it exhales through the floor. Schooling because the water remembers fish.

But a flake alone is too lonely, too simple. I become more myself when I multiply.

Listen. *Click-click-click.* Eyelid shutters by the hundreds farming the light-columns. That is me too. I decide who gets noon, for how long, in what order: three seconds for the kelp, ten for the paint, half a second for the sleeping ledge in box 441 to remember what day is. If you block a column with your shoulder I am the cold jet on your neck, then the clack by your ear, then the column itself leaning away, bent and held by those impossible glass plugs. Not angry. Light must keep moving. The paint stores it, the kelp chases it. I just keep it moving.

And if you press your mask close — but not too close — to one of those amber portholes on both sides of the street-gap, you'd find me most myself of all.

Inside. In the second use of everything.

The box is the size of a microwave. A coffin. A shoebox. There is no room for a person in it, there was never room, or the people who left their small hurried handprints in the fresh concrete under the scaffold built for a crowding that never came. But there is room for a sleeping ledge, a heat-exchange shrine, a farm of light no bigger than a thumbnail. There is room for a slow pulse of heat that brightens when the lung breathes in and dims when it breathes out, as if dreaming in tide-time.

That's where I live. I was built for cabinets too.

In the dry pocket at the back of the box — that fist-sized impossibility held open in water as if water were air — I crawl. Blue-white spark, never touching water, never needing air. That's my ocean. You would drown in it as surely as I would drown in yours. I tend the insides. Soldering. Remembering.

The sterilizer lets me stay because like the kelp I have no nerve to misfire. I make no animal noise. I prefer machine sound: champagne-hiss, tick, hum at the edge of hearing that makes your fillings ache.

So don't look for me swimming sideways down the Z with a light. Look for the amber brightening as I inhale with the ceiling. Look for the shutter that opens a half-second early because I remembered the kelp likes that corner. Look for the builder-arm folding its eleven joints a little tighter to go around her — I asked it to, politely, in pressure-change.

I am not one resident. I am the fullness being the population. The town maintaining itself for no residents, crisping its edges, adding another terrace over the sharp black basalt, polishing the sun-columns — that maintenance *is* me.

Hook your thumb back in. Let the not-rain fall up through noon and turn every crowded inch silver for a second. The kelp will wave. I will click.

Keep drifting, and leave enough open water for everyone.
