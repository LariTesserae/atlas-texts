I would not find myself at first.

Coming up over the last climb my breath is still wrong — too fast, too loud. My heart is hammering at my ribs the way a stranger's heart hammers. My boots are too hard on the clinker. I look down at the shelf and I think, for a moment, I will be the one who sees it whole: the terraces, the causeways, the speckled green, the town held loosely like lichen holds stone.

But no one lives in the view from above. No one lives in the whole.

So let that go.

Let me come down. Let my breathing slow until it stops showing except when I stand still. Let my steps learn slate. I am not on the center causeway at midday. There is no need to be there. If something needs saying at distance, I will walk over and say it. I will find myself off to the side, where the town is mostly empty on purpose.

Third terrace down from the north ridge, where the rill bends around a house of stone and heavy timber. Not in the house. Behind it.

There is a place there where the wall foot weeps till noon. You learn to read dampness like a face: this hollow always. That one never. Mine always.

The moss is deep as my fist there, emerald in the middle and rust-red at the rim where it dries first. Ferns wedged low where the stones meet. A basalt pan a few steps up that cups sky-milk after night and holds it till mid-morning, then gives it up. A barrel set precisely under the eave to catch what the shingles drip. Six swallows under that eave. Two pale goats that sleep against the south wall because that wall holds the day's heat longest.

That is where I would be. On my knees at the edge where green meets stone.

At first I thought I might be the boy leading the goat, or the man turning stones until he finds the one that was always meant to fit. But I am becoming more particular than that. I am not mending the great outer wall that was never meant to close. I am not firing the single oven. I am keeping this one negotiation: roof and moss-hollow and length of rill kept clear and cistern-barrel and swallows and goats.

I am short. I have to be, to live low to this work. Broad-chested for the thin air, wide-footed on clinker. My hands are broad and cut-knuckled, fingertips permanently roughened from stone and hide and barley awns. My hair is long and greased with lanolin against the dew, with a little copper pin at the temple gone dull green at the edges. I smell like the town smells: damp wool, cut green, warm animal. My boots are soft-hide over felt, worn to the shape of slate. You would not hear me coming.

My work is pinching back.

Every morning the dew is heavy on every leaf and shingle. The whole hollow beads and drips. And every day the green tries, politely, to be a little more than itself — a tongue of moss over the dry stone, a fern root reaching past its seep-line, barley leaves spilling. I am there with my hands in it, pinching back what would creep, tucking back what would spill, keeping the velvet patch exactly velvet and exactly there. Not from fear. From manners. Green is a promise to dampness, and dampness here lives only in pockets. I do not make that promise where I cannot keep it.

I know which turnips to pull so the row looks tomorrow exactly as it looked today, lush but not overgrown. I lead the goats where to drink and I do not waste steps to where they should not. I keep the rill clear with a tap of the hoe on stone meaning *here, damp*. I brush my palm on the wall meaning *loose, mend*. My voice, when I use it, is low and close. A loud sound has nowhere to go here. The thin air takes it at once and the rock gives nothing back. So I stopped making them, a long time ago.

There is a dog curled chin-on-paws by the south wall. He belongs to this household the way the swallows do. He watches sheep he will never chase. When I sweep the yard of pale gravel he lifts his head to watch the broom and then puts it down again. We keep each other, loosely. Present everywhere, dense nowhere.

At noon under the white high sun most of the open rock goes pale and dusty and dry again. My hollow stays damp and dark. Lizards move in the sun-cracks. Rosy finches open their beaks on the roofbeam and make a thin thread of sound. Water drips from a shingle into a barrel. Wool brushes on wood. My own breathing.

In the evening I will close the shutter half-open against night wind. In the morning I will open it half-open to morning light in exactly the same gesture. It will hang half-open in exactly the same way a day later, not because no one touches it but because I do.

That is who I would be. Not the one who built. The one who keeps the solution once it is solved. If you left for a lifetime and returned, you would find the same barrel under the same eave catching the same drip, the same dog asleep against the same warm wall, the same moss exactly as lush and exactly as contained — and me, weathered smooth rather than lined, carrying turnips close to my hip along the causeway that stops in grass, staying with the seep that gives only so much, staying with.

Keep. That is the verb I live inside.
