I thought I would stay on the rim.

That's where you come to it — there is no other way — and for a long time you can stand there with the dry grass hissing at your ankles and just watch. The whole bowl below you at once, gray and packed, tink-tink rising faint as insects. It is easier to be an eye up there. The wind is still yours. Your breath doesn't show.

But the cold pulls.

I came down because I wanted to see the chisel marks up close. Then because the stair-alley wanted a shoulder to turn sideways. Then because my knees learned the bend before I told them to.

You don't decide all at once here. The place decides you downward and inward, like everything else.

If you were looking for me now you wouldn't find me at first. From the rim I am not a person yet. Just movement between roofs, a head bobbing up a switchback and gone. You'd have to come down where the air gets colder around your knees, then your waist, then your mouth. Where morning fog still sits white in the bottom two meters and smells of wet stone and hearth ash.

I live low. Very low. Third switchback from the bottom, where two chipped channels meet and lead to the communal pit we lined with puddled clay.

That's where you'd find me: in the alley that never quite gets sun, even at noon. The walls press close on both sides — my left hand can trail all day if I want, and I do want. The rock here is fresh-broken basalt, keen as bread crust, dark where it is damp. Behind a loose slab at the back of the shared wall, the seep weeps. Plip. Plip. Into a jar. Into another jar when the first is full.

In the beginning I thought I would be a cutter. Everyone wants to be the one with the wedge and mallet in the cold morning when the stone rings brittle and right. Tink — tink — pause to listen for the crack — tink. I tried. My hands are broad enough, cut along the cuticles in those little white lines like everyone else's. The dust is ground so deep in my whorls my fingertips are permanently gray.

But I listen better than I strike.

The old one who can no longer climb sits at the rim of a cutting and tells by sound if the block will come clean. I am not that old yet. I am the one just under her: the one who can tell by smell if a seep is about to shift.

Come close in the dawn fog and I'll show you. I don't stand straight — no one here does for long, ceilings are low to save stone and hold heat — so I go bent-kneed, head-forward beside you. My cheeks go red fast in the cold, then white again. My eyes don't squint; I'm used to flicker, sun to shade to tallow.

Hold your hand out. No, like this. Back of the hand first, to the mortar. Do you feel it? A shade darker means damper. Here, under this slab. Here, where we coax the moss flat with our palms so it holds the moisture and doesn't let it evaporate. It is startlingly green against all that gray, isn't it? We carry the dirt for it up in baskets, a handful at a time. We defend it like an onion tray.

I know the map of water better than the map of names. Who gets the morning drip — the widow with the twins two roofs over. Who gets the evening overflow — the crew cutting back the eastern wall. My eave is tilted to drain to their jar. Their eave is tilted to mine. It was never a law. Just obvious. You do not let seep-water fall to waste.

Midday, when the sun reaches straight down and burns the fog off and the shadows climb fast like water filling a cup, I sit with my spoon in my bowl — barley, onion tops, goat milk thinned with seep-water — and I can hear the place drink. Bucket creak on rope. Condensation ticking off thatch. A pigeon settling on my scaffolding that I don't shoo. A goat leaning its hard head against my leg for kitchen scraps. The dog sighing in the tamped quarry dust and turning over. The rats will come later along the drainage cuts at my feet. I tolerate them. Difficult cousins.

Children pass me all day with baskets of fines from the upper cuttings to tamp down my lane. They scuff, they don't step. They've learned the sideways turn already. They sort as they walk without looking: wall-stone, cap-stone, fill. One of them will take my jar when my hands get too stiff. That's how birth and building are the same verb here. *We are making room.*

At dusk I stop being low.

The tink-tink stops all at once, as if the stone itself called pause. The sun leaves the floor. Only the western upper terraces still hold light. I climb — one hand always on the wall — up to my cousin's roof, which is my floor, which is our neighbor's garden. We sit shoulder to shoulder, knees touching, a hide shared against the pooling cold coming off fresh stone. Hundreds of small tallow flickers come on in niches chipped for exactly that purpose. The whole bowl glows faintly like embers in a pit.

We don't go inside yet. Inside is for deep night, for sleeping packed tight in one warm room while frost pries outside at yesterday's cut and leaves fresh flakes for morning. Dusk is for being together above the fog, breathing together, letting the day's dust settle. From up here I am gray as the rock from a distance, green in my small tended patches up close.

I breathe out and my breath shows. So does yours. So does everyone's, half the year. A low white cloud that joins the fog gathering again between the roofs.

That's me. Not the mason, not the child, not the hawk that circles once and goes elsewhere. The damp-finder. The moss-keeper. The woman in the bottom alley who keeps her palm to the shared wall all night so she'll know by morning where the water has moved to, and re-chips the channel before anyone is thirsty.
