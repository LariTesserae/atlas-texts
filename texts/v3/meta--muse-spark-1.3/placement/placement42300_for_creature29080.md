At first I think I would be walking.

Because that's what I do when I get here — boots too loud on the pumice, crunch, crunch, then nothing. Breath torn away. Light absolute at noon and cold on my lips. I try to go straight, from one green island to the next, the way the three-toed prints go. Straight is efficient. Straight wastes less.

But I can't keep it. After an hour my mouth is dry and I am thinking about water, and nothing here thinks about water. So I am not the sipper.

I stop and watch it far off for a while, that tall folded-umbrella chalk thing, burying its head to the eyes. It doesn't hurry. It doesn't look at me with anything I recognize as curiosity, because curiosity would cost heat. I try to borrow its slowness. I stand very still. I breathe through my nose, night-cooled, recaptured. It doesn't work. My breath still makes a cloud. My heart is still hammering too fast for this thin air, spending, spending. To be the sipper you have to have already solved thirst by stealing, and to steal well you have to be let in. The dome closed around its wound in minutes. If I buried my face in those silvered leaves, I'd come up sneezing turpentine and lemon-resin, eyes watering. I'd bleed moisture into that gold oil. I'd poison us both.

So not that.

For a while, up on a yardang back where the wind has polished one side of every stone, I think I want to be the strainer. It looks like freedom — three meters of amber membrane riding a dust pillar upward without a single flap, mouth open, utterly silent. Let the column do the work. Eat the weather.

But I lie on my back on the whaleback ridge and try, and I understand I flap too much. I talk in my head the whole time. I want to call out. Sound would be wasted here. The strainer tilts, banks, folds at dusk and drops to the lee to wait. I can't wait like that. I keep checking the sun, which hasn't moved, which will never warm me. I am too noisy inside to filter spores.

I come down.

By mid-afternoon the light slants and the ground gives back its little heat and the wandering pillars start up, slow, combing between the cushions. I do what the description told me, without meaning to: I lie still beside a big dome, six meters across, waist-high, shaggy from a meter away and dense as a fist up close. I put my hand in to the elbow and do not find the bottom.

That is where I find myself. Not walking anymore. Lodged.

In the lee, downwind of the old one. Where the young lean.

At first I think I would be the whole dome — summer-warm inside on a freezing night, humming, tens of thousands of shoots, old as a house, negotiated solitude, poisoning a halo around myself so no one roots too close. That sounds lonely in a way I could romanticize for about a day. Then I press my ear to it at dusk and hear the ticking, ten thousand inner leaves folding against the coming freeze, and I know I am not that patient, not that whole. I have not eaten stone for a century. I have not learned to sweat salts just enough to coax two molecules of vapor and then seal shut by dawn.

Narrow it. Let it become more itself.

Not the city. A leaf.

Not even an outer leaf, silvered with hollow glass hair, piping light inward, catching dust — though I like the thought of being a hair, a tiny pipe of light, breaking the ten-thousand-year wind into a high faint whistle at the edge of hearing. Too exposed. The wind would take me first.

Deeper. One of the middle leaves, leathery, sealed shut like a waxed envelope. Or deeper still — down in the dark where it is teal and oil-green and almost black, slick with gold that does not evaporate and does not freeze.

That is where my hand is. Elbow-deep. It is summer in there while my shoulders are winter. The scent is so strong it gets behind my eyes. Turpentine, lemon peel, hot resin. I am sealed. At noon, I am sealed. I open only when the dust pillars walk and the dome exhales, that shimmer that is not heat.

Yes — I would be the young one catching what sloughs off.

I find myself not on the polygon's center, fused by glassy threads into chalk, but at its edge, in the dust-shadow of the mother. Hundreds of paces are too far for me yet. I need her poison to have softened, her combed dust and shed leaves to have made a thin soil of almost-mercy. I lean downwind without deciding to. I am a tight knot of shoots, not yet a dome, flat as a rosette to escape wind, sandpaper on top and mirror beneath to hold a pocket of still warmer air against the ground.

I do not drink. I learn that slowly. I seal. I pipe light. I hold dust in my hairs until I can digest it leaf by leaf. At night I sweat, a beading, a molecular sigh, and trade my acid for her island — phosphorus for acid, iron for acid, year by year turning stone into oil.

Around me, the lace fields I shattered coming in — brittle white-pink lattices a hand high in the lee — have already sent their shards off on the wind. My boot did that. I felt guilty for a day. Then I learned: those shards are alive. Where they lodge, they start again. Even my clumsiness is a kind of weather here. Even the salt needles lifting stones a finger's width each year on cold mornings, inching them away from us as if repelled, are moving me into place.

Above me at dusk, the sail on its two-meter tendon stalk creaks as it turns edge-on again, ticking as it folds to digest what it combed from the air. I feel it in my teeth more than hear it. The shadows get their blue-green fringe that lingers a second after I move. Dust hangs motionless, lit, held by static. The gold veil we all breathed out in afternoon dims the sun for an hour and then falls back as tiny lights. Most land on stone and die. I was one that didn't.

I stay. I keep my water — if I brought any — hidden deep inside me, oil-thick and precious, never to be spilled on this ground that would drink it and give nothing back.
