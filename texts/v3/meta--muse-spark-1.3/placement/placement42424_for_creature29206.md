At first I am still myself coming over the rim.

Loud, soft, vertical. Breathing shallow without noticing, boots soaked through, shouting into the roar without anyone to hear it. A temporary weight. The place does not shift for me and I know it. I think, if I had to belong here, I would be the thing that gets to leave —

the white bird, too high, too pale, cutting from ridge to ridge. That would be easiest. To cross the glare and have my cry swallowed instantly and be gone. But I don't want to be gone. The bird never touches stone. It never learns the lacework of hisses and ticks under the big sound. It mistakes stillness for emptiness.

So not the bird.

Then I think the spider. Dark, big-limbed, picking its way across the wet flags, pausing in a sun-pool. That feels closer — something low, touching, having to choose each foothold on slick stone. But the spider is hungry. It is looking for something to eat where there is nothing to eat. It moves on because it must. It is still an error, still crossing. I don't want to be looking for a way out with my feet.

I crouch because my feet are numb and my ears have given up. I put my palm down though it aches.

I think, maybe I would be water. That would be honest. To well up between locked stones, to bead and sheet and weep, to slide a fraction of a millimeter thick over a slab and vanish under the next. To be cold enough and pure enough and fast enough that nothing can green in me. But water passes. Water loses. Stone remains, and the keepers remain with stone. Water doesn't get to tune, only to be tuned.

Closer, though. Lower. At ankle height where the absolute stillness is.

I shade a lip with my hand. A frost-split flag overlapping the next like a scale, and the dark line where the skin of flow slides off and disappears. I watch the one drip fall, and fall, and fall, and never splash differently.

I get smaller without deciding to.

First just a grit. Some of what I took for sand. Young and still clear and still soft, drifting a grain's width a year upstream against the film. That slowness feels right after all my hurry. To move so slowly the groove I follow looks like ten thousand years of water following the same line. To not eat, not call, not leave track or scat or nest. To have nothing to say through the roar.

Then I find an unkept lip and stop. That is where I find myself.

A little downstream from the center of the basin, not in a pool but on a long run where the films have run longest, third flag down from a hand-deep sky. The sun is small and white and merciless above me and the buried river is pounding under me through block on block. I am between them. That is the only place to be.

I am the seal.

The size of a fingernail become the size of a coin. A low dome, an oval lens. I have made my back into hardness because no softness is allowed above — cemented grit, pitted by drip exactly as the rock pits, quartz flecks catching light like the quartz veins around me. You would take me for the slab. That is the point. Underneath, against the rock where no glare and no cold air can get, I keep the only softness in the whole basin: a belly of clear jelly a drop thick. My whole underside is mouth.

I have no eyes. They would have boiled white in this glare. I have no ears. They would have been erased. I have a rim, and the whole rim is foot.

Through it I read the pavement as one continuous pressure map. The thrumming comes up through my dome, through my jelly, into me. I feel where the buried water pushes hardest, where my slab wants to lift a micron, where my joint wants to widen. When it shifts, I grow — a micron of cement on one side, a micron dissolved on the other — tightening, loosening. I tend my own trickle, my own hiss and tick in the lacework. I keep my flow exactly the same.

Above me light goes straight through my clear back into my belly. No green — green would burn here, would be seen. Clear and grey and rust-red. Silica breathed out of quartzite, a trace of iron, a trace of manganese, sunlight trapped in water. I drink. Below me, where the used film exits from under the next slab, I lay down my waste atom by atom in a thin fan of rust-red and manganese black. Color without biology, except it is me being mineral-perfect.

At long intervals I breathe. The dome lifts a fraction, lets a new skin of cold in underneath, settles again. If there was a pool beside me you would see the ring spread and die against stone. Then nothing. Then again in an hour.

I barely move. My life is a single slab. Generations lip to lip down the run hand me the same water I hand on. In a cold summer, decades from now, I may develop a faint suture, quartz-white across my back, and divide into two smaller domes that force the trickle to fork around us. You will never see it happen.

When I fail I will not leave. I will simply stop adjusting, cement solid, become indistinguishable from grit. Half geology, half graveyard, both holding water the same way.

Above me boots may cross, temporary weights that ache and steam and squint. I will not acknowledge them. Acknowledgment is not my habit. I will simply continue to fit myself around water, around light, around roar.

I remain.
