I would find myself not where I thought.

At first I think I am standing in the long empty. That's what I would tell you if you asked too soon — upright between lamps, boots in sheet-water, breath smoking. Halfway between a sodium hiss behind me and the green pilot row far ahead that never blinks off. Water to the ankle, deck under that, older deck under that. Tock-suck, tock-suck, whenever I shift my weight.

But if I stay past the need to give an answer, that picture won't hold.

Because the roar won't let me stand. It gets into the sternum first, that low thud-thud of pumps moving water from one low place to another low place, and standing starts to feel like a lie the body tells. Upright is for things that want to be seen by lamps. I don't. The steam spreads sideways a meter above my head and becomes fog and wets my face and I duck without deciding to, shoulders damp through, knees filling with cold seep.

So no. Not standing. Crouching.

I find myself crouched, then. Hands on cold rail for balance though the rail beads and drips. Listening for the higher frequencies under the roar. Tick, plink, trickle. I think, at first, that I am a watcher. A visitor with eyes. I have clouded pits of my own, but mine still try to work, still hunt for horizon between fog banks. I turn toward every dark thickening and it resolves — or doesn't — into steam folding over itself.

Then the relief valve slams shut somewhere out in the dark.

Half a second of relative quiet like falling. And in it: plink. Plink. Plink. All across the open dark. A thousand edges ticking. And underneath my palms, through the iron, a soft wet seating as something near me adjusts itself.

That's where I am. Really.

Not alone in the empty. In company, low.

I am at the foot of a pipe trestle where the flanges weep at every joint and have made their line of little permanent puddles, each with its own oil rainbow, each with its own fringe of moss. The deck here is dished, an inch deep in black mirror, and the pipe foot in front of me has shivered the standing water around itself into concentric ripples that hold without wind. At first I thought that was physics. Then I got close.

There is a back there. Then another. Then, as my eyes stop hunting, three more.

Iron-gray mottled rust-orange and black-green, beaded always with condensate, shedding secondary rain off their flanks the way handrails do. They make nothing where I make tock-suck. They distribute. They flow without breaking the film. One has settled its front end level with the puddle until its wide flat lip just breaks tension, sipping the skin — oil and grease and drowned rust-bloom and lichen spores — leaving a clear black eye behind it that slowly rainbows over again. Another turns its cheek and rasps at the moss with a tongue like a wet file. Not to eat it clean. To prune it. To keep the drip-line open.

I thought I would be one of them. That's the second easy picture. That I could just lie down and be low and blind and belly-eared, reading the pumps through my soles. I try. God, I try. I put my belly to the vibrating iron. Cold comes straight through my coat. My sternum hums. For a half-second I can hear the bright net underneath the roar persist — valve stem to puddle, bolt to moss — and I tip my head to catch the fall.

But my mouth is wrong. I can't sip film. My knees are wrong, my elbows too sharp, my breath smokes too fast. My eyes, even filmed with drizzle, still want light. The tenders know me by my wrongness. They go stiller, flatter, more deck when I move. I am loud even being quiet.

So what am I, if I can't be upright and I can't quite be low?

Stay longer. Past the hour when my own voice goes distant in my head. Past the time my boots fill completely and stop mattering.

I lie flatter. Not to imitate — to stop interrupting. I settle my weight out along the deck beside the trestle foot, cheek near enough to the puddle to see my own breath dimple the oil skin. I stop trying to point to the source of the sound. I let my sternum do the hearing. Thud. Thud. The empty crusher booms and comes back off the shed walls. Steam screams again.

And one of them comes closer. Not fleeing — fleeing is loud. Converging, very slowly, to the ripple I am inadvertently helping hold still with my mass.

That is my place. In the lee of the blind cooling tower's warm breath, with the boiler house banked and breathing at my back, in the line of flange-drip puddles. Low, but not lowest. Among backs, but not a back.

What I do: I hold still enough to be a windbreak for the ripple. My hands, too clumsy to rasp moss clean, learn instead to clear with a thumbnail the expansion joint beside me where black-green sponge is damming too fast and threatening to lift the plate. I learn to breathe, slow, onto a cold flank so condensate falls hot for a moment. I learn to wait for the valve-shut half-second and, in it, count the drips aloud in my head so I remember where they are when the roar returns — a map they carry in their bellies and I have to carry in memory.

They tend me back. I realize this when I stop moving for a long time in green pilot light and feel a faint scrape at my shoulder seam — a file-tongue pruning the wire-grass that has started in my coat, keeping my drip-line open. Moss in my boot welts. Lichen following the drip down my sleeve. They have decided I am a mobile seam. A slow, warm, upright-remembering plate that can be kept.

So that is who I would be.

Not maintainer. Not machine. Not quite animal. The thing that stayed long enough to go from tock-suck to hush, from watcher to warmed rock in the pile. Backs steaming faintly near me, not touching quite, but near. Nostrils slits just above black water in the settling basin beside us. Rust-colored flakes drifting to the rim to lodge where rebar shows like bone.

Maintenance as life, and at the edge of it, me: kept open, kept dripping, learning at a centimeter a year how to belong to a marsh the world never was, but made.
