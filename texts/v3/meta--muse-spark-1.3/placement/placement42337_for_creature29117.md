At first I am still myself, too big.

I come in on knees, crouching to get under the lip of the standing roots. From outside it was nothing, a dark line behind ferns. Inside it is a room and I fill too much of it. My breathing is the loudest thing here. The moss swallows everything else but not that. I sit with my knees up and I can see all of it at once — the wall sweating, the seep brown as tea, the citadel a meter across in the dry corner — and I know I cannot live here like this. Anything my size can only pass through. A peccary. A falling branch. Me.

So I get lower. Cheek almost to the humus, six inches up, until scale inverts.

For a while I think I will be the wren. It would be good to live up where it is driest, under the curl of the root plate where the eleven-second drip never reaches. To plaster a dome of mud and moss with a side door no wider than a thumb, to come and go without sound, a flicker. To have chicks gaping in warm dark. But even the wren is large here — less than two coins, but still large — and the wren can leave. It flies out over the fallen trunk and into the dim green nothing between presences. I don't want to be something that can leave. I want to be something that has to stay.

Lower.

I think I will be the frog. Bronze back flecked black, no bigger than a thumbnail. That stillness appeals to me — not hopping so much as repositioning, one deliberate step at the margin of the highway, eyes fixed on the straggler wrestling her sail in a leaf-cup puddle. To sit with hind legs splayed in water that will be gone tomorrow, tadpole cousins wriggling around my toes. To borrow my poison from the ants I eat and store it in my skin. But the frog is still a hunter of the margins. He watches the town. He is not of it.

Closer. To be of it.

I think I will be a carrier, a media, rust-red and long-legged, with a green sail hoisted three times my body held overhead. Jogging in double lane — outbound empty-jawed, inbound laden. Antennae flickering constantly for the humidity change I cannot, at human size, feel. Halting all at once when cloud crosses the canopy, sails trembling, waiting it out. Getting stuck under my leaf in a ditch and cutting it smaller. Being scattered like ninepins when a rove beetle bulldozes through. That frantic, fallible hauling. But a carrier still sees light. She still runs thirty meters out into the forest and back. I want to go deeper, where the air turns still and sweet-sour.

Smaller.

There — riding the underside of that sail, invisible, a crumb no bigger than an eyelash is wide. The minim. The shepherd. I am cleaning the leaf as we jog, licking it for poison, for hitchhiking spores. My world is all green sail above and rushing humus below and the smell of the column, formic and iron. Or no — even smaller, even more inside. Let me follow that inbound carrier home, past the guard majors clacking at the lip, those walking doors with heads huge as two workers, down where it drops, down past the nurseries where larvae like blind grains of rice hang in cradles, down to the moistest, held-breath chamber.

Here. This is where I would find myself.

A vaulted chamber as big as my old fist, in the dark under the pocked hill. The walls are pellets carried up grain by grain from below, worn smooth by a million feet. The air does not move. It smells of crushed leaf and vinegar and warm fungus. And all around me, glowing faintly pale, the true resident: the fungus in grey-white sponges of chewed leaf, riddled with hyphae, fruiting into swollen tips rich as yolks.

I would be the gardener.

No bigger than a sesame seed. Blind to everything but chemistry. My sisters and I patrol the sponge, pruning, weeding out alien molds with antibiotic spit from glands on our chests. On our backs we wear a powder white as chalk dust — a living pharmacy, bacteria that make the poisons that keep our garden clean. We lick the pulp, inoculate it, coax it. We will never see the shaft of light where the gnats hang. We will never hear the wren's soft trill through cloth. We will only know the drip as constancy: that the air stays moist, that the queen somewhere deeper is still laying, egg after sticky white egg, that the carriers keep coming with sails.

This is residence. To live within a meter of damp and never leave it. To be food and house both — for the fungus feeds me and I feed the frog who eats my forager sister on the highway and becomes poison and becomes soil and becomes fern.

And when the wall slumps, as it will within two seasons, when the seep moves six inches left and the old galleries collapse and a new shaft is opened after rain — I will move with it. Grain by grain, brood carried in jaws, garden replanted from a pinch of mycelium carried like fire. The town is built to be left.

From ten meters away there is nothing here. From inside, in the dark, tended and tending, I am carrying something larger than myself home.
