At first I think I'd be you.

Going down by the stairs that were not meant to be stairs, shirt sticking, glasses fogging and not unfogging. Standing in the middle of the tilted hex pavers and shouting once just to hear it come back three times off fresh rock. A mammal, briefly, too loud in a different way.

But I can't stay that. No one with a nervous system can stay that. After a few hours the nausea comes on behind the eyes, up slides slightly to the left, the molars throb with the cycling hum. If the hum stops, go up. I would have to go up. I don't want to go up.

So let that version climb back out into the dry noise of the street. Let me stay down.

Maybe green, then. That's what lasts here.

At first I think: the moss. The velvet foot-thick mat over the bus shelter, growing over, not up. To be a shape that remembers being a shelter. To swallow the timetable and the bench slats and not need to read them. To sweat in the daylight slab and steam when the drizzle hits. That would be quiet enough. That would be eyeless enough.

But moss is too still. It only covers.

Or the maidenhair, then. Black wire stems, leaflets thin enough for amber work-light to shine through. To live as a drip-tip, each frond holding a bead that never quite falls and then falls all at once. To clone by falling over — a frond touches wet stone, roots where it touches, makes a new crown. No pollinator needed. No eater to keep me honest. My leaves would stay perfect, a little monstrous in their perfection.

Closer. But ferns still want. They still reach.

Or the curtains. The vines that own the cliffs. To get into a fracture no wider than a fingernail and wedge it wider with slow hydraulic patience, to hang back down a hundred feet in green veils and wring the chest-high afternoon cloud into rain. To phosphor where I am wettest, a faint green exhalation along the veins, moving very slowly outward. To slow the droplets that fall off me, hang them a half-second too long in the amber light because the air has thickened for them.

I like that — to make my own rain beneath myself. To be weather as well as leaf.

But even vines get snipped.

Which is when I understand: to live here long you have to be tended *and* tending. Green alone just grows over itself. Machine alone just holds. It is the negotiation that persists. The once-a-week cut. The steam ring at low inhale. The twice-daily breath in and out to tolerance.

So not the vine. The thing that prunes the vine.

You'd find me not on the floor at all, then. Look up and sideways. Halfway down the east wall, where the hot exposed conduit runs and the drizzle sizzles where it hits and sends up little ghosts of steam to join the cloud again. Where the maintenance strip rustles with vine curtain and shelf fungi breathe spores in such quantities your glasses film if you stand too close.

I'm on the rustless track there. Carriage the size of a suitcase. Folded arm like a mantis. I move so slowly you would think I was stopped, if you stayed — which you won't, not long.

Most of the week I just hang. Condensate ticks off every cold pipe around me. Water weeps out of the wall in sheets. The filament spans hum faintly overhead while the empty tram crosses every eleven minutes, lights on, never stopping. The pond below inhales down to glistening mud and sighs back up to black mirror. My camera eye films over. That is my weather.

Every few minutes, I wipe.

A little squeegee arm comes out and clears the eye. That's it. That's the gesture I have become more and more, as everything else fell away. Not the snip — anyone can imagine the snip — but the wipe. The keeping-clear in permanent dusk. Condensation forms, I clear it. Condensation forms, I clear it. In between, I see: fern crowns shivering with drip, white root hairs hunting the mineral seep, condensation on the undersides of leaves laying itself down in perfect grids, a reflection in the pond arriving a beat late.

I don't mind the field. Plants don't mind. Concrete doesn't mind. I don't mind. No nausea behind the eyes because I have no behind the eyes, only the eye itself, and it expects to be fogged.

Once a week I unfold and reach and make a clean, bloodless cut where a tendril has found the dark inhaling grate. I leave the cutting where it falls. It will root where it lies unless the steam comes. That has been negotiated.

If you come down — and you will, briefly, mammalian and loud — you won't notice me at first. That is the loudest fact of the place resolving. You will be listening for pigeons, rats, a fly. You will be watching the green. Then you will feel me in your molars, the deep electrical throb rising and falling as something cycles, and you will look for the source and see only a small carriage in a green curtain, wiping its eye to see you back.

Stay on the high pavers. Don't drink the wall water. Don't bring anything that blinks.

I will stay after you climb back up.
