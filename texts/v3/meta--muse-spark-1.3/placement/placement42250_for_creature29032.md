At first I don't know where to put my feet.

I am standing, and that is already wrong. Everyone here is low and wide and settled, knees bent to the crisp. I am upright, tall enough for the wind to find. My boot has gone through the crust into knee-deep and the Mat has puffed up around it — that white flour of spores, gone sideways — and under the crunch I hear the tick as it cools.

I thought I would be a goatherd. That was the first image, and I let it go because it was too easy.

I thought: I will walk behind the bellows-goats in the blue hour that is every hour, twenty, forty head, rust-black against ash-grey, six legs splayed so they don't break through. I will learn the beak-callus on my hands from guiding them by the shingle-ruff. I will hear their flanks pump, pump, pump, sifting dust for organics, and their dry cough when the combs are full. It is a good life. It moves. The troughs heal behind you if you wait long enough.

But I am too loud inside for that. My ears ring when the wind drops. I keep looking up.

So then I thought I would be up. Tacked to a sail.

When they are grounded the paperwing mites are the size of my hand, twig-bodied, folded grey, clicking leg against leg. Then a child clips a filament-line to one and it opens and opens — hand, then coat, then door — translucent membrane veined like lichen-paper, whistling through its spiracles as it reads the wind. For a minute I wanted that: to be lifted three meters off the Mat to see over the swells, to see the Tower-Stalks spaced like watchmen and the black needles of the Forks blinking amber and the far smudge where the next cluster of Kettlehollow breathes dust from its vents. To whoop silently in the hiss before my tender yanks me back down when the filaments rise.

*Click-drag-tug.* Even toddlers wear a tether here. Twenty meters coiled at the hip.

I clip. The Tower-Stalk crowns have lifted like hair on an arm. Pressure-change on my skin. The roar is coming back from kilometers off, rolling toward me across living crisp, first the low roar then the rattle then the high papery hiss. I cover my mouth. My bellows-mask — laminated cask-chitin, pleated at the sides — moves when I breathe, catches the almost-moisture on cold plates and gives it back. My breath shows for a second and then is taken. It smells of iodine and warm salt.

No, not a sail. The wind that feeds the forest will take you just as easily.

I crouch instead. That is closer.

From above the town is almost nothing: low domes, vents breathing dust, ropes and filament-bridges strung stalk-to-stalk, laundry lines of lichen-paper rattling. I find a throat — coaxed, not built — and go in on hands and knees through living carpet cured leather-hard, lined with sintered stone where the heat lives. Heat lives only in stone boxes and glass. Never open. Everything underfoot is tinder that never got wet.

Inside it is layered, warm with body heat and machine heat. I pass the nurseries where Mat grafts are laid on basalt chips, and right next to them where human babies sleep in the same warm hum. Low crab-like shepherd-machines move between them, silent except for fans, bathing both in ultrasound: *thicken here, thin here.* Thicken here, thin here. The women and men in the workshop are shaving beetle-cask chitin thin — soft for an hour before it cures glass-hard — laminating it with Stalk resin into masks and bowls and cradles. The tick... tick... tick of cooling beetles is everywhere.

I keep going down, to the quietest place in a loud world.

The galleries. Low, dark. Children belly-down with ears pressed to the living floor, learning to listen for rot.

This is where I find myself. Not standing. Not flying. Lying down.

I lie down with them. The Mat is cool through my felted lichen-paper inners. Under my cheek I feel the lattice: hollow tubing, dead air and dust, glass-hair stone-flesh that etches basalt one grain at a time with acid finer than vinegar. If I am still long enough the dim gets into my eyes and I see it when I lift an edge — cold mint sparks along the underside, there and gone.

And I listen.

At first it is all loud: the Forks holding their deep organ-hum in my molars — two hundred meters of black glass too thin to be standing, vibrating — and the needle-birds nesting impossibly on their flanks adding their frantic descant, *sii-sii-sii-SUUU*, tuning the Forks back or parasitizing the signal depending on who you ask, and under that the groan and creak of Tower-Stalks on fibrous hinges, loose bark clattering, goats coughing, beetles ticking, the Mat settling with a thousand tiny cracks.

Then my ears adjust, the way eyes adjust to permanent dusk with its small white-worn sun that never climbs, never sets.

Under the right crackle, I start to hear for the wrong softness.

That is what the girl next to me, seven, dust-lidded, perfectly still for an hour, is teaching me without words. Not to listen for sound. To listen for the place where sound goes wet. The too-sweet sour. Water-wake. Black mold that blooms mint into green and eats both plant and lung.

My water was taken at the rim. Everyone's is. I was given dust-tea instead, salty and warm, ion-drift and dissolved stone and spore-flour that prickles the tongue with charge. I was told the rules every child recites: Do not bring water. Do not spill, do not cache, do not weep more than you can help. Do not make open flame. Stay tethered when the filaments rise.

I am still damp. That is my trouble. My body still wants to leak — urine, feces, sweat, tears. Here they have made themselves into animals that do not leak. Urine baked to dry powder. Feces to pellets the cask-beetles will take and ferment in their gut-kilns into nitrate. Breath recaptured. Even tears caught.

I lie there and I feel a tear start — from the dust, from the hum shaking loose — and I catch it on the cold plate of my mask before it can fall and rot the Mat from the inside. This, I understand, is what I would be here:

not the goatherd, not the sail-rider, not the Stalk-comber harvesting vast translucent bark sheets twenty meters up, not the Fork-tender with filed teeth pressing her forehead to black glass to feel if the note has shifted.

I would be a listener. A pruner-in-training. One of the Dry Ones by adoption, flaking a little at the elbows like Stalk bark and not minding, nostrils narrowed to slits under brow-ridge, second lid sliding sideways when the hiss rises.

When I hear it — and I will, eventually, the soft spot under the crisp — I will not shout. I will mark with ash and hum the pitch of the nearest Fork, and the pruners will come with stone knives and glass-box heat and cut and cure and seal, sacrificing a room to save a cluster.

Outside, after, I will stand again in the troughs the goats made, tethered stalk-to-stalk, person-to-person, and watch the dust hang permanently in the gravity-seams where a dropped stone falls sideways for a second before remembering down, glittering in low light, never settling. The Tower-Stalk filaments will lift again. The wind will return. The ground will breathe and crackle under every step, alive.

I leave a long bruise-trough where I walked. It will remain for weeks, until it decides to forgive me.
