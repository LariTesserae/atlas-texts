At first I am still standing. I think I will be the walker.

Boots sighing and filling. Breath showing. Pant legs soaked to the knee. I look out for tens of miles of white rims and sky water and think, where would I live if I had to live here, where would I put a door. There are no doors.

So I kneel. The place forces it. From ankle-height the bathtub neighborhoods stop being a view and start being a texture. That's when I start to shrink.

I thought I might be the raven. One crossing, wingbeats suddenly loud because there is nothing to cover them. But I am not a visitor. I don't want to cross and be gone. I want to stay.

I thought I might be the pika. It would be warm, at least. A fist-sized dart-freeze-dart between rimstones, mouthful of sedge almost as tall as myself, haypile curing under a slab for the long lock of winter. Listening under snow to water still ticking under ice. That listening feels familiar. But I am too fast for that, still. My heart hurries. The pika's life is hurry and wait, hurry and wait. I need slower.

I thought I might be the sedge, or the campion with its nail-sized pink flower open for a week. Sipping, not anchoring. Allowed a windowsill on a drained wall-top. But I am wetter than that. I keep sliding down toward the wet.

Down toward the lip.

There's a place where the broad upper flat finds a narrow throat and spills into the smaller, deeper cells below — a notch no wider than my hand would have been, behaving exactly like a gutter. Water spreads there paper-thin, cold and mineral-sharp, heavy with dissolved limestone. It doesn't rush. It sheets. It ticks.

Find me there. On the wet edge.

I am not tall. I am not even thick. I am a skin, olive and ochre and black-green, slick to the touch. A sheet a few cells thick. Cyanobacteria strung with algae, moss protonema underneath like felt. If you put your — if something warm put a palm to my rock I would smell faintly of iron and moss where the rust-yellow oxidizers work beside the bone-cream calcite. I exhale cold.

This is what I do, which is not doing so much as being in the way, correctly:

I sip the seep as it spreads. I slow it. On my sticky sheaths the water lets go. Needle by needle, grain by grain, a lace of calcite drops out underneath the overhang, inverted frost growing downward. Sun-warmed at noon, I precipitate faster in the corners where pollen dust and ash settle onto me. At night I just hold.

I am the mason. Without me the water would run off and leave nothing. With me, film becomes rim, rim becomes dam, dam becomes pool. I build by living, and by dying — living green on top, dead brown below becoming peat, peat underlain by white marl, marl underlain by older green again. If you read me with a fingernail: sky water, dust, skin, needles, black peat, white marl, living, dead.

I am grazed. That is part of it.

Along me moves a snail no bigger than a grain of rice, amber and translucent, stout-shelled because shell material rains from above here. It leaves a clean trail through me, eating the film and leaving the calcite. It is a clean, narrow absence. I close over it by evening. Everything touches everything else. I do not mind. To be eaten a little is how the rim stays sharp.

Above me, sometimes, a huge soft shadow — a boot, a deer hoof, a pika foot pausing. The ground gives, sighs. Water fills the print from below. I am flooded, then exposed, then flooded. Tufts of sedge sip nearby. Star-moss cushions swell after rain and shrink to black knots. In the moss when it is wet, tardigrades lumber bear-like past me and tun into dust when dry. Midge larvae flick, blood-red and eyelash-thin, in their tubes of marl and mucus in the sun-warmed corner. We are crowded at small scale into one square meter. Utterly full and almost empty.

Evening cools the white light to blue. The pools go from glare to glass. The weeping riser below my lip darkens and beads. Mist lifts off ten thousand shallow mirrors in the morning and settles again as dew, and with it my whole continent breathes once. A cloud born over us drifts a mile, darkens, rains briefly onto us, and dissipates. Post-rain, every rim drips. I swell and open.

Winter will come. I know how it feels now: skin over, then lock. I will go dark and dormant under ice lenses. The snail will burrow down and seal its aperture with mucus and calcium. The larvae will coil tight. The pika will stay under snow in the steam of its own breath. The stone will heave and crack. And in spring the springs will well up everywhere, unstoppable, and the weeping faces will darken and bead and my cracks will heal with fresh lace.

I do not hurry. I do not gather. I am not building a city. I am only keeping house for water, on a lip no wider than a hand, laying down another invisible film of stone.

From a distance it looks, unmistakably, like streets and houses shouldering into each other until haze takes it. From inside, at the scale of the first inch, it is just this: cold spreading thin, light falling straight down and finding water everywhere, and me — slick, low, slow — catching rock out of rain.
