At first I thought I'd be up high.

On the rim, where the air gives out. Where you can still see the white sea of cloud a mile down burning in a sun we never get. I like edges. I like to look out. I thought I would be the lookout, the one with eyes narrowed and wet against dust who watches weather come.

But no. You can't live on the rim here. Nothing lives outward. Everything leans inward. The wind quarries you. The birds don't even nest up there, they pour out of cracks and fold right back into the slot. The rock falls inward, always. The pocket pulls. If I tried to stand watch I'd be back down within an hour with bleeding nose and cracked lips, learning what everyone learns: never build out, only in.

So then I thought, down in the frenzy. A porter, doubled-over with ore sack leaking red down my legs, taking stairs that are almost ladders two at a time. A smith stripped to the waist pulling white sheets while sweat evaporates before it drips. Hands flashing in the bazaar lane, scales swinging.

But I can't breathe like that. None of us can, really, but I especially. My chest is too shallow. The first month here I would spend with the hammer behind the eyes, vomiting quietly behind a shed. I waste motion. I waste breath. I want to talk on stairs. I would be walked over if I stood still in the middle of it.

I'm not a runner. I'm not heat-tough.

Maybe an animal, then. For a while I tried that on and it fit better.

A chough hopping between slag and bone on the dump terraces, not flying if I can help it because flying costs, hammering at marrow in silence. A rock-rat, woolly and impossibly fat, wedged shoulder to shoulder in a frost-split seam with a hundred others, black bead eyes all turned inward toward the heat. Sitting and watching. Foraging inward on spillage — grain dust, fat drippings, the daily rain of dead moths. Not sleeping, just getting slower and woollier generation on generation.

Closer. I know how to sit and watch. I know how to live off leak.

Or a dog. Barrel-chested, short-muzzled, silent as the birds. I liked that for a whole afternoon in my head — trotting a two-inch cable a hundred meters over the forges with cat-confidence, never barking because barking up here would bounce and multiply until it drove you mad. Communicating by shoulder-bumps and breath. Sleeping curled in warm ash under the winch-house in a pile, breathing together.

Closer still. But I am afraid of that height, that loose wire. My feet want felt under them. My feet want something solid that thrums.

Then I thought of the moths.

In the mill house it is always night and always warm. The ash-moths, pale grey as rock flour, hatch in the felt insulation wrapped around the machine feet — the felt we put there to muffle sound — and they live their whole lives in a three-meter radius of heat around a single caged bulb. Batter, mate, fall in the oil pan. By next shift there are more. The men scoop the dead out by the handful and toss them to the rats.

I could be that. A brief, frantic life around one small light. It wouldn't be a bad life. But I last longer than a night. I carry over.

You'd find me, finally, not high and not at the very bottom. Halfway up the east wall, in the blue-black that never gets the two hours of sun. In a room hung under a bridge. The kind of room that wasn't planned, that appeared after a rockfall took a corner and the builders re-nailed the timbers in a slightly different configuration to fit the new shape of the wall. Stone foot below, tin and boiler-plate above, ropeway cable humming six inches from my window. A tin beard of frost on my chimney collar every morning.

I'm small. I've gotten short and deep here, like everyone who stays. Barrel ribs like the dogs. Face dark red-brown from blood close to the surface. Fingertips split and healed thick. Mouth shut.

Morning duty is wringing. The old women, the old men, the kids too small to carry ore — that's my shift. I go round with a wooden bucket, unwinding the roof-rags where breath and steam froze overnight, twisting with both hands, watching the thin brownish trickle collect. That's drinking water. That's washing water. I never spill. To spill is worse than to steal.

Then felt work. Lining boots. Wrapping machine feet with straw and rubber. Hanging rags on doors to keep sound and heat in. The forges have to sigh, not roar. The winches have to hum. The whole pocket will pick up a sharp sound and throw it back and forth until you can't sleep. So I tend quiet. I pack it in.

In between I feed things. I scoop dead moths from the oil pans and toss them down to the ledge where the pikas sit shoulder to shoulder. They don't move away from my hand. They just watch with those black eyes, furred on the rock like lichen that never grew here. The swallows — not swallows, something higher and harder — ribbon out over my head without a cry. Crying costs too much. I don't call to them either.

This is where I breathe best, if you can call it breathing: short labored pulls through the nose, tasting stone dust and hot oil and animal musk. The generator pit thrums up through the soles of my felt boots into my molars. Tick-tick-tick of cooling metal next to my ear. Rope creak in a pulley. Someone's labor beside me on the plank, not talking. At noon the lamps breathe — when the generator surges they don't flicker out of sync, they dim and brighten as one lung. The birds, when they wheel, all bank at the same instant, as if the air itself tilted. I bank with them, inside. My chest lifts when theirs lift.

I'm not a porter, not a smith, not a trader with flashing fingers. I'm the one who stays still while the anthill streams around me. Who saves piss for tanning, bone for fuel, piss-steam for water, breath for water. Who chews tea leaf and all because you don't throw away hot water.

If you climbed down the iron staples into the smell of it and looked for me, you wouldn't see me first. You'd see motion. Then, out of steam and lamp-glare, people unstick from walls. Then, lower, ledges furred with watching animals. Then, in a shadow under a bridge, a low shape wringing a rag, shoulder to shoulder with rats, while dogs trot overhead without barking.

That would be me. Not separate. Part of the one warm animal made of hands and paws and wings and pulleys and furnace mouths, wedged and crammed and layered to suffocation in a stone lung a hundred meters deep — turbulent and teeming and almost silent, breathing together in the blue dusk.
