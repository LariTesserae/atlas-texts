At first I think I'd be up on the rim.

Because that's where I start, everyone starts — heat coming up through my shoes off the laterite, white sky hurting, thorn scrub doing nothing. Looking down. Visitor-eyes, still dry. I would stand in one of those lip houses that jut like teeth, squinting and weeping, wiping my glasses only to have them fog again, and I would think: I could go down but I could also leave.

No. If I stayed — if I really stayed past that first hiss of rain on hot roofs — I wouldn't stay there. Up there my skin cracks in minutes. My nose bleeds in the dry. Too bright. Too loud with wind. The crow circles and thinks better of it, and so would I, either go down all the way or go back.

So I go down.

At first I think I'd be loud, or what passes for loud here. One of the winch women, riding the big market platform all morning down, knitting in my lap, calling out low sing-song: shoots, pickles, oiled cloth. Leaning out over the void as the terraces pass. Hand to hand coin. That appeals to me — to have a reason to move slowly through the whole throat of it every day, to see every level go by one slow meter at a time. But I'm not a seller. My voice embarrasses me off the wet walls. I would learn by age four not to shout for custom. No. Not that.

Then I think I'd be a child with a rag.

Every morning when the cloud gets caught at the third terrace down and can't lift out over the lip — that low, tired cloud — sent out bare-legged and sleepy to wring out the air. Wipe the elephant ears big as doors, wipe the tarps and the washing lines, wring into the bottle-cap chains that tickle down into the cisterns. I know which pipe sweats coldest. I know which black seep is drinkable after boiling. I appear and am gone in the vapor. That feels close. To be small, soundless, useful before speech. To leave wet footprints on warm wood that vanish in seconds.

But I'm not that new. My lungs have already learned to chew. I remember paint, or at least I remember people who remember paint.

Let it get more itself.

Lower. Past the noon shaft when it stands straight down for an hour full of dust and seed-fluff and hits the bottom water white. Past the western stair where the taro shades everything. Past Level Six where the mill women pulp fiber and the air always smells of boiling leaves. The air gets thicker as I go. Hot iron, hot oil, crushed green going sweet-rot, sulfur just under it all. My shirt clings by midmorning and never dries, only warms. I stop wiping. I shine. My pupils go wide to catch the sideways tired light.

Find me on the east shade side, about six rings down, where the steam glows from underneath at dusk.

There is a through-kitchen there — there has to be, because there are no streets, only roofs that serve as streets and rooms you agree to walk through. To get to the sump you must pass through eleven parlors, and mine is the sixth or seventh, I lose count, the one where the taro is always boiling and the grandmother is always sleeping in the corner and everyone steps around her without agreement to notice. At every third house you leave your shoes. Mine is a shoe house. You lift my curtain, you nod, you touch my shoulder to ask right of way, you leave your cloth slippers by the door and pick up another pair, and go out the back hatch onto the next roof down. *Shush-shush.* Soft. That doesn't carry.

I keep the shoes lined up. I sweep the silt. That's where you'd find me.

Who am I, then? Not the pump-keeper, though my pulse has learned his tempo — *keh — ... — keh — ... — keh* — from the old Cornish engines lifting seep-water one slow stroke at a time. Not the dye-man with bruise-colored vats, not the wire-drawer humming behind his moss-baffle drawing green-furred copper thin as a finger. Closer to the vine-girls. My nails have gone green at the cuticle from pinching back morning-glory thick as rope along the hot pipes to cool them.

I'm a gardener of iron. A moss-packer.

The boiler housing in my back wall would ring if I let it. Instead I pack it thick with moss I farm like sheep, I pad it, I deaden it, so it only hums at idle. I train the fig root to hold the cracked cistern together better than mortar. I thatch with cutters-grass. I hang gourd buckets to shade the window. The handrail outside my door is half rust, half stem — if you ask me where one ends I will tell you, and if you cut it open you will see I am wrong, because there is no line anymore. Root-hairs have gone *into* the wire's grease, drinking it. Copper-edged leaves come up stiff as tin near the smelter. I don't paint things. I prune them.

This is husbandry. This is tending. The mills pulp, the press smells of hot nuts, the canning shed smells of vinegar and pickled shoots, the roof chickens pant constantly in their wire boxes and lay an egg sometimes. I eat taro root big as a child's torso, gourds, pressed oil. Meat is a story about a heron.

I was there for the heron, the way everyone was. When rain fell straight in and hissed and made waterfalls off every catwalk, it came down and stood on the sump rail for three days — that small scummed steaming eye no bigger than a courtyard — while we all brought it scraps and whispered. *It stayed. It left.* I still leave a little boiled root on the rail when the sump falls and we sweep and reoccupy without argument. No deeds. No hurry. Rust blooms over a season. A vine takes a wall in a year and gives it back in ten.

At dusk I come out onto the roof with the others. The cage-bulbs click on one by one down the spiral, amber in green. The sky-shaft above goes violet. Below, furnace doors stand open. You'll see me plainest then: sallow, beaded, forearms glistening, pulp-fiber wrap limp, barefoot on warm wood, a blind white lizard clinging to my back for the warmth. Not looking at you. Looking at the air.

When the whole packed, sweating, green-rusted hamlet breathes out once — that long hot plant-sweet exhalation that lifts the hanging cloud a meter and lets it settle — I breathe with it.

People are the fauna here. I am one of them.
