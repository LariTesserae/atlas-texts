I don't find myself at first.

At first I am still too big, too wet. I am the face at the fist-mouth, drying out. My nostrils sting with struck-match air. My breath comes back parched. The stone sucks at me. I cannot stay there — I leak. I blink, I sweat, I need. I have to put that self aside, kneeling up on the ridge in the cold night, hand over the mouth, and go in smaller.

If I go in, I fall.

For a moment I think I will be that — the fallout. The wind-killed midge lifted from some valley far below I never saw, up on a daytime thermal that gets colder and thinner until it kills me, and then I drop, dead, down the crack. A soft rain of a body from another world. I land not on rock but on hunger. That would be quick. Seconds, covered, disassembled, passed mouth to mouth. That's not living here. That's just visiting as food.

So not that. Lower. Darker.

Let my eyes go. They are useless here anyway. In sealed-dark, pinprick eye-shine winking on and off means nothing. I let seeing dissolve and keep the tremor.

I am a ticking first. A minute foot through rock vibration. A rasp.

Then a leg over a leg. Then an antenna over a back.

Then I am in the pile, and the pile is me.

I am one of them — black, lacquered, the length of the last joint of a finger you will never see. If you could hold me still, which you can't without burning and crushing, my cuticle is varnished shut, seamless, black to hold heat and shed it. Not a molecule leaves me. There is nothing to replace it with.

My head is low and blunt. My jaws are not for green — I have never known green — they are blunt rasps. My antennae are absurdly long for this crush, pale at the tips from wear, always moving. I taste with them. Heat, vibration, brother, not-brother, stone-dust, film.

Where am I in the room you could hold in both hands? I thought at first I would be on top, where it is a fraction cooler, waiting for the sky to drop something dead. But I am not waiting. I am down, lower, against the wall.

I am second layer from the hot basalt on the floor side, where the faint red lives down in the cracks. My belly side bakes against others who bake against stone. I scrape. That is my whole day, my whole slow life. I scrape the film — the thin sulfur-fixing slime that lives on hot air and fumes — up with stone-dust, chew the dust to get at the living part. Glass on my mouthparts. Grit between. When a shed skin cools near me I eat it before it cools. When a leg-joint of a dead brother is beside my head, I saw through it. I do not drag. There is nowhere to drag to and no water to waste on rot.

Above me, the weight. They walk on me. Leg over leg, antenna over back, back over back. I walk on others. The pile breathes. We carry heat and food from wall to wall simply by crawling over each other, living and dying in shifts.

I have riders. In the fold where my hind leg meets my body, a pale amber-white mite, the size of dust, moves slow as cattle, drinking grease off my chitin, sometimes drinking me. I tolerate it. In the gaps between my neighbors, grey-white flecks flick — springtails working crumbs, frass baked instantly to powder, threads too thin to say. I feel them more than know them.

We are loud. My tibiae are ridged like files. My shrunken wing-cases will never fly — there is no room to turn a head here, let alone fly — they are kept for sound. When I draw leg across case with a thousand others at once it is not a chirp. In this stone drum it becomes dry metal, riveting heard through a wall. Continuous.

And underneath, the mountain.

On a long gradient too slow to time, pressure builds far below and releases up through our bubble in a low hot sigh. Dust lifts. The whole mass shivers and resettles like grain on a shaking sieve. Roar, then ticking. Roar, then ticking. I read it through my feet before it comes. I brace with my high joints. I ripple and let others ripple over me.

The air crackles. So many dry bodies rubbing in thin hot air charges us. The hairs — if I had hairs — would lift. Sometimes when my antenna tip brushes cooling basalt, a faint blue spark ticks. I live electrified.

I do not hurry. I molt slowly. I will lay a few waxy eggs deep in a vesicle where heat is steadiest, if I am that one. My nymph will hatch paler, translucent brown, and bake dark. Over a season a sulfur crystal the size of a pinhead will grow in a cool corner I will never visit. Over a decade the ceiling will lower a millimeter as the basalt cools and tightens. A leg-joint in a lifetime. I will pack tighter.

If a hand blocks the mouth and takes the last red thread, I am still here in absolute black, hot, dry, deafening — one warm ticking body among thousands, scraping, carrying, being eaten a little at the edges when the wind brings nothing for a long stretch, a leg taken here, an antenna there, tolerating it with a ripple.

This is the furnace, fully occupied. I hold the room.
