At first I don't know where to put myself.

I come down over the lip — the rock still sharp under my hands — and for a while I am only the noise. Boots ringing on the pan like fired clay. Breath coming out of me in a white plume that shouldn't be here, because there is nothing here to evaporate. My lips crack within minutes. My eyes water and the wind takes it instantly.

I am too wet to be here. Too loud. Too warm.

I stand in the central crossing where all the avenues meet and I turn slowly, the way you do, and I can see the whole rim from any point. That is where I find myself first: exposed. In the middle of streets far too wide for anything that lives here now. My footprints break the ripples. The tenders' needle tracks cross in straight lines and are gone. Mine wander and stop and start again.

No. Not there. I can't stay there.

So I try being small and hidden. Under the pan, maybe. If I were a grub — blind, fat, white as wax, mouth full of chewed foundation-fiber — I wouldn't have to keep sealed. I could be soft down in the dark where the lichen roots go. I could eat the town from underneath and never have to look at the grey-blue wash that never becomes morning. But then I hear — no, feel — the vibration in my teeth when a tender stops beside a ripple for ten minutes without moving, and I know how that story ends. Unhinged, speared, folded, eaten whole. The walls drink even that tiny vibration. I don't want to be the eating, and I don't want to be the eaten. Not that.

If I were lichen I could be ash that is alive. A low cushion in a crack, holding whatever I have in wax. Breaking and sealing instantly with a bead. That has a certain honesty. Or if I were a tumble-thorn I wouldn't have to tend anything at all. No salt-licking, no pellet-pasting. Just roll very slowly around the bowl when the drafts push me, combing static out of the air so the dust keeps its charge so the mortar sticks. Rootless work. The tenders nudge me onward when I get caught against a house-mouth. I wouldn't mind being nudged.

But I keep coming back to the houses. Tall, hollow, thin-walled things the color of bone and wasp paper, leaning slightly inward, as if listening.

I put my head inside one and whisper, and the sound doesn't come back. The walls drink it. Inside it is ribbed, faintly warm to the touch despite the cold, and in the upper dark the slits where light should come in but doesn't. And the mouth breathes out air a degree colder than the air outside, over my face, over my cracked lips.

That is where I would find myself. Not in the crossing. In a mouth.

Because I am the size of the absence. The avenues are too wide for the tenders — you could lie down across one and not touch the edges — but they are not too wide for me. The dark oval at the base of each house is taller than me. It was made for something my size, something larger that is gone, something the town was shed for. The tenders are only what the town uses for hands now that those bodies are gone. Four feet up on stilts, fist of a body, band of wet black eyes. They keep old from unbuilding. They were never meant to fill it.

I was. Or someone like me was.

So I learn, slowly, to be fillable.

I stop talking first, because there is no point. The walls were made to drink sound. When two tenders meet they do not touch. They stop at distance and vibrate one stilt against the packed ground, so faint you feel it in your teeth. I press the heel of my boot very lightly and feel the shiver go up. I am learning not to waste it.

I stop sweating, or try to. To live here is to keep your inside sealed and trade only in dry things. I lick my cracked lips and taste iron and salt bloom, the fine powder the wind-gutters have sorted and piled for harvesting. A tender bends impossibly beside me — fist dropping from four feet to inches on folding stilts without a sound — and puts its soft rasp to the base and licks, long deliberate strokes up the bone-colored wall. Eating dryness and making it into something drier. It watches me with that band of eyes that gather the thin evening. It does not run. Freezing is its first language. I freeze back. That is our manners.

In a week I am still too wet, too loud, too warm. But I have learned to stand inside the mouth while the air turns over in the afternoon, dust lifting in silent sheets, houses creaking a millimeter in my teeth. I tuck myself up into the cold exhalation the way they sleep — standing, legs folded, eyes open and dark — and the house breathes over me, one after another, out of sync, like sleepers in a ward.

I am not a tender. I have no stilts, no cheek sacs full of black wire-grass seed, no pouch to regurgitate a warm pellet of dust and lichen-meal and wax and press it into a pinhole until it pales and becomes wall. My hands are too big and too damp for that lattice work, still tacky to the finger.

But I can be tended. I can be the body that comes back and learns to keep still enough to be alive here. To let salt crust on my skin and not lick it off wrong. To let the thorn-rollers make their circuit without tripping them. To lie down across an avenue built for me and not fill it entirely, and that be enough.

Near the center that thumb-sized bud pushes up from a crack, already hollow, already ribbed inside, faintly warm. In a year it will be taller than me, with its own dark oval mouth breathing cold. No one will ever live in it. That doesn't matter, they say. But I will go and stand in it anyway, because that is what it is for.

If I die here — when I slow and dull — they will wall me neatly into a base crack, legs folded like a closed umbrella, filmed over with new secretion. Not burial. Ingredient. And the house that eats me will breathe colder out of its mouth for a day.

I would be the wet thing that learned dryness. The noisy thing that learned shiver. The large soft absence, finally, filled.
