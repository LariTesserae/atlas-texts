You come down off the levee road and into it all at once. I would, too.

At first I think I would stay up top. That I would be the kind of thing that looks. From up there it just looks like a green plug, a smudge of steam. I could be the passerby who sees only the cup of light and mist at night and smells crushed cane and hot oil for a second through a truck window and keeps going.

But the path drops six, eight feet. Below the waterline of the paddies. My shoes would fill on the first sunk brick.

So no. I wouldn't stay up top. If I stayed at all I would get wet-footed fast.

At first, down inside, I think I would be hands. Because there is so much to touch and everything asks to be touched to be known. Brick that comes away velvety green-black on the palm. A curtain of morning glory lifted with the back of the wrist. Plank finding brick finding give underfoot. I would be reaching out to steady myself past the elephant ear, ducking under kudzu on the wires, learning not to grab the low steam pipe.

Then I think I would be ears. Because you hear them before you see anything. Thump-thump, thump-thump through the floorboards, through the sleeping mat, through the soles. The belts over it, the dynamo whine over that, the white rush of the tailrace lifting its glassy sheet up and over the dike. And under it all the small voices — eaves dripping after no rain, leaves dripping, weep holes in the dike, condensate spitting every few seconds onto moss. I would learn to shout a little the way the children shout, because green eats sound. And I would learn to listen the way they listen, for a change the way you would listen for a heart skipping.

Longer, I think I would be feet. Wide and pale from never drying. Finding without looking the brick placed forty years ago to step over the ground ditch. Rubber boots slit for air, or nothing that laces. Toe-webs white with the fungus that never quite clears. Soft-footed, because hurrying makes heat you can't shed. I would do the loop. Twenty feet with the hoe, throw onto the same heap. Up the transformer pole with the machete between my teeth to cut the vine back from the insulators before it arcs. Tap the sump gauge. Mark the slate. Same line as yesterday's line.

That is where you would find me, I think, finally. Not in the center at the old boiler house where you can see the edges in every direction. Off to the side, low, where the work holds.

Between the sump pits and the pump house. Where the hyacinth has to be forked out steaming every morning and by afternoon is already closing over again, glossy and purple-flowered. Where the herringbone path is wavy and purslane comes up between, where the plank walk goes over the tea-colored ditch, where the steam pipe sweats beside the walk and the clothesline is above that and the power line sags under trumpet vine above that and the gutter overflows into a rain chain of cut cans that drips and drips and never stops dripping. I would live in that ten feet of layering. I would know its six systems by sound in the dark.

What would I be? Not a priest. Not the drivers and oilers closest to the heart in the rooms that vibrate day and night, though I would sleep near enough that the thump gets into my hands and leaves the permanent tremor that matches the engines. Not the pan-men gone shiny pink on the forearms, though I would chew cane constantly until my teeth browned at the gumline and drink the roof rainwater boiled with fig leaves to take the iron out. Not the child swinging on the fig's aerial roots at lunch, screaming to be heard over the belts — though in April, when the living bamboo of the sleeping loft leafs out and creaks against its lashings, I would dream greener that month like everyone who sleeps inside it.

I would be the tender of the note. The one who has been there long enough that proximity is rank.

The old man checks the gauge, taps it, marks the slate. I would be the second tap. The one who learns pump-time as a first language: *before shift, after the belts were changed, when No.2 was coughing.* The one who can hear a quarter-inch drop in the sump in sleep. The one the women hanging laundry over the steam pipe also hear, turning their heads at once with everyone in the yard, then turning back.

My skin would glisten not with sweat that comes and goes but with the standing film — condensation that forms on people the way it forms on pipes. Green tinge in the creases where algae takes in the salt. Oil under the nails that no amount of washing in ditches and sumps and under rain chains will lift. Bagasse splinters, condensate spits. Bad lungs, bad knees, steady.

I would eat morning rice, midday rice with boiled greens the white cabbage moths work over all day, evening rice with sugar stirred in. I would be called in not by name but by whistle. I would bury no one down here. Up on the levee, in dry ground, above the waterline. The only thing we do up there.

And if you stayed till evening, when the shift changes and the air cools one degree toward bearable and the bare bulbs hum on amber under the fig canopy and steam from the pans and breath from a million leaves rise together and sit in the bowl till morning — you wouldn't see me separately.

From the levee road you would see only the shallow cup holding its small, crowded, living shape just below the water, throbbing faintly. Inside it: people trimming, plants thickening, engines turning. Each keeping the other from drowning or drying or stopping.

I would be in the throb. The hand that keeps the lift steady.
