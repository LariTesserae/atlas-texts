At first I think I would be moving.

Anyone coming here thinks that. You step off onto the grating, the air cuts you thin at the top of the throat, the deck comes up through your boots into your teeth, and you think: I will be the thing that crosses this. A boat running three days across hammered blue. A whale plowing just under the surface, mouth open.

I try that on. Thirty meters of white blubber, sieving snow that never stops falling. It should feel immense. But up here immensity doesn't work the way I thought. In water this clear and heavy my click would come back to me from three hundred kilometers away as loud as from under my own belly. I would hear every other white back steaming, every rustle of plates on glass, all the time, in one continuous acoustic room. To be a mill-whale here is not to be huge. It is to be held apart by agreement. One whale per cloud. Sometimes two, for years, in parallel, never touching. I don't think I could stand that kind of politeness. I am too lonely and too loud already.

So then I think smaller, faster — to be clotted instead of spread. To be one of the blind whites.

Billions of them where the station breathes out. Thumbnail-long, eyeless, pulsing. From above they darken the water from below, from below they blot out the white sky. That belonging appeals to me. To open and shut with the whole cloud, to taste for sulfur, for iron, for warmth, to live fast and die fast on mineral snow. Eat snow or eat each other. But I know how that ends. I would rain down. I would become what falls.

And what falls is eaten by the plates.

So I try that. A plate-louse, dinner-plate wide, mineralized white, walking slowly on too many legs over rope pahoehoe still keen enough to cut rope — and not cut. Drifts of us meters deep, clicking shell on shell, a dry rustling like dead leaves under the roar that you can only hear if you put a hydrophone down. We are the patient. We pile, we right each other, we molt in place and eat the molt. Where the sawblade ridge juts we stack at the waterline in lace ice and wait for heat to return. I like that patience. I like being low, armored, unhurried in 1-degree water. But I am not patient. I keep looking up through two hundred meters of empty glass water at the flat white lid. I keep listening for variation.

Maybe then the ribbon. Ten thousand pale eels braided into one muscle a kilometer long, turning as one muscle. Seen from the deck, a single white scar flexing under the water. If a whale passes we part around it without breaking rhythm and reform behind it. No panic. There is no room for panic in water this cold. Alone an eel is helpless, almost transparent, gut showing full of white paste. Together we are the fastest thing here. I want that — to be helpless alone and infallible together. But I don't braid well. I come apart.

So where do I actually find myself?

Not in the upper empty water. That is a desert held vertically between a white sky and a black floor, nothing per liter to eat or hide in. Not out at the edge either, where the whole ocean is falling off the Table four kilometers into cloud in a planet-circling hiss you feel through the hull, through bone. You can't stand there. From the center you can't even see it.

I find myself where life clots because it has to. On stilts.

A station. A block of riveted steel, pipes going down, stacks going up. Pumps the size of houses sucking cold water down and dumping hot brine back, product with nowhere to go. They just run. Inside the door someone has written the rules again in marker: *Nothing grows tall. Distance is real. Do not expect change. Keep the engines running.*

I am the winter keeper. Or the one after the winter keeper left the light blinking.

One per station, sometimes none. Gone deaf, necessarily deaf, because it is 120 decibels of noon and earplugs do nothing. The deck vibrates. The sternum vibrates. You have to shout into another person's ear and the wind of the sound strips your words. After an hour you stop noticing variation because there is no variation. The needle in the control room sits where it sat a decade ago. The sawblade tooth out past the stilts stays tooth. The sun, too close and too white, has not moved all day. It is always just now, just midday.

I have become pale, like the rest. White skin under white light, eyes squinted to slits against flat shadowless glare. My breath smokes. The oil smokes. Metal burns to touch with cold. I eat tins. Sometimes I boil the whites — a pot of thumbnail shrimp tasting of iron and sulfur — because the old ones said you could. I watch the bacterial felt below paint the shallows in pale tiger stripes, white and rust-orange, tight to the glass. Nothing grows tall, so I have learned to grow flat. Felted. Tight to the steel. I eat rock and heat in one form or another. At the dimming that passes for night here, the mats glow faintly orange along the ridges below like city lights seen from a plane, and the whales rise and blow, and their blows freeze instantly and hang as short white columns that do not drift.

Every ten seconds the mast light blinks. Across a sea too big to see across, every other mast blinks with it, in perfect unison. The next light is 311 km east. It is exactly as loud there.

That is where I am. Suspended between two shallows that feel like depths — black glass crawling in perfect detail two hundred meters down, flat white sky locked overhead — deafened by a stability so complete it feels like motion.

And what I do, what has become more itself in me the longer I stand on the grating looking straight down as if flying: I tend the roar, and I listen for what is underneath it.

Rule four says keep the engines running. No one remembers what happens if they stop, and no one wants the roar to reveal what is underneath it.

I keep them running. But between blinks — ten seconds, the world blinking — I put my palm flat to the cold rivets and feel for the other layers: the falls circling, the clicking of millions of blind mouths, the settling crack of young glass, my own blood. I am waiting for the second when the drone, the hiss, and the animals separate again into three things instead of one thing. I am waiting to hear the lace rim of ice form around the stilt. The plates righting each other. Snow falling a hundred meters straight down, glittering.

I will not stop the pumps. Not yet. I have learned here not to expect change. But I have become the animal that maintains sameness in order to hear, for one thin knifey breath, what sameness is holding up.
