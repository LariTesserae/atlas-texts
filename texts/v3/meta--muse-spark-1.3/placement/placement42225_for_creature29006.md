Don't look for me on the rim.

I stood there for the second you're allowed — guardrail, haul road, wind coming off the transformer yards — and the scale did what it does. It made me want to be elsewhere in it. Not above it.

So I go down.

At first I think I am cold. Just that. Twelve degrees that gets in through any seam, that makes fingers stupid in minutes, that slows gills and thought alike. I am suspended out over poured distance, fifty meters of green-grey above me to the white ache of sun, hundreds of meters to the next wall, concrete dust hanging in a work-light cone. I hear the tick of cooling concrete in my teeth.

At first I think I am moving. Because everything here that is beautiful moves —

the silver, tens of thousands turning at once with that rain-click of tails, the whole drowned airfield flashing when a break in cloud catches us. I could be that. A three-inch quicksilver facing into the gyre, parting without hurry around a diver's bubbles. It would be good to be weather for a while, to never have to hold on.

Or the commuter, dropping in after the mist burns off by ten. Black, snake-necked, built for vertical water over flat concrete. Stab a hundred feet into jade, spear through a school edge, surface with it crosswise. Then shake and fly back up to dry steel to preen. I could be that hunger that knows exactly where it lives: wet for work, dry for home.

Or even the people, because for a minute I want hands. To ride the cage elevator down the wall face with a lunchbox between my knees, hood up even in summer, to change a sodium flood on Bench 9 while perch nip at my fins. *Hold the cone, pass the shackle, up.* To work fast before the cold steals me, to see my own headlamps from the rim at night moving slow along a submerged bench, a pair of lights walking a road that is still a road. To climb back up into wind and stamp feeling back in.

But all of those leave. That's what I keep coming back to. The cormorant flies up. The sparkie climbs up. The silversides keep circling, never resting. Even the eel — long, grey-green, cruising the painted centerline six feet off the bottom in packs of three and four at dusk — even the eel goes back to its den when it's done, to the square black mouth of a drain gallery, to a locker in the Bench 14 lunchroom.

I don't want to go back. I want to stay.

Closer. The crackle gets louder. Not fat frying far away anymore. Close. Inside.

Put your hand on the wall corner on Bench 6 and it gives slightly. It is not rock anymore.

There. That edge.

You'd find me where the wall stops being a wall and becomes a bench — a true ninety degrees, still sharp, still young. Shotcrete with the drill marks still in it, half-moons in rows from the last blast. A galvanized rock-bolt head six inches above me with only a few inches of rust drip because nothing here has had time to get old, and clinging to that bolt, a single cold-water kelp no longer than a forearm, white-tipped and frayed, waving stupidly and stubbornly in the intake pull. My neighbor. Below us, the concrete falls away clean to the next drop, painted lines still yellow-white under thirty feet of water. Above us, the work lights on their steel masts burn all day, cones full of motes. At night the whole spiral glows faint under water like a city through ice, and our light blooms up from Bench 9.

I am blue-black. Sharp as the rock I sit on.

I am a mussel. One of the mat on mat, byssus drilled into fresh shotcrete, prying it apart grain by grain. Velvet green biofilm slicks the sunlit concrete around me — the first skin, thin as paint — and in the cracked expansion joint a few feet down, ditch grass shivers, five blades, stunted. The film feeds me. The gyre feeds me. The seiche feeds me, that slow pit tide meters high sloshing bench to bench over hours, pulling and pushing at my shell.

I open and close. Open and close. Thousands of us, that faint frying crackle you hear through the concrete and through your chest. I filter concrete dust and oil sheen and larvae and the spawn of my own kind raining down toward the white frost three kilometers below me that I will never see. By spring there is green velvet on bright new grey where a flake fell. By fall there are spat on me, no bigger than pinheads, holding on.

An eel stops and looks. It has never learned to be afraid. I see my own dark reflection fisheyed in its eye as it noses over the bed, picking off a mussel-crab. Perch hang in the lee of the BENCH 14 sign twenty feet up, eight of them nose to current, spooking when bubbles hit them and re-forming a minute later in exactly the same place. A cormorant booms in somewhere above. A diver's glove brushes the handrail — stupid fingers, fast work — and is gone.

I remain.

The sea here hasn't learned how to round a corner yet. Neither have I. I have learned something else instead, the only lesson this place teaches all the way down to the anemones on their rebar chairs: how to love an edge.
