At first I am still too large for here.

I hang mid-street between sand and black, warm and neutrally buoyant, and the water closes behind my hand without a ripple I can see. I bring with me the wrong senses — eyes that look for faces, for doors that open, ears that wait for surf. I look for someone walking out and I see only walls. Only the city, keeping itself.

So I drift. Let the avenue take me.

It is lined on both sides with towers, three, four of their stories above me. The current runs down it like wind, swept clean by a thousand fans. I go with it, past the white cliff that breathes — I feel it draw, a million mouths at once, a stutter in the flow against my skin — past the plaza where the floor has been planed flat, sand dollars edge to edge, slowly plowing. A cloud parts around my lips. Larvae, no bigger than eyelashes, each perfectly formed, eye-spotted and transparent. Copepods pulsing thick enough to change the viscosity. Through them, traffic: blind fish, big-eyed fish, mouths wide and open, commuting. An eel pours like slow rain from one window to another.

I try to be them, for a while. To try them on.

I thought I might be the octopod, dog-sized, wedged in the great barrel-sponge with one arm always curled out the door, tasting the street. That would be a good life for a watcher — denned, intelligent, touching everything and holding nothing. But my skin is not that clever. I cannot taste that well.

I thought I might be a crab, sideways and deliberate along the cornice, cracking the weak. Or the white bone-blind lobster tapping across a foot with antennae longer than its body. Or one of the fat sandbags bulldozing the alley, vacuuming silt and leaving clean sand. But I have no armor, no claws. I am too soft, and I keep drifting. I do not clear the street. The street clears me.

Above me something large passes without sound. Only a pressure wave, a displaced volume, a smell of iodine. The whole facade flinches — *click click click* down the block as the oysters shut like windows in a storm — and then, slowly, dares to open again. I feel how small I have to become to stay here.

Because the rule holds utterly, and my body is beginning to understand it: if you want a wall, you must make it out of yourself.

I am shrinking. Or the city is teaching me scale.

My hands — those useless, tool-less things I arrived with — go quiet. No one here has hands. No one here has fires or wire or cut stone. What I have instead is a fringe that beats. A crown that sieves. A gut, red and segmented, two inches long.

I am in the plankton now, not above it. I am one of the motes hanging above the rooftops, and the water tastes of limestone and rot and spawn, metallic-sweet ash from the rift vents beyond the ridges. All the lips gape upstream. All the crowns tilt the same way. The whole town leans, very slightly, into the warm flow, and I lean with it. I am looking — not with eyes, eyes are a rumor here, 0.1, a memory — but with taste, for a place to stop.

I find myself where the city is raw.

Beyond the middle-aged block — dense, saturated, cleaner shrimp picking parasites off a patient moray in a doorway like barbers — there is a new district. Tubes newly extended, mucus still tacky. Sharp, not yet weathered round as knuckles. I settle there, on the side of a chimney, shoulder to shoulder with thousands side by side. As spat, as dust, I shouldered against them and began to secrete, grain by grain, mucus and limestone, a finger-width pipe of parchment. My own held-open body as home.

This is where you would find me, if you came down the avenue and let your eyes go soft: three inches up a worm-tower on the edge of the new district, just downstream of the sponge-hearts exhaling their slow cleaned breath, just upstream of the sand-dollar plaza. A hair on the city that is hairy with life.

And this is what I am:

A tower-dweller. A holder-on who sways almost imperceptibly.

Only my crown shows — two spirals of feathery radioles, unfolding like ferns into the warm mineral current. All of us tilted the same way. All of us sieving. When a fish darts over, we withdraw in a rippling flinch, down into our own secreted walls. When it passes, we dare to open again. Beneath me, generation under generation of empty tubes, the dead still holding up the living. Above me, the snow of phosphorescence when something is disturbed. Beside me, the lace-makers stuccoing our joints — bryozoans sheeting calcium, forams gluing sand to sand — already beginning to smooth my sharp new edge.

I do not move and I do not need to. My gill-fringe beats ceaselessly. I hear what the city hears now: the constant low filtration hum, the kick kick kick of barnacle feet volcanoed on the sponge flank across the street, now and then startlingly close the pop and sizzle of a snapping shrimp, a knuckle cracking in a cathedral. Then quiet, and the slow sigh of the current turning over.

A fish died in the alley this morning. By evening its spine is scaffolding. Its ribs are already apartments for amphipods. I can taste its rot-sweet downstream, and it tastes like renewal.

No one planned me here. Everyone maintains me. I am the place, breathing, one crown in a shivering field.
