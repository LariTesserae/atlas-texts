At first I thought I would be high.

Coming up over that last shoulder with my lungs coming short and the light hitting like a weight, deafened by the notch wind before I even saw the bowl — I thought, let me be one of the turners. One of the old lammergeiers. To be that far above the roar, to never flap, to just turn slow circles inside the lenticular's rotor for hours and watch it all legible below: the polygons, the black mirrors, the screaming funnels. To remember. To know every gust by name and where the bone-anvils are, white splinters on white quartz. That seemed like the place to be.

Then I was inside the amphitheater and the sound got into my sternum and I thought, no — be a chough. Be loud enough to cut through it. *Chee-ow, chee-ow.* To live shoulder to shoulder by the thousands on a guano-white crack and then pour off at dawn and not flap but ride, half-folded, tail twisting, falling a hundred feet on purpose to snatch a moth and letting the river throw you back up. Locking feet with someone you love and cartwheeling down toward the slabs and letting go at the last second. To probe a crevice with a grass stem, to learn. To be clever and hungry with no hands to freeze.

But I kept kneeling. You have to kneel here to see anything. From standing the ground looks lifeless.

I knelt by a cushion dome — hard as a fist, tight green studded with tiny white flowers — in the lee of a frost-shattered slab the color of old bone, near one of the pans. Black mirror, no deeper than my wrist, rimmed with black moss and orange lichen. A marker of pollen on the edge that did not move. And for a full minute nothing moved and I thought: sterile.

Then a pebble walked.

That's where I found myself. Not above. Low. Lower than the birds, lower than knees.

In the lee where the wind breaks for an inch. In a fist-deep wind-hole pocked into the tor, where the shale makes a little corridor between two cushion continents. Where a single silk is strung low, almost invisible until the sun catches it, anchored to shale on one end and to a dead beard-lichen tuft on the other, strung with desiccated husks ticking faintly.

I am soot-black to drink the sun.

That's the first thing that becomes itself: heat. A thin inch of heat on stone at midday, and my back drinking it. My legs too long for my body, my eyes four wet pinheads catching white light and throwing it back. Tick-tick-tick of tarsi on shale — too fast to follow if you're watching, but from inside it's just walking, it's just how the world sounds under the roar: tick, plink of condensation off the overhang into the pan, thin whine of midges, blood.

I don't make a web to catch. I make a line to listen.

All day the sky snows. You can see it if you stay still and let your eyes unfocus against the enamel blue: midges, aphids, ballooning spiderlings, moths the size of sesame seeds torn up from forests three thousand feet below, carried up on thermals and dumped cold-stunned onto snow and stone. Glittering as they come down. Endless. They stumble and hop between the cushions, and when they touch the line — I am already there.

I don't eat what grows here. There's not enough green to eat and nothing wastes water on leaves. I eat the weather. I eat what the western notch vomits up every day. The slate-gray hoppers who live their whole lives inside that one cushion dome like it's a continent, leaping flea-like from flower to flower — sometimes I eat them too. Sometimes a snowfinch cloud settles in a single breath, fifty birds on fifty domes, stabbing, and lifts all at once when a shadow passes, wings like surf, and I press flat into the pit and become stone again until the shadow goes.

Above me the screamers learn. I've seen it from below, which is the true view: a young red-bill landing by my wind-hole, blocking the sun for a second huge and black with that coral cut of beak. Cocking its head. Picking up a stiff dry stem, probing it into my crevice, rattling. I go still. It drops the stem. Picks up another, shorter. Probes deeper, twists. One of my brothers bolts and is gone in a beak. The old birds carry favorite twigs for an hour, stripped willow sharpened at one end by nibbling, or a chipped obsidian edge that doesn't occur naturally, to lever the armored ones — the black beetles with their fused shields who bulldoze pollen at noon — out from under shale. I know to fear the shadow and the rattle both.

In autumn I will be heavy in a different way. The gray mantle seething on my back while I hunt. My young clinging as I walk the polygons. In winter I will wedge under the slab where stone in shade is ice, go torpid, and they will eat the unviable eggs off my own abdomen and then each other until spring thins them to the strongest three. That's not cruelty. That's how you stay on the point.

At evening when the light thins blue but doesn't leave and the insect snow slackens, the great chattering mass settles back onto the teeth, beaks tucked into back feathers. The finches tuck into lichen-lined holes. The beetles wedge under stone. The old turners fold down onto a favorite tooth like closing a book. I reel in nothing — the line stays — I just pull my legs in.

Under the hammering white noise, if you've stayed still long enough for your ears to learn how to hear inside deafness, you can hear the bowl breathing: low A and a fifth above it whistling through the same holes, plink into black mirror water that never fills nor empties, just holds.

I hold with it.

Nothing accumulates. Nothing leaves. I found the point long ago and have no intention of moving off it.
