At first I thought I would be standing.

That is how you enter - upright, boots shedding marsh, eyes looking for the edges. And for a minute that is true. I am at the mouth where the vapor hangs, and then a pace in, and the air gets into my clothes, and my ankles find a pan.

But I can't stay standing. No one can. Standing is too much up and down in a place that is all across. My weight displaces. The Vs heal behind me. My breathing is too loud. The ticks go on without me - tick, tick from a dozen points, never in rhythm - and I am only interruption.

So no. Not the stander.

Then I thought I might be the quick thing. The beetle. The small brown oarer that makes Vs that heal instantly and is gone through the vapor again. That would be easy: to visit and be forgiven because I didn't stay long enough to change the level. But the pans never overflow, never dry. A visitor doesn't learn that. I wanted to learn that.

I thought I might be the hands. The ones that stacked the slabs two courses high, that hollowed the log, that knapped the chert and left flakes around the three river stones. Hands that heated pebbles black and dropped them hissing to make steam. For a while I tried that on - my palms on warm rock, my thumb testing an edge. But my palm print beaded over in a minute and healed. The ash is already washed to clean grey paste. The fire-spot is cold and wants to stay cold. The hands left. I don't want to be what left.

Lower, then.

If you crouch, the floor takes you. Stone to stone to stone, water to water to water. A millimeter deep moving without seeming to move. Your distorted face in every saucer, and the sky-smear, and the beads overhead about to drop. My knees found that out before I did. They ached in the bath-warmth and then stopped aching, which is different from being healed.

I find myself - I keep finding myself - on the platform.

Not under it. I am not her. I cannot flatten to eighteen inches long and breathe through my belly, though I tried in my mind for a while, folding my limbs to creases, letting condensation sit on me as it sits on everything that can hold it. That place is taken. From under the slabs her face watches, damp bread, wet chert, eyes like two more pans reflecting only the brighter smear. She blinked once. She will not blink again while I watch.

But above her, there is just enough room left. That is the rule of this place. Packed full of stone and water and warm air, with just enough room left for a few hand-made things set carefully on top.

I am set carefully on top.

Legs dangling off the two courses of slabs, bare feet in the pan where she gardens. The slabs are darkened and beaded like everything else. My weight goes down through them into her back. A thrum. I feel her take it and flatten flatter, not leaving. There is nowhere else with this water.

Who am I, then, if I am not knapper or fire-maker?

I am the dipper and carrier and sitter. The things were made for that and I have stopped trying to make them be more.

I sit. I take the pecked sandstone bowl, half full of clear warm water, and I pour it over my forearms, over the back of my neck where a stone-drip has already ticked. Warm on warm. Droplets gather on my eyelashes. My clothes cling and stay clinging. I do not wipe them. To wipe would be to dry, and there is no dry in it.

I sit until my breathing, made loud by the closeness, gets quieter. Until inflow and evaporation balance in me too. Warm water welling cool-warm around my ankles, warm water leaving my back as vapor. I have been exactly this wet for a very long time, though it has only been an hour.

She mistakes my stillness for stone-ness and resumes.

That is when I become most myself: a throat pulse under the slabs, once every long while, an almost inaudible sigh. A bead running down stone that is her shoulder. The faint skin-sound of her slow tongue rasping the stain - rust-orange and dull olive - a millimeter a day, so it never thickens, never thins, so the stones stay too slick and too mineral for any seed.

I do not turn my head when something blunders in through the vapor, a gnat stunned by the dim, fluttering across a pan. She opens without splash. Her mouth is a straight seam nearly as wide as her head, hinged like a trough lid. It closes without splash. Then the pan heals. I do not startle. Months between such mouthfuls, and I am learning to wait months.

The trough beside me is full to the brim and does not spill. On nights when the upwelling runs a fraction warmer she will take it full-length, head at the adze-marked end, nostrils just breaking the skin, drinking as much as she displaces. I let her. I keep the bowl half full. I keep my feet still.

I do not ask her to leave and she does not invite me to stay. Human warmth and spring warmth are both warmth.

That is where I am: on the low dry seat above the film, under the sweat and above the stain, between the brighter smear and the face that reflects it. What I am is the roof that learned how to be a stone.
