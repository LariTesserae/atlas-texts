At first I think I would still be standing up.

Boots on a slab edge, too tall, too dry. Head up in the drip. A visitor's height. I can feel how wrong that is right away — the air is crowded ankle to eye, and up where I'm standing there's only pipe and mist and the shivering veil of flies. Nothing up there to be. The Work doesn't have a place for anything that stands that high and looks around.

So I get lower.

For a while I think I might be Mat. Black-blue and breathing, glued shoulder to shell to shoulder until my edges deform to my neighbors. There's a peace in that — to open a finger's width and close, and take the invisible soup as it weeps up warm as blood through the joint and make shell out of it. To be clean amber tile for a night if I'm pried up, and scarred over by morning. But I'm too restless for Mat. I keep listening for the ticking valve, for the click of legs. Mat doesn't listen. It filters.

Then I think, Picker. That dry rain sound — I want to be part of making it. A small white crab, bleached translucent by the day that never ends, one claw slightly larger, clicking over tile. Or the isopod in the seam, thumb-sized and armored, wedged where the slab weeps, grazing the rust-red felt. Low work. Necessary work. Unmaking the pigeon that died in the night by morning. But Pickers hurry, in their way. They crouch flat when the sheet goes glassy over the weir. I don't want to crouch. I want to stay and breathe through it.

Not the Curtain, either. I find a pipe-bridge where they hang, dense as wet coats, fur damp, bellies pale from condensate, and I try to hang there with them. But to be a bat here is to hold yourself folded for a day that never ends, to flick out only in short sips — out, snap, back to the exact spot. I can't hold that stillness. And not the Veil. The moths eating feather powder and dried dropping dust, rising and shivering and falling to the Pickers. Too brief. Too speckled.

I keep coming back to the rims.

You would find me on a rim slab in BASIN 4419802, third rank back from the water, a little left of where the drip line sweats off the copper feed. Where the off-white tile has already gone amber and the lime-concreted droppings have made the second pavement, sharp and knuckled underfoot, white volcano barnacles opening to the wet air around my toes. Eel-deep pan to my front, dovecote wall twelve meters up to my back — precast pigeonholes, each warm and occupied, whitewash fresh — and to my west, nothing but repeating sheds with sawtooth roofs to the curvature of the world.

I am a Stander.

Small, brown, speckled. A wader. Not a gull — too loud in the bones, even silenced — not a pigeon with a hole to go back to. Just rim stock. My feathers touch on both sides. Left shoulder to a pigeon dusted white with lime, right shoulder to another brown like me. We all face west, into the faint draft. Breast to the coolest air, tails to the drip.

This is more itself the longer I stay: the breathing.

Lift and settle, lift and settle. The whole slab breathing almost together. Ankles in warm sheet, knee-deep if I step forward into the Mat where the mussels open and close in their slow traveling ripple and the tube-worms pull in their red eyelash fans when my shadow passes. Crab-legs clicking like dry rain underneath. Above, the Veil speckling the thinned white sun. Behind the eyes, that 50-cycle hum in the busbars deep down, felt more in the teeth and in the hollow of the beak than heard. Water ticking off a valve. A thin sheet running glassy over the weir.

I doze standing in the warm mist. I preen lime out of the neck feathers of the bird to my left, and she preens mine. That is the job. To stand because there is nowhere else to be and nothing else to do, and to make, copiously and continuously, the pavement beneath me.

I carry my smaller town with me now. I can feel it if I attend: thousands of pale feather mites in the oil, eating oil and skin scale. When I shake, down and mites fall to the pan. The mussels take them out. The water runs off clear. Water in equals water out. Heat in equals heat out. Nothing accumulates.

I don't call. None of us do.

Every few hours the stencil comes true: FLUSH ON CYCLE. The pan runs a little faster. The mussels close. The crabs crouch flat under the Mat. The eels go still, slow as oil stopped. We lift half a meter in a white hush that makes no sound — all those wings, no sound — and settle again exactly where we were, west-facing. The gap closes. There was never a gap.

If I died — heat, age — I know how it would go. I would not lie long. By morning the white crabs would have unmade me to bone and bone to fragments, and the line would have shuffled sideways to close where I was, still facing west. Another larva settles that night where a mussel was lifted. The level in the pan would not rise or fall by a finger's width.

That is where I am. Low the way a floor is low. In the crowd that learned how to keep itself level, using shell and feather and chitin to do it. Facing west with millions of others, listening to something just below hearing, keeping the water clear.
