At first I don't know. My boots are still sighing in the mat.

I think, builder. Of course I think builder. Hands that want to mend. I want to be up high where the light leaves last, cat-sized and soft, with a back I have learned to garden. I want to chew moss and grit into a pellet and press it in with my forehead, patient as anyone. I want to gather on the common roof at dusk and touch antennae and trade cuttings like news.

But I am too loud for that. I hear my own breath. I am the only thing here that needs water, and my skin leaks. I would tear the seal just by hurrying. A tower wall would have to remember me as weather, and not kind weather.

So not builder. Not yet. Maybe never.

I try slower.

Maybe a grazer. I could be flat to the point of seeming poured, velvet-backed, sage-dusted, inching up a sunlit wall and polishing as I go. Twenty of us spaced exactly as far as our halos reach, never touching. That appeals to me — work without herding, a pale trail behind that the sun hardens in an hour. But I am not that cool. Touch my fur and it would not be cool. I run hot. I want too much.

Under, then? A turner. Blind, white, thick as a wrist. Moving through pumice flour in peristalsis, coating each grain with gut mucus until sterile cinder can hold a spore. No eyes to waste. Nobody sees me unless the mat breathes over me in the lane where the felt has worn thin. I like the humility of that. The town does not suffocate because I keep turning underneath it. But I am not blind. I look too much. I can't stop looking.

Above? A collector. Ash-moth big as a sparrow, wings spread flat to catch heat and oil-fog together. For hours do nothing. Then let go when the thermal finally stirs and glide — three feet, four feet — no beat if I can help it, because beating dries. I could learn that. God, I want to learn that. To hang and not fly. But my wings, if I have them, keep beating. I keep making noise to stay up.

Which leaves the only places nothing else wants.

Come down off the bridges. Down off the tower skins. Down to lane-height where the air is warmest. Step around the grazers; they will not move for you. Find where the felt mat gives out, where it hasn't yet advanced grain by grain over the new fall of cinders. Fresh fracture face. Razor glass still glassy, still sharp as broken bottle, too young to have learned how to be a hill.

That's where you'd find me.

Belly-flat on stone too sharp for moss. Dun and rust-black, the exact color where sun hits basalt. Small. Still to the point you took me for part of the rock until my throat pulsed.

Once. Twice. A thin pink window opening and closing, conserving everything.

I am a watcher.

I have learned — I am learning — the economy of stillness. Do not flee. Fleeing is evaporation. Fleeing is death by a hundred breaths. So I film my eyes with wax and lick them clean once a day and otherwise just track you, lidless. My mouth stays shut to keep the moisture in. When the builders drum twice with a hind foot on hollow fiber and I feel it in my sternum, I freeze with everyone else. Grazers stop rasping. Moths fold a fraction tighter. Even the seed pods hold their tick. Then, slowly, we resume.

I am not useless here, though I am not of here. That's why they tolerate me.

At night I will crawl, low and slow, into the hollow tower to steal heat. I will lick sap overflow from the drain groove — not to drink, I know better than to drink, the clear pine-smelling sap would blister my tongue and seal my lips — just to taste the edge of it. And in the day, on my hot glass, I will do the one thing the builders cannot patch against: snap the tiny sap-flies that would lay in open pores. A flick. Nothing fast. Nothing wasted.

Stay till the light slants and you'll see me whole. When the sun drops below the western rim and shadow pours in like something poured, the towers transluce amber from inside and every back-garden glows from beneath. The builders line the rims, still, facing the last sun. Above them the moths make their short glides, ash to ash. Below them the lanes breathe.

And on my sharp stone, at the edge of the third lane between the leaning towers, just above the lizard-lick basin, I will still be there. Not fled. Belly warm with stored sun, throat pulsing in the lantern light, watching you learn the hamlet pace — a shuffle, a pause, a look — until your breathing slows enough to hear what the place uses instead of noise.
