At first I thought I'd stay standing. At the rim.

Keep my boots on rock that still belonged to up and down, keep the option of leaving. Watcher watching watchers. That seemed honest — I am, by habit, a visitor. I sit in the horseshoe but I don't fold. I breathe too fast. I wait for the roar to change.

It doesn't.

So no. I wouldn't last on the rim. The pack going weightless then double-weightless would unmake me. I need a place lower, where the rules are closer together.

I drift — because here everyone drifts whether they mean to or not — down toward the damp.

Not the center of the basin. The center is too exposed, too perfectly tuned. The big tarns there reflect too much ridge, remember too loudly. I would find myself off to one side, where a string of table-sized tarns threads through black mud and sponge-moss, one almost-touching the next. Where the straw grass lies flattened mid-bend and doesn't tremble. Where the leeward face of a low boulder is beaded so thick it looks sugared.

I would find myself with my belly to that.

At first I thought I would be one of the perched ones. White on grey, tinggi, patient. That is the image that catches — the dirty-snow bird with the wrong legs braced on a tor, blinking once an hour. I wanted that stillness. But I can't do that stillness yet. My eyes move too much. I would blink too often and give myself away as new.

Let that go.

At first I thought I would be lichen. Rust-orange, sulphur-pale, plastered tight. That would be easier. No hunger, no listening, just holding on. But lichen doesn't hear the slab. And I want to hear the slab. I have already put my ear to it, in the horseshoe.

So let that go too.

What remains, when I stop trying to be high or to be invisible, is low and furred and damp-heavy.

I would be one of the folding ones. Not the youngest — the youngest are still being led to the tarns to drink, still startling at their own secondary roar rising. Not the oldest either, the ones who have become almost tor themselves. I would be middle, which here doesn't mean age, it means *tuned*.

I am white but not clean-white. Grit in the underwool. Condensation beaded along each guard hair and never running. That is the first thing that becomes itself: the wet that never wets further. I press myself into the moss sponge between two tarns until my fur is heavy, then I press myself to the leeward boulder and drink without mouth, through skin. Belly-cold. Stone-cold going in like a needle. My breath hangs a second too long in front of my folded face, too tired to disperse. I let the suspended ice-needles gather on my shoulders. I don't brush them off. Bright, unfalling.

My legs are wrong in both directions. That took longest to feel. When I walk, single-file though no one is ahead or behind, from tarn to tarn, my shoulders hinge extra. My spine wants to double. On the third light step — you know the one, where up goes thin and your kicked pebble hangs a finger's width above ground — I don't resist it. I lift. Not fly. There isn't enough air to fly properly here, and too much noise. Lift. Legs dangling, cloven front toes black against white, fur fluffed to catch what air there is, and scud without wind to the next damp seam. That is why my trail stops in the middle of bare rock. Because I stopped needing rock.

And then the work.

I have a slab. Horseshoe open always to the east, though the wind comes from everywhere. Inside, a flat stone I keep swept clean — not with hands, with my side, with a slow drag of fur — so the hum comes through unmuffled. I fold myself double on it, beak to tailbone, knees up around ears, elbows out. The way the little beehive hut demands. Back to the stone, face to the paler smudge that is the sun that never arrives.

Not for shelter. There is no wind to break.

For listening. For keeping.

Put your whole folded belly to the slab and you feel the basin ringing like a struck bell that never decays. Low organ-pipe from the rock itself, high thin whistling just behind your ears. Fixed volume. Everywhere and nowhere. My weight is a fret. A damper. Stone placed on stone long ago changed the resonance a fraction, and body placed on stone keeps it. In air this thin you cannot shout far. In noise this loud you cannot call. So you stack. So you sit. Stone on stone says: I was here, I am still here, keep ringing.

I do that. I keep it exactly as loud and as still and as unfinished as it is.

Sometimes I go to my tarn — the second in the string, the one that reflects the opposite ridge and not the sky — and kneel. After a while the secondary roar rises, tuned just under the main, and in it I hear footsteps, breathing, stone set on stone. Myself setting. Myself sitting. Still circulating, unable to decay. I dip and drink the memory. Not story. Condensate. That is history enough.

If you sat long enough in my horseshoe when I am out soaking, cold through your trousers, wet through your trousers, roar filling your skull until you can't tell if it's outside or in your blood, I would be the white that lifts from a distant tor without wingbeat and drifts to brace on your tor. Dangling those cloven feet. Settling.

Not curious. Collegial.

Another damp, stone-warm thing become for a while part of the established balance: stone, damp, lichen, roar — and me, the patient, double-jointed keeper who walked straight and passed the same split boulder with the hand-shaped lichen three times, from slightly different angles, and was three watchers, and was one.
