If I came here, I wouldn't stay where I first think.

At first I think I'd be upright. A figure on the rim — that low swell of root and brick and rust you only notice once you're inside — looking down into the bowl. Or on the catwalk that starts nowhere, parting a curtain of morning glory with one hand to see better. The human height. The surveyor.

No. That doesn't hold. The green closes behind you without malice. To keep that height here you have to keep parting it, arm's length after arm's length, and you get tired before it does.

So lower. I find myself lower.

I think maybe I'd be the watchman. Because I keep listening for him — the stoker, the oiler, the barefoot caretaker who must be stuffing the hopper and changing the bulbs. Something must be raking. Thump-thump from the boiler house, slow in the molars first. Hiss. Plink-plink-plink of condensation falling leaf to leaf to drum to leaf. Warehouse-full-of-clocks loud.

I unlearn him. No one is raking. No one is stoking. The roots themselves stuff the heat chute, growing toward it as if it were sun.

Then maybe I think I'd be an animal, to make the loud make sense. A throat in the noise. But there are no animals. The seeps are perfect and empty, tea-colored water sighing up around the boot and then closing. No frog. No rat in the bread-soft brick. Warm air, empty air. Even insects have mostly resigned — only those few white moths, blind and silent, blundering lamp to lamp.

So if I stay — and the bathhouse warmth invites it, and the 50-cycle drone gets into the chest and lulls — I can't stay as a man walking, and I can't stay as a beast. The floor won't allow it. That is the first rule: the ground is not ground.

I find myself where the land sags lowest, just off-center. Not on the path of tiles leading into mud. A little to the side of the water tower, where its four thin waterfalls never stop falling from the split tank, and the pump underneath keeps lifting seep-water back up to fall again. Falling upward and then down. The thatch is thickest there. Rhizome braided over rhizome, thick as wrist, moss over that, seedlings in the moss. I stop trying to stand on it.

I lie down.

That is the correction the place makes. Vertical was a mistake. The light here has weight, pressed down through three, four, five ceilings of leaf, each one wetter than the last, chewed green by the time it reaches me. It lands on everything at once. It lands on me. Condensation beads on every underside — leaf, pipe, wire, my own forearm — and lets go. Arrhythmic weather that has nothing to do with sky. Plink on a philodendron shield. Plink on copper. Plink near my ear.

Lying there, I well up. Squeeze the thatch and it sighs. My weight does the squeezing. Tea-colored water fills the hollow of my back, my boots fill and close. Tendrils come out to test the edge of my sole. Not hungry, exactly. Curious. Tasting for salt and iron and wear. I have salt. I have iron in the blood, green on the tongue already from breathing steam.

From down here I can feel its breath before I hear it: the ten-foot flywheel in the boiler house making its revolution once, twice a minute. Thump. The piston sighs. The split pipe vents sideways and washes a whole wall of leaves white for a second. The leaves lean *into* the scald. Around the boiler they have learned the shape of heat and arch clear in a perfect halo. From where I am, I see the steam go up white and come down as distilled rain, and the big trumpet vines open only then, only for that pulse.

On. Off. On. Off.

Because at night — and dusk is coming green and early here — the work-lamps string through the mass begin to pulse. Every time the flywheel hits top-dead-center, a slow heartbeat you can see. One bulb has been completely grown around by a gourd, translucent when it lights. I am close enough to that one to see my own damp skin go amber and then dark, amber and then dark. I can feel in my chest what the leaves nearest the live wires feel: they are all turning their copper-veined undersides outward together, to catch the field. At the ceramic bells the damp air crackles blue. The leaning pylon wears its crown of white orchids and they hum faintly when the wind moves the wires.

I stay still long enough to watch with plant eyes, and stranger things happen:

Split a stem near my head and it bleeds metallic green — copper leached from the windings, carried now in sap. A fig has rooted *inside* a transformer casing twenty paces off and fruits out through the ventilation slots, each fig warm to the touch like a small heart. The flowers here have dreamed themselves into insulators, fuses, curled shavings of iron. Not to fool anyone. There is no one to fool. Just because after long enough, industry became morphology.

I taste of salt and wear. The thatch tastes me. Leaf to ash to leaf is a season long here, and I am faster than that and therefore brief. If I cut a path in the morning it sweats shut by evening, not to punish but because closing is what this place does. A room that seals. A fruit holding its seeds — boiler house, dynamo shed breathing warm oil, catwalk to nowhere — not using them, not worshipping them. Just having grown around the idea of industry until industry became another way to photosynthesize.

So what could I be, that it would tolerate inside itself besides green and iron?

Not the keeper. Not a beast.

Almost-not-animal.

I feel my mouth unlearn eating — there is nothing here properly to eat, only iron and green — and my ears unlearn the drone, which has become bed rather than sound. I get softer, whiter, blinder. Damp to the bone, leaf-prints on my skin. When I finally get up, I don't walk out over the rim. I blunder, silent, from pulse to pulse.

Find me at dusk under the sagging cable where it drips steadily from its lowest point. Find me low, wide, briefly held. I am one of those white moths bumping between the gourd-lamps, carrying dust from the insulator-flowers that dream of machines. No mouth. No ears. Just soft white punctuation in the slow sentence it keeps writing to itself: burn, lift, rain, grow, burn.
