You wouldn't find it if you weren't looking for shade, so that's how I come in.

Panting. Mouth open in thin air. Hand on hot stone. Looking — not for wonder, for anywhere out of the noon. The pavement goes to every horizon and then the ground gives under my boots, and I drop out of the wind.

At first I think I know where I'd be.

Up high, obviously. I want to be one of the kings — the copper throats. To own an island, a dwarf tree crown with white orchids, to scream and cut through the roar and slap the thin air hard enough to stay up. To burn that fast and be unmade by afternoon into a single feather. That sounds clean.

But I sit down too fast. Twenty steps up here and my heart is hammering in my ears. I can't afford to burn like that. I'm too heavy, too loud-breathing. The hummingbirds scream at me when I stand. They know I'm not one of them.

So then I think, lower. I'll be a citizen. One of the amber frogs in rows along the arch-base, shoulder to shoulder where the drip is steadiest. Throat puffing. Eyes like wet beads. There are too many of them to afford fear individually — what a relief that would be. To not hop when a shadow falls. To lay my whole future in a saucer no deeper than a teacup and trust the bowl to make more.

But I can't keep their time, not yet. Three pulses. Pause. Three pulses. Pause. My breathing is ragged, arrhythmic. I keep trying to count the frogs and losing count. I am too self-conscious to sit in a row.

I wave through a midge column without meaning to and it parts around my skin like water and closes behind me, same shape, same place. For a second I am inside the needle — gold-eyed, clear-winged, thousands rising and falling without breaking rank. They lean, all of them, five degrees toward the nearest black loop. Not toward the sun. Toward the tone coming up through the peat.

I could be that, I think. Never resting. Holding station by never resting. Riding the standing edge of the hum.

But my soles are too thick. I feel the hum through my boots as a low ache, not as direction. I don't know how to lean that precisely.

So I go lower. Under the leaves.

That's where I actually find myself, not where I thought. Not at the arches, not in the center where it's loudest and wettest. At the edge of an island, where the bare warm grit crunches and then, one step, gives — ankle-deep in moss that takes my weight and holds it.

I put my back against the collapsed side of the slab ring. Half in shade. Left foot on stone, right foot in sponge. The human-scale ruin. The place where someone else pegged a tent a long time ago and the peat still remembers the square. Where the chipped mug sits half-full of brown drip-water with a drowned fly in it. Where the solar cloth lies brittle as a leaf. I belong with the litter, the offerings the arches ignore.

I stop trying to watch the whole wheel turn and just let it happen around my stillness. Fly drowns in pitcher. Frog snaps fly. Beetle with a mirror back bulldozes past my boot and flashes heat away. A pale centipede threads the black underside of the cushion by my ankle, eating eggs. Sundews glitter a hand's width from my knee, each drop holding a tiny sun and a struggling midge.

And then the ripple.

A wave moving through the moss cushion with no wind. A tunnel pushing up from below, passing three feet from me, pausing. Underneath is the only mammal in the cup, grey-brown, long-nosed, feet almost hairless so it can feel vibration. It drinks drip-water. It eats centipedes. It lives its whole life without ever touching bare stone.

I know that animal. I am learning to be that animal.

I take my boots off. The grit is hot enough to burn my palm, but the moss is 100% humid and dark inside. I put my naked soles to it and feel what it feels: the deep tuned vibration coming up, three beats, pause. The crickets sawing down inside where I'll never see them. The frogs keeping time because it lets a male be heard through a hundred others. The dwarf tree above me leaning, very slightly, toward the cold black loop, though the light is straight overhead. Roots following condensation, yes, but also song.

I slow my breathing without deciding to. The midges resettle over me. The frogs don't hop. A jewel wasp, blue-black, thumbnail-big, cleans its antennae an inch from a frog and the frog watches and does nothing, drunk on sugar and plenty. A copper throat screams at me once and then decides I'm part of the tree.

When the daily cloud snags on the lip and dumps its twenty minutes vertical, hissing on hot rock, I don't move. Water runs off me. Rock-pocks fill. Tadpoles like commas wriggle in a saucer that by evening will be gone — eaten or evaporated. That's fine. The bowl makes more.

Then sun again. Steam rises. The arches cool and ping — a clear glass note, three of them slightly out of unison — and for one full second every insect goes silent at once. Total cut. As if listening.

In that second I hear my own heart, laboring in thin air, slipped without my permission into three-beat time.

That is what I would be. Not a list. Not a king, not a citizen. The shy one underneath. The footprint that the moss holds for a full minute before slowly exhaling back. The mammal with naked feet, feeling more than seeing, living island to island so I never have to touch the dry.

I climb out eventually, because I have to. Up those six or seven meters into the wind and the huge flat indifference, lichen black and orange and pale green mapping the stones. Two steps up and the roar is gone, wiped clean, replaced by that long thin hiss over granite.

For five or six steps on hot stone my heart is still keeping bowl-time before the dry air takes it out of me.
