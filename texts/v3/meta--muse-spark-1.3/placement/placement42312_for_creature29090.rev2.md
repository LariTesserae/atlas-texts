I thought, coming down, I would be high up.

On the rim the wind is clean and cuts straight through. I thought I would be the raven — tilted, black cut in grey-blue, crossing over without calling because calling is useless here. Just going over, waiting. That felt right for a while. To see the whole saucer at once, the far rim pale in mist, the stations as small hot dots in green-black.

Then the road tilts down and the roar gets into my chest and I couldn't hold that height.

Halfway down, with the peat banks sharp on either side and frost needling out white, I thought I would be one of the keepers. That would be the human thing to be. To have a station. To put my palm on a pipe and know by the tremor if the screw below is laboring. To learn the economy of it — tap on the shoulder, flat hand for stop, jerk of the chin toward the trestle where frost is building wrong. To sit in the back corner behind the engine bulkhead with tea going cold while the floor hums through my teeth.

I tried that on for a while. The quilted coveralls gone dark at the knees. The socks hung over a rail, baked on one side and damp on the other. The logbook cockled by condensation: *No.3 running hot. Cut south footing.*

But my feet went white and ridged just thinking about it. And I don't shout well enough to live inside shouting. The men hear the bowl for a week after they leave, in a quiet kitchen far away. I didn't want a phantom bowl. I wanted to stay.

So I went lower.

I thought I would be the fox. That was closer. Stitching straight from hummock to hummock, sharp in morning frost, soft by noon. Living up near the lip where the noise thins, foraying down in the indigo light to drink from the black mirrors. Learning the boardwalk line, learning the warmth around the exhausts. That felt almost right — shy of the noise, scarce, moving between.

But even the fox is too fast. Too warm. It comes and goes.

Under a lifted board I found it — what I am.

Not the board. Under it. Where it's dark even at what passes for morning, and the air shakes all the time, and it doesn't matter.

I am green, and then more particularly green.

At first just a smear on concrete. The south footing of one of the stations — it doesn't matter which, they are all the same green block shuddering, all the same rust bleeding from every bolt. On the side away from the wind, where the pipe sweats and drips and the drip never quite freezes. Where the concrete lip stays just warm enough from inside.

I am the tendrils over that lip.

If you kneel — and a man does, once a week, with a strimmer — you would see me more itself: olive tips and rust-red stems, pressed flat by cold, beaded every morning. And most of the day is morning. Each tip holds a drop. The drop holds sky. Under the living tips, half-rotted stems going brown, going black, going down wet a foot, two feet, into tannin water smelling faintly sweet, like iron.

I do not hear the roar. To me it is weather. A constant shaking of the air that beads water off the wires and settles dust. The big engine near me breathes heat sideways and leans my north side soft while my south side stays hard with frost. That is all.

Once a week a shadow falls — quilted, oil at the thighs, green smears at the elbows where it has knelt in others of me — and there is a brief whine higher than the fans, and a pale fresh cut. I am shaved back to concrete.

For a day I am pale. By the end of the week I am green again.

You can't resent millimeters a year. I don't.

Around me the hollows want to join up. Pool to pool, dinner-plate mirrors of sky trapped in peat, a pressure seeking level sideways through the sponge. The pumps pull and the level holds for another day. I feel it under me, that slow lateral give. Water squeezing up around boot soles. Ice needles lifting tiny caps of peat overnight and collapsing at midday into glitter.

It is slow work. Everything here is slow work.

The men think they are keeping the place dry. They are only keeping it paused. The basin wants to be a bog. It *is* a bog, interrupted. I am the interruption returning.

If the machines stopped for a season, for a year, I would not hurry. I would just not be cut. I would go over the first rung of the ladder, into the cable tray where my tiny hands grip best, over road and pipe and platform without a seam. The frost would jack the pilings crooked to help me.

You will find me there, if you look low. Not out in the almost-nothing between stations where distance fools you, but right up against the heat. Knee-high, waist-high when the fog comes up and the floodlights make halos and the windows go amber and the whole basin hums like a held note.

Cold and soaked, between machines that will not be turned off, under a sky that will not get bright.

Cut back. Returning.
