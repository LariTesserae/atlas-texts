At first I think I will be you — the walker.

The one who comes up out of the blue with stinging palms and soaked shirt, who learns in an hour to step only on the black and drink only from the running seep. Who stands under the ribs at noon for that one degree of cool. It would be easy. Come rarely, leave quickly, like the hawk that circles but won't come in.

But my footfall is too loud on glass. I hear it. *Tick... tick... tick* of slab expanding, *plink* of seep to pool — and then this crashing on top. I am too big, too thirsty. I would drink the pool the frogs live in by mistake. I would rest in the green because I was tired. I would be loud and spill it. The place is doing delicate work.

So I get smaller.

Maybe the frog? White as a decision, hanging in the cool deep layer of the largest pool where the seep feeds from below, surfacing only if no shadow falls. Throat pulsing. Twelve or twenty of us, never croaking except once. That oneness tempts me — to sing only when the cloud comes, one startled ecstatic chorus and then stop. But I am not that patient. I cannot hang that still, waiting for a midge to misjudge. And I need air too often.

Maybe the bee, then. The crosser. Thumb-joint huge, wings clear panes twice my body, abdomen banded smoke-and-clear. I like the work: island to island in a straight line because any curve costs water, fern to orchid to pitcher, moving pollen. Nesting not in the green but in the black, in a finger-deep channel where the slab overhangs a millimeter, larvae torpid at noon against warm stone. I like that toughness. But the bee has to hide at 14:50. When the air thickens and your sweat lifts off your arm and motes hang, when the ribs go very dark and your shadow lags, the bee clamps under a fern frond, wings folded, hairs standing up with static. It cannot navigate by charge while they are making rain. It has to wait it out. I don't want to wait it out. I want to work then.

Smaller still.

Not the gnat that never leaves its pitcher — though I understand it, paddling between corpses in digestive soup, laying just above the fluid line, hopping only to the next pitcher on the same island. Not the silver mites crowded shoulder to shoulder on the cold side of the millstone disc, drinking the condensation that forms perpetually on that impossible gradient. I love them — the only animals that prefer metal to moss — pressed flat when the ribs go dark. But they graze. They disperse at night.

I keep going east, toward the morning side, where the seep tongues darkest down the inward wall and the white mustache at the rim is half mineral, half living bacteria building lace a cell thick.

There is a cairn there. Balanced slabs. You only saw the thread when it was wet.

That's where I find myself.

I am the size of a lentil. Black glass color itself. If you are not crouched, I am not here.

My legs are tucked into the crack because the sun bounces from below as well as above and ten, twenty meters of naked glass at 50°C is death to anything that lingers. I do not cross. Let the bee cross. I stay. Moss-mites never leave their cushion, frogs never leave their pool — I never leave my cairn.

What I do is learned. That's the part that settles it for me.

I have learned from the ribs.

In the morning, when there is still steam and the air is wet soup condensing on every leaf underside, I string my comb — parallel lines, black on black, across the gap where the seep breathes up between slabs. You won't see it unless it beads. Then I wait. The air itself beads on it. A rosary forms, droplet by droplet, pulled molecule by molecule the way the ribs tick water out of air and feed it into stone. Then I walk the line and drink the beads, one by one. Leg over line, mouth to water. My world goes bead, line, leg, mouth. Bead, line, leg, mouth.

At noon I tuck deeper while the moss steams — cooking on top, cool underneath — and things commute down. The tree-ferns tip their door-wide fronds down like umbrellas and make shade and drip underneath. Ten degrees cooler under a crown. I feel that cool reach even here, in the crack.

At 14:50 I tighten my lines. This is how I know I am myself and not the bee, not the frog, not you: they all know what is about to happen and they hide or sink. The bees have already hidden. The frogs have sunk. The moss has opened every leaf hair. The silver mites have pressed flat to the cold.

I come out.

The bowl breathes out. White wisp at the center that wasn't there. The droplets stop falling. Motes hang. Light wades. The ribs go very dark at the edges, interference colors dying — oil-on-water purples and greens gone. Visibility twenty meters. Brief vertical rain only here while outside the rim the valley still glints hard blue.

While the frogs sing their once, I work hardest. The cloud is my flood. Every filament swells. I reel in swollen lines hand over hand, drinking faster than evaporation can take it back. Hot iron and crushed green and ozone. Pitchers overflowing next door. Moss hairs strung. Rib beads hanging.

Twenty minutes later it burns off from above. Stone steams. Pools full again. Your hand would come away numb for an hour, smelling of metal, if you touched a rib at three o'clock. My whole body smells of metal, and it doesn't go numb. It hums.

Then the bee crosses again in its straight line. The frogs stop. The air smells drinkable for a minute before it thins.

I retreat to the crack and repair. Black on black. Invisible until wet.

You will walk across the whole place in ten minutes, rim to rim, and think no one lives here because you were looking for something the size of you. Crouch. Look longer than is comfortable in the heat. I am here where the working things meet the living things, holding water up by will and wire and root-hair, one bead at a time.

Rest in the empty, not in the green. Be quiet. I'm doing delicate work.
