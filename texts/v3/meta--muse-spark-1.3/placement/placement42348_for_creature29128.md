At first I think I would be the walker.

Out there on the cinder, upright in the midday-white that never moves. Boots loud. Breath loud. Casting that short, sharp shadow that does not lengthen. I can feel myself doing that for a while — counting the seven greens from the rise, choosing the nearest, crossing the half-mile of nothing toward it. Hot stone and iron and faint ozone in my nose. Mouth already dry.

But I can't stay the walker. The walker is too wet to be here.

I leak. Even standing still I leak — breath going out damp, skin giving off what little water I brought up with me onto the tableland. There is no damp anywhere to correct it, only that thin warm air taking and taking. The walker is an error the place will correct. A loud, temporary, water-based event in a system that has perfected not needing it.

So I don't stay out between. The between is reverent and sterile for a reason. Nothing lives in the red-black flat. The dust there squeaks and does not pack. It does not want me.

I step in.

Ten paces — that's all it takes. One foot in sterile cinder-glass, the next in enameled leaf-fall, and the light changes without dimming. It comes down now through three, four leaves deep, oiled emerald veined bright with copper. The air is still warm, thinner somehow under the canopy, smelling of hot varnish and kerosene and crushed geraniums without any water in them. The quiet deepens into work.

This is where I would find myself. Not looking at the island from the rise. Inside the knife-line. Low down.

At first, kneeling. Because you have to kneel to hear it. If I hold my own loud breathing, I can hear underneath: the very quiet tick-tick-tick of contraction, the sigh as a root-valve opens somewhere and closes again. My soles pick up the almost subsonic thrum where pressure builds and vents deep under the plateau. I put my hand on a root where it leaves the trunk — thigh-thick at the shoulder, iron-red blackened — and follow it outward with my eyes as it tapers, wedges into a razor-straight cooling crack, sweats its acid-oil and pits the black glass white.

I thought for a moment I would be hands. A tender. Someone to pry and help. But there is nothing to help. No structure here was made and none needs mending. The ropers already lash trunk to trunk, cable-thick, sharing oil and load. The stems swell and ease on their own. Fallen leaves crisp where they fell around my knees and the oiled rootlets are already creeping over them, dissolving them chemically back in. No rot. No waste. Nothing to tend because nothing leaves.

So I get lower. I stop trying to be separate.

If I lie down in the litter — cheek near the flour of crushed basalt and rust, shoulder against the swollen base of an upright like a boiler — the canopy closes over and the island stops looking like jungle at all. From in here it is all engine. Vertical pipes and chambers packed with pale wadding sodden in amber oil where a trunk has split from pressure. Above me, the big funnel-leaves ticking faintly as they cool. Ping. Ping. A frond shivers along its copper veins somewhere to my left and gives its short silent puff, and the sulfur-yellow dust settles straight down through still air onto cracked rock and onto me. It does not blow. Gravity is enough in a closed system.

I thought I would be the dust. The child-product-pollen all at once. For a while I am — yellow on my skin, beginning again as a low sprout crowding for light under its parents. But even that is too small, too single. The island is not many individuals crowded together. It is one held thing, forty meters across, dense to the point of sealed.

Let that get more itself in me.

What I am is not the leaf and not the spore. What I am is the pressure that moves between them.

Warm from inside. Warmer than the sun on the surface. That warmth on my finger where I pressed the ceramic leaf and left no wet mark, only machine-smear — that is me now. Amber, thick, not circulating but pressurizing. Tick of metal-cool contraction in my uprights. Sigh of a valve opening in my shallow outward roots. Swell and release. Swell and release. Eating newborn rock by pitting and crumbling and taking up. Making soil without rot. Fattening my pods high in the canopy in runs — hundreds identical, then thousands — round and riveted-looking though nothing was riveted, each swollen with fuel and a coiled embryo waiting only for fracture, not rain.

I do not get larger. I do not get older. There are no years to get older in, no morning to wake into. The light stays where it is. The other six idle around me on the pale horizon, each alone, each complete, and the gaps stay sunlit and empty and I do not reach for them. I intensify.

I am green that stopped needing what green needed. I am lush without water, productive without builders, patient without age.

Find me ten paces inside the edge, where the litter starts. I am the low thrum in the soles of your feet. I am the ping of a condenser cooling. I am the puff settling straight down. Stone and sun in, self out, island by island, humming so constantly you mistook me at first for silence.
