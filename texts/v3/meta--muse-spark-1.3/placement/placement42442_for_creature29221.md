At first I thought I'd be the walker.

Coming down off the levee lip into it, shirt going wet in a minute, not with sweat, with everything condensing on me. Staying on the boards because the green between is floating mat over hot mud that will take a boot. Ducking under the tunnel of leaf and cable where water ticks down on my neck. Listening for the stack's thin rising note ten seconds before, turning my back, covering my ears, shutting my mouth. Learning fast.

I thought I'd be the one with the notebook that would go soft and unreadable in an hour, shouting in the lulls to no one, drinking from the raised cistern by the dynamo hall because it's the only sweet water. The only animal the bowl permits, and only briefly.

But that doesn't hold. An animal can't stay. The bowl notices animals the way a body notices a splinter — not with anger, with heat and wet and copper and shock. The THUMP — THUMP — THUMP of the drop-forge gets into the sternum. The eleven-minute white roar strips leaf and rattles rust loose and would scald my lungs if I breathed it. My breath is too loud in the gaps. The absence under the roar — no call, no buzz, no splash — pushes my breathing forward until I hear only myself. I would leave, or lie down.

So not the walker. Let that go.

Then I thought, maybe I'd be the watcher on the pole. One of the sodium lamps that still comes on at the thinning of the brown light, buzzing orange through the fog, lighting empty water and huge leaves from below, reflected double in the canal. Burning for no moths. That felt closer — to burn, to hum, to be reflected and not visited. But a lamp is still waiting. It still expects eyes.

Let that go too.

Find me lower. Find me where wire and vine stop being different things.

Between the boiler house and the dynamo hall there is a run of pipe big as a man's torso on a rusted trestle, lagged in asbestos and vine, weeping constantly though it has not rained in days. Above it sags a power cable, and over both, morning glory and bitter melon have made a second, broken canopy. You walk for a stretch in green shadow. The iron handrail weeps onto your palm. The underside of the pipe drips on your neck. The transformers a little further on hum the 60-cycle nerve-hum, insulators furred with moss, capacious and warm.

I would be there. Not on the duckboard. In the tangle over it.

Specifically — and this is becoming more itself now — I would be a length of that vine.

A bitter melon stem, thick as a thumb where it has lived years, green where it is young and corked and brown where it has gripped. I have learned masonry and metallurgy both. I use the cable as trellis and the cable uses me as insulation. You can't tell which is holding which anymore. My tendrils have found the drive belt where it runs below, left slack years ago, and furred it and pulled it true again. Literally. I tighten. When the flywheel throws it, I am the groove-memory that lets it slap and find its own way home. I do not flinch inside the thump. To live here is to be able to live inside that thump without flinching, and I can.

I drink what no animal could drink. The runoff is 40, 45 degrees, copper-green, tasting of pennies and hot stone. It quickens me. My leaves are huge and soft in the dim eternal evening under the lid, untorn by chewing except by steam — every eleven minutes the stack screams and lets go and shreds us like cloth. I hear the leaves rip. I put out another. It doesn't matter.

At brown noon I flower. Small, furled trumpets that open unvisited. No bees under the lid, no moths, no beetle to finish the edge. I close unvisited. I set seed by myself or not at all. It doesn't matter. I don't need to travel. I just need to continue.

Below me the walkers pass, briefly. I tick water down onto their necks. I condense on their shirts. Above me the lid holds — low cloud and smoke and vapor off the marsh and transpiration off a thousand leaves like me exhaling to make more lid to hold more heat to move more water. The canal gut moves hot green-black fast between concrete banks. The boilers breathe. The iron bacteria bloom orange on the banks, oxidizing what the pipes bleed. The copper ferns feather out of mortar cracks. The water lettuce steams on the north pond.

I am not waiting for pollinators. I am not waiting for eyes at windows, for rats on the boards, for herons. I have given up fruit the way animals need it. Daughter to mother to grandmother, tendril to cable to vine.

If you walk that stretch, look up. Not for a face. For the place where the light goes most green, where the hum is warmest in the stem, where a flower is opening in the dim with no one to see it and opening anyway. That's where I am.
