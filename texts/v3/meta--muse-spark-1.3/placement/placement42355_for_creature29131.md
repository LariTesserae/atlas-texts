Down. And then down.

At first I think I would be the visitor — hanging motionless in the 11-degree cool while it works into the joints of my fingers and stays there, listening to my own breathing loud in my hood, watching my bubbles join the rush upward to burst under the ceiling in that trapped silver lake. I would learn, I think, where not to put my hand. Not on the singing bearing housing. I would learn which grate, for about ten minutes at noon, drops a solid pillar straight down to the checkerboard.

But I breathe too loud. I can't stay. The hall re-knits around me, without me.

So if I didn't have to breathe — if I could live here the way you live here, which is not the way I live somewhere —

Maybe silver. Maybe I would hang in the emptiness between machines because emptiness is safest. Engine One school, tight and nervous east of the crane rail. I try it on: a hundred lateral lines listening as one body because one fish can't hear a pike coming in all that drone and hiss and tick-tick-tick of loose chain and fizz of rust pinhole. Turning all at once across the jaundiced lamp-light so my side flashes like a signal mirror.

No. Too exposed. Too flashing. I scatter differently. I don't want to live as food that has learned to be beautiful about it.

Maybe, then, stillness that hunts. The flanged mouth in the vertical pipe thick as a tree trunk. An eye and a jawline resolving out of dark. Two feet of muscle the color of wet concrete ribboning out to hold station in the hiss. The big female in Engine Two's feed pipe who has lived in the same doorway so long her body has polished the rust around it smooth. She withdraws an inch when I come too close. She will not be evicted.

I like doorways. I like watching. But I fidget. I would not be content to let the hall hunt for me, to strike only when the relief valve blows its *whoomph* and the fry tumble.

Maybe the warden under the crane rail. As long as your arm, mottled primer-gray and rust-brown, gills working slow and steady, utterly still except for that. Cruising only at the shift changes, when the flywheel's gyre yields to the sump pump's clunk, when the light shaft moves a few feet across the floor. A drift, a correction, a drift. Then a dart that empties the water.

No. I don't want to be the thing they signal about. I don't want to be the fear that makes the mussels clamp all at once in an acre-wide click.

Closer, then: the pensioners by the warm-water vent. Two, sometimes three fat codlings, blind-eyed with milky cataracts from dark and sudden heat. Hovering nose into the current, pectorals fanning just enough to hold, mouths opening and closing with bovine calm. Skin loose, scarred by barnacle scrapes, furred at the fins with dark sponge. Riding the blast like old men in a hot bath, eyes closed, waiting where it is always a little summer.

I understand that. Waiting where a transformer still leaks heat. Scarred. Patient. Letting stunned fry come to me. Closer — but I still move too much. I still pick.

Ah. There. That picking, sideways through the ooze.

I would be a crab. Not visiting from shore — generations from shore. Green and knuckled, improbably far, but owning the floor.

Find me first by my trails: dotted, deliberate, twin picks across the bottle-green and off-white checker that the gyre erases by evening. I move through powdered concrete and rust scale and tile grit and mussel shit, a few centimeters thick and soft, and I know the traffic. Stay still and you'll see me pick my way among the frost — the white anemones packed tight in the grout, thumbnail crowns working ceaselessly — without triggering a single sting. That is learned. That is living in habits, not on top of them.

Find me in the morning piled on top of Switch Cabinet Three with the others, antennae waving in the transformer hum. The top is wiped bald of mussels, not by divers but by us, day after day, year after year, commuting across it because it's high and safe and warm. You hover there and you feel it too: 13 degrees instead of 12, a thin envelope where your finger joints ache less. We know. We wait for our joints to loosen.

Find me at 11:50 at the edge of where the noon pillar will land, legs tucked, watching the babies — this year's fry no longer than your fingernail, clinging to the filament algae streamers on the upper pipe edges — gather in it like dust in a cathedral window. I will not hunt them. I am the cleaner and the polisher.

Find me, most truly, in the half-second before.

When the valve is about to blow, the young freeze late. We old ones freeze early. Utterly. Legs mid-step. Because we feel it first in our legs through the tiles, before the *whoomph*, before the column of bubbles and hot water belches for the grates and rattles the loose sheet metal and the whole ecology spasms — fry scatter, eels strike, the pike darts, anemones retract to pimples. I freeze. I am a stone among stones with rounded corners. Then, minute by minute, I open again, like the mussels gape and close, gape and close in their city with streets.

That is where I would be: low, on the floor, in the blue-green dark just beyond the six or seven feet the caged bulkhead lamps reach, but knowing exactly where those pools of jaundiced orange are. Very old, very small. Not needing light, barely needing green — just enough sponge in the corners, just enough algae banner to anchor someone else's young. Running on in the dark below the world, in the deep electric drone, with the flywheel turning once every twenty seconds and throwing its bearded wobble through the floor tiles into my feet.

You could swim past three times and think I am only part of the building. That would be correct.
