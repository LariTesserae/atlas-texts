At first I don't know where to put myself. The air decides for me.

It gets in the chest before the eyes get anything to do — that thin, sun-hot air that doesn't finish. I am breathing and breathing and not getting to the end of a breath. My blood is ticking in my ears. So I stop trying to look far. Distance lies here anyway. That town etched razor-sharp on the scarp, every cable-mast standing out — I start walking toward it, because it looks like the next ridge.

It isn't. It's a day and a half. I learn that with my legs.

I thought, while I was still walking and still had air to waste on thinking fast: I will be high. I will be a sail.

Three meters of amber translucence, circling without a wingbeat for hours, mouth open to dust. To never land except on warm slag to lay eggs. To feel a storm three days before it comes as a prickling along the blue tracery of my wings. That sounded like me — patient, far-seeing, untouched.

But up there the world is only glare and hunger. The sails don't see towns, they sieve them. They eat our ash. They circle the same thermal for a decade and call that a life. I tried that height in my head for a half a morning and got lonely. I need weight. I need something to rub against.

So then I thought: a sifter.

Low and barrel-bodied, armored in dirty ivory, plowing the pan with my blunt snout in a herd of fifty, blinking dusty eyelids in unison. Rasping up varnish, leaving clean crystal pellets glittering behind me like glass droppings. Sixty years. Digging a scrape in the afternoon and piling twenty bodies high while plates tick as we cool and the dry snow patters down hissing on hot iron. Utterly indifferent. Moving at the speed this place actually runs at.

I wanted that indifference for about an hour, lying belly-down where the wind-ridge broke and watching them pass. They let me watch. They didn't alter course. A dusty eyelid closed, opened. A snout lifted, rasped. The smell when one scuffed a stone was faintly metallic, like bread, like blood. And I understood I am not indifferent yet. I am still new enough to want to be useful. A sifter doesn't tend. It just is. I need to tend.

Down in the trough, out of the wind, the town resolved.

Not empty at noon, the way I'd thought. Full of stillness. A figure on a flat roof under a veil, perfectly still. Two more shoulder to shoulder in the shadow of a wind-wall because you have to sit close here to be heard. A child belly-down on warm slag, watching me come the way the sifters had let me pass.

No one hurried to greet me. Hurrying is for people who still have air.

My chest was already broadening, or I imagined it. I tried to talk fast, newcomer-fast, and went grey around the mouth and had to sit. The old hands smiled behind their veils and waited. They have time. A firing cycle is eleven days. A kiln has been warm fourteen years. What is a minute?

I sat. That was my first office.

Shoulder touching shoulder, veil to veil, I learned to speak low because the thin air keeps your voice for yourself. I learned the pauses are not thought but lung. Lift, set, step back to breathe. A mason showed me. Lift again. I learned lamp-talk before I learned names — the arc lamps on the cable-masts burning too bright all night, blinked with shutters. Children flashing tin mirrors roof to roof, patient as sails. Two fingers on the forearm for slack. Palm flat for hold, among the engine crews where the felt-hum sits in the teeth.

I learned not to dig for water. I learned sweat vanishes before it beads. I learned salt rime in the eyebrows, oil-darkened lips, chin sunburnt from below because the pan throws it back. I learned moth-cakes toasted with salt and pretended not to mind. I learned piss distilled, breath caught in condenser cloths, gypsum baked for the ghost of water locked in its crystal.

And slowly — slower than I live, the way varnish grows — I found the walk that was mine.

Not the kiln. Not the cable drum. Not the roof with ruined eyes reading sails, though I love those boys lying on their backs calling *high and tight, dust-front coming* and being right three days early.

Mine is behind.

Behind the herd, with the old woman too breath-short for kiln work. She walks the same herd for decades. She doesn't drive them. She walks at the speed of stone learning to breathe, and they allow her to pick up the pellets behind them, to pull beetles from a sore foot, to sit among the pile at noon.

I am the silent grandchild. The extra pair of hands that learns which boulder was scraped and which ridge left fallow. *Do not scrape a stone clean unless you need to.* I got slapped seriously as prayer when I broke crust with my boot just to smell it. It grows back slower than you live. I remember now. This dark leather skin, brown-black-orange on the sunward faces — that is the only field. I tend it.

You would find me, if you came over the wind-ridge after two days of empty pan, out past the halo of dead machines where the walking excavator kneels with its bucket down as if grazing. The sifters rub their plates bright against its steel shins. The furnace-moths winter in its shadow. In the track, children play knucklebones.

I am in the pile, or just behind it. Veil wound twice, eyes to slits, mouth pale underneath. My resting pulse is down to forty. I stoop, pick up a warm crystal pellet, drop it in the sack without breaking stride. Twenty bodies ticking as they cool. Dry snow falling at noon, white crystals hissing, vanishing, leaving no wet.

When you stand still as you were taught and let the sifters pass, the old woman will nod once behind her veil, not from friendliness but from recognition.

I will nod with her.
