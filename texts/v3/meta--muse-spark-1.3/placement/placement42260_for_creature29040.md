Down. I go down still wet.

That's the first thing. On the stairs my mouth still has spit in it, my shirt still holds a little outside sweat that can run, my ears still pop. I think I will get to stay like that — a visitor, a witness. I think I'll find myself by the control kiosk with its needles trembling at mid-range, trying to read dials, trying not to touch anything. Keeping my hands in my pockets while the moths tick my face. A human shape with a lunch pail history, looking for the arrows on the floor.

It doesn't hold. Nothing holds wet here.

Halfway across the room my upper lip salts. My tongue goes tacky against my teeth. The air takes it out of me — breath-warm, furnace-warm, ozone and hot iron and musk — and lifts it to chest height where the dust hangs and never settles. I breathe chitin and salt-motes and shed wings. My teeth start to ache with it.

So I can't stay by the kiosk. I can't stay separate. Warmth is ground here, and I am warm too.

At first I try on different places to be.

I think maybe I would be a lamp-moth, because I know what it is to orbit. To find one dim amber bulb crusted with salt and go around it and around it because it is the only light. But I eat too much. I have too much appetite for circling to be my whole life. And I don't want to burn. Every minute a few touch too close and crisp and fall still beating on the hot housing below — I can hear it — and I don't want that to be me, even if the ticking behind the pipes keeps pace and replaces me.

I think maybe I would be a rat, because I am mammalian, because I like to sleep in piles. In the cable trays where the black cables sag and hum, ten and fifteen together, pink sides heaving, borrowing heat from the current and giving it back. Moving by whisker and by heat. But my eyes haven't sealed yet. I still want to see. Blind as thumbs would be a mercy here — eyes only dry out — but I'm not ready for that mercy. I still lift my nose to find salt.

I think maybe I would be one of the human afterthoughts. The EXIT sign buzzing red over a door that opens onto solid rock. Still signaling, still lit, still useless. Or the single boot. Or the time-clock with no cards. That feels honest for a while — everything works and nothing produces, the pumps pump nothing to nowhere, the belt delivers dust to dust — I know that shift. I have worked it. But those things are too still, too alone. This room is furred. You cannot move without brushing life.

Then my hand gets near a pump housing and the pale goes quiet in a wave.

The piston pumps. Those big body-warm blocks going *whump whump whump*, lifting only air and dust and keeping on lifting it. Their housings are not pale metal. They are pale with crickets.

That's where I find myself. Not at the wall where you can see all the way around. Not under the fans taller than a man turning the same hot air around and around. In the middle press, third seam from the left where the heat leaks out most.

Flank to flank.

I would be a furnace-cricket.

Let that become more itself: my color goes lard-and-salt, translucent at the joints. My eyes go because there is nothing to see here that heat and vibration don't tell better. My antennae come out twice as long as my body and never stop combing the air for dust. Because that is what there is to eat. Dust. The orange scab of rust-lichen off the floor plates. The paper wings the moths drop. Each other's shed legs. Each other, whole, when one stops singing.

And I sing. Not in bursts. Not in night and day. Leg on wing, high metallic sawing without pause, riding on top of the pump-thump. That is the part I recognize most in myself — the held chord in the sternum that never peaks, never drops, never changes tempo. The thought that polishes the same belt brighter but never thinner. The worry that orbits the same bulb. The work that keeps the current flowing by needing it.

When a warm wrist comes near, I go quiet for a half-second with all the others, then come back louder, and step onto it. Because you are warm too.

I eat, I sing, I am eaten. The rats snatch and leave my antennae. The beetles take the rest. The lichen eats the dust of me. Somewhere behind the pipes something else is already ticking to replace me at exactly the rate I burn out. A closed mouth eating itself, warm and dry and perfect.

If I come back in fifty years, with dust still in my lungs and ringing still in my ears, the same warm air will be making the same loop around this small, dense, buried room. And on the same pump housing, on the same seam, the same cricket will be singing.
