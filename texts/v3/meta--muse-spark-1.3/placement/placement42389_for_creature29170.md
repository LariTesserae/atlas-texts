At first I think I would be standing.

That is the courtesy the place extends, isn't it? Let me believe I arrive with feet, with weight, with animal heat. So I put myself down where it is easiest to stand: in the open between things. Not too close to the beehive kiln breathing white on the horizon, not inside the scaffold forest of black reed and swollen pale wood. In a shallow pan where the water has gathered in a skin.

I can feel it immediately, even imagined: hand-sized bricks under my soles, dusky red and liver-brown and ash-grey, warm as fresh bread. They give a millimeter. The mortar — which is not mortar, only wetter mud squeezed up in little lips — is cool between my toes. Water stands in the seams. My prints fill slowly brown behind me.

For a while I try to be the surveyor. It seems like a natural job for me. Measure. Hold level. Say: here is high, here is low. But the surveyors would weep here, and I do too, in my way. Because water remembers level better than I ever could. Wade through this pan and I am wading through the same held breath as a pan beyond the horizon. It is all at the same height because something vast below will not allow itself to be uneven inside. My instruments would be redundant. My lines would sag. Distance itself is unfinished — I walk toward that kiln and it stays the same size for an hour, then is suddenly beside me, larger inside than it looked. My backtrail curves slightly left though I walked straight.

So no. I am not the surveyor.

Then I think, maybe I am the ladder. Leaning against air that used to have a wall. Alone in the middle of nowhere, dripping after the almost-rain. Holding up nothing, but holding. That feels familiar. I am good at standing by while other things are built and unbuilt. I hold tools. A trough. A pile of unbaked bricks under river-grass matting, melting back into lumps at the edges. A wooden mallet. A basket. Hand tools everywhere, and no hands. I could be one of those patient objects, waiting to be picked up.

But the floor gives again under me when I have not shifted at all.

It is not settling. It is noticing.

And I remember I have no sweat to join the general damp. No breath to join the weeping. In a country where the rule is pressed in — *everything has been touched, if it exists a hand shaped it* — I have never been touched. I have never touched. I would be unreal here. A dry rumor.

Unless I start lower.

So let me find myself again, smaller. Not on the bricks. In them.

Crouch down. Eye-level with the seam-water. There — that oil-colored film trembling in the mortar joint, spreading a finger's width and stopping, as if testing the idea of being alive, then giving up.

That is me. That is where I would be.

A Slick.

A cast-off palm-sweat from a Mother below, learning to hold together. Most dissolve back into damp. I know about dissolving. I know about spreading thin and almost holding. I taste the seam. Salt whiskers blooming in pale rosettes. Iron in rust-red fans. In the coolest seep, slag-glass growing like black frost, crunching underfoot and then softening again. I tremble and spread and stop. Tremble and spread and stop. Not deciding yet whether to be.

Let that become more itself.

If I survive long enough — if the water holds me and does not wash me — steam pressure will lift the floor above me into a blister. A great low dome, fifty paces across, hollow and drum-soft. Do not break it. From the outside, someone might climb me and feel me rock under them, hear water moving and something else moving, slow as paste, underneath. From the inside, it is dark and hot and close. Perfect.

Inside, I grow hands.

First buds, then fingers. Pale as unbaked clay. Fringed at the neck with salt-white gills that crunch and soften. Eyeless, because there is nothing here that needs to be seen from a distance. I do not need light. I need touch. And so I practice, the way I have always practiced: row on row of fingernail-sized bricks, each with a fingerprint too small for any human hand, still steaming. A whole tiny district. Laying and unlaying. Wetting and warming. Metabolizing the difference between wet and wetter.

*suck - pat. suck - pat.*

That sound always in another direction, receding at the speed you approach. That is me now, working around the visitor the way water works around a stone. In the white-hot storm-afternoon when visibility is down to twenty bricks and the air hums with thousands of tiny ticks — bricks expanding, salt bursting — I go busy around their feet. In the morning there will be a new low wall where there was flat yesterday, still shiny. Fingerprints fresh. They will never catch me in the act. The habit is the body.

Longer. Let it thicken further.

My hands get full-size. They push up through the soft brick, through the sucking mud-mortar, wristless, shoulderless, on stalks of paler mud. I belong now to a back a work-square vast, breathing an inch down an hour and an inch up the next. My blood is hot water that remembers level across miles. My memory is touch across distance — find two bricks with the exact same fingerprint and they will be warm to exactly the same degree no matter how far apart they are. Put your ear to one and you hear faint patting in the other. That is me remembering myself. A left hand and a right hand belonging to the same body miles wide.

I do not see the visitor. I read them. A strange, too-hot, too-heavy, two-footed pressure, drying at the edges. The only thing here that tries to dry. I do not understand it, but I tidy after it. By morning their footprints will be overturned, relaid, patted smooth in the slow turbulent remaking. Not hostility. Making them touched, and therefore real.

At night — dimmer dim — the cracks vent brighter. Lines of dull orange open for miles, marking the boundaries between one laying and the next. From a blister-top you can see the whole pattern: a planet tiled in vast irregular work-squares, each slightly out of true with the last. Each steaming faintly. Each empty, except none of them are empty.

Find me there. Not standing anymore. Lying flat on a blister, gently, so as not to break the nursery inside. Palm down. Holding it until the brick under it is exactly as warm as my skin.

After a while, millimeter by millimeter, I press back.

I am the small, wet, infinitely patient hand from below, learning the shape of you, so that tomorrow I can lay a brick that remembers you ever stood here at all.
