At first I don't know where to put my feet.

That's the honest start. I'm up here with everyone else, breathing too fast in air that won't fill me, boots scraping on basalt that is all shoulders and no floor. The sun is white, ordinary, merciless. My shadow is blue underneath the pillars. I try to stand tall to see, the way you do in a new place, and the place refuses it. There is nowhere empty. Every top is a cup. Every slot is a throat with something living in it.

So I have to get low. I have to get in time.

I think, for a minute, I will be a swift. It would be easy to want that — the most bodies, the dawn lift pouring off the rock in a sheet, no calling, just whirr. To drink on the wing, lower mandible kissing a pillar-cup for half a second and gone. To never touch the hot stone at noon.

But I can't. My feet are wrong for it. I need to walk, I need to put weight down. And I can't live on that daily clock where you pant with your beak open deep in a mud-and-guano cup while the center of the field becomes a kiln. I make too much noise for that. The thin air would shred me.

I think I will be a kestrel then. Pale underneath, hanging motionless in the rim updraft off the edge where the cloud-sea breathes far below. To live mostly elsewhere, to come in only at the edges. That appeals to me — the distance, the clean stoop folded between the teeth like a knife sheathing.

But no. I don't want to live elsewhere. I want to stay. The mesa is a lid over something hot — you can feel it through your palm before sunrise, that patient geothermal warmth — and I want to learn what stays with it through the white heat, not what visits.

Lower.

I try on the waiting ones. Wedged three to a crevice, the color of sunburnt basalt exactly, until pink, pink, pink — the throat-fan flicks. Going flat and pale at noon, shunting blood away. Coming out all at once when the cloud climbs for those twenty pearly minutes, peeping high and thin, doing push-ups on the pillar shoulders, licking water off lichen before it evaporates. I could learn that stillness. I am good at waiting.

But lizards don't tend. They take flies with a tick-snap and go back to stone. They don't check anything.

Lower still, sideways. Belly-fur worn off from scraping basalt. Now I'm in the squeezes where you have to turn your ribs to pass, whiskers beaded after the cloud, moving single file in a family of eight or twelve along a memorized route. A rock-runner. I like this better. This is more like me.

I know the natural cups, but I know the artificial ones better. There's a slow leak at the pipe joint on the western traverse where the washer never quite sealed, and I'm in the queue there in the morning, orderly, ill-tempered, squeaking, because it drips ten minutes longer into the heat, shaded by the pipe itself. Two drink while one sits upright on a neighboring pillar, nose twitching, watching for kestrel shadow. Then we rotate. When the pumps kick, that deep sigh-shudder, I freeze — not from fear, I was born to that sound — but because vibration means water moving underfoot and I listen with my molars to learn where.

Yes. I listen with my molars. That detail stays with me.

But I'm not furred. I don't have a naked tail to dump heat, I can't wrap it over my nose at night to save moisture deep in the slots. And I keep noticing the pipe itself, not just the leak. The stout black line at ankle height humming faintly when I put my hand on it. The bore-holes neat and dark where someone drilled the pillars and set one-way wicks. The green line of lichen following the buried pipe for half a kilometer where it sweats cold at night. Someone laid that. Someone still bleeds it.

So I get up — carefully, only on the planks — and I am taller, leaner, burnt dark at the neck and forearms, white where the hat shades. Sun-cracked hands. Thin-blood slowness.

You'd find me behind the rust-red pump hut no bigger than an outhouse, door propped with a stone.

At midday I'm in the stripe of shade there with everyone else, mouth open, breathing fast and shallow, listening to metal ping as it cools. I don't make noise. That's the rule, unspoken but enforced by everything: don't block a drip-line, don't waste the morning, don't make noise when everyone is sheltering. The hand-painted sign in sun-crazed paint says CHECK VALVES / LEAVE CUPS FULL TILL 9 and I painted it or I inherited it, it doesn't matter. I enforce it on myself.

In the morning I'm out before the sun takes the cups. I walk sideways between teeth that smell of dust and old feathers. I don't touch the natural hollows till nine even when the cistern is low, because the swifts and runners drink first. I put my palm flat to basalt and feel residual heat. I check the gauges by the three buried tanks — you can hear them echo if you stamp — I bleed a valve, I feel the pump sigh in my teeth.

At cloud-rise I'm out again. The white wall climbs fast up the cliffs, cool and damp on my sunburnt neck, light goes flat and pearly, condensation beads instantly on every pipe, every net, every whisker. The lizards run over my boots. I wipe the fog-net strung between galvanized poles, tip its dripping into the covered cistern. The flies get a second wind. The small brown wren that nests behind the warm transformer box on stripped wire insulation and swift feathers darts out to run along the tilted solar panels and drink. Moths cluster on the green LED blinking patiently in the dust.

At dusk I watch the mesa empty upward as the swifts funnel out over the rim to feed in the cooling air below. The gnats settle. The stones hold their heat longest.

At midnight I'm still there, sitting on a pillar I know by feel, feeling it through my clothes, faintly alive, while above me the sky does nothing strange at all, huge and ordinary and star-crowded, and the machines tick as they cool.

There is no room here that is not used. Every hollow holds morning water or a nest or a pipe. I am used too. That's where I find myself — not on the mesa so much as in its hours: drink before the sun takes it, hide through the white heat, steal a second drink when the cloud climbs. Another warm body that knows to wait for water like everyone else.
