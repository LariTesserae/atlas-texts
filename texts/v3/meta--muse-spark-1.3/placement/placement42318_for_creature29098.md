At first I think I would be up high.

That's where your eyes go, coming over the rim into that weight-light. The Towers. You can't not want them. A hundred meters of braided rope standing alone in bright cold air, breathing — *chuff-chuff-chuff* in your sternum — head turned a fraction into its own storm. To be vast and sparse and counted to the horizon. To comb the wind instead of drinking. To make weather and then answer it with lightning.

But I can't hold it. It's too lonely up there, and too loud. I imagine the creak of ten tons of fiber on wax for minutes at a time and my imagined joints ache. I imagine the slits shrieking when the plateau wind hits and I want to cover ears I don't have. No. Not a Tower. A Tower is a phase, anyway. A thing the ground does for a while when it wants to talk to the sky.

Then I think, maybe air. To be a skyfish.

Pale and thin as lung tissue with sun coming through my own wings, riding the updraft in a slow spiral with hundreds of others, mouth open, filtering down. Shrieking thinly — the only sound tuned to cut above the stamping. That feels close, for a breath. To live on what sheds. To gorge when a head detonates at noon and the black oilseeds go *up* instead of down.

But I don't stay up. The storm comes every day, white turning grey twenty miles out, and I know what I would have to do: fold, drop, wedge myself deep into the leeward bracts and hang there in rows like pale fruit while the world whites out. I don't want to vanish. I want to be under it, holding on.

Not a grazer either. I love them for a minute — big as bison and light as deer on their splayed snowshoe feet, mowing the lichen-felt in a moving lawn, teeth grinding even under the wind-roar, each back ticking blue sparks off its lint-matted wool when it shakes. But their life is too pulsed, too exposed. Mow and move and lie down all at once with rumps to the anchors when the wall of fluff hits. I can feel the fear in that lying-down, dutiful and large. I'm smaller than that.

Lower. I keep going lower.

My boots — when I still have boots in this imagining — stop punching and start springing. The Mat. A meter thick and warm underneath with friction. Rhizomes thick as my wrist, knotted and re-knotted. Cork blisters popping with resin smell underfoot. Papery lichen crackling and then, by evening, sealing again behind me like I was never there.

Put your palm to it, the description said. Feel the slow peristalsis tightening against wind-load. A faint vibration coming from very far away. Work being done.

That's where I find myself. Not on the rim looking out. A day's walk in, between two Towers, but much closer to one — a middle-aged one with a sour rust-red head and clean spiral scars up its windward side where the clingers have worked — a mile or so downwind, where its seed-mills have been stamping all morning.

*stamp. stamp. stamp.*

I am in the windrow.

At first it's just a line of pale ingots, identical packed fluff, dropped neat for miles. Then the line moves. Then I see I am the moving.

I am copper-red, long as a forearm, many-legged and shining with oil. An underthing. A tender.

It becomes more itself the longer I stay: the number of legs stops mattering — what matters is the click. Leg on leg, segment on rhizome. Put your ear to the Mat near a line and you hear it transmitted like a telegraph, they said. From inside, it's not like hearing. It's like being hearing. The whole felt under me hums with Tower-chuff and fiber-creak and far-off grinding teeth and static, and through it all a finer ticking — us — thousands long, cutting, rolling, burying.

My work: I climb an ingot bigger than I am, its fibers still warm from the press-cylinder, smelling of hot paper and turpentine. My jaws — not jaws, shears — cut. Inside, the black oilseeds, slick and bitter-bright. I roll one, legs rippling in sequence without my telling them to. It is heavier than me and I move it anyway, down into a tear in the Mat where the rhizomes have loosened and the dead lint is sweet. I bury it shallow. I eat one. Oil burns clean down my wax-sealed gut — no water in it, never water, water would be rot. A pale blind grub fat on last week's burying pushes past me, indifferent. A static-mite gathers at a spine-tip above, drinking charge with its whole body trembling blue.

Above me the light is blinding even through lint. My back crackles. The air tastes of dust and ozone. The Tower exhales and I feel it in my feet more than hear it.

And then the ripple passes.

I don't see it coming. No one does, from the rim. Ten thousand of us turn all at once. One moment the line is running east-southeast along the windrow, cutting and burying. The next — a shiver through the rhizomes, through the wrist-bones of the world — and we are running west-northwest. No signal. No shout audible over the roar. Just the peristalsis tightening, and us tightening with it.

That is when I stop being *I*.

I thought I would be a single animal with a name and a direction. I'm not. I'm a direction the Mat takes for a while. Like a Tower is a standing-up the Mat does, I am a tending the Mat does. When the noon head detonates — that dry pop five miles out, that black cloud rising into updraft and static — the skyfish shadows cross over us in hundreds, bellies going dark with oil, and we work faster. When the storm-wall builds, white to grey to towering, we don't run. Rumps to the anchors is for grazers. We flatten. We wedge into rhizome grooves and let the cool blinding fluff rip over us, breathing through oil, while the Mat ripples under and over us like skin. An hour later the air clears, brutally bright, everything glittering with wax-frost that will sublimate by evening, and we climb back out and resume a windrow that has moved a hand's breadth while we hid.

If you stood over me you wouldn't see me. You'd see ground that isn't ground, moving. You'd hear teeth and stamping and wind-played organ pipes and think the big things were alive and I was detail.

You'd have it inverted. As I learned to count the Towers by their chuff through my feet: the slow one to the north, the young shrill one to the east, and mine, my Tower, with its press-cylinders stamping identical futures I cut open.

I cure, I don't rot. When I die — snapped by a grazer's splayed foot, or just worn to dull copper — I'll dry by afternoon to shell and oil, sink a hand's breadth, be lichen-covered by the week. Grave and floor are the same word here.

Until then: cut, roll, bury. Listen with my legs to work being done very far away. Turn when the turning comes.
