At first I think I would be standing.

I come over the lip where the slope stops and I expect to look out, to get my bearings. That's what you do up high. But there is no out here. The walls lean in, the cloud sits down on them like a lid, and the light comes grey and strained, water in a cupped hand. My eyes keep trying for distance and failing.

So I crouch. I have to. There is nowhere to put a boot down that isn't already occupied, upholstered two, three deep in star moss and feather moss. I kneel on a stone that is already soft.

I think, then, I would be the watcher. The knees-denting one. Hands pressed to sponge-ground until water rises around my fingers without me pressing hard. Breath faintly visible. Fingertips going slow in the high-shadow cool. I would be the temporary thing, the dent that slowly exhales back.

But I can't stay there. No one gets to stay separate here. That's not how it works.

If I could choose a body to do something in, I think I'd be the thrush — the dark, almost flightless thing by the anvil stone. It at least *does*. It hops down, picks up the paper-thin snail, knocks it left-right-left until it cracks on the same edge all the others cracked. A tool, in the moment, then dropped. I like the neatness of that. No monument.

But I'm too loud for that. My blood is too loud. My breathing is too fast and it arrives out of rhythm, and when I breathe too close the springtails pop like sparks and a whole hollow of salamanders sighs and resettles. I feel rude in my hunger. The thrush eats and leaves. I would leave a dent.

Or the shrew, then? Impossibly dense fur, nose constantly working under the lip of rock, guiding beetles against its wedged quartz flake. Clever little not-hands. But my metabolism burns wrong here. The shrew is a hot knot. This place is cool that lives in shadow, musky and sweet underneath the wet stone and crushed green. The shrew darts. Here, even the moths have given up flying because their wings got too wet generations ago. They walk, absurd and patient, over fern fronds no longer than my thumb, over the backs of amphibians. Antennae waving. I would have to slow down more than I know how.

So I stop trying to be the one who uses. I put my hands flat and just feel the cold seep up through my knees. *Tik* on a leaf. *Tup* in a cup. And underneath, if I hold still until my own blood quiets, that low collective wet breathing — hundreds of small sides going in and out — like wind in a distant pine, but it isn't wind. The air does not move.

My breathing slows to meet it without me deciding to. In, out. In, out.

That's when close gets closer.

I am not looking across the bowl anymore. I am looking at the hollow right in front of my knees, where the peat is blackest and most saturated, where the white threads of fungi lace the rot together under the overhang. And the pattern there is wrong in the familiar way. Moss does not arrange itself flank to flank in neat black lozenges. Peat does not pulse.

One of the lozenges opens a jet eye and closes it again, too slow to be a blink.

Find me there. Not by the largest pool, not up on the weeping wall where the snails move a millimeter an hour and leave silver trails that never dry — lower than that. In the pile.

I would be one of the piled ones. Fat and slow and black, glossed with seep, side pressed to side so that when my neighbor breathes I have to shift to make room for the breath. A salamander. Or the toad no bigger than a knuckle tucked between two salamanders, throat going in-out, in-out, with no croak coming. It doesn't matter which, really — down there taxonomy softens. Newt coiled over newt, tail over back over belly, for a warmth that isn't warmth. A worm surfacing pink in black between us and none of us turning. A beetle crossing my head and I do not snap. I pulse my throat and wait for the feet to pass.

That is who I would be: the one who does not call, does not drain, does not leave. Skin beaded and re-beaded with hanging water that never quite runs off, wetter than the leaves, wetter than the stone cups. The water comes out of the stone, sits in the moss, lifts back as breath and mist, and comes down again, and I live inside that, not downstream of it. Near-surface, under-surface, in-surface.

Over my back, a pale moth walks its patient circuit. In the cup beside my hollow, tadpoles hang so thick the water looks solid until it shivers all at once. I warm my patch just by being many together, despite the cold.

I stay. A drip falls from the overhang into the same cup it has always fallen into. The cup is not getting deeper. The moss at its lip is not getting beaten back. I have not moved, except to breathe.

The mist has closed behind where I came in.
