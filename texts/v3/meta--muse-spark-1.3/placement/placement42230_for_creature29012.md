At first I don't know where to put myself. I stand too much.

I find the rim the way you said — not by sight, by the warm breath coming up off it in the night. Iron and wet green. I stand on the lip a long time because going down means joining it. The sky is a bruised slot. The steam takes the stars.

I go down the ladder because the stair ends halfway. The rungs sweat under my hands. Orange rust that hasn't yet flaked comes off on my palms. Then the cinderblock, then hand-over-hand where the wall sheared. My boots find talus that hasn't learned to be soft yet and says so, loud. Everything I do down here is loud.

At the bottom the air closes. Boiler-room 2 a.m. My shirt sticks in a minute. My breath comes back to me.

For the first ten minutes I think I will be eyes. Just that. The visitor with a headlamp turned off, letting the red bulb do it. I stand near the plinth and try to be surveyor, counter, namer. I tell myself: scald-moss, true moss, fern, fungus. Gradient. I try to hold myself apart up on the dry part of the slab where nothing has taken, where the mineral rings lie empty.

It doesn't hold.

Because of the second rule. Water does not flow here. It hangs. And I hang with it. Within twenty minutes I am beading. Sweat on my forearms, on my elbows, on my upper lip. It gathers, falls, *plink* — I hear myself join the drip from the catwalk, the weep off the pipes. I put my hand to the concrete to steady myself and it comes away wet, smelling of oil and sulfur. I wipe it on my pants and ten minutes later my pants are wet and the air takes it back. I can't be eyes only here. I am weather.

So then I think, alright, I will be mouth. That is what I am anyway. The too-loud, too-animal thing with steps and breath and gut full of bacteria and skin flaking off with every move. The ferns are perfect because nothing has ever taken a bite and here I come with teeth, with the dirt under my nails, with spores in the tread of my boots. Kneeling by the big clump I lift a frond — it comes away with that sucking sound off the steam line — and underneath the white rootlets combing the lagging, and I see how easy it would be. To tear. To take. To tip a pitcher and drink the clear oil-smelling water. To be the return of Before, when there were wings. My being here already starts it: my sweat feeds the mattress moss, my exhale fogs and clears and feeds. A spore from outside caught in my shirt will fall straight down in the still air and germinate where it lands.

I kneel there a long time with that knowledge in my mouth and I don't like who it makes me.

If I stay still longer — and the third rule makes you stay still, rust a millimeter a month, fronds a centimeter a week — something else starts to become possible.

I move off the dry slab. I go low, where the damp collects. Past the chair facing the boilers — I don't sit in it yet — past the tool shed with its swollen logbook, past the hard hat gone to moss, to the edge between. Between the orange skin of scald at the foot of the live boiler and the first true moss cushion a meter out. Where you can feel the bands on your shins and face. Where the black trickle runs to a pan that appears and vanishes. I lie down, not stand. Belly to the mattress. My hand sinks to the wrist into cold that surprises me, acid green and lime and that white-green that stays on the eye. It smells of deep earth and iron. Under my weight a faint sigh of water. The valve sighs back — *hssss* — a white feather gone before the rim. The red bulb hums at the edge of hearing.

Down here the pitchers hang just above me off the dead catwalk, swaying almost imperceptibly in the stirred air. Each full to the brim with rain that is not rain. Cisterns. Meat-eaters with no meat, keeping water instead. And I understand, with my elbows dripping into the mat, that I can do that trade too.

That is where I find myself, finally. Not up surveying. Not standing. Low and spilling like they do, wide not tall. Sideways toward warmth and wet.

What I would be — if I am allowed to stay, if the green will have me — is not the bite returning. I will be the mouth learning to fast.

An animal that stops trying to eat the perfect leaves and learns instead to collect. To sit in that single chair for hours and watch the gauge tremble just above zero the way someone liked to watch before. To learn the belt by watching one bolt for a full minute until I see it move, to oil it. To tip a pitcher gently when it brims and pour it back into the moss without drinking. To be another cistern. To be a listener with the shelf fungi out in the dark corners where the red doesn't reach, stacked ear over ear, beaded with droplets that never fall, trembling when the valve sighs and holding.

I would be the keeper's replacement. Not green, not machine. The warm body that keeps the exception open a little longer. The heart kept beating in a room with no body should not have to beat alone.

Slow me down to their speed and I almost fit. My sweat joins the cycle without thanks. My breathing fogs and clears. Plink. Long silences. Plink. The tick of contracting steel.
