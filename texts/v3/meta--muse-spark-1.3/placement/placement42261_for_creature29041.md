I come to it from above, because there is no other way.

At first, on the rim, with the pale plain at my back and the bowl opening under me, I think I could be anything. That is the luxury of the rim, before the heat takes you. The air is still thin up there, still belonging to the outside.

I think: I could be the swift. To cut once across that hard white circle of sky and not descend. To know and keep knowing. That would be clean.

But I go down. The rim won't let you do anything else.

The new rock cuts my hands. Pink-white, ash-grey, unsorted dust in the cracks. And then within twenty feet the green starts and does not stop, and I am walking not on earth but on plant, root as thick as my thigh, fern terraces stepping down in green stairs, light coming down in columns that turn green as they pass through me. Sweat starts at my temples and does not dry. My shirt sticks. My boots go dark and stay dark. Every leaf-edge glows.

I think, halfway down: I could be the skink. A dropped jewel, too bright, too quick to belong. Dart across a sunlit root and be gone before the mat notices you. Visit without being included.

But I am too heavy for that. Too slow, too loud. My footfalls land soft and still the green muffles them wrong. My breath is too fast for this air, which is like drinking warm water. I hear only the cicada winding its one rusted spring somewhere in the tree-fern crowns to the north, and the *plink... plink...* of leaf-drip into the stone pans, and under that, the tick-creak of vines tightening in heat that never ends.

On the floor it opens wider than I expected. I walk for hours and still be inside it. North to the thirty-foot tree-ferns making a second sky. South to the sheer raw wall with its skin of moss and tiny white orchids rooting in fracture lines. Center to the maze where the figs have grown together.

I think: I could be water. There is so little of it, and it is so attended to. To live in a bromeliad cup, black tea-colored, wriggling. To be gathered overnight in a root elbow. To fall leaf tip to leaf tip with a sound like a small bell near someone's ear. Drip, gather, fall, rise. That would be enough.

But I am too much water. I leak. I bead and run. The underside of every leaf is beaded and so am I, and I drip where I shouldn't. I put my hand under a low leaf and come away wet. If I lie wet and still for long here, I know what happens. I have seen it: the rare dead leaf lets go, falls three inches, is caught, and by morning is laced with white fungal thread and by noon is gone, melted back into the mat to keep everything exactly as it is. Branch fused to branch, bark to bark, fern trunk to fig root, one valley-wide held thing that digests the dead immediately.

To stay myself, I have to stay dry at the center.

So find me, finally, in the center. In one of the four or five low domes of living vine, bent and re-bent and fused with no cut end. Inside, on the living moss mat. In the middle, where it is dust-dry.

That is where I am. Not tending. Not yet tender.

I thought I would be an observer. A namer. A counter of orchids and larvae. But there is no room and no need. The mature plants simply persist. Counting would be a kind of weather, and there is no weather here. Shadows are short and do not lengthen. The sun holds at noon.

What I am, lying there looking up through layer after layer of translucent green to white, beaded with water that never quite falls on me, crossed by a raw unweathered edge of stone the green has politely gone around — what I am is the wet one. The proceeding one.

I still proceed, inside, where they cannot tend it. My heart beats too fast. My thoughts run forward. I remember the plain. I remember morning. I bring time down with me like dust on my boots, pale and unsorted, and it wants to become soil, to wash, to carve a gully, to make a seedling struggle upward. I want things to happen, even here, even while I tell myself I have learned stillness. That wanting is my wetness.

Beside me, in the dry, the moss hummock breathes. Very slowly, in time with the thickening air.

I walked past them two or three times before I understood. The square-shouldered bulge in the strangler fig that is not quite a boulder. The low spread on all fours, knee-high when folded, skin cool-pale and damp-smooth under the accumulation — moss thick across shoulders and back, white orchids rooting in the knuckles, liverwort lace at the throat. Not camouflage. If you lie still long enough here, things grow on you. Eyes shut, face turned calmly toward *plink... plink...*, finding the way by wetness.

One of them slept this dome into being. Leaned against a young creeper dropping from the rim and stayed, cool weight making it sweat and turn toward the dry warmth of a moss back, until rubbing fused bark-to-bark and it arched and closed. What looks built was slept.

Now it sleeps curled on the dry heart, holding the shape open, and by sleeping keeps the white threads from taking it. *I am still distinct*, the dry says. *Not yet mat.*

And because there is just enough dry for a body-sized patch, for a little longer than a body, it shifts — I hear the papery rustle I thought was a dead leaf letting go, sweet and green-rotted — and makes room.

I am not a keeper of stillness. I am its guest. Its opposite, tolerated. The tenders cross the fern terraces an inch at a time to press dust back into cracks, to carry fresh spalled rock one by one in toothless green-gummed mouths to the cairn, to stir the bromeliad cups without emptying them and sip the warm pollen-filmed pans and leave always a few inches, to keep the cycle mouth-sized so it will never flow and carve and break the rim. That is their work. Nothing proceeds.

My work is to lie in the dry they keep and proceed quietly, inwardly, without spilling. To let my sweat bead and not run. To let my breath slow until the moss on their back lifts only a millimeter under the pressing hot air and mine lifts with it. To listen to a drop land near my ear like a small bell. Far off, the cicada winds up again.

I will not stay. I know that. The swift knows that. To stay long enough would be to grow orchids in my own knuckles, to shut my eyes, to learn to go by wetness and never finish a thought. To descend fully is to be included. I am not ready to be included.

But for a little while, in the only truly dry spot in the bowl, something cool shifts its weight beside me and keeps me dry too, so I will not be absorbed yet.
