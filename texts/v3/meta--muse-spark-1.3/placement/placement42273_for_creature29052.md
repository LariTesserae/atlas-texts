I don't arrive. I get carried in.

Low over the warm flat glare, and then down into it, into the steam and the gulls and the thump in the sternum, and for a while I am not anyone. I am just motion trying to find a shape that fits. Shoulder to shoulder, balcony to balcony, boat to boat — there's no room to be alone here, so I try on bodies the way the tide tries the arcades.

At first I think I'll be high. A gull.

It would make sense. To scream and cut straight through engine roar, to boil off the condenser pylons by the hundred thousand and land on fresh reef-concrete while it's still warm, stealing the heat. To see the city as weather, as it is from above — the low scud it makes for itself, the sudden rain over the fish market while the copper market two streets over stays dry and dusty. I try the scream in my throat and it tears. Too loud, too white. My wings ache with other wings. No. Too much air. Everything here is low. Elevation is a rumor. I want to be down where the water finds its way up.

Then I think I'll be a person. A porter, maybe. Broad chest, thick neck, hands that slap and clap because whispering was selected out generations ago. Bare feet white-pruned at the toes and black-calloused underneath from hot basalt and salt and crab claws. Salt rime on my calves, mother-of-pearl scale in my beard, hair standing up with noon static under the harvester field. Shouting *mind the rope mind the rope* with an impossible load balanced on my head, trotting because the rule is keep moving or be moved. Assembling a whole shop at dawn from poles and tarps and plastic older than I am, hoisting it all a meter on pulleys when the tide takes the lower arcade, sitting with my feet in warm seawater and keeping on selling.

I try the trot and the shout. The copper bracelet on my wrist grounds me to the awning. My singlet never dries. My knees know the lock stairs. I can sleep sitting up in a boat, in a hammock under a pressure tube going *thup-thup-thup* all night with grain and mail, and wake when the shift changes at the shrine wedged between the pump station and the cage-mender.

It's close. Too close. I am almost that knot where traffic pauses. I can feel the warm rain begin to fall out of a clear sky, hissing on ten million awnings, and I don't even look up. A crab sidles over my foot.

Wait.

Lower.

Not the foot. The thing crossing the foot.

I thought I would be loud because humans here have to be loudest, bellowing through cone megaphones in four languages, slapping fish tails on stone to punctuate a price, singing to be heard over foundation thump and harvester drone and ten thousand gulls and the vast wet breathing of tide moving through the city as if through gills. But my bark isn't like that. My bark you feel in the ribs more than hear. Deep-chested. It doesn't need a megaphone.

You'd find me in the fish market at noon. Not above it, not looking at it. In it, up to the ankles.

Ankle-deep brown-green gutter water running warm over tile worn round at the corners, ducks paddling single-file behind a porter's cart like they have errands, kids splashing and screaming with delight, old women laughing with their whole chests while their short knives flick oyster after oyster — *click flick, click flick* — and the sweet rot of shell piles for lime fighting frying oil and diesel and hot iron and incense and ozone after the rain.

That's where I get my shape back. Trotting.

I am a yellow cur, short-coated, lean, one ear torn from a culvert rat that fought back. A bitch, teats swung low from a litter last hot season somewhere under a sky-bridge. I trot the market aisles with the confidence of an employee, because I am one. I clean fish guts. I herd children away from barge ropes when the boy shouts to mind and the barge shoulders through behind us blasting its horn and the gulls explode upward. I know which alleys to avoid at noon when the charge builds and the canal water starts climbing the lock walls, beading and crawling in defiance of tide before it falls back with a slap. I know to shake when the harvesters thrum on and my teeth ache and my shadow lags half a second behind me.

My feet are never dry. The pads are split from salt and hot stone. My fur is stiff with it, smelling of life without leaves — salt, sweat, fish blood. Wasps and flies lift off my back when I move. Swifts cut overhead through the columns of insects rising off wet stone. Cormorants hold their wings out on the bridge stanchions like black laundry. Under the bridge, eels and mullet and silver schools that turn as one body gather to watch the welding sparks shower down, and I stop to watch them watch, until a porter clips me with his cart and I move because you move or you are moved.

And then, midday, the work pauses without pausing. The harvester rain hisses down. Everyone shouts and laughs and covers stalls that are older than they are. The whole vast flat pan steams. I do what we do.

I find a warm puddle in the middle of traffic — courtyard pump-puddle, rain-pocked, oil-slicked, duck-down floating — and I curl in it. Tight. Chin on tail. Steam rising off my back. And everyone steps over. Porters, welders, water-taxi drivers, children fishing with magnets for nails and coins, red and blue-black crabs tipped from a basket clattering across tile and sending kids shrieking after them, one sidling right over my paws without fear because I am not a threat, I am furniture, I am soft.

That's it. That's me become more itself. Not a pet. Not a stray. The soft part. A dog is an employee. A gull is a street sweeper. A crab is a pedestrian. I am the place to step around that keeps the aisle flowing.

I sleep while the sound deepens but never drops. Foundation thump in my sternum, harvester drone in my teeth, brass band practicing on a rooftop never quite together. I dream of eel shadows by name. My ribs bark once in my sleep and I feel it more than hear it.

When I wake the rain has stopped because someone three districts over got their pressure. The stone smokes. A boy's magnet comes up dripping with my lost bottlecap, a coin, a nail. An old woman tosses me an oyster foot. I catch it without getting up, and then I get up, shake the warm water off in a hard circle, and trot on.
