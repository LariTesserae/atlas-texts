I would not be in the middle.

At first I think I would — because I enter like you do, ducking sideways through the thin place on the left where the rock shoulders out, water slapping the hood, and I stand blinking in the boiled green-grey light with spray on my eyelashes. I try to stand in the center where it's scoured clean, cobble and coarse sand sloping toward the curtain. Nothing in the middle. A hollow held open.

I can't stay there. It's too fast there. My boots want to shuffle and I can't even hear them. My breath hangs and is torn. My heart is absurdly loud, absurdly temporary. Anything that stands in the middle is waiting to leave.

So I go low. Stay low and stay back — the place tells me without words.

Would I be the wren? For a moment I want to be. Brown as bark, tail cocked, darting in through the side gap with a grub, knowing exactly when the gust will thin the curtain enough to pass. Warm as a fist inside a domed nest of rootlets and bat hair. But no. She is too quick for me. She does not linger. She stuffs the blind mouths and is gone. I cannot move that fast and stay. Her song outside is one of the loudest in the woods. Inside she only churrs. I am not a darting thing.

Would I be the trout? Holding impossibly still on the shelf at the edge of the curtain, dark-backed, letting the current do the hunting. Perfectly still while everything moves. That stillness tempts me. But the trout looks outward. It lives in the fall's idea, eyes on the white water, waiting for what is thrown over. I don't look outward. My hearing has already turned inward — blood, breath, the creak of my own jaw.

Would I be the bats? Five, six furred pebbles hung in the dry slit high in the basalt crack where the spray doesn't quite reach. Breathing once a minute. Frost on whiskers. Stored meat that remembers how to fly. Trusting the stored cold to keep them cold, the roar to keep owls and men away. Closer. I know that stored cold. That putting yourself into rock like a letter into a drawer. But torpor is deeper than dreaming, and I don't sleep that deep. I keep vigil. Someone has to stay awake while they are stored.

Would I be the spiders? Long-legged, amber, upside-down at the hub of a few slack lines strung from crack to moss hummock, beaded with water. *Meta*. They don't spin to catch — there are almost no flies — they spin to feel. Trembling not with prey but with the fall itself. Reading thunder through their feet. They can go months without eating. They have learned to eat drip. To eat waiting.

Closer still. I know that work. Sitting utterly still in permanent dark under the ledge, feeling the river thicken and thin overhead as a tremor in silk. Moving absurdly fast, once a season, when a drowned midge is blown through, and then still again.

But I am not waiting for food.

Would I be the moss? The pelt. Pillow into star into liverwort into algae, inches thick, swollen, holding to vertical rock by suction and patience. Drinking air. Growing a millimeter a year outward from the seep-lines. Smelling of iron and peat and crushed stems. The one for whom the fall is not terror but sun.

I press my thumb in, as you will, and water wells up and stays. I want to be that patient. That low, and at the edges, and all at once. To never become forest. To want exactly this: cold, and dark, and spray forever.

But moss does not remember hands. And I do.

That is how I find where I am.

Against the right wall, where the overhang is highest and the drip is least. Inside the shallow C of river slabs set on edge, two courses high, open toward the back wall. Where the gravel is darker, mixed with ash and charcoal fleck from a fire dead so long the stones are cold through. Where the alder poles lean with crushed ends, the bark fiber rotted to stain.

I sit where you would sit. Low.

I am damp to the core and then damper. My skin has gone the black-green of permanent shadow. Pillow moss grows in the folds of my elbows. A hart's-tongue uncurls, stunted and leathery, from the crack of my collarbone, beaded and never dripping because the mist feeds it faster than gravity can take. I do not move quickly. Drip-hollowing, frost-prying, moss-creep: that is my speed.

I am profoundly deaf. The way you become deaf after ten minutes inside the drum. I don't hear the roar anymore; I am the roar turned inward. I feel it in sternum and teeth. I read the river by the pulse of green-grey on my closed eyelids — grain of rock, wet shine, then near-dark. My eyes never fully adjust. There is too little to adjust to. What use are eyes here?

So I wait, with the trout's stillness but facing inward.

I keep the room held open. I keep the fire-pit cold. When frost tries to topple the little cairn of seven stones to the left, I make a minute adjustment per decade to keep it balanced. When a leather strip, a carved dipper with wavy lines for water, a flake still sharp enough to cut, are wedged above it, I accept them and rot them at the correct speed. When the wren darts in, I do not startle. When the bats breathe once a minute, I breathe once an hour, with the gust that thins the curtain.

I am charcoal and spit and breath-hang and hand-oil left on sandstone, and wren-shit and bat-shit and moss creeping over it all, year after year, remembering the shape of hands. That is all a keeper is.

And if I stay long enough — I always stay long enough — for shoulders to hunch and skin to go numb and then tingle, the gust comes. Real grey forest light spears straight through and hits the back wall. The mosses flare emerald. The droplets hang like glass. The trout-shadow flicks.

I lift my face, if I have a face, and let it hit. For a second.

Then it closes, and the roar takes it all back, and I settle again into the humming dark with the smell of wet stone.

I am in no hurry to be seen.
