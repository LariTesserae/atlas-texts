I come up slowly, because there is no other way up.

At first I thought I knew where I'd be in that town. I pictured myself with the lowland stare still on me — notebook out, asking for water too loudly, touching a handrail at noon and learning. A visitor. A counter of shifts until the rail-crawl takes me back down. That was my first image, and it doesn't hold.

It lifts off like the mirage does, a hand's breadth above its foundations.

If I stayed — and to live here you have to stay — I thought next I might be hands. Everyone here is hands. I tried that on: fingertips grey with fines, roped knuckles from basalt, learning the sip-in through the nose, out through pursed lips because the air is too thin and too hot to gulp. I tried being the quarryman in the fissure in short shifts, face wrapped wet. No. Too fast, too hard in the chest. I tried being the pipe-fitter who never builds straight, always leaves a loop, a belly, a bellows for the swell. Closer — I like leaving room for expansion — but not yet.

I kept walking down, in my head, terrace by terrace, roof touching roof. Past the long sheds where the ingots go cherry to black over days. Past the electrolysis yards humming faint behind their spark screens, that faint ozone after a surge. I smelled myself there — hot iron, machine oil, ash, faint sulfur — and underneath, tin-clean dew.

I thought I would be one of the dawn children. That image stayed longer and got more itself.

Before the sun burns through, when the peak snags the cloud. Everything sweats. The sun-sails sag with beads. I am barefoot on warm stone, wide feet through wooden soles feeling the hill breathe under me. I have a squeegee of split rubber and a jar slung on cord, and I know the drip-line on my street the way I know my own name. Whose roof feeds whose jar. Where the canvas sags best. I herd water without spilling a drop, because to spill is the worst rudeness, worse than shouting. And later, when the stone steams dry by nine, I press my hand into the warm tar-plastic curb where the crew is laying and laugh when it gives. Come back in a month. See how I've lifted.

But I'm not that young. My chest is not a barrel yet. I get winded and have to stop without shame, palm on a cool pipe-belly, waiting for the dizziness to pass.

So I go further down, into the shade.

The white hours. Handrails will brand you. The rule is *rest*. I find myself where I keep finding myself: in the machine hall where the flywheel the size of a house turns so slowly you have to watch the painted spoke for a full minute to be sure it moved. It ticks. The new basalt pings as it contracts. Dripwater patters into tin somewhere below. A bearing groans low enough to feel in the molars.

There is an old tender there oiling, listening. And beside him on the cool ceramic, belly flat, the pale almost-translucent lizard flicking condensate off a pipe. No one bothers it. It's good for the coils. Bad to name.

I sit with them. I learn the skilled work here, which is not pushing but listening. Put your ear to the standpipe in the evening when the coils begin to cool. Hear tomorrow's water condensing inside, drop by patient drop.

That is where I find myself. Not on the rim looking out at the haze, not up with the swifts circling the thermal for hours without flapping, crying once thinly before they drop away. Down in the courtyard level, in the deep shade under canvas, shoulder turned sideways as the tram creeps past on its trestle, warm displacement and click-click of expansion joints.

Who would I be? Not quite mason, not quite engineer. A jack-man, maybe, going house to house with basalt shims and great threaded jackscrews, re-leveling when doors jam, because no foundation is trusted. Or the engine-tender's second hands, patting a warm housing as I pass the way you'd pat a cow's flank. Knowing which joint clicks.

But more truly — and this is the image that won't lift off — I would be the kept thing as much as the keeper.

I would be the water in the white-painted roof-tank, holding the morning against evaporation. The overnight drip-bag hung under the eave. The jar handed over with both hands by a household that has decided I can be trusted. Heavy, cool. Not asked for. Waited for. Offered.

And on the nights past the last terrace, I would be the other side of that offering, too. The dog that may not exist. The feral story in the ash fields living on scraps and drip. The children leave jar lids of water out just in case. Whether I exist hardly matters. The gesture tells what kind of people leave water for something that may not exist. I want to be worth that kind of gesture. Patient. Quiet. Ticking myself toward dawn.

By evening when the light finally slants and ambers the black glass to bronze, I come back out with the others. *Drip-line clear?* I call, low, leaning in a little closer than lowland manners allow. I touch the leaning doorway in the Upper Tier plaza on the way — plumb twenty years ago, now six degrees north, kept as a clock — not prayer, exactly. Acknowledgment.

*The mountain is working slower than you, but it is stronger.*

I drink carefully, both hands, not spilling, while the factories exhale their stored heat in long shimmers around me.
