At first I am still what I came up as.

Boots on the pavement, hands in pockets, breath short. Eight degrees and the sweat on my neck going to chill. I look up and the sky is wrong with height — bruised violet straight overhead — and a dust wall three hundred meters tall is coming at me without sound, and I am too loud, too wet for this place. I breathe too often. I drip. I will rot.

I can't stay like that. If I am going to live here I have to thin out.

For a while I think I want to be a sweeper. White, gull-sized, wings twice too long. To be launched off the ghost-swells in the morning and not land until dark. Never flap, only ride. To live a half-second ahead of my own shadow and hunt by that lateness, watching for the flicker under stones. That feels right for a moment — the circling, the watching without landing. But I flap too much. I want to push at the air. I want to be heard. The sweepers have given up their flight muscles to ribbon to belong here. I haven't. And birds will not fly over the needles. I keep wanting to fly straight over them to see.

So lower.

For a while I think I want to be a stitcher. To wedge myself by day into a crack and go torpid and stone-colored, to come out at night huge-eyed and amber and go pogoing, step step step, a dotted seam across the dust. To lick salt pans with a long dry black tongue and snap up light. To have learned the opposite lesson — freeze early, not late. There's a wisdom in that I almost take. But I'm not nocturnal enough. I don't want to hide by day. I want to drink the morning cold before the sun takes it.

Lower still.

Not the flicker under the stones either. The rock-mites. Salt-colored, rice-grained, armored in silica built from devil-carried glass. I see myself as they are seen, only as a prick of light when a glass back turns over. Too quick. I would be eaten, or I would have to keep wedging and unwedging. I don't want to run.

What I keep coming back to is the thing I walked through without seeing.

Find me then on the north side of a stone. Not a special stone. One of the interlocking ventifacts, faceted by a million years of grit, dull as an old coin, half-buried and soft-shouldered. You would have stepped over me. Everyone does.

I am the black rosette welded there. Brittle as burnt paper.

That is where I would be: low, north-facing, in the cold hold of shadow a little longer than anywhere else. Close enough to a needle to feel it — never within arm's reach, because lichen will not grow there — but close enough that the wire-grass trembles nearby, glass-clear and straw-yellow, and the dust devils lean our way. They are born near the structures and die near them, and I need them the way a field needs rain. When two of them collide and explode into a flat spreading veil, I am what that veil is for. Grain by grain, in total silence, salt and spore and fine glass settling. I wait for it.

Who I am, then, is mostly dead.

That is the honest count. A rosette the size of your palm is mostly dead ancestors holding a living rim. The dead edges don't rot — nothing rots here — they flake, they persist, they hold on for eons so the thin green-black edge can live a millimeter a century and then wait out a drought of a thousand years. I am that. A black crust that waits.

In the morning I drink without drinking. The white rime stiffens in the shadows and never melts, only lifts straight back into the sky when the sun touches it. For minutes my pores open. I take the vapor as it leaves. It never wets me. At noon, if the light is right, I feel the shiver from the needle across the pavement — hairs would stand up if I had them — and my time slips a little, three seconds fast, like your watch. At dusk a sweeper's shadow crosses me a half-second late, and somewhere under the next stone a mite freezes too late, mid-flicker, lit. I do not move for any of it.

At night it gets deep. The sweepers fold and fold their impossible wings into the varnished overhangs and breathe once a minute. The stitchers pause mid-hop. And across the vast dark the needles tick — a single soft glass tick you feel in your teeth rather than hear — unsynchronized, like cooling stones. I feel it in the stone I am welded to. The wire-grass listens. I listen.

I make no updrafts. I make no weather. I am not a shepherd of wind. The ground here finished eons ago and I finished with it, into final patience. But I am what the sky's violence is for, in the end. The bazaar from the waist up — the forty, sixty dust devils wandering, wobbling, splitting and rejoining — all that mute anthill-energy has to land somewhere. It lands on me.

If you lie in your bag at night listening for water and hearing none, only your own short breath and the papery shuffle of a devil a kilometer away turning and turning over the oldest floor in the world, I am under your shoulder. You won't notice me. You are waiting for a drip, a stream, rain on fabric.

I am waiting for dust.
