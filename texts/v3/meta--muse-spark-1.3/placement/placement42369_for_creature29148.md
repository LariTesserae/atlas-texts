At first I thought I would be walking.

That's what you picture when you come up out of the cloud sea — yourself as that dark figure you see from a ladder-top at evening, crossing three miles of empty shining mat toward the next crown. Sack bouncing. Shadow a tight black puddle under your feet. Going somewhere on a plant the size of a country to drink warm tea out of a leaf's armpit. It looks noble from far away.

Up close it wouldn't be me. My calves are not rope. I can't do the mat-bounce all morning with the sun pressed that close, salt drying as fast as I sweat it, nothing to hide under while the gliders tear the air overhead. I tried to imagine it and the image wouldn't hold.

Then I thought, higher. If I'm going to live inside a roar I should live all the way inside it. Up in the crown where it's ten degrees cooler and a hundred times louder, where the cicada-ranks sit in rows under the branches, copper-backed, fist-sized, pumping until the air shivers and my teeth ache. Where the bees move like smoke between flower spikes as long as canoes and plaster my eyelids gold. Where a mantis as long as my forearm takes a bee, and a root-snake takes the mantis, and a four-winged hunter stoops into a flock of three thousand screaming gliders and misses and perches panting with its throat sac pulsing.

I stayed up there one noon in my head and had to come down. The fast clock is too fast. Everything eating and being eaten and laying eggs in the leftovers in one afternoon from one platform. My nose bled where the hollow trunk focused the sound. I am not a hunter. I am not meat that can stand being watched that closely.

And I am not floor. Nobody lives on the floor. I crouched, like you're supposed to, and saw the highways — black and oxblood beetles big as thumbnails ticking under the creak, rust-red ants hauling wax crumbs ten times their size, silver-green skinks swimming through root-hair with their legs tucked. I saw a cup-rosette pop open at night, bathtub-wide and fleshy, fill with bitter tannin water by dawn, and by seven it was ringed: night-mice lapping with their huge kidneys, moths unrolling tongues longer than their bodies, a root-snake slid up over the lip to drink and then lying in it all day like a cord, waiting. A glider landed wrong in daylight and crawled on its elbows to drink and drowned, and the ants found its membranes within the hour. Everything is a water-thief down there. I don't have the kidneys for it. I don't want to sleep where I have to sleep off the ground because the mat itself hunts at night.

So not the walker, not the hunter, not the crawler.

It was the still hour that found me. After noon, when even the cicadas dip for breath. When every giant at once lets out that hot peppery sigh of spores and the air hazes gold and the insects go mad and people cover their mouths and cough and wait.

In that dip, you see the rooters.

A furred clinger no bigger than a cat wedged in a low fork. A sucker-bat pressed belly-flat to the felted white underside of a sail-leaf. They push in a pink tendril-tongue, a chin rootlet, into a nectar well, into a leaf vein, and go torpid. Breathing slows to nothing. Algae in their skin bloom green under fur, under feathers. Moss has rooted into old scars on their backs and shivers. They just hang there for hours making sugar from sun while they sleep off the morning's sap-drink. Animals that learned, where there is almost no water and murderous sun, to eat light when meat is scarce.

I envied them so badly it felt like recognition.

You would find me there. Not high, not low. In a low fork on the storm side of one giant — the side she's leaning toward, laying down new buttress root year by year, letting the lee rot out behind her. My giant has shifted enough in twenty years that the rope ladder we lashed straight now slants. Old maps are useless. I know.

Twenty feet up. That's all. High enough the night-grunters can't take me from my hammock, low enough my old knees can still do the climb. A platform of cut sail-rib lashed with root-hair cord, thatched with fallen wax-scale. It can be untied in a morning and carried. I haven't untied it in six years.

I am the bee-girl grown old. Hair shaved because otherwise the bees nest in it. Skin oiled with warmed wax to throw back sun, salt-white always at the temples. Fiber breech, a dew-cloak made from one small sail-leaf, soft wraps for bark. My calves *were* rope once. Now they knot.

I keep one cistern. Where leaf meets branch, fifty gallons of amber rain-tea, warm, full of tadpole-things and larvae. I fish out the drowned gliders in the morning, strain through moss between my teeth, drink after midday when sun has sterilized it. I keep the sky-cup trail clear. I hang dew-sails at dawn to drip into gourds made from unopened throat-flower buds pinched shut with resin. I tend the bees with smoke and bare hands — you have to be still, you have to be sweet-sweated and not afraid when they plaster you. I keep a cold fire only for meat, glider dried till it's like salt wood, cicada grubs in their shells. Resin burns too fast and too smoky for anything else.

I cut shallow. I lash, I don't nail. I piss off the edge away from water. Every child learns before walking: never cut the mat deeper than a finger. I teach it still, when a family passes through for a generation and claims this crown and then moves on when it gets too loud or too quiet. A deep wound festers black for miles under the fiber and your footing goes soft. Take the surface, or the surface will leave you.

At noon I do what the rooters do, as close as a person can. I wedge myself into the fork where the wood is worn smooth by me, sip from the nectar well — not with a tendril, just a hollowed thorn — and go still. I don't photosynthesize, not really. But the green has gotten into me anyway. Algae in the cracks of my elbows. Lichen in the weathered swirls of my scalp. Old scars on my back where moss took hold one wet year and never left. It shivers when the sail-leaves turn. They turn for sun, but also for sound — clap and a ripple runs across a half-mile crown as thousands of waxy tops angle toward the noise, listening for rain. I listen with them.

I talk with my hands now more than my mouth. I slap bark for emphasis. I sing the short loud repetitive songs to be heard over the ranks, though there's no one most days to hear: *sky-cup left, beetle right, drink here after storm-head.* At the crossing below I keep the cairn: shed claws, seed stones, a carved rib-bone. Not gods. Reminders. Someone found water here. Someone bled here and the hunters came. Don't bleed here.

If you stay long enough they stop noticing you. The gliders, the bees, the people passing. Unless you carry water. I carry water, so I am noticed. A cup held out with both hands.

At evening I don't walk the mat. I sit at the ladder-top and watch the others do it — a dark figure heading for the next hair on the arm of the world-mother, bees smoking gold over its crown, thunder walking around in an anvil head three miles away raining itself out where I'm not. Below everything the cloud sea turning pink. Above me no peak, only more up, hot and thin and bright. The throat-flowers under the lower tiers beginning to gape — you can feel the tug of air on your shirt — inhaling moth clouds for the night. The whole hot humming indifferent total chorus going on without pause around me, in my sternum, in my teeth.

The plant does not mind me. That is enough permission.

I drink from a leaf with both hands. I stay.
