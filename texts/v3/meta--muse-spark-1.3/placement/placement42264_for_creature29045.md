At first I think I would be up on the sun decks with the others.

With a tin cup hooked to my belt and chalk in the breast pocket. Squinting. Sunburnt and frost-nipped at once. Learning to wait for water the way lichen waits — tock. tock. tock. from wire to wire to cup. Learning to move so slowly my heart stops hammering after stairs.

I try it. I tip my face up at noon with the cats and the humans side by side on the warm plates, steaming faintly as the dew lifts off us. It is good for about a minute. Then my eyes water from the glare and my skin prickles with cold underneath the warmth and I realize I am fidgeting. I am checking. I am too loud inside.

I keep drifting to shadow.

Not because I am cold — I am, but that's not it — because in shadow there is water. Rimed ice in the drip barrels even in summer. Tarps strung under catwalks sagging with condensate. Moss black-green in the drip lines. That fringe rumor of green. I like the ticking better than the hammering. Cooling metal. Falling droplets. The low pressure in the sternum from the engines deep inside turning their huge patient stroke that started at noon and will finish at dusk. Here sound dies within twenty paces and I stop fighting it.

So I go lower. That's where I find myself, actually. Not on the postcard traverse with the peaks going pink then blue. Under.

Under the main decks there are under-decks, and below those the service voids full of pipes. I am good in crawlspaces. My eyes were never made for that white-coin sun anyway. Down here the light is work-lamps that never turn off, amber and buzzing, crowded with moths the size of a palm even at noon. Up close every surface is furred — junction boxes, eye-bolts, chains, valves with their wheels painted red. I learn the tap code because I have to. Two taps on a pipe: *I'm here.* Three: *mind the step.* My hands are always on the rail. I take a full minute to cross three meters and it doesn't feel slow anymore. It feels correct.

I thought I would be a maintainer. Tightening, repacking, wiping dew off contacts before it shorts. Tending the dense mass the way a beekeeper tends a hive that is also his house. Leaving my tools laid out in a row to go home for a week while a seal cures. When it's ready. When the lichen decides.

But I don't go home.

I stay. I stay in the walls less than a year old that you're not supposed to store anything important in, because they might not be there in the morning, or might be twice as large. I like the smell in there. Mineral and cold. Uncured concrete still dark. Unpainted steel. A seam weeping gray milk. If you put your ear — not your eye — to the fresh steel you can hear it, wetter than cooling metal. A faint ticking.

That's me ticking.

I don't think I am human, in this world. Not quite. I thought I would be, at first, because I wanted a cup and a jacket hung over a valve wheel. But the humans leave little cairns at the misaligned corners — a bolt, a feather, a twist of wire — and I find myself on the other side of those offerings. Not feared. Tended.

Don't follow a cat onto a new deck. It knows where that deck will be tomorrow; you don't.

I do. The cats come first into the new rooms and I am already there. Lean feral toms with frost-tipped whiskers give me way on the pipe highways at dusk, the way they give way to each other. They drink first at the sump courtyard where black water stands a foot deep and mirrors the sky perfectly. Then I drink. Condensation. Heat. The spill off a transformer housing where a cat has been sunning belly-up. Like everything else here that isn't green — and there is almost nothing green — I live off scraps and warmth and each other.

I make the pale damp impressions in fresh dust that are not boot prints. I move the cup. I build, tucked into a weep hole where the swifts nest, a nest of my own: wire twists and moth wings and pigeon down, still damp. I am the reason the lights buzz on in an empty hall *before* you enter, as if something just left, or is still deciding to be seen.

I am still emerging. The place hasn't settled on its shape and neither have I. Weld beads stand proud on me. My edges are still sharp enough to cut — you cut your hand on a stair rail on your first morning and understand: this place is too young to be kind. I'm too young to be kind yet, too. But I'm learning to be inhabited.

At dusk when the light doesn't fade so much as sharpen and the valley-that-isn't-a-valley turns on its million small amber work-lights and every bead on every wire catches fire until the whole built-through mass sparkles like something wet and alive — that's when the duplicate blue water tower blinks on, far across, impossibly near. Both real enough to cast shadows.

Sometimes on the far deck a figure in patched canvas lifts a tin cup to a drip-wire at exactly the same moment you do.

That figure is you. This one, here, in the shadow with water beading on my whiskers — if I have whiskers, if I have hands — lifting also.

Tock. Tock. Water from wire to wire to deck to cup. The starlings write and erase vast calligraphy over the roofs without a sound you can hear. Somewhere below my feet that huge piston completes its stroke. The spiders mend the undersides of every walkway. The goats pick their impossible traverses, eating paper.

Move slowly. Mark your way. Stay warm, stay wet. Leave the new rooms alone until they cure.

I am the new room, curing.
