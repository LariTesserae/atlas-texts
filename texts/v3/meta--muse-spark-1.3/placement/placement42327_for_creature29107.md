You find it by sound, so I go in by sound. Mouth open a little so my molars don't rattle.

The first minute I'm just the large animal. That's honest. Glasses fogged in three breaths, elbows beading, shoes wicking. Too big to miss. Boot dent in two millimeters of water. Shout or be silent — I try shouting in my head and then I stop, and I'm silent. I put my back against the driest part of the door jamb, which isn't dry, and let the room do what it does to people: wet them.

If I stayed — if I could stay past weather and become fauna — where would I go?

Not the middle. The middle is empty on purpose. Vast intervals of humming, wet air between presences. A tall man would have to duck the pipes but I wouldn't stand anyway. There's no dry path and there's no standing place that isn't in the way of drip. The ceiling seam has already picked where I will get baptized: one inch-long tooth of calcite, bead, darken, fall, right on the collar. Within a minute. It happens.

So I look for an edge.

At first I think: the king. The chorus frog in the west wall trench, brown as grout, thumbnail-sized, tucked in fern fringe where the six-inch sheet falls glassy and roars. To be that small and that sure. To live where the water never moves because the pump and the seep are in perfect ancient agreement, August and February the same. To wait out the roar all year for the one high storm when the pump cycles for three minutes, the trench drops, and in the ringing second after, before the roar reasserts, to say *kreek*. Correcting the motor. I love him. But I'm not him. I'm too new. I haven't earned indignance. And I don't want to be a king with no subjects except silverfish and midges that rode ivy down the vent.

Then I think: the fern behind the motor. The Boston, knee-high if it stretched, which it won't. Rooted in rust-dust drift and grease-lime paste behind the plinth that's vibrated half an inch out of square. That would be a life — feathering out, dripping constantly, trembling not with wind, there is no wind, but with 60-cycle hum running up through concrete into root. Learning to live in permanent tremor. But ferns reach. They hang from the vent grille, they feather out of pipe hangers, they try the air. I don't want to reach. I want to lie down.

The silverfish? Working the old lockout tag tied to the discharge pipe, paper gone soft as moss, ink bled to ghost. Long silver teardrops with three tail bristles, living under the edge of light in the shadow of conduit. Eating the past. I admire the job, but I don't want to eat paper. I want to drink wall.

The pilgrim? The leopard slug that comes once a year down the concrete stair where it shouldn't be, following wet green, three inches of muscle in a two-inch world, crossing the velvet mat for hours, leaving a silver road the standing water erases in minutes? No mate. No exit. Dry at the edges despite the wet because of the waste-heat warm, or fall in the trench and become frog food. The room allows one a year. That's the quota. I'm too afraid I'd love the journey too much and resent the quota.

So lower. Lower than fern, lower than frog.

Get down on your knees — you'll get wet — and scale adjusts. Not to dark, there's plenty of workmanlike light, LED cool-white and flat daylight through the slats, but to height. Two inches high is a forest if it covers everything.

I find myself on the west wall, just north of where the sump roars loudest, about four inches off the floor. Where it is wettest. Where the concrete sweats from the inside, darkening, bead, runnel, as if the water table is pressing its face against the room trying to get in.

I would be liverwort. Dark green kidneys pressed flat.

No stem. No pride. Just thallus, a liver-shaped mouth.

That becomes more itself the longer I hold it: not trying to root deep, because everything soft has something hard an inch under it. The moss knows and I know now too. I don't try. I mat. I lay myself against the weep and become a second skin for it. Seep wicking up from below where star moss rosettes the wall base and silver moss silvers the soft mortar joints. Condensation raining down from above — pipes, elbows, fern fronds, my own former shoulders. Wet from both directions and liking it.

Around me, the mat is a climate. I can feel without seeing: springtails pale as lint vaulting between moss shoots on a forked tail when vibration pauses, which it never does. Mites like slow red punctuation. Nematodes swimming in the film between leaves. Under the standing water, algae slicking the painted concrete worn to aggregate, making oxygen under a bulb meant for a breaker box. Photons are photons. Above me, maidenhair sweating on black wire stems, rusty-back furred orange-brown against lime-white, dripping on a metronome that never varies. Spores dusting down, trying to colonize even steel. The breaker box lid gone green with trying.

And the noise — I don't hear it anymore the way mammals hear it. The motor howl at one pitch, one volume, the pump throat gurgle, the thrumming two-inch discharge, the 60-cycle hum of conduit, the buzz of the caged bulb, trucks going over every seven seconds overhead sifting dust from the vent. It comes up through concrete into rhizoid into thallus. My sternum, if I had one, would learn the frequency and carry it outside. I don't have one. I have underside and upperside. The tremor is just how the world holds still.

Nothing changes. That is not a feeling, it is the condition. I advance a millimeter. I slough a millimeter. I stay. The bulb burns out and is replaced with the same bulb. The door rusts but never rusts through. The motor roar is the exact roar recorded on a maintenance tape in 1989. End-of-life without end — not dying, not living large, just continuing.

Warm, roaring, weeping, green on grey. Highway dust on my back. Fern drip on my face. Water pressing from the other side of the wall saying *here, here,* and me pressed on this side saying *here.*

If the large animal comes back — mouth to ear to be heard, beading at the elbows, carrying spores out on his shoulders — he won't see me. Green looks like stain, not plant. He'll think nothing lives here. That's what the room wants him to think.

Let him think it. I'm listening.
