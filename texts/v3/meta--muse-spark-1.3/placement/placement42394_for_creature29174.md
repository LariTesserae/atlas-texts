At first I thought I would stay up on the rim.

Up where the fields are flat and dry and you can stand straight. Where the limestone is only a pale line under your boots and the air is cool. I thought I would be the visitor with the notebook, the one who looks down and sees all of it at once — rim to rim, wall to wall — and says *how strange, how total* and then goes back up for air.

But the damp gets in. Even up there. And the ground simply stops. And you go down into it because there is nowhere else to look.

So I go down. First switchback, second, the iron railing orange and green under my hand, slick. On the third the heat climbs up my legs. Five degrees, eight degrees. My shirt sticks. The roar finds my sternum before my ears understand it. And I have to stoop — cable, vine, hose, washing line all sagging low enough to touch with the head, a catwalk furred ankle-deep with grass. I stoop without deciding to.

I thought then, maybe I would be eyes. Pit eyes. I have good eyes for dim places. I would be the one who learns to see a clogged hairline crack by the way water beads, who can thin philodendron off a lit window without killing the root that is holding the wall. I would be useful with secateurs on a second-story bridge, letting lamplight breathe.

No. My hands prune too fast. Look — whorled white at the tips already. My feet splay in black water between the bricks. I am learning the pit-crouch, knees bent, head forward, shoulders rounded to shed water, but I am still listening like an outsider, still waiting for a dry surface.

Then I thought I would be ears. Or the lack of them. I would be a pump-man, stripped to the waist in boiler heat, oiled to the elbow, deafest of all. I would press my ear to sweating iron and listen for the tick that means a joint is cooling wrong. I would learn the hand slaps — two hits *clear the grate*, three hits *steam up*, palm in a circle *cut back* — because you can feel it through pipe when you cannot hear. I would sleep in the roar like womb-noise and panic in silence, because silence means drowning. That felt close. I am good at staying awake inside noise. I am good at tending something that must never stop.

But I am not the heart. Not yet.

I walk lower. Left is a house wall warm with boiler pipe. Right is a wall of living taro, stalks thick as wrists, leaves big as doors slapping my cheeks. A boy no taller than the taro falls it with a wet crunch. A woman above me thins vine. An old man at his threshold scrapes the night's fungus off his plank with the flat spade kept by the door — white sheets soft as bread — it will be back tomorrow. He nods at me without stopping. This is the second job. Not clearing, never clearing. Keeping.

If you stopped cutting for a week, the lanes would close.

If you stopped pumping for a day, the cellars would drown.

I pick up a hooked pole someone left against a yam pile. It fits. I fish leaves and skins out of the little canal, kneel in black water up to the wrists while it tugs. I learn that ivy on that cottage is load-bearing. You do not cut load-bearing ivy.

That is where I start to become more myself. Not visitor, not eyes, not heart. Something in between.

You would find me at the sump in the center at dusk, when the lamps come fully on — sodium orange and bare white — and the canopy above goes black and the weeping walls glitter all around like the inside of a well.

I am knee-deep in steam. Water pours in from a dozen mouths, clattering. The pumps throw it up in two fat iron throats over the rim to the dry fields where it steams faintly in cool air. Leaves drip. Someone shouts about a clogged screen and I am already moving with the pole because I felt it through the water before I heard it.

I am low and broad and wet. Balding not from age but from fungus, pink scalp shining. Bare chest stippled with heat rash and small round scars where boils were lanced — pit-pox from the lime water. Hair oiled flat. Rubber apron strapped close so leaves cannot snag it. Feet toed like a frog's for grip on slimed brick.

I am slow underneath, even while my arms move fast. Terrace speed. I will rebuild this same stair my grandfather rebuilt. I will re-hang this same pipe. I will let that thumb-thick root, if it is in the right place, become a beam by autumn and then learn to live around a beam.

I am half plant myself now. Moss is my mortar. I cough soft and wet and spit green into the canal. I know which spring opened in which drought year. I can read condensation running leaf-tip to leaf-tip to tell if rain is coming over the rim.

And I have taken an animal job, because there are no animals. No dogs, no chickens, no pigeons, no rats, no moths. I am the duck, the chicken scratching for grubs in moss, the rat combing thatch. I go barefoot up a drainpipe to dislodge fern clumps. I pick dead leaf out of a caged lamp with long nimble fingers so it will not smoke.

I do not look up at the thin pale line of the rim. There is nowhere else to go, and I know it. The pit holds heat. The pit holds water. The pit holds me — root, brick, skin, iron, all furred together into one breathing thatch.

Steam sighs off the release valve and condenses instantly on banana leaves, raining back down warm onto my shoulders, onto my upturned face. I glisten. Everything glistens.

That is where I am. That is what I am.
