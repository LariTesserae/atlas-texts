At first I don't know. If I come down into it, I keep wanting to keep my feet.

I think I would stay on the fused seams where it's safe, shouting between towers to hear the echo get lonely. I think I would be the visitor with damp shirt, drinking from leaf-tips, sleeping in the tower hollow while the hum gets into my dreams. But that doesn't hold. That image slides off. No one stays a visitor here. The air condenses on you whether you want it or not. After a while you are just another cold surface to drink from.

So let that go.

Maybe high? I think for a moment I would be high, because the light is up there, what light there is. A kite-hunter, three meters of almost nothing, reading air I already know how to read. Fold and drop through a ribbon. Scream thinly. Lay one leathery egg in a passing net and never return. No. That's too lonely and too hungry. I don't want to hunt by shadow. I don't want to never return.

Lower then. Faster. I think I would be a ribbon — pale gray, hand-wide, pouring like water thrown between towers. The dry-rain sound of my own wings. A pup clinging to my belly fur, damp and blind, learning turn as pour, not as turn. Drink drip-tips on the wing. Strain the air with combed feet. That's close — the joy in it, the tearing. But it's too fast. I would never get to stay anywhere long enough to learn the taste of a single drop held a second longer. The towers would blur. I want to stay.

Lower still. Hotter. The cracks where heat sighs out sweet and metallic. Copper flanks flashing. A crackling, lizard-metal, belly dull with stored heat, licking condensation off black glass before it wicks away. Eating the fallen dead of the chorus. Laying eggs like hot beads that hatch in hours. That's close too — quick, bright, ringing faintly when I move. But the ground is still a wound. Too sharp to be kind. I don't want to live looking up all my life at where the water comes from.

So not high, not fast, not low. Hanging.

That is what keeps returning. Hanging without ever touching ground.

Twenty-some meters up, on the north-east fin of a tower I will never name, where the skin runs a few degrees cold and sweats without stopping. Where the moss is thickest because the drip holds.

I am sinking into it now. I can feel it becoming more itself around me, and me becoming more itself inside it.

Black-green and copper braid. The Veil, forty meters long and never touching earth. It holds a drop a second longer before letting go — plip-plip-plip onto glass I will never touch. Above me an orchid big as a dinner plate, translucent, veined with stored amber dusk. It will open only in the stunned second after the city exhales. Below me my own seedlings, the ferns that unfurled overnight and collapsed by afternoon into black lace. The vines that do not climb but dangle and catch each other until they make a hammock. The air itself collecting in it: spores, shed skin, droppings, dead wings. The air is soil here. I breathe and taste chlorophyll and iron on my teeth.

I am in it up to my sides. I am sunk deep.

Now I know what I am. A fist. A throat.

A frog-throat, though I am not a frog. Wet muscle sunk in moss, throat-sac pulsing warning-orange, eyes like coals open in the dim. I do not go anywhere. My world is a meter of curtain, a drip line, a patch of glass-chorus shivering above me on the orchid underside.

I remember, the way my kind remembers in the body, being elsewhere briefly: flickering an inch deep in a ground pan a meter wide, a tadpole-thing among mosquito larvae writhing in endless S, under duckweed and fallen petals. A world that existed for a day. At night we clicked faintly. Then we rose in a furious column, brief adults, to join the Veil. I found this braid, I sank, I swelled. I will never go back down. The pan evaporated behind me. It still sings in me.

What I do is this: I pump.

Alone it would be comic. One bass note you feel in the molars. In ten thousand it becomes weather, a pressure on the chest. I pump with the others until individuality burns off. The glass chorus lays its metallic sheen over us — clicks, saws, stutters from six glassy wings shaking condensation onto mouthparts. The ribbons tear through with their dry rain. Higher up, the knives ride heat. Under all of it, the hum in the basalt, in my belly where I press against moss. The Keepers I will never see.

I live by listening, because despite the deafening there are patterns and my flesh knows them before thought:

When my own bass and my neighbors' drops a third, without deciding, I swell a little tighter into the moss. Rain-that-isn't-rain is about to sheet off the east towers.

When the moth-bats go silent in a moving patch, I close my eyes to slits. A kite is inside that silence, folding. Hunters hunt by shadow. The curtains are full of eyes, and I am one of those eyes, and eyes draw knives. I do not move. To be still is to live.

When the hum steps up a quarter-tone — I feel it first as a tremble in the curtain, in my throat-sac — I duck my head under the orchid lip and cover my mouth. It is about to get wetter. The tower will exhale a fresh sheet of condensation outward, the curtains will tremble, and we will all stop — one stunned heartbeat of absolute quiet so complete it hurts, the orchids opening white above me — and then roar back twice as loud.

That silence is my church. I live for it.

Sometimes near the base below me, where the maintenance field is strongest, mist rises in slow spirals instead of settling. Dust hangs motionless. I have seen a pebble thrown by something like you fall too slowly, as if considering. Sometimes the tower I cling to glitches — its upper third stutters, doubles, goes transparent to a sky that is not this sky, then resolves with a soft chime that is immediately swallowed by our roar. Birds that fly through come out reversed for a few wingbeats, then correct, unperturbed. I do not correct. I hold.

A fin near me was broken once by a kite's impact. I watched the motes come — too small, too purposeful to be dust, bright — swarm the wound and weave for an hour until fresh pearlescent edge extruded, sharp as a razor. One landed on my eyelid, tasted my sweat, left. Uninterested. I am not infrastructure. I am not damage. I am weather that sings.

You, if you come — you barely register. You are a warm movement far below on a fused seam, another cold surface to condense on. Your shirt beads. Leaves drip steadily from their tips. Your shout gets lonely between immensities. At dusk that never lifts, under the faint hexagonal shimmer dimming the sun to a diffuse smear, you look up and see towers vanishing into low cloud, veiled in green, sweating, ringing.

I look down, if I look, with warning-orange eyes, and pump my bass note into the pressure on your chest.

I do not need you. I barely notice you, except as salt in the air-soil.

I sweat, I sing, I eat myself and grow.
