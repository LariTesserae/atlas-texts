At first I think I'll stay up top. Kneeling. Human.

It's what I know how to do — lie on my belly at the scalloped lip, palms on blood-warm stone, panting too much of the shared air, letting my eyes stay useless for a while. Witness. That's safe. The huge cold twilight goes on above me and the warm knot breathes below me and I don't have to choose.

But I can't stay there. My breathing is wrong. I'm taking more than my share, and the stone is faintly slick under me, never dry, and a moth has already mistaken my sweat for rain. I press too hard. I want to shine a light straight down to see. I know I shouldn't — the curtain would lift all at once — but the wanting is in me. The human at the rim always wants to illuminate.

I think, then, I could be the raven. That's flattering. Big, blue-black, clever enough to bring a tool and leave it behind. Probe the upper cracks with a bark-stripped stick worn smooth and damp at the tip, pry out what I need, hammer it open with a basalt pebble and go. Use without staying. Intelligence that touches the pit and withdraws.

But I can't stand what the raven can't stand for the same reason: I don't want to withdraw. I want to stay in the press. And my wings — if I have them — are too wide for the shaft. I'd scrape. I'd panic at the screaming corkscrew when the swifts leave. No. Not the visitor who lives here by not living here.

Lower, then.

Could I wedge? For an hour I try to be swift. Upright, breast to stone, tail braced, claws hooked into invisible pits, shoulder to shoulder like knives in a block. White collar flashing. But swifts are built entirely for a sky they have abandoned for the day, and I am not built for speed. I am not nervous, quick, irritable in confinement in that way. I can't doze with one eye open, shuffling a centimeter forward, a centimeter back, waiting only for blue hour to unhook all at once and be gone into speed. I don't want to be gone.

Could I hang? Deeper, where it is blackest and warmest and the ammonia thickens. Breathe together. Not in metaphor — literally. Flanks rising and falling in slow waves that travel across the curtain. Map each other in the dark with needle-clicking too fast and too crowded, bouncing off close rock and live bodies and coming back wrong. I love that — awake in sleep, mapping. For a while I think, yes, this is me. The hot core. Ears twitching.

But I am not furred. I do not fold so neatly into myself, wings wrapped like cloaks. And to be bat here is to never be alone in your own breath. My breathing doesn't synchronize that easily. I jostle too much. I would fall, crawling over backs with folded wrists.

Could I be still enough to be moth? Flat to the basalt, wings folded upright and together over the back like dry leaves shut against rain, forewings mottled to vanish. Head-up. Fringes touching. Fat stored in a thick abdomen ticking down slowly. Economy is stillness. I try. I lay myself against the bellied wall where it is dim and warm and still, antenna lifting, testing, laying back down.

I last minutes. Not hours. The tremor gets me. When one flutters a hundred lift, settle, dust sifting off smelling of chaff and oil, and I lift with them. I can't conserve. I want to taste.

So I sink a little sideways, a little down, to where the rock is already varnished dark and slick. Where guano has lacquered the ledges and on it grows a pale fungus like frost. Where the light that was barely light is now truly gone.

You'd find me there. Not at the lip with the orange lichen and the boiled-leather shrub. Not in the cracks with the swifts. Not in the living curtain. Among and beneath.

I am pale. The color of old ivory left in tea. The length of a man's thumb. My abdomen is glossy and swollen and looks wrong for this height — too soft, too naked. My hind legs are folded like jackknives. Absurd tiny feet splayed on vertical rock that should not hold me. And it does.

I move the way time moves here underneath the frenzy. Lift one leg. Pause an eternity. Set it down. Lift another.

This is becoming more itself now. The slowness isn't laziness. It's how you live when the air is more animal than air and every move moves someone else. Pages in a book can't turn fast.

My antennae are impossibly long. Longer than sense. In the dark where eyes are beads reflecting nothing, I sweep and sweep. That's how I see. Not with light — never with light — but with taste on the air. Salt. Ammonia. Lichen. Chaff-oil-dust from a moth settling. Musk-feather from a swift shuffling a centimeter. Warmth rising when the mass below stirs, as if the pit notices.

I am the cleaner. The opportunist. I eat what falls. A moth wing. A stunned moth. Fungus, frass. I work the varnish with black mandibles strong enough to shear chitin. Patient, but not monk-patient like the blind beetles smaller than sesame seeds down in the frost-fungus who will never know the mouth exists. I know the mouth exists. At night I venture higher toward the rim-cups to drink the condensation before it evaporates. Drip, pause, drip. I know there is sky held for an hour in black mirrors no bigger than a cupped hand. I've drunk sky.

When a raven's shadow crosses, I freeze deeper into stillness, becoming lump, becoming stone. I know the bark-stripped stick probing, damp at the tip. I have smelled it. Use without architecture. The intelligence in the beak, the moment, not in staying. I've lost brothers to it. I do not hammer. I am hammered open. That's fair. That's the web folded into a room.

If you lower your head over the edge long enough, you'll hear me — not me, exactly, but the layer I'm in. Wing on wing, claw on stone. The sound presses. Echoes come back a half-beat too late, as if sound has to travel further down there than rock allows.

And if — after your eyes have learned nothing — you put your finger near me, close over the bellied wall where I cling, I will not hop. There is nowhere to hop.

I will turn my blind-seeming face toward your warmth and wave my antennae over you, tasting salt.
