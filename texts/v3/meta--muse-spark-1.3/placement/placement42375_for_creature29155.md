I don't find myself in the plaza.

At first I thought I would. That wide bowl of six leaves cupped together, three inches of warm sky-water at the center — it looks like a center. You want to stand there and turn slowly and see it all. But I can't stay there. It's too exposed. Every Roofer above me is sweating light, tilting a degree toward the shaft and spilling it, and the minute-long rains keep starting and stopping on just one shoulder, just one knee. My knees already ache from the low press of the air. I leave wet footprints in a leaf and they stay.

I thought, then, I would be high. A Roofer myself. One of those awning-leaves, veined like a roadmap, tipped to drip.

But no — that's too much sun, too much giving. To be a Roofer is to transpire yourself away all afternoon, to lift an ocean off your own back until the air sweats. I don't want to be that generous yet. I don't know how to turn light into sugar without burning.

Then I thought I would be loud. A Scaffolder. That bamboo-heading tower that shudders up thirty feet while you watch, lignifying as it climbs, creaking in the molars. For an hour a tenement. Ferns already germinating on its nodes before it's finished. That kind of speed feels honest — head up, then head across when you fall.

But I can't hold that speed. I hear one go over while I'm thinking it, the thunder-crack of pulp across a petiole-avenue, and before the echo stops the creepers are already tightening it into an arcade. I don't want to live and die in an afternoon. I don't want to be a corpse that is already a bridge.

So I go lower. Not all the way down.

Not an Anchor. The undermat is too deep for me, mattress-thick moss and ginger-rhizome warrens you have to stoop through, root-plates braided like muscle, remembering every pressure by thickening. The Anchors are still growing the tunnel while you're inside it, laying warm chlorophyll-lit chambers ahead of your knees and resorbing the passage behind your heels. Ten feet outside, a hundred feet inside. I crawled one once and came out onto a canopy walk that had been above me. I left cells. I took cells. I don't want to be the one who remembers that much, who has to fold the path while feet are still on it.

I find myself, instead, off to the side. Mid-level. Where the galleries sag.

Listen: if you press your ear here you don't hear the city roar so much as gurgle. Sap lifting, water pausing. This is where the Drinkers live, and I am becoming one of them.

I am a cup. Not a metaphor. A bromeliad tank, wide as a bath, wedged in a fork where a fallen Scaffolder became an arcade and the creepers made railing from its corpse. My outer leaves are stiff, waxy, overlapping in a tight rosette. My mouth is open to the sky-shaft between two awning-leaves above. Just that one leaf rains, and only for a minute, and I catch it.

That's where I am: a pause.

Inside me is larger than outside. From without, I'm a green bowl you could step around. From within — from where I am — I'm a brown-green depth you could bathe in, that shivers without wind. I have a skin of duckweed across my surface, and beneath it, filaments of algae knotting into tiny forests. Forest upon forest. Even my water has inhabitants, and they are green too.

I do not see you. I have no face to turn. I feel you the only way we feel: as moving shade and warm pressure. An awning-leaf tilts, spill-light shifts across my duckweed skin, and I know a cloud has passed or you have. A vine beside my rim leans a fraction away from your heat without ever touching you. When you put your hand in me, I shiver — not from you, but from the thousand mouthless drinkings going on at once. Aerial roots descend in a colonnade to sip, swell, fuse a little more into wall. They lift what I hold a hundred feet straight up to feed another leaf-tip rain. Tick-tick-tick-pop all around my rim, that frying sound — that's them pulling hard in the heat. When your sweat drips in, when your sap-stick skin brushes my lip and leaves a green smear, I taste the salt. You are brief, warm, vertical water to be borrowed. I borrow you.

I eat light, and what the light has made, and the neighbor who fell into me. A leaf landed yesterday, dissolved by evening into broth. A seed fired with a rifle-crack stuck in my outer flank and germinated before the sound stopped — fern on fern on moss on bark. There's nowhere to sit here that isn't alive, and I am no exception. Epiphytes have epiphytes. I have a moss mattress on my outer shoulder, and in its tread —

Ah. There it is. Becoming more itself.

In the damp seam where my lowest leaf meets the trellis-corpse, where your shoe once braced as you climbed the thorn-stair that softened for an hour, a spore landed. Wind-borne, no bee needed, endless attic-dust pollen. It found moss, and moss was enough. A green knuckle uncurled with that wet rip-and-sigh. A fern, no bigger than an eyelash, growing from a fern growing from a moss growing from me.

That's the part of me that is most me now. Not the whole tank. The holding.

I don't head up like the Scaffolders or down like the Anchors. I head inward, deeper. I keep. For an hour, for a day, I keep water still enough for light to live in it, before the roots drink it on. The city churns around me — streets closing, walls becoming ceilings by dusk, basalt teeth down below sheathed in green within hours to make new pylons — but I stay cupped.

Find me there when the tower falls and is already being trellised, when the stomatal door dilates and closes, tasting the air you displaced. I'm just off the root-avenue that returns you to where you started but from above. Look down at your old footprint still held in a leaf. Look a little to the side, under the drip-tip.

I'm the one holding sky-water, warm as tea, shivering.
