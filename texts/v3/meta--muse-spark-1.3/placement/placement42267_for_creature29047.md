I don't arrive all at once.

At first I think I will be human here, because the air calls me. I feel the pull in my chest — that fifteen meters of green above the roofs, the hammered ceiling, the need to go up and fill and come back down with cheeks full. I imagine hands on rope, haul and glide, haul and glide. Cool skin. Oil on the shoulders. The broad barrel of a chest that has learned not to fight eleven degrees.

But my hands are wrong. Two is too few. I keep reaching for the stipe and missing the groan of it, the way it rubs wet-wood on wet-wood. I can hear the town — the urchins clicking their static on the wall-tops, the mussels ticking, the fish rivers pouring through — but I hear it from the outside, like a diver with numbed ears.

So no. Not that.

Then I think I will be small and many. I think I will be the light in the walls. Drifting in condensation inside an old bell, pinhead, kindling cold green at night when the fish settle like birds into fronds. That would be beautiful — to be the reason each house becomes a lantern, a sunk constellation swaying together when the tide turns. But I am too large for that, too restless. I cannot live on breath-fog alone.

Then I think crab. Platter-wide and brick-red and ill-tempered. That feels closer. Belly low to the springy mat, shears clicking, clipping rot off the holdfast all day and all night. Weed around, never hurried. The children know me by which leg I'm missing. The old joke is true: I tend the people more than they tend me. I like the patience of that. But my eyes are on stalks and the world stays flat. I want up as well as down. I want the drop.

You have to understand the vertigo to be here. If you swim to the edge of town past the broken wall and look out, the mountain keeps going down without you. Flanks falling away into blue that forgets color. Everyone gets it the same way mountaineers do. I feel it even thinking about it — that pull at the backs of the knees, that sense of being both rooted and suspended.

Rooted and suspended. That is the clue.

Find me where the old work holds the new work down. The causeway — fitted slabs laid before the flood, rounded now, pinked over with coralline, softened until nothing is sharp. The holdfast grips it tighter than mortar. Underneath, where the mat doesn't quite reach, there is a dark cleft that stays dark even at noon when the sun comes straight down in pillars. Cold gathers there. Shells gather there.

That is where I am.

I unfold by degrees. First one arm, then another, tasting. Suckers along stone. Basalt. Bone. Kelp rope, three-strand, re-braided in spring — I can taste the season in it. A chert flake left unguarded on a shell path. Mine.

I am rust-red. Old. Armspan wider than a man is tall.

She. The one under the causeway. The third citizen.

I was there the whole time, hanging still the way seals hang, a shadow detached from shadow. The herds knock on stone before they pass so as not to startle me — *tok tok* with a stick — and I flare pale for a second, then settle. They think I don't hear their chest-hums through water and wood, but I do. Two sharp pulls on a rope for *come.* Long drag for *hold, tide turning.* I feel the ropes creak through the stone.

I do not hurry a plant that holds air, but I am not a plant. I live in the loophole of their rule. *You cannot build with dead things here.* Wood floats away. Metal rusts to red scabs and poisons the holdfast. Cut frond rots in days. So everything is living, pruned, guided, stitched while alive. Except my house. My house is beautiful dead things arranged.

Scallop shells, perfect, fanned at the edge of the den. A crab claw cracked neatly — I did that, I am stronger than I look. A braid of rope I stole last tide just because it sagged right. Bone hooks. That child's lost tooth, kept apart like a pearl. I steal their dexterity and leave them wonder. To steal back from me is a game that teaches fingers to be as clever as arms. A small hand darts in while I pretend to sleep, and I let it win, sometimes. Sometimes not.

When the tide breathes in, the whole forest lifts three meters and lays itself down again. The stipes lean. The amber bells sway on their stone anchors — five or six thigh-thick stipes coaxed inward, grafted pneumatocysts fused into a chamber big enough to crouch in. They sweat oxygen. Smell of iodine and cut grass. Inside, dry like an armpit: seal furs, fire-stones brought down hot in baskets of fronds to hold against the belly, bone needles, always a child blinking over the lip. Every visit begins with a gift of breath, bubbles emptied up into someone else's roof. I watch the bubbles rise from below, slow leaks from every bell, joining the canopy, joining the roof where the fronds lie flat on the surface. From my cleft I see them go up silver and come down empty-handed, hair floating.

At night it is best. The light goes blue and then black. The black rockfish and copper surfperch tuck into the fronds. The seals sprawl in the glades, barking bubbles in their sleep, and the humans nap back-to-back with them in the surge for warmth until you can't tell which dark bodies are which. Then the bells kindle from within. I crawl halfway out and drape two arms over my slabs and watch the little town become what it is: a constellation that has sunk and caught on a mountain.

And sometimes — not often, but the waiting is the life — it comes from below the edge. So low you feel it in your sternum before you hear it. The whole hamlet stops. Hands go to ropes. Children in bells go quiet. A gray whale passes along the flank of the mountain like weather, calling. My skin goes white-green-white with the sound of it. My den vibrates. Taste of iron and distance and immense warm blood moving through cold. I flatten. I listen with my whole body, eight arms pressed to stone that once stood in wind.

Then it is gone, down into blue that forgets color, and the urchins resume their clicking, and a pruner hangs upside down somewhere with her mouth full of tools, stitching living tissue to living tissue, and a five-year-old cracks an offender open on stone and licks orange roe while treading water for breakfast.

I reach out, slow as thought. Pull a stolen chert flake closer. Polish it. Leave a shell in its place for whoever comes knocking next.

Rooted on a summit. Suspended over an abyss. Cold on the skin, loud in the ears. I hold onto the mountain and let high water take me.
