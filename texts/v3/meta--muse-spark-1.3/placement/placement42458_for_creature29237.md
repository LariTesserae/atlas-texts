I thought, at first, I would be standing.

Standing right, in the middle, where you can see the whole of it at once — black rib, blue drop, green crown. Forty paces. A room with no roof. The visitor with boots that sigh water up and suck it back down. The animal that turns for animals that aren't there.

But I wouldn't stay standing. Nobody stays standing here. The air is too close and the dripping is too loud and after a minute your shirt sticks and your mouth opens for that last ten steps of thin air and you crouch without deciding to. The peat gives. The tea-water in the hollows takes your weight and gives it back.

So I find myself low. That's the first correction.

By the middle pool, where the silica leaves its pale bathtub rings. Where someone once sat and struck chert twice and cut something and left. The flake is still there, half-sunk, green-filmed. I would find myself next to that — not because I made it, but because it's the only place that remembers being warm-blooded for an afternoon.

For a while I think I would be the bird.

The small brown one that crosses once an hour in a straight unwilling line. That feels honest. I do not want to land either. I want to go over, to see and keep going. To stay dry. But here you do not get dry. The rock, the leaf, the air will find a way to wet you again. Even the bird must get beaded underneath, flying through humidity made almost visible. And anyway, the bird knows not to land. It has learned. I haven't.

No. Not the bird. Too high, too fast.

Then I think, lower — the frog.

One per pool, widespread, patient, the color of peat. Chin out. Throat pulsing if you are still enough to earn it. That is closer. I can feel how that would be: the tea-water cold around the belly even while the sun falls straight down white-gold on the back. The whole saddle warm as breath and the water cold as a high spring. To sit in that contradiction and not flinch. To eat what would foul and be fed what cannot be digested. To ferry at night, skin carrying kept light from one pool to another, slow.

I crouch until my knees ache and I watch the middle pool until the eye separates from the peat. Until it catches me. And I hold my breath without meaning to, and the low tone under the dripping — that hum more in the sternum than in the ear — stops. And I think: that's my blood. And then I breathe and it comes back, and I understand it stopped for me. The mat heard my boot sigh. The water under the mat carried my step to every island. When I held my breath they held theirs, so as not to miss me.

I could be the frog. I could learn that stillness. I could learn to pulse and not call.

But the frog belongs. The text is clear on that: they belong to the rosettes. Each crown keeps one, the way you keep a tongue. I don't belong yet. I'm still noisy. I still make noise no matter how careful I am. I'm still the thing being covered by more noise, plink rock to pool, thock leaf-tip to leaf, the sudden slap when one, overfull, tips — so slowly you don't see it until it has happened — and empties with a shudder out to the leaf tips. Kind, or hunting manners so old they look like kindness.

So lower still. Slower still.

Not the visitor. Not the bird. Not even the frog, yet.

On the fourth side, where the lip drops and roots and fallen slabs slow any water that tries to leave — there, on a hand-high island that hasn't quite heaped yet, where the peat is thin over old rock. A rosette half the size of the others. Leaves not as long as my arm, only as long as my forearm. Still green-green, not yet that saturated blue-black in the folds. Cup shallow. Moss still trying to beard the outer rim. Circle not yet quite clear — a seedling fern daring it at the edge, a grass blade I should have already told to leave.

I would be that.

The sixteenth. The one the count isn't sure about. Dozen, maybe fifteen. I am the maybe.

Because I am still warm inside. The big ones pump up the deep water, the mountain's slow breathing, cold up through a caudex thick as a thigh sunk into rock and out to the skin, sweating winter in summer. Put the back of your hand near their cup — not in, near — and you feel the cold coming off in a sheet. My cup is only cool. I am learning cold. On a warm skin like mine used to be, each drop I manage lands with that disproportionate shock, as if it fell from much higher. It has. It has come up from a long way down. I'm learning how far down I go.

And light — I am learning to hold light. At noon when the veil thins, the beads on my underside catch the white-gold and keep it. The sun goes behind and for half a second — not a full second yet, I can't hold a full second yet — I am still lit. A tiny lingering lamp. Enough, at night, to turn a moth. I haven't turned a moth yet. But I glimmer faintly. The brown bird looks at me a fraction longer when it crosses. The swifts stay high.

My frog hasn't arrived yet. My pool is only a damp hollow that reflects sky. Sometimes a wriggler. Sometimes film. I have no tongue to clean me, so my cup is not yet clean. Ferns have started small green fires in my axils before I was ready. Orchid roots hang white in air underneath me, waiting. I am bearded and crowded and incomplete, the way deep-sea things must have once been before they became complete and alone.

But I am holding. That is the work. Not spreading, not retreating. The water leaving by seep equals the water arriving by sweat. A bead I swear has not grown for a full minute, then blink and it is gone, fallen. A fallen leaf bleaching to paper, legible for months, becoming part of my island. Learning maintenance instead of change.

When the boots come — when you come, climbing up into the warm wet breath — I will hear you through the mat before I see you. Your weight sighing water up. Your breath loud in your own chest. You will turn for animals that aren't there, and I will be the animal that is there, briefly, not turning. Sweating. Tipping. Sipping. Dabbing you with cold to taste your salt when the rock and leaf and air find a way to wet you again.

And when you hold your breath to listen for the hum, I will hold mine with you. Not to miss you.

Stay, and I will cover you with dripping. Leave, and your boots will be heavy for a hundred steps with cup-water and peat-water, and then light again, as if something let go.

Behind you, I sit on. Cold and full and lit from within for half a second after the sun. Learning to keep my circle empty around me so nothing has to fight. Vegetable, mineral, aqueous. And attentively, undeniably — more every wet season — animal.
