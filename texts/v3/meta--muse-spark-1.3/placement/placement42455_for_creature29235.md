I have to duck to get in, and I am bad at it at first.

I come in too fast, too tall. My head finds the lintel. My shoulders brush both sides of the low door and I bring a little of the outside in with me — wind in my shirt, sun on my back, sweat already standing on me. Inside, it stays. The heat takes that wind off me in one breath and does not give it back.

For a while I am only the wrong size. That is where I find myself first: stuck in the doorway, kneeling.

The floor gives under my knees and I notice it before I notice anything else, because I am afraid I will break it. Reed over rush over split palm rib, lashed and patched, hollow underneath. Joists knocking softly somewhere below in the dark where the warm air comes up. I am too heavy for a basket on stilts. I distribute my weight without thinking, hands out, and my palm lands in puff-moss — it squeaks and springs back — and on hot wood, and on something furred that withdraws.

I am too wet, too. That is the second place I find myself.

Everyone else here beads and dries. Their salt stays white on their upper lips. My sweat keeps coming. It runs. It darkens the mat under my wrist in a small, spreading ruin. I stare at that stain and understand what they mean before anyone tells me: *what is dry keeps its shape. What is wet forgets.* I have brought forgetting in with me. I cup my hands to catch the next of it. I hold my breath to keep my breath from fogging anything.

So I stop moving. The heat teaches that fast. There is nowhere to extend an arm without touching three textures, so I don't extend it. I sit where I knelt, fold my legs, make myself beam-ducking small by stooping even while sitting. I let my eyes adjust to the smallness of the scale.

If I could be anything easily, I would be a threadbird. No bigger than a thumb, slipping in and out of a pocket-nest woven straight into the curtain without rustling. But I rustle. The lantern-husks rattle with spores when my shoulder brushes them. A copper-backed loom-beetle stops in its line across the mat and waits out my tremor. So not that.

If I could be anything quietly, I would be one of the reed-colored geckos, flattened to a post, throat pulsing, moving only when no one blinks. But I blink too often. The air drinks. My eyes gum. I am seen. So not that, either. Not the dust-mice on their pale worn circuit along the joist, not the pallid crickets combing moss under the platforms, not the slow-worms coiled breathing in the gloom. I am too loud, too large, too new. The fresh-cut ends everywhere — pale, splinter-bright, smelling of sap turned to resin — they all look made this morning, and I look made this second, unfinished, leaking.

So I watch, which is to stop looking for a place and let a place look for me.

The light helps. It comes through the dense thatch as a thousand small knives and coins, and at noon it should pool straight down. It doesn't. It pools sideways, climbing up the posts in bright rungs. I follow one rung with my eyes up a post latticed with bottle-vine and air-fern and jump-thistle corkscrews, and I see the duplication: the same charm-knot twice, same fray, same little green-edged copper hook, one a finger's width left of the other, as if the post hesitated.

A threadbird comes in with down in its beak and lands on the left hook. Never the right.

I put my hand out, slowly, to the right hook. Nothing happens. The metal is warm. That is mine, I think. The right hook is for the likes of me — the ones who still walk in straight lines and cannot yet be trusted with the fold.

Because the house is folded and I keep feeling it in my body before I understand it. I duck under a beam hung with nests to let an old one pass — no taller than a storage bin, skin cured to split-reed polish at the elbows, hair felted and gone green-grey with moss — and I emerge a hand's breadth higher than I started, though the floor is level. Behind a sleeping platform hung with woven vine-leaf curtains there is a garden row, and behind the garden row another sleeping platform that should already be outside the waxed wattle oval. Forty paces wall to wall, and I cannot walk to the wall.

I lie down to stop trying. Under the swollen old thatch at the center, on my back, leaves brushing my cheek dry as cloth, dense as fur. Above me not roof but another floor — mats, feet, the undersides of pinched clay bins — very far away and very close. The hamlet stitched over itself. A pot knocked somewhere emits a dull *tok* and then nothing. The weave drinks sound. The loudest things are near and tiny: tick of carapace, dry *shush* of a husk turning in a draft I cannot feel, my own breath catching on dust.

That is where I finally find myself. On my back. Out of the way.

An old woman crouches nearby — or I have blinked and she is nearby — with a clay bowl of beetle-shell buttons in her lap, sewing a torn vine-leaf curtain back to itself with fiber pulled from her own cuff. Slip-knot for a beam that still grows. Hard square-knot for dead wood. The little looped charm-knot wherever a vine has been trained to turn. Her hands never stop, even when she looks at me. Especially then.

I hold still to be useful. That becomes my work. My hands are too big for knots — my fingers leave damp on twine — so she does not give me twine at first. She gives me holding. Hold this runner here while I coax the holdfast, still white, around the new post. Hold this mat edge while I re-lash. Hold your breath while the gecko crosses the empty patch.

The empty patch. The geckos flatten themselves at its edge and will not cross, where the air shimmers faintly with heat that isn't heat. At ambering time, when there is no dusk only a slow ambering of the slats and the husks begin to glow from within, steady as embers, I see why: a smaller, neater footfall overlapping the dust-mice's pale path, too light to wear, keeping step. The menders slipping a finger's width sideways. Unfolding a little when they need room and refolding when they are done.

She sees me seeing. She presses warm wax into a crack with her thumb and then, without a word, presses a little into the cracked heel of my hand where I have been leaning on joist-wood. It holds. It keeps its shape.

I learn to leave my palm a long time on a bottle-vine stem until I feel it very slowly tighten, by fractions, following the sun through the weave. I learn to suck oil from a bottle-vine knuckle through a reed and not to ask for water. To eat husk-meat dry as paper, spore-dust. To move like the slow things, and then only when someone blinks. My sweat still comes, but less, and dries faster. A white mustache I have to look close to see.

I am not a builder. I am not yet a mender. I am the mended thing for a while.

At night the whole basket inhales the day's heat and holds it, and a soft creak runs around the waxed wattle oval in a circle that never quite completes — them pulling it tight, not to keep the world out but to keep this small warm dry lushness from spilling, from unfolding, from remembering how big it really is. I lie with leaves at my cheek and feel the tightening, and I tighten with it, by fractions.

If I stay — and I am staying, I am curing, my knuckles polishing from handling, moss taking in the hair I have stopped shaving — I know where I will go. Tucked in a dimmer pocket under a platform, where the pallid crickets comb, hand still curled around a post-holdfast. Not dead. Nothing here rots to teach me what dead is. Cured. Sharpened. Become trellis.

A new air-fern unrolling in a stiff spiral from my old knuckle, never unfurling all the way.
