I would not find myself at first.

Coming over the rim, I would look for where to belong and see only the plan — too large, too empty, too straight. Two causeways crossing in standing water. A plaza for thousands holding twelve. Houses spread so thin the green takes them back between one and the next. I would walk down thinking I was still a visitor, with visitor's feet, stumbling on the sharp scoria, listening without meaning to for something that is not here.

For a week I would still listen. For a dog, for insects, for a hinge. For someone to call out.

Then I would stop.

Where I would find myself is not at the crossing. Not at the cistern, though I go there every morning now to learn the level. Not in a house — I have one, low walls to the waist and reed above, thatch ticking one degree cooler at night, gourds hanging, porch narrow — but I am rarely in it except to sleep on the raised mat above the damp.

You would find me hundred yards from any house. That is the rule here. Nothing crowds.

Shin-deep. That is where I am most often.

There is a ditch I tend with three taro beds on one side and a young sweet potato mound on the other. The sluice between them is only a plank in a wooden frame, silvery with wet, adze-smoothed. In the morning the mist condenses out of nothing and the banana leaf beside me pings water down, plink, plink, into standing water. I lift the plank an inch. I watch. I set it down. I lift it again. The hiss changes. That is enough conversation for an hour.

I thought, at first, I would be a builder. The work is visible everywhere half-finished — a wall going up course by course, hammer-pitted basalt, a new canal with spoil still wet beside it — and I wanted to leave something straight. But building here is not raising. It is seating. You set a block, you wet the joint, you look at it from both sides, you leave it for rain to settle. My hands were too impatient.

I thought I would be a sorter in the plaza, with the old people, learning tubers by feel, because I liked the idea of sitting dry on the edge of the stepped platform while children kicked their feet. But my feel was not yet in my fingertips. My hands were still dry-handed underneath, still expecting gloves, still wiping themselves.

What emerged, slowly, was smaller and wetter.

At noon when the gourd flowers open white and brief on the trellis I mended badly, I go plant to plant. I did not decide to. The old man who showed me without speaking — just came close and spoke low, three words, breathed audibly in the thin warm air — left his feather-on-a-stick leaning there one day and I picked it up. Then I put it down and used my fingertip, because the fingertip learns pressure better.

Touch inside, do not crush. Move pollen where wind won't. Next flower. Next. Squat so you do not splash. Do not hurry, hurrying muddies water.

That is who I would be. Not a name that matters. The bowl does not date by names.

I am one of the animals that did not come up the cliffs, so I became it with my own body. Pollinator. Heron. Frog with bare splayed feet gone yellow-grey and thick, finding stone to stone without looking down. Manure — carrying rotted green and ash in a coiled basket tight enough to hold mud, night-soil to the far mound away from the cistern, nothing wasted because nothing comes up except on a back.

My clothes never dry. I have stopped negotiating. Seam at the hem dark and heavy, skin beaded, hair stuck. Palms pale and ridged always, as if just from bathing. Feet the opposite, toughened. I wear a reed cape when the mist thickens, but more often I just get wet. It is the same temperature as my skin. After a minute you stop noticing temperature at all.

You would pass me on the main causeway and almost miss me, a hundred yards off through reeds. A soft knock of wood, a footstep in mud, water taking it back. I would not call out. Sound dies at twenty yards here. I would nod.

In the evening I am on the porch while the tiny fire under the lean-to boils taro. Smoke rises straight up a few feet and dissolves. No lamp. There is nothing to see after dark that can't wait for plain midday light. The thatch drips even when it has not rained. The rim waterfalls thin to white threads. The springs weep at the same rate. The cistern stays full to the brim.

I sleep early. I do not listen for birds anymore.

If you stay, as the bowl asks you to stay, you narrow: stone underfoot, water at the ankle, green at the waist, warm air, far black rim holding it all up out of the clouds. And a job for your fingers that an animal would have done, down below, without thinking.

I tend my level. I keep it.
