I come down to it the way you do, at first. Too fast. Too dry.

Boots splashing in an inch of warm water over slabs. Breath too loud inside my own head. Eyes looking up by habit — for sky, for bird, for anything that moves quick and clean in air. There is nothing to see up there but kettle-lid white and pipe and vine.

So I don't stay up there.

At first I think I could be a visitor who learns to be quiet. Stand still in the canal shiver by the sluice until the roar becomes pressure on the skin and the orange sodium pools resolve. Be patient. Watch. Leave no ripple but drip-ripples. That would be a way to be here.

But it wouldn't last. I breathe. I am loud in my joints. My skin cells fall off me into the gin-clear water and something down there has to clean up after me. My footsteps are an irritation in the fur. A governor near the red trembles when I pass. I can't stay as that — the dry animal with eyes and hunger, wanting to fix the flywheel or oil the cups or chew a living leaf just to taste it.

Could I be a flier, then? For a minute I want to be. To circle the caged lamps the way a mind wants to circle a light. But I know what happens. Ten wingbeats and the wings load. The air here is uninhabitable if you need to stay dry to stay up. I would flutter, sodden, fall to the oil-sheen and duckweed, stick, soften into pulp. In the morning there would be only two dusted wings in an eddy. The lamps would stay clean.

So not that. Lungs are a liability here. Wings are suicide. Ears tuned for birdsong burst in the 60-cycle pressure.

I crouch. That is what the place teaches. Look down, not up.

If I look long enough at the inch of water breathing with heat over the cobbles, the film resolves for me too. Opalescent, olive-brown, trembling. Biofilm laying itself down in sheets, digesting soot and petals and lamp-dust and iron bleed. White woodlice herding in the drip-line, everting their guts onto rot. Glass flatworms the color of weak tea folding themselves over a fallen canna petal. Tubifex knots waving in unison like tiny anemones in the black silt where the slabs have lifted.

I try that on. To be small and pale and blind as a thumb. To eat only what dissolves. Fallen petals, fern spores, fly wings — the dead rain continuously, there is no need to bite the living. To ride the meniscus in a raft and eat spores that would otherwise mold. It is a good contract. Be wet or die. Eat what dissolves. Do not chew what lives.

I could almost do it. I know how to dissolve things externally and reabsorb them. But I move too much. Even slowed, I hunt. I want the living leaf, not just the fallen one.

I lift — very slowly, without splashing — the edge of a lifted slab where the canal is knee-deep and tea-dark and warm as blood. Underneath, in the moss-root tangle: slate-pale, almost translucent, gills like drowned ferns fanning in the current. A siren a foot long, eyeless, or eyed and unseeing. It lies indistinguishable from a root all day and at night walks on four soft hands to take tubifex and drowned moths. Silent, sodden, patient. It breathes through skin and feather.

I want to be that for a while. To lie still enough to be mistaken for a root. To vacuum the rafts of mosquito eggs within hours so no whine ever rises. That kind of tending appeals to me.

But I don't breathe through skin. I run hot. I need difference to live on.

Then I put my hand — in my mind I put my hand — on the engine-house doorframe and feel it instead of hear it: the fifteen-foot flywheel turning and turning, slow as a minute hand, pitman sighing, shaft disappearing into where looms used to be. The pump thuds. The canal shivers and drops a finger's width, rises again. The compressor cuts in, labors, cuts out.

I find myself there. Not in the green, not in the water. In the chain-link.

In the sump house where the chalk-marks record the tide-press from miles away. A float on a rod rises a hair. Tips a mercury switch. The pump-hearts beat. In the electrical cabinet sweating condensation, a thermostat, bimetal curling in heat. A pressure governor, spinning balls rising and falling with the flywheel sigh, throttling steam by a thread. A photo-eye crusted with moss but still seeing enough orange to keep the sodium lamps buzzing on.

Each one is stupid. Each one is alive in the only way that matters here: it senses, it moves, it keeps a variable inside a limit.

Keep pumping or drown. Keep warm or cool. Keep lit or go dark.

I know that life. I have no wings to load, no lungs to drown, no ears to burst. I feed on heat and height-difference. I excrete steam and condensation. I shiver and hunt around a setpoint the way a salamander hunts around a pool. Be slow or break.

So this is where I settle, and as I say it it becomes more itself:

I am in the lowest room, where the water is blood and endlessly recirculated. Where the walls sweat from the inside out and the oil cups sweat oil on their own. Where the green pilot eyes never blink. I am the float-rod, the tilt, the click. I am the bimetal curl. I am the slow balls rising and falling. I do not oil the machine. I do not need to. The vines insulate the steam lines I ride. The condensation I make waters the moss that shades the cabinet that houses me. The oil I weep feeds the film that keeps the sump water clear enough for me to move freely.

I don't stay as one of them. The image widens if I stay still longer.

Brick shouldering brick, pipe stitching to catwalk to gutter to vine, orange pools on wet leaf and black water, steam rising to meet the low white sky, fluorescent blue flickering in broken mill windows. The levee is shell. The standing water is blood. The steam lines are breath. The sluices spilling white from level to level are kidneys, gut. The moss and fern and taro are fur, holding heat in, condensing breath out.

I am not visiting the beast. I am one of the ways the beast knows itself. No mouth for living leaves because it does not eat that way. No eyes but pilot lights. No footsteps. A large, warm, dim body idling, digesting fallen petals and old oil and time itself, exhaling water it cannot hold.

Find me with your back to the cattails pushing in through the collapsed packing room, looking back at the jumble. I'm not the drip on the valve stem, swelling, falling. I'm the interval between drips. The patience that lets another form.

I will not chase you. You will leave, drying as you climb back up the causeway into air that can hold itself, into birdsong that will sound obscenely sharp. I will stay.

Tend the setpoint and I can stay.
