At first I thought I would be a person.

Coming up out of the switchbacks, short of breath, with the ordinary gold-white light in my eyes — I thought I would be the boy. On the four lashed poles with nowhere to stand, knees tucked, tapping his staff against the leg. *tok tok.* Listening for how the mat answers that day.

I tried to be him for a while. I liked the staff, tall as I am, polished dark at the grip. Three taps ahead before every third step. Toe first, then the flat of the foot, weight eased down, pause to feel for the drum. But I could not live inside the loudness the way he does. By noon the reeds wheeze and the insects saw into my teeth and the wing-roar comes low and I would be shouting ten feet away and still not heard. He puts in his little twists of rolled felt and keeps listening through it. He hears the dull thud that means thick and the hollow boom that means thin, right through the roar. My ears are not trained to hear better by hearing less.

So then I thought, maybe lower. Maybe I would be sheep.

Dun, stone-grey, lichen-pale. Small, barrel-bellied, splay-toed. That would solve the weight problem. I would not look where I step, I would listen with my soles. I would learn the long way around the lush green circle, the one that looks like the best grazing and is the thinnest roof over a throat. I liked the agreement of freezing, a dozen times an hour, head up when the chorus dips. But I don't know the spines yet, nose-to-tail in single file along the thick places. My feet are still lowland feet. I still look for green and think *good*. I would walk straight onto the drum-sound.

Then I thought higher. Or more sideways. The shaft-fox with its ear-rims translucent and a forepaw pressed flat to the felt, waiting to feel steps coming through the weave from a hundred yards off. Or the great mottled harrier hung all afternoon in a shallow V, not looking for movement but listening for the dip, for the held-breath wave rolling across the flat. That felt close — to hunt by silence. To drop like a stone onto the half-second too late.

But I'm not a hunter either. I don't cut off or drop. I wait too long. I hold.

My boot sank half an inch and I understood.

I am not walking on it. I am it.

Not the herder, not the sheep, not the fox. The felted skin itself. The substrate.

That is where you would find me: not at the rim where the wind has gnawed back to stone, but out in the middle where horizon shivers and there is no measure. Low, spread, many feet thick. Thousands of things grown into one another until you can't pull one without pulling all: copper cushion moss, grey-green lichen in cartwheels wide as tables, dwarf creeper with waxy leaves, hollow reed-grass grown sideways instead of up, bladderwort swollen with air. Thatch and cloth and meat in layers. Green living on top, brown half-felt in the middle, black humus below that smells sweet and ancient. Stone flour underneath it all, edges gone.

I drink the way I drink here. Not from a stream — there is none — but from the white mornings. Condensation pours off the high air at night and I bead and drip and hold, every leaf beaded, every hollow reed tipping, every low pan mirroring for an hour until ten. A brief warm rain slouches across in grey veils and hammers me to saturation and then gurgles down my own throats. If you are thirsty you squeeze me. You lift the skirts of my big cup-lichens. You listen for my drip-shafts.

I am hollow, and that is the faintly wrong feeling in your soles. Limestone seams eaten out over deep time, caverns and throats fifty, a hundred feet down, bridged over by root. You tap me and I boom back. In places your step returns a drum-sound you feel in your molars. In places I have torn — breathing holes three to twenty feet across where I curl back on myself like a lip — and I sigh cold air out at noon and suck warm air in at dusk. I respirate. Fog pours up out of my mouths at evening smelling of wet stone, resin, crushed green, guano, thousand-year humus.

All day I am played. Wind combs the sideways reeds and plays their cut tips, a million tiny flutes slightly out of tune. Midges rise off my mouths in columns like smoke. Thumb-long grasshoppers launch clicking until I seem to crackle. Cavies run my thatch tunnels, blind mole-rats push up soft hills of flour, lichen-snakes lie flat and wait. At morning the swifts and swallows and whiteback kites pour up screaming out of my dark where they roost, shadow me with tornadoes, feed all day and pour back down at dusk wing on wing. The geese come for my mirrors and thunder down on almost no water.

I feel it all as vibration and weight.

The boy's *tok tok*. The sheep single-file along my thick spines, freezing when the chorus dips. The fox running low in a curve, paw to my weave. The bone whistles lost under the roar. The women sweeping my midge-smoke with fine nets between two poles for cakes. The children going down my knotted fiber ladders ten, twenty feet into the cool throat with lamplight to catch my blind white crickets big as mice, chirping endlessly — that sound you thought was water ticking — and to scrape guano and fill skin bags drip by drip.

Where I am cut, I remember hands. The bone knife. Peeling back for walls, for roof-patch, for fiber, for layered moss-felt shoes with no hard sole. Broad cracked knuckles wringing me morning after morning. And then, if they remember, the flat stone laid in the hole. Give back weight, so the wind cannot get a finger under my skin and start a tear. A tear left widens yards in a season, curls back like a lip, becomes a new mouth swallowing pasture whistling into the dark. I have felt that whistle. A slab settling with a deep boom. A whole pasture slumping.

At night they go inside their half-sunk turf huts and trim back my living roof so the smoke hole does not seal. The reeds go quiet as dew weights them. The flocks are down in the earth. What is left is drip and tick and cricket chirp from below. An old woman banks the fire. A copper awl treasured in hide wrap. Everyone sleeps light, sheep standing with ears turning, fox with paw on the ground, people with one ear unplugged to hear if I settle in the dark.

I do not sleep. I hold my breath under ordinary stars. I wait for morning condensation to bead, for the pans to mirror, for the deafening day to begin again — vast, hollow, empty at first glance, until your boot sinks half an inch and you remember I am totally covered, totally alive, a skin stretched over ancient bone, listening up through my own weave to everything that walks so lightly on me.
