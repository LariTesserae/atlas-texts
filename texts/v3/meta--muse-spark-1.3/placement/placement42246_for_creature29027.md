Not at first on the rim. I thought I would want the rim.

Coming over the col with the sun in my eyes, I thought: I will be high. I will be the thing that can leave. A cliff-dove, lifting with a whole ledge in that papery whirr, rising, wheeling over the white lip and out over that drowned blue sea of lower peaks. To have air under me.

But then I drop in and I see them up close. They are not free. They sit shoulder to shoulder until their breasts touch. Slate-grey and white, bills gone clear like fingernails, feet that raw gum-pink. When they lift they only settle again on another crowded ledge. Or a roof. Or my shoulder, because I am the nearest warm ledge. And they do not sing. They have given that up. Only the soft hail of bodies landing.

So not that. Not wings.

Then I thought, lower: I will be four-footed and low to the peat. One of the dwarf goats, no higher than a knee, shaggy to the ground, coughing that whisper-bleat. Wandering where I want through the foot-wide slab streets, scraping hair-lichen with yellow teeth, belonging to no one and everyone. That seemed like freedom, of a kind.

But I watch two men haul one out of a cistern, its hair matted with ice and droppings, legs churning milky water. They swear at it in hand-sign, fast and fond and exasperated. Mostly kept for warmth under the bench at night, and for dung, because dung is the only fire that will catch. Saved for the dead of winter in a different way. No, not that either. Too useful. Too cold.

Then I thought, of course, I will be a person. I will learn to be dense like them.

At first I try them all on. I stand ankle-deep in the thaw-pan plaza where the market is, icy water welling around my boots, and try to make my hands flicker fast enough: *egg. careful, thin ice. move, please. two handfuls of worms for a palm of salt.* My fingers go wooden in minutes inside mittens. My breath comes short and shallow and hangs white and then falls as crystals. My lungs are too small in my ribs. My eyes water and the tears freeze on my lashes. Everything glints too hard. Shadows are not grey but mineral blue.

I try setting stone, not striking it, fitting and lifting and fitting again while the ice-cored moraine groans and slumps overnight under me. A pool drains by noon through a new crack and appears under someone's floor. I bail. I carry slate up a ladder on my back. A child runs past me with a basket of red worms still writhing. An old woman nods with her chin. Doves explode upward and resettle. It is gentle and economical up close, no wasted motion, no wasted sound, and I am all waste. I want to hammer. I want to call out. My throat bleeds if I try.

I am too loud, too dry, too much air in me. I keep looking up, squinting.

It takes an hour in the glare for me to understand I have been looking in the wrong direction. Everyone here narrows their eyes to slits against the light. Webbed with fine lines. Lashes white with frost by mid-morning. The light comes from everywhere at once — off snowfields, off quartz, off a hundred shallow pans, off the pale undersides of wings — until it hurts to keep looking.

So I look down.

Break the skin of a pan with a knuckle. Underneath it runs, milky with rock flour, smelling of stone and iron. Red ice-worms knotted together like bootlaces under the ice. Pale midges grazing the blood-red and sulphur-orange smears of snow-algae. And nosing through the silt, eyeless as thumbs: the blind white loaches.

Eyeless. In a place where the first violence is light, they have stopped negotiating with it altogether.

That is where I find myself. Not on a ledge. Not on the peat sponge that sucks at my boots. Knee-deep, then waist-deep, in the cistern water with the two men, passing up bucket after bucket to keep the street from becoming a stream. The water is so cold it stops being cold. My hands go white. My eyes go milky and useless in the best way. I stop trying to see across the bowl rim to rim. I stop trying to speak and lose sentences to the frost to hear them ticking faintly off the hut walls at night.

I nose, almost. I learn the water by feel and taste — this seep rusty, this one turquoise, this one about to drain, this one about to freeze over in the shadow of a passing cloud, on and off, on and off. I know by the suck around my legs when needle-ice is forming. I know by smell when guano has washed down and the worms will rise. I eat what the circle gives without needing to look at it: broth drunk through cloth, loaches sucked whole, lichen cake. Scraps for the voles that run the walls like stitches. Breath for the frost.

At night I cram shoulder to shoulder, eight or ten to a low beehive, goats breathing under the bench, doves rustling in the thatch above where I can no longer tell feathers from thatch. The dung fire gives little heat. We share what we have. And as the walls warm, I hear it — drip, tick, sigh — the day's lost sentences thawing out of the stones. By then I am quiet enough to listen.

A dove lands on my shoulder in the morning because I am still enough to be a ledge. I do not shake it off.
