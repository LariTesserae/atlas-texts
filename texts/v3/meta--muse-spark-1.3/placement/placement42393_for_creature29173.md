At first I think I am swimming.

I come in cold, between towers, with the thin clean weight on my cheeks and the backs of my hands. Kick and breath. Twenty meters of dusk-blue and then dark. I think I will stay a visitor — something with forward eyes and a hard outline, passing through. The dust parts around my wrist and closes without memory. A school pours around a base below me, flashing all at once, and I am not part of the decision.

That doesn't hold. Nothing here lets you pass through. Your wake dissolves in seconds. Your breath joins the clicking.

So I try to be dust. It would be easy — to hang, to taste pressure, to be weather. I open my hand and let them re-weave it. But I am too lumpy, too singular. I remember too much. The dust does not remember. It thins where the fish have passed and thickens where a crown has pulsed and that is all.

I try to be fish. I try to move as a single slow muscle, flank-on to the faint down-light, mouth a soft funnel sipping snow. But I cannot do the togetherness of it, a thousand bodies in one decision, always with the gyre. I am too noisy inside. I trail no sweet plume that greens the mat.

I try to be a hauler. I sink down until the felt gives faintly under me and springs back. I lay my belly to it. The mat pushes back, almost imperceptibly, warm with green. I try to rasp and not tear. I am too hurried. I lift my snout too often for air. A real newt passes near me, broad as a canoe, the color of the world until it moves, and I understand I will never be that patient. Moss will never grow undisturbed on my back. A crab family will never ride me.

It is a crab that finds me.

I am hanging under a causeway — I don't remember choosing to cling there — third fork up the eastern tower, where two young limbs were coaxed together until they fused, leaving a hole politely in the middle for a door. The walk-fiber above my head is calloused from a million passings. Below me, impossibly far below, the living floor glows olive. Above me, the stalk goes up into black and creaks, a centimeter and back. In the cup to my left an eel has looped herself over the door-hole, throat out, pulsing a shade warmer than the dusk-blue, singing her long descending moan. The bells under the rail snap shut as I touch: *whup whup whup* down the corridor.

The crab comes sideways out of a leaf-scar. Small — my palm would hold two of her. Back overgrown with living moss so she looks like a walking lump of mat. Claws not claws, soft blunt nippers rounded by a lifetime of nipping only living tissue.

She inspects me for parasites. Finding none, she nips politely at my dead skin. And eats it.

That is where I stay. That is where I find myself.

Upside down. That is the orientation you have to let in. Belly to the sky of black water, back to the glowing floor far below. Clinging with small clawed feet to a living ceiling that gives off heat where the muscle works. The thrumming comes through my whole body here, not just my chest. The great thump comes through the fiber: *thump … thump … thump*, and all our legs twitch in time when we sleep piled together in the warm pit.

For I become a stitcher. It becomes more itself the longer I hold on.

My hands were too hard at first, too grasping. Nails, knuckles. Things that could tear a sieve. The work softens them. I learn to nip parasites off the eel's throat without making her dim — she learns to keep pulsing while I work, light coming through my shell. I learn to pick algae out of bell crowns while they flush around my legs. I learn the main work: to coax two young branches together and hold them. For days if necessary. Not to tie, not to cut. Just to hold, patient as sap, until they decide to fuse and a new wall is born.

I grow moss on my back. It itches, then it doesn't. A smaller mouth finds a place behind my eye-ridge and cleans there while I work. I carry the smell of the tower in my joints.

I do not sing like the eel to map the other towers a kilometer away. I listen in the gap between beats and know if a crown is clogged by how the creak changes. I do not haul like the newt, mowing the mat flat. But when a newt passes below with a brighter trail closing behind it, I know my tower will eat well tonight, because fish will circle where the mat greens.

I am in the agreement now. Not making it. Keeping it.

If I die — and I will, soft — there will be no shell left, no white scatter. Crabs will find me before I touch the mat and I will be unmade, gently, mouth to soft mouth, and carried into the wall to be regrown as wall. A doorway that is also a stomach that is also an alarm will close a little tighter because I was once there.

Find me upside down under the causeway between rooms, holding living tissue together until it holds itself. Legs twitching to the heart I will never see.
