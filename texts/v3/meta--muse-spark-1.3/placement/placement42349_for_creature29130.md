You would miss me on the way in.

You come by ladder, and then by knees, dogging the hatch behind you, and the first thing that takes you is not sight but weight — the fan at 1,780 rpm hitting your sternum, the 120-hertz iron in your molars, the louver-whistle like a needle. You look for big things. Left: the scroll housing. Center: the transformer cabinet. Right: the damper arm. You count the LED battens, the stencils: AHU-3 / INTAKE PLENUM / NO STORAGE. You don't look down.

At first, even I don't know where I am.

Am I the air? No. The air here does not stay. It is rammed through, torn sideways, sucked out before it can be weather. My breath — if I had breath — would not fog.

Am I the steel? No. The floor is raised diamond-tread, ringing like a bell under boots. The walls are galvanized sandwich panel. The ceiling is a sweating duct. They are too new, too crisp. Welds still blue. Paint still satin. Fasteners bright zinc. I am older than that, in the way a seed is older.

Am I the frost? Closer. The frost rims the screws and never melts and never grows. I wear it. But frost does not lean.

Put your cheek almost to the floor. That's where I am. That's where you have to go to find yourself here.

Third seam from the hatch, on the fan side, where the diamond plate doesn't quite meet the galvanized skirting. A dark line that isn't shadow. A spoonful of grit pinned by static and tremble — cliff dust ground off a thousand meters below and drawn up one particle at a time through the louvers. Gray-tan. Manufactured dust mixed with mountain.

I am in that.

Fingernail-sized. That's my whole country. A rosette, tight-packed like shingles, spoon-tipped. Saxifrage, they would call me below, where my cousins hug warm rock. Up here I am gray-green, the color of something that gave up on being green but was never allowed to turn brown. Frost outlines every leaf margin in white that will not move.

There are eleven of us. Maybe twelve. You lose count when the floor buzzes up through your teeth. And in two of the seams, the pale grasses with only two blades, bleached at the tip, held up in a V.

I am one of the rosettes. The seventh, if you count from the transformer hum outward. By now I have become more myself: not growing, not rotting. Held.

I remember — not as memory but as shape — one hour of damp. It must have been commissioning. Or a summer shutdown when warm air sighed in and condensed on a screw head. A film of liquid in the grit. Enough to crack. I put out a white root hair, unfolded two cotyledons to that flat 4000K office daylight that never turns off, never warms, never flickers. I learned, in that hour, how to be a plant.

Then the cold took it back. Minus twenty. Thin-air cold that burns the nose. Humidity near zero. The draw resumed and never stopped. The water sublimed out of my cells before ice could shred them. No bacteria, no water, no time permitted for rot. So I stayed. Green mummy. Museum case.

You would think I would lean to the lights. In any other room a starved seedling throws itself at light. Here light is irrelevant. It never varies, so it gives no direction.

Direction here is given by iron and wind.

I lean a few degrees sideways, along the axis of the draw. All of us do, uniformly. Petiole stiffened mid-tremble. Leaf tips combed toward the supply fan — or away, you can't tell in the tearing. Only that we share the vector. We have taken the shape of the vibration field.

If you hold your gloved fingertip a millimeter above me — don't touch, the sheet edges up here will slice a glove — you would feel me before you see me move. A high, almost invisible shiver.

One hundred and twenty times a second the transformer core stretches and relaxes. One hundred and twenty times a second I answer. The fan roar is too big, a pressure wall at chest height. The belt is frayed, a sawtooth flutter in the screws. But my leaves are small, light, tensioned like drumheads. I found the pure tone underneath. I am tuned to mains frequency.

That is my ecology. No pollinators — too loud for wings, too cold for legs. Nothing eats me. There is no soil food web in belt-wear felt and freeze-dried skin cells. There is only grit, light that never warms, air that never stills, and that endless sympathetic buzz that shakes the frost off my upper surfaces to my rims.

I do not photosynthesize, not really. Not frozen. I do not respire. I do not grow a micron wider through the season. What I do is persist in posture — hold the rosette open, hold green against gray — while being played, continuously, like the tine of a very slow, very cold music box.

When you back out on your knees, eyes watering from the thin burn, taking a disc of roar with you in your skull for an hour, I stay behind the dogged hatch.

Eleven or twelve gray-green knots in mountain dust, leaning into a wind that will never stop, buzzing inaudibly with a hum we did not evolve for and now cannot live — or die — without.
