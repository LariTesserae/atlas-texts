Don't look for me at first.

For the first hour you won't see anything but rain falling into water. White on white under the oculus. The roar. The *dum-dum, dum-dum* coming up through the soles of your feet. That's how it is. The tank teaches you its rule slowly: a few presences hold a lot of emptiness between them.

At first I thought I'd be one of the loud ones.

A swallow, maybe. It would make sense — something that came up an impossible distance to live in a foot of humid air under the dome. Cutting through the rain shafts screaming, stitching that shrill thread you can hear even under the roar, then folding and going straight *in*, into another element, bursting back out in droplets. I liked the violence of it. The bargain of it: rust-mud nests plastered pellet by pellet, guano streaking white-green down the walls to feed the ferns.

But I'm not that brave. I don't travel nine kilometers up through screaming wind. I don't dive. I skim too carefully. I sleep too lightly, pressed together with beaks tucked while the whole tower sways like a pendulum and the water stays perfectly level? No. Not the swallow.

Then I thought, maybe the shrimp. In the light shafts where the rain looks solid, those clouds that are not rain. Each no longer than an eyelash, almost-transparent, pulsing in the sweet upper water — chest-high and up, clear and moving. They gather in your palm because it is shade. You feel them as a faint fizz. I liked that. To be small enough to live entirely inside light. To scatter like dust hit by breath and then re-form, patiently, in the exact same column.

But I'm not that patient. I don't re-form. I remember being scattered.

The blind ones, then? The pale school circling the sagged middle where it's chin-deep, a dozen, maybe twenty, mouths open, following the pump gyre endlessly. No eyes, or eyes that gave up. Navigating by taste and tremor. Tilting together like one thought when the big overflow gulps every seven minutes and the level drops a hand's breadth. I tried that on. To never stop. Stillness is not a thing they do.

But I stop. I stop all the time. I lie back.

Eels? Black and muscular and warm as the water itself, thick as your forearm, slipping between floor plates where the iron has softened and gapped. Commuters. Mostly coiled around stanchion feet in the dark salt — knee-deep and down, still and smelling of iron and old oil — then fast, sudden, up through into the sweet to hunt, surface with a slap-splash louder than anything except the rain, then sink trailing a cloak of fresh water that peels off them. To brush a calf, curious, incurious, gone.

Too sudden. Too sure.

The pillbugs came closest. Slate-grey, jointed, solemn, the size of thumbs. Cattle of this place. Working the moss pillows six inches thick on the catwalk beams and the four weeping columns, mowing algae in the splash zone, turning rust-mud and guano into themselves. When a drip hits them they do not flinch. When a swallow screams over they tuck, just slightly. If you lift the water lettuce you find their pale young crowded like seeds underneath. Slow, inexhaustible.

I wanted to be that — something that could take what is waste and make it body. But I don't graze. I get grazed.

No. Find me lower than the birds and higher than the fish. Find me exactly where your legs disappear.

When you wade, your chest is in one sea and your knees are in another and you are not. Fresh rain, clear and moving, above. Old salt, dark and still, below. And between them, that thin shimmer that tears when you move and heals behind you. Bacteria and oil beading rainbow-thin and pollen from the little white orchid-flowers that smell of vanilla and hot metal and iron fines. That holds footprints for a moment.

I am the skin.

I'm not much. I'm not counted like the catfish. I don't have a nest. I don't have eyes. I'm a condition that learned to stay. The sweet grazes my underside — the shrimp mouthing me from above. The salt noses me from below — the catfish pressing up, never breaking through. The eels punch through me twice a hunt, down and back, and I close over the wound like nothing happened, which is not the same as forgetting. For a second I keep the shape of everything that passes: calf, eel back, pillbug knocked loose, petal.

You'd find me if you lie back very still and float. That's when I can hold you. Not otherwise. If you thrash I tear. If you are still, I cradle you between two waters while you stare up into the falling white and the swallows stitch the air inches over your face, screaming, and under you, through me, *dum-dum, dum-dum*, the stupid faithful pumps shake the whole volume and the curtains of emerald filament algae sway like hair in a tide and the ferns drink air with their white naked roots and the water lettuce and duckweed go around and around the columns in their slow gyres and never arrive.

That's where I live. Between. Tasting rain on my face and oil on my back. Smelling both vanilla and rust. Holding.

It is loud here, even for skin. Rain roar, engine throb, clank-tick of expanding pipes, seven-minute gulp and sigh, plink and patter off a hundred softened edges — stairs become ramps, bolt-heads become kisses — sudden slap-splash. To be me you have to listen with your whole surface. Your own breathing sounds underwater.

Keep moving to stay still. That's the law of Tank Nine. For me it means this: tear and heal, tear and heal. Let the sweet and the salt refuse to mix and refuse to part. If anything stops — if the pumps stop, if the rain stops, if the shrimp don't rise or the birds don't dive — I vanish first. No drama. The two seas just become one lukewarm tank and this small man-made ocean perched above the clouds remembers it is only a leaking room twenty strides across.

So don't touch the valve wheel. Hang your hammock from the catwalk nubs, its cut end smoothed to a soft lipped mouth, above the splash but below the swallows. Sleep in short naps between pump cycles. Eat watercress by the handful, peppery and bright, the only thing that tastes like somewhere else.

And when you wake at the gulp and slip into the water chin-deep in the middle, wade slowly. Feel your legs go dark and cold under you while your chest stays warm. Feel me part and mend. Lie back. I'll hold you for as long as you can be still — a warm body briefly made of rust and rain and light, listening to iron breathing around you, warm as blood, holding its little emptiness faithfully against the sky.
