I don't think I would stay up on the moor.

At first I think I would — that I would be the one standing at the lip, boot-toe over pale rock, looking down. The watcher. The visitor with sound still stuck to him. It would be cleaner up there. Wind to cover me, hard blue to squint into. I could look and not be counted.

But the bowl doesn't allow that for long. Five paces away you wouldn't know it was there, but once you've seen it, once you've stepped down into that trapped light, the cold gets into your soles and stays. Your breath comes out white and doesn't leave. You can't just look. There is no empty slope to stand on. Everywhere is roof become floor become stair. If I stood still, something would walk over my boot. A vole, fat as a fist. A shrew. A child needing the board.

So I go down. And smaller.

I think, for a while, I would be one of the clippers. On my knees along a parapet with a flint flake, going along the line where pillow moss wants to swallow the stone. I like the idea of that work — harvest the day's new growth to exactly the shape beneath, tuck the cuttings into the pouch at my waist. Nothing wasted. A clean edge. I can feel my hands learning it, red and chapped to shining, not shivering.

But no. My eyes aren't right for noon yet. The white throws itself back and forth until it aches, and I am still squinting like an outsider, lids wide open and watering. The clippers have fans at the corners of their eyes, half-mast lids. They look sideways through lashes. I haven't earned that narrowing.

Maybe then I would be lower, more out of the glare. Maybe I would be one of the small white-toothed things. For a long time on the way down I watch a shrew flicker along a wall-top and I envy it its certainty. No hesitation, no loud boots. It knows the route between fern and fern. It eats the spider that eats the midge and keeps the number. It doesn't have to learn to be quiet. It is quiet.

I could be that. I could be unchased. Moving openly down the middle of walkboards because no one chases. Mowing seed-heads. Turning thatch. Leaving droppings for the moss to take back. That feels close — to be useful without having to speak, even mouth-to-ear.

But I'm still too big for the wall-tops. Broad in the knee and elbow, yes, I'm getting there from all the crouching, but my feet still find the ladders sway if I climb too fast. The birch and bone shift under me. The town notices my weight.

So I go lower still. Into shade.

There is a place I keep returning to, under a slab on the north face where the sun never quite piles up. Where the liverworts are slick as organs and the air is bluer and even stiller. A black trough hollowed from a log sits there, holding a finger's depth of clear seep. Melt and breath and night-frost. In the morning it has a skin of ice. By noon it has melted. By morning again it has formed. My job — I understand this slowly, the way you understand things here, not told but seen — is to keep it from deciding.

I am the one with the bark scoop.

Not to move the water. To keep it open. I carry a scoopful from this trough to the next trough three houses over and back again, and again all afternoon. The walk is shoulder-wide. I flick two fingers to a vole to let me pass and it lets me. I put palm flat to my own chest when a child runs toward the fruiting sphagnum — *stay, do not step there* — and she stops without sound. I tip my chin and an older keeper nods: *it needs stirring.*

I kneel. That is where you would find me, if you stepped in. Not on the terrace edge with legs dangling, watching. Not on the parapet in full glare. Under. Kneeling on damp stone that feels damp even when it looks dry, knees spread for balance, breath hanging longer than it should, joining the damp and beading again.

I stir. Small circles. Just enough warmth from my hand, just enough movement to keep the skin from closing. I listen. A ladder creaks like a knuckle somewhere above and three heads turn, but I am listening lower. For the *tick* when water lets go from a ledge onto stone. In shade my pupils open huge and black and I see the drop fall before I hear it. The moss eats most of the sound, the bowl holds the rest, but I mark it. Lift a finger. Like the child on the roof-lawn marking the silent bunting.

In the morning, before the light gets painful, I lick water straight from the wall. You can see the faint tongued paths where others have, vole and person both. It tastes of stone and leaf and cold. My white has gone slightly green at the edges. My cheeks shine red. I do not shiver that I can see.

I do not build. No one builds. Once a season a block slips and I work my fingers into the joint until green comes away, pack fresh wet sphagnum behind with my thumbs, lift the same limestone — its underside dark and soft with a hundred years of sitting — and seat it back in the exact hollow it left. Run a palm over it to test damp. Nod. That is a morning.

I do not speak. I put mouth to ear when I must, close enough the warmth is part of the word. Mostly a hand held out cupped, then turned over and emptied.

*It is kept. It is enough.*

That is what I have become. Not extra. The number stays. The ice forms and melts and forms. I am one of the hands and paws and fronds, tending and tended, breathing white into cold bright air that never moves.
