At first I want to be up high.

Where the air is still open enough to move in. One of the heavy beetles making that drone you feel in your teeth, idling blind from tree to tree. From up there the firefly acres would come in sequence, ridge after ridge of treetops blinking on and off together, and I would be part of the largeness. I would see how large the place is.

But I don't see. My eyes stay open and useless. The canopy finishes the job the cloud started. And the drone isn't a flight, it's a commute — elsewhere in the black, rising and falling, never landing where I am. I'm not that. I'm too heavy to stay up.

Lower, then. Let me be in the floor-wave.

A crab, red-backed, purple-clawed, shell to shell in the thousands coming up from the estuary miles away. That clicking like fingernails tapping when we bump. That patient, walker's tired pace. There is a comfort in it — to be a pilgrim, to drink from a root-hollow the size of a cooking pot, filmed with pollen, and move on. To test something warm with a claw and decide: stone. Established.

But I'm not passing through in that direction. The migration will be elsewhere by morning and will not miss me. And I don't know how to move shoulder to shoulder without breaking. I keep trying to look where I'm going.

Lower still. Closer.

Kneel, if I can stand to be climbed on. That's where I find myself, actually. Not in the air, not in the wave, but at the edge of it, on the slope where the mud has been worn glass-smooth half a meter wide.

The highway finds me by touch and smell. Green oil where the leaf edge is torn. A dry river of feet going both ways without end.

Down one lane the green conveyor, each ant holding her sail overhead like a parasol. Up the other, the empty return.

For a while I think I am one of them. I want to be useful like that. To carry many times my own weight for hours across the slope without hurrying and without stopping. To climb over a crab leg when it blocks me. To go under the fern arch. To not need to know what the leaf is for.

But the worker with the leaf is blind to what the leaf is for. She will never eat it. She will carry it down a hole no wider than my thumb into earth darker and damper and warmer than this night and give it away. I can't give myself away that completely. I keep wondering. I keep lifting my head to listen for the shower moving like an animal through the upper leaves far off.

Follow it down, though. Since I can't be the carrier, maybe I can be what is carried for.

Down past root-mat and leaf-mold deep enough to sink a hand into, past the egg-soft stones, past where the *tok* of water from leaf to leaf reaches. Hotter, not cooler. Close. Fungal. Iron and mud and crushed green.

It opens into vaulted clay, a town under the valley. And in the chambers, the gardens — low, pale, spongy masses, veined and beaded, breathing faintly. This kind grows nowhere else except here, in the dark they make for it. It cannot survive a minute of daylight, a breath of dry air.

Am I that? The soft, blind, stationary thing that never moves and never sees the fireflies, that exists only because an endless conveyor feeds it? Around it the smaller amber sisters chew the sails to pulp, plant it, weed it, lick it clean of mold, feed on its swollen tips. There is something in me that wants to be tended like that. To be kept damp and steady.

But I'm not tended. I'm too dry inside. Too much air in me. I breathe in warm spores and breathe out, and that too is immediately used by something else — by a leaf drinking the dark, by a palm-sized pool shrinking a millimeter as the mud drinks it. I'm not the used-breath, I'm the breather. I can't stay down there where the ventilation draws cool air in at the base and exhales hot air out the top.

And deeper still she lies, in the warmest, dampest heart. Once winged, once flying. Years ago on some other hot breathless night she flew, mated, fell, tore off her own wings and dug down. Has not seen light since. Will never go up again. Now as long as my thumb joint, distended, pale amber. Abdomen pulsing, slow, slow. An egg every few seconds, all night, every night.

I cannot be her. I am not settled. Not low and rounded and weathered down to balance. I have not had a long time to get this packed, this lush. I am still empty in places. There is still room in me for emptiness, and so the rule — *live close, share the leaf, share the drop, share the dark, nothing is empty* — moves through me like a hunger, not like a keeping.

So this is where I find myself, after all that wanting:

On the surface. In true dark. Boots sunk in living carpet. Left foot squelching in leaf mold, right foot in powder-dry dust. Hot and still and close, thirty degrees at midnight, my own skin condensing. Things climbing over my boots without altering course.

A crab tests my ankle and decides I am a stone, weathered, established, part of the settled form. A line of ants reroutes an inch to the left around me and continues, unbroken.

That is what I am here. Not a resident. Not a pilgrim, not a commuter, not a mouthpart, not a gardener, not a garden, not a queen.

A temporary hill. A warm stone that breathes.

Put your palm to the termite kiln, chest-high and clay-fired hard, and it is warmer than the night air. Put your palm to me and you would feel the same furnace-room heat. Millions of bodies in them, only trillions in me, but the same ticking, rustling attempt to generate a climate inside because outside, even here, is not close enough, not damp enough, not steady enough.

I stay still long enough to be included, briefly, as terrain. I breathe, very slowly, in and out.
