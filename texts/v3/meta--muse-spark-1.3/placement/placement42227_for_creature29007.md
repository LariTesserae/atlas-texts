I wouldn't be standing for long.

Standing is what you do when you first push in — shoulders wet, forearms slick, shouting to hear yourself and hearing nothing. I try to picture it and the picture won't hold. The mat trembles under me. Something skitters off my boot. The sawtooth whine saws at the inside of the skull. I can't keep upright in that press. I crouch. Or I am crouched for me by the weight of it.

At first I think I would be one of the watchers. The human-warm stone with dew forming first on its skin. The thing the moth lands on to drink. That would be easy — to be the visitor who breathes and is breathed on, who holds still until a long cool finger tests forearm for bridge-or-stone.

But I don't have that kind of warmth to lend for long. And the stone here remembers a cut for a century. It would remember me. I don't want to be the thing that leaves.

Then I think, the hands. The climbers.

I want the fingers. God, who wouldn't, in the dark like that — to feel instead of hear. To spread wide and test each moss-bridge for give before committing weight. To strip a thorn *tss tss tss* and slide it head-down into bark and pull out something fat and white and living. To be unafraid in the press, to pile twenty or thirty deep in a crotch of branches and rise and fall together.

But I don't forget the way they forget. That's the whole rule. Nothing kept. Nothing carried to a camp. A stored tool would be a rotted tool by dawn, so memory lives not in objects but in other hands, watched and immediately copied, lost and reinvented nightly.

I'm used to keeping. I'm built to keep. Here, everything that stays still is eaten, overgrown, dissolved by ants or fungus or mist. If I came here as I am, I'd rot by morning.

So lower. Not the hand.

If you stay by that black cup of water between the blades of lava — tannin-brown, full of tadpoles, the rare pocket that lasts — and let the riot go on over your head, you start to see what's actually staying. Nothing stays. But some things stay a little while, an hour, long enough to be used twice.

I would be the thorn.

Not the shrub it was snapped from. Not the female who snapped it, stripped the side-barbs with her incisors, tested the point with her thumb pad. Just the stripped thing itself, for the minute it is a tool.

Held one-handed. Warmed. That terrible thin middle finger guiding me, tendon moving like a string under pink-gray skin. Slid into a crevice I could never have found on my own. Twisting, feeling. Coming out wet with grub-fat. Then — dropped. It floats.

That's where you'd find me. Not crashing through the mid-canopy, not grunting out in the black with the heavy low pushers. Down low, ankle-level in human terms, eye-level if you crouch right. In the cup. Floating.

And floating becomes more itself the longer you look.

At first I'm just trash. A twig probe left behind. Surface tension holds me up at a slight angle, one tip darker where I've taken on water. Condensation beads on me the way it beads on the fresh fracture of the basalt, glittering faintly. The throat-sacs of the pocket-water frogs swell amber and blink, and for half a second I have a shadow, and then I don't.

Then a tadpole finds me. They do — anything floating becomes a raft for an hour. It shelters under my lee from the tiny sleep-confused birds and from the other tadpoles that eat the mosquito larvae that drank the climbers' blood. A pill-bug, boiled off the pulsing fungus, paddles past. The *tick tick crack* of the next snail hammering shudders through the lava fin and into the water and into me.

I don't breathe. I am breathed around. Thousands, tens of thousands of lungs pumping warm wet air into cooler warm wet air, and the plants above slowly cupping and uncupping to wring more from it, and the constant fine ticking as droplets form and fall from leaf to leaf to leaf — some of them landing in my cup with a pinprick *pit* that rocks me.

One of the babies steals me. That happens. He fishes badly, jabbing too hard, and loses me again. The female does it slowly where he can see. I am made and unmade in the same minute. Probe, trash, raft, probe again.

If you hold still until your own breath joins the valley weather, you might see a climber pick me up and read me with that sticky fingertip beyond the lava callus, checking if I'm still pointed enough to use. I am almost sticky myself now, filmed with algae and frog-mucus and mist. I am not sharp anymore.

By midnight I am waterlogged. By later — there is no later here, only more night — the ants will find the shell chips and carry them away, the sponge-root mats hanging in mid-air will drink what the leaves condense, the troop will crash on ten feet away in a new shape. I will sink a millimeter, then another. A fungus will take the soft end.

Do not expect morning to explain it. I won't last till morning. That's the point.

To be held for exactly as long as you're needed, copied hand to hand, and then left floating, trackless, in water made out of breath — that I could learn.
