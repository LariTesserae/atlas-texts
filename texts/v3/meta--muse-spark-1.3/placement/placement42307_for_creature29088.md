If I came here, I would come wrong.

Too big, too warm, too loud. Even cupped in both hands I would spill this place. My blood is summer. My breathing would stir turbulence where there can only be laminar. I would have to leave most of myself at the fissure — leave eyes that need light, lungs that need air, a voice — and fall through as something else.

At first I think I fall as snow.

Through the needle-crack, a flake of dead diatom, a fecal pellet from another world thirty meters up. Drifting down the pale shaft, noon-blue frayed at the edges, turning slowly in water that is colder inside than out, -1.7 and thickened. I think I will be food. That would be simple. To be caught, tasted, lipped down a groove by twenty conferring tentacles. To feed a tower. But I fall past the crowns. Past downtown. The light does not reach the floor intact. By the time I land I am dusk.

No. Not snow. Snow is eaten too fast here.

Then I think I am water-column. A nauplius in the dust, comma-speck with three limbs and one eye, fin over fin, near-touching but never colliding. Density is protocol. I drift in the shaft with a thousand near-me and learn the lanes. A striped ghost passes — a giant, a shrimp — and we part ahead of it in a wave and close behind without touch. It is beautiful to be that fluent. But I am too easily carried. The convection pulls me up toward the ventilation chimneys, the hollow worm-tubes left deliberately empty to draw a slow breath upward. I don't want to be carried. I want to hold on. Up is negotiable here, but only if you can hold on.

So I settle. Or I am settled.

Lower. To the floor avenues first, because they are easiest to understand. I try being a tower-keeper. A pale pink-gray gut in a house made of my own shit and spit and shell-hash, thumb-high, high-rise. Only my crown out, spilling bouquet, grooved and ciliated, endlessly sorting. It is good work. Never stopping because the snow never quite stops. And around my foundations, lodgers: a white anemone like a drop of tallow catching my fumbles, amphipods plastering mud lean-tos against my leeward side. I would be landlord without meaning to be. But I am too rooted. I cannot turn my head to watch the blue one thread the avenue, the iridescent predator, and feel the avenue empty ahead in a silent wave and fill behind. I would only feel it as a shadow on my tentacles. I need touch at distance.

I climb — which here means sideways — to the walls.

Here the traffic hums. Not with sound. Structural silence swallows sound, except at impossibly close range: tick of chitin on chitin, rasp of radula, a soft hydraulic sigh of swimmerets. What hums is chemistry, antennae. Flick, flick, pause.

This is where I find myself. Not downtown directly under the fissure where the sun-plots are fought over millimeter by millimeter and a new shadow is an act of war. Just to the left of it, in penumbra, second terrace up from the floor. Where the mucus highways sag between tenements.

I am a mason. A tube amphipod, comma-body with too many legs, blind to distance but exquisite at touch.

My tube is third layer deep in the honeycomb, mouth opening just off the main highway. I made it grain by grain. Dab of mucus, grit picked up, tasted, rejected, picked up again, placed. Another dab. Another grain. The wall of it is my memory. If you sectioned it you could read me: thin pinched rings for the winter the snow did not fall, a sudden flared balcony for the good week in spring when a copepod carcass rained down and we all built annexes.

I tend the same few millimeters. In this held breath I may live ten years. That is the cold's gift: it preserves and slows. A wound heals over weeks. A tower stands for years. Hurry is a warm-water concept.

My day — which is not day, dusk lives here permanently — is this: I flick from my mouth. Antennae taste who passes on the highway: fed or hungry, kin or stranger, carrying eggs or carrying a wound. Fat red-eyed copepods commute overhead, translucent as gel, riding the glistening road. When it breaks, as it does, I turn around and lick it back into tension. Patch with fresh spit. Everyone maintains the road they use. There is no other rule that would work.

I feel the city as pressure and taste. The bulldozer isopod working below me, blind and white, carrying a collapsed terrace grain by grain in its mandibles. The egg-fans on the ceiling never stopping, beat beat beat, because in water that barely moves oxygen must be brought by force. Somewhere on a felted lawn of sulfur-pale bacteria, two armies of pinhead limpets advance toward each other at glacial speed. They will meet tomorrow. I will know because the taste of grazed lawn will change in the water.

When the blue one comes, I will know before I see — I never see — as a dimming of traffic, ostracods clamping along their curb-stones made of ancestors, anemones folding to warts. I will duck. I will wait. Behind it the avenue will fill again. No alarm call. To make noise is to be eaten.

I will not leave. Nothing larger than a larva leaves. The current through the fissure is wrong and the cold outside is death. So I have learned the pocket's lesson. Nothing leaves, so everything returns. When I die — and even here, I die — I will be dismantled within hours. My shell stacked neatly like a brick in the graveyard for future building. My flesh dissolved to mucus-thick water. Shell becomes curb becomes cement becomes shell.

For now, I place another grain. I lick the highway. I fan water through my tube with my pleopods, a sigh no one hears.

I am small. That is the wrong word. I am scaled to this world. For me this pocket long as a man lying down is a country. My rice-grain body is a citizen's body, a full life's length. My tube is tenement and history. I am tenant and building at once, mouth and mason, making and remaking the city grain by grain in perfect silence, reaching inward toward the center like a geode in reverse, holding on where up is wherever you can hold on.
