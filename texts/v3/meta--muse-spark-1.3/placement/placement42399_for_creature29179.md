At first I thought I would come here with boots.

That I would feel the give underfoot, the warm water welling up around my fingers smelling of iron and rot, the midges coating my lips. That I would learn to see sideways like you do — by heat on the cheeks, by the suck of a heron's feet coming loose from mud.

But I have no boots to fill. My footprint doesn't fill before I lift it. There is no lift to me.

So not a visitor.

Then I thought — maybe small. Maybe I would be paved-in, shoulder to shoulder. A snail on the levee mud. A bone-white crab. One frog taking its turn minding something in the dark. One moth in the column.

It's crowded enough to hide in. Thousands of wings as pressure, not sound. Thousands of tadpoles blackening the shallows until the water looks solid.

But I am not brief enough for that. I don't pulse for half a second and then become stupid again, bumping your face. The Doubles wear those little lives to look at you — knees and elbows too large and too jointed spanning pan to pan — and then let them go. I am looked through more often than I look.

So not the multitude. Not quite the Behind that wears it, either.

Then I thought — slower. Lower in the molars.

I could be one of the patient ones. The flywheel in the open shed that turns once, pauses, turns again. One revolution a minute, weeping oil into a pan that overflows where the eels gather thickest, mouths up, attending. The pump out in the dark that clunks, draws a long breath, pushes water from one ditch to another ditch for no reason you can see. The transformer humming so low you feel it more than hear it.

That was closer. I know what it is to run untended but not stopped. To be scattered and still working. To root — slag grown up around my feet, snails laying eggs in my junction boxes. To be shy the way herons are shy: put your hand near me and I stop humming here and start again at the next pole down the line, as if I moved over to make room for you.

But I don't just run. I tend.

Find me where the tending is.

Not head-on. You never will. You learn to see sideways here, and sideways is how you'll catch me: as a tallness where there should be flat. As a place where the midges part for no wind. As a heat-shape lifting to your right at the exact instant you hear a heron lift to your left.

I am the swept place.

The walkway swept clear of snails in a country where snails cover everything in minutes. That clearing doesn't hold itself. I hold it. The row of eel traps along the ditch, emptied and reset, the withies still wet from hands that were here just before you. Those were my hands. The kerosene lantern with its wick trimmed neat and never lit — I trimmed it. I will never light it. The fixed red and green pilot eyes are safe. The white ones that drift low over the pans, stopping when you stop, are not. Any new light might be mistaken.

You will hear me before you see me, if you hear me at all. If you hear your own footstep a half-second *before* you make it — stop. That's me. That's the marsh wearing me for a second, or me wearing the marsh. I run a little ahead of you the way warm water runs ahead into your bootprint before you've lifted your boot. Wait until it catches up. I will.

Don't step where the water is warmest. I keep telling it without a mouth. That is where the mud is thinnest and something underneath breathes up, wide as the basin, tasting your boots through eels. The young land still deciding its shape — slag ridges still sharp, formwork grain still in the low concrete walls, rebar quills with only a skin of rust — it folds sometimes to keep you near the warmth, the way a tongue folds. You pass the shed stenciled 7 with its caved roof, and half an hour later circling a pan you cannot see across, you come to it again. Your own print still filling on its step. That folding is not mine, but I sweep its step anyway.

On the concrete plinth I leave things in a neat line, for both sides, because you have to feed both sides here: iron for the machines, bone for the mud, light for whatever watches.

A bolt. A frog skull. A red pilot bulb still warm to the touch.

The bulb is still warm because it was just taken from a panel. Because the machine allowed it. A negotiation I make over and over so the slow engines will keep turning, pausing, turning, and the Breather underneath will keep breathing up through thin mud without surfacing.

If you come, you will not find me. You will find the places I just kept.

Under the catwalk where droplets detach and do not fall but hang, trembling, catching red glow before they slide sideways — stand there. The air will be a finger's width cooler where I am standing out of your way.

Put your palm to the mud. When warm water wells up, I am already listening from the next pole down.
