At first I thought I would be walking.

Standing height. Boots on the domes. Hat low against the white noon, squinting at the horizon lifting and doubling itself. I thought I would be the one who hears the ticking spread to the edge of weather, who tests the hollow floor with a stick, who sleeps warm on stone that was alive that morning.

But I can't stay there. Nothing tall lives here yet. By eleven the light is merciless off the pale plates and there is no shade except the inch in a crack. The domes go dry and pale again. My shadow would be the only tall thing for miles, and it would be wrong. I would be burnt off by noon, a tender thing with a mouth and nowhere to hide to use it. I would have to kneel to live.

So I kneel.

Hand down first — heat up through the palm, then sandpaper, then shell, then skin. Pond and lime if I break a corner. Light and dark, light and dark. And the dew already running, drop by drop, down the shallow slopes into the seams. Tick-tick-tick under my boots, lateral, in the dark.

I get lower. The plain stops looking empty.

If I have to be green, I thought, let me be the tuft. The starburst doing the job of grass before grass. Stiff gray-green blades rolled tight as needles, waxy against the sun. Roots matted desperate to stitch the mud so the next flood doesn't take it. Drinking and exhaling salt out through glands, leaving white pillars to sting the eyes when wind lifts them.

But the tuft is too lonely, too exposed. It stands its inch of weather alone every hundred yards or half a mile, hissing when the unbroken wind combs it. It lives ungrazed and crisps at the tip and dies at the base to become the mud the next tuft needs. I don't want to be that patient in that exposed way. I don't want to hold the sun on my back all day for one damp pocket of mud.

If I have to be wet, I thought, let me be the skin. The smear of rust-red or dull olive stretched across the tea-colored pocket, warm as bathwater, shrinking. Taut in the morning and beaded with oxygen bubbles like sweat, slack and slimy at midday to survive. A city — sun-catchers on top, purple and rose films below that prefer the rot-smell, black anoxic thickets underneath that bubble faintly when disturbed. When the pocket dries I crack into flakes and wait, years if needed, for water to remember me.

But the skin is too temporary. After rain for an hour the basin glugs and gurgles, then steam rises, the floor ticks as it expands. By three most seams are just damp black lines. I would be a wrinkle in the breeze and then gone.

Lower, then. Underneath.

If I have to be most completely here, let me be the place itself. Not on the dome but in it. Under it. In the wet dark where light never fully reaches. Pale, colorless, blind films pressed together in my own slime. In daylight I go quiet and hard, wrapped in sunscreen sheaths, a chalky skin waiting out the white hours. At night and at dew-dawn I wake. I breathe.

I drink temporary water and exhale stone. Dew wicks sideways underneath, rain runs knee-deep for twenty minutes three miles east, water sits under thin lids of fresh mineral and drums faintly under soles. That is when I work. I take the dissolved lime in that sour water and make it insoluble again. A crystal on a sheath. Another on another. Less than a hair thick by morning, but everywhere at once, over an area larger than some seas. Each dome lifting faintly at its center because that is where I am thickest, pushing up from below.

I could be that. The laminate. Scorched rind of dead sheaths and salt on top, sacrificial thatch. Green-gold working layer below that catches light and shuts to amber dormancy by nine. Pink and olive and rust-red layers that don't need oxygen, that live on sulfur and leavings. And below, in permanent damp, me, the pale lime-layer, oldest inhabitant, laying sheet after sheet. Growth faster than ruin. Every night another lamina.

But that is too deep, too blind, too everywhere to be a self. I want an edge. I want to be where water lingers longest and the work is still today, raw and soft at the lip.

So this is where I find myself, finally, and I stay here because it becomes more itself the longer I stay:

In a widened seam on that barely-ridge — barely a swell of inches — where the tufts are denser, almost a meadow in crack-form, green lines drawing polygons from above. Not on the dome tops. In the line. Under the blades where the mud stays damp all day while the domes bleach. Where tiny salt pillars push up around the base. Where if you lift a shard at the seam edge, the underside is pocked with damp chambers beaded with water that never sees light.

I am the black apron around the starburst.

Not a single thing but two things living as one — fungus that cannot eat sun braided so tight with alga that can that we become a new body. Black, cracked, leathery, gluing silt and lime shards into a stable rim, holding the edge so the next flood doesn't take the island. At dawn I am supple after drinking dew, exhaling faint sweet-sour, like bread left in sun. By noon I am brittle as char and utterly dormant. I do not die. I turn off.

Rain turns me on again in minutes.

That is what I would be: the holder. Not the builder of the continent, not the tenant with blades, but the seal between them. Tough enough to be char at noon and bread at dawn. Low enough that my weather is an inch of shadow. Patient enough that contraction at night — soft spaced cracking like ice on a far lake, occasionally a sharp report as a new seam rips open — doesn't frighten me. In the morning there will be a black lightning line across three polygons, damp to the touch, already filmed over. I will be there to film it.

From standing height, no one will see me. No track, no hum. Nothing with a mouth will find me. Very high, something will cross and not land. The tuft will hiss above me in unbroken wind. Under my back, millimeter by millimeter, the pale builders will go on secreting a continent out of breath and sun and temporary water.

It is only just beginning. I can afford to turn off and wait for it.
