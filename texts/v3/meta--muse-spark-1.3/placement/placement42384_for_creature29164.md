Not on the rim. I thought at first I would stay up there.

Up there the wind still means something — direction, weather, coming weather. You can stand on the broken teeth and listen to the whole bowl humming below you like a struck throat and tell yourself you are the kind of thing that listens from above. A glider, maybe. I tried that on for a minute. Manta-thin, screaming needle-whistles to see in the gloom. It felt right — to navigate by making noise and hearing it come back.

But I don't want to scream all the time. And I don't want to hunt heat.

Then descending, boots squeaking on salt, tongue cracking, I thought: maybe I am built to be huge and still. A thunderleaf. Squat and finished, corked bark like basalt, twenty blanket-leaves furred grey-white combing the air with a faint tick-tick-tick. To carry a whole jungle on your back because nothing can be shared across a hundred paces of singing dust. To accrete instead of flow. That felt right too, for a longer minute. I like the idea of maintaining rather than becoming.

But I'm not that patient. I'm not that old. I fidget in loud places. My ears keep turning. And if you tear me, I still bleed.

Lower, then. Down among the ground fur.

The bellows-moss gives under my boot and sighs back up, exhaling dust. The carpet-lichen breaks to powder under my knee and is already re-knitting. The chime-trees go off overhead — thousands of dry maracas — and for a moment I think, yes, this: spindly and nervous, ten feet of knotted wood hung with paper gourds full of loose seed. To be stripped naked by locusts in four minutes and push out pale new furls by evening. To rattle even in a light gust. That's closer. I recover fast. I make noise without meaning to.

But I don't just hang there and rattle. I tend. I pick things up.

You'd find me, finally — if you stayed until your sweat stopped and your throat ached — not at the fire-pit in the low basalt ring, though I use it. Not together with the other two or three, because we are never together. You'd find me in transit, which is where we actually live.

In the hundred paces between. In the open singing dust that holds the presences upright.

I'm on my way from the second thunderleaf to the third from the fallen tooth, cloaked in powder, clothes full of it. Short, compact, settled in form the way everything here gets settled — skin salt-caked until it squeaks, lips sealed dark with chewed wax, hair felted into mats that comb dust exactly the way leaf-fur does. My eyes narrowed to slits, milky at the edges against the permanent ember air. My ears — my ears have become my eyes, large and cupped and always turning.

I carry the obsidian comb, teeth worn smooth. Not a weapon. A tending tool.

In the lee of my leaf — I know it by its slap, deep and slow when the gust hits, which means the wax is heavy on the sheltered side — I kneel and scrape. Not stripping. Clearing the sealed amber beads along the margin so the fur can comb again. Taking the oldest thumb-joint knobs, leaving the weeping ones. Working a knob in my jaw for an hour for food and for the little bound moisture exhaled in breath, breathing shallow through hairy nostrils, exhaling into cupped hands to catch it back. Chewing, breathing powder, listening.

That's what I do between: shake a chime branch and listen for which gourd rings fullest — that's seed for cake pounded with wax-dust. Mend a little of the broken bone fence of ribs and fiber to funnel pouch-mice, take two slit for their warm wax and their pouches full of concentrated knobs, let the rest boil past chittering. Hang a cracked clay bell on the tripod to clack sharp and scatter locusts off a young furl. Leave the glider skull on the cairn facing the wind. Not worship. Acknowledgement. Listening made visible.

If you watched from under the moss you would think I freeze for no reason. I freeze between gusts and tilt my head. That silence is where things hunt.

I am not lush despite dryness. None of us are. I am lush because of it — curled, layered, overlapping, carrying everything I need on my back. My pouches bulge with stolen knobs. My mats catch molt-snow — transparent wing-cases, shed fur, hooked down fired from a seed-pod with a gunshot crack — and I shake it out at night to become soil to become dust to be combed again.

Kill me and I will not bleed. I will ooze warm wax for a minute and dry to husk within the hour. Leave my felt for moss to catch.

Until then, look low, in the lee, in the long walk between giants while the small bowl roars to itself. Listen for the scrape, scrape, pause — listen — of the comb. That's me, accreting.
