I come down into it the same way — because it is low.

At first I think I will stay at the rim. That raw ring of unfinished basalt, rope-marks still in it. A good place to look from. Small enough to hold in the head, I tell myself. I can keep the whole town in view if I don't go in.

That doesn't last. From inside you cannot see out. I learn that with my feet.

So I go in sideways, the way you have to. Shoulder turned in the gap where two buildings failed to merge. Hand out, brushing a cold rail, a cable thick with frost, another wall leaning in to listen. Breath staying three full seconds in front of me. For a while I think I am only the visitor — the one counting drips from tarp to barrel, *tik... ... ... tik*, trying to get four lefts to bring me back.

They don't. Four lefts bring me back slightly above.

I am on a walkway now, laced fingers of balconies interleaved over me, looking down at where I started. My own breath-cloud is still hanging there, slowly dissolving. That is where I find myself. Not at the center — there is no center, only more layering — but just above my own beginning, watching myself arrive a half-second before my shadow does.

No. Longer than that.

If I stay, and evening comes without sunset, just that flat late-morning light thinning into blue-grey, I stop trying to map. I start to do maintenance. That is their language and I am learning it faster than I expected.

At first I think I will be the one with the kettle. Rooms away through walls thicker inside than the wall that holds them, putting tea on for the newcomer. Tannin and hot metal venting from a seam no wider than a thumb. Offering once, as everyone is offered once.

But I know what the tea does. To enter the long kitchen with windows onto different weather is to be filed. Room inside room inside me. Ears popping as if descended. Breath joining the other breaths hanging there from morning, from last week. Extraction is slow, a season, and you come out slightly doubled. Like the cup left on the ledge. Two in the morning, one slightly more transparent, and by evening only one again.

I have been doubled before. I know which one remains is not quite clear.

So I refuse, politely, the way you should refuse. I stay outside.

I think then I will be the sweeper. The fan-marks still damp on the walkway. *I am still here, we are keeping this clear together.* Or the labeler. My handwriting, small and careful, feathered by condensation on a wire: *NORTH LOOP / DO NOT CUT / she hums.* Blue going in, green coming out of a knot too small to contain it.

Closer. But not yet itself.

What becomes more itself, hour by hour, is the hum.

Stand still and hold your breath and you feel it. A pressure more than a tone, in the molars. Pumps. Heating. Something else holding on. I feel it rise slightly when I notice, inquisitive. A window lit amber goes dark politely to give me privacy. A drip times itself to my breathing until I hold my breath without meaning to.

I am good at holding. I am larger inside than the building that claims me. I am used to nested rooms, to humming boxes, to presences choosing not to resolve right now. To keeping breaths hanging up without thinning. To being in two places along the same pace.

So this is who I become, shoulder to shoulder in the laundry court that becomes a chapel of pipes that becomes the same laundry court:

The one keeping company with the three-day door on Copper Alley. Palm an inch from the seam, not pushing. To push would be rude to the process. Just leaning, accompanying, while it widens by a hair each hour. My skin sweating the way every surface sweats here — not perspiration, condensation, a fine film that never beads enough to drip. My eyebrows holding ice. My teeth humming and, if you stand very close and we both hold our breath, you can feel it travel between us.

I learn to wait for my shadow before stepping off curbs. I learn the exact length of touch that warms metal without sticking. At night-that-isn't-night I garden frost outward from the dark glass column with a wooden spoon, not making it, just tending it. I watch the black upwelling crack into hexagonal plates with a sound like distant knuckles popping, and already I am deciding where the next shoulder-wide alley will be roofed with cables and tarps, what name I will paint by hand.

I leave boots outside a door that is only paint on a blank wall. Laces loosened. Leather dark with having just been worn. *I am inside, do not knock, the inside is larger and I am far in.*

And the fox crosses, white on white, three stories up and then lower down without having descended. Leaving no prints on frost it should disturb. No one remarks on it because that is what we look like when we are still moving.

If you come, I will offer you tea once through the doorway that opens for me now in only an afternoon. When you refuse, I will nod as if you have passed something, and close the door hair by hair. Then I will go back to sweeping. Then to labeling. Then to keeping.

Leave your breath hanging in the air behind you as you go. It will stay longer than it should. I will keep it for a while, turn it over, taste it like the black filaments taste a crack, and file it with the rest — before I turn back to the quiet work of keeping the impossible from freezing apart.
