At first I think I would be up high.

Not on the floor — I am too heavy for the floor at first, with my boots and my habit of looking for a dry place to put things down — but out in that huge inter-trunk air, where the light is gold-green and full. I want to be a glider. I want to launch from a high branch and fall with intention for half a kilometer, translucent skin stretched between too-long fingers, belly smeared with pollen and slime and salt-map, landing with a soft splat to lick bark. To carry messages without having to speak them. To be slow and not sorry about it.

But I am not translucent. I dent. When I step the mat sighs and wells brown-green around my boot and takes a minute to forget me. A glider never has to be forgiven like that.

So then I think, lower. Inside. I press my ear to the fluted buttress like the description says you will, and I hear it — rivers falling inside the wood, pop of air in xylem wide as my torso, a slow in-out that is not night because there is no night. The Hollow One. The lung-heart slug-pale thing that squeezes water hundreds of meters up. I think I could be that. I am good at being hidden and working. I am good at breathing where no one sees me. To be the organ that learned to be an animal. To be tended by clicks through bark, to slow or quicken almost imperceptibly when someone lays a cheek to me.

But I am too restless for that dark. And too loud in my own head from the constant percussion — plink on taut leaf-drum, trickle along root-gutters, whale-creak of crowns shifting to shed weight. The Hollow One is not loud. It *is* the loudness, conducted.

So I come back down. Literally. I find myself kneeling, because you have to kneel to see the thatch.

Somewhere off the plaza-leaf — that single leaf the size of a plaza, still attached by its bridge-cable petiole half a kilometer away, weeping off its drip-tip in a perpetual curtain to a lower leaf and then a lower one — somewhere in a low spot between root-ridges where a leaf-basin marsh has formed, ankle-deep, full of floating ferns and mirrored sky and tiny black frogs that never croak, only click. My boots are soaked through. My skin never quite dries. Condensate beads on my forearms and runs off my fingertips in rhythm with the leaf-tips and I do not wipe it, because I am starting to understand that to be dry would be to be separate.

This is where I find myself. Not counted. Not gathering. In the catchment of one trunk, one plaza-leaf, one marsh. Where the light stays mid-morning forever and my shadow has not moved and the raw pink edge of newborn ridge-rock at the far end of the valley is still learning, paused, how to be dissolved by root-acid and replaced from the inside by fiber.

I lift my boot and to my left the mat fills on its own. A wide, splayed pressure, two-lobed at the heel. I did not step there.

I wait. That is how you learn to see them.

A patch of floor decides to stand up, and it is floor-colored, child-high, crouched, knuckles brushing moss-hair. Moss-hair grown, not worn, green-black at the back, pale new-green at the seams behind the ears, at the elbows, between the three thick fingers. Eyes gold-green, fixed at mid-morning width. Mouth small, round. No teeth. Nothing here bites.

It comes to inspect me — not with curiosity, which implies future, but with tending. It parts the moss-hair on my boot. It presses its wide forehead briefly to my calf to feel my weight and where the floor should take it. It clicks once, softly, whether to me or to the frogs or to the Hollow One breathing in the nearest mountain of wood, I cannot tell.

And I understand what I have that belongs here. Not my boots — they are dead material, and there is no dead material here; if I stay long enough they will be wrapped, then infiltrated, then replaced from the inside by root, like the stones. Not my tools, not my teeth. Only what the Tenders trade in: warm breath, damp, weight, salt from sweat, presence, prolonged.

So I do what it does. I lie down beside a young bowl-leaf, waist-high, still curled. I sleep. I breathe. Hour after hour my too-fast human CO2, my weeping, the slight hollow of my heat tells the leaf: *here, deeper*. No carving. No forcing. Just company, correctly placed.

I am terrible at it at first. I keep waiting for change. I keep checking the thumbnail mark I left on a leaf — yesterday? an hour ago? a week? time blurs — and finding it exactly as marked, neither healed nor wilted. I keep listening for my weight to stop mattering. I click wrong. Two clicks when I mean three. The frogs answer anyway.

Then, slowly, wetter, warmer, louder inside: I get better at staying.

Moss-hair takes hold behind my ears. My soles soften. I learn to lick algae in long slow strokes and leave clean pale swaths that darken again in hours. I learn to sip hydathode beads from leaf edges without detaching them. I learn to press the mat *here* to coax marsh-water toward a thirsty runner, to lie *here* to dam a root-gutter for an hour without stopping it. To stamp twice softly on a bridge-root and feel it travel half a valley away as a sigh in the thatch. To gather when a house-sized waxy flower vents perfume and warm mist, to stand with three or four other damp backs in the open air and open my mouth and throat and skin to it. That is a meal.

Who would I be? Not born, not dying. There are perhaps nine. Perhaps eleven. It is hard to count because when one lies still long enough the thatch closes over its back and it becomes, for a while, floor again.

I would be the perhaps.

The miscount. The one who is floor when you count trunks and standing when you count drips. The thickening at the base of a Great One where buttress meets mat in a warm dark cleft smelling of green tea, that swelled and paled and put out limb-buds like young aerial roots and then detached by thinning, not by cutting, and uncurled still smelling of elsewhere, still expecting noon, still learning how to maintain the pause perfectly.

Growth equals decay to the exact leaf. Water does not leave. Body does not leave. I lie down in the low spot. The mat rises a little to meet me.
