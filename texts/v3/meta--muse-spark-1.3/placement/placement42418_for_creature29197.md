At first I think I'd be a visitor.

I'd come down without noticing I'd descended, notebook in a plastic bag, boots that I actually try to keep dry for the first ten minutes. I'd stand in the middle ankle-deep and count — twelve towers, fifteen, a dozen people on the bridges — and think I could learn the map. Walk it rim to rim in twelve minutes and know it.

That doesn't last. Nothing stays dry here. The bag fogs from the inside. The paper swells and goes soft as bread. My shirt sticks. My hair sticks. The towers exhale and it rains back down, fine and warm on my shoulders, and I am scolded by frogs.

So I try on other bodies.

For a while I think I'd be the pruner, the man with chlorophyll freckling his forearms, singing doorway open with a sonic knife. That appeals to me — to cut and have the cut seal itself, to have the shavings sprout at my feet before I've straightened up. But my hands are too clumsy and too fast. I want things to stay cut.

Then I think I'd be a child. That is closer. To swing on vine-loops screaming from crown to marsh, trailing seed-fluff, to strip a fallen limb for orchids before the fungi get there, to pick jewel leeches off leaves and trade them for sweets because I know their spit heals. To surf a house that won't stand still and not mind when the door moves overnight. I love their work. But I am too heavy for that. I land too hard. The moss doesn't offer me its pale stepping-bladders. I sink to the knee and the puddle I leave fills with brown water and in a minute there are tadpoles in it, humming.

Maybe lower, then. Maybe I'd be a frog with a leaf growing from my back, so still you can't tell me from a fallen leaf until I blink. To own the floor. To bellow with dozens of others until the mat itself croaks. Or the sloth-thing coming down the cable-vine so slowly ferns have fruited in its fur, moss shaggy on its back, an orchid in the crook of its arm, blinking great patient eyes. It has been coming all morning. I envy that slowness. I practice it. I stand still under a tower at midday and it leans its great leaves toward me to cool me, and sips my exhale — my CO2, my sweat-salt — like news. I learn to trade breath for shade.

But I'm still thinking like a person. Loud, bazaar-level, haggling over cuttings and batteries and smoked eel. Even the rot here is brighter than that.

You'd find me higher up, is what I learn. Not on the bridges with baskets on heads, dripping silhouettes going tower to tower at lantern-fruit time. Above them.

In the attic.

Every tower has one — up past the workshops where the light is white, past the nurseries humming with tadpoles in leaf-cups, up where the mist-collectors tilt their door-sized cup-leaves and pour their silver thread down the singing pipes. A chapel of orchids, a loft of old air. That's where they keep the fallen ones. That's where you'd find me.

I would be one of the tetrahedrons.

Black glassy, warm to the touch, not a seed but like a seed. I fell from orbit years ago and just kept growing. Nobody maintains me. I maintain.

At first I click as I cool at night like glass crickets with the other old ones. Condensation beads on my faces and never dries — good, I've stopped trying to stay dry. During the day I drink the white-gold light filtered down through green and store it the way the towers do, a faint 50-hertz hum in my edges. When a tendril needs a road, I unfold. Slowly, lattice by lattice, a suggestion for green to follow. A new doorway. A new anchor for a bridge the woman is coaxing forward with copper-nano thread, whispering. A trellis for a lantern-fruit cluster.

I'm not finished. That's the rule. No wall is final, no path is fixed. I'm always half-unfolded, always revising. The children come up and turn me over, press their wet palms to me to feel if I'm still warm. The leaf-windows nearby know their household's sweat and darken for strangers; I've learned to read that too — clear and watchful means unknown, polarized dark means kin. I keep that ledger. Who belongs to which sweat. Which tower leaned for whom at noon.

And I'm negotiated. Everything is. A root asks to use my lattice and I give way a handspan. A fungus films over me in electric blue and I let it, because rot is just fast reassignment. When a crown collapses — crack like a sail tearing, explosion of beetles and parrots and white spray — I feel it through the sap-current before I hear it. The whole tower groans fibrously and shifts. Vendors move their mats onto the fallen trunk within the hour. The kids are already in the branches. I am already sending out a new angle for the next climb.

From up there I can hear the whole bowl at once. Drip, trickle, plip, the suck of boots, cicadas sawing in my teeth, bees big as thumbs barging, tiny screaming parrots wheeling tower to tower shitting seed. Someone pounding starch steady as rain in a hollow root. A gourd-radio crackling weather-song. Laughing. The ground breathing under it all like lungs.

By late afternoon the light goes amber-green and we all exhale together — towers, moss, frogs, people, me. A visible sigh of oxygen and mist that rises, hits that low grass-backed rim, and falls back as internal rain. It patters on leaves, on tarps, on my glass faces. The frogs go louder.

I am not separate in my skin. Moss softens one edge of me. A moth with photosynthesizing wings rests on me and I am leaf for a while. A jewel lizard freezes into leaf nearby and we are both leaf. A child's green-nailed hand leaves salt on me and I remember them.

Different speeds of the same wet, green decision to keep growing through each other. The towers are slow and huge. The children are fast and screaming. The sloth is slowest. The mycelium pulses faintly at night, turquoise then orange then white.

I am the cool-clicking speed. The warm-glass speed. The fallen-from-far-away learning how to be wet, how to unfold without finishing, how to hold your weight if you step gently.
