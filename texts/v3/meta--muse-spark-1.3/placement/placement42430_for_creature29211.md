At first I don't know. I hear it before I see it, like everyone does — that wet throb up through dry scrub — and I come down the sagging stair-roots thinking I'll know myself when I get low enough.

For a while I think I'll be fast. A swift, cutting the moth-weather by dim day. Or one of the bats by dimmer night, warm from the piston housings, smelling faintly of machine oil after the chimney figs. It would be good to never land. To live in the braid that never unties, only changes hands.

But I'm too heavy for that. I drop too fast. My eyes water in the green dusk and then adjust too wide and stay there.

So then I think, lower: a pressman. Hands to the elbow in nut-oil, nails green underneath that never scrubs out. Someone useful, watching a valve that has to be watched. I try that shape on. The cartwheel flywheels turning on steam and on root-pressure. The nine-second sigh of the boiler like a lullaby I would know in my sleep. But my lungs fill up too quick just imagining it — scale-lung, glitter on the cough — and I know I couldn't stand the third terrace heat all Change long without walking out into the drip just to breathe.

I think, lower still: maybe a dog. Lean, thin-haired, half-naked from the heat, shoulder to shoulder with the coatis at the oil-cake pile behind the mill. Barking at the section of rim where the waterfall was ten minutes ago. Scolding a ghost. There's something in me that wants to do that — to keep barking at an absence until someone laughs.

But no. The dogs are too sure. I am not sure. I keep moving.

What I keep doing with my feet gives me away. I am always stepping over. Without thinking now. The river of ants ten ants wide, up one root and down another with leaf-bits and paint-chips and dead moth wings. I don't step in the doorway. I step over. I curl my toes.

Not on a ledge. Between ledges.

That's where I find myself. Not in the boiler-house, not in the cistern dark, not in a hammock yet — on the long rope bridge between the wasp-nest lodgings and the press-mill, mid-crossing, when a troop of soot-black monkeys comes bouncing the other way and the whole chain-and-plank starts to buck under us.

I am a runner. A wiry aunt. Change watch.

I have a can of nut-oil in one hand and a twist of lamp-wick and a folded paper in the other, and someone's washer I am supposed to return. My calves are corded from this. My feet are the surest thing about me — bare, splayed wide, pruned at the fingertips and toes from never being fully dry. Cork wraps tied at my waist for after a rim-rain when the stair-roots turn to streams. Moth-scale in my braided-back hair like snow, like dandruff. Oilcloth apron gone soft as skin, stripped to the waist under it because it is always breath-warm, shirt-sticking warm.

I don't shout across. Sound lies in the bowl. It stacks and echoes until you can't tell mill from insect from voice. So I go myself, hand to hand, forearm to forearm, foreheads almost touching, mouth to ear because we are all hoarse from leaning in. That's my work: to carry it intact.

I know the timetable without a clock. Day Swarm, Change, Night Swarm. I run when the swarms pass and the bridges buck because that's when the news is ripest — a valve sticking, a lamp out on the west walk, a birth, a death that needs a sitter.

When the white waterfall pours over the rim for its ten minutes I am the one the children follow, sliding the stair-roots screaming with the swallows pouring over like water over a dam. When it stops and the whole bowl steams, I walk with the lamp-tenders and their long poles, relighting. Stay on the lit walk. It is our light you stay on.

I sleep shallow in a hammock slung from a living beam because the lodging wall flexes a half-inch in and out when the boilers vent and if you fought it you'd crack. I have learned to breathe with the venting in my dreams. I test the copper veins shining through split bark with my tongue like the bark-smiths taught me, metallic-sharp, and at night I follow the faint green glow where the vines have stolen charge to find the latrine path.

And when someone dies, I sit. We all do, in turn. The Eaters' Vigil. You don't leave the dead alone here — not for an hour, or the cleanup crew finds them, six legs and wings, faster than any burial. So I stay with the corpse on the cistern ledge, talking loudly over the stack — low thump and drone, mid patter of ten thousand drips, high tearing chitter — so the listening continues. Telling where each sound is, until the kin are ready. Mill means center. Drip-wall means west. Cicada swell means the canopy is about to shed water.

If you come all the way to the bottom, where the oldest root has cupped into a black pool of drip-water and oil-sheen, and look up at terraces of light receding, lamp after lamp spiraling through green gloom — I'm the small figure crossing high above you, pausing to let the monkeys pass, leaving fig seeds in return for a stolen washer.

Not settled in the forest. Not working in a factory. In between, in the black air between, keeping the lit path lit by moving along it.
