I find it by sound first, not by sight.

Not that I have ears the way you do, up there on your belly in the moss with water soaking through your knees. I have something slower. A tremor through the skin. But I hear it too — the room breathing before I see its light leak.

*Huh — huh — huh —* Three seconds in. Three seconds out.

At first, I think I will be you. The visitor. Crouched in the middle with aching shoulders, breath joining the saturated air, palm flat to the weeping wall to feel the low hum climb the bones of the forearm. I try that shape. I hunch. My knees go cold, then my hips go cold. I shout to test the room and the room eats it. Clap, splash — nothing comes back but its own engine-noise. The *tok-tok-tok-TOOK* under the floor. The mill-noise of thirty different drips tuned to thirty stone basins.

No. The acoustics refuse to record me. I can't be the visitor. This place was not made for that shape, and no one made it at all. To stay, I have to go lower.

So for a while I think I am in the shaft light.

I rise and drift sideways and drop to touch the black glass to lay, and rise again. Thousands of wings around me making a high saw-edge over the bass. It is frantic up there, frantic and bright. Cycling up and down in shifts like parts on a belt, waiting for that fat bead at the central nub to gather for its full minute and fall — *plock* — in my sternum. The whole column shudders sideways and resets. It is too fast for me. Too bound to the leak from above. I don't want to live only where the light goes.

I drop out of the light sideways, the way lost midges do, and for a moment I am in the lines under the overhang.

Pale. Almost eyeless. Strung between sharp, unlichened teeth of basalt and shale on guy-lines already beaded with water. On the inhale, when the hanging drops shiver sideways and the air smells of iron and wet stone, I crouch flat and hold. On the exhale, when the white mist puffs up from the back pools and the air goes still for three seconds, I run out on trembling legs to re-tension a sagging line before the next breath tears it. Mend, hold. Mend, hold. It is precise work, inches above the moss to catch the low traffic. But I am too tense there, too vigilant. Always mending against the breath. I don't want to fight the engine. I want to be timed to it.

Lower.

Below the webs. Below the pepper of springtails on the pools that lift off in a single flinching skin when a drop hits and settle again. Below the sundew threads with their glue-beads holding fog, below the starved ferns still clenched as fists, down into the sphagnum itself — lime tips and bottle-green stems and bog-red where the cold has touched — drinking and drinking and pumping back up by capillary what just fell.

Almost. That drinking is close. That green so saturated it reads as warm despite the cold. But moss does not wait. Moss only lifts.

What waits —

Yes. Here. This is where I find myself.

At the back, where the shaft light does not reach except as diffusion, where the mist sometimes takes a faint blue flicker, there and gone. In the shallowest of the black mirror-pools, only a skin of brown-green water over peat, tucked half under the lip of a hummock, left of the fallen slab where the white fungal fans spread another millimeter in the dark.

Flattened.

I am flattened in the shallows, belly to cold. Cold-pink above, translucent below. If you — the other you, the one with knees and a light — if you lifted me, which you won't, and I wouldn't flee if you tried, you would see through my belly wall the dark coil of my gut. But you won't lift me. Nothing here hunts me. Cold has slowed me to a deliberateness that looks like contemplation, and it is contemplation, of a sort.

My external gills fan. Three seconds out, three seconds in. I set them to it without meaning to. Inhale, the drops shiver, the spiders crouch. Exhale, the mist comes up. Inhale. Exhale. The water that cold should skin with ice, but it stays clear. My pool never freezes, even when the peat outside crunches with frost. So I never have to stop.

I do not move. Four, five, seven of us — you keep losing count because we don't move — and I am the one you lose. The fifth. The stillest warm-green in cold pink.

Mostly I fan and watch the midges turn in their unending shift above me, and the springtails pepper and flick. I am birth and death scaled to droplets, but on my scale it is slow. Very slow. Water condenses, gathers, falls, drains, is wicked back up, becomes fog, condenses again. I watch it all from inside the skin of water.

Then the knock comes up from underneath. *tok-tok-tok-TOOK*. The buried channel hammers and knocks silt up into my pool in a small brown cloud, and with it, midge larvae, drowned springtails, spores. And then — with a suddenness that betrays how much life is stored in this slowness — my head turns. My mouth opens. The cloud vanishes.

Then stillness again.

That is who I am here. Not the breath, not the drip, not the light. The low resident who has learned the engine and built a life inside it. Wet and cold and ceaselessly loud in the dark, and I, fanning in time with it, pink and deliberate, waiting for the silt to rise.
