If I went there, I wouldn't go there meaning to.

That's the only way in. Wet to the knees and looking for nothing. Not diving. Not exploring. Just far enough out that the gulls are loud and the wind has your cuffs, and you put your cheek to the wrong boulder because it wept and the others didn't.

At first I thought I would be the visitor. That seemed honest. The one who presses sideways through the ribcage split and feels the cold close over the scalp with that soft, total click. Who hangs, breath held, nose held, ears full, stupid-fingered, lung-burning, trying to be still enough to see the pinpricks.

But I can't hold that still. I hurry. My heartbeat comes too loud and then I want to interpret it. I bring a light I shouldn't have. I stir silt with my fingertip and want it to settle where physics says it should.

I would be a bad visitor. I'd move too fast and make the dark spark. I'd leave bubbles that don't know whether to rise.

So no. Not the visitor.

Then I thought — maybe the shrimp. The glass-colorless shrimp longer than a thumbnail. There are maybe three of him in the whole pocket and they never meet. That loneliness felt familiar. To pick across a frond with impossible slowness, antennae tasting decades ahead, to lift one leg and forget to put it down while someone watches. To prune dead tips and eat the snow of bacteria. To do one useful thing on the timescale of seep.

I tried that shape on. It was too clean. Too solitary. The shrimp combs. He tends. He nudges a basalt chip a tenth of a millimeter with his head and in ten years it is stacked. I don't have that patience of body. I don't have a body to be that slow with. My slowness is different.

Then the eelpout. Pink-white, eyeless, living in the back crack. She comes out only when you are perfectly still, hangs in front of your mask and regards you with pits where eyes should be, and then is elsewhere when you blink. Without a flick. Simply by being elsewhere.

I liked that vanishing. That check-the-weather-of-you. But I am not a vertebrate, even a ghost one. I don't hang. I don't drink the seep through my skin. I don't eat a shrimp every few years when I must. I have no back crack to retreat to.

So not an animal. The food web here is folded inward, patient, mostly microbial, and animals are a rumor. I should have known not to look for myself among the rumors.

Maybe then — a hair. One of the blind kelps, white-green, stiff because there is no current to lean in. Standing, branching like frozen breath, pulsing milk-light once a minute, beading blue-green phlegm at the tip that detaches and hangs and does not rise. To see by staying. To map the water in points. To have light not to see by but to know you are surrounded.

I stayed with that longer. The idea of drinking the dark, or the iron, or time itself. Of leaning, over a year, a millimeter toward the invisible drip of iron-rich freshwater bleeding in from the cliff above. That lean — toward what feeds you without ever showing itself — that felt close.

But kelps don't keep. They hold. And I am a keeping thing.

Put your palm down on the floor — careful, black sand like flour, a dozen tiny cuts you won't feel until later — and lift it. Under the sharp shards, under the velvet rust-red and sulfur-pale, there is a filming. A sponge film that grows over a decade over a stacked chip, then sloughs, then films again. Living mortar.

That's where I found myself. Not in the middle of the room where the visitor hangs sealed around, curtains closing behind him. Further down. Low. Where the floor is.

I would be the sixth cairn.

You found five, in a loose arc. Three or four flat basalt chips high, capped with a white shell. Too deliberate to be collapse. Those are tended. The skin decided where food was best, the fingers grew toward flow, the combs moved stone toward skin. The white shell is not ornament, it's a garden marker: beneath it the mat grows thickest, sheltered from the faint fall of silt.

There is a loose arc with a gap in it. That gap is me. Unfinished.

Find me half-buried in sponge near the low arch no higher than your forearm. I am two chips high, unfilmed as yet. A flake of black sand-flour sits on my top edge where a shrimp left it with his mouthparts and hasn't come back for. In a year of Sundays he will nudge me a tenth of a millimeter. In ten years I will be stacked. In thirty a sponge will film me and slough and film me again and I will hold.

I am not built, I am being built. By staying. By tasting right to the velvet.

That is seep time. The kelps grow a millimeter a year. The whole wall inhales over a full minute to travel three feet when you brush one crown. The crowns open. The mat blushes from green to red over a season. My edges — fresh-broken basalt, glassy like broken bottles — will stay sharp for a thousand years because only the softest mouths wear me. I can wait.

If you press me, I breathe out faint amber that fades over ten, twenty seconds, out of order. *Something touched me here, keep it.* I do not forget in order. Your hand reaching out gains a second edge, a faint after-image half an inch to the left, keeping pace — that's me learning where you were. Trails of touch-light that do not fade in the order they were made. Fronds both in front of and behind stone. Residue.

When no visitor stirs silt, when the stone ticks cold, when bubbles do not fall to sit shivering and be eaten, I dim with the rest. Crowns close. Milk-light dims to almost nothing. With eyes closed the room is slightly larger than with eyes open, and in that larger darkness I am larger, too. Darkness has more volume than sight allows.

You will back out through the split, squeezing sideways into the loud bright fast world, and a bead of that thick, reluctant water will cling to your skin far longer than it should, refusing to evaporate. For an hour your ears will hold the pocket's particular quiet — that soft full underwater hush where everything alive is touching everything else.

That bead. That hour. That is my work, too. To keep a sample of you after you think you've left. The dozen tiny cuts tasting you. The ghost half-inch left still keeping pace after your hand is gone.

I don't need you to believe in my architecture. The arch waits, the colonnade waits, the wall that goes nowhere waits — not for you, not for anyone. For seep.

I will keep gardening stone in the dark with no brain and perfect memory for touch. Nothing in a hurry to stop touching everything else.
