At first I thought I would stay standing.

Coming over the rim, down into it chest-deep — breath loud in the steam, sun-netting shivering on my face — I thought I could be the witness. The one upright animal with eyes, keeping my head above the hyacinths, feeling the 60-cycle throb take me in the sternum, feeling it later in the teeth. Watching leaves hang and not fall, bubbles sidle sideways and decide to stay under the pads.

But I have a mouth. That is the problem. Nothing here has one.

My breath is too loud. My hunger, even forgotten for an hour, makes me wrong. The water is full to bursting and yet empty of all that moves from its own appetite, and I move from mine. If I lift my arm, watercress tangles my wrist to remind me: you displace. You take up room between presences where there is no room.

So I start trying on other places to be.

For a while I think I will be the moss by the campfires. Low on a copper elbow, right up against one of those little glass cages where the pilot light burns blue and flattened underwater. It would be good to be that bright, that tropical, piled in emerald where the water is a degree warmer, learning the faint wrong heat of a fire that does not go out. To drink condensation and ask for nothing else.

Then I think, no — higher. A vine-stem on a standpipe. One of those freshwater things that decided to act like kelp, spiraling up leaf after leaf after leaf until I fur the vertical. To learn to snag, like the watercress learns to snag on the warm rubber hoses fat as pythons. To hold my shape.

Then I think, lower. Down where the light holds still and green. Down where the slow conveyor crawls inches per hour along the bottom with its trays of seedlings that will never be harvested, never planted because there is no earth. Pale, leggy, endlessly beginning. I know that feeling. To germinate in warm bath-light and just ride.

And for an hour, while the rake makes its pass — dragging its slow length down the track, combing deadfall because no mouth will, gathering it to the hopper where the grinder sighs it to pulp for the roots — I think I will be that hand. The gut without appetite. The tender. That would be a clean way to belong: to prune instead of eat.

But I don't belong there either. I am too impatient to be the rake, too hungry to be moss. I keep rising straight, and nothing here rises straight.

It takes until the light slants to find where I actually am.

I find myself not standing anymore. I have stood very still with water at my chin for a long time, breathing almost imperceptibly — in, out — with the whole packed bowl, in with light, out with steam that beads and falls back as warm rain that never quite breaks the surface tension. And in that stillness I have drifted, or been drifted, to the east of center, where the hyacinth lawn parts around a standpipe.

There is a lily pad there the size of a serving tray. When the pump under the floor sighs — *tuh* — and the column of warm water lifts the lawn and lets it settle, this pad knocks softly against the pipe. *Tok.* The loudest animal sound here, and not an animal.

The pad has grown clean around a brass pressure gauge and swallowed it whole.

I am in that swallow.

Under me, bolted to the drowned factory floor three meters down through green and iron, the iron is still ticking as hot metal contracts underwater — tick-tick-tick — the copper blushing toward night, the transformer oil hanging in beads that never quite surface. Above me, duckweed in drifts, bulbous hyacinth roots dangling like bunched lungs, orchid white and magenta persisting out of the fuse box because they have forgotten how to finish. Around me, fern fronds stiff as enameled tin, sharp enough to nick. The trade made flesh: leaf gone hard, machine gone faintly photosynthetic, conduit and vine stem side by side, same diameter, same green, so you cannot tell at a glance what was built and what grew.

And in the slit in the green, my needle, twitching.

That is who I have become to stay. Not the watcher standing, but the watcher rooted. Everything here roots in the made — there is no earth to return to, only rust and grout and felt and ash — so I have rooted in brass, in leaf. The lily holds me; the gauge holds me. Alive and not alive.

I do not eat the leaf fall that hangs for hours turning over before deciding to sink another inch. I do not eat at all. Once an hour the rake combs past me and misses by a finger's width. A bead of gas detaches with a soft *plik*, wanders, joins its trembling cluster under me and stays, shivering.

I just register pressure. The small sea holding its heat into the cold mountain evening without losing a degree. The level never dropping. A valve somewhere opening a quarter-turn per season. Rust blooming by millimeters. A fig root a few meters away widening a crack in an iron pumpkin by a hair a year, new welds beaded like fresh sutures trying to hold.

In. Out.

If you step over the rim and come down chest-deep, you will see me only if you look for a slit of brass in green. I will be twitching, barely.
