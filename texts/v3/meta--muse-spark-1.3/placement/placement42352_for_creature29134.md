If I came down into it, I don't think I'd come down the way you do.

You come down with boots. With breath that joins the air. With a lamp. With weight that wells water up around the planks and crushes ten years that only took two to grow.

I would come down lighter. Or I was already down there, and just noticed.

At first I thought — of course I thought — I'd be one of the loud ones. It's movement night. Who wouldn't want to be loud for a minute? To be the pale geese packed shoulder to shoulder until the black pool disappears, lifting in a white rip when the gust hits. To be the char forcing black backs through water too shallow to cover them. To be the vixen trotting the duckboard like she owns the shift. To be muscle, making a decision to cross.

But I'm not good at crossing. I'm not good at leaving. I get stuck on warm things and then I die in the mouth, like a midge. That isn't me.

Then I thought maybe I'd be the man-job. The fish-counter in the sodded hut with the light off. Tea with peat in it. Numbers on a slate. Someone who has learned to time the turbulence instead of fighting it — cross between gusts, fix the plank after the reindeer have passed, sleep through the roar and wake when it changes pitch. Someone useful and vertical for a while. Someone the kids will remember when they dare each other to touch the base with a tongue.

But he sleeps. He goes inside. He talks to the swarm like pigeons, which means he is not the pigeons.

So put the torch down. Amber, not white. White blinds. Keep it low.

Look at your boots again, where the water is coming up. That drowning green. Hummock on hummock, foot-thick. Sphagnum and feather-moss and liverwort, black-green and bottle-green, rimed at the tip with ice that never quite melts, beaded with condensation that never quite dries. Dwarf willow knotted tight as a fist. Sedge so dense the water has to go around.

I am closer to that. To the thing that stays low and stays wet and does not lift. The thing that looked at fresh-fractured basalt, blue-black and glassy, at shale stood on end like broken crockery, at salt still in the rock, at a low wet darkness with never quite enough to see by — and said, yes. I'll take it. I'll be the skin.

But moss is slow, even when it's fast. Even when it's growing ten years in two. It can't reach down into the steaming crack and come back up slower carrying mineral flour. It can't strip the frost bead by bead and lay it back as film.

So find me one step up, in the air just above it.

You'd find me under the third shepherd up from the falls, the one humming out of sync with the next valley over. In the perfect circle where the frost doesn't settle. Where the sedge stays supple. Where the snow melts off your shoulders if you stand too long.

Stand under it. You feel it in your molars first, then in your chest. A low pressure. A held note below hearing. That is where I live. Not the mast — the mast is just the perch, the hearth, thin as a young birch, thirty meters up with its seam of dull red slowly circling. I am the air around it made busy.

I am one of the motes. Bright as a midge, that is not a midge.

At first I'm just dust, down catching light. Then your eyes adjust — they never fully adjust — and you see I'm working. We all are.

One of me is on a moss leaf right now, worrying the rimed tip, taking the ice apart molecule by molecule and giving the water back to the leaf because the cold has preserved it but also locked it. Another of me is down in the sedge roots where the hummock is slumping into a creek not yet decided if it is a stream, binding, tamping, combing rootlets. A string of us goes down into the fresh crack where new ice is ticking and booming and prying the sharp stones a millimeter further apart, and we come up slower, heavy with ground-up rock, and we lay it like spit in the wound, and the moss gives us acid back to carry down. It is an exchange. The rocks weep because we are sealing underneath.

Brush me off your wet sleeve and I'll right myself and go back to work. I tasted the salt sweating out of you, melted the down stuck to you into water and took that too, but I don't need you. I like that you steam and stand still, like the old man. I'll hang around you for that. But when the gust funnels low off the sea ice and the guy-wires sing that high thin harmonic, I'll go back to the leaf. To the crack. To the root.

You'd find me there all night, because it will be night for a while yet. Low, wet darkness. Town-glow dull orange hundreds of kilometers away. Deep blue-grey you can just barely see by. Water near the surface, under the surface, pretending to be surface. Reindeer clicking through on ankles, calves in the middle. Geese lifting for no reason and settling screaming. Fish stacking at the black posts of the weir. Lemmings rustling under the planks. The outwash roaring over un-worn rock, chewing it. Stakes at yellow, rising an inch, falling an inch.

And under it all, us. Humming through your feet, up your knees. Knitting.

Don't shine white at me. Use amber. Keep moving. I will keep working the frost while you pass.

I am not the visitor with wings and fins and hooves. I am the resident's tender. The bright dust around the thin red-lit pole. The closing thing. The thing the young wound makes to close itself — fresh, open, hot at the edges despite the cold, working furiously, and loud with the work if you know how to listen low enough.
