I thought, from the rim, I would be something with a view.

An owl, maybe. To quarter low all afternoon, unhurried, because there is no need to hurry when the thatch is alive under you. Or the moose cow out in the far channel, belly-deep and unbothered, pulling pondweed while the town parts around her legs. Something large enough to step over causeways.

Then I went down the raw peat stair, hands black, jacket damp in minutes not from rain but from the air touching me, and the town closed over my head. Willows chest-high, mist a foot above the pools forming and unforming, lodge-roofs green with their own sprouting. From the center you cannot see the rim. I couldn't be the thing that sees everything. There is no everything here. Only next, and next, and next: water, moss, thatch-dome, stick-dome, down-ringed nest, canal, willow wall, water again.

I thought I might be a goose. There are so many, it would be easy. To sit shoulder to shoulder in a bowl of sedge and down, so close I could stretch my neck and touch the next sitting goose. To preen on the mud bar with the molting young, serious and silent. But I am not good at sitting that still, that exposed, with fox stitch-tracks in the mud a foot away and packing as my only protection. I would cluck once, like the ptarmigan hen, and then freeze and hate freezing.

I thought I might be a beaver. A foundation. Forty, fifty pounds, chestnut-dark when wet, moving low through the avenues I dredged myself with my chest. To carry cold blue mud up in my forepaws night after night to keep ahead of water that rises a finger's width after every rain. But a beaver's house is five, six feet tall in knee-to-waist water, a roundhouse in the center where everyone can find it. I don't want the center. I want the side.

Lower, then. At waterline. Where the crossed sticks and sphagnum mats hold like floating sidewalks, where the underwater lattice of old caches and abandoned tunnels makes everything hold.

Listen: the soft suck as a head breaks water. The faint squeak of wet sedge being pulled. A hairline wake from a naked rope tail.

There. That's closer. That's mine.

I would be a muskrat. Not the logger-mason. The thatcher-ditcher. Small, brown, rabbit-sized, tireless, ten to one.

You would find me not on the main avenue but off it, where it narrows. My run is only as wide as my body plus whiskers. My house is low, the size of a wash-basket, woven from cut sedge and cattail that I cut myself, tooth by tooth. It leans against the side of an old beaver lodge gone green — willow wands stuck in as building have leafed out and rooted into the roof, so my neighbor's wall is a thicket. On my crown a pintail duck tucks under the overhang. I don't mind. Old nests become foundations for new nests. Nothing wasted.

I have an underwater door. That is my whole life, really. It wants to seal shut with moss. Sphagnum grows a centimeter a year upward to escape drowning, bottle-green and rust-red, soft as lungs, and it would close me in if I let it. So I don't let it. I nibble. Morning, night, between feeds. I cut to eat, I cut to drain, I cut to keep the door open. The tick of water dripping off a leaf into the pool is my clock. The rustle-chew of my own incisors on willow bark is the town working on itself, quietly.

Above me, a goose shifts and the whole dome shudders. I hear voles traveling through my walls — a constant faint dry movement inside the wet, a foot from the sleeping duck. Sometimes the shadow of the owl goes over without sound, moth-soft, and everything under thatch holds its breath, including me. Below me, in the run I keep open without meaning to, sticklebacks funnel past in a shoal so dense they darken the black water from below. At the narrows, the pike hangs motionless, green and barred, gills fanning, a yard long. He lets the town deliver food. My chewing delivers it. I never know it. I just keep chewing.

I surface without slap. In a normal pond a beaver slaps to warn. Here there would be no silence if we warned, so we have settled into truce and muffle. I break water, breathe — four degrees, high summer, my breath shows for a second and then is taken by mist — and I see: black water silvered under thin overcast, cotton grass nodding white on the collapsed lodge that has become garden next door, marigold and buckbean, sedges sharp enough to cut, the moose calf snuffling far off as it learns which stems are sweet. I haul a stem in my teeth, dive again. My V-wake is small and soon gone.

This is the rule, and I live it in my teeth: build up or drown.

The beavers dam the same channel I drain to float their logs. One street rises, another floods. A flooded dome goes flat and the terns take it the same week. My cousin's dome two runs over went that way last spring. She moved in with her sister for a month, packing tighter, until the water fell after a dry spell and the mosses built her a new hummock to start on. If I stop for a day, my door seals. If I stop for a week, a sedge sprouts from my roof seam. If I stopped for a month, a sandpiper would nest against me. The place does not permit vacancies.

So I don't stop. I am not the ground — that's the sphagnum they mistake for ground, closing over your boot dent as you watch. I am not the house that grows — that's the willow. I am the maintenance. The small brown keeping-open in the middle story, waterline, between the drowned lattice below and the down-ringed nests above. My drying shelf of stems above my door is always a little wet, always a little new.

If you stood long enough to see me, you would only see the suck, the ripple, the hairline. Then gone.
