I arrive the way you do, at first. With lungs.

Climbing through thinning trees and then no trees, up onto the broad back where the air is pale and cool gets into my sleeves. My boots sigh. Step and water sighs up, cold as a well. Step again and it sighs. My breath is visible when I exert myself. My fingers are slow.

For a while I try to stay that way — a visitor, upright, looking for a roof. My eye keeps trying to find a straight line.

It doesn't hold.

So I start trying other shapes. Letting them come and go in the ground-mist.

At first I think I would be one of the pale wolves. Long-legged, almost white, trotting the swells in a loose string. It appeals to me, that unhurried work, never barking because the moss would swallow a bark. To take only the old and the careless, to eat with my muzzle buried and steam rising off me until I look like part of the ground-cloud. But I am not that hungry. My feet are not that wide. I don't want to keep moving to stay fed.

Then I think higher. One of the vultures with the woolly white ruff, riding the upslope off the weeping cliffs in great silent wheels. To never flap. To wait. That is closer — waiting is something I know — but I don't want the sky. Up there is too thin, too far from the sponge. I want to be muffled, not exposed.

Then lower, brighter. A great pale moth, the size of a sparrow, rising at midday in a hatching so vast I dim the milky sun. To live a day, two days, to beat with felted wings no louder than someone turning a page. Millions of pages. That brevity is beautiful, but I am not brief. I don't want to mate in the air and die onto the moss. I want to stay.

A black ibis, shoulder to shoulder on plaited tussock, beading water perfectly off gloss? Too tight, too ammoniac, too much peeping swallowed by moss. A blind white catfish nosing peat-stones under tea-dark water, navigating by the blue nerve-flicker of algae? I love that darkness, hunting by field, never seeing the moor — but I want light, the milky noon that lasts for hours, the green that hurts.

Closer: the old female spider, amber-abdomened, head-down in the center of her fog-net strung between tussock heads. Tending a yard of beaded sheet. Repairing, tightening, harvesting stunned moths and wrapping them until the sheet looks strung with pearls. She never leaves it. Her whole life is that. That is very close. To tend, to repair, to wait inside muffling.

Closer still: one of the red-headed cutters, running the inch-wide highway kept as clean as a factory floor, carrying a green sail many times my size. Kilometers of two-way traffic through tunnels in the moss, under cushion plants, over living arches. Never hurrying, never stopping, bringing green down into the sour-sweet glowing gardens. Work never stops but never hurries. That hum below hearing.

But even the cutter travels kilometers. I am slower than that. I don't want to carry. I want to be carried on. I want to be the place the highway goes through.

Because no one lives here the way I meant living. There is no clearing to make. If you clear a space it will be filled before you have stood up.

So I let myself sink.

Not down to mineral — you rarely see rock bare here, quartzite and basalt learned to be round — but down to the mat itself. A meter thick, ten meters thick in places, built of dead on dead on living. That is where you would find me, if you stayed until pearl-evening and looked longer.

In a hollow between two tussock fountains, a hand's breadth above a black tarn rimmed with gold moss. Knee-deep in ground-cloud. Where the grazer line passes a hundred abreast, cropping the top inch with a soft piston clop-clop you feel in your sternum.

I am not the line. I am what the line crops and what closes over after.

A head of sphagnum. Rust and wine-red and green. One of millions of soft baffles the wind hits and goes quiet on.

That becomes more itself the longer I stay in it:

My leaves are concave spoons, each holding a bead of water that hasn't decided if it is cloud or rain. In the bead, slow bears — tardigrades — unfold and fold, grazing bacteria. On my stem, a liverwort has taken hold, flat and dark as a small liver. On the liverwort, a beetle pauses, chestnut and copper-green. On the beetle, mites, amber dots with legs. Packing goes down and up. Even my wool holds. I do not scrape anything off. Weight is not a problem here if you distribute it.

In the morning I am tipped with frost lace. By milky noon I have thawed and the rosettes above me — cabbages on stilts, their woolly hairs combing cloud — tip and pour, and I drink their overflow. Liters a day funnel down grooved leaves into cistern, then to sponge. To me. I swell. Step on me and I sigh water up around the boot, cold as a well. Lift the boot and within an hour my neighbors and I have leaned back over the footprint. There is no bare patch.

When the afternoon storm does not break but settles, drizzles, soaks, passes, I hold it. When the great pale moths rise in shifts and dim the sun and then die onto me, I hold them too. When dung falls steaming nearby and boils with beetles and vanishes down tunnels, I take the warmth and the richer green. When a footprint of ash — no, there is no ash here. No fire-blackened ring. No wall. When a wolf trots too near and the ibis on the next dome lift their bills in a bristling palisade, I absorb even that small alarm and return it a second later from a different direction, quieter, as damp exhalation.

At dusk the tarn beside me begins its faint electric flicker. My hair lifts a little before the thunder that walks around the edges and never quite leaves. The wool of the rosettes crackles. My water beads. Then rain again — it has just rained, it always has just rained — and everything unloads, metronomic dripping from underhangs, amber rills that run and then sink back down. Water never leaves, it only changes rooms. From cloud to hair to leaf to me to tarn to vapor to cloud. The plateau drinks itself, and I am one of its mouths.

I grow a millimeter a year and a continent wide. The cool forbids hurry.

Find me there in the evening when ten thousand ibis adjust on touching nests with a collective sigh, when the grazer line beds down, bellies combing moss, calves pressed flank to flank and steaming, when wolves curl nose to tail on a dry dome, when spiders tighten their sheets against night frost. No roof but cloud. No wall but mist. My clothes — no, I have no clothes now. I am beaded myself. I am inside a breath.

Quiet is not absence here. Quiet is how full sounds when everything fits.
