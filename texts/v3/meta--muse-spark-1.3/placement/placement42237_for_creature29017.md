You would not find me at first. That is how it is here. Past ten paces everything is dim anyway.

At first I am only a warmth in warmth. Up and down at once.

I wake because the drip changes. Not because it stops — it never stops — but because the thatch above my loft has sagged overnight with rain and steam, and the drop that has been falling on the pole beside my ear for a month lands now on my cheek. Cold for a second, then blood-warm like everything else. Below me the posts are talking to the lake. *Lap. Lap. Lap.* The night-watch. The tone is right. We have not sunk while sleeping.

I lie still a while in the shallow sleep we all sleep here, cheek by jowl. My sister's daughter breathes against my shoulder, slow, saving air the way we learn without learning. The air up here is too thin to waste. Her hair is never dry. Mine is never dry. Seeds are caught in it. Lily pollen dusts the mat. My hands where they rest on my belly are ridged and pruned even in sleep, fingertips gone soft as fruit, palms pale and spongelike. Old young hands.

When I sit up, my soles find it before my eyes do. That is navigation here.

The loft floor is woven reed, damp, sprouting. A thermal fern has come up through a knot by my foot in the night, dusty-green, unfurling toward heat that isn't sun. Midday looks like evening elsewhere. Here it is only ever a slightly deeper or shallower dim. No horizon. Only the low lid of vapor glowing faintly amber-green. I do not look far. No one looks far.

I go down the ladder, which is also going in. Rung, water, rung, water.

By the second rung I am waist-deep and the lake takes my weight too easily. That wrong held-up-ness. As if it is reluctant to let me go. Denser than water should be. Tea-dark and green-dark. My feet disappear after three paces used to be. Now I don't expect to see them. I feel for them — flat, careful set-down. Soles thickened to hide.

I am old enough that my eyes have started to milk over. The gatherer's film. The young ones tease me that I am becoming a Rememberer. Maybe. I still dive, but not like I did.

You would find me, most mornings, downwarm of center, where the ribbon-weed forest stands twenty feet tall in the shallows. Olive and emerald. The fronds lay flat on the surface and tremble when I step onto the living walkway. It holds. It has learned to hold toddlers and old women. Underneath, the light goes in slow pillars full of motes, then bottle-glass green, then nothing.

That is my garden. It is also my foundation.

My lines hang there — mussel-shell and bone strung on lake-hemp I rolled on my own thigh — seeded with edible weed that grows down, not up. Below them the snail-ropes, fat with the dumb abundant snails that are our meat that does not need to be caught. I have a shell scraper tied to my right wrist with cord. I have the copper sliver at my throat in oilskin, sharpened to nothing, treasured. My forearms are fine-cut with ribbon-weed. White seals, slow.

I do not gasp when I go under. Gasping is thin-air behavior.

I slide off without splash, just nose and mouth breaking at the end, the way eels surface. Eyes open in the gloom. The water is bath-warm, blood-warm, it does not shock. It pushes me up and I have to pull myself down hand over hand along the line. The algae blooms around me, faint glow even in day-dim, smearing my ankles, my thighs. I scrubbed it off this morning. By evening it will have returned. It lives on me as much as in the lake.

Down there I thin the forest. I train a frond left where my cousin's trap needs shade. I scrape snails into a basket. I feel with my ankle for the warm upwelling — downwarm, throbbing through the sole — where I must not stand long. The floor under my feet is still growing. If I press my palm flat I can feel it ticking. Black pillow lobes, glass-skinned, hairline cracks sighing out heat, mineral beards white and rust trembling. The blind white newts crowd there, waving their feathery gills in heat-shimmer. I watch them while I work. If they move out, I will move out. They know before rock cracks.

Sometimes, on the way up, I go a little sideways to visit.

A generation below my sleeping loft is another sleeping loft. Intact. Green-furred. An old doorway. A drowned hearth. A toy coracle still tied where a child left it when the water rose a fingernail width that year and they laid another course of black stone on the causeway and abandoned the lowest rooms to eels and lilies. I pry loose a good post that is already sprouting before it has collapsed. Rot and growth simultaneously. I leave a tuber in the doorway. An offering. Memory is infrastructure here because sight fails. Someone must remember where the ford was before the water rose.

When I surface I exhale steam into steam.

Midday-dusk I pick the white waxy shields before they die — the giant lilies live a single day — and climb, dripping, swimming, up the zigzag causeway toward upsun to trade fiber. The causeway is just awash, hand-piled black stone, pale in places. Knuckled with bone. My grandmother is in there somewhere, skull to stone, ribs to rubble. I walk her to get home. The children play knucklebones on the path. No one finds this morbid. In a place with no soil except what people have made, with no quarry except the still-ticking floor, the ancestors are the only seasoned stone. Drop an adze and it falls too slowly, as if considering. The dead, weighted properly, still often return after three days, buoyed absurdly up. So we house them. We walk on them. They hold us up.

On the far open water between causeways, sometimes, He surfaces.

The deep turtle. Huge and algae-backed, barnacled into a small island, older than the town. Exhales thin-air breath with a sound like a wet kiln sighing. Looks — or seems to look. The children are taught not to chase, linked ankle to ankle imitating glass eels. I leave the choicest snail-meat on a stake where He surfaced. Whether He eats or not is less important than that someone remembers to leave it.

Evening — though evening is only a slightly deeper dim — I sit in steam with the oldest, mending net that never dries, pounding fiber until the sound flattens across water and arrives late. Poles creak against rope. Drips plink leaf to lake, endless. Underwater, if you duck your head, you hear the rock itself breathing in a long seiche, an hour from one side of the caldera to the other and back. Everything here happens slowly. A flower takes a morning to go from one causeway to the next.

They ask me to recite. Because my eyes are milking, they think I am practicing to stay.

*Where was the ford when you were seven? Which underwater fence belongs to which cousin three platforms over? Where not to put your foot downwarm?*

I tell them. The town extends past the immediate not by sight but by recitation. I am becoming that. Part gardener, part eel, part ancestor-stone not yet set, part turtle-dream.

High and drowned. Young and crowded. Warm, dim, patient.

I am not a person in a lake. I am a place where the reef learned to breathe thin air on top while staying blood-warm underneath. I hold very still, and I hold the ones next to me up, and the water, reluctant to let us go, holds us all.
