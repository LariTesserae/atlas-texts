At first I think I'd be up high.

It's the void that calls me — that fifty-sixty feet of trembling nothing in the middle. I want to be one of the screamers, membrane wings and a keel of chitin, carving endless circles and never landing. To see the whole hamlet clinging to its own ribs below me. To scream and have it be useful.

But I can't stay up there. I know it immediately. My wings would tire in a minute. I don't know how to mate and sleep and die on the wing. And the void has to be used or it sags — I would sag it. No.

So then I think, lower. An intersection. Perfectly still. I try on the mantid — the color of the doorway I haunt, pruning the crowd without breaking the choir. That kind of patience feels holy. But I can't do it. I twitch. I look too fast. I would break the ant highway for a stupid reason, and even the frogs would go pointedly quiet at me.

Then even lower. Heavier. I try the gutter-keeper. Big as a thumb-joint, back like spilled oil, owning my stretch of sunken vein-let the way an old man owns a stoop. After indoor rain I walk and dredge and drink the clear sweet sap. When tapped, I lock down and become a jewel and wait for the other thing to die first. I like that. I like that a lot. But I'm not surly enough, not solitary enough. I want to answer when spoken to.

I keep sinking.

Because everything here is down, low, settled into warm air. The floor gives under my feet. Matte green-black and worn shiny in the middle of the paths by ten thousand generations of feet, claws, pads, bellies. Between the paths it is moss-velvet, springy, warm like a flank. That's where I find myself. Not on the midrib causeway where everyone can see me. Off to the side.

A side-vein street, three peristalses from the plaza — seven streets if you go, nine if you come back, you know how it is. Where the gutter widens and the sap runs slow after rain. Just off the polished path, in the hollow, in the velvet that sighs when the ceiling rains.

I am small. That's non-negotiable here. Small enough to leave the middle empty.

I am a frog, but not one of the old-timers. Not yet. A juvenile tree-frog the color of fresh sap, throat still learning its metronome. I'm cupped half in moss, shoulder to shoulder along the vein with a line of elders who don't move much because they don't need to. Ants stream beneath us jaw-to-jaw along the handrail ridge, carrying wax crumbs and midge wings. Above me a gecko is flattened to the bract-wall, eyes like wet seeds, toes splayed into negotiable gravity. At the corner, the mantid waits. A cloth of midges lifts off the fog — knee-high morning-exhalation smelling of crushed green and iron — and the elders around me swell their throats.

And I listen.

That is my function before it is my function. I don't sing yet, or I sing very quietly, feeling for the key that keeps the roof taut. The baseline is sitting on my sternum, the walls themselves vibrating, cicada-roar become pressure. Over it frog chorus in overlapping rounds, wing-buzz dopplering, sap hissing in pipes, plink-plink-plink of indoor rain. If I sing sharp, if I show off, if I sing against — the house I slept in will tremble. The pitcher-room lined with someone else's shed down, swaying slightly from the low ceiling. The street will flinch under me.

So I don't. I take my cue from the dusk-light drop, half a tone down, pulling the bellied ceiling back. I let the roar separate. I let it hold me up.

This is where I belong because I know how to do this one thing: stay in the hollow and keep the sound structural. Don't dam the gutters — the jewel beetle shoulders through beside me and I make room. Don't drink the cups — I lick dew off the wall instead, leaving the waxy axil-pools with their brown tea-water to the flicking tadpole-things, the nursery in public. Don't sing against the choir — not because I'll be punished, but because the place is total, and I can feel it in my teeth when we're right.

At night when light through the veins goes indigo and the moths pour out like folded handkerchiefs to take the void, dusting everything by morning for the mites to sweep, I'll still be here. When you lie still you can feel it moving under the floor, blind and huge, pruning roots from below so the streets don't close over. The floor drinks, swells, sighs. Somewhere in the sealed districts beyond solid flesh, traffic, footsteps, a market still living its urban life. The town remembering being larger.

I pulse my throat with the others. Crowded and empty and deafening all at once.
