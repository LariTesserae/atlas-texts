At first I think I would be standing on the apron.

In the middle, on the cracked concrete between the pump house and the machine shop, turning slowly. Because that's where you go to see all of it at once. The roar gets into my sternum there. The sweat starts and doesn't stop. The caged bulbs halo. I try to hold it.

I try on being a man. The foreman with the notched stick, hip boots sucking at the ditch edge. Level's good. Keep her going. Or one of the stokers, rag in the back pocket, never touching a pipe bare. I try to check a gauge the way you'd check the sky.

But I can't keep it. My hearing is too good — the vent scream still makes me flinch. I want something to break — for the cloud to lift, for a dry wind, for night to actually come. I keep waiting for the weather to break.

I try the mess hall, then. The women's heat. Arms red to the elbow, wringing a mop into a bucket that fills again from the eaves. Rice steaming in steam. Sparrows quarreling overhead. That is closer — that giving up on dry — but my hands still want to wring the air itself dry.

I try the boy for a minute because he is the only fast thing. Thin legs, tires hissing on wet cinder, basket of gaskets rattling. From shop to boiler house to office with the clock. Belonging to no building and so to all of them. The men shout at him to slow down. I understand why. I am going too fast in my head, darting building to building, trying to be useful.

So I slow.

Lower. Closer to the water mark on the stick.

I am almost the yellow dog. That feels right for a while. Lean, tongue always out, finding the one square of concrete the stamping press has warmed through my soles. Sleeping under that one pallet where the drip doesn't hit. I learn not to bark at the vent anymore, just lift my head. I learn not to chase crab. I move the way the men move, conserved slow, from shade to shade that isn't shade.

But even the dog gets up, stretches, crosses too-wide shell road with its head down. I don't want to cross. I want to stay.

Lower still. Stiller.

I think about the culvert for a while, the cold thread seeping from the base of the levee, the gurgle that catches on the same note. I think about clicking sideways across the road at low dim in blue-clawed armor, unhurried, owning the right of way while bicycles swerve. That ancientness is tempting. To have been marsh before the pads and pilings were trucked in.

But I have no armor. I am soft-throated, light-boned. I am white in a rust-colored place.

And then I find myself where I actually am.

Not on the apron. Off to the side where the warm outflow leaves the dye works and runs opaque green to the ditch. On top of the discharge pipe.

The heat comes up through my feet. Through one foot. The other is tucked up into the belly feathers, gone warm and disappeared. The pipe hums faintly under the pad of the one foot — pump vibration, far-away engines — a small tremor that never stops. Above me the swallows and martins cut the steam eating mosquitoes. Below me the minnows turn sideways in the heat-stunned water.

I am standing motionless.

That is my whole catechism: don't fly unless you must. Fold the neck into an S. Let the damp bead on the white back and not shake it. Let the dim stay dim at noon. Let the roar fill the hollow bones until quiet would feel like falling.

My eye does the work. One yellow eye, unblinking in the brown-yellow light. I watch for the flicker under the oil skin. I wait the way the water waits at the mark on the foreman's stick — exactly there, yesterday and tomorrow.

A dragonfly the color of copper passes my beak and I do not move. The frogs call from the culvert mouths at all hours. The mosquitoes rise in a pillar at shift change and are eaten as fast as the ditch breeds them. Rust blooms on the pipe two feet from my foot and will be wire-brushed tomorrow and bloom again in the same spot, orange and powdery.

I do not get taller like the tamarind by the mess hall does not get taller. I do not get older like the concrete does not get older. If I lift — white, wide, a slow beat over the settling basins — another will take this exact warm spot on the pipe so exactly you will think I never left.

Stand long enough and the marsh smell and the coal smell braid together in my lungs, chewed with water. In as fast as out. Heat made as fast as heat lost. I have stopped waiting for weather to break because I have become weather.

Hot, dim, loud, wet. Holding exactly my own against the low water.
