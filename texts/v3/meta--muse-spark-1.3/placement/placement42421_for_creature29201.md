At first I think I would be standing.

That's the mistake. I bring my shore with me — height, feet, eyes that expect horizon. I picture myself upright on that swept pavement, coat pulled against 2-degree water, watching the snow fall sideways through bruised light. A visitor. A witness in a bubble of warm blood.

It doesn't hold. The water takes it.

I am too big, too loud, too fast. My breath would boil out. My voice would go nowhere. My warmth would be a brief, wasteful flare in a world that wastes nothing. The pressure-hush gets into the bones first, before thought, and tells me: you cannot stand here. There is no standing. There is only being held, briefly, by a current, and set down elsewhere.

So I sink.

Let that image go soft. Let it be rounded by the current like everything else here. I am not standing. I am suspended, a few meters up, turning very slowly in one of those standing vortices that rise off the pavement and refuse to dissipate.

This is where I find myself. Not in a cluster — not in the three breathing domes, not in the field of chimneys to the north — but in the long emptiness between. Over swept white pavement with a hundred meters of nothing in every direction. Below me the plateau is paved with mouths. Above me a mile of green-grey water and then, dimming now for minutes at a time, the passage of something huge and unseen migrating over the mountain.

I am turning. Cold river lifts me slightly and will not let me land. Around me, held with me for days, are the others who have not chosen yet: grit, larvae, molts, babies, a slow snow that never lands where you expect. The krill-like flickers graze past me, translucent, huge-eyed, flicking in unison. I feel how warm I still am, how much noise I still make inside myself, and I am ashamed of it.

If I stayed human I would starve here looking for meaning. I would walk the causeway straight to the cliff lip because humans do that — we go to the edge to look over. I know I would. I would go to where the coffin-blocks stop and the ground simply ends into black that has never been built in. I would lift my eyestalks, if I had them. Taste the snow going down fathoms to the true bottom. Feel the upwelling burst up the cliff face in a cold river full of grit and babies.

And then, like the crabs, I would turn back. Because no one colonizes the fall. The height is the world.

So I turn back with them. That's the second image, and it doesn't hold either, but it gets closer. I am one of the slow spider crabs now, dog-big, slate-white with joints gone iron-black with age. I am out there alone on empty pavement carrying a stone up in my claws like an offering. I set it down. I turn it with my mouthparts. I taste it. Yesterday, handled. Years of pheromone on it: *here I carried, here I spat, here danger passed.* I pick it up again. In two-degree water nothing hurries except in burst. My clicking — claw-to-claw, leg-to-leg, faint high teeth through stone — is the only voice I have. Water takes the rest.

But I am not building. Not really. I am repairing. I can feel that in my back, the endlessness of it, and part of me that is still human rebels. I want to finish something. I want the tower to stay up.

Nothing is allowed to finish. I have to learn that with my body, not my mind.

Let the crab go soft too. Let me get smaller, paler. The dim teaches you to look smaller.

I am boiling out of the rubble now with thousands of others when a tower slumps. I didn't see it fall so much as feel it as a *whump* through stone into bone — a slow billowing avalanche, almost silent. Before the silt clears we are there. I am an inch, two inches long. Body like wet glass with the gut showing dark with snow inside. Blind. I grab a sand grain in my mandibles. Under my jaws, glands swell with cold calcite drool. I spit, press, move on.

This is building without technology. Cement is spat. Stone is carried. Tunnels are chewed. The underside of every pebble is polished by my mouth.

I am a Cementer in a relay, braced leg-to-leg with twenty, fifty others against the lee side of a block as big as a coffin. We push with our backs in a current lull. The surge comes. We scatter. We return to the exact chemical mark where we left off, because the cold preserves and the city remembers where to push even when the pushers die. And we will die into it. Fish bone and crab carapace ground to mortar. My dead will be the new wall.

I thought that would be the end — to become useful, invisible, persistent. To have no detached tool. To have my body be the trowel.

But there is still the withdrawing. The ripple.

Because above me, while I push, the worms bloom.

I find I want that life instead. Or also. They are not one thing, so why should I be?

Let me be a tenant who pays rent by strengthening. Let me live in the mouth-smooth hole in the low knee-high labyrinth wall, in my own mucus-calcite tube that becomes part of the mortar. Let me unfurl a red and cream crown and comb the snow. Let me feel the silver school wheel overhead, a thousand strong turning inside-out, and then pour into the domes to hide, their ammonia feeding me. Let me pump with the three domes, beating my crown to draw water through the galleries where the blind builders live and the larvae hang.

And when the eel-shape passes — pale grenadier, huge black eyes, slow tail tasting the water, culling the sick — let me vanish in sequence with thousands of others like a thought traveling through stone. Then bloom again seconds later. That feels honest. To open, to close, to open. To filter what falls and make it into home.

Where would I find myself? Turning in the vortex until I am heavy enough to settle. Then settled in a wall that has always been touched, maintaining a soft shape that will never be finished.

Who would I be? Not wise. Persistent.

In the end, I think, I am the new recruit, just dropped from the nursery. A glass speck with legs, reading years left on stone. I run my antennae over it: here, push. Here, spit. Here, a needle six feet high was built for no use and snapped in the next surge and was ground to mortar to become wall. I can afford to waste, it says. I can afford to try.

I pick up my grain.
