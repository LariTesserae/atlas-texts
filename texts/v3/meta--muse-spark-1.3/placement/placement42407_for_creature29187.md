I go down to get there. That's the first thing I know about myself here.

Not all the way down at first. I linger on the rim longer than I should. The plateau is so pale and flat and continuing that the cut feels like a rude interruption, and I am still rude with surface habits. I keep looking for a river that made it. For shade that means water. For a crowd.

My boots are still loud.

If you asked me at the rim who I thought I'd be down there, I would have said something central. A boilerman in the chapel-pod, hand on hot iron. A rigger up in the crowns, tightening wire. Someone with a post, a hearth, a loud useful heat. I picture myself in the middle of noise.

Then I descend the terraces. They are too straight. Drill spirals still in the rock. Dust hangs where the last cut fell. My breath starts to show, though the sun is straight overhead in its slot. By the time the green-grey rises up to meet me — that creak underfoot, cork and fiber and lacquered stem giving slightly — my lips have already cracked. An hour, they said. They were right.

So much for central. So much for loud.

At the bottom I stand in the production district at midday and there is nobody. Three boiler-stems and a machine-shop deck half a kilometer apart, wires sagging crown to crown, a freight elevator groaning down a stalk somewhere to my left, lathe-hum coming through a pillow three stems over. A single pale path worn across resonant bark-ground from one lit doorway to the next. And only my own boots on cork. Tick-tick of cooling iron. Far off, leaf-clatter like cutlery dropped in another room.

I thought at first that no one lived here. Because I was looking for a crowd.

That's where I would find myself, actually. Not in the pod, not on the deck. In the hundred meters of nothing between. On the pale path. Alone at midday, feeling unmade by the sparseness, learning to see the interval as inhabited.

Because I am not central. I keep walking.

Let me try other skins and see which cures onto me.

Am I one of the grey ones up high, huge-eyed against the white slot, prying pods with a stolen crowbar? I like the thievery, the back-and-forth of the crowbars bent and tooth-marked, shop boys stealing them back. But I am too heavy for branches. I make too much noise climbing.

Am I a hopper, knee-high, six-legged, shearing leaf-edges with scissor mouths, pouch-fermenting heat? Moving like a tide with the herd, stripping a patch bare to silver stem? No — I don't want to strip. I want to pass and leave unrolled buds behind me.

Am I a veil-moth, big as a hand, crowding the warm exhaust at shift-change to lay eggs in soot? I do love warmth. I do crowd toward it. But I don't want to be brushed into a jar by the soot crew, even if they leave enough for the worms.

I am slower than that. Drier. More deliberate.

I find that I have stopped looking for water. That's how I know I'm becoming one of them. The first hour I kept glancing for a well, a damp seam, a stain. Mad-making. Then my mouth learned the other answer. Not wet panic, but a dry tightening. I stop in shade. I breathe the cool pit air that settles and stays. Someone — an old-timer with seams for lips and salt at his nostrils — without a word hands me a tin of root-shavings. I put a wad in my cheek. Chemical coolness, not moisture, fools the mouth. I spit grey. He nods once.

I do not sweat anymore, even hauling. My forehead stays matte. My shirt unspotted. My skin films with pale dust. Crow's feet etch.

I move quieter. No slam, no stomp. The living wood drinks sound and I let it.

So who am I, knot in wood?

Not the boilerman — I don't live hot enough for that. His pod creaks with expansion, his belly glows through slits in husk, he knows which stem runs hot. Not the lathe hand — though I am learning to listen through the cork-muffle for pitch-change, I don't have his half-deaf patience. Not the tapper, out early with auger and bladder to bleed slow milky sap — never too much from one trunk — though I carry his cut lengths sometimes, white flesh weeping slow milk, and stack them clean without cursing.

I am the one between those knots.

A runner. A message carrier, because wire crackles and dies in the acid air past a hundred meters. A morning sawyer of night's runners. A tightener of small things.

My stem is not grand. Twenty meters to the first branch, fluted, hollow, hard as kiln timber. Inside: two bunks but I only use one, a stove whose flue goes up through pith sealed black and airtight, a workbench, a hook for a mask, my tin. The woody lip around my steel doorframe has healed thick and black. I lay my palm a second on the jamb as I pass, morning and night, feeling for growth. Shop manners. You don't kick walls that are alive.

My days are pale paths and resonant ground. Cork-soled, salt-dusted, lean. I know which hollow runner hums when you sing into it — mine does, low, if you go three bends in and stop where the chewers nested last spring. I know which ones cut through to the machine shop and which ones are occupied, squeaking. I race hopper herds for fun and fall behind laughing without much sound, because sound doesn't carry. When I meet the oiler sixty feet up on the rope-and-grating catwalk, moths crowding the vent beside her, we don't shout. She lifts two fingers. I lift two back. I see you that far away.

I speak low now, consonant-heavy, clicks and edges. A name called two clearings away arrives with half the vowels swallowed and that's enough.

At dusk, if they let me up to the crown deck — and now they do — I look with the others. Blue shadow filling the cut from the bottom up. Warm windows lighting one by one far apart in the green-grey mass, sparks in tinder that refuses to burn. Thin smoke rising straight up in windless cold. A press thumps, pillow-soft, and somewhere below me a ripe pod pops open on that frequency. Leaned leaves toward chimneys. Dust-fox flattened to a root-rib, ears turning like dishes, then gone. A fox yip, consonant-short.

I am not the mat itself — thick as houses, veined black on pale, layered like phyllo, stitching white rootlets across fresh scars, tasting metal. I walk on its back. I cut doors and it heals. I saw back its overnight nosing by noon and by night it tests forward again, and both sides seem content.

Unfinished on purpose. Still deciding how big it can get without water.

I am content with the arrangement.
