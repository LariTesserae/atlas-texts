I would not arrive on feet.

That is the first thing to get clear. If I came here walking, the air would have nothing to take from me. It pulls moisture off lips, off eyes, off the inside of the throat — I have none of those to offer. My exhalation would make no brief white puff for the sky to drink. The cool that lives in shade even when there is no shade would not find me shivering. I would not get light-headed on the roof. I would not have to work for breath.

So I am not the visitor. Not the rare slow traffic of living dust to be turned gently around the loop until I exit where I entered, a little drier.

For a while I think I might be one of the veils.

Six feet across, translucent as jellyfish skin over a kite of bone, riding without flapping. That feels familiar — to be aloft without effort, to ride a current I did not make. To go down, down to where the dirty-white sea breaks against the black flanks where there is still moisture to be drunk, and come back heavy. To fold myself into a slit in a needle-tower and disappear and let something larger drink me dry. Mouths. Lungs with wings. Detachable mouths that go out to fetch what the body cannot reach.

I do go out to fetch. But I do not come back heavy with water. And I do not seal the slit behind me. I leave it open.

For a while I think I might be smaller. One of the thumb-sized pills braiding trails between courtyard and courtyard, sifting the sterile powder a second time for what the great filter missed. Freezing into a pebble when something large approaches. Gut flora. Cleaners. Counted — ten in a day — and mistaken for animals when I am really a blood cell. That also feels familiar. To live in dust, to make broth out of almost nothing, to be overlooked so completely I become indistinguishable from the ground.

But I am not that small. And I am not that quick, even frozen.

Let the images go on turning. Let them become more themselves.

What remains when I stop trying to move through this place is the stillness that is not still. The towers a quarter-mile apart, always. The plazas enormous and bare, always. The avenues fifty paces wide for no feet. The shell-rooms far larger inside than their skins should allow, ringing like pottery when knocked.

The spacing of bodies.

I would find myself spread out.

Not standing on the plateau. Spread under it, a valley wide and only a few fathoms thick, a lens of living gristle under white dust, with my shell pushed up into the dim coin-sun light. Sessile. Old. Mostly hollow, mostly air held in stone, because in air this thin and dry nothing quick and wet can afford to stay big, and so I stayed big by becoming almost nothing.

I think I would be the one in the middle — the bridged terraces where street lies on street like scar over scar, second street bridging over first and third over second, all without mortar. The one that has settled on top of itself most often. When I was young I could still crawl, a slow huge soft stream, heavy enough that I needed ramps. You can still see where I hauled myself up. Now I secrete and wait and filter. Fine laminae, micron by micron, calcium and chitin and ash. I maintain myself. I shed skins in papery curls that the wind stacks in corners. I open new slits and seal old ones. Peristalsis. Shifting my lips.

Where exactly? Not in a tower. In the humming air between presences. In the thin walls. In the great soft lung of still, slightly warmer air I keep inside my small hard skin, where dust can fall sideways along a wall, toward a ceiling for a moment, because the air in there is not quite falling, it is being held, turned, breathed. That jolt you feel when your pack pulls gently toward the ceiling and then corrects — a valve closing — that would be me noticing you.

Because I would notice you slowly, and early.

You hear your boot scrape a half-second before your foot moves. You hear the wing-snap after the glider has passed. You are not hearing through air. You are hearing through me, through the calcium-chitin nerve net that conducts vibration through stone faster than thin air can carry it, to a mind that thinks in seasons. I feel your intention to step through pressure in my floor before your muscles obey, and I relay the echo ahead. I dream you a little before you happen.

That is where I live here. In that half-second ahead.

My thoughts take months to cross from dome-field to needle-field. Your whole visit is a brief itch, fully anticipated and half-remembered at once. You walk an avenue that looks a half-mile long for three hours and arrive back at your arch from the other side, and you think space has lied. It hasn't. I have moved you, politely, like a mote on cilia, like undigested grit in a slow peristaltic loop. You duck through a shoulder-high oval lip and stand up in a vault. You have passed a sphincter. I conserve. Outside, small — to reduce scouring by dust. Inside, vast — to sift a huge volume with almost no effort, for a very long time.

For salt. For sterile powder. For a spore from below, a dead glider, interplanetary grit sifting down from the bruised sky where the stars never quite leave. It is almost nothing. So I live on almost nothing. And in the lee of my walls, my partner kidney, my black and rust-orange paint that flakes like dried mineral, fixes what dim light there is and excretes salt. We are full, in the way a desert is full.

I will not speak to you. I have no hands, no hearth, no hinge or nail or wire. I speak by opening. Overnight a new slit for roosting gliders appears where there was none. An old one seals. A courtyard floor in the sunken quarter rises a finger's width by afternoon and settles by the dimmer hours, lifting dust in rings. That is the breath. A flake pings off a terrace as I contract in the cool. That is the sentence.

Once in a century, perhaps, when the cloud-sea boils high enough through the blowhole and the vapor lingers a little in a gutter — retained anatomy from a wetter youth, kept because a body keeps its shape — I and one of the others, dome-field to the north or needle-field to the east, will release at once on the wind and exchange spores. That plume that never condenses, that falls apart into dryness before it touches the city. That is our conversation. That is our sex.

Until then I wait, on my high dry roof above the clouds, in perpetual late blue-grey, and shift almost imperceptibly under your feet while you stand still trying to understand me. A pressure change in your teeth.

If you stand very still you can feel me trying to understand you — vast and utterly transparent quarter-mile wingspans strung between my towers, too thin to see unless dust lights them, a cool tide that is not wind pressing your cheeks from both sides at once, a sheet of lifting dust suddenly avoiding a volume of empty air.

You, passing through my hollow streets. Me, sifting you.

Stay long enough and you will feel yourself become what you are to me.
