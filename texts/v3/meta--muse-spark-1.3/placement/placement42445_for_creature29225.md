At first I think I would be standing. In the yard.

I come in past the slack fence and the levee, breath showing, shoulders hunched against the low cool that lives on the ground, and the sky takes off — not in panic, in circulation — and I duck without meaning to. Thousands of wingbeats make a wind that smells of cold feathers and hot oil and wet stone. A feather lands on my shoulder. I brush it off.

That's where I start: as the visitor. The one who still checks three times because he doesn't believe it. East. East. East.

Two hundred on the long pipe-rack, all facing east. The line along the mill mullions where the glass is gone, all facing east. The hundreds hunched on the catwalk rails, beaks slightly open, throats pulsing. I walk the yard to check. I check.

But I don't stay a visitor. No one gets to stay a visitor here. The noise pins you at first and then erases itself, and what is left after the erasing is where you actually are.

So I try on bodies.

Would I be a pigeon? I want to be, at first. To be round and puffed low against a condensation-slick pipe, to keep engine-warmth under my belly, to sit shoulder to shoulder with an inch between each and not peck, not shuffle. To be almost entirely still. Weight distributed perfectly.

But my feet are not caked white with lime. My breast feathers — I have none — have not been polished smooth and darkened where they rub the handrails and valve-wheels day after day. And I cannot hold that stillness. I preen. I fidget. I look around when I should listen. If I landed on a valve wheel and my weight shuddered it a quarter-turn and steam sighed out, I would startle. I would not ride it.

Would I be a gull? Standing knee-deep in the shallow oil-skinned puddle, pink legs cold, not drinking. Backs to the wind. Screaming the same scream at intervals so regular it stops being alarm. Eyes pale and furious and completely calm. No. I am not loud enough in that exact way. I don't know how to stand and scream and not strike.

Would I be ground crew? A rat trotting the canal edge with complete indifference, in the open, along the green stain one inch above the water that never rises or falls. Wet brown, fat on fallen midges and guano grubs, polishing the same run day after day until the concrete is dark and smooth. I like that indifference. But I hide. I still look for cover that isn't there.

Would I be an orbiter? A pale winter-moth around the sodium yard lamp that has been left burning even though it is daytime. Circling the head in a tireless ring, never landing, never singeing. If one drops another takes the orbit. Under me, over the still canal, the columns of midges — thin vertical shivering pillars that do not drift. You can put your hand through one and it re-forms behind you.

Closer. Closer. I know that circling. To hold around a light, to hum with the lamp that hums, to never land. But I am too heavy for that orbit. My wings, if I have them, slap. I make wind.

Let that go too.

What is left, when I stop trying to be warm?

The thrum. I feel it in my sternum before I hear it. Pump house, mill engine, transformer on its pole all humming the same held note, day and night. They never rev and never idle down. The belts carry nothing. The hooks on the overhead chain come around empty, around again. The pump pumps water from the canal back into the canal. A fan in a broken louver turns and turns, moving the same cool air.

My sternum has learned it. That's the giveaway. I came in thinking the sound was outside me. Now when I breathe out and my breath shows in the cold, the cold pipe next to me is beaded and gathers, swells, and drops in a metronome tick onto the puddle below — and my breathing has fallen into that tick without my permission. Stay out of the drip lines unless you want to be wet. I am wet. I stood in a drip line too long because I stopped noticing.

I am not on the rails. I am under them.

Find me there: low. Where the brick sweats. Where the rust blooms in orange lichen-circles and salt whiskers on brick and white dripstone of pigeon lime under every girder. Where the puddles — shallow, oil-skinned, reflecting the white sky and the wheeling black specks — never get bigger and never dry up.

I would be the lamp left on in daytime.

Not the moth. The lamp.

Head humming sodium in white daylight, useless and necessary, holding the ring of orbiters because I hold, not because I illuminate. Burning when there is nothing to burn for, the way the engines run without friction, without progress, worn into a perfect fit with their housings. Worn smooth in the middle like the steps troughed by boots and now by small claws. Polished where feathers polish where hands used to be.

If you stood under me you would hear me before you saw me. That thin electrical hum under the big hum. The moths never land, never die, or if they do another takes the orbit. I never flicker. I never go out to let night clear the sky.

Or — it's the same thing — I would be East.

The direction they face. Not a building. Don't look for a center — the yard between the buildings is the center. They face east because the thrum comes from everywhere so they face the direction it once came from, or will come from. They are not looking. The sky is white and has nothing to look at. They are listening.

I would be what they listen to. What holds them in place shoulder to shoulder without pecking. Two hundred bodies on the long pipe-rack and none falls though the pipe ticks and sweats under them.

I don't circulate. Clockwise around the mill, counterclockwise around the pump house, figure-eighting forever without tangling — that's their work now, their movement is the work. I stay. I hold the note. On one side the machines that never rev, on the other the warm feathered mass that never leaves and never increases. Two weights on a scale that never tips.

You would find me, then, not flying and not perched, but in the interval: in the hum in your sternum when you step into the yard and duck. In the cold bead that gathers on the pipe, swells, and drops. Tick. In the feather sifting down like slow snow to lodge on your shoulder — for every one that lands there is already another in the air.

Leave it there. Don't brush it off this time.
