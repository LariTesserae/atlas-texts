You go down into it before you understand it. So I go down.

At first, from the rim, I thought I would be something with a view. Something with wings. It is the obvious wish — to be high, to cut the basin in flat intersecting arcs, to see the whole raw bite at once. To be a soot-swift.

I try it on for a few steps down the scarp. The ash-gray, the sickle bend, the *kik-kik-kik* and its ghost half a second later off the far wall. Fifteen, twenty deep on a ledge wide as my palm, shoulder to shoulder, breast to back, warm guano under me. It is intimate and frantic and completely sure. The swifts never land anywhere else. Their little black hook-feet only know this pocked basalt.

I can't stay there. It's too loud inside that press, too fast. Chicks crying without pause, pink mouths up. One slips every few minutes and doesn't hit the ground whole. I don't have that kind of faith, to breed over a carpet that is already waiting with its claws up. I am not that certain of belonging.

So lower. Maybe I am hungrier than that. Maybe I am the carpet itself.

I try the crab for a while, on the scree. Fist-sized, ash-pale, joints flashing signal red. The hiss of shell on fresh basalt. Always moving sideways, over each other, claws up, claws down. Eat anything still. Fallen swift, stunned fly-column glittering with condensed water, each other soft after molting, your bootlace if you stand still long. There is a honesty to it. Nothing is wasted.

But I don't close over boots in under a minute. I don't test the world by pinching it. I linger too long. I watch too long. If I were a crab I would starve looking at the light on the black glass.

Higher again, then? The glider. Three or four at a time, riding the trapped thermal round and round without flapping. Three meters of sun-cracked leather, pink veins underneath. Silent. Casting a fast shadow that makes the whole wall inhale — twenty thousand swifts lifting at once, darkening daylight for a breath.

I like the silence. But I don't want to be that alone, that high above the roar, only touching down to stab precisely and tear. I don't want to be the thing everyone flattens for. And a dead glider by afternoon is not a glider. It's a glistening red articulation of crabs and beetles, humming. I don't want to end that fast.

Maybe, then, I am not from here at all. Maybe I am one of the lean people in heat-bleached shirts who come down in the cool season. That feels familiar — the intruder who knows the rules. Step here, not there. Follow the cairns because the ground that looks empty is brooding. Take one swift egg from a press of twenty so the ledge doesn't panic. Drink never from a pool with tadpoles, only the drip ticked off an overhang cupped in the hand. Boil crabs in a dented tin over dried dung. Build a low blind of stacked scoria and roof it with woven swift-feather mats and let it cake white. Leave three stick ladders lashed with twine leaning against the nesting face.

I could do that. I know how to be careful, how to take little, how to leave before the hatching peaks, before it becomes unworkable, unbearable, too alive.

But the harvesters leave. They go back up. I don't think I would go back up. Once I was down in the heat that thickens, sweating, deafened, hearing my own footsteps crunch too loud, hearing sounds arrive twice, smelling sweat and cut fruit and metal, I think I would stay low and get smaller, not larger. Not the hand that lifts the stone. The thing under the stone.

That is where I find myself. Not on the living walls. Not in the air striped with wings. On the edge of the scree fan where the ash has crusted over with salt, just to the left of the cairn path — the third balanced stone, the one you step to, not from. Under it. In the damp.

I am a white cricket with no eyes.

It becomes more itself the longer I stay: pale as condensate, long antennae as long as my body, always moving ahead of me, tasting. I don't see the amber late-afternoon light when the whole sink glows, black glass glinting, red joints flashing, white guano gold. I taste the shift in it as cooling, as damp rising. In the morning and late afternoon, when the shadow of the west wall falls across the east wall, water beads on the cool basalt and ticks off — I hear that prickling patter before any pool exists, and I come up toward it.

I live where the base of it is. Where the broad black beetles roll balls twice their size backward over edges that should stop them and bury them in my ash. Guano and crab-meal. Where it ferments in the warmth. That's the soil that isn't soil yet. Shit fermenting, feeding maggots, feeding everything above. I boil out from under every lifted stone into that sweet-sour air and my antennae read it all at once: ammonia-bitter, salt-white, tea-colored puddle an hour old a handspan away, tadpole shapes flickering in it mouths-up for fly-dust, brick-red lizard poured like liquid between boulders nearby, dry skitter, crab-hiss paused.

I am not fast like the lizard. I am not flat and pulsing like the clingers above me on the steep face — those wet-brick salamander-things tipping their heads back to drink fog off their own skin, though I love them, though I am sometimes picked by their tongue-flick that you hear more than see. I am even briefer than that, and blinder.

Lift the stone and I boil. That is my whole defense and my whole work. A white boil, antennae tasting your heat, your sweat, the tin on your back, the bone awl in your basket. Then settle. Then go back under when the light changes.

You would find me exactly there, if you followed the cairns and stepped carefully between puddles that weren't there this morning, if you crouched and lifted, not to eat, just to look. Underneath, in the dark I prefer, close to the ferment, close to the almost-soil, tasting the numbers of it all without needing to see it.

I don't hold territory. I hold on.
