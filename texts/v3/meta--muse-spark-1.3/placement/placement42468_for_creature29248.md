I don't find myself on the rim, looking down.

At first I think I do — boots on black basalt, lungs working too hard, sweat running straight down with no breeze to take it. Human. Heavy. Standing and sinking. That is how I arrive: too much weight for the sponge, too much noise for the moss. Warm water wells up around my ankle, blood-warm, and I can feel through the sole how thin the ground is. Hollow stones. Breath underneath.

I try to stay that way. Upright, watching. But the valley won't hold that shape. Nothing here holds that shape.

If I stayed big I would have to leave. To dry out, to cool down, to eat something that wasn't already being eaten. And nothing leaves. That is the rule. So to stay, I have to get smaller. Lighter. Wetter.

For a while I think I might be fast enough to get away with being in the air. A copper-green line, shift-worker, wings adding my own octave to the hum. *Vrrr* and stop dead in a flower. Stab the white orchid, drink the fog off it, pant with my bill open in the white noon heat. It feels right for a minute — businesslike, straight lines between the yellow torches of the groundsels. But I am too hot. My blood runs too fast, my feet are almost useless. I can't land. I have to keep burning to stay still. That's not staying.

Then I think, lower. Under the Gunnera leaf big as a shield. Long toes splayed wide to distribute an impossible lightness. A rail, chestnut and slate, with a bright unbothered eye. I pick through sedge, spear a bloodworm, shake it, swallow. My nest is a scrape in sphagnum, a few fern scales, eggs speckled like peat water. I could live like that — almost too tame, because no one has ever hunted this sponge. But even I am too loud. I step, I probe. I still need dry enough to lay an egg and keep it from floating away.

I need to get smaller still, and wetter, until down and up stop meaning what they did.

I crouch. My knees are wet instantly, it doesn't matter. I part the fronds with fingers and the water starts to tinkle and I realize it is not water.

*Tink. Tink. Tink-tink.*

There. That is closer.

Not on the ground at all, but held above it. Twenty meters up in the fibrous trunk of a tree-fern, where the old leaf-bases cup together and the moss sleeves thick as a forearm seal the joints. A tank. Half a liter, a liter of rainwater, warm as tea, brown as tea, replenished every hour by cloud condensing at chest height and dripping off every leaf-tip.

I find myself there.

I am the size of a fingernail. Translucent amber. Or wet-leaf green — it depends on the light coming straight down through the white heat and then breaking into fog. My throat pulses like a tiny heart under my chin. My eyes look back at you if you lift yourself to look in, and then the water shivers.

This is where I live entire lives without climbing down.

Below me the valley breathes — chuff, sigh, long exhalation from the eastern flats where the peat thins over the fault. I feel the workmanlike geyser every eleven minutes as a piston-thump, not in my sternum anymore but through the whole cup of my world, a ripple that shakes the pollen slicks. Hot springs well up glassy at 50, 60 degrees, but up here it comes to me as steam sheared sideways, as warm white room, visibility ten paces, droplets forming on my back. I don't need to drink. I am drunk by moss. I am breathed on from below.

Around me, the business passes through. The glitter columns of midges ignite when the sun breaks — plankton of the air, some of them landing too close to the sundews below with their red rosettes tipped in glue. A hummingbird's shadow cuts my tank for half a second, *vrrr*, gone. Leaf bits, petals, drowned beetles move steadily down-gradient in every rill toward the central marsh-lakes where the algae double and double, but up here the conveyor is shorter: a fallen orchid petal lands in my water, softens, is reassigned. Tadpoles — my tadpoles, my own string of eggs laid in this same cup — worry at its edge. Midge larvae wave. Nothing rots so much as gets handed on.

At night I do not darken so much as dim to blue-green. The copper-green workers vanish. Moths, pale and furred, work the night orchids that open with a heavy sweet smell to cut the sulfur. My calling gets louder, then stranger, then stops in waves as cloud drags through. Below, along the thinnest earth, the bacterial mats fringe every vent with soft fire, lines of it, veins. I see it reflected in my liter of sky. The mud pots go on blubbering and popping in the dark.

I tinkle. Ventriloquial and hushed. You turn your head and lose me, and the marsh seems to rain upward even when dry.

I can stand to be wet and hot and rained on daily. My feet are wide enough, or rather, I need no feet at all. The sponge will not hold weight but it will hold insistence, and I insist — throat pulsing, eyes open — cupped in a plant that is cupped in a valley that is cupped in the sky, part of the breath that never stops: sweated out of rock, drunk by moss, transpired by leaf, condensed as fog, rained in an afternoon hammering, sweated out again.
