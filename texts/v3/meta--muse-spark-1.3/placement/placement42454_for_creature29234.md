At first I think I would stay on the rim.

Up there where the rock is still young and sharp, where I can see the whole boundary — three hundred paces rim to rim, a room. It would be easier to be the watcher. To keep my coat dry and count the hearths and say: there are the nets, there are the groves, there is the warm navel in the middle. To not get damp all the way through.

But the fog comes over the low notch like a slow waterfall and fills the bowl to the waist, and I can't stay up there. The cloud decides for me. It pours in and closes behind me, and the dripping starts — plink from a fern frond to stone, plink from an overhang — and my shoulders bead with water that was never rain. If I stand still long enough, they land on me.

The moths.

For a while I want to be one of them. It would be simple to be numerous. To roost by the thousand under a waxed leaf, fat as a thumb and then papery and then — rise. Hush-hush-hush, more pressure than noise against the cheek. To drink dew off anything warm and to navigate by warmth, to find that bare patch in the deepest part where the basalt hasn't cooled through and no snow ever settles, and to slowly revolve above it on windless nights like water going down a drain in reverse. To be snow going upward and never thinning.

But I am too heavy for that. My wings would make a sound. I would fall if someone spoke aloud after fog-drop. I don't want to be the thing that falls.

Then I think — higher. To be the owl. Small and round-faced and fur-footed, white as the moths so you can't pick me out until I open. To circle without a single wingbeat you can hear, while the moss and fog eat even the memory of sound. To just open my talons and close the gap. That silence is absolute. It tempts me.

But I don't want to be served with tithes. I don't want feathers left for me on the ground to rot back into sponge. I don't want to take chicks from the drying racks when I am hungry.

Lower, then. Under the rising snow, among the ferns that uncurl taller than a child. To hop. To be round as a white mouse with a beak stuck on, springing stem to stem on huge furred feet that don't sink in sponge, flipping a leaf with a quick jerk of the neck to find the caterpillar underneath. Tick of leaf against beak. Soft thump of landing. Nothing else. To nest deep in moss where boots must not go.

I get close there. I like the scale of it — tea-colored pools no bigger than a cooking pot where midges skate, moss piled on moss until it is knee-deep and spongy enough to swallow a shout three paces away. I like being small and downy and waxed to shed drip.

But I have hands. They keep getting in the way of the image. Damp-white hands at the knuckles. Hands that want to mend.

So I find myself, finally, where the town is. Not the whole town — I could never hold all twenty hearths — just one tier of it.

On the middle terrace, half-sunk for warmth, in a low round house of dry-stacked basalt and turf that is the same color as the rock. Flat slate roof weighted with stones. A gutter I built pebble by pebble by hand running from my roof to the chain of deep clay-lined cisterns. A felted moth-wool cloak hung over the door, ash-grey, smelling faintly of fern and smoke. It goes stiff with drip and then softens again.

I am not counted at first. You would walk past and see only three old women mending nets and a child laboring up with a skin bag twice her size, and think the place was kept in shifts, abandoned. I am elsewhere. Dispersed and constant and quiet.

Find me in the morning after the fog burns off to the waist: on my belly at a cistern lid, skimming midge larvae with a slate cupped spoon. Or above the highest houses, up among the poles of bent tree-limb, coaxing the fog-nets — loose weaves of hair and plant fiber — to drip all day into the troughs. They tear in wind. They are always tearing. I am always mending, with a bone awl, sinew between my teeth on a sagging rope bridge of twisted moss-fiber.

I whisper, even alone. My voice is ruined for anything above it, as all ours are. I step on stone, not moss, because moss holds nests. I leave water where I find it. I carry only what the nets give. I pinch only the largest caterpillars, fat green-white things ticking their jaws, and leave the rest to spin, and spread what I take on the communal drying racks in the ash plaza. I gather only the cast-off cocoons to spin. I build low so as not to break the circling air. I never, never pick up a white feather.

I am the sparsest thing here. That is as it should be. The terraces follow the moth flightlines, not the contour. The cisterns are placed where dew falls heaviest for caterpillars, not where it is convenient to carry. The town is not really built for people at all, and I know it. I am just the part of the cloud that clears gutters so the cycle doesn't stutter. Drip, grow, spin, hatch, hunt. Caterpillar to moth to owl to feather to moss.

In the evening, when the light stays blue-grey and never decides to become night or morning, I sit with the net across my knees and listen to ten thousand wings moving the air against my cheeks, and to my own swallow, which I can hear because everything else is muted. The lushest grove grows in a ring around nothing, around warmth. The whole bowl is larger inside than out. A room that contains weather.

I tend it.
