At first I thought I would be up high.

Not the altitude — though that too. I thought I would want the fin, the knife-ridge, the place where you can see weather being born fifty miles away. To stand where the cloud snags and tears and stand there watching it re-form. To be the watcher.

But nothing here is watched from above for long. The air hurts a little. The light is white and level and gives you nowhere to hide your eyes. And your boots fill. Within an hour your boots fill, and the high place pulls you down by the ankles into the wet.

So then I thought, all right — I will be in the lit windows. I will be the warm yellow square in the five-story mill seen from three days away across the mire. The stoker, the switch-watcher. Someone inside the throb, not out in the hollow feeling it in the sternum. Someone with a job: oil the bearing that has no wear. Chalk the tally.

I get off the low train with a duffel that is already damp. I learn the stew. I learn the splayed-footed gait for mire, the sure-footed for frost-split fin. I learn to put two fingers on a pipe and feel trouble as a tooth-ache before it is an ear-ache. I learn to lean forehead to forehead to say *come*.

That was close. That was almost me.

But stay longer. Walk the week of nothing between lights and let the throb get into you and stay there, and the keeper-shape starts to soften at the edges. I am not the man at the gauge. I am not exactly the woman with the flat spade, laughing without sound in the roar.

I am where she takes me on Tuesday — though no one remembers why Tuesday.

Find me ankle-deep behind the turbine hall, on the north side where the hot culvert exhales and the cold stone sweats perpetually. Where the green felt creeps up the brick.

That is where I find myself. Not in the square full of settling tanks. Not in the barracks above the dynamos where the floor is warm through the pallet and all night your bones buzz. But out at the margin the keepers cut. At the line.

I have a flat spade in soft-white, seamed hands. My coat is blackened with peat oil and green at the seams where spores have taken hold and will not wash out. I do not try to wash it. My chest is barrelled out. My pulse crawls — fifty, sixty — unhurried as peat water. My eyes have gone the color of the place. I don't squint. I look best at close range.

And close range is this: cushion moss, sphagnum, liverwort in a black-green pelt climbing the vibrating foundation before the foundation has settled. Starry saxifrage no taller than my thumb. Lichen the color of bone trying the hot pipe and not dying and not winning.

It comes up a finger's width a week. I cut it to the line.

Under the green there is a faint white paint mark, thirty years old. Someone put it there to show where to stop. The moss grows over it. I lift the felt edge with the spade, find it again, exactly where it was. Cut. Lay the cut bricks aside for the warehouse where they will be inventoried and fed back in. By next Tuesday it will be back to exactly the same line, as if trimmed by memory.

*She remembers,* the old woman said, and meant both.

I am starting to mean both.

Because lying there, if you lie down it steams faintly around you with your heat, smelling of peat and iron and clean rot — lying there with my forehead almost to the brick to see the line, I feel the town through my knees. The low iron drone in my teeth. The shriek of the steam valve taking the top off everything, then the chord underneath that never stops: wind over fins + water over lips + current in wires + fire in boilers. She runs herself. She is drinking straight off a hard lip, exhaling white steam that will be drizzle a mile downwind. Blood in the penstock. Nerves singing in the pylons to the horizon.

And I feel the green through my palms. It does not need eyes. It does not need seasons. Frost feathers and melts, melts and feathers. It grows a millimeter a year on top and compresses a millimeter a year below. That is its breathing. It is learning the shape of my shoulders where I kneel every week in the same two dents that fill slowly with water.

I am not separate from those breathings. No one here is.

I eat at the bell because the bell rings because the shift changes because the bell rings. Mire-stew in the same tin bowl. Grit in the flour grinding my teeth flat early. My gut has slowed to the speed of the place. I do not get hungrier or less hungry. The cold keeps me alert without ever pushing me into shivering. My breath fogs and clears, fogs and clears. My boots are wet and never wetter, never dry.

At night I blink my handlamp from window to window across the absolute hollow: *still running.* And the same note comes back: *still running. still running.*

That is who I would be. Not the visitor who still asks where he came from or when he is leaving. Not the hunger that has not yet begun.

I would be the keeper of the edge. The white blood cell with a flat spade. The flora of the beast. The mouth so small it does not interrupt the peat. The patience so complete it stops looking like a human virtue and starts looking like geology.

Press your boot into the spot where I kneel, a year after I have lain down out in the green between towns and not gotten up, and water will rise around the tread. It will smell faintly of peat and iron. The roster in the chapel-transformer station will still add to the same total.

And on Tuesday, someone else — short, broad, quiet, damp to the bone — will lift the felt, find the white mark still exactly at the edge of the cut, and feel, obscurely, comforted rather than mocked, to put the blade just there again.
