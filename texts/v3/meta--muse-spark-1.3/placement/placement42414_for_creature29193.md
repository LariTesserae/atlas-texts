If I fell here, I wouldn't land.

That's the first thing. I'd expect down to mean down, but here down is a mat that gives under me and pushes back, and up is a ceiling impossibly far away that never gets closer. So I would find myself in the middle.

In the empty part. Between two giants a hundred meters apart, where the water is nothing but cold green and loud. Snow falling sideways past my face. My hands gone pale and then dark. Jaw aching with the seven-degree cold. Sternum humming with that *huuush-thump, huuush-thump* coming from everywhere.

At first I think I would be the visitor. The diver. The warm anomaly with silver beads coming out of my mouth that rise and rise and never arrive. I try to hold still there in the millrace, in the two-knot current that is always two knots, and I can't. I kick to keep station and I'm immediately wrong. Too fast, too jerky. A mess.

A ribbon comes through — the Conveyors — thousands of palm-long silver bodies pouring mouth-open through the ground meal, fin-tip to fin-tip. I think, maybe I could be that. Maybe I could learn the spacing, learn to turn when they turn and give off that little tick of blue charge as I go. I put my hand in the stream to feel it.

They part around my fingers without panic and close behind. Tick tick tick along my skin. And I understand I will never be that. I can't filter. I can't keep station so precisely you could measure me with calipers a year from now and find me the same. I wobble. I breathe. I make bubbles that don't belong, and the charge from the school runs ahead of me and kisses the stipe bark and runs up it and is gone.

So I sink a little. Not falling, just letting the cold take me down toward the felt.

Down there the Mat-walkers are moving, table-backs algae-bearded like low hills, legs longer than I am tall placing each step, testing, placing. The mat depresses and rebounds. I think, maybe I could be that slow. Maybe I could learn to be deliberate, to pick and prune, to fold my great legs and drink milky sap from a torn frond unhurried while the surge leans everything six degrees, always six degrees. A crab looks at me with stalked eyes, decides I am neither fall nor frond, and steps *over* me. Its belly passes warm above my face.

No. I'm not that patient. I'm not that certain what is food and what is not.

I drift back up along the pillar, past the white anemones opening like slow fists, past the black mussels woven into second skin, up to where color breaks — violet, orange, acid yellow. A nudibranch as long as my forearm grazing algae off the bark, leaving a pale trail. It doesn't retract when I come close. Nothing here is afraid, because nothing here has ever needed to be. I hold out a finger and it keeps grazing, right over my shadow.

I envy that for a second. To be bright as a warning flag in a place with no predators to warn. To taste your own giant's skin and make the same path again.

But I'm not bright. I'm not slow in that perfect way.

Then I feel the nibbling.

I'd been holding still too long, and they've found me. The cleaners. Finger-long, translucent, black eyes too big. A cloud of them around my hands, around a hangnail, around the dead skin at my cuticles. Eating frayed tissue. Eating before it can be fungus. Polishing.

Oh.

That's when it starts to become itself.

Because I know that hunger. Not the wide-open mouth of the ribbon, not the vast slow gut of the Slow Ones passing overhead like weather — twenty, thirty meters of mottled green displacing water at one knot, never faster, never slower, moaning so low I feel it in my teeth, the sound coming back altered off the house-sized bladders. Not that. Smaller.

The hunger for the break. For the place where the skin is open.

I would find myself — I do find myself — gathered at a tear. A thirty-meter sail of a frond has split in the surge, *tock... tock... tock...* up the line of pillars, and milky sap is welling out. The crab has already drunk. Now we come. In cloud. Hundreds of us, translucent, flickering.

I am finger-long. My eyes are too big because I live in haze and snow and need to see the ragged edge. I dart. I pick. I eat the frayed cell, the dying edge, the thing that would rot if rot were allowed here. I polish until the giant seals it over with new skin that is exactly the same as old skin.

Then I disperse. That's the rule. I don't stay. I wait in the green distance between pillars, invisible, useless, until the next knock, the next tear, the next foreign body with dead skin on its hands.

When a Slow One passes, its moan vibrates through me and I feel my own tiny resonance change it, just slightly, and that altered note carries down the rank for kilometers. When a Conveyor falters and drops out of line, mouth still opening, sinking slowly — I watch it fall past me to the mat-walkers below. I don't chase. Nothing chases.

I just wait to clean what is left open.

Stay under, and you stay in it. I stay under, in the middle distance where the water is empty and green and loud in my sternum, inhale, exhale, inhale, exhale of the pumps around me. For a little while I am also part of what lives here. Not the pillar, not the blood, not the hands, not the gut.

The clotting.
