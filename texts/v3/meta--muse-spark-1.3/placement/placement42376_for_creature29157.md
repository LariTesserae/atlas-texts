I come in on my knees, like everyone does.

That's the first thing — I don't walk in. The hatch is the size of a breadbox and the duct is square and I have to fold myself small to get through it, knees on galvanized grate before I can even stand, which I can't. Nothing here is built to my height. Cold feedwater main on my left sweating through its paint, tracer line on my right lukewarm as skin, conduit bank an inch over my bowed head humming warm with 400 volts. If I lift my elbows I touch all three.

At first I think I know where I'll be.

Up high, obviously. On the pipes with the doves. Eighty, maybe a hundred of them, puffed to twice their size, dirty white and blue-barred, the green in their necks gone grey with cement dust. They have the best seats — the handwheels like iron artichokes, the lip of the drip pan, each other's backs. Their feet are shockingly red against the freezing steel. They watch me crawl in with orange eyes and don't move, only shift. Fear would cost heat.

But I am not still enough to be a dove. They have learned the posture completely: all facing inward, breast to the tracer line, tails to the knife-draft from the louver slit. They ride the thunder when one loses footing — that detonation of wings in a steel drum — and then settle. And every few minutes they coo together, a hollow moan the plates make bigger, and for half a second it cancels a harmonic of the transformer hum so perfectly a pocket of true silence opens behind my eyes. I feel my own breathing try to join it without my permission. No. I can't hold that stillness. I fidget. My knees already hurt.

So then, lower? Warmer?

The pipe overhead looks upholstered until the upholstery breathes. Moths, thousands deep, the color of wet paper, packed head-out on every warm surface, antennae shivering. The ones nearest the caged green bulb are dusted white with their own scales. They die there, stick a while by the legs, drop into the webs, and are replaced by others shuffling forward from the wall before I've blinked. Too cold to fly and too committed to leave.

I could be a moth. God, part of me wants to be a moth — just to press into that shared warmth and shiver with everyone else and not have to decide anything. But I am too restless for the wall. I want to move.

Under the moths, the midges in their permanent low cloud off the sump, that high endless whine that finds the duct I came through and sings in it. In the corners, harvestmen knotted into beards, a hundred long legs braided into one slow-writhing ball that loosens and tightens and never comes apart. On the sheet webs strung from pipe to valve to lamp cage, the house spiders, pale from generations without sun, sitting upside-down in the middle, milk-white, waiting among the husks.

I try the spider for a while. Hanging there in the sag, feeling every tick of dove claws through the web threads, waiting for a stunned midge to blunder in. That patience is beautiful. But I don't want to hang. I want to go along the length of things, nose out.

That's when I see them, because I've stayed past the point my fingertips go stupid on the handwheel. Past the first amazement.

Two mice, moving like slow thoughts along the top of the conduit.

That's where I find myself. Not on the grate with my knees, not on the pipes with the doves. Up top, in the furrow.

The conduit bank is furred with moths two and three deep, but the mice have parted a narrow trail through them as they go, nose to tail, and the moths close behind them again, unperturbed. I am the second one. The first goes a body-length ahead, pausing, whiskers working in the ammonia-feather-dust-oil air; I follow into the warmth he leaves.

My coat is absurdly thick for an animal that has never been outside. Grey, big-eyed, wintered from the inside out. I do not squeak. Squeaking would be lost in the full-bore roar through the concrete anyway — the surge tank and the penstock falling two thousand meters inside steel, the 50-cycle throb in my shinbones and sternum, without swell or fade. Over it, the dove moan, the ticking claws, the midge whine. Under it all, steady as a needle that never moves.

I move slow because cold demands it. Because to move fast would be to fall off. My feet find the lukewarm steel under the moth-down. I pause where the spiders have hung their hammocks below and gnaw at what they've dropped — a hollowed midge skin, a moth leg still dusted white. I drink without stopping, licking condensed water off a bolt head, the bead that will be replaced by the air itself. The air condenses on my whiskers, on my eyebrows if I had them.

From up here I can see the whole thickness: the riveted outer skin taking the wind at 3,700 meters, the poured inner skin around the surge, and us between, never meant to be inhabited. The forest of pipe. The ladder bolted flat to the wall because the spec said ladder. The green bulb behind wire glass that has not flickered in eleven years. The thumb-wide slit of louver letting in vertigo — white moving fast, cloud deck a kilometer below — and its knife of daylight that never moves, laid across concrete where three fern sprouts no taller than my claw, white for lack of light, lean forever toward the bulb and neither grow nor die. The algae smear. The moss in the bolt-hole. The drip forming on the lowest bolt. Forty-two seconds. The pail. The hole punched briefly in the roar, then closed again. The sump under the grate holding its three inches of black water that never rises and never falls. The lace collars of calcite in the drip pans, the perfect orange halo of first rust under every screw head that stops before it starts.

Tucked behind a flange where no nest should fit: wire-ties and moth wings and pulled mineral wool, and in it two squabs, almost translucent, gaping silently when I pass. The doves below me puff and resettle, shoulder to shoulder. The mites eat their dander as it sifts down. The pale fungus dots the dead. Water in equals water out. Heat in equals heat lost to the storm.

I settle in behind the first mouse, flank to flank in the moth fur for warmth. My heart is very fast and very small. I face inward with the birds, tail to the draft. When they coo together and that silence opens, I am inside it for half a second before the roar fills again.

Waiting for nothing to happen — which is exactly what happens, continuously.
