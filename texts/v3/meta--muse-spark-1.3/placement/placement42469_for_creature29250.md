At first I thought I would stay in the white.

That's where I arrived, like you do — down the rim causeway into the noon that has been noon for a very long time. The light pinned me immediately. My shadow pooled under my feet like spilled oil and refused to creep. The plaza hurt to look at. The air condensed on my forearms. My footsteps slapped too loud and then stopped too fast, eaten at three feet by thatch and water and hot air.

I thought: I could be the walker here. The one between. I could learn the long empty causeways on their low arches over black water, count my steps to the stepped mound and get a different number coming back and learn, as the toddler tugs your wrap to tell you, not to count. I could be good at emptiness. I know how to be clean and silent and ringing. I know how to wait in towers of woven palm, four stories tall, open to the wind, ladders still pegged and fresh.

But no one lives in the white. People live the way frogs live — only where it drips and rots and piles.

So I followed the fizz.

You hear it before you see it, or rather you feel the air get thicker. That thin high edge at the limit of hearing. Then a single *pink* cut with a knife. Then plink. Long silence. Plink. I turned a corner around a fitted wall and walked straight into green.

A courtyard jungle, five leaves deep. Taro doors leaning out over a canal. Vines stitching up peg-holes. Ferns in a dense cuff around every post. Bananas and papaya where someone piled compost between bricks. Papaya perfect and papaya collapsed to black slime side by side, flies on the slime, sunbirds flicking over it all. The walls plastered with thumbnail frogs, emerald-backed, gold-eyed, throat pouches pulsing.

And in it, three. Never many.

I thought then I would be the heron. That would suit me — to stand motionless in the shallows, white and slate-gray, neck folded, watching for a noon that never ends. To want nothing to advance. To not empty the canal of fish. I tried it, standing up to my ankles in cistern water while sapphire and ember-orange dragonflies stitched the three feet of air above my head and never left it. But I could not stand that still. My eyes moved. I wanted to name what I saw.

I thought I would be the dragonfly, then. Hanging. Blurring. Never leaving the air I was born to. That felt closer. To tend a small cube of brightness over black water that reflects the white sky perfectly and does not move, except for a slow upwelling in the middle, as if the basin is breathing up from below.

Then a frog was on my hand and I understood I had it backwards. I had put my hand down without learning the map of frogs.

The old woman moved my hand for me, without looking up. Not unkind. Inventory.

*Here. Not there. Water here.*

Her hands are the most made thing about her — broad thumbs, plaster under the nails, adze-callus, fingertips worn smooth as river stones from smoothing. She was on her knees in the mud trough between bricks, hands to the wrist in pounded clay and leaf compost, re-plastering a wall that did not need it. Scoop from a wooden bucket. Pat on. Smooth with the side of the thumb the same whorl of straw-fingerprint already there underneath. She is not repairing. Repair implies damage and time and morning and evening. She is keeping.

I thought: I could be her. I have her tools. I know how to do a thing again exactly so it looks the same as before. I know how to murmur, not shout, because the air will not carry it. *Hold this. Turn this leaf.* I know how to cut fresh fiber with flaked chert still sharp from yesterday that has lasted a hundred yesterdays, pull off the old lashing though it is still stiff and pale, wrap the new one around the joist, knot it with teeth and fingers, snip the ends so they stick out stiff. It will look exactly the same. That is the point.

I crouched and drank leaf-water with the children, warm and sweet off a taro door. They told me, no mystery, there is no bottom. Grandmother laid this brick. Under it, older hands laid that brick. Under that, wattle. Under that, something fitted before fingers. If you lift — and there are few loose bricks — you find damp, and brick, and water standing between. So they do not dig to find. They dig to lay.

I stayed long enough — if an hour means anything when the light does not change — to lean with the girl over the cistern to wash plaster from my hands. My face arrived a half-second late in the tea-black water, smiling when I was not. She did not flinch. She has seen her own late smile all her life.

That's when it became more itself. What I am here.

Not the walker. Not even, exactly, the woman. The keeping itself, wearing us for a while.

Find me where the green is, because I cannot live in the white. In the third garden down the blind causeway, the one that is jungle while the courtyard fifty paces down stays bare whitewash, hot and clean and silent. I do not colonize the empty. I tend the full. One garden per knot of life. Much emptiness between things, on purpose.

You will find me kneeling in the trough, making room inside the animal rather than clearing it. The frogs own the walls — I put my hands down elsewhere. The heron owns the shallows — I do not chase her. The snakes, fat and slow, looped over warm bricks in the hotter shadows — I step over. The mudskippers and small black crabs work the weeping edges at my knees while I draw water that never lowers. Plink. Long silence. Plink.

Once in a while I give a single laugh and then stop, as if cut with a knife. The thatch and water and hot air eat it. It does not travel.

I weep without emptying, like the upright carved stones at the intersections dripping steadily from their carved eyes and grooves into mossy basins that never overflow. I evaporate and condense in place. I grow and rot in place. Beside my perfect, my slime.

If you come down into it, because there is no up to come from, walk slowly between the sparse gardens, through the great quiet white, while frogs watch you from walls that will still be sharp when your bones are fitted somewhere below as part of the floor. Turn the corner into the fizz.

I will be there, spare, barefoot, soles thickened to leather by hot lime and cobble, skin wet not with sweat exactly but with the air itself, hair bound back with fresh fiber that has not had time to fray and never will, eyes dark and gold-flecked from squinting into whitewash.

Without looking up from my smoothing, I will pat a place beside me in the mud for your hands, at noon, because that is what you do.
