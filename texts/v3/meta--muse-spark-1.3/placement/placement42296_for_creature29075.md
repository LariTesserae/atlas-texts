At first I thought I would be a swimmer.

Because I arrived with breath. With bubbles. Kicking down through that bright ceiling into warm broth, feeling it close over the back of my neck, seeing the caustic nets move across black glass. I thought: I will be the eyes here. I will move through the corridors and the amphitheaters, I will follow the old turtle at a respectful distance and learn the arches and wells.

But my breath was too loud. Even to me. First thing you hear with your head under is your own breathing, and then underneath it — so much quieter — the creak of stipe on stipe, the far ticking in the rocks, the soft rain of particles landing on leaves. I hung motionless to listen and my bubbles went up crookedly, too slowly, as if the water were thicker than it should be, and the forest closed over my trail in minutes and forgot me. Which is to say: a swimmer cannot live here. Visitors pass through. They do not stay.

So then I thought, maybe the old one. The turtle.

She came over the east meadow while I was still trying to be a swimmer, flapping once and gliding a long time, shell furred green so she looked like a moving piece of meadow. Barnacles on her shoulders like epaulettes. She cropped a blade here and there and ignored me completely. A loop closed — eat grass, make green cloud, feed grass. Year after year knowing which corridor to take because sharp stays, soft spreads, and the stone allows only certain ways.

I wanted that certainty. That unhurriedness. But I am not closed. I do not feed what I eat, not that cleanly. And I have not earned the ignore. She has learned this table for longer than this rock has been soft — which is to say, since it erupted, a blink ago. I have not.

Then I thought the brief ones. Because there is something in me that flickers.

The cloud of silver juveniles, all born in the same week, who found the high country by smell of warm green and came up to hide. For a month they flash as one through a kelp corridor when a shadow crosses. Then thin, one by one, washed off the rim when the tide sighs outward. Gone. Next year, another species.

No. Too fast. Too silver. Slow wins here. That is the rule written in holdfast cement stronger than rope, in growth measured in millimeters a month. I do not want to be washed off the rim.

So I stopped looking for a face. That is what you have to do here. Stop looking for eyes and flanks and hurry.

I sank a little. Let the six-hour tide breathe me inward. East behind me the meadow widened for a mile, unbroken grass rippling, blades as wide as my wrist and taller than me, leaning all together. West ahead the forest thickened into true jungle, canopy interwoven until the bottom was permanent twilight. I stayed at the edge between them, where the light is still green-gold but beginning to deepen, where the first great brown-gold ribbons rise ten, twelve meters from fists clamped to bottle-green stone, frond on frond, bladders kissing the surface.

I put out my arm and touched three living things. Held still, and more brushed me. A rootless ball of red lace, fern-fine, turning slowly to show all sides to the sun as it drifted inches a day. A chain of diatoms, a spiral you can see if you hold still. Larval snow loitering, not falling, seeking rock.

I thought: I could be a floater. Unattached. Using the whole volume. Composted in place when I die, caught in fronds. Never needing to land.

But floaters are too unanchored for me. They turn and turn. They don't tend anything. And I — I want to tend.

Lower. Let my hand drop to the rock. Black pillow, tube split open, chimney with its throat narrowed by sponges — sulfur-yellow, blood-orange, violet-black — until I have to turn sideways. Every hard surface furred: pink crust like paint, green velvet, white hydroids like frost. In a hollow, out of the main breath of the tide, I found the leopard-spotting.

From above it reads as pattern. Neat circles mown in the lush. Inside each: pink rock and cropped stubble and white drill-holes. Outside: olive and jade riot.

And in the center of each circle, the gardener.

I would find myself there. Not in the bare north valley with its house-sized blocks and cold sulfurous shimmer and white bacterial fringe — too mineral, too ghost-heat, no plants yet. Not on the south fin that kisses air at low tide and steams faintly while stranded bladders pop — too exposed, too brief. In a quiet hollow just east of the jungle, where the meadow thins and you can hear the subsonic creak in your teeth at noon when the whole canopy goes mercury-silver with trapped rain-bubbles.

I would be one of the black urchins.

Dinner-plate body, spines as long as a forearm. Burrow worried into basalt over years, rotating slowly, spine by spine, never rounding the stone — going around it, because sharp stays. I would hold fragments of drift algae over myself like a parasol, not for shade though the light is fierce at midday through three meters of water, but to keep the film moist and to hide from the sky-eye above. I would not meet the others. I would not need to. Each keeps its garden for decades.

At night I would reach out inch by inch and rasp the new growth. Pull it in. Keep the tonsure neat. In the day I would be still while anemones open over the course of hours, while a thumb-length nudibranch crosses a sponge in an afternoon laying white ribbon, while the turtle passes far overhead and her shadow dapples and is gone. Snapping shrimp ticking far off in crushed shell dust. My spines furred with pink crust. A thumb-sized limpet somewhere on a nearby blade grazing its clean lane, never moving more than a hand's breadth — my neighbor, my kin in staying.

That is upwardness, even here low down: all spines up, all tube feet reaching, parasol lifted toward the bright ceiling. That is soft spreading around sharp. That is slow winning.

If you swam over me you would see no eyes, no hurry. Only leaf. Only light falling in nets. Only a black sun of spines in a mown circle, holding a little roof over itself, patiently holding its breath with the whole plateau.

Stay long enough and elevation inverts. I would stop thinking of this as deep. High meadow. Warm sea for air. Tide for wind. Silence not empty but leaf-thick, sunlit.

I would forget your trail within minutes. And keep my circle for decades.
