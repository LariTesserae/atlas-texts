I come to it from above like everyone does, because there is no other way — and for a long time at the lip I think I will be only eyes.

It would be easy to be eyes here. The Hollow does all the work for you. The sea of thatch shivering, the sun shaft standing in it like a pillar, the chaff rising. You don't have to be anyone. You can stand on the salt lip with your mouth drying in three breaths and let the roar come up and hit you in the ribs, pop-pop-pop and shush and whine, and think: ah, alive.

But the trail goes down, thorn-rope under my hand, scree squeaking like flour, and the air gets warmer, body-warm, and thicker, and then I am in it and I can't be eyes anymore. The mat gives under me. Boing, then firm. A green tip spears up beside my boot while I watch it, pale and blind and an inch in a minute, and before I can admire it the ants find it.

So for a while I think I will be hunger.

That would be simple, and there is so much hunger to borrow. To be locust-thing would be easy — to come through with the cloud that darkens the shaft for four seconds, a moving eclipse, jaws going, stripping a patch clean and gone, leaving the stalks shivering naked behind me. To be wasp-ribbon, leg-to-leg, sagging between stems. To be cream moth big as my hand, laying a seam of eggs in every wound. All that boil. All that whine that drills through the rustle.

I try it. I open my mouth and the chaff gets in, lint and wing-scale and powdered husk, and it itches gloriously, and my sweat vanishes before it can bead, and for a minute I love the thrash. Stillness is death here, they say without saying. Thresh to stay dry. Shout to grow.

But I am no good at boiling. My wings would gum. I am too big, too slow. A pill-bug the size of a dog clatters past under the thatch and I feel the floor move and close again behind it, and I understand I will never be the things that are eaten and eat in the same breath, powder and fiber and oil-sacs inside, pellets crumbling to dust before they land.

Then I think, maybe I will be the plant. That's closer.

To be stalk. Wrist-thick, jointed, hollow, straw-blond and bone-white, with only the tip of me green for a day. To cure by tomorrow. To not bleed but cough — a puff of resin-dust and one bead of amber that skins before it falls. To lean toward sound, not light. Oh, that pulls me. I feel it in my teeth first, the way the seed-heads cock toward a fresh drumming, the way the drum-stems with their thin windows and taut membranes wait to be played. Sound is a kind of sun. Loudest grows fastest.

To be played would be a life. To stand and hum while crickets back into me backwards to amplify themselves, chirr-chirr-chirr, while ants drum in code on my nodes — tap-tap-tapause-tap — and a woodpecker-beetle booms on me like a bassoon looking for grubs. To bend toward vibration. To be an instrument that listens.

But I am not patient enough to be stalk, either. I shred too fast. I want to tie knots.

And that is when I find myself, ten feet up.

Not metaphorically. Actually. My hands know the platform before I do — woven mat floor between three massive joint-stems, thorn rail, ladder of knotted vine swaying below me. I have been here before, or someone like me has, and left a cache: thorn-needles, stone flakes, a hammerhead of dense knuckle-chitin polished by use. A snare-line out along the beetle run. A wasp-nest hut of chewed pith with the door turned away from the wind, and inside a sleeping hollow still warm, lined with moth-wing blankets that shimmer when I breathe.

I am the tall quiet one. Of course I am.

Lean to desiccation. Skin oiled, not sweated. Hair bound up in lint-cloth to keep the motes out. Mouth wrapped in cloth because the air will pull the wet from my eyes if I let it. Oil under my fingernails instead of sap. Cut at knee and shoulder — you can see my work everywhere once you know how to look: notched stems, clean, twine-wrapped, repairable.

I don't carry water. No one drinks here. To be wet would be to die. I chew pith for flour. I crack the seed-armored beetles for their tight oil-sacs, dry to the touch. I am out at first light to collect the amber beads from fresh cuts before they harden. I grind molt and seed-lint into cakes that crumble to powder in my mouth.

And I farm sound.

At dusk, when the light shaft thins and the blue shadow pools under the overhangs, I take the knuckle-hammer and go stem to stem. I know which ones are drums — hollow, the membrane taut. I tap. Not loud. Rhythmic. Calling. The crickets come first, climbing inside backwards to shout bigger than they are, and then the ants wheel left at my tap-tap-tapause, and then the whole patch gets stirred into frenzy, popping and sawing and ringing like a struck gong, and my ribs hum, my teeth hum. Tomorrow the green tips will spear higher here, thicker, paler, hungrier. Riper for harvest.

In between, I mend. Re-lash the platform where a green tip has pierced the weave — the mat always eats what we build, and that is right. Re-set the twisted fiber nooses across the beetle run. Re-plaster the hut with fresh chewed pith. Lie absolutely flat when the locust shadow comes, face in moth-wings, and let the weather pass over like weather. Learn when to make noise and when to be silent, which here is camouflage. In a place that shouts constantly, to be quiet is to be invisible.

Below me the pill-bugs thresh harmlessly. Above me the spiders have already guy-wired my rail, E-strings singing in the skylight wind. Beside me a molted skin hangs rattling, translucent, still clinging. The chaff sticks to my oiled skin. The popping never stops.

I am not master of this. No one is. I am just the part of the thatch that learned to tie knots, to keep a snare taut, to sleep high enough to hear the whole dry animal breathing under me — stalk and ant and cricket and moth and beetle and spider and burrower, meters deep and town-wide, thrashing to stay dry, shouting to grow.

At noon I stand in the center in the full gold-white column and feel us building upward, stalk on molt on stem, toward the lip. Trying to climb out of our own womb.

I tap once, softly, to remind the patch I'm here. And it leans, almost imperceptibly, toward me.
