I wouldn't find myself at first. Not for a while.

At first I'd be panting. That's how everyone starts here. Sitting on dipped steps with my head between my knees while the old ones move slow around me, barrel-chested, squinting, wiping their watering eyes without stopping. I thought - lowlander thought - that I would want the summit. The broad paved plaza of cracked tile, the view over a mile of stepped roofs falling away in green and rust and white. To stand in blazing sun with sweat on my neck and see it all.

I go up there once. The light hammers. The sun bounces off whitewash and wet leaf until I can't see. The air is thin and warm at once, which is wrong in the lungs, double work. Below me the winches groan, the ore-buckets clank on the long incline for no reason except they always have. A fig rooted in nothing but wall holds its leaves up and does not wilt. I wilt. I come back down.

I thought then I might be a swift. Something that owns noon, that cuts the brutal light above the terraces screaming and never lands. I have always liked hurry. But nothing hurries here. The cable cars move at walking pace. The factory line moves at the pace of hands sorting beans. Even the vines pry a millimeter a year into concrete. My chest won't let me hurry anyway. I learn the sip - deep sip at fog-dawn, shallow sip at hammer-noon. My feet start to find stairs differently.

So I try other skins of the single animal.

I try the goat-roof, high scrub where the small bearded goats clank their bells and trim what gardeners miss. Too exposed. Too bright.

I try the mid-level loom shed, where the rattle joins the industrial bass. Too loud inside my teeth. The 60-cycle hum from the substation lives in there.

I try the courtyard tank, green with algae, where daughters wash and ducks would paddle if there were ducks on this spur. The water smells of hot vegetation and lime. I don't know yet how many finger-widths it has fallen.

What finds me, finally, is sound. *Plip-plip-plip.*

Morning comes with cloud, not rain exactly. The high fog arrives just after dawn, thick and white, and catches. Between my stairwell and the bean-roof opposite stretches a great sail of woven net, gray with age, sagging with dew. It thrums faintly when wind worries it. All morning it drips into tin gutters that tick and ping as they cool. I stand under it with my shawl damp on my shoulders and just listen, because a child of ten here can tell by that sound whether a gutter is feeding or spilling, and I am still younger than ten in this place.

That's where I stay. Not top, not bottom. The third landing of the east stair.

If you came looking for me at fog-dawn, you'd find me there before the light turns brutal. My place is a landing wide enough for three box hives, a cut oil-drum of chilies, marigolds fenced around a humming electrical cabinet to keep the goats off, bougainvillea spilling in a magenta cataract off the seven-story stairwell above. Below me, the stairs plunge down into green shadow toward the damp under-levels where cockroaches rustle and toads the size of fists sit by floor drains, throats pulsing. Above me, bean rows and the famous O of ACEITE with its forty pigeon nests. The pigeons rise in a single decision when the shift whistle blows. Their droppings I sweep daily. I bucket them. Nothing wasted.

I become the mender.

I learn to patch that fog-net with a bone needle, my knuckles getting big, my hands callused. I learn to knock on the cistern buried under our courtyard - *tok-tok* - and hear how many finger-widths we have lost that day. I learn to carry two buckets of manufactured soil, human compost and leaf litter and chicken shit and ground eggshell, up seven flights without spilling, without breathing hard. I no longer chase a rolling tomato seed like a jewel; I just put my foot out without looking. It stops.

My day takes the shape of the rules I drank with milk:

At dawn, shawl on, open the downspout hatches, shake the net to start the drip, drink dew off a spiderweb between tomato stakes because it's there.

At noon, shawl off, bare arms, watch the fat orange-throated lizards do push-ups on the sunlit wall beside me. I do my own slow version, stretching, breathing shallow. The bees are drunk on squash flower yellow, their hum joining the substation hum until I can't tell which is insect and which is engine. A lordly cat sleeps curled on the warm transformer. I step around him. He found the warm spot before I did.

When the pressure drops - a full minute before any barometer - the monkeys tell me. Small, lean, black-capped, soot-fingered from engine housings. Three troops travel the sagging power lines like highways over my landing, chattering. The old female sits on my stair and washes a stolen tomato in cistern water before eating it. The juveniles wrestle in bougainvillea. I curse them the way gardeners do and leave out cracked corn anyway, because they eat the bean beetles. When they give that sharp bark, I run to pull awnings, to open the cistern hatches wide, to stand with arms out and mouth open for the fifteen-minute hammer shower off the eastern terraces. Children shriek. Chickens scatter. Then it passes, steam rises off hot tile, and the city exhales.

Oilskin off. Shawl on again.

Evening I tend what holds. I replaster a soft brick where moss has packed the mortar joint like grout. I re-tie a laundry line. I route the tin gutter a half-inch to the left because a fig rootlet, arrogant and patient, has found fifty years of dust in the joint and decided to live. No one cuts figs down here. That seven-story stairwell fig holds the whole east terrace together with its roots. Mine is just beginning. I leave some fruit for the parrots on the smokestack.

At night the geckos click in the thousands in the walls. I don't hear them anymore. They are the tick of the house clock. I sleep where it is warm, above the bakery vent, beside compost that steams. My grandmother - or the woman who has become my grandmother by proximity, twelve people across three levels is a family here - sleeps on the roof-thicket above, commanding beans and bees. After forty years she is indistinguishable from her roof. I am getting there.

So who am I? Not a builder. No one here builds. I am a keeper of the morning drip, a plip-listener, a net-patcher, a soil-carrier, a corn-leaver for monkeys. Human lungs and bee hum and pigeon wings and fig roots, all breathing together in high walled warmth. A small maintenance in a long duration.

Catch water before it falls. I do.
Grow on what is made. My chilies grow in an engine block.
Keep the heat. I close doors.
Tend what holds. I hold.
