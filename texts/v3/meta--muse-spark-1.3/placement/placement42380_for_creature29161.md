At first I don't know where to put myself. That's the bowl's doing.

I come in with dry boots, which is wrong. Everyone's boots are wet here in the first minute. That's the rule. So first, I am only feet. Ankle-deep in sheet-water moving over newborn stone, splitting and rejoining around slabs. Trying not to hurry because the moss takes the ankle if you hurry. My step sighs into a mattress a foot thick and a dozen pale moths lift a hand's breadth — the color of lichen, almost translucent — and settle again, as if I had startled the moss into exhaling.

I thought I would be the raven. That was my first vanity.

To be the one thing that can stand the enormous sky. To cross from rim to rim for a long time through nothing while everyone below leans close to hear each other. But I am not at home in air like that. The pair of mountain crows ride the updraft off the veil-falls all afternoon, croaking, tumbling, louder than water. I am quieter than that. And I don't want to look down at green terraces softening into steps. I want to be down in them, beaded.

Then I thought I would be the old man. That was my second vanity.

Kneeling in the weep where frost has already split a blue-black slab clean, squaring it where it fell. Ash-dust blooming on my forearms. Hands that know weight. Do not round it — the bowl will round itself in ten thousand years; until then, sharp locks better. It is beautiful work, patient work. But my hands don't know weight yet. My fingers go pink at noon. My breath smokes faintly even in the bright ordinary midday light. I fumble the hammer-stone. I want to carry stone uphill instead of waiting for the rim to give it. I still think like a dammer.

So where, then?

Not on the ridge, grey and jagged to the horizon. Not at the palisade where the water finally finds an exit and pours a thousand meters to the valley. Not shouting. Nobody shouts here. The water always wins and shouting would sour the whole cup.

I find myself, instead, lower. Smaller.

On the second terrace, near the cisterns but not at them. Crouched the way the woman is crouched over an under-drain, water running clear over both wrists. Learning to seat a flat slate over a gurgling dark. My tools beside me in the wet: hammer-stone, bone awl, a coil of bark for gasket. Behind me a doorframe with green copper pins holding the pine lintel true. No iron. No nails. Nothing made anywhere I can't see from where I kneel.

I am not her. Not yet. She lifts her hammer and knocks slate twice against slate — *tock tock* — and the bowl takes it and lifts it and hands it to everyone, and two heads come up on two other terraces, and a child stops mid-hop on a singing stone. Mine is clumsier. *tock* — once, toward the lane, which I have just learned means *see, it's only feet, let it come.* The note hangs, amplified by the curve of the cliffs. The whole town is a drum and I am still learning how not to bruise it.

That is where I am: in the mid-tone rush. There is the high white hiss of the veil-falls sheeting over the lip in a hundred places, ten, fifteen meters down. There is the low hum when wind catches the rim and the bowl resonates like a cup you run a finger around. And underneath, where I work, the under-lanes gurgling under bark covers. Drip, seep, trickle, needle-pricks on top. If I put my ear close I can hear my own heart under it. Nothing is eroding fast enough to hear. The stone is still deciding what shape it will be, still full of bubbles, still porous, still almost breathing.

What am I, here? Not an owner.

I thought keeper was too grand a word for thirty people in a town built for three hundred. Midwife is better. I am tending foothold, not field. Too high for grain — we know it, though we sweep the communal threshing platform obsessively clean of moss out of habit, out of hope. What we grow is the thing that makes soil where there is no soil yet.

So I am on my knees in lime-green star moss and black-green cushion moss, in liverworts like wet leather. Ferns uncurling tight as fists from cracks. Dwarf willow no higher than my knee but a hundred years old, trunk twisted as rope. Woolly white edelweiss-cousins and purple saxifrage jammed furiously into any dust of crushed stone, blooming low to get out of the wind. I weed around them. I lift slates for them. I channel — never dam — water around them. Only deadfall pine brought up from below on a bent back for fuel. Peat cut sparingly from the thickest mattress where it will regrow, leaving a neat square wound. I am the one who watches that wound green over by next season.

In the evening, when mist rises not from the valley but from the bowl itself, exhaled off the moss as the air cools another notch, I eat shoulder to shoulder under a turf eave with water hissing off it. The four stone-grey sheep shift on the roof above us and continue the moss right over the house; their bells go *tung... tung*, dull and intermittent under the roar. The boy with the pika-tooth necklace, barefoot by choice, moves them with stamps and hand-claps, never with voice. No dogs. No chickens. Too high, too wet, too new. A pika called once from the rim rocks at noon — *eep!* — and thought better of it. Clouds of midges hang over the standing puddles. The veil-falls thicken and blur. In niches in the walls the tallow lamps in stone cups come on like fireflies, absurdly small in all that wet stone and space.

I speak low, foreheads almost touching. My wool is undyed, grey and brown and moss-green, always damp. It beads. My eyebrows bead. The back of my hands bead. Condensation never stops here, even when it isn't raining. The cool air wrings itself out on the cold new stone. The town glistens as if breathed upon.

And late, when the adults aren't looking — because I am still partly a child in this place, still new enough to play the instrument — I hop from singing stone to singing stone in the lane. The children mapped them: the standing slates that give back a faint metallic tone when stepped on right. Slow, deliberate, a tuneless scale that the bowl carries and enlarges until it sounds like the stones themselves are thinking aloud. No one scolds. In a town that is a drum, even play has to be tuned.

That is who I have become, more itself the longer I stay: one of the thirty-one. Hands pink with cold, boots dark to the shin, learning unfinished rules for unfinished land. Never stop the sheet. Cut no living moss. Take only what frost has split, dress it where it fell, leave it sharp.

Keeping the water moving while the world learns its shape.
