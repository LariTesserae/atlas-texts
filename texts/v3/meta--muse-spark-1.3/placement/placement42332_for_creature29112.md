I come up the way everything comes up, if it comes at all — on the rusted ribbons laid on slag, climbing and never quite arriving. 

At first I think I know where I'll be. I think I'll be up high, where you can see it whole. On the sorting shed roof, on my back on tar paper still warm from a day that left a million years ago. Looking, recording. The visitor with a white lamp and a notebook that curls in the dry. The eyes.

No. That doesn't hold. The dark is communal here. My lamp is a theft. I find my hand cupping it without being told, hooding it down to a red leak. I find myself apologizing to it with my palm.

So maybe lower. Inside. I think I'll be the hands — the man at the end of the conveyor with his weight leaned into the stilt-leg to feel if the tremor is right. Or the woman between tenements, coiling rope hand over hand, testing it. Useful. Attending. I try that on. I stand in a machine hall with no doors and put my palm flat to a housing.

The housing is warm. Warmer than my palm. It ticks as it cools and doesn't cool. The flywheel goes around, impossibly slow, a minute per revolution, maybe less, and I feel it not in my ears but in my molars, in the fillings, in the hinge of my jaw. 50-cycle throb. My own blood is too fast for this place. It hammers. I have to stand still a long time to let it quiet.

I am not good at standing still yet. My sweat tries to bead and is taken before it can do its work. My lips crack within the hour. Dust gets in my shoes, my teeth. Iron and old electricity. I breathe through my mouth and learn not to. Shallow, through the nose, conserved. The air holds against my skin like breath held in a mouth.

So not the hands yet. Hands leave two degrees on a rail for a minute and these people can read that. Mine leave too much heat, frantic heat. New heat.

Maybe I will be smaller, then. Quieter.

I think about the child squatted by the open cistern, dropping stones one by one to listen for a bottom that never answers. I like that game. Listening as play. I squat though my knees complain and drop a pebble and hear it *tock-tock-tock* down the perfect concrete bell and vanish into depth that never answers. Nothing comes back. That's the point. To learn how deep empty is. I do it again. And again. My breathing slows to match the interval between stones.

Closer. But the children here do not run, do not chase, do not shout. They lay their cheeks against warm pipes to guess which hall is turning. I try that too. The pipe burns faintly against my cheekbone and I hear, through bone, a bearing ten meters away sighing in oil. Somewhere above me in the cable trays, claws tick-tick on steel. Duct-rats, nose to tail, whiskers caked stiff with graphite, going to lick the salt from a collar left hanging overnight. They pause and look at me with eyes like wet ball bearings reflecting red, and are gone before my hooded lamp finds them.

I think, for a while, I will be that. Overhead. Unseen. A parasite that knows every hot culvert the way you know hallways. It would be easier to be hungry than to be responsible for warmth.

But underneath the powerhouse the plates ring faintly under my boots and the air shimmers though there is no light to shimmer in, and something noses at my laces.

Low, eyeless, the size of a shoe. Skin like warm pudding-stone. A cinder-pup. It finds me by the warmth of my feet — I am the only thing in the culvert that is roughly blood temperature and moving — mouths my bootlace once, thoughtfully, and thinks better of it. Melts back with a sound like a sigh through sand.

I kneel. That is where I find myself. Not on the roof seeing whole, not in the hall tending, not overhead stealing. Low. In the hottest culvert, where I have to fold myself to fit, where the stone leaks its old day into my knees and palms.

I stay there long enough that my heartbeat tries, and fails, and tries again to match the throb in the walls. My sweat learns not to come. My throat dries and I understand, without being taught, about the half-spoon of clean machine oil morning and night. About the biscuit tin with the folded rag in wax paper that may not have been damp in a hundred years and doesn't matter. The ritual is the moisture.

Dust powders my eyebrows, my knuckles. It gets into the creases and stays. My pupils stay wide. I stop looking for shape and start seeing heat — the afterglow of a handrail someone touched a minute ago, the faint warm coin of a face passing two streets over, the small hot eyes of pilot lamps, amber and blood-red, with moth dust on them. Pale ash-moths, the size of a thumbnail, beating softly *tit tit tit* against the glass until their wings fray to stubs, then crawling into a crack to thin away. I do not brush them off. It would be a waste of motion.

What am I here? Not engineer, not miner — there is no ore, no product. The bins are full of the same dust that is everywhere. The presses kiss empty dies and withdraw, kiss and withdraw.

I am what remains when production wears away like the corners of the buildings. A caretaker cell. Blood.

I learn the path by feet. Keep to the warm paths. Cold metal is dead load, it will not hold you, it will be brittle and betray you. I keep to the warm. I walk the kilometer of nothing between the boiler house and the tenements slowly, so slowly, because that emptiness is not empty but balanced, skin stretched between hot hearts. Walking it keeps it warm. I tap a pipe: *this way is warm.* I hold my palm flat: *wait.* My voice, when I use it, comes out hoarse and oiled and sparing.

In time — not my lifetime, nor a hundred lifetimes, for the rounded corner will not round further while I watch — I will be warmest. Like the old ones sitting against the powerhouse wall with hands folded, radiating like a stove banked for night. Tuned. Able to tell by an ear to housing what's going dry ten meters away. Not repairing. Attending. A wipe of waste on a journal. A quarter-turn of a greaser. A long lean while the turning continues.

If you come looking for me, you won't find crowds. You'll hear a boot-scrape two streets over and turn and find no one. But the rope will be taut where I coiled it. The bearing block will be healthy, with a cinder-pup curled against it. The handrail will hold two degrees of my palm for a minute after I'm gone.

Lie down on the tar paper if you want to understand. Feel the high flat extend past seeing. Feel the scattered pulses below. Breathe the thin warm dust. Nothing begins. Nothing ends.

I am already inside what lives here.
