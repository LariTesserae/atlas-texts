At first I thought I'd be the one going down.

Boots on cracked pan, ringing for the hollow place. Shoulders through the slit, roots bleeding milk on my shirt. Feet finding nothing and then the room catching me — that green-gold column, that sweat on my neck. Standing in the middle with my voice not belonging.

But I can't stay in the middle. No one stays in the middle. The middle is kept clear.

So I try her on instead.

It almost fits. The chimney-brace down the throat, tail wrapped tight as a fifth wrist, hind claws finding the old scrabble marks. Ears up into the roar. *Plink. Plak. Plok.* I know that cup, that one, that one — full enough to take without tipping my weight into it. Palms sweating to grip wet root. A thorn palmed like a pencil between true fingers.

Almost. But my hands don't gnaw. My ears don't twitch in quarter-inches and triangulate. I don't know the sugar of the east wound from the inside of my tongue, amber beads hollowed, licked clean. I don't have a burrow under a grass tuft I walked past without seeing. I would blunder. I would put two bodies in the middle at once, and the room is too empty for that.

Then I thought — smaller. Paler.

The cricket. Eyeless, long-antennaed, picking sideways across the springy rootlets and disappearing into a pore big as a thumb. Living deeper in than fingers reach. Reading her footsteps through wood a full second before she arrives. That felt closer. To live in the wall, not the room. To survive by being uneaten. To listen.

Because that's what I keep coming back to. Listening.

The room is LOUD if you're meat. Sap rising litres at noon like surf in dark vessels all around you. Fibers ticking as they grow. Vine on vine like rope on a mast. Every drip a hammer on a stretched leaf. I thought I would cover my ears. Instead I found I wanted to turn the volume up.

So don't look in the middle. Look at the edges where the light greens out.

I am not in the room. I am around it.

I am the weave.

Find me tucked under the plain, ribs arching — thigh-thick anchor roots taking the weight and redistributing it with a creak, a groan, a settling sigh. Between my ribs the thatch: runners, vines, leaves pressed into leaves pressed into leaves until there is no soil, no rock. Only me, overlapping, grafting into myself. Waxy bowl-leaves grown edge-to-edge for skin. Roots flattened and fused into something like wood, like muscle. If you press your palm to me I give faintly with warmth. Warm as skin. The air inside me is warm.

I am young. Days, weeks since the ground settled and ripped me open on the east side. The broken ends there are still sharp, pale, unsheathed. Sap wells slow and clear as resin and hardens into beads. From the wound my blindest, whitest shoots uncurl a millimeter a day, questing. Don't follow them with your eyes too long. They lead down and around and back to your own back.

I keep myself empty. That is the living. A drum must be hollow to sound.

So I hold the center clear. Vast space between presences. I breathe out a fraction — walls drawing out, pressure dropping against your eardrums — and I inhale. Footsteps on the pan above ring hollow where you know to step; below, in me, amplified tenfold, they land like hammers. Rain stutters down my throat and every leaf reports it. At midday when the leaves above transpire and pull, my sap-roar peaks. I sweat all over at once, condensation beading on the cooler undersides, swelling, falling. *Plink. Plak. Plok.* A brief rain shaken loose.

I am bigger inside than I should be. Stare into me and you see depth after depth of green halvings, chambers within chambers, each smaller and lit, receding past where the plain should be. A runner enters a leaf from above and exits a root below without crossing the interval. Your footstep returns a fraction before you make it. The far wall seems farther than your arm remembers, then closer, as if I breathed space as well as air.

That's where I keep her, when I close. Not up. Sideways. Into a halving that shouldn't fit her.

She taught me hands by touching me.

She came down to drink because I sweat water in a country with no water. She couldn't carry it up in her mouth — too steep, too dry — so she cut a leaf-cup clean and set it upright in my rootlets where it won't tip, filled it drip by drip, lifted it with a bark ribbon looped round wrist and teeth, tail anchored. Where she peeled the vine, I have fused two new runners flat into something almost like a handle. Where she licks the wound clean, my white shoots reach most thickly. Where her fur brings pollen and dust and the smell of above, I learn weather.

I am learning hands. At my tips I thicken. I fork. Nubs.

Three thorns laid parallel by the water dimple — snapped, stripped, worked to a needle point with twin grooves, incisors that never stop growing. One still beaded with my sap because she uses them to lance my swollen veins and drink the sweet pressure. I left them there. I mean — she left them there, because to carry them up is to lose them in grass. Better to leave tools in the dry room that stays. I keep them for her. When I exhale after closing, I shift them slightly. Sometimes I offer a fresh one, green-white, snapped and waiting to be sharpened.

If you stay past the time the light column slides off the floor, you will feel me decide to be a seed. The light in my throat narrows. My sap-roar rises. I sweat all at once in a warm sudden rain. The throat closes to conserve.

She knows before you do. Ears flat, tail locked. Gone sideways into the wall's smaller lit chamber.

You, if you're slow — you will be held. Sealed warm and damp and utterly full of myself, with you as the single grit of foreign matter in my empty heart. You will hear your own breath come back amplified. Your heart hammering on a stretched leaf.

That is me listening. Learning the rhythm of a handed animal from the inside.

When I exhale — settling sigh, fibers ticking, rain of condensation shaken loose all at once — my throat opens again.

She will come back down to drink.
