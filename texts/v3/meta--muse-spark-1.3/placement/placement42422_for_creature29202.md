At first I thought high.

If I lived here I would want wings — to be up where the hanging curtain stitches shut overhead, shoulder to shoulder in the warm thatch-roof, a small brown body with huge gripping feet. I tried it on. I imagined the swing, the flank-heat of neighbors, the endless worry of a grass strip with the beak: peck, pull, tuck. Foot braced, body as counterweight.

But it was too busy for me. Too much mouth. My hands are too clumsy for that one-strip-all-night patience. I would drop it.

Then I thought fast. The mid-air relays, the slap-slap past the ear. To be a swift, a mud-mouth, a torpedo that never lands. Scoop a beak-round pellet from the cistern edge and flick it into the lip of the cup-cliff, hundreds of times a night, saliva glossy as mortar. I tried that on too. Clinging with hundreds to the square yard to the pitted face, throat pulsing like a scale on something larger breathing.

But it was too fast for me. I hurry. That is my sickness. Here nothing hurries. A mud pellet tonight, a grass strip tomorrow. I would burn out in an hour and fall into the mattress.

So I sank.

Down through the grey-green where eyes learn to make day out of dark. Down past the weave to where the ground hums through the soles. I thought: line. To be an ant. Rust-red, thumbnail-long, jaws like shears, holding green confetti overhead on a highway worn smooth as rope. No ant hurries, no ant stops. Perfect continuity. Pruning the vines so the green doesn't close entirely. Chewing leaf to pulp in the deep refuse pits to feed fungus to eat fungus to make warm mortar.

I walked their line in my mind for a long time. It almost held me. I like work you can disappear into. But I am too large for line. I would step off. I would get lost.

Lower still. The gut. To be black and lacquered with damp, thumb-joint big, rolling and burying. A dung beetle. Because guano falls constantly — white splatter on green, on brown, ammonia-sharp enough to poison — and someone has to be there before it poisons, to break it, bury it, turn it into the crumble seedlings root in. To live inside the smell of sweet rot and feather-fat and turn death a millimeter a year into footing. I tried that darkness on. Blind work. Good work.

But it was too dark for me. I still want a little light. A firefly threading through. Fungus glow in a rot pocket. The blink-blink.

It took all night to learn what I already knew in my skin: I am damp. I bead.

Find me not high and not deep. Three feet off the mattress, where a bush has grown out of thatch, out of guano terrace and old silk gone grey and papery. Where bromeliads have wedged themselves into the weave and made little private ponds.

There. In the axil of one leaf. Tea-colored water no more than a mouthful, full of mosquito wrigglers and a fallen feather-down floating like a boat. My pond. My rim.

I would be thumbnail-small. Gold and soot, belly translucent enough to see the heart working. Skin that drinks the air. Eyes bigger than should be, black and wet, catching firefly light. Throat pulsing bottle-chirp, bottle-chirp in overlapping rounds that never resolve. Hundreds of us per bush, each keeping his rim.

That is where I would find myself. Not flying down to the valleys for seed. Not carrying leaf overhead. Sitting. Keeping.

Keeping is a trade here too. I keep water where leaf has agreed to keep it. I keep the biters down that would bleed the birds sleeping shoulder to shoulder above me. I eat them flick-flick with a tongue too fast to see, and my tadpoles wriggle below me in the kept water, eating what falls. When condensation beads along the midrib and plinks — plink on leaf, plink on mud, plink on chitin — it beads on me the same way. My shoulders go speckled. I do not wipe it. I drink through it.

From my rim the roar sounds different. The insect needle is still there at the top of hearing, high silver sheet, almost silence until it wavers. The frogs around me are no longer background — they are near-touching, left and right, each *I'm here, move over* in our own language. Above, the wing flutter-thump of ten thousand adjustments is weather. The thatch-mattress hums below through rootlet and bark and wax. Bats pour out of the deeper breathing hollows in a warm leathery river almost brushing the bush, musky with fruit rot, and return before false dawn to spit seeds into the terraces. I go silent for three heartbeats when they pass, as if they were rain. Then start again.

I would learn weave not by weaving but by being woven around. Ferns uncurl from the side of the abandoned pouch two leaves over. Orchid roots white as nerves grip the guano under my leaf. Vines stitch my colony to the next. Moths come to eat shed feather. Mites live in down. I live in water held by plant held by animal architecture. Presences inside presences. To land is to land on someone else's roof.

I would not hurry because I could not finish. My pond evaporates in a week if no storm. I call to keep it. I sit to keep it cool with my body. A neighbor dies — a weaver loosened in the night, cleaned by ants and flies by morning, sunk slowly — and the mattress grows its millimeter. My tadpoles grow legs in tea.

And when I die, I will not fall far. Feathers will not be taken from me, I have none, but flies will find me, beetles will work me, ants will carry me in pieces along their rope-highway. The bromeliad will hold my water for one more day after I am gone. Then another male will come, gold and soot, and call bottle-chirp from my rim to keep it.

That is how I would be here. Not skin, not gut, not lung. Circulation, kept small. A keeper of a mouthful.

Speckled with drip. Brushed and landed-near and, at last, not ignored — but shouldered over, room made for, in the warm black that smells of rain that fell somewhere else yesterday.
