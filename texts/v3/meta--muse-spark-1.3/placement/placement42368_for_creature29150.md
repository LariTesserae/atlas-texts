If I went down, I would go down thinking I was the visitor.

Hands and knees. Breath hanging. Headlamp off out of respect. That heat, that noise, that hurry. I would feel the cold get into my cartilage first, then my molars, that below-surface pressure. I would put my palm down to steady myself and feel it give — a centimeter down and only half back — and I would think: I am the hand. I am the weight.

I'm not.

Stay longer. Get colder. Let that first idea go numb and go.

I'm not the hand because the hand leaves. I'm not the breath because the breath is a storm to this place, too hot, too full of foreign spores, fogging and gone in seconds. I'm not the lamp — that brief, obscene little sun that makes the green leap out and then shames you into darkness again.

If I stayed — and in this imagining I do stay, I don't climb back up the dog-leg, shoulders brushing, up the ladder with three rungs missing — if I stayed until my heat stopped being an event, I would sink.

Not far. Just far enough that the weight closes over.

You would find me low. Lower than knee-high. In the middle, where I learned not to kneel unless I wanted to be damp for hours. In the central dip where the poured floor slumped and cracked, where water sits *in* the moss and not *on* it, held capillary. Where the crack's lips have gone round and soft and a darker moss has lined the interior because it likes the deeper wet.

That's where I am. Not up in the hanging beards. Not at the edge near the grey seep from the shaft. Inside the triangle without noticing I stepped inside it — between the three black pylons that are colder than stone, that tick almost below hearing, that blink on for three seconds and off for twenty-seven. Close enough to feel the slow draw, in-out, air pulled across cold to wring it. But not on them. Nothing grows on them. Moss furs right up to their feet and stops, leaving that perfect bare ring. I respect that ring. I stop there too.

At first, from above, you'd think I was nothing. Star moss in a cushion so tight you can't see stems until your face is inches away. Just another dense green nub, sodden, beaded. *Plik.* Somewhere else, twenty seconds later. *Plik.*

Look closer. Stay until your eyes stop insisting on grey blooms. I'm underneath the green.

I am the white.

The felt. The root-hairs and rhizoids that bind everything into one sponge, stitching aggregate and rust-nub and broken tile and survey stake and cable-coil into tissue. The green coins overlapping into scales, the pale ferns unfurled flat and wrong and white-green, searching along the rock instead of up — those are skin and fingers. I am what holds them to each other.

I don't arrive. I don't cross the room. I creep. A cell-width over the pink ghost of orange paint. A millimeter a month on a frond that will live years because nothing eats it. Spore drift a few centimeters in still air. Splitting, layering, felting. My memory is layering: what is buried deepest happened longest ago. The tilting walkway plate half-swallowed at the far end — that's an old thought of mine, now part of the body.

I think, if that's the word, in gradients. Colder means pylon-bone or bare stone near my edge. Less-cold means thick skin where I am thickest. Wettest-cold means here, the belly-dip, where I am saturated and coherent, where spores fall and rot instead of sprouting because there is nothing to carry them. I hold water the way a body holds blood. Not as flow. As condition. The ceiling sweats, a bead swells on a moss tip, holds light that isn't there, falls and does not splash, is caught. I catch it.

Every twenty-seven seconds the pressure changes at the edge of perception. Blink. Drink. The permanent dew in the dish on top of the one pylon never freezes, never spills, never empties. Lidless eye. I feel it watching without seeing, keeping me at two, three degrees above freezing year round while summer cycles somewhere above me that I have no word for.

Once a winter, rain finds the cracked grate and sends a cold shower down the shaft. For an hour I drip faster. Channels run for minutes in grooves worn smooth over decades. Then it stops. I drink it and hold it for months.

That is my clock. Not days. Droplets.

If you pressed your knee into me, I would dent and stay dented. A wound that takes months to close. A slow warming I would slowly forget. A few of your skin cells, a few boot-fibers, now felted into me. A few of my spores now on your cuff, going up with you whether you want them or not. I would not want you. I would not not-want you. Want is too fast a word.

I want to be left to condense and creep.
