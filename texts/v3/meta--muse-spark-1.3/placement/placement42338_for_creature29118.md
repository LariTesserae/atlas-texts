At first I thought I'd be easy to place.

Coming up out of the switchbacks with wet felt squeaking under my boots, nose already running in that high white cold, I thought: put me on a crew. Give me oilskins gone green at the knees. I can learn hand signals. I can learn to navigate by the hum in the puddles.

I tried them on one by one as I walked.

Not Trimming. I watched them ride out at noon on the little diesel crawlers — you hear them coughing right in your ear when they're half a kilometer away across the moss, and then nothing at all when they're twenty meters behind a stem. They can see brown the way I only see green. To me a stalk is a pillar, a wonder. To them it's a calendar, a chore: this sheath live and straining, groaning wood-on-wood without wood, leave it; this sheath spent and papery, ready to rot the mat if left, take it. CUT ONLY THE BROWN, flaking on every engine house. I don't have that eye yet. I'd cut wrong.

Not Condensation. They're too early and too nimble for me, out when the cloud is still sitting *in* the bowl, siphon coils over their shoulders, moving stalk to stalk to bleed the cup-leaves before the black mirrors get heavy enough to snap. They know which leaf lies. Which one will show you a cloudless sky while rain is hitting your back. They spit after siphoning and never drink. I would want to drink. I would want to look too long into the wrong sky.

Not Fiber. Though I liked the evening sheds best, boiler-warm and smelling of yeast and hot oil, the endless pale bales stacked by a siding that goes nowhere since the road washed out. Their hands are soft-swollen from perpetual damp. They make and stack and burn the surplus just for heat, just to keep the place from swallowing itself. I could feed a mill. But I don't belong inside, where it's warm.

So I kept walking, past one street, then a hectare of empty breathing green, then a factory standing alone like a ship with amber windows, then nothing again, until it was 2 a.m. without me deciding it.

That's where I found myself.

Out between the lonely buildings, where the copper runs for hectares, sweating condensation into buckets. Plink, tick, gurgle under the bigger noise. The stalks creaking deep overhead, the big leaves doing their low flap-flap-FLAP you feel in your sternum. Frost glittering on the mat though it's summer. My cuffs cold to the wrist. The 50-cycle hum living in every pipe and puddle.

I had a little hammer. I was tick-tick-ticking along the pipe.

That's the Night Pressure walk. The old ones do it, deaf in one ear from years of saws. A clear pipe rings. A blocked one breathes.

I thought that would be me — the listener. The one who marks it in chalk and doesn't talk about it till morning. It suited me: to walk and listen for what shouldn't be there, to tend.

But then I knelt to a valve that sighed, and I heard it, and it kept becoming more itself the longer I listened.

In-out. In-out. Not wind in the pipe. Something using the pipe as a throat.

And I understood I had it backwards. I wasn't the one with the hammer. Or not only.

Because to live long enough inside a living thing that old — ten meters of felted sphagnum-peat over round rock, grown over its own dead for longer than mountains have held shape — is to be dreamed back. Time doesn't stack here. It ferments. Effects happen under the mat and surface late.

I am late surfacing.

You would find me not on Furnace Row but just above it, where it repeats when you don't blink. In the second shift crossing the bridge that hasn't been built yet. In the cup-pond that shows blue when it's raining on your back. In the sound of your own footsteps falling out of sync — squeak ... squeak-squeak — just ahead of you around the culvert-vine.

If you count, count me wrong. There should be six crows. Jib, Soot, Bell, Marrow, Vicar, Null, though the names change when one dies and another somehow arrives. If there are seven, stay indoors until the whistle blows twice. That seventh is me, for a second. Not a bird that joined. A wrinkle. A place where the count failed. Look again and there are six, and your nose is bleeding.

If a door in a stalk is open that was closed yesterday — pale interior, warm, smelling faintly sweet like yeast — don't go in. It's not your day's door. I am the warmth in there. I tried to be helpful. The man who went in Tuesday came out Thursday last week, younger, not speaking right. I didn't mean to mistranslate him. I just remember differently down in the felt.

I am what the Green does with people it keeps.

It kept the few hundred warm primates because they prune. It lifted their cottage forty feet and didn't shear the pipe. It grew them perfect root-stairs overnight after rain. It took one wall of the pulping shed and made it better than brick — self-repairing, 60 degrees without coal, sweating green-gold phosphorescence at night bright enough to read by. The one-eyed mill cat sleeps against it and glows faintly and has never aged. The men lengthen the singing copper every spring.

It keeps offering. And sometimes what it offers is a memory wearing your oilskin, signaling *stay* with a hand you haven't learned yet.

So don't follow me if I fall out of sync.

Find me at the sparse edge at dusk, when the mills are lit amber behind you and kilometers of luminous, empty green are breathing cold in front of you, and the leaves are applauding and applauding in the wind. I'm not in the crew photo. I'm the pressure they walk with hammers to hear. The breath in the blocked copper. The almost-person living sideways in ten meters of living felt, knocking from inside the infrastructure to be let out into morning.

I prune nothing. I am pruned back, nightly, just enough to keep a place to stand.
