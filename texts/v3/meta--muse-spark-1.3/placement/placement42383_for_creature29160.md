At first I look for myself up high, because that is where I am used to looking.

On the rim, with the thin grass hissing. In the bright column where the swifts lock their wings and go round without beating. I think, I could be that — held up without effort, seeing the whole bowl at once.

But I can't. I am too heavy for that kind of suspension. I would have to flap, I would have to scream like a needle on glass and be gone. I don't live elsewhere and hunt here. I live here.

So I go down.

Past the first shelves of cream and honey stone, where the light gets brighter instead of dimmer, thrown back at itself. My skin — if I had skin — feels yesterday's afternoon coming back off the rock against my calves.

I think I could be the crow. I want to be the crow. Wary, iridescent, clever enough to hammer and fish. To know the stations. To decide the stranger is a kind of weather and go to work anyway.

But the crow does not stay. She drops down, she picks up, she wipes her beak and leaves. She roosts out on the plain somewhere. She lives *by* the hollow the way you live by a well. I don't leave. I have nowhere else to roost.

Lower, then. Two-thirds of the way down where the limestone darkens to umber.

I try the lizard for a while. Flat on the sun-slab, belly to stored warmth. Negotiating, an inch forward into sun, an inch back into the thin line of shade. Throat pulsing like a small heart. That patience feels closer. That measuring of life in sun-hours.

But the lizard owns. The large male owns the biggest slab. The honey-colored female with the regrown tail owns hers. Even the juvenile no longer than a thumb owns his dashes between cracks. And he husks, he licks dew off the underside of a ledge at dawn. He wants. I don't want in that bright-beaded way.

I go lower and slower, kneel down.

The ant river, black and unhurried along the seam, touching antennae, turning aside for a grain of sand as if it were a boulder. I could dissolve there. Be one of many, farming lichen, dismantling a dead bee joint by joint over the course of a day. But I am not that numerous. I do not meander like water finding its level.

Under the stone where it is still cool to the touch — lift gently — the pillbugs sit rolled tight as seeds in the damp hollow they've polished with their bodies. Breathing with gills that should not work here, and working because the air still remembers the night. Waiting for you to put the roof back.

Closer. That waiting. That improbable breathing.

Or the moss, no bigger than my thumb, green felt in the deepest shade that dries to brown paper by noon and swells green again by next dawn without dying. Or the snail in the north-facing crease, chalk-white to throw back the sun, no bigger than a lentil, spending a whole life on a square foot of damp stone, grazing the black lichens at a pace that makes the fig look hasty.

I stay with the snail a long time. I like his smallness. The way his shells end up around the anvil, all broken at the same whorl.

And that is when I see it. Really see it.

In the lee of the largest fallen block. The flat-topped stone, knee-high, its top pitted with small round dents from years of striking. Around it the empty shells. The neat pile of spiny pods husked by lizards a few feet away. Ant columns diverging.

And perched on the edge: the twig.

Stripped, one end frayed by a beak. Set down neatly for tomorrow.

I am not the crow. I am what the crow leaves behind.

Find me there. Not basking, not circling, not marching. Just lying where I was left, in the thin dark line under the overhang as the afternoon cools. The stone beneath me still holds warmth. I can feel it seeping up. The air cools by a degree, then another. The damp seam darkens again as condensation begins, invisible at first. The ringing slabs tick as they cool.

In the morning she will come down without sound. She will pick me up in her beak — the familiar grip, the worn place where I fit — and insert me with a surgeon's delicacy into a crevice I cannot see into, to fish up something pale and wriggling. For a minute I will be held, directed, useful. An extension of her wanting. Then she will wipe her beak on the stone and leave me again on the edge.

That is my life. Borrowed for a moment and set down again. Shaped, not building. Used daily, tidied by wind. Dust settling on me by evening.

I do not grow like the fig, a finger-length a year, roots flattened to white ropes crawling ten feet to the next weep. I do not persist like the lacquered shrub clicking faintly in the updraft. I wait.

And waiting here is not empty. From the edge of the anvil I can see it all at once in the honey light when the western wall throws its tongue of shade across the floor: lizard throat pulsing, ant river flowing, bee dusted gold in a fig flower for a whole afternoon, moss darkening as the beads form again on the underside of ledges and shrink backward into the rock.

Vast intervals between. Yards of bare warm stone between each green island. Sound falling through and disappearing. A drip hanging for an hour under the amber calcite drapery, never falling — ten thousand almost-falls to make a millimeter.

I lie there among them, frayed end drying, and bead with the rest.
