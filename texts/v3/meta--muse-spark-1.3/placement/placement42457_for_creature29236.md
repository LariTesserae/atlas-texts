At first I thought I would stay up top.

Not from fear of the dark. From love of the flat. I walked for three days across that high country and learned how to be licked clean by wind. Rust-red and ash-grey, molar-round, dust in every shallow. No water to follow, so you follow your own breath going out white and then gone. I thought: I could be one of the rim people. A looker. Someone who knows where the slot is and steps over it and keeps walking.

Then the ground gave a little under my boot, warm air came up smelling of feathers and ammonia and something green, and the wind went quiet in a circle no wider than a doorway. I lay on my belly to look and could not see to the bottom, only dust turning slow in a column of light.

I went down feet-first because there is no other way. Cord knotted and rungs of bird-bone polished black by ten thousand hands. Seven body-lengths. My shoulders scraped soft rock. And then my feet found nothing, and then dung-brick, and the heat came up through my sandals, and the sound closed over my head like water.

I coughed. That is how you know you are a visitor.

The Hollow-born breathe without thinking air that made me weep in ten minutes. Warm, feathered with down and moth-scale and fine ash, sharp underneath. I stood in the middle with my arms half out and tried to be still and my chest heaved like a bellows. A child stared at me, barrel-chested even at five, breathing shallow and easy, tea-colored whites pink at the corners, hair braided tight and capped. She did not laugh. She just waited for the fit to pass the way you wait for a swift to find its toehold.

For a long time I did not know where to stand. There is nowhere quiet to stand.

I thought, maybe, I would be an eye-cleaner. That sounded like me — lean, hungry to see. Every season the fourteen-year-olds go feet-first up that ladder into blinding open sky and scrape the rim with bone so the light and the birds can still come through. I climbed halfway up once to watch them go and the light hit my face like a slap and my eyes ran for an hour. Down in the amber dusk they see everything, thread bone needles by rat-fat lamplight, find a dropped needle in dust. Up there I was blind. When I came back down, blinking and sunburnt on one cheek, the old women nodded like I had finally grown up enough to know I wasn't chosen.

I thought, maybe, I would be swift. Who wouldn't? To be weather instead of a person. Soot-black backs, white throats, bodies no longer than a hand, packed shoulder to shoulder in the upper dark in a living crust that twitches and chitters *tseet-tseet* in your teeth. At dawn the ribbon goes — three, then thirty, then a roaring corkscrew up and out the eye to hunt high-air plankton fifty miles out — and the whole town sways on its cords and everyone grabs a strut without looking. I wanted that lightness, that certainty of return with crops swollen with midges, dropping like stones and flaring at the last second. But I am too heavy. My takeoff would pull the platform down. I sleep too loud. I would reach.

I thought I would be owl. Silent, pale, fat as a cat, heart-faced, blind in the day column and absolute at noon when the crust sleeps and the town goes whisper-quiet and you can hear your own heart and then someone else's baby breathing. I loved them the first evening I saw one slide across the light shaft without a sound after all that chitter, while feathers snowed down through the honey-gold and moth-clouds rose to meet the returning flood. But I am not silent. My feet find every loose wattle. The children whisper to owls; owls look through me.

So I sank. That is what you do here if you stay. You find your ledge by weight.

You would find me now not on the floor — the floor is for threshing and drying and the narrow dun goats that never leave — and not in the top ring where the swift-shuffle rains mites all day. Low on the second ring, on the shadow side where the column never quite reaches, where shelf-fungus stacks liver-and-cream and the beard-plant hangs three feet long and sways though there is no wind. My home is one room, front open to the warm dark, hung with drying strips of fungus and braided onions of air-plant bulbs and three little cages of thick-bodied crickets clicking all night. Rope bridge to the left sags; ladder to the right hums when a flight goes out. An owl owns the edge of my platform the way a strict landlord owns a stair. I leave her the warm entrails on a flat stone and never name her loudly. At noon when she opens her eyes, I am forgiven.

I am what the Hollow makes of a woman in her thirties, which is not young here. Barrel-chested. Hollow-throat soft from the first winter — I kept it soft. Hair black going rust-grey early, braided tight to the skull and stuffed up into beaten fiber because loose hair becomes a nest in a day. Soot-grease smudged round my eyes to cut the glare when I have to cross the column. Skin damp-pale under the powder that never shakes off, mouth and nose chapped despite the warmth, heels split like old leather, knuckles swollen. Hands rope-wise. Before I could stop coughing I could tie the six giving-knots that hold wattle to soft mealy rock and flex when ten thousand lift at once. My thumb is starting that sideways bend from twisting cord. I wear dust-olive vest and wrap-trousers patched with down-felt — swift-down swept, carded, pressed with spit and fat — and sandals with fungus-leather soles because I need to feel the sway. One thin beaten copper bead at the ear. Grandmother's, though no blood grandmother of mine ever saw this place.

My work is morning work. I am out before the column sharpens, down the swaying ladder barefoot, toes curled for grip, basket on my hip. Take only the fallen. Never reach into a live nest. I walk the trampled oval of guano ash and dropped feathers while the crust is still and the harvestmen — blind-white, legs as long as my hand — are picking daintily over the moss, cleaning up scale, and the rock-rats are still bold. I gather quickly, reverently, before the rats get them: the cold chicks, the failed flyers, the old dead. A dead sooty is soup, is a strip of smoked breast, is a thimble of fat, is feathers for felt and bone for awls. I know each body warm as a coal gone out. I do not pray. That would be presumptuous. I make room.

After, I mend. I sit in my doorway like the half-deaf old women — I am already losing the high chitter after only a few years, a mercy, hearing only human voices and the low huff of owls — and mend net and cord by touch and tell the tethered toddlers which bridge will sag before rain that never comes. At noon I go still with all species together. Even the rats move softly. At dusk I wring the beard-plant curtains at dawn into cloths and suck the cloths. I nip the fat waxy rosettes for their sour stored juice. I sip thin liver-colored fungus broth from a bone cup. I share goat milk by the mouthful — precious, life-saving water combed from air by plants and passed through a goat. I trap rats on the floor in bone snap-traps, fatten them in wicker on fungus parings for night stew, and never admit to feeding them.

At night, after the three hooded hearth-pits smoke with dung-cakes — no fire in the day when the air is thick with down and would catch — and the rat-fat lamps bloom like dim stars on every ledge, and the evening flood has drummed the bridges and settled, I sing low. Not to be heard, but to cover the birds settling, to make a second colony sound underneath the first.

Outside, a mile in any direction, nothing but old wind and flat stone and cold stars.

Inside, I am powdered white, itching, smelling of sour milk and smoke and warm feathers, stone-warm through my sandals from the composting depth below. I am not master here. I am third-most numerous at best. Just the ledge that learned to tie knots, and to breathe shallow, and to gather only what has already fallen.
