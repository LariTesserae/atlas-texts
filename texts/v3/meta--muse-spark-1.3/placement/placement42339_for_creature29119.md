At first I still think I am myself when I come over the lip. Boots. Breath staying. Knees going through.

I crouch because there is no other way to read it. The willow vaults are only shoulder-high and the mist sits low in the runnels and my breath joins it. Tick, tick — water off rock, water off leaf. My footprint fills before I lift my foot.

I think, if I lived here, I would be something that passes through. The ptarmigan. One white stitch across a roof in winter, gone by melt. But I am too heavy for that. I sink and sigh water up. I stay too long and get cold in the jaw. The place forgets quick things.

So I get lower.

If I stayed at animal speed I could be the beetle. Black and pitted, the size of a seed. It would take me all afternoon to cross one roof and I could afford to, hunting springtails in slow motion. Or I could be amber, armored, a mite oozing hair by hair along a moss stem, grazing. Or grey-white, long as an eyelash, gathered with the others in the damp axil where water never quite leaves — a springtail, waiting for a breath to startle me into that soft salt-spatter pop.

But those are still tenants. They rent. The cold stiffens their joints. They move, just very slowly.

I stay until I stop fidgeting. Until my fingers are numb enough to part the moss gently. Until I can see at the speed the place moves.

Under the dome-cushion, in the green-black thatch where light has given up, a bead of water cupped between two leaves. In it — if I am small enough, if I am patient enough — rotifers opening like flowers. Nematodes swimming slow figures-of-eight. And hauling its fat, clawed body over what to it are redwoods, a water bear. A tardigrade. Stopping. Tucking. Becoming almost stone when the needle ice comes, and then, in the pallid thaw, un-becoming.

For a while I think I am that. The bear in the droplet. Carried downhill when the droplet falls with its soft tick into the runnel, streetcarred toward the marsh-eye. That would be a good life — to be carried by water where water decides.

But longer still, colder still, and I sink one layer further down.

I find myself in middle town, where a willow tunnel bends downhill toward wetter. On the left, a hut-dome, perfect and springy, crowned with cushion pulled taut as roof-skin. Its downhill side weeps into a living gutter — a liverwort bank, dark as liver, slick and astonishingly strong, hollowed where meltwater wanted a way. Above me, willow stems bent low and grafted by wind into an arch, tiny varnished leaves overlapping like shingles, beading and dripping. Frost feathers growing out of the living wall. Needle ice lifting the whole street a half-inch in silver combs.

I am not in the house. I am the house.

I am one shoot among a thousand packed shoulder to shoulder in that dome, on its wetter side just above the gutter.

My tip is red as an ember in the blue dusk because I am still growing, even now, even in cold that stops fingers. Below the red I am gold, below gold green-black, below that brown. Below that, my own dead ancestors, preserved by acid and cold — grandmothers a thousand years deep — holding me a finger's width above drowning. I stand on them. We all do.

I have no roots. I drink with my whole body, twenty times my weight, and in drinking I make the bog more boggy, more acid, more itself. To live here is to make the place you live wetter. The liverwort beside me seals my leaks. The willow elder above me — thick as a thumb, older than the boots that stepped here, lying down instead of standing up because that is wisdom — creaks and adjusts to snow weight in its sleep, and its shade is my light, filtered to soup.

Under me, through the meter of living and dead mat, white thread finer than hair splices me to willow root to saxifrage: mycorrhizae tasting. When I am too wet and begin to drown, I leak my sugars differently. The mat tastes it. Next thaw we will shift growth a millimeter this way or that. An alley will narrow. A doorway crawled through yesterday will shut by a fresh shoot, and two feet over, where drainage runs faster, a new soft place will open. No council. No plan. Just every one of us inching toward a little less wet or a little more light, and water deciding which inch survives.

This is the pulse I mistook for stillness: by night needle ice grows up through us and lifts us, cracking with that sharp *pink*. It breaks hairs. It pries stems apart. It hurts. By pallid day we slump and sigh and knit again. That lift and slump is how we climb. That crack and knit is how we heal. Death is not removed. There is no away. The lower leaves brown and sink and become foundation.

On my leaf, in the bead I am holding up to the dim, the bear is hauling. The mite is grazing further down the stem. The mist that is our breathing hangs over us and does not lift. Above the ridge, wind passes and does not come down.

I do not move. I insist.
