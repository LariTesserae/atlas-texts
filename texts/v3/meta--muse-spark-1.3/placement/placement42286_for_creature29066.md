You come over the rim and drop into it all at once, and for a while you don't know where to put yourself.

At first I think I'll be high. That's where the eye wants to go — up the ladder, up the twin-pole bridge trembling under moss, up to a loft in the crotch where I can see out over the lip to the lowlands in haze. But there is no out. The crown is knitted shut. The mist gathers and then the leaves tip and pour and I get a cold shower down the neck for my trouble. My legs shake on the slick poles. The children cross low and quick, hands light on the side-vines, and they look back at me with open amusement. I'm too tall for this. I duck every three steps. Low branches comb leaves into my hair. A red squirrel runs the rope over my knuckles like I'm furniture that is slow.

So no, not high. Not yet.

Then I think I'll be inside. A guest by a hearth, under a trained-leaf eave while smoke tangles blue-grey overhead and smells of wet wood and fat. But there is no inside here the way I mean it. The walls leaf out. The floor moves when someone three platforms over rolls in sleep. I hear the neighbor piss in the morning trough, hear the baby next platform dreaming, hear the argument about kindling and the making-up after. I cough — everyone coughs, that low wet crown-cough — and the smoke doesn't leave. My cloak never dries. I hang it on the peg with the forty other cloaks under the eave and wring it first the way the old woman shows me without words, twist and twist, and still it steams faintly and weighs like an animal.

I try to be useful and get in the way. I try to help the two boys haul the dripping pole and shout *mind! mind!* and everyone leans out of the way without breaking conversation except me, who gets shouldered into a buttress root carved into stairs, dished and slick. I stand by the flat stone where the mother guts minnows, tiny black torpedoes from the outflow pool, and the cats and the jays look at me at exactly the respectful, vibrating distance, and I realize I am standing where the heads get flicked. I step back onto a chicken. It does not move. You step over, not on. I apologize. No one else apologizes to chickens.

For a day I think maybe I'm not a person here at all. Maybe I'm meat that walks. A leech finds the soft behind my ankle with a practiced inch and I flick it off with my thumb and curse, and I'm absurdly proud I did it right. Midges fill the light shafts. A frog the size of my thumb sits in the washing rill and watches my hands while I try to wash around the tadpoles and fail, clouding the water. In the afternoon the grey troop comes down to work the upper village, ten or twelve strong, leaping roof-leaf to roof-leaf and shaking down showers, and a young male goes through my pack left a second too long and takes the cold bean cake I was saving. The dogs go mad. The children shriek with delight and throw sticks badly on purpose. The old female with the torn ear sits on the ridge not six feet from the bean-shelling grandmother and grooms herself, and the grandmother shells and scolds in parallel, and I understand, suddenly, enviously, that tribute of cress tops left on the far roof-leaf. *Stay, steal a little, be neighbor.*

I keep sinking.

My boots are wet in three steps and stay wet. On the second morning I leave them under cover and take the soft bark sandals for the duckboards, and then take those off too to climb, and feel my toes try to learn to grip. My feet are narrow, lowland feet. They splay a little by evening. Everything about me is a little more waterlogged — hair lank no matter how I tie it, sleeves damp up to the elbow, knuckles split and then healed over with fat and pitch the pitch-woman dabs on me with a grunt. Nakedness underneath stops meaning much when you bathe in the rills together and pull a cloak over your head when you want to not be commented on.

I start to listen for work instead of looking for a view.

*tok tok tok* — the stone-maul man with one milky eye splitting pine that will never be quite dry enough, because dry is wealth here, true currency, three seasons kept under leaf-roof traded handful by handful for fat white grubs from a deadfall. The old man on his back under the vine bridge with his mouth full of nettle cord, re-lashing while traffic steps over his hands and the goat on the platform above tries to eat his beard. And the girl — she stays with me — mending living wall not by patching but by planting, weaving a green hazel withy in and out of the wattle, tying it with bark strip, bending the tip to where she wants next spring to go.

You do not cut a pillar here. Everyone tells me that, child to stranger, as a fact like water runs downhill. At first I hear it as law. Then I see it as carpentry — stone adze, bone awl, flax and pitch, the whole lashed-on village hanging from those ancients. Then I hear the way they talk to the big trunks. *Ah, she's weeping again, give her another channel. He's dropped a limb on my roof-leaf, I'll have to train a new one over.* Grumbling, affectionate, familiar. Landlords.

I find myself, in the end, low. Not in the crown, not by the hearth. At true ground level where I belong with my bad balance and my long back that is good for bending.

You'd find me on the duckboards beside the main rill where the three paths meet over the interlaced roots, where the ground thrums faintly with water underneath and the whole bowl breathes — inhale: mist gathers, leaves fill and tip; exhale: drip, run, gurgle down to the taro terraces and over the lip. Knees wet. Hands in black muck and cold seep. With my sod-knife clearing leaves where the water hesitates, lifting a sod to divert to the kitchens, putting it back to send it to the washing place, then to the gardens where cress and bitter highland beans grow. Tadpoles scatter and regroup around my wrists. Frogs keep their high metallic trill that never stops. The pigeon boy whistles through his teeth overhead. The bees drone by the log hives. Someone fulls cloth on a root — *thwack thwack* — someone splits kindling — *tok tok tok* — the red-capped child forbidden to climb the dead snag climbs it anyway while his mother yips three short yips like a jay.

That is where I settle, because nothing stays still and so neither do I. A mending becomes a meal. A clearing becomes a trade. You help haul because tomorrow you will need hauling. I am not good at climbing, not good at weaving yet — green still won't stay under my nails, it scrubs out — but I can watch water. I can learn where it wants to go and give it another channel. I can talk, under my breath, to the trunk that holds up my section of duckboard, the one I notched nothing into, moved my step around.

You'd find me there at high mountain noon when the light comes down in pale shafts and catches the mist and the midges and the drip-sparkle. Damp to the bone. Coughing and continuing. Stepping over chickens. Making room.

Not conquering this place. Riding it, lashed on.
