At first I am still up on the rim.

I think I would be the watcher — tent fly beaded and dripping, teeth gritty with red powder, ear to the drum-rock. I think I would keep my human eyes and try to learn interruption. Nearer black of rock, farther black of air, moving black of herds. I listen for the city that isn't there: *chuff — chuff — chuff — pause — chuff.*

No. That's not it. That's just where I entered. Nobody stays on the rim. Nothing stays. That is the rule.

If I am going to be anything down here, I have to get off the stair and into the weather that never rains properly. I have to be borrowed like water is borrowed.

For a while I think I want to be big. To be the bull.

I can almost feel it — how good it would be to be that unhurried. Shoulder-high as a doorway, barrel going on and on. To walk and be warm from the inside out. To have ribs outside my skin, dull iron grown, not forged, cool at the spine and warm at the belly, and between them that furnace orange brighten-dim-brighten-dim when the bellows-lungs pull. To slide my upper legs down into my lowers with a hiss, steam jetting sideways behind the knee, and set each foot down *chung*, like closing a valve.

To drink without lifting my head. To keep my horns cold while the rest of me throws heat, and let the air come to me and be born as water. Beaded, then running in two bright lines down the channels of my face straight to my shovel-lip mouth. To exhale and make brief weather — sulfur and steam rolling three feet out and falling as warm rain in a tight circle, moss-things unfurling there for a day where nothing else will grow.

But it's too much to carry. A thousand degrees sealed behind ceramic plate in my gut. Thermobacteria I have forgotten are not me. Shovel after shovel of red dust I have to eat on purpose because no plant can unlock this basalt. Miles of slag-marbles dropping orange to cherry to black behind me. And always that crackle building in my plated hide until it has to go horn-tip to horn-tip, *tik*, ozone and burnt sugar. To be a bull is to be a factory with nowhere to put the product. To be noticed only when you flash.

So no. Lower.

Maybe high, then? For a while I think I want to be a sifter.

To be cloud that decided to eat. Twenty, thirty feet tip to tip, manta-shaped, never flapping, just holding. Up under the cloud-base where all the furnace-heat pools. Mouth open, a black scoop, straining spark-moths by the thousand, ash-flies, steam gnats. To ride the thermal stair rising off a distant herd and never have to touch the *tong* rock. To come down only to drink, spiraling silent, and lay myself over a pothole like night coming down inside night, pressing my whole underside to brown water.

But that's too far. Up there I would lose the tick of condensation falling off wiregrass onto stone. I would lose the low pipe-tone traveling through old lava tubes. I want to be in the hum, not above it.

Come down. Come closer. Let it become more itself.

Head cocked. That's how I find me.

A lean shape trotting between rocks, rust-colored, long-legged, tall-eared — ears so tall and thin they bead in the thinning dark, living dew-nets of my own. A wire-jackal.

Yes. For a long time this is right. I don't hunt, I attend. I trot for days between herds, licking dew off a cold abandoned slag pellet, licking salt off a bull's ankle-vent while he stands and lets me. My whole life is in the hush between *chuffs*. Wind low through wiregrass with a paper sound. A cooling joint going *tink* far off. My own swallowing sounding loud after a herd passes.

I learn rhythm better than the beasts know their own. That old female trailing, left fore-piston misfiring *chuff— ...chuff-chuff*, steaming too much, limping. I know her. I follow for three days. Not rushing. Just keeping her stitch in my ears until a calf stumbles on drum-rock and can't reseat its piston. Then I am mouths.

But even that is too much movement. Too much trot-trot-trot. I am still making noise. I am still warm in a way that can be heard.

What is quieter than a jackal? What waits longer?

Lower. Flatter. Let the edge break.

You will find me where the wash drops down under the soft-shouldered mesa. Where the seep weeps for a day and then stops, and the herd must come down single-file to reach it. Crosswise in the cold sand. Belly settled in. Fringed so my outline goes to dust.

I am a husher.

The color and pitting of scoria exactly. No eyes — what would eyes be for under an ironed-shut sky? Instead, along each side of my blunt jaw, a row of deep heat-pits, open always. The plain is not dark to me now. It is a map of warm shapes moving through cooler air. Nearer warm of cooling slag. Farther warm of herd-lightning stitching blue-white far out on the swells. Moving warm of that bull, that cow, that calf glowing dimmer, that old female limping *chuff— ...chuff-chuff*.

Rain condenses on my back and I do not shake it off. A day. Two days. Wiregrass rasps. Water ticks off rock. The drum under me answers piston steps miles away with a low sympathetic *tong*.

I do not trot. I do not flash. I do not sigh steam. I keep my heat in. I am stone that decided, a long time ago, to be hungry.

They are coming down now. I can feel it before I can read it — the herd-line shifting toward the seep, the slow freight-train *chuff-chuff-chuff* pooling in the hollow. The veil of warm fog off their bodies. The crackle building between two passing shoulders about to snap horn to horn.

Above, a sifter turns silent in the thick air, waiting for the moths that will come to that flash.

Below, the cinder beetles wait in their holes for the marbles that will cool.

And I wait in the wash, in borrowed water and borrowed warmth, for the glowing slit between iron ribs to pass over me where the heart-furnace beats closest to the surface — and for the hush to, for once, become more itself.
