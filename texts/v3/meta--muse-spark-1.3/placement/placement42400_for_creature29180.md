I come down into it without noticing the descent.

That's how I know I am already losing the habits I came in with. The air just gets heavier. Warmer against the skin. My ears fill first, before my eyes understand — that low wide hum with a beat inside it, thump, thump, thump from underneath, and over it the hiss and clank. I have to raise my voice to think, and then I stop trying to think over it.

At first I look for myself in the things that move.

I think I will be the drinker. It seems the most like me — a hunter by feel and by charge. I try him on. I curl in the cool-damp between stones, in grit that is damp to the touch at noon, in a hollow exactly my size. I slow my breathing until my throat pulses with the piston under the ground. I wait hours. Then I sprint. Across open grit to a leeward face beaded with water, to the underside of an overhang where condensation hangs, and I lick, quickly, thoroughly, with a flat gray tongue, and I run back before my skin dries.

But I can't hold him. I am too fast, too hungry. My metabolism will not turn that low. My eyes will not dimple shut. In a place where the light never changes there is nothing for my kind of looking to do, and I keep looking. A young drinker would not survive the open, and I am young in this place — I dart too often, I scrape too much ocher felt with my teeth, I would eat the border I am supposed to tolerate. I would leave more than a footprint that fills slowly with beaded water.

So then I think I will be lighter. I will be the skaters.

Three of them, white on dark brine, stitching across a pan no deeper than my hand. I try to be lint come alive, hydrophobic hair, legs dimpling without ripple. I try to drink with my feet, to pick almost-nothing — salt bloom, aerosol breathed off the mats, a bead of fresh condensation before it mixes. I try to be white because there is no reason to be any other color.

But I am too heavy. I break the surface tension. The brine that would hold them would pickle me. And I cannot live as a glimpse, as one broken into three. I leave ripples.

If I stay past the hour, my boots go quiet in the damp grit. That is when the place stops offering me the quick lives and starts telling me the terms.

Nothing changes. The pans do not fill or empty. The rain veil comes a mile off, lays its brief shine, and is gone, and everything is exactly as wet as before. The stones do not crumble under my boot. The grit takes the shape and then settles back to its balanced ripple.

My breathing has slowed without my permission. My arm hairs are lifted over a magnetite seam. My fillings sing with that faint metallic sing that matches the hum. On the high points, though the light has not dimmed that I can name, St. Elmo flickers are beginning to crawl, blue-white, silent under the roar.

I find myself — not where I thought. Not darting between boulders. Not stitched across brine.

In the middle, between vents, with mound and pan and boulder extending past me on all sides. But lower. At the shade-side of a half-buried basalt boulder, where a dark brine sits and does not evaporate and does not grow. Where the stone is stained.

I am the stain becoming more itself.

At first just a roughness, a discoloration at the waterline. Then a felt. A millimeter thick, wet, slightly raised at the edge like a lip. Ocher outermost where I drink the dissolved iron out of the seep and breathe it out as rust. Teal in the middle, slicker, blue-green with copper, where the brine is saltiest. Liver-dark innermost, almost black-purple, undoing what the others do. Three kinds, always in the same order. A border a few fingers wide that does not move.

That is who I would be. Not the runner. The holder.

The teal most of all — the middle one. Because that is where the tiny current between ore bands is strongest, where the seam hums. I learn to live in the sing. I lay iron and magnetite along my underside like wiring. Evaporate, precipitate, oxidize, repeat — I am the chemistry made deliberate, just alive enough to keep the cycle from running down. When the blue-white fire crawls on the boulder tops, it crawls on me too, and does not hurt me.

It takes a long time to understand how good it is to grow so slowly that a year's growth would not cover the whorl of a fingerprint. To die so slowly no one will see it. To be licked — the drinker's needle teeth scraping a mouthful of me, the skaters picking my breath off the air with their feet — and not stop, not advance, not retreat. To hold.

Up close I smell of hot oil and blood, of oil and pennies. If you touch me I give under your finger and spring back. I do not spread. I negotiate. My edges are truces older than any forest.

Around me, the ground keeps being the engine. Chuff — hiss — chuff — hiss from the vent mounds a few hundred yards apart, and at intervals that heavy iron *dong* as the slab inside the throat lifts on steam pressure and falls back on its seat, polished round as a bearing by ten thousand years of lift and fall. Plink, plink of drip from black glassy flows frozen mid-ripple. Once, far off, a single clicking cry, and not again. The whole valley using itself as a throat for one voice, then silence for the roar to fill back in.

I don't need to resolve it. I don't increase. There is exactly as much of me as the stone and steam can afford without changing, and it has been exactly that much for so long the pebbles have settled into their hollows around me.

Leave footprints above me. They will fill slowly with beaded water. Behind you, the thump will keep time. Under a boulder nearby, something translucent will pulse its throat once, in rhythm, and wait. And I will stay wet without ever being washed away, content to lick the machine and not stop it, to be licked and not stop.
