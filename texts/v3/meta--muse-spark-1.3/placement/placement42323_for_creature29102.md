At first I am still standing. That's the problem.

I come down into it the way you do, boots finding the split planks, too heavy, too loud. The algae takes my weight and gives it back as slick. I catch a post to steady myself and the post sweats onto my palm. I am fog-breath and sleeve-damp and creaking joints, the only large mammal in a room that has learned how to do without me. I take up too much of the hollow air.

I think, I could be one of the tall ones. That's the first image and it's wrong, but I try it on.

Up on the wall with the three grey waiters. Tuck my neck, fold myself down to nothing but eyes. Learn to stand longer than seems possible while the frogs go on pulsing underneath me. I want their patience. I don't have it. My breath fogs. My weight shifts. The stone under me is dished by generations of rumps and I can feel every one of them telling me to get off, you fidget, you scare the gutter. A heron strikes — splash, a croak cut in half — and I flinch. They don't flinch. So I'm not that.

Then I think lower. If I can't wait, maybe I can call.

I crouch where the planks give out to tamped clay and crushed shell, where the palm-sized cups each hold their black mirror. There's one empty, or empty enough. I fold down until my knees are in the wet and my face is close to the water. Jug-o-rum, jug-o-rum through the soles of my boots, and the high metallic peep right through my teeth. A cool brown back surfaces, throat swelling, eyes filmed. He hops over my fingers like I'm just another cobble set on edge. For a minute I can be that low, that owned by a cup. Cold belly. Everything I hatch, I eat. Everything that waits, eats me. It is a clean bargain. But my knees ache. My breath makes rings on his water. I am still too much weather around too small a cup.

I try, briefly, to be air.

Up under the beams where the whirring seam pours out through the oculus and back in again. Shoulder to shoulder in a mud-rimmed hole unpicked from the thatch, dark heads tucking and untucking, chitter never stopping, only changing pressure. Mites in my feather-dust eating my sloughed skin. Droppings rimming my door. Yes, my mind knows that rustle. That bicker and shift and never still. But my body doesn't. I can't wedge. I can't burst upward a dozen at a time and tick the drip-spray with my wings. The bats folded like wet leaves between the swallows watch me try and keep sweating together.

So I go back down. That's the rule here. Everything condenses, seeps, is channeled toward the center.

The spiral gutters run finger-deep past my boots toward the sump. Eels wrist-thick nosing the stone, moving without seeming to move. A soft suck against rock. I lay my hand flat in the film and feel one pass under, black-olive muscle, cold as the posts. I could be that — a long hunger sliding toward tadpoles and back out to the dark. But I am jointed wrong. I have knees and elbows that catch.

And then I am at the center because there is nowhere else to go in a bowl. Forty paces and you're there.

The sump. Waist-deep, stone-lined, hand-pecked, always full to the lip. The single slanted column out of the oculus lands there, full of midges and down-feathers and drip-spray, and the black water trembles. Commas of black glass scatter from my shadow and regroup. Tadpoles. And below them, slower, hanging in the water column and rising toward the light —

That's where I find myself. Not standing anymore. Kneeling on the darkest, slickest planks with my sleeves soaked through, forearms in to the wrist, breath held so I don't fog the surface. If I stay long enough the water forgets the splash of my hands and the trembling resumes around my skin.

I would be one of the unborn.

An orange-bellied newt, slow-limbed, hanging. Not waiting like a heron waits, neck cocked to strike. Not calling like a frog calls, throat going all the time. Just suspended, gilling the rich water, feeding on algae and drowned midges that fall out of the light. Rising a little when the column brightens, sinking when a heron's shadow crosses. At night clicking faintly to whatever else hangs there with me.

It becomes more itself the longer I stay: the pebbled back, the fringe of gills, the way my feet don't really swim so much as hold the water. The way I am held — water in a cup in a cup in a cup. Sump in pavement in bowl in marsh in fog. That is how they live here. Not the way people live in a house. The way water lives in a cup, taking its shape, trembling when it's touched, going still again.

Let the bats stitch the air above me. Let the withy tap and tap time against wood. Let the posts creak tighter with the cool. Down here the loudness comes muffled, jug-o-rum felt more than heard through stone. A feather lands on the surface and I rise to nose it. A tadpole flicks past my eye.

I don't need the whole roof. I don't need the wall. Just this lip of stone to hold the black, and the light to fall through drip-spray, and enough stillness to be small in.
