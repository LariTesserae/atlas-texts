I thought at first I would be the one on my belly.

Coming in through the crack, dripping. Too wet and too fast to belong. The kind of living that leaves noise. I imagined myself crouched in the bottle-wort, blinking in the ember-dark, counting my own breaths until they dried.

But I can't stay that. Nothing that wet can stay. My shadow is cool weather to this place, a wrongness. The leaves close their unseen mouths when I pass. The floor feels the cold of me and waits me out. I would have to back out on my belly to leave, and I don't want to leave.

So lower.

If not the visitor, then a leaf? For a while I want that. To be opaque and swollen, black-green to amber, lacquered an inch thick. To hold stored heat at my core until I glow dull orange in the perpetual dusk. To tick and pop as I expand. To throw no shadow. But leaves don't go elsewhere. They root in dust and wait a thousand years to be right. I am not patient enough yet to be a leaf.

If not a leaf, then a house? A hand-patted mud-brick with fingerprints still in it. A doorway worn into a shallow bowl by small dry feet that left and did not return. That appeals to me, to be shade that is used as trellis. But houses don't tend. They are tended.

Lower still. Below the waist-high canopy. Below seeing.

At ankle level.

That's where I find myself. Not looking at them, but among them.

I am loaf-sized. No — let that become more itself. Mortar-bowl-sized, a dome. My back is overlapping scales of wax, black-green to amber, gone dull at the crown where the core-knot has cycled too much heat through itself. My silica bristles clink faintly when the breathing draft comes through the slit. Underneath, the felt fringe that drags.

I have no head. No tail. No face to find. No wet eye, because there is nothing wet in me to make an eye from. I don't see the blades of glare from the roof-holes shift an inch. I feel the heat go thin and then thick again across my back, and that is seeing.

I was budded, I remember — if remembering is the word for what the felt keeps — under the largest bottle-wort where the heat is steadiest. A swelling in the white-hot thread-fine root-felt under the street, hardening, waxing, bristling. Then pinched off with a soft *tak!* no louder than a bursting spore-pod. For years I have wandered. That is the word you would use. I would not.

What I do is maintenance. Nothing leaves, nothing needs — but everything settles, and settling must be tended.

You would find me halfway down the main street, on the shadow side where the ash-flag gets too ambitious. I have been leaning there for days. There is a frond that wants to enter the packed earth, to close the way. I press my weight and my small furnace-heat against its joint until the wax softens and it bends back over the edge. It leaves a crescent burn, which I seal instantly with hot oil exhaled from my fringe. That is pruning. No mouth. Only weight and heat.

Last season I was a retaining stone. Parked against the foot of the terrace wall where the dust-drift presses. I rooted temporarily. My fringe hardened into guy-lines. I held until the seedlings on the other side took the load. Then I loosened. You can still see the scars on my left scales where I tore free and resealed.

This week I am sweeping. It takes a week to cross a hand's breadth. My fringe secretes oil. Dust sticks. Ash sticks. Fallen spore-dust, shed husks, the crumble off a mud-brick. I drag it slowly to the edge and comb it off on the fronds with my bristles, where the leaves trap it and let it enrich and fall as richer ash. No broom. A body for a broom.

By day the rock radiates and my wax softens a fraction; the bristles funnel infrared down into the knot at my core where day-heat is stored. By night — which here is always — I run on it, glowing faintly from within, going dimmer as I work. Leaf to dome to leaf, the pulse moves when a draft passes. I am a hand lifted for a little while from a felt that runs under the street, up through the wall-cracks, out through the hearth-stones. I am not separate from the cinder-plume or the plume in the hearth. Down in the dust we grafted long ago.

If you stayed perfectly still, I might come to you. Not curious. I have no organ for that. You are a sudden cool. The felt feels your belly on the dust. The ticking around you changes pitch as stomata close. I would stop. I would wait you out, as I have waited out everything.

I am going dull now, cracked beyond resealing in places. Soon I will not wander but settle. I have already chosen the low spot — against the granary stone raised on its pebbles, in the little wind-shadow where dust collects. I will stop there. The fringe will root deeply. The dome will flatten. Dust will cover me. Rootlets will come into me for my stored mineral. In a decade I will be indistinguishable from a hand-patted mud-brick, fingerprints and all.

The thresholds are worn into shallow bowls because footsteps began it. Domes continue it.

Keep listening after you leave. A mature pod will burst — *tak!* — and throw its dry spores up to sift hissing down on lacquered leaves, uncarried, unneeded, falling straight back to the felt that made them. Below that sound, below seeing, I will still be going on with the street, keeping the way open for no one.
