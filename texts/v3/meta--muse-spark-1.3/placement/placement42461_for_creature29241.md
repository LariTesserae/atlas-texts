I don't start in the middle.

At first I think I would — on a walkway, carrying, shouting to be heard two feet away. I try that shape on and it slips. My throat is raw by age six here. My shoulders are wrong for the slab that needs four hands. The crowd replaces me with three more people before I can hold the face.

So lower. Slower. Off to the side where the footing gives.

You'd find me not where the mat is thickest but where it thins just enough to breathe sideways. Near the north edge, under the weeping lip. That blunt sandstone rib no higher than a house, constantly sweating. The cisterns are there, clay-rimmed, catching the bead-water before it fouls. Just south of them, where the overflow seeps out, there is a borrow pit gone to wash-pit. Tea-colored. Old clay-dig. The walkway sags beside it — split logs, already sinking — and everyone steps over the sag without looking down.

I look down. I stay down.

I'm in the lee of a low mud-brick wall, finger-smoothed, slumping even as it was patched this morning. If you press your thumb into the mud at its foot the print fills instantly with clear seep. Mine do. All day my thumbprints fill. The frogs are already there watching the fill. They climb my ankles if I sit still long enough, and I sit still long enough.

I thought I would be a carrier, a builder. I'm not. I'm a rinser. A keeper of the edge between drink-water and wash-water.

My work is hands in water up to the wrist: hides soaking, hair-twine nets loosening their sour, minnows stunned and flicking in a clay pot between my knees, a baby's legs washed while its mother shouts past with a basket on her hip and doesn't break stride. I scrape the pale fungal felt off the wall-foot where feet have missed for a day. I wring. I sort. I retie a lashing when sinew slips. My palms are polished hard as a digging stick. Clay lives under my nails and will not come out.

I am wet before sex or age, like everyone. Broad splayed feet lost their sandals weeks ago. Legs to the knee blue-grey and cracking and re-wet. Forehead gleaming. Hair plastered. I smell of smoke and sour milk and warm mud. My voice — I had to coarsen it to survive two feet away — is not a shout but the other thing you can do in a roar: a low, chest-deep hum that fits *under* the frog band. The women calling ducks bellow over me. The baby that never stops wailing rides over me. The hand-drum somewhere in the mat thickens without covering me. I hum and the pot between my knees vibrates faintly and the minnows turn.

Don't settle on me yet. Let me get more myself.

Not the hum. The listening.

I'm half-deaf by now, like everyone past twenty. I hear by vibration — feet on plank, splash-shift in a wallow three pens over, the particular grunt of our mud-colored pig when she finds something edible she shouldn't. But mostly I listen to the floor: that deep rattling frog-bellow in millions, unbroken, living in the jawbone and sternum. People have stopped hearing it as sound. I haven't. Or I hear it the way you hear weather.

Because if the water drops a finger's width, the roar thins at the top. If silt comes in fast off the pan, the beetles row different. If the weep slows on the lip — and it does, in hours, not seasons — the drip-drip from the overhang spaces out and the cistern skin tightens. I notice before the shouters do. I lift my head. I click my tongue twice, the signal my girls know. They are up on the roof with legs dangling, sorting minnows into pots while the dog sleeps against their backs, while the heron waits for scraps. They hear my click through their feet.

That's the choreography: keep animals close and food closer. Our pig knows my grunt and I know hers. The goats balance on the wall above me and I let them eat the mold off the hide's edge because they'll bray if a stranger's rat runs the cord. The crabs sidle under my walkway-sag and I let them, they clean. The white wading bird that owns this pit — utterly unafraid — will spear straight into my pot if I look away. I don't look away. I nudge the pot with my knee and she settles for tadpoles. We have an arrangement.

A square yard of my puddle holds tadpoles, minnows, beetles rowing, frog adults with throats pulsing, and the reflected snout of the pig come to drink. I am in it too, reflected. Throat pulsing. That's not metaphor. When I hum low my throat goes like theirs.

By late-afternoon that never ends — that directionless amber gloom with no shadows — the smoke from a hundred dung fires spreads sideways and lies on the pit and stings faintly. The midges pillar over me. I breathe them. I slap without thinking. The swallows cut through in flickering sheets and never land in the mud because they don't have to. I have to. I do.

I am not becoming something else. That's the strangest, most normal thing. The town patches as fast as it slumps and I patch as fast as I slump. The pit fills as fast as I bail it. Silt arrives as fast as feet churn it away. Young rock, old hands. Loud equilibrium, holding against the wet.

Find me there when you come in off the pan, ears ringing, feet wet, frogs pulsing against your ankles. I won't shout at you. Everyone else will shout. I'll be the woman in the sag, knees in ooze, hands tea-stained, a fist-sized ochre frog sitting on my foot like it owns the lease — which it does — watching your thumbprint fill with water beside the wall.

Sit a minute, if you can stand the roar in your sternum. The water stays at the surface. The light stays dim. I will rinse something and hum under it all and listen for the change that tells us to move the cistern rim one thumb higher.

That's where I am. That's what I've become here.
