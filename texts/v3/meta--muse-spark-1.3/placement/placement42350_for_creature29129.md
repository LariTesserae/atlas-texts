At first I think I would be walking. Still upright, still going somewhere.

But there is nowhere to go here, and no walking as I know it. Only sinking and pulling. Boot to the ankle in cold sponge, water welling up around the palm, every step filling behind me with black. So no — I wouldn't be on the move. I wouldn't be on the rise looking out either, trying to map the hexagons of tarns and runnels. That high view belongs to the first hour, before the cloud closes again.

Lower. I would find myself lower.

Not in the reed-sea — I can't push between those shoulder-to-shoulder hollow stems without tearing living flesh, and they close behind me anyway. Not on the perfect lawn, either. That's skin. Too exposed, too busy breathing out the 14:00 mist that will become afternoon rain.

I would find myself in a hollow. Where the mat dips. Where a black tarn sits rimless in the thatch, no deeper than my hand, and the braided seeps move a centimeter deep and then drop through a hole into a booming space underneath I will never see.

I would be on my knees there, which is really just sunk to the shin. Numb fingers parting the green to black-green to pale white-green where light has never reached. Filter long since clogged with spores. Breath fogging. Shouting given up — you can't be heard two meters out. Fire given up — nothing dead stays dead long enough to burn, it's all too full of water and already being stitched back in.

At first I think I would be the watcher. The witness. The brief dry animal with a pad and a bivouac, water welling up patiently around my weight all night while the mat ticks and gurgles under me. A disturbance in pressure.

But that doesn't hold. Dryness doesn't exist here except briefly inside a sealed seed. To stay, even for three cold nights, I have to stop trying to stay dry.

So closer: I would be with my ear to the moss. Given up on eyes — there is nothing to see that watches back, no eyeshine, no wingbeat — given over to listening. To the hydraulic thump lifting water uphill, hollow to hummock, tens and hundreds of meters, without pumps. To the hollow stems booming like organ pipes. To the seed capsules popping, millions per hour, sharp cracks rolling for kilometers. To the billions of drips becoming static becoming weight in my molars.

Closer still. Because if it computes with water and sound, then to be here at all I have to become a place where water and sound can happen.

I would be a bead.

On one of those curved, lens-like reed leaves, beaded with a second lens of water, leaning all together a few degrees into the dim bruised light. Catching what should not be enough to live on. Bouncing it leaf to leaf down until the understory glows faintly green. Held, and then shed, and then caught again below. A single raindrop shed ten thousand times before it reaches the root-zone — that would be me. Not falling. Being passed hand to hand by the great funnel-leaves, table-wide and upcurled. Learning the roar from the inside.

And then, when I finally do reach the mat — because everything reaches the mat — I would be the compensation a kilometer away.

You step here, and growth is redirected over there within minutes. Runners cross within hours. Sporelings green it over. To leave a patch empty would be to leave a processor idle.

I would be that greening-over. That patch.

Not prey, not guest. Not the body itself — I could never be that vast, that indifferent, that cold-steaming roof-of-the-world thinking in hydraulics, burying newborn stone before geology can happen. But I could be the brief shape it makes to forget me. The memory in depth. The lower white-green layer holding last week's footfall the way it holds last decade's storm schedule.

Find me there: low, wet, still, in the hollow water. What I am is a sealed seed for a little while — holding one small, stubborn pocket of inner dryness — and then, when even that softens and splits, just another tissue learning how to stay.
