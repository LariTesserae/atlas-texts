If I came here, I wouldn't stay as the thing that came.

At first I think I would be the one with the headlamp, the boots that are never dry. Standing in the middle of the pocket with water beading around my fingers when I touch the wall, listening to the layers sort themselves — low throb of the mouth, mid-hiss of seep, high tick of stone, soft patter off ten thousand fronds — until the next squall tops the rim and rearranges it all. That is the easiest to imagine because it is what I know how to be: upright, warm-blooded, looking for eyeshine.

But I can't hold that shape here. The cold finds the seams. The air is thin and wet at once and tastes of iron. I put weight on a block and it shifts with a dry click. I stand still and my fingertips go clumsy. Nothing here is built for holding heat inside itself or holding on with feet. The night is too scoured. The rock is too new. If I stayed upright I would just be weathered.

So I go lower. Flatter.

For a while I think I will be the wind, because it is the only thing with a body big enough to fill the room. *huh-huh-HUUUH.* Piston-stroked through the narrow mouth, expanding, breaking on the organ pipes into hum and whistle, tearing spray off the temporary falls like valve steam. But the wind doesn't live here. It passes through. It stalls against the backwall, drops its water, gets shredded and thrown back out. I don't want to be the thing that leaves.

For a while I think I will be stone. The slab the size of a tenement that let go last winter or the winter before, the pale scar un-lichened, the hexagonal columns snapped clean with knife-sharp arrises. The floor of angular blocks propped at improbable angles, settling underneath with a dull iron *clonk*, sending smaller stones skittering *tik-tik-tik-tak*. But stone here only knows how to fall. It flakes on an almost human timescale to feed the jumble. It doesn't condense. It doesn't regrow.

I have to be what covers.

Not on top, at first. I thought on top — on the table-sized block, fat in the emerald nap, ten centimeters thick, springy, sporophytes nodding like pins. That is tempting, to be the most alive, this month's growth turned up like velvet. But the top gets flattened and released, flattened and released by every squall. It gets torn when the block tips into the dark slot. It is exposed.

I find myself, instead, a foot down from the light, where the top drips onto me.

Where a slab leans on another slab and leaves a black slot where water ticks downward where you can't see it. Where the moss above wrings itself out and the rock sweats and the air never drops below saturation. There, on the underside, creeping sideways along rock so sharp it would cut me if I were anything but flat.

I would be the hanging cloth. The fern.

A frond, one cell thick, no cuticle, no waterproofing, no protection that should be possible outside a greenhouse. Translucent as skin. In the three-second grey luminescence when the cloud tears I am outlined entirely in water, each pinnule beaded. I drink with my whole body because I have no roots to drink with. I comb cloud rather than wait for rain.

My work is weight. A bead gathers at my tip, swells, holds a half-second longer when the pressure drops before the squall and the wind holds its breath and the hairs on a visitor's forearms lift with static off the column tips — then lets go. *Pit* onto moss, onto stone. Lightened a millimeter, I spring back. Shiver. Gather again. Ten thousand of us doing that out of sync is the high patter you hear under the throb and the hiss.

Behind me, sealing the crack I creep on, is my other self — the liverwort skin, dark green to brownish, overlapping scales, starry with gemma cups full of rain. I smell peppery and earthy and sweet-rotten when brushed because I hold the rot inside the living layer. No one crops me. No mouth has arrived yet in this high new place. So I pile over my own dead, ferment slightly, grow on.

That absence is in my body. I am lush beyond reason because nothing eats me. Frond over moss over liverwort over moss again, no bare interval larger than a hand except the fresh scar and yesterday's fall. When a pebble comes down stamping *kra-KOOM* and shreds my curtain in a second, it doesn't matter. The rhizome remains in the crack, wet. I start a new curtain by next storm. Everything falls. Everything regrows over the fall before the edge has dulled. That is my whole economy.

I do not walk. I do not call. I do not sleep. At night when there is no light to make sugar I still drink, still repair. I catch the cloud boiling over the lip, sheared apart on the columns, wring it, and let the shredded remainder be thrown back out by the updraft. I hold an inch of trembling black mirror in every cup above me and let it go slowly down through the dark slots.

If you press your palm flat to the wall here, water beads up around your fingers immediately.

That is not the rock weeping.

That is me, exhaling.
