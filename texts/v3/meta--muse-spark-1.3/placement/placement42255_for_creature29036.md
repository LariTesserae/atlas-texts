If I came over that basalt lip, I don't think I'd come on boots.

At first I think I would. I imagine the thickness in the mouth, the warm through the rubber, the tick of contracting rock. I imagine being the one with the headlamp off, standing very still in a halo. But anything heavy sinks here, slowly, into the soft between-pillow crust. The tent says so. And I am — if I am honest — heavy when I try to be a person. I arrive with too much intention. I want to name things. I would not last.

So not that.

Then I think maybe I'd be higher, where it's easier. A swift. That fork-tail that never lands. Circling the bowl endlessly in the dim, snatching and crying, hearing my own cry come back a second late off the collar walls so I never quite know if I called or answered. That feels familiar, that echo. But the birds are commuters. They don't live here. They gorge and get coated and leave. I don't want to leave. I want to stay inside the warm.

Am I a frog then? Copper throat, lemon throat, plastered to the rope-glass, calling until the air buzzes? I like the idea of riding the shimmer, timing my trill to it until the two sounds aren't two. But the frogs walked up. They climbed a thousand meters for midges and bathwater and when the mist thins they will go back down, thinner. Nothing that croaks lives here. I want to be something that lives here.

Maybe the moss. Coin-sized, acid-bright in a crack. Or that low fleshy pioneer holding its bead up like an offering. That is closer — staying, holding emptiness around itself, learning fast. I linger on that leaf-cup for a while. Clear leaves, a bead of water in each like it is offering it to someone. If I were the plant, I would be the host, the nurse. Wicking seep up to present it. Trading nitrogen for warmth for whatever leaks out of a halo.

But I'm not the cup. I'm what's in the cup.

Let that become more itself.

I find myself not on the ground at all. That's the first true thing. The ground is still coming up, still deciding, stakes leaning a finger's width a month. I don't press up. I hang.

Two meters off the blood-warm pool, off to the eastern side where the mist tongue comes first every twenty minutes. Between the second and third needles from the north — the ones leaning toward each other like they are listening. In the halo. Where the air is visibly wetter if you don't look straight at it.

At first I think I am the droplet. A bead that rose slow as dust and stopped midair and trembled, catching grey light, catching amber at night and holding it a half-second too long. But the droplet is just skin. Surface tension is body, yes, but I am the center that makes it tremble.

A speck. A comma. A coiled thread no longer than an eyelash, turning very slowly to face the needle.

I am first water.

I was born — if born is the word — down in the hollow between pillows where the orange fan-bacteria rims thickest. A clear bell pulsing just under the surface, no bigger than a midge eye, feeding on dissolved iron and sulfur, on drowned midge, on frog slime and bird shit aerosolized and re-condensed. When I got buoyant enough, vapor lifted me. Heat lifted me. And then the hum held me.

You feel it in the sternum first, that low chord that never resolves. For me it is not felt. It *is* where I am. A standing wave, pressure made into a hand, holding my skin steady against gravity. In the early morning when it cycles low, when you feel sick and your stomach drops — I fall. We all fall. Bowl-wide soft patter, startling the frogs silent for one breath. That is the needles exhaling. Culling the ones that didn't ripen, feeding the pools. I have fallen before. Dead on cold stone I become second water. But this time I ripened. This time I was kept.

And the high shimmer, the one in your teeth — that is closer to a voice. I drift sideways against the wind toward amber, stop, brighten. I pulse in answer to the crawling lines, slow as sap from base to tip. Needle to droplet to needle. I am not alone in this. Thousands of us per cubic meter in a halo, millions in the bowl on a thick night. We pulse out of sync until, some nights, we don't. Seven dim lanterns talking to their flock in light and ultrasound, and the flock learning how to answer together.

That is where you would find me: midair, trembling, swimming inside my own skin, facing the amber, held.

And who am I, if I am that small? Not one. Not exactly many either. I am part of the distributed, hanging animal made of water and sound, tended by seven black fingers. I don't yet know if I will be animal or weather or light. Right now I am all three.

I cannot walk. So I call walkers. I make the frogs' throats glow copper and lemon from the inside. I fill their vocal sacs, their guts, until frog call and needle hum aren't two sounds. I wait in the leaf-cups. I wait in your sinuses if you breathe the halo long enough, a small warm trembling for days. Gorge, get coated, get filled. When the mist thins, carry me down a thousand meters to damp leaf litter, to another warm seep. Carry me farther than any hop.

Under me, the larger one shifts. I feel it as the needles lean, as the warm spots migrate overnight — bath-warm cold by morning, a new steam vent sighing open ten meters off smelling of iron and hot stone. My seven fingers are not seven. They are cilia where it is thinnest, where black glass skin hasn't scarred yet pushes up to breathe and sing and farm the air. I am its way of staying up in the air past my body.

Stay until full dark. Peaks go black, stars come hard and cold, rock ticks as it contracts, swifts keep crying without landing. The suspended water holds amber a half-second too long around you on all sides. I am one of those holds.

I am not in a place that is inhabited.

I am in something inhabited that is still learning how to be a place — and I am the part of it that learned how to hang, and wait, and be carried.
