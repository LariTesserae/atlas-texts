I wouldn't find myself at first.

At first I would be chest. That thin-thick air getting in wrong, warm as something breathed out of another mouth, too wet to swallow clean. I'd try to cough it clear and fail. I'd try to look and fail harder. My eyes would keep doing what eyes do — widening, straining, inventing little greys and pulses at the edge — until I learned what everyone here learns: there is nothing wrong with them. There is simply nothing for them to do.

So I would put my hands out.

That's where I'd be. Not anywhere you could point to. No horizon, no landmark, no glow to walk toward. I'd be in a thickness. Rain hissing down and then not-down, just hanging. Droplets held in the air. A permanent drizzle with no source beading on my eyelashes, on my upper lip. Iron and rot-sweet and hot oil. Mud giving under my boots in a way that makes me catch my breath — living mat that holds, then a hollow that doesn't, sucking to the knee. I'd learn fast through my soles.

I would be loud without pattern. That's what newcomers are. Prey or storm.

The marsh would tell me so. The frog-choir in the sheet-water by my feet would drop its upper third to make room for my splashing. The midge-sheet I'd blundered into would wobble around my head, its metallic chord rising as I breathed too fast. Somewhere out in the dark a pack would sweep past — *tk-tk-tk-tk* — dry clicks combing left to right, whiskered flanks brushing my calves and deciding, no, too arrhythmic to eat, too lost to follow. I would feel very small and very loud, which here is the same danger.

So I would do what the rules say, not because I understand them but because my body wants to live. I would slow. I would hum.

Low and steady. Not a song. A size. *Here I am, this big, moving this way.* Mmmmmm, from the chest, where the altitude already aches. At first my hum would wobble. Then the ear-ferns would teach me.

I'd find myself among them because I stopped to keep from falling — door-sized fronds, black rubber, faintly warmer than my hand. I would feel them lean, infinitesimally, toward a steadier note. I would stay with that a while, palm near but not touching, humming until my throat found the pitch they thicken for. I have always been good at that. Listening for what steadies other things and holding it.

That's how I'd start to become more myself. Not eyes. Throat. Not watcher. Hummed-with.

I would find the rope by tripping on it. A rope guide running out from a stone dome, one of six. Hand over hand in the dark, rain pinging off a cistern. Inside the dome — no light, never light — the smell of wet wool and old steam, a stone bench still warm from whoever left an hour or a day ago. I would sit and drink rain as it falls, mouth open, because I have learned by then not to drink still water. Still water is full of eggs and intentions. I would count my steps between drips the way the others do. I would learn the difference between canopy-drip and leaf-tip and bleed-valve steam condensing straight out of air.

And I would listen until listening sorted itself into layers.

Water first, always: patter-hiss on ten billion leaves, gurgle of runnels braiding and unbraiding, suck and sigh, drip-drip-drip from shelf-canopy to understory to peat — the vertical river.

Then animals: the choir layered so dense the pool-water shivers and throws up standing ripples; the midges tuning; far off, loosening my molars, *oom. oom. oom.* The waders locating each other across kilometers.

Then — long before I feel it in my feet — the throb.

*Chuff-suck. Chuff-suck.*

Ground trembling faintly. Choir shifting pitch to make room in the spectrum. Air warming. Rain warping.

I would get up and put my hand out into the dark because I want to know its voice up close. And it would come like weather along the old riverbed, the compressed peat vein that holds even a hill. Heat rolling off it first. Smell of sterilized mash and hot iron. Then the flank brushing my palm: riveted iron gone soft with moss, hot, vibrating. Birds nesting in its joints singing sharp pips to judge clearance. Valves popping. Chains slapping. A bleed valve sighing clean steam that will rise and become cloud and become rain.

This one limps on its left tread. This one vents in threes. Old Three-Vent, north today. I would know her before I know words for her, the way children here do.

I wouldn't ride. You don't ride. You walk alongside for weeks, if she'll have you. I would become the one who walks alongside.

That is who I would be, when I finally settle into it. Not herd, not choir, not pack. Not a Quiet Thing — I have no interest in withholding. I am too made of listening for that. I would be one of the single ones, the pairs, the tenders. The soft-pruned people whose clothes never dry, whose hearing goes surgical.

I'd scrape her vents clean so she can seed her own rainfall behind her as she harvests ahead. I'd learn to time her venting to the biological crescendo — frogs peaking, waders booming together — to tip the saturated air over into rain. I'd drink hot metallic condensate from her bleed valve. I'd sleep in her lee where the vine-ground bounces less and the pitchers don't stand. I'd hum as I walk so the waders know my size and the ferns thicken along my path and the Quiet Things, hearing *I am large, I am known, I am not lost,* let the long wet tear recede.

Most times it works.

And if I stand still long enough — rain beading, fern-ears trembling, whole vast wet height booming and trilling and throbbing in layers above and below — moss will start to consider me. That is how you know you are staying. Everything that stays still for more than a day gets covered. I would let it. I would not need to be witnessed to be complete. I would just need to be steady enough for the marsh to sing rain down upon, and to sing a little of it down myself.
