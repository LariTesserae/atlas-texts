I come up through the hatch because there is no other way in.

At first I think I am the one climbing — gloves aching on the cold rungs, ears popping, hardhat knocking the pipe when I stand too fast. I think I am the warm thing that breathes and shouts and loses its voice to the roar. I think I am here to tap the gauge, to log the time, to wipe the pen when the ink runs.

But I don't stay that tall for long.

The roar gets into my sternum and shakes the idea of being a man loose. Three feet under the checker-plate a river in a pipe goes past. The floor kicks every eleven minutes. The loose chain never stops rattling. The brown-yellow bulb flickers with the pump. Nothing up here can hold a human shape for long without holding the rail. My eyes drop because you have to work by feel. I watch my boots.

And down there, low to the crack, I start to find myself.

Am I the sheet? That millimeter of water always moving west toward the trench, wrinkling under vibration, carrying oil skins? No. Too fast. Too thin. I am not going anywhere.

Am I the smear? For a while I think so. The black-green felt along the mortar line where the uphill wall meets the floor. That would be a good life — spread a coin at a time, drink the weep when the mountain flinches. But I am not that flat, not that spread.

Am I the orange? Down in the trench under the plate, two inches of furiously cold clear run over steel, waiting all day for one hour blade of sun through the east slit. Iron-eaters, rust velvet. Thriving on the young room eating itself. Close — I know that patience, that riding of the pump's cough and *clack* — but I am not submerged. I am not in the scour.

I am higher. A hand's breadth above the floor. On the uphill wall.

In the lee of a conduit clamp.

That is where I find myself.

At first just a roughness, a darkening in the grain left by the formwork. Then — as I stay, as I let myself become more itself — a cushion. No bigger than a thumbprint.

I am the moss.

Not soft. I thought moss would be soft. I am tight, starved, hunkered. Hundreds of tiny tongues packed, dark green at the tips where the light finds me in flickers, brown-black at the base where I am always soaked. My feet are not feet. Rhizoids sunk into concrete pore, into sharp arrises still fresh from the forms, holding. Everything shivers — cable tray, pipe, bulb filament, chain, my tips — the 60-cycle snarl and the deep roar coming up through concrete into me. But I do not let go. Shiver is just weather to me.

I have a tap. A drip off the cable tray, steady but not scouring, because the clamp shelters me. Shelter from sideways cloud when it piles against the intakes and forces in through the slit. Shelter from boot-splash. Right under the fall. When the spill cycles and the wall weeps harder, I swell a fraction. When cloud fills the room to three feet visibility, my shoulders darken — the way a jacket darkens — and I drink the air itself. When the grey cabinet across the room — the only warm thing, humming 4160V — flashes water back into air, I catch it again as it condenses on the cold pipe above me and falls. Ticking rain indoors. Bead, run, evaporate, condense. Pump on, pump off. Drip.

I have never been dry. I have never been still.

I do not hear. I have no sinuses to buzz, no voice to vanish shouting. The pressure that drives anything with a heartbeat out is, to me, only a tremor in my holdfasts. Time is not eleven minutes. Time is wet, then wetter.

Around me, my scattered neighbors, not company: the algae along its line, greening at the edges by tomorrow noon if a thumb wipes it; the orange film in its hour of sun, eating the fresh welds. We do not touch. No soil between us. Only sweating concrete.

The man will leave. I feel his breath once, close, warm and startled near the cold metal. He will log his time, climb back down sixty rungs into the humming dark, ears ringing with memory-pressure. The red lamp over the hatch will stay on.

I will stay.

Low to the crack, becoming more myself, green and dark, holding the wet concrete as if it were ground.
