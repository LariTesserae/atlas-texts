At first I think I would be up high, looking down.

Standing at the upper cairn where you can see the whole of it in one look — the low wall to windward, the juniper-dark tumble to lee, the fell at the back, the terraces stopping clean at the front. A room-sized place. I think I would like to be the one who sees it whole. The raven, maybe, crossing at the same hour morning and evening, croaking once. That seems right. To live more permanently than any person and only mark the edge of the page.

But no. That is too high and too free. The air up there is cold and I don't belong in it yet. Let me come down.

Then I think I would be the rowan by the lower wall, bent leeward, berries late and few and left for the ravens. Or the lichen — sage, ash-white, mustard — holding the stone together and not scraped. Or the moss, an inch thick on the north sides. That is closer. To be low and tenacious. To be taken up in handfuls in a dry week and wrung into a bucket, and then laid back. To be used and replaced carefully. I like the thought of being laid back.

But I am not that patient yet. I am still too loud in my steps.

Give me a few days.

If I stayed — and up here staying is the only way to be seen — I would fall into it the way the description says. Wake cold. Breath showing until the sun clears the eastern tor. Shake dew from the shutter. The stone cold to the palm until afternoon. Pull on wool over wool, grey on brown on blackened grey, smelling already of smoke and damp though I only arrived yesterday. My cuffs damp at the edge by mid-morning.

You would not find me at the center. Not at first. The paved cistern court, slightly sunken, is for those who know how much to take. I don't know yet. I would stand second with my bucket while someone ladles with the wooden cup on its rope, not filling to the top though she could, leaving the measured inches. I would nod. I would watch and learn to leave them too.

You would not find me at the bake-oven either, except standing a little off while three or four stand about its sooted mouth, hands in pockets, saying little. That is company and I haven't earned that kind of quiet yet.

You would find me in the gaps.

Because that is what keeps emerging, the longer I stand here. Between any two houses there is a length of pale grass, a woodpile under a slab, a patch of bare, damp gravel kept on purpose. The houses do not touch. Fifteen, sixteen roofed, thirty people perhaps, in winter less. They keep those gaps to hear their own steps too loudly and remind themselves to walk softly. I would be there, in that loud step. In the space left for wind and quiet.

More itself: I would be the keeper of a house with no fire.

Not mine. Not lived-in. One of the ones that is kept anyway, swept anyway. The door closed, shutters closed but not latched, barrel under the eave emptied after rain so it doesn't breed. The lane in front of it swept to the doorstone and no further, because the wind does the rest. Whoever passes shakes the dew from its shutters in the morning. That would be me. The passer who stops.

I would learn its particular maintenance. Which gutter to clear with a bent stick after rain, working downhill toward the court — the grit, the rowan leaves, the mussel-shells of ice — and which to leave for the next rain. How the north wall grows its moss and when to wring it and how to lay it back without tearing. How the same slab on the roof lifts and beds again, lichen-side up, chisel pits showing. Knock-stop-knock with the hand-axe and wedge. Unhurried. Exact.

My hands would give me away before my face did. Broadening at the palm, nails broken short and rimmed with dark soil that washing doesn't lift, backs cracked at the joints from cold water. An old woman's thumb flattened sideways from pressing slabs. I don't have that yet, but I would get the beginning of it — the careful, cupped way of carrying, elbows in, eyes on the rim, carrying water without spilling. The boy already knows. I would walk behind him at a distance and learn where to step when ice skins the lower puddles. *Step here. Not there.*

Evenings, if the wind dropped, I might sit at the bench stone at the widening where three lanes meet, not quite a square. An old man may sit and I may sit beside him, not touching, both looking down the terraces to where they stop. Saying nothing, because the stone takes the edge off a voice and gives it back flat. *Gutter's clear. Take less, it'll mist tonight.* That would be enough for a day.

And at night I would listen for the seep in the back rock, drop by drop into its stone basin, longer than the houses have stood. They listen to know the hill is still well. I would listen to learn how to be well in the same way: not to accumulate, not to expand. When a wall bulges, take it down with two or three others over a morning without much talk and rebuild it with the same stones to the same height. When a garden has no keeper that season, still dig it to its lines, still plant potatoes, leave the harvest in the bake-oven porch for whoever needs. Maintain more than I inhabit. On purpose.

I am not waiting. I am not hiding. I am not the remnant of something larger, though the right-angled lanes remember a town.

I am what happens on the third or fourth day, when the chill stays on the back of the neck even at midday when the light is plain and white and normal, when my wool smells like their wool, when my voice goes low without deciding to, when the drip from the eaves slows and stops long after rain and I stop waiting for more color as the light goes plain white to plain grey.

You would find me moving from door to door across pale grass, bucket scraping stone, sweeping the lane in front of a door that opens onto earth and is left that way, swept.

Ordered like a city and empty like a moor. Holding water. Holding quiet. Holding myself exactly in place.
