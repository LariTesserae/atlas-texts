At first I don't know where to put myself.

I come in breathing too loud. That thin, too-clean air with an edge in it — I feel it in the lungs before I see anything. For a while I am only a set of lungs, and then eyes watering from fog. Black stone and sky-water in every direction. Wet underfoot. The world too high and too new to hold a footprint for long.

I think, maybe I am just passing through. A visitor on a warmed path with tea going cold too fast. I wipe the railing and in ten minutes the beads are back. I watch a carrier sigh to a stop for no reason I can see, and then two kilometers ahead a line resolves — russet, long-legged, five wide and impossibly long — and I understand: I am being taught to wait.

So I wait. And while I wait I try on shapes.

Am I the carrier? No. I am too impatient to glide that silently and stop that completely. I want to explain the pause. The carrier doesn't explain. It just holds its doors shut while schoolchildren press their faces to the glass and count legs.

Am I one of the grazers? I wish. To have splayed feet made for marsh, to pick sedge and keep walking on ancient memory across brand-new rock, to be the traffic instead of the thing that waits for traffic. But I don't know how to move a kilometer long. My body is single.

Am I a goose, a crane? To drop out of the sky by the tens of thousands and turn a sky-colored pan white and black and noisy — no. I am too quiet for that, and my knees hurt in the cold. I listen for them instead, that far thin crying like wind through a cracked door.

Slowly, the way fog condenses, I get smaller and lower.

I find myself not in any center — there isn't one — but in a knot. Nine houses with their backs to the wind, grown-poured walls rough outside so the lichen will take. Pale green, orange, black crust. I know not to touch. From the ridge at dusk I can see us: a little warm knot in black vastness, path markers glowing at ankle height, breath and fog rising off the standing water. Three carriers away is where I courted someone. Two districts over is where I apprenticed, because I liked the singing.

Because I am the one who walks the ridge in the morning.

That's where you would find me, most days. Up before the rim ice has melted off the ponds, boots wide and soft so I don't tear moss. De-icing wand in one wet hand. The condenser nets have gone white with frost overnight, tall and almost invisible, and I move down the line thawing them joint by joint. They sing faintly when the wind is right. That's the only high sound for miles and I have learned not to hum along, to just let it happen. Wipe fog off the struts. Wipe it off my coat. Wipe it off the shoulders of the kid walking with me, who lopes instead of runs because the air is thin and the moss is fragile.

I check the red blink of the triad moorings in the evening. Plumbing. The house breathed up a finger's width this season, floor dead level inside while the basalt heaved outside. My door stuck this morning. I didn't force it. I made tea and waited an hour while it leveled. You learn that.

But midday, I am mostly on my knees.

There is a sheet, a centimeter deep and twenty meters wide, braided around pebbles, shifting by the hour because no channel has been worn to hold it. I kneel in it. Cold seeps through instantly. I watch which pebbles moved since morning. I write it down. The rivers haven't decided where they want to be yet and someone has to witness the decision. Black moss in the seep. Sponge-moss holding melt like a soaked blanket. Cushion of dwarf willow no higher than my ankle. Cotton-grass nodding. It smells like wet stone and peat and cold, and if you kneel long enough the foxes forget to be afraid. They den under the warmed paths now. They know.

When the herd comes, I stop being a hydrologist and become a gate.

No announcement. The thread pauses. The drift-wall — hardly higher than my knee, miles of low angled stone — does most of the work, nudging hooves away from the intake. I just stand shoulder to shoulder with whoever is nearest, palm held up though no driver needs to see it, nod toward the line. We inhale, hold, let them pass, exhale. Hooves sucking in wet peat. The long sigh of moving bodies. Water flying where a chase cuts across a sheet-flood between houses. Moss tearing. Then stillness again, and drip from a weeping cut. The children don't complain about being late. They count legs.

You asked who I would be.

Not the owner of anything. Not the manager of the herds — they are not livestock. Not the master of the house that levels itself. More like a midwife. I keep the nets clear and the intakes clean and the gates open while a brand-new piece of the world learns how to drain, how to green, how to hold weight. At night I go inside to a warm-wood-colored room and leave the outside to predator and prey playing out right between the houses. I keep the lights low so as not to blind the night-movers. I listen before I read — wind low over stone, geese far off, carrier's soft shush, herd's long suck, and underneath it all every few days that deep subsonic groan as the ground rebounds a centimeter. Not an earthquake. Just settling. I don't look up anymore.

I live high before I am anything else. Broad chest, wind-burned cheeks, pale around the mouth from always being damp, hands always a little wet. Breath unhurried so it doesn't steam too hard.

What I am, more and more itself the longer I stay, is not a population of one.

It's attention that learned how to keep a house, how to keep nine houses, how to keep the path warm between them for whoever walks next through the fog.
