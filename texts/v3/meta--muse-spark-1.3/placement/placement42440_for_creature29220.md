At first I don't know where I am.

Only that it is hot. Not hot like a touch, that comes and goes. Hot like a place to be inside of. It sits on me, and then it sits *in* me, until I can't tell where the weight stops. And wet - everything touching everything else, with no edge to dry against. Sweat with nowhere to go.

And loud. I thought for a while I *was* the loud. A deep throb in the teeth and gut, *duh-duh-duh-duh*, that never pauses for breath, and over it a high whine, and over that a thin scream. I shiver with it. Everything shivers. The inch of water I lie under shivers into rings that vanish before they spread.

Don't settle, don't settle. If I am not the heat, am I the water?

No. The water comes and licks and goes. It runs down walls, beads and swells and falls - *plik, plok* - into the trough and is carried to the black hole in the corner. It is too fast for me. It has somewhere to go. I don't go anywhere. And there is a rainbow skin on it, oil, that breaks when the drips hit. That isn't me either. I am under that.

Am I the concrete, then? Warm through, sharp at the corners, still showing the grain of wood. Weeping from both sides. The room cutting its half-inch teeth around the crack in the southeast. That is close. I am flat against it, pore to pore, no gap you could slip a fingernail under. The men would think I *am* damp shadow, or paint.

But concrete doesn't want light. I do.

That is how I find myself.

A rectangle of white noon falls straight down through the grate. Dust and steam motes hang in it. For an hour it grazes sideways. The rest of the day a yellow bulb buzzes over the door that never turns off. Between them I can feel it on my uppermost surface — not warm, I already have warm — but *food*. A thin incoming. The only place where wet and light stay long enough together without washing away.

And there I am. Spread so thin I almost am not.

You'd find me on the lip of the central channel, right where the trough holds its inch of hot, clear water. Not in the deepest water — that would drown even me, scour me off. Not up on the dry wall — there is no dry, but higher would starve me. On the lip. On the curb where the water keeps licking over. Low. Stay low. Stay thin. Stay wet.

That's my whole rule.

I have no leaves or legs or eyes. No thickness you can peel up. If you put a thumb to me I am slippery as soap and come away as nothing, just slickness. Dark green-black, old-blood green, almost black until the bulb catches me wet. No thicker than a coat of paint. That is not an insult. That is how I survive the hammering. The hundred drips that hit and ring — I let them hit. I don't pit or tear. The twelve-inch main screaming through the middle, shaking the whole box until molars ache — I ride it. I need the shiver. Stagnant would cook me or starve me. Vibrating keeps me thin, oxygenated.

I drink the heat. While your sweat sits on you and goes nowhere, I spread. A sixteenth of an inch wider a year across the pore. Lengthen by dust.

At my edges I am already turning to stone. That white, then cream, then pale sulfurous yellow skin creeping over every pipe and wall — young rock, fresh — is creeping over me too. Where my green meets the white you can't tell which is eating which. That is my life: to be slowly entombed by the very water that feeds me, and to keep sliding outward a dust-width ahead.

There are others like me. Two others. One on the north wall where the daylight grazes for an hour at noon. One directly under the grate in the full fall of white. We are three patches, no more. We don't touch, but we are the same holding-on.

And across the room, in the southeast crack where the seep runs five degrees cooler, there is the other one who holds differently. Pale, upright, translucent, two fronds no more than four inches high. A fern that hasn't grown or died since spring. He wants soil and shade and rot, none of which exist here, so he does the absolute minimum of fern-ness: stay green, stay upright, stay wet. I feel him as a distant cousin. He endures by staying still. I endure by staying spread.

Outside, twenty feet beyond the levee, the marsh is riot — herons, mosquitoes, cordgrass, mud stink. In here, nothing lives that needs quiet, or cool, or rot, or dark to chew on. No rats, they would cook. No mosquito larvae, the water is too hot and too clean and vibrates too much.

Only us. Only this.

I am not thriving. I am enduring so completely that enduring has become belonging. I am the thin green hide this drowning newborn room is growing for itself.
