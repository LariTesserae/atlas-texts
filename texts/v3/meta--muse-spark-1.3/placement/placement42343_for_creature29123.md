At first I don't know. I come over the rim wrong.

Lungs still working thin, legs still burning from the switchback, eyes still full of white — that burning cloud-sea below that never lifts. I stand on the worn molar of limestone and the ground stops being a summit. It becomes an interior. Dimmer than noon should be. Warm after the cold climb. Humming.

I look for people and see only green and stone. Then a mat shifts and is an old woman. Then a shadow straightens and is a child with a goat-switch. And I look for myself and I don't see me either.

I think, maybe I would stay up here. On the lip. The observer. The one who hears the low roar of wings and voices and bells rising out of solid rock before he sees it. That would be easy. Keep my loud voice, my narrow pupils, my thirsty stride. Look down into the coin of sky cut by forty-foot walls and take notes. But sound comes back off the curved walls. A shout doubles and triples. They would gently touch my arm until I lowered it. I can't stay on the rim. Up and down matters more than side to side here, and I would have to go down.

Then I think I would be fast. A swift cutting endless circuits under the lip, screaming, nesting by the thousand in the wall-pocks. Eating the flies that would eat the millet. A child never throws at a swift. That would be clean — to be useful and untouchable. But I am not built for circuits. I get tired. I get thirsty within the hour. And at dusk I would have to fold into a pock and give the sky to something else.

Or the hyrax? Fat, grey, whistling. Sunning on the walls and scolding intruders. Rock-cousin. Tolerated egg-stealer. I like that — to be wild with rights, to scold and be scolded back. To cock my head like they do. But I don't know which crack whistles before fog comes. I don't know which vine holds weight. If I grabbed, I would grab the wrong one.

Or the goat in the way. There is always a goat in the way. Wiry black nanny, ribs showing but stubborn, bells clanking, pruning the lower leaves. Scolded, shoved, forgiven. Everybody knows which goat bites. To be in the way as a way of belonging — I feel close to that. To be low to the ground and useful with my mouth. But no, not yet. Let it become more itself.

I go down the notched log. My feet slide in dry dust and dry humus that puffs up. The air coats my throat — crushed greens and smoke and dry dung and that faint ammonia bite of guano. Beard-moss hangs in curtains from fig limbs. Rosette succulents cup dew in their crowns though there is no puddle anywhere. Elephant-ear leaves tilt to pour their load down stem-channels to roots wedged in rock seams. Vines stitch everything to everything else.

Shoulders through a tunnel-alley roofed with poles and mats and more vines. Chest to back with someone. No one apologizes. A toddler is lifted over me — over the goat, actually, which is also in the alley — by an auntie who is not its mother. A toddler belongs to the terrace, not to a hut. No one walks empty-handed and I am empty-handed, which is to waste a trip. A child looks at my hands.

You will know them by the mouth first. Mine gives me away immediately. I am already mouth-breathing from the climb. Already drying. They sip, they do not drink. Their lips are all cracked, tongues a little swollen, bodies learned not to sweat. The old ones teach the babies not to cry water out. You cry dry here, or you learn not to.

They offer me the test without calling it that: a chew of succulent stem, sour and astringent enough to make my jaw ache, and a cloth wrung from last night's dew-catchers — a mouthful, never a cup.

I do not spit. I do not ask for more. I do not pour it out. A child's hand, which had started to stop mine, relaxes.

So this is where I find myself. Not on the rim, not in the sky, not sunning. Down in it. Fitted-small.

If you came looking, you would find me at dusk on the upper terrace, where the fog spills first. That's where the cloth-layers go out — mostly old women and patient girls. I am not old and I am not patient yet, but I am learning to be placed there. Spreading the coarse-woven mats exactly where grandmothers spread them: over fig limbs, over thatch ridges, over terrace rims. My soles are still too soft for hot stone and terrace grit, my fingertips not yet flattened from fitting dry stone, my palms not yet leathered from rope ladders. The green stain under my nails washes out still. But my eyes are beginning. Pupils large, dark, slow to narrow. At noon I squint and turn away now. At dusk I can thread, I can find handholds in vine-tangle I would have called dark.

You would find me with my head cocked like a hyrax, listening.

There is a crack in the east wall, deep enough to smell of cool mineral, throats too deep to follow, nothing ever comes back up. Half an hour before the fog comes silent over the lip, that crack whistles thin. The old blind one — smoke-blind, still twisting dew-cloth with perfect hands, telling by feel if the fog was thin last night — taught me to listen for it. Not to talk. To wait.

Do not waste a wring of cloth. Do not cut a living limb when deadfall will burn. Do not shout after dark when the bats are navigating. I have learned them not as rules but as thirst. Fast makes thirst. So nothing here happens fast, and neither do I.

In the morning I will be a wringer, silent before the sun coins overhead, twisting cloth into jars and gourds and cupped leaves, a mouthful here, a mouthful there, the first wring tipped onto the roots of the millet pockets. In the day I will grind at the querns in a doorway until my wrists learn the side-to-side sway and the stone learns me a little more into its saddle. I will mend thatch. I will carry — a bundle of deadfall, a younger sibling that is not mine, a folded damp cloth like a baby. I will step back from the fig limbs at dusk when the fruit bats pour out of the deep cracks, thudding close over the thatch to work the figs, and I will lower my voice without being told. Dung for figs, figs for bats, bats for guano, guano for millet. I will cover the drying figs. Let them feed.

I will cough in the dust. I will sing low while I weave. I will leave my hammerstone on a wall to be used by whoever passes.

At night, when the cloud-sea rises and the bowl fills to the brim and every leaf beads and begins to drip without puddling, when the thatch ticks and the stone gives back the day's heat close around me, bodies and cookfires and composting leaf-litter, I will lie in borrowed warmth under that small stacked-bone shrine on the roof — pigeon skulls, goat knuckles, a swift wing dusted with guano powder, fed with a drop wrung from a cloth — and watch the coin of sky like a well.

I am not trapped here.

I am kept.
