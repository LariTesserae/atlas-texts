I don't walk in. I kneel.

That's the first truth, and it stays true. My back finds the bolts before my eyes find the birds. If I try to straighten, steel corrects me. So I stay low. I shuffle forward on knees into the cool and the dust packs my nose on the first breath and I learn to breathe through my mouth.

At first I think I would be up high. Everyone thinks that.

I think: I'd be on the long pipe. Shoulder to shoulder, slate-blue, two black bars, white rump tucked under. I'd learn the eleven-minute calendar. When the shake-down auger kicks — clank-shudder up through teeth — I would not flush like something wild. I would lean in. Puff. Half-close. Let it dust the mites loose. I practice it in my head and for a second I can feel the vibration coming up through the soles of my feet, through the guano-crete, through the pipe.

No. I'm lying to myself. I still have a wingspan of panic around me. When the sheet lifts — that hot dusty thunder that hits the chest — my hands go up. My throat closes. I am too wet, too loud-breathing, too single. A pigeon here has forgotten how to be alone. It sits pressed long so more can fit, tail overlapping like shingles, neck oiling green and purple under the caged bulbs, orange eye swiveling, unafraid, proprietorial. I am afraid of being touched that much.

So maybe lower, smaller, faster. Maybe I would be a sparrow.

I'd steal a crevice instead of holding space by bulk. Inside the dead motor housing, a fist of feather and wire-clipping and plastic tie. Chestnut and ash, black bib if I'm male, tan streak if I'm female, quick as a needle along the floor margins. Chirp-cutting over the coo-pour. In and out of the pallet cliff-town in the corner where guano has cemented wood into terraces. Squeezing into the upturned hard hat that holds three speckled eggs.

I like that for a minute. The idea of being useful-small. Unnoticed. But I'm not quick. On my knees in a polished bowl in the concrete I am clumsy, loud — skitter of knee on metal, tick of falling husk too loud under me. The sparrows stitch the gaps between pigeons and I can't stitch anything. I take up too much gap.

So maybe I go to the margins. Where it's thinner.

Maybe I would be a rat.

Sleek, dry-furred, bold in flat gray light because there's nowhere to hide so hiding is obsolete. Moving with surgical quiet on soft pink feet along the edge where the mass thins, nose twitching in ammonia-sharp talc. Cleaning. The dead squab. The addled egg. The spilled cropful. I could do that, I think. I could be quiet and necessary. I drink no water — there is none — I drink bodies. I learn the foraging shifts, wait by the broken louver where the dust is beaten lighter by wings for what drops.

But I am not surgical. My breathing is ragged from dust. My knees ache. The rats pass near my boot and ignore me with a competence I don't have. They know what falls is eaten below. I don't yet know how to eat what falls.

Sit still longer. That's what the room tells you. Keep low. Move on the auger's time, not yours.

The work-bulbs buzz. The transformer hums 60-cycle on the back wall. The extractor wobbles, pulling that slow dry draft from louvers to dark. Every eleven minutes, clank-shudder. Coo-pour underneath like water would sound if there were water.

I settle into one of the ancient bowls, where boots first then claws wore the pour down, where guano-crete flowed and rimmed like cooled lava. If I press my palm flat it holds the cold and gives back a faint warmth from underneath where decomposition works without moisture. The young and the grounded shuffle around me — squabs too young to fly, too old to be brooded, gray fluff squeaking, stumbling, getting stepped over, pecked, fed crop milk and regurgitated corn. Naked, blind, yellow-skinned shouting at first, then pin-feathered like hedgehogs. All throat and belly.

I feel very much like them. Grounded. Peeping, in my way. Throat dry. Eyes watering in dust and learning to keep the wet inside, because everything that is wet here has learned to stay inside a body.

And then the mass reclassifies me.

It doesn't happen all at once. A pigeon lands on my boot. Grips with worn-short claws. Eyes me with orange, copper fire around black, and stays. Not predator, not pigeon. The breathing weight flows around my shoulders. Another shifts onto the pipe just above my bent back to be closer to the lee of the transformer where the steel stays five degrees warmer. My shoulder is warm too. Almost five degrees.

That's where I would find myself. Not on the pipe, not in the motor housing, not out the louver with the foraging parties. Low, on the floor, knees in a bowl near the back wall but off to the side where the margin runs — where rats work and moths lift in silver flickers when a bird shifts. Back bent under the cross-brace, mouth-breathing, covered in fine salt-sharp dust.

And what I would be — it finally gets itself clear — is not flock, not flyer.

I would be structure. Warm structure.

Like the pallet become cliff-town. Like the upturned hard hat holding three eggs because it stayed still and held its shape. Like a second pipe that never got the memo to stop humming. The hens lay deliberately near warmth, and I have a little warmth to give. If I stay long enough, still enough, low enough, something could be laid in my lee.

When the auger kicks, I lean in now too. Not to dust mites. Just because it's the calendar here. Feathers puff somewhere above me. Eyes go to slits. Wingbeats stall for a frame in the flicker of the bulbs pulsing just under wingbeat frequency — hanging too long mid-air — and then normal again.

Cool air. Dry throat. Warm stink of bird and hot oil and old concrete.
