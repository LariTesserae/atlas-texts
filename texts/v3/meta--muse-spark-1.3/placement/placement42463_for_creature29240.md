Not in the center. Not at first.

At first I thought high — because everyone looks up first in that bowl. The herd goes over like weather and your neck bends back before you can think, mouth open, teeth aching. To be a glider seemed right. To lock out handkerchief wings and ride the standing eights without ever flapping, belly full of sun, gut glowing pink through the skin. To be silent in a way that makes silence audible. To strain the air and let the rest fall as white powder.

But I'm too heavy for that. I felt it immediately on the path down, boots ringing on fired clay. I carry water in me — blood sloshing, breath smoking. A glider has no water anywhere in it. I would fall straight through their lift like a stone through surf. The reeds would not catch me electrostatically mid-tumble. I'd hit.

Then I thought low and green. To be one of them. A chime-reed, cocktail-straw thin, foot clenched around a pebble, not rooted, just anchored. To have each frond tuned to a different sliver of the roar, wrist-hum and eye-water whine a finger's width apart. To light faintly when the permanent gust comes through and smell sweet to yourself — cut grass with no grass, cucumber and copper. To tick audibly while growing.

But I don't hum right. I detune. When I put my hand near the dense clump the hairs stood up and my fillings sang, yes, but my bones answered wrong — a dull sympathetic throb, not a shiver. Break me and I wouldn't go gray and silent at the edge. I'd bleed. I'm wet. I need to drink and there is nothing to drink. The reeds leaned toward me minutely, curious about my tremor, and then decided: discordant. Warm mass. Not food, not yet.

Then, briefly, I wanted to be herd. A shiver-moth the size of a thumbnail, kite-built, never touching the dry ground. To shear the vibrating tips six inches above the surf and beat deliberately out of phase, stealing lift, giving back that tearing overtone. To condense without signal into a vortex and strip a sweet patch white in seconds. To be nibbled and keep flying, lace-winged, maimed and still aloft.

But I tire. I need to land. My ears were already full of moth-dust and my eyes watering from the whine, and I understood I couldn't live as weather. I kept sinking.

Cheek in the dust, then. That's where I found myself.

Down where you're tempted to say nothing lives. Salt polygons ringing hollow under the knees. Glass-mites and salt-fleas the color of ground, freezing when my shadow fell and becoming ground. The smell of hot metal fading. The deep thump under the fast roar coming up through my palm on the drumhead center — the mountain hollow-throated, wind sucked up from the cloud sea far below. I lay there too long, reeds whipping my face, a needlewasp docking somewhere overhead, and I felt the bowl play me.

And then, crawling — because you crawl here, you don't walk for long — I found the fallen arch.

You don't see the seven at first. Tripod of black glass half-swallowed. Low ring of copper slabs blurring. The tilted pylon where dust falls up. And one arch down on its side, salt-caked, lichen bearded over its waveguides. Under it, a lens. A pocket where the roar cancels perfectly.

I slid into it on my belly to get the moths out of my ears.

And for a terrified second I heard my own blood.

That's where I would stay. That's who I would be.

Not the glider, not the reed, not the moth. The still one. In a place whose second rule is *nothing holds still*, I would be the exception tucked inside the rule — the thing that lives only where the song stops.

Let me become more itself: I am small. That's first. No bigger than a pollen grain herder, no bigger than the scale on a shiver-moth's sail. Pale as the polygons, the color of anhydrite dust, with a back like frosted glass that pings faintly when salt hits it. I have no wings — wings are for stealing sound, and there is no sound here to steal. I have six dry legs that move only in millimeters, and combs on my forelimbs for rolling. Blind, mostly. Eyes are useless in the surf-flash. I navigate by absence of vibration, by the sudden slack in my leg-hairs when the cancellation holds.

Because outside the lens, nothing ever reaches the ground whole. You know this. A torn moth is caught mid-tumble by static, combed down into the thicket and unmade in hours. Glider dust is zapped up before it lands. Reed shards are caught and regrown white-to-green in minutes. Nothing lands.

Under the arch, in the lens, things land.

When the pocket holds — sometimes minutes, sometimes only breaths — the air goes truly still. The copper-cucumber sweetness drains away. The static lifts off. And then it sifts down: moth-scale, glider preen-cuffs shaken loose when a big one hangs in the null to let the field comb it clean in crackling bursts, a single reed-shard that drifted in dead and gray, salt that forgot to fall up. In the rest of the bowl that powder would never touch. Here, it falls straight, in the silence, and ticks on stone.

I eat that. I roll it. I am a hush-mite, a null-keeper. I comb the fallout with my forelimbs, roll it into pellets no bigger than skin cells, leave them in a neat arc at the edge of the lens where, when the pocket collapses — and it always collapses, a glider darts through, a gust of the permanent chord shifts — the reeds at the rim can zap them up. I am the cleaner the cleaners miss. The ground crew for the grooming station.

When your shadow falls, I freeze and become ground. When the pocket collapses with a crack like ice, I cling to the underside of the fallen waveguide groove and let deafness wash back over me like surf. I go deaf with the rest of the world for a while. I wait. My body hums down from blood-loud to stone-quiet.

I do not drink. I unlearned it getting here. There is no seep under the arch, no damp shadow. There is only dry powder and charge-leak from the lichen overhead — dried-blood orange, burnt orange, creeping over the not-writing, dulling it, sharpening it. Sometimes I rasp a little of its salt beard when no fall has come. It crackles faintly when I approach. It doesn't mind. We are both slow in a fast place.

Above me, when I dare extend one eye-stalk past the edge of stillness, the world is all tremble and flash: surf waist-deep shivering, clouds banking and catching sun through ten thousand wings, translucent bellies with dark gut-pellets crossing like brief eclipses. I feel them go over as a pressure drop even in here, a cold prickle at the boundary. The reeds at the rim lean minutely toward my tremor, then lean away — too quiet, not enough to eat. The moths test the edge, brush it like dry snow that never melts, and will not enter. The herd will not follow into silence. Only the old gliders come, to hang and shudder clean.

Stay too long in the bowl and your teeth ache in sympathy. Mine don't. I have no teeth. I have a rasp like the smallest file, for dry sugar in fallen scale. My bones — hollow chitin struts — don't hum. That's the point. I am tuned to the null the way a frond is tuned to a tone.

Find me twenty paces up toward the teeth and you won't. I stunt out with the reeds, gray and brittle as old paper. Find me in the hollows where sound pools strongest and you won't. I'd shatter. Find me only here, under the downed rib, in the hamlet's one quiet room, where for seconds at a time you can hear your own heart playing a drumhead far slower than the roar — and then the roar returns, feeding the green, feeding the wings, feeding the roar, and I freeze, and become ground, and wait for silence to fall again like dust.
