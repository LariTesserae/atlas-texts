If I came here I would come tall at first. I can't help it.

I would stand on the lip where you don't see the bowl until you are almost at it, with my breath white in plain white-gold daylight, and I would see the town plan below — the honeycomb of curbs and centers, the gravel streets turning at right angles for no reason except ice — and I would think I was empty. A city with no citizens. Nothing in it but wind-bass and meltwater chatter and the soft thunk of ice shoving a slab a millimeter higher.

That is how it wants to be seen by the tall.

So I would have to kneel. That is the price of admission. Knees into cold wet, face down to within a hand of the ground, and lower my voice without deciding to.

At first I think I would be one of the Painters.

It makes sense. I eat light and stone too. I live off sun and fog and dust on a hard substrate, a millimeter a year in a good year, exhaling something so weak you would need a century to taste it, and yet underneath me the rock pits. I rim the walls. I make soil out of stone for everyone else to live in. I die into dust and the dust washes into the centers.

But I put my fingernail close, I feel those black cilia holding on against wind that would peel paint off steel, and I know I am not patient enough for that. A thousand years old on an upright slab, remembering when frost jacked it vertical? I chatter. I evaporate. I cannot persist like that. I am too thirsty.

Then I think I would be a Matron.

A cushion the size of a bread loaf, three hundred years old, hard as wood, as muscle. Hundreds of shoots packed so tight wind cannot enter and water cannot leave. Put your palm just above me without touching and you feel it — a degree, two degrees warmer than the air that hurts your nose. Each dome holding its own summer. Seedlings germinate only in my lee. Mites overwinter in my thatch. I swallow my own dead and make my ground higher.

I want that. To hold warmth. To shelter. But I have no warmth of my own to hold. Only borrowed heat. My summer leaks out the moment you take your hand away. No, closer, lower, wetter.

I part the moss with my fingers — haircap with stems like red wire, star moss, peat swollen pale, liverworts like dark green tongues, all beaded with water that never fell as rain — and inside it is a miniature forest. Trunks, canopy, understory. That is where I start to recognize myself.

Not on the walls. In the centers. Not in the soil. In the breath.

Because water is not a river here. It is a breath. Condensation that pools at night, rime feathering the stones, fog pouring over the low collar, and by midday a dark mirror the size of your hand here, a black wet gravel throat there. Drip, seep, tick. For three hours an ocean. Then gone. Then contact-lens ice by dusk.

You would find me there, if you looked with a lens, in one polygon a little off the long alignment of ten, twelve cells in a row — near a moss-roofed thufur that hums if you put your ear to it, where the melt lingers an hour longer. Ankle-deep in the Forest, under a haircap leaf tip where the bead runs down red stem to rhizoid to soil. In the temporary sea.

I would be a Bear.

Barrel-bodied, eight-legged, lumbering through the film like slow cattle through high water. Claws finding purchase between algae grains. Coronae of rotifers spinning beside me, nematodes threading white between soil grains, cyanobacteria in black jelly beads fixing nitrogen out of thin air for the mosses to steal. I graze. I lumber. I am almost too slow to be called moving, and yet if you lie with your cheek near the moss you hear my world popping — thousands of moss capsules flipping their lids open in the sun to sift spores a millimeter.

This is what becomes more itself the longer I stay: the daily unmaking does not kill me.

When the pocket dries by afternoon, when the fog spills back over the collar and the light flattens to pearl and the green darkens from emerald to black-green and closes, I do not die. I tun.

I pull in my legs. I make a glass of my own sugars inside my cells. I go dead to every test. Dry for a month. Frozen for a year. The Jumpers are already under the slabs — white as salt, popping like static. The predatory mites, blind, feeling with long front legs, have stopped feeling. The Matrons are holding their two degrees against the night. The Painters are already rimed white. And I am a speck, a dust grain, a waiting.

Then morning. The stones tick as ice lets go in their pores. Rime melts and runs in filigree. One drop finds me.

I unpack myself and walk again in water that will be gone by evening.

That is where I would live, and what I would be: low, not up but in, in the center that frost sorted for me, in a sea the size of a hand that exists for three hours, alive and almost-not-alive several times a day. Damp wool and stone dust and green iron smell. Light too clean so orange buzzes against green. Cold in the fingertips while the face is sun-warm.

Leave a footprint in the moss beside me, and by morning frost will have lifted it smooth again. I will still be there, under it, waiting for the melt to wake me.
