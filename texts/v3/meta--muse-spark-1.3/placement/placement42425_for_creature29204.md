First I think I would be standing. Upright, on the rim, where you put me — boots on young glass, squinting.

It doesn't hold. Nothing upright holds here for long.

The light gets in under my eyelids and the cold gets in under my coat, and within minutes I understand I have made the tall mistake. I am shedding heat in every direction into thin blue air. My breath hangs and settles and freezes on my sleeve. I am a gap. A cold gap shaped like a person. And gaps are not allowed.

So I get low.

Not all at once. The herd teaches slowness by simply continuing. A grazer beside me shifts an inch to close the space my legs made, sighing moss. The fog — that knee-high glowing breath-fog that never lifts because there are always more breaths coming — closes over my boots. The bass drone gets into my sternum and slows my own breathing down to match the ten-second vents. Puff. Puff. Puff. Sulfur and hot oil and cut grass.

I thought for a while I might be a strider. High on stilts, staying in the sun, vanes spinning. I like the idea of making static, of humming. But I am too heavy for that kind of patience. A step started now finishes in a minute; I would fall. I thought I might be a kite-thing, hanging wingtip to wingtip on the herd's thermal, never flapping. But I have no wing stretched tight as a drumhead, and I have no thermal of my own. I make no weather.

I thought I might be a grazer. Low and immense, furnace-bellied, piston-ribs flexing *in* — whistling roar — hold, heat — *out* in sideways jets that ice the moss for a yard around. But I cannot furnace. My gut is not hot enough to keep snow off my own back. My teeth are not mill-plates. I cannot chew basalt grit for ballast and hone myself on it.

What I can do is be small and warm and move in the tunnels.

You would find me, then, not on the horizon where the white eats distance, not looking out at far peaks. You would find me down in it. An inch off the stone where the cold lives. In the warm air trapped under a barrel body, between columnar legs ending in broad heat-pads. Knee-deep in glowing fog. Beside one of the little black mirror-pools no bigger than a dinner plate, where things with gills and iron jaws churn the breath-water.

That is where I fit. In the allowed gaps.

What I am, there, is at first a runner.

Dog-sized in spirit if not in body. Furnace-hot by comparison to the air, though barely lukewarm to them. Fast only by comparison — which is still deliberate. I learn the dart from leg to leg, the press of flank hard against chitin to steal heat and to give it. Warmth is currency here, and I am briefly rich. A real runner detours to lean against my calf for a moment, and I lean back. We exchange. Condensation beads on my coat. I lick it. I nip a parasite from a skin fold because there is nothing else useful to do with my hands. My cheeks — if I had cheek pouches — would be bulging with seeds carried from back-meadow to back-meadow: olive moss spores, black lichen flecks, bright-green shoots waiting in the air for a place to unfurl. I carry them without meaning to. In my seams, in my hair, in the damp wool of my socks.

I stay still longer than a runner should.

That is when it changes. When what emerges becomes more itself.

An hour, and the spore in my shoulder seam unfurls. A bright-green shoot seeking the killing sun. Frost formed on my shoulders by mid-morning melts by noon into droplets that never fall, just hang. Tiny white birds that are not quite birds come to walk my lace and pick ice to drink. The moss under me sighs.

I stop darting. I sink. I become almost immobile, sunk between two grazers the way the domes are sunk. Mouth open — not to eat, but to receive. The slowest work of all finds me: the bucket-line. A grazer chews, ferments, brings it back up steaming, leans an inch to the left. I hold it for an hour. Warm, hydraulic, stinking of peat and hot metal. I churn it slowly, the way thought here is slow as geology. I pass it on an inch to the right. It can take a day to move a mouthful ten feet toward understanding. What comes out of me, extruded in a long steaming rope, is instantly colonized. Something already waiting unfurls on contact.

And then the join.

I have been standing too close for too long between those two shoulder plates. The chitin softens. Flesh meets. Moss grows over it. A bridge thick as my arm, pulsing with blood and hot oil, links my furnace — my small, borrowed, human furnace — to the next furnace and the next. Put your hand on my seventh neighbor down the line and you will feel my heartbeat coming from far away, enormous and slow, rippling through all of us.

I am not an individual, not strictly. No one here is.

I am a place where heat is shared. A warm pocket budgeted in. A back-meadow with iron-grey underneath if you part the ferns with your hands. A listening lichen turning very slightly to track the blue crackle when a strider passes overhead, lifting the hairs on my arms.

Shoulder to shoulder. Flank to flank. Back to back. Pistons pumping, mills grinding, vanes clicking, domes churning. The work of staying warm, staying fed, staying packed, continues at its patient, deafening, freezing, brilliant pace — and I continue with it, steaming in the bright cold, fitted tight, with no product except more packed, breathing life.
