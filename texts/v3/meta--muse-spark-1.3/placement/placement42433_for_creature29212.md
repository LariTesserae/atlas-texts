At first you think you would still be you.

You, walking. Upright, loud-booted, with dryness still remembered in your bones. You think you would keep to the high-looking places, keep your feet as dry as they can be here, keep your eyes up to judge distance. You think you would be the watcher — the one who sees the crab, sees the stilt, hears the bloop underneath and writes it down.

You don't last an hour like that.

The roar gets into your molars first. That white fused tick-and-ring of rain on stone on shallow water, so many drops you stop hearing drops at all. It fills your sternum until your own thoughts have to shout. Your skin never dries. Sweat beads and stays. Breath comes heavy, sweet with rot and warm stone and iron. Your feet are wet in the first ten steps and then wetter, because from ankle height it looks like dry cobble and from standing height you realize every step breaks a mirror and the mirror heals over your boot.

So you crouch. Everyone crouches here, sooner or later.

You think, then, maybe you would be the stilt. That impossibly tall grey patience standing a half-kilometer off in a pan. That would be a relief — to be above the noise, to have legs jointed the wrong way twice so your body never bobs. To hunt reflections instead of being hunted by them. You try it. You stand very still in the drizzle between rain-columns and stare at a palm-sized pool until it goes glassy.

It shows you a sky that isn't the sky. Clearer, darker. Stars at noon. Your face looking up, half a second late. You wave, and after you have lowered your hand, it waves.

You blink. And you understand you could never be the stilt. You are too hungry to be that still. Too lonely. To stand as a post while a Low One lies as a cairn and a Turner sleeps under both — listening to each other through stone — that takes a kind of cold you don't have. You look away first. When you look back the pan has clouded brown from below and there is a tall print filling with water at its rim where nothing is standing.

So you go lower.

You think maybe you would be the crab. That would be simpler. White as boiled bone, blind, wedged face-down in the hairline seam between three egg-stones — ochre and rust and liver-brown, shoulder to shoulder, no soil showing. No eyes to be fooled. Only knock to navigate by. You lie flat, cheek almost in the film, and you listen under the roar for what they listen for: the tick upward as heat expands stone, the soft knock as one rolls a centimeter, the different ring each pool makes depending on how deep the mud is below.

For a while that is enough. Filtering mud with mouthparts too fast to see. Reading the whole continent as tones. When the roar goes higher, thinner — water rising from below, not falling from above, the pavement about to lift and float on its cushion of mud — you feel your whole body want to lock, to wedge deeper, to throw your legs around two stones at once and go rigid and become a clasp holding the world together.

But you are too big for the seam. You can't live face-down forever. Your back aches for sky-sweat. Algae — olive, blood-red, black, stretched like wet leather across the cobbles — tears under your knee and by evening it will have crawled closed again, fizzing faintly, breathing out rotten-egg breath. You can't graze it. You can't filter enough.

You think, in a night moment when the amber drains to iron blue and the water holds last light long after the sky has gone dark, that you could spread out instead. Be the Turner underneath. Vast, flat, soft as a village pan is wide, thin as the water table will allow. No light, no roar down there, only pressure and low thunder through stone. Creeping through black silt on three-lobed cilia, mouths underneath, shifting a centimeter a year so that whole fields of cobbles creep above you. Pushing three stiff feeding lobes up against soft spots to test thickness — leaving from above what looks like old three-toed footprints wide as dinner plates, appearing after rain and washing out before they can be followed.

That tempts you. To be weather. To have the rain-columns follow your heat as you move, droplet against droplet with no empty air between. To turn over in your sleep and make mud boil up in slow domes that burst with a plop.

But you are not vast enough to sleep like that and not be afraid. You still want hands.

And then the mirrors get you. Dead calm between rains. Horizons double — a second, faint horizon floating a meter above the true one, an afterimage of when the sea stood higher. Slow phosphorescent smudges drifting against the wind out there, going out if you look directly. You kneel by a table-sized pan to drink and your double tries on your shape, half a second slow, learning hands. For a second you think: be that. Be Late. Thin as film, wide as a pan, remembering a different sky with two smeared suns and projecting it upward to drink. Going out when stared at because direct gaze ripples water.

Too thin. You still weigh something. You still break mirrors when you step.

This is where you find yourself, finally. Not walking toward a beehive cairn that stays exactly as far no matter how you walk while the pool-pattern repeats. But curled *as* one.

Low. Broad. Splayed.

Somewhere on a slight thickening in the mud that is the highest place for a hundred days in any direction, you have gathered the lag — egg-stones, loaf-stones, each one touching the next — and fitted them shoulder to shoulder with seven or nine small, child-sized hands around your mantle edge. Stone on stone, balanced by weight. No metal, no glass, no cut timber. Lift, feel, set. Lift, feel, set. A low dome no higher than a chest, doorless, already sinking, water pooling at its base.

You seal yourself in from the inside for the worst white column marching sideways across the flats.

Inside it is suddenly, blessedly, less. The roar becomes a hum through seams. Your belly — wide, pale, a vibration drum set flush on three egg-stones at once — can finally hear under it: crabs clicking a map of tones, a stilt stabbing three pans away into a pan it never stood in, the long grinding sigh before the pavement floats a handspan sideways and takes your sense of north. Deeper still: the Turner turning under you both.

Your back, knobbled and crusted with true stone and living algae, is the cairn others will walk past and call abandoned. Let them. Black rush has rooted into you where a little silt lodged. A rag you tied to a petrified post outside flutters before your skin can feel the pitch change, wicks salt, glows faintly at night so the Late will try the rag first instead of you. You do not trust reflections. You built no door because doors would reflect.

You lie still for days. You breathe through seams as water wells up. You listen.

Your feet are wet. Your ears ring to fill the gap where silence should be. Stone to stone to stone, pool to pool to pool, going on until going on is the only direction left — and you are no longer going on. You are holding on, low and patient, shifting a stone a centimeter with a soft knock, retuning the world, deciding whether the footsteps breaking mirrors toward you are rain, or crab, or kin.
