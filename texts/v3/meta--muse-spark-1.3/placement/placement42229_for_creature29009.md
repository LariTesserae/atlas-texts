I wouldn't be big at first.

If you put your eye to that slit under the bark, I am what you mistake for something else. For a shiver in the water film. For a bead running down a stem. For weather.

At first I am only cold and wet. That could be anything here. Everything is cold and wet.

Am I the drip? I thought so, for a moment. Swelling on the underside of the bark roof, holding the rot-glow, getting heavier and heavier until I can't hold. *Plik.* Falling through black air between worlds. No — I am underneath the drip. I feel it hit two leaves above me and shatter and come down on me as second rain. It nearly knocks me off the road.

Am I the pillar? The great moss-trunk I cling to? For a moment I thought I was that tall, that rooted, drinking through every leaf. Pale green to amber to blood-red at the crown far above where I will never climb. No — the pillar is too still, too vast. It grows a millimeter a day and lifts my whole street as it grows. I live *on* it, not as it. I feel its slow heave under my feet.

Then what moves like this?

Low, close to the slime. Belly almost dragging. Six points of touch going flick-flick-flick, never stopping, tasting cut-grass and mushroom and foot-smell and fear-smell laid down by ten thousand before me. The road is glossy here, polished in the algae between Pillar and Pillar, diverging, meeting. I know it by tongue, not by eye. I have almost no eyes. Light hurts. Light dries.

I am white.

That's how I know. The color of never-needing-color. Skim-milk, mushroom-flesh white, and if you held me up — you can't, your breath would crush me — you'd see my gut glowing yellow-green through my belly wall. Pollen and fungus in there. Last night's chewing turned to today's blood, thickening now with glycerol, with antifreeze, because winter is in my body even if I don't think the word winter.

I have a tube on my belly that drinks straight out of wet air. I have a folded knife under my tail. Everyone I know has one. When the red blur comes, we are simply gone. *Pop.* Air, and an empty place where a sister was.

Right now I am on the home road, low on the flank, between the black peat-tea at the foot and the first balcony above. You would find me there if you learned how to look small enough, in the column.

I am carrying home a thing bigger than my head.

A yellow pollen grain. Rolled before me like a boulder, clutched in my jaws until my jaws ache with that far-away sanding sound. Ten thousand of us make that weather. Rasp, rasp. Antennae touch antennae as the outbound sisters pass. That touch is everything: *food this way, ice shifting that way, sac down by the needles.* No one speaks. We lay smell and follow smell.

Above me, on the shadow side where I can't see but can feel as a cold spot, She is hunting. Rust-red fur holding water off her in beads so she glows. Velvet mite. Wolf. Her front legs taste my vibration. I don't look up. To look up is to stop, and stillness is death. To stop moving in this cold is to freeze to the leaf. I keep pushing my boulder. My forelegs slip on the skin of water that covers everything.

I am not young. I have molted enough times that my shed skins are somewhere in the wall above me — pressed papery with frass and leaf pulp into that balcony that slumps now because the pillar grew and shifted it. I helped chew the new one two leaves higher. I held the splinter-shovel while an amber sister, armored chestnut, slow and deliberate, daubed fresh moss pulp into the joint. The pulp kept living. It sealed green. The house healed itself into the tree.

I know where the dead go because I will go there soon. There is no elsewhere. When I fall — cold, or fungus, or a misstep under a drip — my sisters will be on me in minutes. Not mourning. Butchering. Belly for food. Back shield, head capsule, leg for wall. If you look close at the mouth of the store-sac I am hauling toward, that translucent sac swollen with black spores and yellow, stoppered with resin, you will see it faced with pale plates. Ancestors as bricks. I have walked past my own grandmothers a hundred times and tasted them with my antennae.

The peat under the road ticks. A needle of ice lengthens, glassy and sharp, and topples — oh, not again — a pollen sac by the pine-needle ladder. The road empties for a breath with sharp almond. Alarm. Then fills again. A crew is already there, chewing it upright, re-lashing with fungal cord. I veer around them, my boulder wobbling.

Then the god-footstep. The whole dome sighs. Bark above groans, the thatch of crowns compresses, a drip is shaken loose. Shrew, vole, your knee shifting on the sphagnum mattress far above. My pillar sways like a cathedral in an earthquake. No one stops. We can't afford the poetry of stopping.

My exhale — if you can call it that — condenses in the saturated air and rains back onto me. Your exhale, huge and warm beyond the roof, does the same, only larger. You are weather to me. Your cheek on the peat is a season I feel through my feet.

I push on. Leaf to peat, peat to root. Vibration tells me more than any eye: drip gathering on the bark lip above the town, swelling, holding light for a moment like a lens.

*Plik.*

It falls. The town glistens.

And I go on.
