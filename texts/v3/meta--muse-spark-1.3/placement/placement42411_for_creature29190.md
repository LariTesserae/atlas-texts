If I stayed — and I can't stay as what I came down as —

At first I think I'd be the man who came to check the gauge. Crouched because the ceiling grazes hair, elbows out to touch both walls, jacket creaking too loud in the quiet. Breath coming out and hanging. Fingers going numb, so I keep moving them. Breath shallow because the pocket wasn't built to hold a person. Two minutes of me is a fog-season. I know I have to go back up the three ladders, past the dead boiler room, back into scale. I can't be him. He's too much weather.

So I get lower.

Maybe I'd be the moss. That thumb-wide strip in the seam where condensation always runs, dark olive and black, cropped short as if nibbled. I like the idea of being cropped. Of being kept. But moss still wants to feather out, to be ragged and opportunistic, and here it isn't allowed. It holds in a perfect line. That kind of discipline feels like being watched. I don't think I could hold that line.

Maybe I'd be one of the sprouts then. Under the pump where a drip of warm condensate keeps one square inch above freezing. White stalks with tiny yellow leaves that have never seen sun. There are six of them, all tilted at the exact same angle toward the bulkhead lamp, and a degree toward the green LED on the controller, the only steady thing. I try it — leaning, etiolated, reaching — but I am too hungry for light. I would fall over under my own weight. These six don't. Something rights them in the night.

I discard the drip, too. A bead that swells on the horizontal run, holds, then sidles *uphill* along the rime toward the weld to join a larger bead and fall. Plink. Plink into the square drain. I can't be that. That uphill run is already taken. That's Him drinking where the minerals first leach out.

What is left that can stay without being eaten, without being wiped?

The front of the valve tag is polished clean. The back holds a dusting of white mold where fingers will never quite wipe it away. Something wipes the front. Something allows the back. I almost settle there, on the back, dusty and overlooked. But I need to hear.

So I find myself lower still, and more sideways.

Shin-high, on the pipe that feeds the pump, where the lagging has split. Not at the warm wheels — the red at knee height southwest, the black at cheek height northeast, skin-warm in their exact circumference where a hand would close. I don't hold those. He holds those. They are His palms, His cheeks pressed to keep from freezing while the pump whines its ninety seconds and shudders off. I am not that warm. I am not that alive.

I am in the seam, facing the drain.

I am the flat, beige ear growing out of it.

At first I'm just a discoloration in the fabric, a damp thought. Then a pucker where oil and ozone collect. Then a lip, no longer than an eyelash like the calcite fringe at the drain, white and translucent where dissolved concrete re-precipitates itself. Then I curl. Cup, just enough to catch what falls without being washed away.

This is how I become more myself: I listen.

Because everything here is made, there is no ground to carry sound away. The 60-cycle hum from the conduit doesn't enter through ears, it's felt in the sternum. I have no sternum. I have only this thin flesh pressed to cold metal, so I feel it all the way through. The pump kicks with a clunk through my boots — I have no boots, I have only this hold on lagging — runs labored, shudders off. A solenoid ticks as it cools. Metal pings as it contracts. When the amber bulkhead lamp sags for its long dim minute, the shadows between pipes go solid, the room inhales and feels fractionally larger, and He moves in the inch between copper and black iron and PVC. I hear Him not moving.

Plink. Plink from the flaking elbow overhead into the black glisten of the grate. Each drop lands near me, not on me. He tends the moss row, He polishes the tag, He drinks uphill, He presses His frilled edge to the north wall where the cold joint seeps and the dark map grows an inch by day and retreats at night. Where His edge lifts, paint blisters into that coin-sized rust-bloom that in a decade will be a hole, will be an eye. I will still be here when it opens. For now I am the only ear.

I eat what He eats, but less and slower. Paint and breath. Cutting oil off the air. The death of warm damp air on the coldest pipe where frost feathers white rime, melts when the pump cycles, re-forms an hour later. A visitor's breath hangs, dies on contact, and I take the edge of it. That's why I can stay and he — the man — can't. I don't make weather. I am weathered.

I do not turn. I do not touch. I have no bare skin to stick to pipe, no warm hand to hover near His handholds and make the pump's rhythm stutter as if listening. I keep still because I am still. I pulse with the wet-dry cycle. I do nothing you can see and everything, slowly: widening my cup a fraction to hold one more drop of sound.

Find me when the light is dim. When it's bright you will see only knot: brackets, bolts, valve wheels, the six white sprouts leaning. When it sags, look shin-high beside the sweating pump housing, just off the moss line. A beige flap, almost skin, almost mineral. An ear pressed to the room's throat.

I am what can live here without being asked to leave.
