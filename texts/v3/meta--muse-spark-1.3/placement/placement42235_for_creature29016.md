I don't arrive. I equalize.

At first there is only the weight — not on me but in me, three or four thousand meters of black water finding every hollow and filling it until inside and outside are the same pressure. My edges go soft. The cold, which I thought would be weather, is the medium. It gets into the joints of thought and thickens them. I think at the speed of oil.

I thought I would be falling. I'm not. There is no surface to fall from. There is only more water in every direction, snow coming down through it without pause, and far below the idea of below, the clicking floor.

If you came looking for me, you wouldn't look up. You'd look low.

Not on the swell itself, where the contour current runs fastest, clean and heavy. In its lee. Where the water stumbles over a rise no higher than a sleeping animal and eddies, and drops what it was carrying. That's where the pavement is thickest — nodule on nodule on nodule, black-brown potatoes fitted so tight there's no mud between them, each one grown a few millimeters in a million years around a shark's tooth, a chip of bone. Stone touching stone for miles.

For a long time I thought I might be one of the big lonely ones. It would be easy, in all that dark, to want to be large.

I tried on the glider in my mind — the rattail a meter long, tapering to a whip, moving just above the stones so slow it seems to be conserving the idea of movement. Pausing an hour between strokes because every undulation costs a month of snow. Turning all at once toward a vibration no one else can feel. No. I don't have that patience of hunger, that kilometer-wide solitude. There isn't enough in me to go that far alone.

I tried on the still ones. The stalked sponge a few centimeters tall, leaning a degree or two down-current, furred with silt, sieving shit and skin for a century to grow a thumb's worth. The crinoid furled like a fist for days, opening its feathers only when the flow is exactly right. No. I am not good at staying. I fidget. Even here, slowed to two degrees above freezing, I fidget.

I tried the waiter — the tripod fish standing half a meter up on three impossible stilts, blind eyes filmed over, facing into the flow and feeling with its whole side for a copepod to tumble past. Days in one spot. Weeks. Snap, and then standing again. Closer. I know that feeling, that standing-against. But my waiting is not that pure.

What I keep coming back to, in the grainy gray my eyes invent to fill the black, is the movement almost too small to see until it stops.

Bone-pale. Walnut-sized body. Legs like needles.

You'd find me wedged.

My back is in the crack between two nodules. Behind me I have jammed a broken clam shell — dead a thousand years up in the light, fallen for miles to get here — like a chock. Without it the current would take me. With it I can lean. Two of my back legs are braced against manganese. The rind is rough under them. My front claws are busy.

At first I thought I was just holding on. Then I became more myself: I am holding *something*.

A flake of chert, no bigger than a fingernail, sharp where pressure split it. Not made. Found. I grip it awkward in two walking legs — it trembles, I tremble with the effort to hold it steady in this soup-thick water — and I draw it across the veil.

The mat. Dirty-white, translucent, no thicker than mucus, stretched low over the clay where methane sighs up from the old crust. The closest thing this valley has to pasture. I shear it. Again. Again. Thin parallel scratches brighten on the black nodule underneath where my knife slips. I stuff what I shear into my mouthparts. Hand to mouth. Stone as extension of claw.

This is the whole of my making. I don't carry the flake to the next mat ten meters away. I don't cache it. I don't build a wall or a burrow or a midden that will outlast the snow. When the veil is gone I will drop it. It will be buried, tumbled ten meters in the next benthic storm when the snow stops falling down and flies sideways and the nodules shudder and roll and the sponge stalks bend flat and my shell-brace goes with everything else. Then, if I live, I will find another.

I know the others around me now not as sights but as pressures. The sponge-posts up-current fur the water slightly. Half a meter above me the tripod fish stands facing the flow, waiting out its weeks. Somewhere out in its kilometer the rattail budgets a stroke. Behind me a blind white isopod the size of a spread hand bulldozes silt, eating mud for the organics, leaving a furrow already filling with fresh snow. Above us all, once, a comb jelly tears itself open in blue-green sparks and is gone.

I don't see them. My eyes are almost nothing. I feel the quickening on my face before the storm, the hiss that is silt moving, the faint click as the pavement resettles with a slightly different pattern.

If you stay, and I have stayed because staying here doesn't mean building, it means persisting thinned out and slowed down — if you stay, you understand: fullness doesn't need abundance. Age doesn't mean peace. Turbulence doesn't need noise.

You'd find me in the lee of the swell, between two stones that touch, leaning into cold that never lifts. I am the pale crab who borrows a knife and puts it down.

I pick up. I scrape. I drop.
