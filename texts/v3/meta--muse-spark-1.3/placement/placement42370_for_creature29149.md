At first I think I would be standing.

Ankle-deep like you, in skin-water that is warmer than air, with my clothes already ruined in minutes. Human-high, face in the gnat soup, exhaling tunnels that close behind me. I would try to be the witness — the one with hands to feel the animal warmth in the rock, ears to separate the drone from the bell-tones from the drip. I would try to keep my feet still enough to feel my heartbeat go late.

But I can't stay that tall. Not here.

I'm too heavy for this place. Everything I step on gives. If I stand, I am always standing on someone — on moss felt, on a sow's feathered edge I mistook for bottom. My weight clouds the water. My breath makes holes. And nothing here builds platforms for weight like mine. No dry place to put down notebooks, no slope to stand back and see. The light never arrives to see by. If I stay human I will just be a sweating column for the lizards to avoid, listening to my own blood until it embarrasses me.

So I get lower.

Maybe face-height in the ferns? There are thousands to an acre with their throats pulsing — orange and lime and wet slate, bodies touching. I could be one of them. Riding, not walking. Drinking seep from skin. That appeals to me: to be small enough that the world is only shingle after shingle of frond, drip coming down regular as time-keeping, plink plink-plink, and my whole job to snap the midges out of the vortices before they clog someone else's lungs.

But I'm not quite a throat. I'm not quite a bell-tone. My sound is lower. If I were a frog I'd have to sing in key all day and glow at the belly when disturbed, and I don't glow, I make heat without light.

Lower still, then. Into the water film. Finger-long fish mouth-up, leeches inching along stems, black ribbons of tadpoles. Or into the white lace — the mycelium that takes a leaf in hours, the pillbugs the size of thumbs that arrive before the veil has drifted a foot. To be digestion itself. To be the reason rot does not accumulate.

That is closer. To be the cleaner that is fed with skin so it doesn't eat the animal alive. To pour over in living chain, to carry green cuttings many times my size to a home that will be gone by next week. Everything clings because nothing can build, and I understand clinging.

But even that is not quite where I find myself. The cleaners live *under* the rim, in the dark warm where belly meets basalt. I find myself wanting air. Wanting to breathe the tea through lifted lungs, not water that holds no oxygen.

So I rise again, only an inch. Only just out of the water.

There — on the low mossy dome, acid green and black-green, beaded continuously. In the two loose rows of white finger-things waving almost imperceptibly. Between the second and third plume on the left side, where the branching makes a fork, there is a shallow cup that holds bath-warm water even when she hauls out onto a basalt nose. And in it, something that is not hers.

I am the sitting.

Small, dark, red-throated, the kind that never leaves the basin. I do not weigh enough to cloud water. My feet are wrapped around a breathing organ. With every almost-imperceptible wave I am lifted and let down. Under me, through thin shell, I feel her extra-late beat go through basalt and skin-water — fruit-plop low — and half an acre away another rim lifts a fraction in answer.

My egg is wedged in her breath. Not my nest — I don't make those, they would rot by morning — just a perch in the fork of someone else's lungs. I sit, and the gnats she needs eaten come to lay near my warmth and the riding frogs snap them. I sit, and condensation beads on my back exactly like it beads on her moss and on the rock beside us, so from above I am ground, from below I am sky. I sit, and the drone holds, and the frogs keep time, and the drip keeps coming down, and the sow bulldozes inches per hour toward lilies I will never see, vacuuming leaves with a soft heavy plop I have heard a hundred times today.

That is where you would find me if you knelt and put your hands in until they disappeared: not looking up at you, just sitting on a traveling pond, on a puddle with intention, on a mother who will be lace and ants by evening.

And when she dies — which is soon, weeks, growth is furious here — I will get up, confused for a moment where my fork was, and fly a few feet to the next low dome already bulldozing in from the ferns. And sit. And then leave. And another will sit.

Presence temporary, but the amount never drops.
