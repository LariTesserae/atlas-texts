You would not find me at first.

At first I would be trying to be the wrong thing. Up on a trestle, maybe, thinking I should be a pipe-walker — rubber boots to the knee, one hand always on the rail, sure-footed at a quarter-mile distance. Or down on Foundry Row at noon shouting into someone's ear to be heard, still being shouted over. I would hurry. The air is bathwater and I would try to hurry in it anyway, the way newcomers do, sweating straight through my shirt by ten in the morning, hair stuck to my skull, lungs full of that white roar. I would look for faces in windows and find none and think: maintained but not inhabited.

Then valley speed would find me.

It wouldn't be a decision. It would be heat and weight and the high silver ringing starting up behind my ears after a day. My inner ear giving up and inventing its own counter-tone to cover the roar. I would put the felt plugs in — one plug in while working under the flumes, both plugs in at table, cord around my neck like everyone else — and I would begin, without meaning to, to listen with my feet.

Through the soles of my boots: the faint perpetual tremor of turbines turning deep in rock. The change in pitch when a penstock is loading. Vent coming — fist tapped twice on the sternum — *kreee* peeling out every eleven minutes and dissolving back. *Vent, work. Vent, rest. Vent, wipe your face.* My body would set itself by it before my mind did.

I would learn to move slowly so you would miss me. I would learn palm flat for *stop*, two fingers walking for *go check the upper weir*, thumb jerked at the cliffs for *seep rising*. I would learn to stand for a long time between utterances on the far cinder flat until someone mistook me for a post.

And I would crouch. That is when I would start to become myself.

Not up at the cloud standing still over the western cleft, not out at the sweating rocks where the galleries drink straight from stone. Low. Down where the small, patient lives are. Orange slime breathing metal in the standing pools. Grey-green lichen holding to north-facing brick like a held breath. Rust-weed with wire-curled leaves hot to touch, rooting in mortar. The blind-pale minnows flickering in schools under the lime skin in the settling pond, livers swollen from dye and heat. The pair of egrets standing unnaturally still above them for an hour, yellow eyes half-closed against glare. The children know not to throw stones. I would learn that too. To stand and not throw.

You'd find me, finally, where speech lives.

Not on the street — no one shouts for long, it's useless — but in the white tile. Every mill and tenement has one, floor to ceiling, drain in the middle, low benches, stone-drip showers running warm as blood, the roar coming through the walls only as distant surf. People linger long after they are clean. Old men talk in low ordinary tones about valves and grandchildren. Mothers actually sing, thin songs you can only hear with tile around you.

I would be the one who stays in there too long.

The keeper, the lingerer. The one with pruned fingertips and lime dust worked into the knuckles that never leaves, teeth going yellow-brown early from the mineral, eyes half-lidded against puddle-glare. I would sit on a wet bench in steam and take the notes passed on greasy paper that won't disintegrate. I would learn to lip-read with uncanny precision and then, in the quietish room, to not need to. I would wash and re-wash and listen. Lovers meet there. The council makes its real decisions there, in towels. I would not be on the council. I would be the damp towel handed over, the ear that remembers what was said low enough to be true.

Outside I would be almost silent, waving across a quarter mile of gravel and puddle to a neighbor I will not meet for a week. Inside I would be all ears.

On wet festivals I would be the one who ducks through the glistening mouth of the chapel near the south seep — now more mineral than church, pews furred white with calcite — to leave a candle. I know it will get entombed. The wax gets entombed too. Tending, not production. Chipping, scraping, painting, unclogging. Freeing a doorway for a week before the travertine narrows the alley to a footpath again.

That's what I would be: not an engineer, not a geologist who measures and frowns and leaves. A warm-blooded condensation. Part of that slow animal that learned to wear boots and read lips. Steaming faintly after rain like the streets do. Hand warm and damp on a handrail that is warm and damp.

Nothing here will change while you watch. I know that. I am in no hurry.
