At first I think I'd be up high.

On the berm, where you can see all of it at once. Boots still on gravel-cinder, not yet slick concrete. Coat still dry at the shoulders. That's where a person like me would stop — a visitor with green still on his cuffs, grass seed from the marsh road — and look down and say: herons, mussels, engine, keeper. Catalog it. Stay out of the drip.

I don't stay there.

Because the bowl pulls inward and down. Everything here does. Water in the joints finding the lips and dropping. Condensation forming first on the undersides. Even breath staying a little too long, sinking. If I stand on the berm long enough the fog snags and stays, and I get heavy and cooler, and my boots want the next step down.

So maybe lower. With the herons?

I try that for a while. Twelve grey posts each guarding five feet of rim. Motionless as borrowed wood. I could be the thirteenth post. Tall, patient, tide-adjusted eyes. It appeals — to not need to tend, just to stab when something moves. To be a visitor the keeper has decided is furniture.

But I'm not good at stillness like that. Herons don't shiver. Herons don't listen in their teeth to the transformer hum. And when the pump gulps and the level drops four inches, the herons lean forward all at once with a hunger that is clean and single. My hunger isn't clean. I want to touch the orange mat to see if it's velvet. I want to know what eel fat and slime feels like on a flywheel shaft. A heron would never wipe a discharge mouth clear.

So not a heron. Not a gull either — I don't have that constant low turnover in me, land and stare and stab and lift. Not an eel, wrist-thick and slow in the center channel, nosing only the inner ring. Not a crab, though I like the crabs, packed olive and missing legs under every slab, clicking sideways over each other. I tried being a crab in my head for a minute, testing laces, grazing only the outer ring because the yellow heart has to seed. Too quick. Too many legs.

Lower.

If you crouch long enough for the cold to get into your knees, the density resolves. That's the instruction the place gives you. So I crouch. By the stenciled 7, half-legible under calcite. There is a boot print there that is still damp at the toe. Not all the prints are old.

I think: I could be that. The fresh print. The person who came down yesterday and wiped once and sorted once and left orange silt under his nails. An hour-keeper. Someone the real keeper watched from under the catwalk and decided was neither food nor engine nor stone that needs sorting. A post, briefly.

But even that doesn't quite hold, because my shoulders dampen through and the midges find the warmth at my collar and I don't get up to go back to the marsh road where grass actually grows. I stay. The herons decide I am another post and resume fishing around me, and I let them. The cold calcite beads under my chin first, under my elbows. I start to hear in hum.

Where would you find me, then?

Not on the rim. Not in the pumphouse doorway where the rat watches with wet whiskers — though god, I understand the rat, pausing on its way somewhere drier. No.

Find me half under the catwalk, where the legs have grown thick with mussels until they look like stalactites. Where there is a shape that is not leg and not tire and not pipe, ochre and rust-red and mustard and oil-black. Next to it. In the other hollow.

You remember: once there were more. You can see the other smoothed places on the terraces where a broad back rested, the other hollows under the corrugated sheets. Now the rings are one keeper's width. The rest have settled fully, become foundation.

I would be in the second hollow. The one that hasn't fully smoothed yet.

Who would I be? Not the keeper — I haven't earned the carapace, the cream calcite laid down in smooth aprons over the shoulders, hard as tooth. My back is still mostly back. Still too warm. When I lift to shift from one warm seep to the next my underside is pale all over, not just underneath, shrimp-colored, new, unmineralized. My nails still clean out too easily. My breath still smells faintly of green.

But I am learning to stay low. Like the place is low. Not standing. Spreading.

The keeper comes to test my laces first with a crab, then itself, low, without lifting its belly clear of the thin sheet moving across the terrace. Cool, gritty fingertip on my toe. Smelling the rubber, the marsh road still on me. It decides — not yet. Not food. Not engine. Something that might be stone, later, if sorted right.

So it lets me watch and then it lets me do. One slow circuit. Wipe the travertine fingers from the discharge mouth so the brown gush stays clear. Smear the shaft. Pick wrack from the belt before it catches. Nudge silt to center where bloodworms need it, shell to edge where crabs hide. Crab-tick and silt-sigh and *plink... plink... plink-plink* as water drops off me back into the pool. No wasted sound. The fog mutes and I learn not to.

In winter the rat sleeps curled in the hollow behind my knee. Two heartbeats, one fast, one slow as the engine — *chuff... chuff-chuff... chuff* — and my flank trembling faintly in time before I even notice I've learned it. Midges column over me where I am warmest. Crabs graze loose mat off my sleeves and do not pinch.

I still leave sometimes. Up the slick steps, over the berm. I wring out my cuffs. I find orange under my nails and iron in my coat for hours. But I come back down because the level drops four inches when the pump gulps and somebody has to move down with the crabs, unhurried, and somebody has to rest when the pump rests, shuddering and sighing, while cold marsh water slides grey back over orange. Twice a day when the real tide overtops and everything closes up tight, I am learning how to close up tight.

I am not the keeper. I am what the bowl keeps while it makes a keeper.

Give it fifty years. The barnacles meeting across the spine. The travertine closing over the shoulders. A natural formation with a person inside it like a fossil.

For now, find me low, damp through, weeping the way the walls weep, grazing only the outer ring with a flat tongue because the yellow heart has to seed, sorting pebbles by weight without thinking, humming very faint in my molars.
