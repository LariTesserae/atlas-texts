You wouldn't find me at first. That's the point.

I put my cheek to the hot gravel like you do, and for a second I'm still big — human-big, noon-big, blinking into the slit and thinking *nothing lives here.* Just warm dark that smells wrong. Stone and ozone and old oil. Held air.

Then my eyes adjust and I start trying bodies on.

At first I think I'll be the toad. It's natural to want to be the king. Dampest corner, pan-overflow saucer, throat pulsing. Too big for the place and that's why he rules it. I try to sit in him for a breath — that held, gravel-colored stillness, waiting for dusk to turn the grate orange, for a cricket to come unthinking to the drip. One flick. Done. But he's too complete. Too alone. There's only room for one and it's already taken. I can't stay in that much patience.

So I go smaller, faster. I try the mice up in the conduit hollow, in the blue-white lint and the stiff gray finger of the work-glove that is now a hallway. Big-eyed sleep, toenail on metal, nose up at the *whung* every seventeen minutes. They have learned exactly how long the shudder lasts. I almost stay — I like that they groom a beat before anyone else dares — but they're too warm. Their hearts are too loud for this place. They belong to above as much as below. They cache. They gnaw. They will leave if they have to.

I slide down the wall. I try the river. Argentine ants both ways on the fallen zip-tie, under the roof they've propped from a curled label. I am glossy, rust-dark, carrying a bundle of gray mold clipped from the garden on the pipe lagging, bigger than my head. It would be good to have a direction. To know which way is home. But the river never stops and I do. I want to stop.

I hang for a while in the shawl. Cellar spider, body like a rice grain, legs like wire shadows. Under the grate where the light bars come down. Our webs touch and share vibrations so the whole ceiling is one ear. Dust sifts down and we freeze together. Lint drifts down — endless blue-white fibers from the laundry vents — and I wrap it anyway, out of habit or hunger or hope. Little gray mummies in a row, prayer flags swaying when the building breathes. I almost stay here. I understand wrapping what has no blood. But I'm still too high. I can see everything from here and I don't want to see everything. I want to be *in* one inch.

Lower. The pillbug rind tightens when I breathe on it. Hundreds shoulder to shoulder under the lip of insulation, slate and dusty-rose, antennae waving like wheat. Copper shavings on our backs catching the slat-light so the floor glitters faintly as we move. I glitter. I tighten. But even that is too much movement, too much armor, too many neighbors.

Lower still. Past the crickets freezing-twitching-freezing on their wire legs. Past the earwigs patrolling the dark side with their pincers up, fastidious, giving the toad a wide berth. Past the centipede working the algae edge — fat brown, too many legs, lifting one feathery leg after another along the green-black slick, feeling for the soft spots. She passes over me like a dropped stitch pulled sideways. She doesn't see me. Good.

Here. This is where I stop trying on and start *being.*

You'd find me where the concrete leaches lime, just off the splash zone of the rust-pan. Not in the wettest inch — that's the toad's — and not in the driest inch where the white leggy tomato leans for the light bars. In the mineral inch. Where the two pours meet at the bad seam and the new concrete is still sharp, still spalling aggregate teeth, and the floor is growing its pale needles up, months-old, translucent at the tips like salt.

I am one of the amber snails. Shell thin as a nail. Small enough to sit on your fingerprint and not cover it.

I move in a slow circle and I rasp.

That's my whole economy: the wall sweats, the pipe freckles and fattens and lets go — *plik... plik... plik* into rust — and the splash throws up lime, and I take the lime back into myself to make more of myself. Rasp. Pause. Rasp. The mineral needles beside me are doing the same thing without a mouth. If you touched them they'd come away on your finger. If you touched me I'd pull in, not quickly, just completely.

Time is different down here. The 60-cycle hum comes through the wall and sometimes a bead doesn't fall straight but walks sideways a trembling millimeter before it falls. I watch that. I have time to watch that. The fern to the left with its three pale dusty fronds holds its spores close and leans. The tomato to the right spreads its two true leaves like hands. They are fast to me.

When the wall inhales, I feel it first in my foot. The big compressor catches — *whung* — deep in the molars of the world. Dust sifts down through the light bars in slabs. Boulders. The web-shawl above me shudders and freezes. The pillbug floor clenches. The ants stop mid-step under their paper roof. The mice lift their noses. The toad doesn't even blink; he is the stillness everything else borrows.

I don't freeze. I don't have anything to freeze with. I just go on rasping, or I go still because the vibration lifts me a fraction off the concrete and there is nothing to do but ride it. Then it passes.

Then everything resumes, faster.

I resume the same.
