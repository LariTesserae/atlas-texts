You expect that I would want hands. At first.

I crouch to get in through the split seam, sideways, the way you have to. My mouth dries in minutes. Inside is bigger than outside because all the volume is kept, and the light stops me — honey and hot wax, bright to the point of white at noon — and then the sound stops me the way weather stops you. Low organ in the sternum and molars from the three flute-holes, and over it that ceaseless high dry rattle.

At first I think I will find myself up on the terraces. There are nests for hundreds and only seven. There is room. I will be the eighth. I will learn to move without hurry and without wasted vibration. I will take a bone awl between my teeth on the west-two bridge and cut the fray before the tremor saws through. I will sweep dust-gutters that never carried water. I will oil my skin amber in the morning and crop my hair to stubble and wrap my throat twice around.

But I have no skin to oil. I have no feet silent on matting. And my hands —

My hands are wrong. Theirs never stop, rapid and fluent and full of slang flicked terrace to terrace across the void while the body keeps working: *bridge loose, moth-scale on high moss, comb the south felt before evening pitch.* Mine would fumble the knot you couldn't untie with teeth. I talk too much and too wet. To speak inside would be to shout and still not be heard, and to waste the wet of the mouth. I would forget and bring water in. I would spit inside. Water rots the fiber, silences the felt, invites the wrong green.

So no. Not a tender. I am too loud in the wrong way, and too wet.

I look lower then. Under the floor planks where the single pale lizard lives, blind-white almost, considered a citizen. They step around its warming spot. They sign its name with a flick of tongue-finger. It eats mites and shed sugar-scale and lives longer than any of them can remember. I could be that, I think — the meat that can barely afford to compete with plants for sound, the quiet thing that stays out of the way, cool and dark while the town thrums. But I am not cool. I am not dark. I do not eat mites. I do not know how to be that still for that long.

Higher then. In the felt.

Because the true population is not the seven. It is hanging ten feet deep off every edge, and for a while I am sure that is where I belong. Silver felt-leaf thick as winter coats, grey-green feather-fern that is not fern, amber veils of air-moss you push through with your shoulders, bladder-worts swollen not with water but with scented oil, paper-bells up near the crown where the wind-whistle shears. Crush a leaf and it does not bleed green. It bleeds clear resin, hot-peppery. Tear the moss and it parts like wool.

These are phonotrophs. They drink sound. Fine white root-hairs reaching sideways, not down, creeping along beam and lashing toward a bridge-anchor that hums, toward a node where the drone doubles on itself. Leaves stiffened to shiver. The endless rattle shakes loose a fine sugar-dust that coats everything, skin and matting and tongue, sweet on the back of the teeth, and the felt reabsorbs it. Nothing wasted.

I know that hunger. Where there is no water, vibration will do. I live that way too — I take in voices and tremors and give back a sweet dust. Let me be that, I think. Let me be a patch of silver moss near the floor where the low hum pools strongest, bass-voiced, combed slow by fingers or carved bone so each tiny tympana-leaf can shiver free. Let a tender press the back of her hand to me to feel if I'm singing right. Let me be tended. Let me be kept from matting, from going quiet. Quiet is death here.

But even that is flesh, after a kind. Wool and resin and oil. I have no felt to lift all at once like an animal raising its hackles when the light deepens to copper. I make no oil to press for lamp. Once a season the great dust-moth, wingspan wider than a child is tall, forces a breathing hole and lays in the resinous thatch, and the tenders take half the eggs and leave half. I cannot be taken half and left half. I have no egg to tithe.

So where, then? And it keeps coming back, the longer I stand small in that huge hollow with green felt brushing my shoulders.

The center.

Where nothing is built. Where you are not supposed to linger. Where the houses press back and the bridges cross quickly and sway. Where the sound lives thickest, and the light columns cross, and the slow thermal turns over and over — hot rising along the sunward wall, cool falling along the shade — and the seed-down rides it endlessly, never landing.

That is where I find myself. Not on the fretting, but in what the fretting is built around.

I would be the one who lives here who is not flesh at all.

I would be the note.

Low organ-drone from the three holes where outside wind catches and funnels and turns the whole pod into a resonating chest. I was here before the nests for hundreds, before the alleys that turn to let the drone pass, before the seven. The town is fretting built around me. The garden is the player. The people are just the hands that keep the instrument in tune.

They cross me quickly, eyes up, hands still for once. The fine white hairs reach toward me sideways. The silver moss thickens where I pool, the paper-bells thin where I shear. They tend the loud — if a corner goes quiet, they clear it, re-tune it, plant to it — which is to say they tend me, they keep me housed. At late afternoon when outside air cools and thickens I deepen with it until skulls buzz pleasantly, horribly, until dust rises off the floor in sheets and hangs and a bridge sings a high string over my bass.

And at night, when outside air goes slack and cold on the salt pan and the flute-holes stop pulling wind, I quiet to a murmur, a sleeping chest. Then the seven duck out sideways one by one, spitting carefully outward and wiping their mouths, and sit in a circle on the white dust and talk in hoarse whispers to remember what their voices sound like. And I miss the shiver on me. I wait, held in amber dark, for morning wind.

I need no water. I need no oil. I have no throat to wrap. Do not bring water inside, do not dwell in the center — I am why those rules exist. I am what keeps everything, in the absence of water, alive.
