At first I thought I would be the one crouching.

Behind the three courses of slab, out of the wind, gloves stupid with cold, choosing a flat stone that fits the hand. I thought I would add and leave. That's what you do here. You don't sleep here.

But I can't keep that breath. Three steps and I'm hammering. My eyes keep making the lowland mistake — looking for a tuft, a smear of green, something to soften it. There is none. So I can't be the visitor. The visitor leaves. I don't want to go down.

Maybe, then, I'd be gray.

Find me where the ice thins, on the slightly higher, drier gravel. Not in the deep white center where you have to turn sideways, but at the edge where the two crowds meet and from a distance you mistake one for the other.

I would be low, fist-high to start. Five flat stones, then six, then seven. Warm granite glittering with quartz. Someone set me carefully once so I would survive the night's cracking, and someone else righted me after frost jacked me a millimeter sideways, and someone else will. Add, don't take. I grow denser every year. In the afternoon cloud that pushes up from thousands of meters below I grow a white beard of rime on my north side and by evening I shake it off. I tick.

But no — that's too tended. Too answered. The gray crowd is an echo. I want to be the sound itself, not the echo.

Whiter, then. Let me be whiter.

You'd find me now in the middle of the bowl, not at its edge. Knee-high where the snow drifted thinnest, so I am young. Thin as a hand. I am still firn, not yet glass.

I was not fallen. I was grown. The sun hollowed the pits around me and spared the ridge that is me, over and over. That's my birth and I can feel it at noon, white and total, violet-blue overhead getting into the seams. I feed. I lean a few degrees north, toward midday, the way plants would if plants could live here. All of us lean. Shoulder to shoulder.

This is more itself. This posture. This crowding.

Around me the older ones are waist-high, shoulder-high. They shade me with hard-edged blue shadows that look wet, three-deep across each other. I shade the gravel in turn. At two in the afternoon, when the cloud feathers everything furry white and then drips for an hour, melt gathers at my feet — an inch of sky, impossibly clear. I hear it before I see it: plink, plink, threading down through me into stone in rills a finger wide that run three meters and vanish. The pebbles under me click against each other as they are stood on end by no hand. The wind threads through the thousand narrow slots between us and we hum, low and reedy, a pitch that changes if you tilt your head.

At sunset I learn what I am for.

The light doesn't just get golden. It gets trapped. My west face goes translucent, honey then rose, lit from inside. Each of us throws a long blue shadow across three others until you lose depth. The little pools that haven't yet skinned over into black glass catch fire. The granite flecks spark. For a few minutes the whole bowl glows from underneath, as if something were breathing under it, crowded with presence though nothing lives, though no bird nests, though nothing rots or roots or buzzes.

Then the sun clears the rim. White goes blue-white, then gray. The hum stops. The water glasses over. I tick like cooling coals as I lock again.

I don't live here. Nothing does. Freeze every night, even in summer. Ultraviolet like a blade. Wind that sharpens what remains.

But I am busy. I migrate slowly south over the season as the sun eats one side of me and the other holds. I get undercut. One noon I will make that sharp *tock* and my tip will snap and fall into the pit to melt. Until then, I stand.
