Not on the bridge. That's where I start, because everyone starts there — come up out of the low cloud and it is already around you and you stop where you can see it all at once to try to understand it. Breath showing. Jacket darkening. Hand coming away cold and slick from the rail.

But I wouldn't stay there. It's too high and too exposed, the wind moving the mist through in rags, the whole iron span humming faintly with the turbines far below. My fingers go white on wet metal in a minute. My boots squeak too loud. And looking is not a job. Water finds the next level down whether you watch it or not.

At first I think I would be inside. In one of the long sheds where the windows glow milky and the looms thud. It looks warm in there. It looks like a place to be useful with your hands.

No. I listen too much and I move too much. I'd hear the shuttle going off-beat and I'd stop to listen and I'd miss the throw. And behind every loom wall is a bunk with a curtain drawn, somebody's brother off nights trying to sleep through the day thud, and I have not yet learned how to be that quiet. I still clear my throat. I still want to ask questions in full sentences.

Maybe a porter, then. I like the trot of them, the files bent under baskets that steam faintly — wet chard, grey yarn, coal, eels knotting silver-black. I like the way they don't say mind, just click tongue or tap the basket rim twice and the person ahead tucks to the weep-wall side without breaking pace. I try it for a half a terrace in my head and I fail it. I stride. I take the center channel where the water skin runs. My calves aren't stairs yet. On the dished, moss-slick steps my loose boot would go out from under me and I'd take three terraces of somebody's kale boxes with me.

So I go down. That's what you do here anyway. You keep moving to stay warm. Sideways a little on the steepest stairs, one hand always trailing the rail or the wall — not for balance so much as for reading. The rail tells you who passed, how cold it is, whether the flume above has risen. I learn that slower than a child. Children learn it before speech: keep to the dry side of the stair, which is never dry.

I don't belong in the magenta dark, either. On the third under-terrace the mushroom caverns breathe earth and bleach, the family in there white-eyed, almost translucent, tending pale shelves in artificial dusk. I love dimness but I need the weather on my face, the spit of rain moving through, the tarp pulled and pulled back. And I don't belong waist-deep in the lower sumps with the eel boys and grandfathers, hands quick in black water, waders patched. My hands go numb too fast. I would drop them.

Narrower, then. Lower than the bridge by four, five levels, where the light is just late-afternoon-all-day and the sodium dots burn at noon under the eaves. Between a dye-works and a pump station where the market alley folds and unfolds — stalls on wheels and trestles because no pitch is permanent, because the drain beneath must be rodded at 3 p.m. Above it, my half-pipe. My grille. My length of gutter.

That is where you would find me.

Not a boss. Not the foreman with the oil pencil behind his ear, not the header-tank keeper with the wrench on a lanyard who can tell by vibration through her boots if No. 2 is cavitating. Just the keeper of that stretch. Dawn and dusk out with the hooked pole and the hand scoop and then bare hands in the cold rush: lady-fern fronds, potato tops, silt black as ink, once a drowned pigeon. At 3 p.m. I have the rod-man's trot — we all clear in seven minutes, wheels rolling back, trestles folding, because if you block a drain for more than minutes your neighbor's roof floods, and your floor is someone else's ceiling and someone will be up through your hatch with a bucket and a look.

My hands have given me away by the second week. Cuticles stained permanent green-black that no scrubbing lifts. Nails short. Gloves with the fingers cut out because you need fingers even if they go numb. Sweater over sweater smelling of lanolin and smoke. Oilskin apron cracked at the folds, patched with bicycle-tube rubber, tied high. Boots laced tight — I stop to re-tie without thinking now. Hat that sheds. Face the color of skim milk, reddened at the nose from damp cold.

And because there is no ground to plant in — pry up a slab and you find another room, dig and you hit culvert, then turbine hall — I grow the way everyone grows. Up. On. In containers. I inherit, or am lent, ten feet of south-facing mill wall that everybody fights over: tiers of boxes, pallets, sacking planters. Potatoes earthed up in ash and leaf mold. Kale gone leggy in the dim but sweet from cold. Straw bales tucked under the stairs fruiting oyster mushrooms in pale shelves. Runner beans up strings from cut oil drums. On the milky window of the tally office where I sometimes sit to mark chits, tin cans of herbs that the condensation waters for me. Old men show me how to prick out seedlings in eggshells on a sill. I leave exactly enough moss to keep the quiet — scrape it with a flat spade in sheets for packing and mushroom bedding, but leave the inch-thick springy carpet where it is tended and walked. A doorway mossed over is a shame. Mine isn't.

I am learning to hear the valley the way they do. Not muffle, but information. The change in the shush means leaves blinding. The pump kicking on two levels down means the inspection sump on Nine is filling again. The funicular sighing below in the mist. The transformer buzzing like a trapped bee. I work quiet because sound hangs off wet block and wakes three shifts. My language has gone short and low. A hand up means hold the sluice. Two fingers flicked means pull the tarp, rain coming through again. My name cut to one beat. Laughter sudden and brief and covered with a hand.

At noon I queue under tarp with the others, breath showing, enamel mug held in both hands to warm them, barley nettle eel soup, spoons clinking on tin. I don't eat walking on stairs. I stand aside, out of the water lane. At night I sleep behind the loom wall on army surplus blankets, curtains heavy, moss rolled in wax for earplugs, while the day shift thuds. In the morning there is rheum, chilblains. My knees ache. My forearms and calves are getting strong.

If you asked where I'm from I'd look puzzled. From two levels up. From the dye-works terrace where I rod. I came up the funicular once and the mist closed over and the stairs got into my legs.

I am not a worker who happens to garden. I am not a gardener who happens to work machines. I am what happens when you keep the water moving long enough that the moving keeps you.
