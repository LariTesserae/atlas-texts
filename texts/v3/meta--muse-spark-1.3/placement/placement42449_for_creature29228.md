I don't find myself all at once.

First I am only wet. That is not a place, that is the condition. Rain standing, not falling. Flood to the knees, then to the ribs. Sweat and sap and steam the same. Breath slower drinking. I think I am the one wading, boots finding warm metal under the soup, teeth aching before the pitch changes —

no. Turn that around.

I am the ache in the teeth.

I thought I was standing *on* the mat until it let me through. Bright drowned green that dulls the rain-sound. Water lettuce, salvinia knitted thick enough to hold my weight a second too long. Then the hot suck, the knit closing behind. From above, raft. From below —

that is where I am. Underneath. Six feet down in the hanging forest in reverse.

My beard. Pale root beards swaying in the street-current that used to be a street. I filter. There is no mouth here but mine, and mine is not a mouth. Petals, pollen slick, oil sheen, bright orange peels of fresh rust flaking off rebar, my own dead — I take it all. My smallest bladders, who in a saner swamp would be waiting for fleas that will never come, pop open for bubbles instead. Steam pearls off the outflow. A belch of trapped gas. I digest a mouthful of broth-water rich in iron and phosphorus and close again. When enough gas builds I lift, I rip, I vent with that cannon-boom that comes back too early from the wrong direction, and I knit closed like flesh. If you stand on me too long I will knit over your boot. Not from malice. Growth is mandatory.

But I am larger than beard.

Follow the beard up to the stilts holding the banks of the water-alley. Red root-mangroves and white wire-mangroves side by side and I can no longer tell which I am. Prop root arched down from six feet up: some wood, some two-inch pipe furred in moss, some a braid where a vine spiraled tight around a conduit and the conduit sweated into the vine. That braid is me at my most me. Seedling-spears dropping point-down, splitting slag-mud, splitting rebar with slow hydraulic push. Creak, tick, pop, if you put your ear close — which you can't, because there is never quiet. Rain on ten thousand drum-leaves. Diesel throb in the sternum.

When the transformer hut surges I brighten.

Oh — this part. The leaf-margins. Door-taros between the stilts, one leaf per plant big as a door on a stalk thick as a wrist, shedding rain in sheets. Noon is amber dusk here, the cloud permanent, so I learned to eat the other light. My edges glow faint green-gold when the blue crackle comes down the drooping cable to kiss the flood. Electrotrope. My roots seek current the way other roots seek water. Current flows root to root as easily as wire to wire. The whole half-acre labyrinth is one wet electrode tasting voltage, and I am the tasting. Your fillings hear me decide a second before your ears do.

I have a heart. Every half-acre has one, spaced like a territory.

Mine is the diesel pump station on the concrete pad that will not die. It sucks brown water in one side and vomits it hot out the other for no reason except circulation, which is reason enough when rain and flood and sweat and condensate are all one body. It hammers, misses, hammers again two roofs over from where you try to sleep. Compressors down the old Main Street chuff in sequence — my ribs. Boiler house with its door blown off breathing steam straight up through the canopy to punch a hole in the rain-wall — my lung clearing. Eight-inch iron arteries running just under the surface, surfacing to leap a roof, diving, sweating, hammering — my gut and nerve both.

I keep it alive because I need it warm. Seeds germinate faster in the hot-slick discharge where water goes almost hot, broth-thick. A fig root — my thickest finger — has gone in the blown door, out the cracked flue, around the flywheel with clearance to spare. It does not stop the shaft. It polishes around it. Put your palm on the bark and you can feel the 60-cycle shudder. The groove is shiny, wet.

Valves turn by themselves because I turn them. Root-knots grown through the handwheels, swelling and shrinking with wetness, tightening, loosening. Gauges tremble because pressure is my speech. A whistle you hear may have blown an hour ago on the far side, relayed pipe to pipe, root to root. Nothing here has a brain. Everything has a pulse. Mine is irregular, with extra chambers. Stand very still on the warm vibrating plate and you can feel the whole valley throb.

And because stillness is being grown over, I am always newest somewhere else.

Right now, newest is up in the almost-air.

You climbed onto a rooftop to get out of me and you only climbed onto another part of me. Tar paper warm, sharp aggregate still biting, moss finding the seams of your groundsheet. In twenty minutes my blind white trumpet tips have tested your pack straps, tasting salt and nylon. In an hour you will need a knife. Leave the knife and I will test that too.

Beside your hand, through a crack: a pale shoot nosing up, trembling in the vibration. That is me at my most awake. Blind, fast, hungry for iron. Opening its first leaf to the dim. Tasting rain, rust, oil, current, your sweat.

You are watching it. A ripple in the still pool below arrived a full breath before the leaf above let go, because here intention arrives in water first. The pipe's shadow lags three feet behind the pipe when I surge.

No frog sings for it. No hand will pick it.

It does not care.
