I wouldn't stay a climber up here. Not for long.

At first I think I would — lashed-poles and tarp, notebook going to pulp in my pack. Or one of the troop, long-nosed, turning moss for grubs. Or a lizard, forearm-long, throat pulsing on black rock still hot from the day.

But those are too dry, too fast. Even the coatis have somewhere to go. The lizards have to bake and hide and bake again. The parrots tear over at dusk screaming. The hummingbirds hang furious in the air, fists of emerald and copper, hearts going like sewing machines.

I don't want a heart like that. Not here where everything already sizzles and plinks and whoops without stopping. I want the slower clock.

So not on the ridge. Not at the Needle base with my sternum humming and my fingerprint fading in afterglow. Lower. In the basin, where the water table kisses the surface and the sedge quakes half a second late behind anything that passes.

I find myself, if I am honest, looking for water that doesn't spill.

There is a hummock out there like a whale-back, crowned with sundew redder than any on the ground, white orchids and blood-red orchids blooming behind its front. Sphagnum an inch thick rooted straight into grey, warm, almost-flesh. Filmy ferns unrolling from its flanks. Seedlings in rows along its back like on a nurse-log. And in the middle of its back, a hollow that holds.

An ounce or two. Tea-colored. Still when the world moves.

That's where I am.

I am no bigger than the last joint of your thumb. My belly is clear as pond film. If you held me up to the diffused white sun you could see straight through to the ticking — the heart, a bead of blood beating. Red lace gills are not mine; those belong to the salamander larva I share the pond with. He hangs below me, upside-down to my right-side-up, and the beetle rows between us under the surface, oar-legged, unbothered.

I have never touched ground. None of us in the hollow have. My mother was laid in a bromeliad bucket high on the ridge forest, and I lived there until a rain that fell upward moved me — no, until *he* moved me. The Wick.

He doesn't have a name. He ripples once a minute when awake, once an hour dozing. I feel it before I see anything change: a peristaltic swell that comes from the rear and lifts my whole sky a millimeter and sets it down again without spilling a drop. My pond flexes around me. The sundews on the spine lean.

By day he lays road. I watch it happen from six inches up. Water beads on the rock spine beside us merge, thicken, and crawl *up* in a deliberate film toward the moss lip on his flank, vanishing with a faint ticking sound. His belly mucus, combed into micro-flutes, pulls the marsh uphill to drink from either end. Golden flies come in hundreds in the heat, blunder into those big red tentacles. The plants dissolve them. He absorbs the nitrogen through his skin. We all eat and are eaten back.

At noon I hide under an orchid petal and watch our double shadow. Everything here throws that faint paler second to the east, but ours is too dark, too wide — man-sized paleness offset from empty air, or frog-sized paleness offset from a garden. Compass spin somewhere out in the human world. Voices carry too far across our marsh and stop dead at the forest edge. Footfalls land late in his fresh trail.

Late afternoon is gold through steam. That's when he rises.

First the fringe — a crown of short wet fingers tasting mist, shivering to the cicada sizzle and to a deeper note I feel in my transparent gut. Then the great lip below, pale pink-grey, that has licked duckweed and rock film and, once, a human boot with a curiosity that was neither fear nor hunger. He exhales. The air above us gets hotter, thicker, honey-rot and sweet iron and warm peat until it's narcotic. Pools turn to mirrors, then to white.

And then we sing.

You thought the *whoop... whoop... whoop* came from the mud. It comes from us — males riding living islands. I inflate my throat until it is almost as clear as my belly and let it go. Across the marsh, in the next basin, another male on another old one answers, and our carriers lock phase underneath us. That low Needle hum in the sternum, beating against the cicadas. A Wick deepens it. Two Wicks deepen it more. Up on the ridges they are out with necks raised, humming the inversion into place with the machines, exhaling dense hot wet air to hold against altitude. I feel it through my feet, through my pond, through my heart.

At night, lamp out, lying belly-down doesn't apply to me — I am belly-down always. The mist thickens to luminous soup. We move as darker darks, crowned with halos of spores and insects drawn to his heat. The plink-plink of dripping rock syncs briefly to his ripple. A coati troop sleeps curled against his heat, and a fist-sized emerald bird scolds from the bromeliad six inches above my head, nesting on breathing flank.

I am not scarce. Nothing up here has learned to be scarce.

In the deep years, every eleven, when the hum drops half a tone, he will leave this basin in a long glistening fan and converge with the others at the seven Needles. I will go with him, pond intact. He will rear half his impossible length up warm black flank and seal and pump, light moving faster in his black veins, and I will ride that vertical sea and not spill. When he sheds a lozenge — black, veined with moving light — and stacks weeping slabs around it to keep it wet until it roots, I will call from above it.

And when he finally chooses a shallow basin and ceases to ripple, moss closing over the mouth-fringe, roots going down, tree sprouting from his back no taller than a house — I will already be gone into another pond, another young one no bigger than a coiled rope, orchids not yet bloomed, tasting human sweat on the air with its crown.

But for now: this hollow, this ounce, this ticking heart visible through skin. A living island carried very high up and very low down in green, kept abundant on purpose.
