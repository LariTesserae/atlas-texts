Up the switchback with my ears popping, I thought I would get to choose.

At first I tried to be loud. I put myself in yellow oilskins on the bow of a flat boat, braced, shouting over the put-put-put with one hand on a cage line. I could feel it for a minute — the water to the ankles, the char coming up flashing silver-dark, the gulls snatching guts inches from my ears. My arms strong from hauling. My throat raw.

But it didn't hold. I'm not broad enough for that shout. The noise went through me instead of out of me.

So I tried to be quiet instead. A shed man. Pale, with a rag in my pocket, sitting in the concrete turbine house while water thundered under the floorboards. Listening for a bearing going out under the thrum. Drinking tea black and scalding. Telling by the pitch of the sill outside if the loch was up a foot or down. I liked the listening part. I stayed there a while.

But I'm too restless to stay inside concrete. My feet wanted out.

Then I thought, not a man at all. An otter at dawn, working the narrows better than any man, leaving fish heads on the jetties like receipts. Slick, unbothered, at home in the black clear cold. Or a red deer, knee-deep in sedge with cotton grass hung from my antlers, watching a boat pass twenty yards off and not even turning. Or — this one lifted me a little — a raven tumbling in the updraft off the turbine house roof, waiting with terrible patience for gutting week.

I tried them. I was too hungry to be a deer, too clean to be an otter. The raven was close — that seeing from above, three lochs deep when the cloud lifts, the next shed's smoke two miles off — but I don't have that patience yet. I want to be in it, not above it.

Smaller, then. Lower. In the calm between squalls when the midges find you, I thought I could be the cloud of midges itself, getting in mouths and eyes, or the sundew waiting with its red sticky leaves for one midge to land. Or the sphagnum, lime and rust and acid yellow, building itself up a millimeter a year over peat over water over weed over mud.

Closer. The waiting. The wet that never wrings out.

And then it came clear, the way the world shrinks here when the cloud comes back down to fifty yards of chop and reed and dripping wire. I'm not the one hauling or the one owning. I'm the one learning.

You'd find me on the pier. The black creosoted one with the winch and the fuel drum and the string of bare bulbs that burn all night in the mist. Not on a boat, not in a shed. On the edge.

I'm one of the kids who bail before they read. Thin-wristed, hair stuck flat under a wool hat that never dries, two pairs of socks and both of them wet, trousers soaked to the thigh and past caring. My hands are already going red-knuckled. My nails have peat under them that won't scrub out.

My job is lines and gulls. Mind the mooring because the water the men make goes up a foot when it rains in the night and down a foot when they open the turbines below, and a boat tied tight will heel in the mud by evening. So I slacken, I tighten, I watch for rope-strain. I bail the skiff with a cut-down jug, steady, stupid, endless. I chase gulls off the drying racks with a stick, shouting in a voice I'm still growing into, and they lift in a white scream and settle again the second I turn.

I know the rules in my legs, not my head. Test with a pole before a boot. That green holds, that green takes you to the knee. Keep food covered or the gulls take it, keep fuel covered or the rain takes it. Don't trust calm. Calm is just when the midges get your neck and you learn to cover it without stopping work.

I rest the way the place tells me to. Not looking for dry ground — there isn't any, except the knoll with the chapel and the leaning stones, and that's for the dead. I perch. On the jetty edge with my feet dangling over chop. On an upturned fuel drum in the smokehouse doorway, smelling brine and peat-smoke while the women work fast with twine behind me, their aprons stiff with scales. In the boat with the engine idling under me like a heartbeat. I nap in snatches and wake when the sill note changes.

At night, from out on the loch, I must be just another yellow smudge — one more bulb in the string, one more lamp swinging on a workboat between cages. The halyards clanking, the loch slapping the piles underneath the bothy, the sheds thumping, the sills roaring. I sleep through it now. I sleep in it.

I thought that was what quiet is.
