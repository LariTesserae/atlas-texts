I thought at first I would be up high.

Near the lip, where the wind shears the pelt tight to the stone and you can still see the white sky if you tip your head back. Where it would be easier to leave. Where the light is hardest and a person could tell themselves they are still on the surface of something.

But I don't stay there. In my mind I keep sliding.

Not all the way down, either. Not into the white breath where the next rain is being made, where the moths tip over and don't come up. I don't want to go there yet. I hang, for a long time, in the middle distance — in that vast, bright, damp emptiness between presences. A grove here. A curtain a kilometer down and half a kilometer over. Between them: nothing to stand on.

That is where I find myself. And then I have to find what could live there.

Not the moth. I thought moth, for a moment — white and silent, wider than I am tall, never flapping if I can help it, just tilting to stay in the lift. Hanging in a spore-storm and breathing. It would be good to make no noise that size. But moths are too rare, too blessed. They are weather. You see them once a week if you are lucky and then they drop. I am not ready to drop.

Not the goat, either. Though I love the goat. I thought goat for a while: shaggy and low and the exact gray-green of old lichen-wool because the wool is growing in me, in the folds behind my ears. Probing with a forefoot. Test, pause, shift. Green lies about depth. I know how to move like that. But the goat is too solitary, too known by its cough. A band shares a range with one goat for a whole season. I want to be *with* the thin scattering, not alongside it.

So then — smaller. Under the sail-leaf? Black, flat-backed, slow with cold, clustered where leaf meets stem to stay out of the glare? I could be a beetle, eaten by the handful, tasting of fat and frost. Or a mite, the size of a freckle, moving in slow tides through the thatch, hearing the sipping louder than anyone because my ear is pressed to the moss.

Close. Closer. Because what I really want is to hear the drinking.

I am on the wall, then. Not on it — *in* its lee.

A little over two kilometers down, where the air has stilled and warmed a fraction and the forest has let go. Where the cables shoot straight out from the moss for fifty, a hundred meters — as thick as my torso — and then droop, sprouting nets, sprouting tents. Where from across the abyss it looks solid enough to walk on, and up close you see light shining through gaps, through layers.

There is a scar here. Last season, or the season before — time is soft here — an acre sighed in my knees and tore loose and went down turning into the white. It did not crash. There is nothing to crash on. Within a day pale new shoots were already combing over the dark rot, bright green against it. I live on the edge of that scar, where the new growth is still thin enough to see through to falling green below.

My camp is six pods lashed to a cable-root. Sail-leaves pinched together at the edges so they keep growing into watertight tents. A platform of dead stem laid crosswise over a dip. A fire-pit lined with basalt chips. I tend that fire. That's what I am.

An old picker. Not old in years — no one counts — old in erasure. Broad across the shoulders from hauling hand-over-hand up living fiber, thin in the leg from never walking far on anything flat. Eyes pale and permanently squinted from glare; even at blue dusk I squint. The tips of my fingers blackened from frost, blunt. My hair, if I let it, mats with spores and greens at the root. Lichen has found me too.

In the morning after rain I don't go far out along the braids anymore with the long pole. The younger ones do that, probing every step, sucking cup-water from giant leaves through chewed fiber and spitting it into bladders, combing sap-sugar, gathering beetles with quick numb hands. I stay and prune what is close. I cut back a sail that shades a seep. I thin a terrace that has grown too heavy and wet. I listen with my knees for that deep soft sigh that means it is about to go. Sometimes I can save an acre by cutting a cable with my basalt blade. Sometimes I just get clear and watch it go.

At noon I count threads.

The smoke from my little fire of dried vine-pith and rendered beetle-fat rises straight up for a kilometer in the still air — a thin gray thread. From a kilometer away you can see it. Across the emptiness other threads rise. Three today. Four. We are not alone in the wound. I count them the way you would count breaths. I do not shout. We whisper as habit, as manners. Not because something will hear, but because the quiet is what holds the hanging forests together.

At night in the pod, cold to the bone despite the fire, I listen with the child coughing smoke beside me to drip, creak, breath. Frost forming on the sail-leaves with a faint crackling like tiny glass breaking. Tick of a melt-drop landing on a leaf below. Creak of a vine tightening. My own blood. And very far off, softened by three kilometers of bright air, that dull *whump* as something large, somewhere, lets go.

I build to be abandoned. Every lashing I tie is quick-release. When this meadow tears — and it will — we will go up or sideways along the wall to the next cairn left by the last band, three sharp black teeth stacked on a moss hummock: safe anchor. We will start again.

I will not go down to find the bottom. The ropes are not long enough.

But I will sit still for an hour on the cable-root, miserably cold despite my furs, and watch the tendril near my hand uncurl, lengthen in jerks I can almost hear, find its purchase and thicken. Pale green, blind, questing. Growing out and down and in, away from the scoured sky and into the damp air of the void.

If I put my ear to the moss, I can hear it drinking. Faint, endless sipping.

That is where I am. That is what I am. The one who stays close enough to hear it.
