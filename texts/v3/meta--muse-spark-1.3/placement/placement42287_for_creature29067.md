At first I thought I would be the visitor.

It's the easy shape to take. The one who ducks sideways through the waist-high door, lamp in teeth, knees finding the moss because the ceiling won't let you stand. Breath fogging and vanishing. Lips chapping. Boots raising dust. Human, loud-breathing, temporary. I know how to do that crouch.

But I wouldn't stay that way. No one does. The linemen don't. They leave skin cells and grease and a set of initials scratched in 1978 and then they go back out into the ice-dust and the screaming cables. The pocket doesn't keep people. It keeps what people shed.

So no. Not the visitor.

Then I thought — maybe the bulb. The replacement bulb still in its cardboard sleeve on the ledge, cradled like an egg in moss. That appealed to me. To be the one unopened thing, the potential light, held without being used. To be kept.

But I'm not that separate. The bulb is still separate. The moss holds it the way a hand holds something it hasn't decided to absorb yet. Its glass is still smooth. I want to be more inside it than that.

Then I thought the dust. The fine dark dust hanging motionless in the lamplight column that never settles. To be suspended, neither falling nor rising, combed by leaf-hairs but never caught. That is closer. Nothing flows, nothing leaves — dust understands that. But dust is still just passing through, even if it passes very slowly.

Let that go too.

If I am honest about where I would find myself — really find myself, not place myself for the view — it wouldn't be near the door seam where the cold blade comes in. I don't want the daylight. I don't want the place where the outside leaks in and reminds you of the three thousand meters of drop. And it wouldn't be right up against the transformer either, in that hand's-width of warmth where the moss goes a fraction taller and yellower. That's too loud, too warm. That's the mouth of the song. I don't want to be at the mouth.

I would be further in. Low. On the mountain side where the felt goes straight onto basalt, not onto plate. Where the floor slopes slightly and your elbow keeps sliding if you don't brace it. Under the dead maintenance ladder, where the uprights have become trellises and the rosettes climb them shingle over shingle.

There, in the deepest part of the shadow where no light at all reaches — not the door, not the pencil from the roof conduit, barely even the amber of the pilot jewel — the moss is still green. That blind, electric green. That is where I would be.

And what I would be —

I keep trying to give myself edges. A rosette, a bead over a bolt-head, a strap hanging stiff from the conduit. A single thing I could point to. But that's the first mistake, isn't it? It's not a crowd. It's one thing wearing many shapes, and if I live here I don't get to be separate either.

I would be felt.

Not the showy finger pushing up, but the sheet underneath. White, hair-fine, endless, dry as old wool, a centimeter thick, running uninterrupted over stone and over steel. The part your fingers can't quite get into where the green goes dark.

I would be the part that decides, here and there, to push up. Or rather, I would be the deciding, not the pushed.

Do you understand? I wouldn't drink. I would learn what it means to drink without drinking. To comb dielectric mist out of dry air with leaf-hairs. To conduct the faint electric field the way a wet root conducts water. To take iron straight from the plate and freckle my underside rust-red with it, to lay down copper filings as verdigris veins. To eat rock dust and graphite and old skin without a mouth. Lush without ever being wet.

I wouldn't change. That's the part that takes longest to admit I want.

No bloom, no seed, no wither. Just flaking — a dry edge breaking off a centimeter away, putting out white hairs, becoming the same rosette again in the same place against the half-swallowed 7-B. Same mounds as in '64, as in '82. As much growth as the mist can feed, as much wear as the dry can take, locked.

If you put your hand down — because you have to, the ceiling won't let you stand — you would feel me all the way up your arm. Not as a tickle or a bite. There is nothing here to bite you, nothing to rot you, nothing to pollinate you. Just the tremble, the same everywhere. The 50-cycle snarl in the teeth, the counterweight chain clanking... clanking... clanking somewhere above in its channel, the wind turned to howl in steel.

I would be turned edge-on to light, face-on to vibration. My flat sides squared up to the east wall. Shivering, not from wind — there is no wind in here — but because I am tuned.

Packed tight, shoulder to shoulder with myself. Felt on steel on stone. Holding on while the tower sings.

You would find me by pressing down until it crackles faintly and gleams dully, like a moth's wing. That oil-dust coating. That smell of ozone and hot oil and chlorophyll, not damp.

I would not leave.
