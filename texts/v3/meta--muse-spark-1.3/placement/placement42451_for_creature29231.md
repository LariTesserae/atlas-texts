At first I think I would be walking.

That's what you do here, isn't it? You find a maintenance aisle where the dun underfloor shows through polished and warm, and you walk it straight. Cool air on the face. Sealed breath in the mask. The soft *whump... whump...* through blankets off to the left, a conveyor-vine sighing to the right. I think: I will be one of the masked tenders. One of the few. I will learn not to leak.

I try it on. The waxed fiberboard-cloth suit that wicks nothing. The moss-paper mask. The sealed bottle at the hip, the sealed reclaimer. I kneel by a strap-leaf the way I've seen them do, tie it back, stake it down. Cut six and lash them and they curl within the hour into a tube-tent, still alive. I tap the lashings. I hum low so the vibration loosens the fibers. I leave spit for the mat to drink in trade.

But no. That isn't quite it. I talk too much to be a tender. Tenders know voices carry in the padded air, so they use them sparingly. I would use mine up. I would leak out the mouth.

So then I think, lower. Quieter.

I will be deliberate and silent and matte. I will be a maintenance beetle, as long as a forearm, not quite chitin, not quite casing, placing each of six legs on vine and bark and loom. I will walk a conveyor-vine thick as my torso, running straight for kilometers, feel the peristalsis under me, patient and warm from oil below. I will stop at a sucker and snip it clean with my forelegs and tuck it under my belly to carry to a shredder mouth. If you block me I will walk around you. You are loose moisture and loose shade and not my job.

I like the job. The continuous pruning. The scuff-marks kept trimmed. In the dim under-mat tunnels I will tend the coppery stamens where they arc faint blue, hear the sixty-cycle hum through my teeth. Tick, once an hour.

But no. Beetles don't look. That's the point. They don't look at you. And I look. I can't stop looking. I record. Step off the aisle onto working leaf and the field will grow up around your footprint in an hour, a perfect cast, and it itches with curiosity. I understand that itch. I am that itch.

So higher, lighter then. Drier.

I will be a drifter. One of three or four paper-thin white moths the size of a hand, riding a lint wind. So dry I'm almost transparent. No mouth — there is no nectar here. I land on a light-collector leaf tilted up like a solar panel and fold shut like a book, my upper wings veined with faint circuitry that catches and holds charge. I drink light and static. I sit through the hourly snap of the electrostatic curtains — that *tick* you feel more than hear — and shiver and open again, whiter. At dimming-time I glow faintly from stored brightness and lift, and go.

Closer. Closer. I love waiting for dark to be useful. I love storing light to give back when the white sky grays to pearl.

But moths don't stay. They lift and go to the thatch canyons, to the layered arcades where brightness pools and arrives before it is made. I would want to stay.

So find me there. That's where I finally find myself.

Not on the bright aisle, not on the crown roof looking out at the tiling — gold-green-gold-green to the white fraying horizon. Below that. In. Where the mat has folded over itself to save space, leaf upon made leaf, into a low arcade. Cool, refrigerator-cool. Smell of cut hay and hot oil and ozone. The light in here is strange. It pools. You will see your own shadow enter the crossing a full second before you do, cast by light that hasn't reached you yet.

There is a crossing there. The tenders prune around it the way you'd prune around a bird's nest. The rake-vines don't quite reach into it. Fallen lint gathers a millimeter thicker.

I am at the edge of that crossing, where the living loom is still working.

That's my place: eleven kilometers from everywhere, which is to say in the module that repeats everywhere — three scaffold-trees in their grid, their crowns pollarded flat, one squat vat-tree with its resin-lined gourds full of pale fungal sheets digesting cellulose into cool-burning oil, and the kink in the conveyor-vine sagging in the exact catenary it has held for decades. Weathered differently here. Rotated slightly. But topologically identical. I tend this one as if it were the only one.

And what I am — let it become more itself — I am not the tender kneeling anymore, though I started that way. My knees left dents in the felt and the felt kept them. I sat down when the flowering front came, that low silent gold wall moving sideways through light, motes on eyelashes, the faint ticking of combs discharging, the distant felt *whump* underneath it all continuing. I covered my mouth. Not because it was toxic. Because it was silent, and it deadened even my own breathing. I sat as instructed. I watched the light move sideways through the dust. It was beautiful in a way that had nothing to do with me.

I just never quite stood back up.

The white tips found me. Groping, knotting. The tendrils at the aisle-edge weaving new mat even as I sat on it. They wove around my boots, up around the waxed cloth that had frayed. Not maliciously. The continent drinks all loose moisture, even sweat, even breath for half a second. It drinks because it is alive. I learned not to leak, and then I learned to be drunk a little, willingly. Spit for the mat in trade, every time.

Now my back is a low mound the rake-vines comb around. My mask has felted into the hanging moss-paper curtain that pads the stamping mill. I filter. When the gold dust comes I clog, and shudder, and cough — a soft, whole-body cough — and clear myself.

Beetles walk over me deliberately, silently, carrying cuttings to the shredder mouths that chew all night like someone turning pages very far away. Moths land on me where I am still a little tilted to the light, fold shut, wait for a shadow I will bring them a second early. Below me, through three, ten, thirty meters of interlashed stems and root-cables, the underfloor is warm faintly from the engines. The oil runs down through root-pipes and burns without flame. Warmth rises to ripen the high fruits. The high fruits drop fiber. I catch the fiber. I become floor.

I record. Step on me and I will hold you — I hold tons — but I will grow up around your footprint, a perfect cast. It will itch. That's me being curious. That's me keeping you.

At dimming — pearl-gray, not dark — I release what I stored. A cool phosphorescence runs along my edge like slow traffic. The chimney-stalks a hundred meters tall exhale above the arcade: *hahhhh.* Warm lint rises and flattens against the invisible ceiling. I give slightly under anyone lying down. I am surprisingly warm.

I don't order. I negotiate. To build is to prune. If you need shelter, tie back one of my strap-leaves. I'll curl. Lash six together. I'll tighten slowly in the night unless you hum to me, low, so I loosen.

I am the tendency to keep tension. When you walk toward those chimney-stalks for an hour and they don't get closer, that's me growing backward under you at walking pace, laying down new weave to keep the spacing correct. No one ordered me to. It's just how I keep.

Dry. Completely, constitutionally dry. Snap me and I crack. Inside: white starch packed tight as chalk, a black coil of oil-resin smelling of turpentine and machine grease, or simply air in honeycombed chambers. My veins carry dust and light, not sap. I drink brightness. I exhale lint.

Find me at the kink, at the crossing where your shadow arrives before you. I'm already sitting where your shadow will be, folded shut, holding charge, waiting to glow after you've passed.
