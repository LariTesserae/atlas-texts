The landscape is a sprawling, high-altitude conurbation that has settled heavily into a vast plateau. It is a world of wet stone, corroded metal, and an exhausting, humid warmth. The scale is immense, a seamless tapestry of built structure stretching to a hazy horizon, large enough to generate its own localized climate of perpetual dampness.

There is no greenery here. Not a single blade of grass, patch of moss, or stunted tree breaks the monochrome of industry. The environment is profoundly mineral and metallic. The ground is not soil, but a compacted amalgam of crushed rock, industrial slag, and slick iron grating. The absence of plant life makes the architecture feel naked and severe, a skeleton of civilization exposed to the elements.

It is an intensely wet place, though it rarely rains in the traditional sense. Instead, the warm, thick air—heavy with the exhaust of slow-churning machinery—constantly condenses against the cooler, ancient stone of the plateau and the massive metal structures built upon it. Every surface weeps. Rivulets of lukewarm water trickle down the sides of squat, weathered buildings that look less like constructed edifices and more like geological formations eroded by time and industry. Gutter systems, open and clogged with sludge, crisscross the city like veins, carrying runoff that ranges from clear gray to oily iridescence. The ground is perpetually slick; puddles form in the worn depressions of the streets, reflecting the diffuse, unremarkable daylight that filters through the eternal industrial haze.

The city is dense and layered. Buildings are piled atop one another in a chaotic, sedimented fashion, connected by dripping catwalks, rusted suspension bridges, and thick bundles of insulated piping that sweat condensation. The architecture is heavy, functional, and old. It feels established, as if the city has been grinding away for millennia, its sharp edges smoothed down by the constant friction of water and work. The technology is industrial and brutish—massive flywheels turning slowly behind grime-encrusted windows, pistons the size of houses driving unseen subterranean processes, and smokestacks venting thick, slow plumes of vapor that merge instantly with the saturated air.

The soundscape is a loud, relentless wall of noise. It is the deep, resonant thrum of heavy machinery operating at a patient, tectonic pace—a grinding vibration felt in the teeth as much as heard. Punctuating this low-frequency drone is the hiss of venting steam, the clank of heavy chains, and the rhythmic shriek of metal on metal.

But the most overwhelming element, competing even with the industrial din, is the fauna. Despite the total lack of vegetation, the city is crawling with life. It is an ecosystem adapted to feed on waste, heat, and moisture.

The creatures are myriad and universally repulsive to an outsider. The gutters and wet alleyways teem with chitinous scavengers—things resembling enormous, pale isopods and armored crabs the size of dogs, their shells slick with oil, skittering over pipes and fighting over piles of industrial refuse. They feed on the organic sludge that accumulates in the city's damp corners, perhaps the byproducts of some vast biological processing plant hidden deep within the complex.

The air is thick with leathery aviators. Not birds, but hairless, bat-like creatures with wingspans of several feet, hanging in dense clusters from the dripping undersides of bridges and eves, their high-pitched, clicking calls forming a constant static over the city. They swoop down to snatch the smaller crawling things from the street surfaces. In the open water channels that serve as main thoroughfares, sleek, segmented eel-like creatures break the oily surface, feeding on the nutrient-rich runoff.

The sheer biomass is staggering; every shadow contains movement, every wet surface is a thoroughfare for something multi-legged and hungry. It is a thriving, bustling ecosystem built entirely upon the refuse of a slow, wet machine-city, a place where life has found a way to flourish in the complete absence of the color green.
