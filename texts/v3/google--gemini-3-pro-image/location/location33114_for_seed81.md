The air here is not merely hot; it is a physical weight, a suffocating blanket of superheated vapor hanging over a high-altitude expanse. We are far above the common clouds, situated on a vast, fractured plateau that might once have been the caldera of an unimaginably large volcano. The light is perpetually dim, a bruised purple twilight filtering through layers of geothermal steam that refuses to dissipate into the thinner atmosphere above.

There is water everywhere, but none of it is cool, and none of it feeds life as we know it. The ground is a slick, treacherous fusion of volcanic rock—obsidian, basalt, pumice—and ancient, corroded metal. It is a wet landscape, but it never truly rains. Instead, the intense heat from beneath meets the cooler upper air, causing immediate, heavy condensation. Everything weeps. Moisture beads on every surface, running in scalding rivulets down the faces of black cliffs and massive, man-made structures, pooling in hissing iron grates before draining into the unknown depths below.

This is a city, of a sort, but it is utterly hollow. The scale is immense, an urban sprawl extending into the foggy horizon, but the density is zero. There are no streets for traffic, only precarious catwalks and colossal conveyer systems that have not moved in centuries. The "buildings" are not habitations; they are industrial ziggaurats, mountain-sized processing units built from monolithic slabs of cast iron and dark stone, fused together by time and heat. They seem less constructed than grown from the mineral substrate, their original purposes lost to erosion.

It is a mineral world, entirely devoid of vegetation. The constant hot dampness supports no moss, no algae, no fungi. The only color comes from chemical stains: vibrant streaks of sulfur yellow, copper patina green, and rust reds that bleed down the architecture like wounds in the metal. Crystalline formations create glittering, razor-sharp crusts around venting pipes, mimicking coral in a dead ocean of air.

Despite the industrial scale, it is mostly quiet. There is no roar of engines or clamor of work. The soundscape is composed of deep, resonant vibrations that you feel in your teeth more than you hear—the tectonic groaning of geothermal taps deep underground. The dynamic nature of the place is slow, almost geological. Every few hours, a massive pressure release valve somewhere in the distance will open with a sound like a dying whale, sending a geyser of scalding white steam thousands of feet into the dark sky, temporarily thickening the fog.

Life is almost a rumor here. The environment is too hostile. Yet, if you look closely at the banks of the boiling runoff channels, you might see small, six-legged creatures clad in chitinous armor thick enough to withstand the temperatures, scavenging mineral deposits from the filtration screens. They move slowly, blind and indifferent to the vast, empty machine-world that surrounds them. The weirdness of the place is its permanence; it is a high-altitude graveyard of industry, churning endlessly on automatic, waiting for masters who will never return, preserved in a state of wet, hot, slow decay.
