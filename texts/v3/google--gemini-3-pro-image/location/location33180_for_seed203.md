This is a hollow world, a vast, subterranean cavity of continental scale, where the concept of "ground" is a temporary agreement between massive, shifting forces. It is an immense geode where geology has been entirely supplanted by botany.

There is no rock here. No soil, no magma, no traditional core. The structure of this reality is an incomprehensibly vast lattice consisting entirely of plant matter. It is a threedimensional web of colossal root systems, calcified tendrils thick as city blocks, and sweeping, translucent boughs that bridge voids so immense they generate their own internal weather patterns.

The sensation is one of overwhelming, icy brightness. Despite being far below any surface, the world is brilliantly illuminated. The light does not come from a sun, but from the vegetation itself. Every surface—from the titanic structural vines to the delicate, moss-like integument covering them—emits a fierce, sterile white radiance. It is a cold light, without warmth, casting long, sharp shadows that crisscross the emptiness. The visual effect is like being inside a diamond illuminated by an arc welder; it is beautiful, clinical, and blinding.

The temperature is bitterly cold, hovering just below freezing. The air is thin, sharp to breathe, and carries the scent of ozone and crushed, frozen sap. While there are no oceans or rivers, the world is perpetually damp. The clash between the latent heat of rapid biological growth and the ambient freezing temperatures creates constant condensation. Every luminous leaf is slick with cold sweat; icy fog banks drift sluggishly through the enormous gaps between the root-bridges; sudden, localized hoarfrost storms coat entire biomes in glittering rime in minutes. The moisture doesn't flow; it clings, drips, and freezes.

This universe is young and violently dynamic. The plant-matter isn't ancient or petrified; it is in a spastic state of hyper-accelerated growth and destruction. The landscape is turbulent, a slow-motion explosion of life. A spiraling tower of pale, glass-hard bamboo might erupt from a nexus point, growing hundreds of feet in a "day," its segments groaning with tension. Just as quickly, the unstable architecture might buckle, shattering into millions of razor-sharp, glowing splinters that rain down into the luminous abyss, clattering silently off lower tiers of vegetation.

It is a quiet turbulence. The sheer scale of the void swallows noise. The snap of a growing branch the size of a cathedral registers only as a dull thud in the distance. The dominant sound is the delicate, crystalline tinkling of ice falling from high branches, or the soft hiss of freezing mist.

Life here is almost entirely botanical. Fauna is rare, specialized, and silent. Occasionally, one might glimpse a creature, pale as gristle and translucent, skittering across a glowing expanse of leaf on too many legs, scavenging for frozen nectar or dead spores. They are ghosts in the machine, dwarfed by the vegetable architecture.

Scattered throughout this chaotic, glowing wilderness are the faint marks of human—or near-human—presence. They are small, desperate things, clinging like barnacles to the undersides of the most stable root-masses, seeking shelter from the constant upheaval of growth. Their structures are primitive, woven from tough, fibrous membranes and reinforced with the discarded, scythe-like thorns of larger plants.

Their technology is born of necessity and scarcity. They possess no metal, instead fashioning tools and weapons from the super-hard, silica-rich outer layers of the glowing plants. A dropped tool, a abandoned lean-to made of woven husk-fiber, a bridge of knotted vines across a small chasm—these are the only signs that thinking beings are attempting to survive in the cold, bright, churning gut of the world-plant. They are not masters here; they are barely tolerated parasites living in the eye of a botanical storm.
