You have descended far past the point where the air feels cool or fresh.

The space is a high-pressure cavity buried deep within the crust, a blister in the rock. Upon entering, the first thing that hits you is the heat. It is a physical weight, a thick, oppressive blanket of thermal energy radiating from the rock itself and from the dormant machinery that crowds the space. It smells of hot mineral oil, wet ozone, and the rich, decaying scent of accelerated plant growth.

This is not a natural cavern, though jagged, violently fractured walls of obsidian-black basalt peek through gaps in the construction. These rock faces look young, sharp, as if ripped open by immense geological trauma only recently. But humans—or something like them—immediately rushed to fill the void.

The chamber is roughly the size of a small cathedral, vaulted high overhead, but the feeling is the opposite of spaciousness. The density is suffocating. Every cubic meter of space is occupied by a chaotic palimpsest of industrial infrastructure. It is an urban density applied to machinery. Four-story dynamos, silent and sweating oil, form the "buildings" of this place. Catwalks made of heavy iron grating bridge the gaps between them, forming narrow, dizzying alleys. Bundles of insulated piping, thick as tree trunks, snake over, under, and through every available aperture, creating a Gordian knot of conduits meant to move steam, magma, or slurry.

There is no natural light. The space is dimly lit by a failing constellation of heavy-duty filament bulbs caged in wire mesh, casting a sickly amber glow that barely penetrates the shadows. Supplementing this are patches of bioluminescence emanating from the fungi that thrive in the dark corners.

Despite the industrial clutter, the environment is incredibly damp. It doesn't rain, but the clash between the geothermal heat and the slightly cooler metal surfaces creates a perpetual state of condensation. Everything is slick with moisture. Steam wisps sluggishly from corroded valve joints, instantly turning to water on the overhead girders and raining down in slow, hot drips. Puddles of oily water collect on the uneven metal floors, reflecting the weak amber lights.

This persistent, heated moisture has allowed for an explosion of strange, lush vegetation to claim the machinery. This is not sun-loving surface life. These plants are adapted to heat, darkness, and chemical runoff. Great mats of velvety, deep-violet moss choke the gears of stalled engines. Ferns with fronds like oxidized copper sheets sprout from cracked boiler casings, their roots delving deep into the mineral wool insulation. Pale, fleshy fungi, some the size of dinner plates, bracket the sides of pressure tanks, feeding on the grease and the rust. The distinction between the built environment and the organic growth is blurred; the machines seem to be the soil from which this strange ecology springs.

It is unnervingly quiet. The visual noise of heavy industry leads you to expect a deafening roar, but the great engines are still. The soundscape is composed of small, intimate noises magnified by the silence: the incessant wet *plip-plop* of condensation hitting metal, the slow, agonizing creak of an iron walkway expanding in the heat, the soft hiss of escaping steam. Occasionally, a sound is felt rather than heard—a deep, sub-sonic thrum vibrating up through the soles of your feet, evidence of the magma chambers pulsing somewhere further below.

Life here is rare and secretive. The heat is too intense for most things. Perhaps, out of the corner of your eye, you might glimpse a pale, translucent segmented creature, blind and many-legged, scuttling into the darkness inside a broken turbine—a scavenger evolved to consume rust and fungal spores in the hot dark.

The place feels suspended in a slow-motion process of transformation. It is not static, but its dynamics play out over centuries. The rust blooms like slow flowers on the ironwork; the mineral deposits build up millimeter by millimeter around the leaky pipes; the violet moss gradually consumes a steel girder. It is a high-temperature terrarium where a forgotten industrial civilization is being very slowly metabolized by the earth it tried to conquer.
