This is a place entirely absent of natural bedrock. You are underground, but you are not surrounded by earth; you are entombed within a colossal, ancient structure that has swallowed its own history. It is a hive, a warren, a buried city constructed wholly from excavated clay bricks, hand-hewn timber blackened by age, and woven reed matting that has turned to dust and been replaced a thousand times over.

There is no "ground" here, only the impacted floor of the level below. The scale is deceptive. While the total volume might be vast, your immediate experience is one of intense confinement. It is a dense accumulation of small spaces—rooms the size of closets, corridors barely wide enough for two people to pass sideways, communal halls with ceilings so low the smoke from oil lamps pools around your head. It feels like a towering, precarious shantytown that sank into the earth centuries ago and just kept being reinforced from within.

The air is thick, warm, and heavily textured with smells: burning animal fat from crude lamps, unwashed wool, the musk of close-quartered livestock, and the underlying, pervasive scent of damp clay. Moisture is a constant, slick presence. The walls, built from varied mixes of mud and straw, sweat with condensation generated by the sheer volume of breathing, living things packed inside. Drops collect on the underside of ancient, sagging support beams—timbers thick as barrels, smoothed to a high polish by countless hands gripping them for balance over generations. This moisture feeds sparse, pale patches of fuzzy mold that cling to the rotting edges of wooden walkways, the only "vegetation" to be seen.

The "erosion" here is human erosion. Every surface has been rounded by touch. Stone stairs, imported from somewhere else long ago and set into the clay, are worn into deep, precarious bowls in the center. Doorways, mere gaps in the wattle-and-daub walls, have widened and softened at the edges where shoulders rub against them daily. The whole structure feels settled, heavy, and incredibly old, held together not by engineering prowess but by the sheer compression of the earth above and the friction of its own densely packed components.

Visibility is functional but chaotic. The light comes from thousands of small, flickering sources—clay bowls filled with tallow and simple fiber wicks, or small, smoky hearths built into corners with precarious clay chimneys threading upward through the layers. The light is warm, orange, and restless, throwing dancing shadows that make the cramped spaces feel even more crowded.

And it *is* crowded. The density of life is overwhelming. Every available cubic foot is utilized. Hammocks are strung in layers across sleeping alcoves. Storage jars sit on shelves built over walkways. Yet, it is not just humans. Fauna is abundant and integrated entirely into the living space. Small, ragged goats are tethered in kitchen corners, chewing on spilled grain. Chickens roost on rafters above sleeping families, their droppings contributing to the complex aroma. sleek, dark rats—tolerated as an inevitable part of the ecosystem—scurry along the bundles of conduit-like reeds that channel smoke away.

The sound is deafening. It is a relentless wall of noise: the bleating of animals, the squalling of infants, the rhythmic thud of wooden tools pounding grain, the shouting of conversations across narrow shafts, the constant creaking and groaning of the wooden superstructure under the weight of the earth and its inhabitants. It is an aggressively active environment. People are constantly moving, climbing crude ladders between levels, carrying water in leaky skin sacks from central collection cisterns where condensation is harvested, repairing crumbling walls with fresh handfuls of mud, and navigating the endless, loud, warm labyrinth of their buried, entirely constructed world.
