This place is not a world, but a pocket; a forgotten cyst buried deep within a larger, irrelevant substrate. It is a single, vast chamber, perhaps the size of a cathedral nave, but felt rather than seen in its entirety due to the intense crowding.

The air is cool, sitting motionless at a constant fifty degrees Fahrenheit. It is shockingly dry. There is no moisture here, not a drop of condensation, not a hint of humidity. The atmosphere tastes fiercely metallic, like sucking on an old penny, dusty and abrasive to the throat.

You are standing in the "Rust Hollow."

The environment is defined by extreme age and a bizarre form of erosion. The walls, floor, and ceiling were once defined by heavy industry—riveted bulkheads, I-beams thick as tree trunks, complex gearing systems, and pipes meant to carry non-aqueous fluids. But time, and the inhabitants, have changed them.

Everything has been worn down to soft, undulating curves. The iron has the smooth, tactile finish of river-tumbled stone, polished to a dull, hematite sheen. There are no sharp edges left in the entire chamber. The "scattered built" elements—a stubborn gear cluster here, a recognizable hydraulic piston there—jut from the smooth walls like bones protruding from dry earth, the last recognizable remnants of the original structure that haven't been ground away.

The dominant feature, however, is the density of life. It is overwhelming. The chamber is not empty space; it is a nearly solid volume of moving, clicking bodies.

The fauna here are litho-metallovores, creatures that subsist on minerals and refined metals. There are billions of them. They blanket the floor in a shifting dune sea three feet deep. They coat the walls in shivering tapestries. They hang in swinging stalactites from the ceiling girders.

They are crustacean-like, arthropodal things, ranging in size from a grain of sand to a dinner plate. Their exoskeletons are made of the very materials they consume: burnished bronze, matte cast iron, silicate crystalline structures, and dull lead. They are a riot of industrial drabness. They do not look biological so much as clockwork—segmented creatures of articulated armor plates and multi-jointed legs ending in diamond-hard rasping claws.

The sound is deafening. It is a relentless, dry roar. Imagine millions of stiff wire brushes scrubbing against sheet metal, underpinned by the incessant clicking of chitin against iron and the high-pitched squeal of metal-on-metal friction as larger creatures shoulder past one another.

The ecosystem is intensely dynamic and fiercely competitive. The floor is a churning convection current of bodies. The smaller creatures graze on the metal dust worn off the structure, while larger, armored scavengers prey on the smaller ones, cracking their metal shells with hydraulic-like pincers to get at the softer mineral slurry inside.

The light is normal, provided by ancient, resilient industrial arc lamps recessed into the ceiling. They buzz with a furious violet-white light, casting sharp, vibrating shadows that make the sea of metallic bodies glitter with a nauseating energy.

It is a sealed system, a dry, cool hell of metal eating metal, grinding the ancient industrial tomb down to dust, forever.
