The air here is catastrophically thin, a chilling vapor that bites the lungs. You stand on the shoulder of a high-altitude plateau, a world raised so far above the common earth that the sky is permanently a near-black violet, against which the sun burns as a searing, merciless white void. The light is incredibly bright, sharp, and unforgiving, casting shadows pure enough to cut yourself on.

Below and around you stretches a regional labyrinth of tortured stone. It is a immense expanse of erosion carried to its absolute extreme. The land is densely packed with towering fins, needle-spires, and interlocking slot canyons, so crowded together that in many places you could brace your back against one cliff face and touch the opposing one with your fingertips. It looks less like a landscape formed by water and wind, and more like the exposed, fossilized musculature of a dead titan.

Everything is slick. Though it is not currently raining, the geology weeps incessantly. Water beads on the vitrified, greenish-black rock faces, sliding down into the shadowed chasms below. The air is supersaturated with cold moisture; your breath fogs instantly, joining the constant, swirling drifts of vapor that cling to the canyon floors and wreath the spires. The temperature hovers just above freezing, a persistent, soaking chill.

There is absolutely no recognizable biological life. No alpine grasses, no hardy lichens, no insects crawling on the wet stone. Yet, the landscape is not sterile. It is covered in a "geoflora"—a mineral mimicry of life. Rapidly growing crystalline dendrites, colored deep indigo and rusted copper, burst from the rock fractures like frozen lightning. They grow with visible speed, branching and interlocking, only to shatter under their own weight or the stress of thermal contraction, sending showers of glassy shards clattering down the cliff faces.

This shattering contributes to the overwhelming noise. The place is incredibly loud. It is a constant, roaring cacophony of geology in accelerated motion. You hear the grinding groan of massive stone plates shifting deep underground, the high-pitched ping and shatter of the mineral growths, and the ceaseless rushing sound of unseen water plunging down thousands of hidden chutes and subterranean aqueducts. The wind doesn't howl here; it screams through the narrow gaps in the stone density, a multi-toned whistle that never stops.

The weirdness of the place manifests in the geometry. As you look across the dense field of spires, perspective fails. Distant peaks seem larger than near ones. Looking down into a slot canyon, the walls appear to curve inward and meet, though you know they are parallel. Gravity feels unreliable in certain pockets; step between two specific rock needles, and you feel a sickening lurch, a heaviness in your stomach as if the "down" direction has tilted fifteen degrees to the northeast.

Scattered throughout this bewildering maze are the remains of human—or at least intelligent—effort. They are primitive, terrified marks upon the land. You find crude cairns made of the shattered mineral growths piled precariously on ledges. In a sheltered overhang, there is a squat, circular hut built of uneven dry stone, its doorway too low for an adult to enter upright. Inside, there are no tools of survival, only a hammered copper disk roughly etched with spiraling, nonsensical lines that seem to try, and fail, to map the shifting reality outside. These are not settlements; they are desperate shrines or warnings, left by people using stone hammers and soft metals, attempting to appease or understand a landscape whose fundamental rules are broken.
