This place is known to its inhabitants as the Sliver.

It is a geological wound, a deep, narrow chasm riven into the earth by a recent tectonic spasm. The surrounding geography is low, a depressed basin that traps weather, keeping the air perpetually cool and saturated with moisture.

The terrain itself dictates everything. The canyon walls are not rounded by eons of wind or water; they are incredibly young, a violence frozen in time. The rock is vitreous, black volcanic glass and basalt, fractured into razor-sharp conchoidal planes. Every edge is a potential knife. There is no true soil, only a slurry of shattered rock dust and organic muck accumulating in the bottom of the fissure. Vegetation is almost nonexistent, unable to gain a foothold on the glassy slopes; only slick patches of black algae and stubborn, pale lichens cling to the wetter overhangs.

The dominant feature of the Sliver, however, is not the geology, but the suffocating density of human habitation jammed within it.

Driven by some unknown necessity—perhaps the shelter the canyon offers from surface winds, or the defensive advantage of a single, easily guarded entrance—a primitive urban center has coalesced here. The scale is cathedral-like in its verticality, but claustrophobic in its footprint. Because the canyon floor is narrow and subject to flooding, the settlement has been forced upward.

It is a chaotic, towering favela of Neolithic construction Clinging to the sheer, glassy walls are hundreds of dwellings stacked precariously atop one another. They are built from rough-hewn, sodden timber dragged from afar, lashed together with thick fiber ropes, and anchored into the rock fissures with hammered stone wedges. Piles of loose, sharp basalt shards form retaining walls for small terraces. Crude rope bridges and rickety wooden ladders crisscross the chasm, forming a dizzying, multi-layered web of transit that blocks much of the sky.

The density is absolute. There is no open space. Every buildable ledge, every nook capable of holding a support beam, is occupied. The structures lean against each other for support, a huddle of dark wood and gray stone shivering in the damp air. Below the main habitation levels, the canyon floor is a mire of shallow, black water that rises and falls with the frequent rains.

Water is omnipresent. It doesn't flow in rivers, but weeps. The cool air constantly condenses against the colder, glassy rock faces, causing them to slick with moisture that drips incessantly onto the structures below. The timber of the buildings is permanently waterlogged, dark and swollen. A persistent, chilly mist hangs in the lower reaches of the canyon, smelling of cold stone, wet ashes, and unwashed bodies.

Despite the packed population, the Sliver is surprisingly quiet. The heavy, water-laden air dampens acoustics. Sounds do not echo; they die quickly. The soundscape is composed of the ceaseless *plip-plip-plip* of dripping water, the creaking of overburdened wooden beams rubbing against stone, and the soft padding of calloused feet on wet planks. Voices are kept low, a murmur swallowed by the fog.

The environment is dynamic and subtly hostile. The "young" nature of the rock means it is not entirely stable. Thermal stresses from the cool air hitting rock warmed deeper underground cause sudden, loud fractures. Shards of obsidian, ranging from finger-sized splinters to massive slabs, periodically peel off the canyon walls, raining down on the roofs below. The inhabitants have learned to build their roofs with steep pitches and thick layers of scrap stone to deflect these geological missiles.

Life here is a constant, active negotiation with gravity and moisture, a primitive civilization clinging like barnacles to the sharp, weeping wound of the world.
