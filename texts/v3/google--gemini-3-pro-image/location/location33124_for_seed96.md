This is a landscape of total saturation and total construction. There is no "ground" here in the planetary sense; there is only the uppermost layer of an unimaginably dense, dizzyingly high architectural accumulation. It is a mountain range built entirely by hand.

You stand on a terrace made of titanic, roughly hewn slate slabs, jammed together with such frantic density that not even a knife blade could fit between them. This terrace is merely the roof of another complex beneath it, which sits upon another, cascading downwards into a seemingly bottomless gorge of masonry. The scale is regional; you can look across a valley—not a valley of earth, but a V-shaped chasm formed by two opposing slopes of stacked stone structures rising to meet a blindingly bright sky.

The elevation is extreme. The air is thin, cold, and biting. It is a place perpetually inside or just above the cloud layer. Because of this, everything is drenched. The environment is unbelievably wet, but without rivers or lakes. Instead, the water is a pervasive, weeping presence. Every vertical face of rough-chiseled granite slicks with condensation. Water continuously pools on the flat terrace roofs, spills over cornices in shimmering sheets, and trickles down the complex, angular gutters carved into every available surface. The soundscape is dominated by this water: a hush of endless dripping, slow trickling, and the occasional distant, muffled splash of runoff hitting a lower tier.

Despite the pervasive moisture, there is absolute sterility. There is no moss, no lichen, no algae slicking the wet stone. No weeds sprout from the joints. The mineral world reigns supreme. No insects crawl; no birds nest in the millions of available crannies. The silence, apart from the water, is profound.

The architecture itself is baffling. It is total—there is no natural substrate visible anywhere—yet it is technologically crude. There is no concrete, no steel, no precision engineering. It is the apotheosis of brute-force masonry: cyclopean blocks of quarried rock piled in complex, interlocking patterns, held together by gravity and friction. The sheer volume of labor implies a civilization of billions working with hammer and chisel, yet the area is completely devoid of life.

Furthermore, the construction feels shockingly young. The chisel marks on the grey and black stones are sharp and distinct, not worn by wind or time. The edges of the stairs are crisp. It looks as if the last mason set down their tool only yesterday and vanished. There is no ruin here, only a pristine, terrifyingly dense emptiness.

The light is intense, a high-altitude glare from a sun that feels too close. It reflects off the billions of wet, multifaceted stone surfaces, creating a glittering, harsh brightness that makes the shadows in the deep recesses of the stonework appear pitch black.

The only dynamic element in this frozen ocean of stone is the slow, downward migration of water. The structure itself is settling, but on a geological timescale. Perhaps once a month, deep within the labyrinthine guts of the construct, a massive foundation stone shifts a fraction of an inch under the crushing weight. The resulting sound is a low, vibrating groan that travels through the wet rock for miles, a slow-motion seismic creak that takes minutes to fade back into the quiet dripping.
