The land here has been worn down to its absolute hardest bones. It is a vast, low-lying basin of polished stone that stretches to a dim, indeterminate horizon. There is no soil, only rock that has been scoured smooth by eons of unrelenting water. The terrain undulates in gentle, slick humps of obsidian and slate-grey gneiss, resembling the backs of enormous, sleeping aquatic beasts.

It is incredibly wet. The air is a chilly, saturated vapor that clings to everything, condensing instantly on the cold stone surfaces so that the ground is perpetually coated in a thin, shivering film of water. It doesn't just rain; the atmosphere itself seems to be liquefying. This constant moisture feeds the defining feature of the landscape: the roar.

The sound is deafening, a physical pressure against the eardrums and chest. It is the colossal, unceasing thrum of water grinding against stone at a planetary scale. Beneath the smooth rock pavement, a labyrinthine network of subterranean rivers rushes with furious speed. In many places, the ancient ceilings of these caverns have collapsed, creating deep, churning chasms where white water boils furiously before diving back underground. Geysers erupt with startling regularity, shrieking jets of mineral-rich steam that pierce the lower, bass-heavy roar of the rivers.

There is absolutely no vegetation. No trees, no grasses, not even moss clings to the weeping rock faces. Yet, life is abundant here, thriving on a different energy source entirely.

The ecosystem is fueled by chemosynthesis. Where geothermal vents crack the smooth stone floor, thick, slick mats of bacterial life proliferate in vibrant, unnatural colors—sulfur yellows, rust reds, and virulent coppers. These bacterial fields are grazed upon by multitudes of armored creatures. They resemble giant, flattened chitons or segmented trilobites, their shells smoothed by the same erosion that shaped the land. They move slowly, clamping tight to the slick rock with immense suction, scraping up the bacterial slime.

Predators hunt these grazers. Blind, pale things, somewhere between amphibians and eels, they sense vibration through the wet rock. They burst from the churning chasms to snatch a grazer, dragging it back into the subterranean torrents. The struggle is brief and brutal, usually obscured by the constant mist. Despite the abundance of life, the sheer scale of the stone expanse means these creatures are scattered; you might walk for an hour seeing nothing but slick rock and steam, only to stumble upon a dense cluster of life around a fresh thermal vent.

There are signs that something else survives here, something possessing rudimentary intelligence. Scattered across the higher humps of rock, away from the most violent geysers, are low, domed shelters. They are built from polished river stones stacked with improbable precision, held together by gravity and friction rather than mortar. They are squat and sturdy, designed to deflect the endless spray and dampen the crushing noise.

Near some of the calmer chasms, notches have been laboriously chipped into the stone lips, perhaps to anchor simple nets or traps woven from durable, non-plant fibers—maybe sinew or gut. These artifacts are few and far between, speaking of a sparse population living precariously on the edges of the roaring water, subsisting on the strange bounty of this mineral world. The light never rises above a bruised, watery twilight, blurring the line between the wet stone, the heavy air, and the dark water that shapes it all.
