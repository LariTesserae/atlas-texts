This is a high place, a continental shelf thrust up into a perpetual, chilling layer of cloud. It is a landscape where geology has been entirely supplanted by botany.

There is no naked earth here. To stand "on the ground" is merely to stand upon the calcified, interwoven husk of the previous millennia's growth—a substrate of rock-hard cellulose and silicate-reinforced roots that stretches down for miles. The scale is immense, a sprawling, elevated botanical plateau that curves with the planet, vast enough to generate its own internal weather patterns of trapped fog and localized, drenching downpours that never reach a true soil.

The density is absolute. The sensation is one of being entombed in endless, living matter. There is no horizon, only the immediate, overwhelming wall of green, teal, and bruised purple vegetation pressing in from inches away. It is a fractal lattice of life occupying every available cubic centimeter of space. Gigantic columnar trunks, thicker than skyscrapers and appearing to be bundles of millions of smaller, fused vascular tubes, shoot upwards into the gloom. Between them, webs of thick, translucent cables—some the size of hawsers, others massive as transit tunnels—are strung taut, carrying pressurized fluids.

It is a wet world, colder than freezing but prevented from icing over by the sheer kinetic energy of the ecosystem. The air is a suspended ocean of aerosolized moisture. Every surface is slick, weeping water that pools in the cupped leaves of pitcher-plants the size of swimming pools, before overflowing and cascading down through the multi-layered canopy. You breathe water as much as air; a thick, cool vapor that tastes faintly metallic and deeply organic.

The light is dim, a perpetual sub-aquatic twilight filtering through kilometers of overhead biomass. What light does penetrate is diffused and shifted into alien spectrums—bioluminescent fungi and mosses cling to the underside of the great arches, pulsing with weak, cold light in shades of electric indigo and pale acid-green, providing the only real illumination in the deep folds of the landscape.

But the defining characteristic of this place is the noise. It is deafening.

Given the total absence of animal life—no birds, no insects, no rustling mammals—the roar is shocking. It is the sound of hyper-accelerated growth on a planetary scale. It is the colossal creaking of cellulose structures under impossible tension, a billion simultaneous groans of wood straining against wood. It is the hydraulic thunder of billions of gallons of water being pumped vertically through the vascular systems of the titan-trees, a rushing, sibilant roar that vibrates in the chest. It is the sound of tissues tearing and healing instantly, the *snap-crackle-pop* of rapid cell division amplified to the volume of a jet engine. The landscape is screaming with the effort of its own existence.

This is not nature as understood by biology. It is a "beyond modern" technology operating as ecology. The speed at which this landscape constructs itself—the "emerging erosion" where new landmass is generated hourly by the hardened secretions of the plants—suggests an underlying engine, a purpose fueled by energy sources far exceeding simple photosynthesis. The plants are not merely growing; they are executing a program, fabricating a world with terrifying efficiency and an inexhaustible power supply, indifferent to any observer, drowning out all thought with the roar of their terrible vitality.
