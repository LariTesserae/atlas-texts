This is the Verdant Crust, a high-altitude continent where geology has been entirely supplanted by botany.

It is immense, a rolling plateau suspended high above a unseen surface, stretching so far that the horizon isn't a line, but a hazy curvature of endless green against a violet-tinged sky. The air here is thin, offering little shielding from a punishingly hot sun, yet it is also thick with humidity. It is a suffocating, steamy heat that clings to the skin.

There is no mineral soil. The "ground" beneath your feet is a solid, kilometers-thick amalgam of ancient, calcified wood, compressed mulch, and thick, muscular roots fused together over eons. It is durable as granite but yields slightly under pressure, sweating a warm, slick resin. The landscape is established, weathered not by wind or flowing rivers, but by the slow, grinding cycles of growth and decay. Great chasms and valleys are merely gaps between the trunks of gargantuan, skyscraper-sized mother-plants that anchor the world. These structures are so old their bark has hardened into chitinous plates, scabbed over with smaller parasitic ecosystems.

The density of biomass is absolute, yet the scale creates a strange kind of sparseness. Between the monumental pillars of the primary flora are vast distances—kilometers of undulating, spongy terrain covered in a single, continuous mat of secondary growth: rubbery mosses, waist-high ferns with razor edges, and flowering vines that pulse with visible sap flow.

Water does not flow; it accumulates. The dampness is omnipresent but localized. It hangs as a heavy morning mist that burns off into a shimmering haze by midday. The primary water sources are enormous, basin-shaped leaves, some fifty meters across, that act as catchments for condensation and rare, brief downpours. These tepid, stagnant pools are choked with algae and serve as the watering holes for this world. Smaller pockets of moisture exist inside hollowed-out stems or trapped beneath thick layers of peelable bark.

The defining characteristic of the Verdant Crust, beyond its sight, is its sound. It is deafening. A relentless, physical wall of noise pressing in from all sides. It is the sound of overwhelming fauna.

There is no silence here, ever. The base layer is the high-pitched, hydraulic whine of billions of insects—chitin rubbing against chitin, wings beating the heavy air. Over this lies the varied shrieks, hoots, and guttural booming of larger creatures hidden within the dense canopy-floor. You feel the sound vibration in your chest cavity as much as you hear it.

The fauna is omnipresent, a churning sea of life adapted to this architectural biology. Most creatures are arboreal or burrowing. There are six-legged simian analogues with hooked claws that swing through the mid-layers, their fur matted with moss for camouflage. Enormous, segmented millipedes, the size of commuter trains, bore slowly through the deepest layers of deadwood, their passing grinding like tectonic plates. In the air, leathery-winged avian-reptiles glide on thermal currents rising from the steaming vegetation, snatching smaller prey that dares to cross the open spaces.

The weirdness of the place lies in the blurring of lines. Plants here behave like slow animals; some carnivorous pitcher-plants are large enough to digest a human, their lids snapping shut with bone-breaking force. Conversely, many animals have adopted plant-like traits, moving so slowly they grow lichen on their backs, only snapping into action when prey brushes past.

Human presence is minimal, scattered, and tenuous. You might travel for weeks across the springy, treacherous terrain without seeing a sign of them. When found, their settlements are primitive, woven directly into the landscape. A village might consist of platforms suspended inside the hollow core of a dead mother-plant, accessible only by climbing ropes made of braided vines.

Their technology is limited to what they can harvest. They use tools of shaped bone, hardened resin, and fibrous wood. They have no metal. Their existence is a constant negotiation with the aggressive ecosystem. They harvest the nectar from the giant pitcher-plants, hunt the oversized insects for protein, and use the toxic sap of certain vines to coat their spears, necessary to bring down the larger, armored beasts.

The environment is active. You can watch it change. Bamboo-like shoots rocket upward meters in a single day, cracking with explosive growth. Entire sections of the canopy-floor slump and collapse under their own weight, releasing clouds of fungal spores and opening new chasms that are immediately colonized by opportunistic species. It is a world in a constant state of frantic creation and digestion, hot, loud, and utterly indifferent to anything but its own perpetuation.
