This is a place defined by a deafening, screaming roar that never ceases. It is the sound of a world furiously making itself, a nonstop explosion of matter birthing into a void.

You are submerged, suspended in a medium that is neither air nor standard ocean, but a hyper-oxygenated, chilling liquid that fills the space between emerging realities. It is a high-altitude sea, an impossible volume of cold, crystalline water hanging in the upper reaches of a planetary atmosphere, held in place by strange gravitational eddies. The pressure is immense, yet the environment feels oddly airy because of the intense, normal daylight that filters down through the churning surface miles above. It is a bright, lucid nightmare of noise and cold.

This specific location is a "drift"—a pocket-scale micro-environment embedded within the vaster, empty turbulence. It is hidden not by darkness, but by the sheer scale of the surrounding chaos. Here, geology is raw and embryonic. There is no erosion because nothing has existed long enough to erode. Matter is erupting from unseen fissures in the reality substrate—jagged spars of obsidian-like rock, razor-sharp lattices of silicate, and steaming blobs of cooling metal are flashing into existence right in front of you. They shudder and groan as they solidify, adding their birth-pangs to the universal roar.

The density of matter is incredibly low. These birthing islands of raw geology are separated by vast, terrifying stretches of open, illuminated water. To look out from one drift is to see mostly brightly lit emptiness, punctured only by distant, violent spasms where other landmasses are being violently forged.

Despite the hostility, life—and intelligence—has touched this place. Clinging desperately to the newly formed, shivering spires of rock is sparse vegetation: translucent, whip-like kelp strands that seem spun from glass, anchoring themselves into the cooling fissures. They don't photosynthesize; they feed on the raw mineral discharge of the forming rock. Rarely, a piece of fauna glides past—flat, skate-like creatures, completely transparent save for their nervous systems, riding the violent thermal currents kicked up by the geological births.

More strange are the marks of builders. Scattered across the most stable of the new-formed rocks are crude, primitive structures. They seem impossibly fragile against the environment. Thick ropes braided from the glassy kelp fibers are lashed around stone spurs, tethering rough-hewn baskets of woven basalt fiber weighted with unworked ore chunks. Perhaps they are traps, or perhaps offerings to the roaring tumult. On a sheltered ledge of a cooling iron-spire, there might be a small, beehive-shaped hut stacked from raw slate, held together only by friction and hope. Inside, a single, crudely chipped stone spear leans against a wall.

There are no complex machines here, only the simplest applications of leverage and knots, humanity (or something like it) trying to catch crumbs from the table of a violent creation event. The dynamic is one of constant, terrifying flux. You might tether yourself to a spire of rock that didn't exist an hour ago, only to have it shatter back into raw energy in the next minute as the turbulent forces of the high-sea shift. The noise beats against you, a physical pressure, the sound of a universe hammering itself into being, over and over again.
