This is the realm of the Sky-Mires.

It is a landscape elevated miles above a planetary surface that is no longer visible, lost beneath perpetual, crushing haze. Up here, the air is thin, but you will rarely breathe it. The primary medium of existence is warm, suspended water.

The terrain is defined by colossal peaks of primordial granite. These are not jagged mountains; aeons of erosion have ground them down into immense, rounded humps, polished smoother than river stones, yet on a continental scale. They are the color of wet iron and bruised plums.

The weirdness of this place lies in gravity’s infidelity. These ancient peaks exert a localized, intense pull that hoards moisture. As a result, vast, globular oceans cling to the sides and summits of the mountains, defying the empty drop below. These aren't just clouds; they are coherent, swimmable bodies of dense liquid, warm as blood, suspended in the high atmosphere.

To travel here is to swim through a twilight world. The light is perpetually dim, filtered through kilometers of hovering water and atmospheric vapor, casting everything in saturated hues of deep teal and indigo.

Despite the dimness, the vegetation is riotously lush. Because the water is warm and nutrient-rich from hydrothermal seeps in the ancient rock, plant life chokes every available surface. It is not algal or delicate. Massive, rubbery fronds, the size of mainsails, anchor to the stone. They are dark crimson and oily black, adapted to absorb the scant light. Thickets of cylindrical, segmented stalks, like fleshy bamboo, create dense, impenetrable forests underwater. Everything is slick, coated in thick, protective mucilage. The density of life is profound; you are always brushing against a slick leaf, a velvet patch of aquatic moss, or a drifting tangle of root structures that draw moisture from the humid air between the water-blobs.

It is shockingly quiet. The sheer volume of water and the dense mats of vegetation dampen all vibration. There is no crashing surf, only the muffled, hydraulic hush of deep currents. The loudest sound is often the thrum of your own pulse caused by the pressure.

Yet, the landscape is intensely dynamic. The water is never still. Powered by internal heat from the rock peaks, massive convection currents circulate silently. Plumes of hot, silt-laden water rise like slow-motion smoke, while cooler, clearer water sinks in twisting columns. The vegetation grows with visible speed in this hothouse environment, unfurling new leaves that shudder in the thermal drafts.

Sometimes, the gravity fluctuates. A massive globule of water attached to one peak might stretch, deform, and then tear away, drifting across the open air gap to collide and merge with another suspended sea. To move between the peaks, one must swim to the edge of a water-mass, push through the surprisingly firm meniscus, and maintain momentum to "fall" through the humid air until splashing into the next clinging ocean.

Animal life is exceptionally rare. You might spend days swimming through the warm, dark forests and see nothing that moves under its own power. Perhaps, rarely, a translucent, gelatinous ribbon, meters long, will drift past in the current, absorbed entirely in the slow filtration of microorganisms.

There are traces of something else, though. On the flattest, most ancient shelves of submerged rock, you might find indentations. They are not buildings. They are smooth, perfectly hemispherical depressions worn into the granite, arranged in complex, sprawling spirals that span miles. They feel slightly warmer than the surrounding stone. They seem too precise for natural erosion, yet bear no mark of tool or machine—as if the rock was convinced to change its shape by some long-forgotten, patient presence that needed a place to rest in the warmth.
