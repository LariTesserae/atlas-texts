This is a landscape where geology has been entirely supplanted by biology. There is no stone here, no soil born of eroded rock. The world is built.

You stand upon a terrace of hardened, calcified secretions—a material somewhere between bone, ancient coral, and industrial concrete, yet secreted by countless generations of minuscule architects over millennia. It feels osseous underfoot, slick with perpetual moisture. The terrain is a vast, complex topography of these biological structures: towering spires like immense, hardened termite mounds that scrape the sky, connected by sprawling, sweeping bridges and buttresses that form artificial valleys and ridges. It is an architecture of instinct, weathered by time into smooth, settled curves, immense and intricate.

The altitude is significant. The air is thin but surprisingly warm, holding a heavy, cloying humidity. Below, the "valleys" between the great constructed ridges are filled with a thick, grey mist that rarely clears. Above, the sky is a bruise of permanent twilight, heavily overcast, allowing only a dim, diffused grey light to filter down. It is a world of perpetual deep dusk.

Because of the dampness—the constant dripping from overhangs, the slick condensation on every surface, the shallow pools that gather in the cupped depressions of the architecture—life has colonized the structure aggressively. The "stone" is carpeted in thick, spongy mats of moss and liverworts in deep greens and browns. Ferns with fronds the size of doors explode from crevices, their roots anchoring into the accumulated detritus of countless life cycles. Massive pitcher plants and bromeliads cling to the vertical faces, their bowls overflowing with rainwater and drowning insects. It is lush, but paracitic; a green skin growing upon a dead, constructed skeleton.

The defining characteristic of this place, however, is the noise. It is deafening.

The air is a solid wall of sound, a relentless, high-pitched roar created by billions of stridulating entities. The fauna here is overwhelming, a density of animal life that defies belief. Every surface is crawling. If you place your hand on a balustrade of calcified lattice, you cover a dozen tiny, armored beetles. The air is thick with clouds of drifting moths and heavy, droning flyers that bump blindly into obstacles in the gloom.

In the crevices of the great walls, countless crustaceans scuttle in the wet dark. Millipedes as long as an arm graze on the moss carpets. The noise comes from the sheer volume of them—the clicking of chitin on hard surfaces, the whir of wings, and above all, the endless chirping, scraping calls of legions of insectoid creatures echoing off the hard, complex geometry of the landscape. It is a cacophony so intense it becomes a physical pressure on the eardrums.

Nothing changes quickly here. The grand biological structures were built over epochal timeframes and are now slowly, patiently weathering under the warm damp. The life cycle is frantic and fast, but the landscape itself exists in a slow, deep time, a monumental testament to the sheer accumulation of biological effort. It is a towering, dark hive-world, breathing its own humid air, roaring with its own excess life.
