The world here is vertical, a limitless lattice of manufactured strata ascending into a blinding sky. There is no ground, no horizon where earth meets air. If you look down through the gaps in the heavy grate-flooring, past the dripping undersides of catwalks and the thick bundles of corroded conduits, you see only more of the same—a descending fractal of girders and platforms fading into a warm, brownish haze miles below. If a wrench is dropped, it never hits "bottom"; it just clatters against metal further and further down until the sound is swallowed by the distance.

Every surface is constructed. The environment is a colossal, ancient industrial mechanism whose original purpose has been forgotten, settled into a loud, slow-moving equilibrium. It is built from heavy cast iron, thick concrete cladding, riveted steel plates, and ceramics glazed by centuries of heat. But the artificiality is choked by life.

This is a hanging jungle suspended within a machine. Vegetation is lush and aggressive, capitalizing on the warmth and perpetual dampness. Thick, ropey vines, engineered by time to grip smooth metal, strangle massive support columns like constrictor snakes. Their leaves are broad and waxy, designed to shed the constant condensation. Where massive gears slowly turn—some the size of town squares—mosses and ferns have colonized the non-moving parts, creating thick, spongy blankets of vibrant green over rusted iron.

There are no rivers, but everything is wet. The air is heavy with humidity and the smell of warm grease, wet metal, and decaying plant matter. Water constantly bleeds from the structure itself. Cooling towers vent thick plumes of steam that condense on overhead plates, raining down in perpetual lukewarm showers. Pipes weep moisture, creating slick, algae-covered waterfalls down the faces of concrete bulkheads. Puddles collect in the sagging centers of metal walkways, pooling until they overflow into the level below.

The light is intense. Because of the extreme elevation, the sun strikes hard against the upper tiers, filtering down through the layers of mesh and foliage in sharp, varied shafts. Where the light hits the wet metal and glossy leaves, the world glitters. It is a bright, steamy greenhouse built from the skeleton of a dead titan.

The density is suffocating. There is no empty space. Every volume is filled with pipes, structural bracing, wiring harnesses thick as tree trunks, or the encroaching biomass. Walking requires navigating a three-dimensional maze of gantries, ladders, and precarious bridges formed by fallen machinery parts now welded together by rust and roots.

It is incredibly loud. The background radiation of sound is a deep, resonant thrum that vibrates through the soles of the feet—the heartbeat of the massive, slow-turning engines deep within the structure. Layered over this is the hiss of escaping steam, the rhythmic *clank-shriek* of ancient chains hauling unknown weights, and the ceaseless dripping of water on metal drums.

And above the industrial din is the shrieking of fauna. Life is abundant here, adapted to a world of hard edges and long drops. Small, agile primates with gripping claws swing effortlessly from cabling and vine alike, their fur stained with rust and oil. Brightly colored avian creatures nest in the sheltered housings of disused floodlights; their calls are metallic and piercing, evolved to cut through the noise of machinery. Insects the size of dinner plates crawl along the heated pipes, feeding on the specially adapted fungi that thrive on hydrocarbon residue.

The entire system is in a state of slow, established flux. Rust blooms like slow coral, eventually cemented by calcified mineral deposits from the dripping water. A great iron beam might take two hundred years to finally corrode through and shift five inches, a seismic event for the local ecosystem. The giant ventilation fans turn with agonizing slowness, taking an hour to complete a single revolution, their blades thick with decades of compacted dust and moss. It is a world of churning, patient, mechanical metabolism, overgrown and enduring.
