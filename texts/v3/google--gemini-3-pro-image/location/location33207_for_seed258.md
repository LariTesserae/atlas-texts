This is a landscape located in the high altitudes, a vast regional plateau suspended above an unseen world below. There is no geology here in the traditional sense—no granite, no limestone, no soil derived from weathered stone. The entire reality, stretching to the distant, hazy horizons, is constructed from living matter. It is a built environment, but the architect and the material are one and the same: vegetation rendered on a geological scale.

The terrain is composed of an immense, seamless tapestry of fused botanical elements that have hardened into the consistency of cured concrete or iron. The "ground" is a sprawling lattice of calcified roots, trunks gigantic beyond measure, and overlapped, shield-like leaves the size of town squares, all welded together into a single, continuous substrate. It feels artificial in its totality, a planet-sized mega-structure of cellulose and lignin.

Despite being made entirely of life, the landscape’s appearance is one of raw, violent emergence that has been inexplicably frozen in time. The topography looks like a photograph of an explosion just as it breached the surface, or thick dough mid-rise, suddenly petrified. Great bulging ridges of fibrous material heave upwards, looking untamed and embryonic, as if they were just beginning to take shape before being arrested forever. There are no smooth gradients or gentle slopes; everything is lumpy, unfinished, and chaotically formed, yet possessing the structural integrity of bedrock.

The scale is enormous and open. You can see for dozens of miles across this undulating, botanical skin. Yet, the density of distinct features is low. Rising from the churned floor at vast intervals—kilometers apart—are colossal bastions of the same fused plant-matter. They look like half-formed termite mounds the size of mountains, or bulbous, organic skyscrapers whose growth was violently interrupted. They stand isolated against the sky, monumental and solitary.

The dominant sensation here is wetness. The air is warm, thick, and saturated, clinging to the skin like a heavy blanket. Although there are no flowing rivers or crashing rain—the environment is static, trapped in equilibrium—water is ubiquitous. Every surface of the hardened plant-scape weeps. Moisture beads on the calcified leaves and runs in slow, viscous rivulets down the raw flanks of the root-ridges. Shallow, perfectly still pools of clear water sit in the depressions of the terrain, mirroring the sky. The atmosphere is so burdened with moisture that banks of warm, thick mist hang motionless between the distant spires, obscuring their bases.

A deafening, constant sound fills the air. It is not the noise of wind or moving water, but a deep, resonant thrum that vibrates in the chest. It sounds like the lowest octave of a massive pipe organ being held indefinitely. This noise seems to emanate from the structures themselves, perhaps the resonant frequency of billions of tons of water-gorged, hardened plant matter existing under immense pressure. It is a loud, monotonous drone that never varies in pitch or intensity.

Fauna is almost nonexistent. The only animal life to be found are tiny, translucent organisms, smaller than a fingernail, that cling almost invisibly to the wet undersides of the massive, petrified leaves, living their entire lives within a single bead of condensation. They are utterly insignificant against the backdrop of the silent, roaring, static giants of biology that form the world. The light is normal daylight, filtering evenly through the high moisture, casting soft shadows that do not move. The landscape is a grand, wet, unfinished monument, screaming its existence into an eternal stillness.
