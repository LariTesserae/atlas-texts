This is the high gorge city of Oakhaven, though the name is a misnomer; there are no oaks here, only colossal, nameless timber-giants whose crowns are lost in the perpetual mist and whose roots form the fundamental architecture of the settlement.

The location is a deep, high-altitude cleft in ancient mountains, a place where the clouds snag and condense. The scale is cathedral-like, a vertical slot where the sky is merely a brighter shade of gray far above. It is eternally cool, a damp chill that seeps through wool and leather. The air itself feels thick enough to ring out, saturated with atomized water from the countless waterfalls that streak the canyon walls and the roaring river that churns unseen in the darkness far below the lowest habitable levels.

Vegetation is not merely present; it is the absolute dictator of reality. There is almost no exposed stone. The sheer walls of the gorge are upholstered in mosses feet thick, dripping sponges of vibrant green. Ferns the size of small houses cantilever over the abyss. The city is built entirely within and upon the lattice of massive, ancient trees that have spanned the gorge, their trunks thicker than the largest stone towers of lower lands.

The "ground" is a complex, shaky weave of massive roots, packed mulch, and living fungal shelves, all slick with eternal moisture. You do not walk on earth; you walk on biomass in various stages of life and decay.

The built environment is a dense, chaotic hive of primitive ingenuity woven directly into this living matrix. It is an urban space, packed tight. Thousands of people live here in structures cantilevered off tree trunks, suspended in massive baskets of woven vine, or hollowed out of the woody tumors of the giant trees. Bridges are everywhere—terrifyingly complex webs of braided hemp and rough-hewn planks that crisscross the misty void, connecting neighborhoods separated by hundreds of feet of empty, dripping air.

The technology is entirely based on fiber, wood, and bone. There is no metal here, save for the rarest, rusted heirloom blade. Yet, the engineering is sophisticated; enormous counter-weighted pulley systems made of ironwood gears and thick ropes haul goods vertically through the layers of the canopy. Aqueducts of split bamboo channel water from the weeping cliff faces into communal cisterns. It is a city of master weavers and woodworkers, where knots are stronger than nails.

The atmosphere is intensely turbulent and loud. The roar of water is the foundational sound, a ceaseless white noise. Layered over that is a deafening biological cacophony. The fauna is overwhelming. Troops of shaggy, dexterous primates swarm the rigging of the city, as common as rats, thieves of food and shiny objects. Birds with plumage of iridescent blues and reds, evolved to pierce the gloom, scream from the high branches. Insects the size of dinner plates buzz heavily through the humid air. The human element adds to the din: shouts of workers, the crying of wares in cramped, precarious bazaars, the rhythmic thud of axes fighting back the ever-encroaching growth.

It is a high-energy, unstable environment. The city is in a constant state of frantic maintenance. The vegetation grows with terrifying speed in the wet environment; a walkway left untended for a week will be consumed by creepers. The "turbulence" here is the endless, sweaty war between the builders and the jungle. Structures rot and are rebuilt in days. A shift in the root systems might tilt an entire district five degrees, necessitating an immediate, frantic re-lashing of supports. It is an anthill of human activity, a desperate, vibrant bazaar clinging to the wet, green walls of a mountain cathedral, eternally dripping, eternally growing, and eternally loud.
