This is a landscape at the very end of geology. It is a place that has been ground down, washed over, and eroded for so many eons that the very concept of a "hill" has been forgotten by the earth.

The terrain is a vast, near-perfect flatness extending to a hazy horizon on all sides. It is a low basin, sitting just barely above sea level, dominated by bedrock that has been polished smooth by water and time. There is no soil, only pockets of warm, silty sludge collected in shallow depressions. The ground is a mosaic of varying shades of wet stone—iron greys, oxidized reds, and bruised purples—all glistening under a uniform, bright sky spread with high, thin clouds.

It is incredibly wet and oppressively warm. The air is thick, heavy with humidity that feels like a physical weight. It is a sauna without walls. Because the land is so flat, the water has nowhere to go. It sits on the surface in an endless network of tepid, ankle-deep pools, sluggish channels, and stretches of weeping rock. Heat haze shimmers constantly above the wetter patches, blurring the already indistinct horizon. If it isn't currently raining, it just has, or is about to. The atmosphere is one of constant, warm condensation.

Vegetation is strangely absent given the abundance of water and warmth. The ancient rock offers no purchase for roots. There are no trees, no shrubs, not even grasses. Instead, the "plant" life is microbial and algal. Slick, gelatinous mats coat the submerged rocks in vibrant, sometimes unsettling colors—neon greens, mustard yellows, and deep, bloody reds. Occasionally, in deeper sediment pockets, a lone, prehistoric-looking reed might stand stiffly, thick and leathery, topped with a heavy spore cluster, separated from its nearest neighbor by hundreds of yards of open water and stone.

Despite the lack of cover, the landscape is incredibly loud. The sonic environment is a dense wall of biological noise. The air vibrates with the ceaseless, high-pitched thrum of massive insects—dragonflies with two-foot wingspans hawking over the pools, and armored beetles the size of fists droning heavily through the humid air. Beneath this electric buzz is a constant chorus from the water: deep, resonating croaks, wet bellows, and sudden, violent splashes that speak to large things moving just beneath the surface of the opaque pools.

Fauna is abundant, but spread thinly across the immense scale of the landscape. You might look out over a mile of shimmering wet rock and see only three distinct living shapes, but you know thousands more are hidden in the mud and water. Large, amphibious creatures, vaguely salamander-like but bulky and rough-skinned, haul themselves slowly onto the warm rock slabs to bask, their slate-grey bodies blending perfectly with the wet stone. In the channels, oily wakes V-shape across the surface, indicating the passage of heavy, unseen swimmers feeding on the thick algal bloom.

The dynamic of the world is glacially slow. There are no seasons here, only centuries-long cycles of slightly wetter or slightly drier conditions. The erosion is practically complete; the water is merely polishing what is already smooth. It is a world settled into a permanent, noisy stasis, a humid cradle of ancient biology continuing its slow, loud existence on the flattest earth imaginable.
