This place is a vertical wound in the sky. You are inside a colossal ventilation shaft, a cathedral of industry whose floor and ceiling are lost in the atmospheric haze above and below. The scale is deceptive; while the shaft itself is enormous—perhaps a kilometer across—the traversable space is minuscule, limited to catwalks, gantries, and maintenance platforms clinging precariously to the inner circumference.

Every surface is manufactured. There is no stone, no soil, only riveting, girders, corrugated plating, and immense, dormant machinery bolted to the walls. It is a totally built environment, yet it has been reclaimed. This structure is ancient, weathered not by geological time but by the patient, relentless grind of industrial endurance and subsequent neglect. The metal is swollen with rust, showing the deep, intricate texture of established erosion, held together as much by habit as by structural integrity.

The primary sensation is a suffocating, warm dampness. It is not raining in the traditional sense; rather, the entire shaft acts as a condenser for the clouds it pierces. The air is thick, heavy, and warm, clinging to the skin like an oily rag. Moisture drips constantly—a steady, chaotic percussion on metal plating. Rivulets of warm water run down the walls, pooling on sagging walkways before spilling over edges into the void below. The light is diffused and milky, filtering down from an opening far above, bright enough to see clearly but lacking distinct shadows, giving everything a flat, humid luminescence.

In this warm, wet metal ecosystem, vegetation has gone berserk. It is a vertical jungle rooted in rust and grease. Lush, heavy vines, thick as a person’s thigh, wrap around pipes and conduit bundles, their weight threatening to pull down the structures they cling to. Enormous ferns sprout from electrical junction boxes, their fronds slick with condensation. Thick mats of velvet moss cover entire bulkheads, drinking the constant moisture and slowly eating the iron beneath. The colors are deep, bruised greens and oxidised oranges.

The space is deafening. It is not the sharp clang of active construction, but a continuous, overwhelming roar that vibrates in the chest. It is the sound of massive air displacement, a wind-tunnel effect created by thermal drafts rushing up the shaft, compounded by the groan of the structure itself settling under the weight of water and biomass. Somewhere far below, beneath the visible fog, ancient, mechanized components still turn in slow, patient cycles, sending low-frequency tremors up through the girders—a deep, unending industrial thrum.

Despite the hostility of the architecture, life is abundant here. The air space in the center of the shaft is mostly empty—a terrifying, hollow drop—but the periphery is alive. Flocks of avian creatures, adapted with oily feathers and gripping claws, nest in the rusted alcoves of turbine housings. Insects the size of dinner plates crawl slowly along the wet undersides of platforms, grazing on the algae slime. You glimpse things skittering across the vertical faces—multi-limbed mammals that move with the uncanny adhesion of geckos, their calls lost in the overarching roar of the shaft. It is a weird, isolated biome where nature has evolved to treat machinery as bedrock.
