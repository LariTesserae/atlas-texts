This is the Ossuary Plateau, a drowned continent of cold, pressurized water located far above the abyssal plains, yet still unimaginably deep. There is no rock here, no sediment derived from geology. The entire landscape, stretching into the dim distance, is constructed.

It is a world built from the calcified remains of a billion years of pre-photosynthetic titans.

You hang suspended in near-freezing water, a liquid so heavy it feels like pressing against cold gelatin. The light is a failing twilight, a monochrome gloom filtering down from a surface miles above that has never seen the sun, only perpetual atmospheric gray. Visibility is surprisingly good due to the lack of organic particulate matter, allowing the eye to travel across vast, open spaces.

The "terrain" below and around you is smooth, polished, and rounded by an eternity of erosion. Great sweeping arches, bulbous towers the size of mountains, and intricate, lattice-like canyons are all formed from a substance somewhere between ivory, obsidian, and old bone. This is the "built" environment: the fused, fossilized exoskeletons of a forgotten mega-fauna that once grew upon each other layer by layer, raising this plateau out of the depths before going extinct eons ago. There is no natural substrate left; if there ever was rock, it is buried miles beneath this shell of ancient biology.

It is a monument to deep time, settled into its final form. Every edge has been worn away by the violence of the water.

Despite the appearance of frozen stability, the environment is intensely dynamic. The silence is heavy, a crushing pressure on the ears, broken only by a persistent, ultra-low-frequency vibration that thrums through the water column. This is the sound of the currents.

The water does not flow; it collides. Massive, unseen rivers within the ocean—internal waves hundreds of feet high—slam against the smooth bone-structures. The turbulence is extreme, a chaotic churning that would rip a modern machine apart. Yet, because the structures are so ancient and smoothed, the water flows around them with deceptive grace, creating powerful, shearing vortices in the open spaces between the towers.

There is absolutely no plant life. No green, no kelp, no fungal mats. The ecosystem is entirely dependent on the energy generated by the crushing currents and the slow dissolution of the ancient bone-substrate.

Yet, fauna is abundant. The wildlife has evolved not merely to survive the turbulence, but to require it.

Sleek, pale creatures, ranging from the size of a hand to the size of a bus, cling to the polished surfaces using powerful suction pads and hook-like claws. They are flattened, hydrodynamic things, their bodies armored in similar osseous material to the landscape. They move slowly, creeping along the path of least resistance, scraping nutrients from the ancient calcium.

In the open water between the spires, enormous schools of translucent, eel-like organisms ride the violent upwellings. They do not swim; they fold their bodies into rigid shapes to catch the chaotic flows, hurtling through the gloom in synchronized chaos, mouths open to filter invisibly small mineral particles kicked up by the grinding currents.

Occasionally, a larger shadow detaches itself from a bone-cliff—a slow-moving predator, perhaps blind, navigating solely by the pressure waves of the turbulent water, hunting the things that cling to the dead world.

It is a place of cold, hard surfaces and violent motion, a cathedral of extinct life serving as the foundation for a strange, desperate new one, all hidden beneath miles of crushing black water.
