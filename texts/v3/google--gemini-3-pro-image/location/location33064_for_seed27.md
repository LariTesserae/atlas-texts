The air here is thin, painfully dry, and permanently cold. It tastes of ozone and ancient dust. You stand on a high plateau, a region immense enough that the horizon shows the distinct curvature of the world, yet the sky above is a dim, bruised violet, lacking a clear sun.

This world does not have soil. It does not have rock. The ground under your feet, the mountains in the distance, the valleys dropping away into purple obscurity—it is all vegetation. But it is vegetation that has never known rain.

The terrain is composed of the braided, fused, and calcified trunks of gargantuan organisms that defy terrestrial biology. Imagine redwoods magnified by a factor of twenty, but made of a substance harder than ironwood, drier than parchment, and colored in shades of rust, slate-grey, and oxidized copper. This is the "Ur-Weave."

These colossal plant-forms grew aeons ago, competing for a light source that has since dimmed. Over millennia, they choked each other out, fell, decayed without moisture into skeletal husks, and were overgrown by new layers. The current landscape is the wrinkled, deeply eroded surface of this planetary biomass, smoothed not by flowing water, but by the relentless friction of grit-laden winds over millions of years. The topography is rolling, defined by the immense, sweeping curves of petrified grain and knot-holes the size of craters.

There is no water chemistry here. Life relies on direct energy transduction. The "plants" are actually complex biological superconductors.

The "city" is not built upon this surface; it is carved into and grown out of it. The architecture is a sparse collection of towering, spiraling structures that resemble dried seed pods or thorns, jutting hundreds of feet into the twilight air. They are separated by miles of open, textured ground. These structures are inhabited by a civilization operating on technology far beyond mechanical physics. They have re-ignited the dormant conductive pathways of the ancient Ur-Weave.

You see this technology as veins of softly glowing light—electric blues and pale golds—pulsing just beneath the translucent, fibrous skin of the ground and the buildings. The city is a nervous system laid bare. There are no roads, only currents of energy that inhabitants can ride using gravimetric harnesses.

It is incredibly loud. The primary sound is the wind. In this high, thin atmosphere, the wind doesn't rush; it screams through the trillions of flute-like hollows and splintered gaps in the petrified vegetation. The planet itself acts as a massive, discordant pipe organ, producing a ceaseless, vibrating drone that you feel in your teeth.

Beneath this drone is the chittering and clanking of abundant fauna. There are no soft bodies here. The creatures are arthropodal things made of silicate chitin and conductive metals, evolved to feed on the ambient electrical charges of the Weave or to graze on the brittle, metallic "leaves" that grow like jagged crystals in sheltered crevices.

Great herds of multi-legged grazers, resembling ambulatory geodes the size of bison, scrape their way across the plains, their movement creating showers of sparks. In the air, flocks of avian constructs with wings of spun glass dive and swoop, hunting energy signatures. The ecosystem is a frantic exchange of voltage and static charge. The very air crackles, and movement is constant—a dry, electric fever dream of life clinging to an ancient, wooden corpse of a world.
