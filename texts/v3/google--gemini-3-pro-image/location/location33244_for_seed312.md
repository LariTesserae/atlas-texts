The air here is thin, painfully sharp in the lungs, and utterly devoid of warmth. This is a world defined by immense, crushing verticality. You stand on the spine of a continent, a vast plateau splintered by mountain ranges that are geologically young—violent upheavals of rock that haven't yet been dulled by time. The peaks are jagged, saw-toothed ridges of granite and slate that tear at the sky, stretching away into distances that make the mind reel. The scale is so enormous that weather patterns seem frozen against the horizon; a storm blowing fifty miles away looks like a static bruise on the blue sky.

It is a cold desert, but it is damp. The moisture doesn't come from generous rain, but from the reluctance of the cold to let go of anything. Every morning, a heavy, freezing fog clings to the lower slopes and fills the chasms. By noon, the weak but piercing high-altitude sun melts the frost, turning the thin soil into a slick, black paste and coating the rocks in a treacherous sheen of condensation. By twilight, it all freezes solid again.

Vegetation is a grudge held against the rock. There are no trees here—they gave up thousands of feet below. Instead, the ground is patinated with crusty lichens in shades of oxidized copper, sulfur yellow, and burnt orange. In the wind-shadows of boulders, tough, wiry grasses and cushions of dense moss form spongy hummocks that squelch freezing water when stepped on.

Yet, in this inhospitable vastness, there is an intense, improbable density of life.

Clinging to the precipitous flank of one of the largest mountains is a city that defies gravity and sense. It is a sprawling, layered hive of humanity stacked upon itself. Because flat land doesn't exist, the architecture climbs. Buildings made of rough-hewn grey stone, dark timber hauled up from the distant lowlands, and sod bricks are piled five, six, ten stories high, leaning against each other for support across narrow chasms. Terraces are carved into the living rock, linked by precarious wooden suspension bridges that sway over thousand-foot drops.

It is a primitive density. There is no whir of machinery, only the sounds of manual labor muffled by the vast space—the *clink-clink-clink* of chisels on stone, the rhythmic thud of hammers on cold copper, the groaning of yaks under heavy loads. Smoke from tens of thousands of hearths, burning dried dung and precious wood, forms a permanent grey pall over the settlement, smelling acrid and thick.

The streets are narrow, winding alleyways barely wide enough for two people to pass without shoulders brushing, often becoming steep staircases slick with mud and ice. The density is overwhelming; humanity is packed tight to share warmth. You move through a constant press of bodies wrapped in thick, felted wool and furs.

Fauna is abundant, woven into the fabric of the city. Great, shaggy beasts of burden—something between an ox and a mountain goat, with wool so long it drags on the ground—are everywhere, their breath steaming in plumes. They occupy the ground floors of buildings, their body heat warming the residents above. Smaller, nimble goat-creatures clatter over the slate roofs, scavenging. Broad-winged carrion birds circle patiently in the thermal updrafts rising from the city's heat.

The overall impression is one of immense, quiet solidity fighting against a hostile expanse. The silence of the surrounding peaks is absolute, a vacuum that tries to suck the life out of the settlement. But the city holds its own, a stubborn, dense clot of warmth and slow, patient industry clinging to the frozen edge of the world. Things change slowly here; a new terrace might take a generation to carve, a stone path a century to wear smooth. It is a place built by hands, numb with cold, over endless years.
