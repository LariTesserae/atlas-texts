The entry is difficult. You have to squeeze through a jagged fissure in cooling basalt, a throat of rock that angles sharply downward away from the surface world. The air immediately changes—it loses all humidity, becoming aggressively dry and intensely warm, like the inside of a kiln just after firing.

You drop down into the cavity. It is not large—perhaps thirty meters across and half as high—a hidden pocket formed by a gas bubble trapped in ancient lava that has only recently cracked open. The walls are not smooth. Because the formation is geologically young, the rock is sheer, fractured into razor-sharp edges and obsidian splinters that catch what little light exists.

The light is dim, a sullen, heavy red glow that emanates not from above, but from beneath the fractured floor, where deep fissures reveal veins of slowly cooling magma far below. This radiant heat is the engine of the room.

There is absolutely no water here. No dampness on the walls, no condensation, no pools. The chemistry of this place is entirely anhydrous. Yet, the cavern is lush.

It is a garden made of minerals and metal.

Crystalline structures riot across the floor and up the sharp walls. They mimic the shapes of organic life with uncanny precision but share none of their biology. Great fractal ferns made of bismuth compounds unfurl in iridescent geometric patterns, their 'fronds' rigid and sharp enough to slice skin. Clusters of quartz and selenium crystals imitate thick succulents, glowing with a faint, internal piezoelectric charge generated by the geothermal pressure. There are towering stalks of sulfur and arsenic sulfides, brilliant yellows and poisonous reds, that branch out like rigid trees, their leaves thin sheets of translucent mica.

This vegetation does not sway. It is brittle, fixed, and immensely heavy. It feeds not on rain and soil, but on the mineral vapors rising from the cracks and the intense, steady heat. It is a frozen explosion of geology pretending to be biology.

The density is peculiar. While the individual crystal-plants are complex and lush in their own structure, they are not crowded together. There is significant negative space. Wide avenues of broken, glassy floor separate the major growths, allowing the heat to rise unimpeded.

The first thing that truly assaults you, however, is not the heat or the strange sight, but the noise. This hidden pocket is deafeningly loud.

A foundational, resonant bass note vibrates through the soles of your feet—the grinding, tectonic thrum of the planet operating far below. Layered over this is a ceaseless, high-pitched cacophony. The crystal flora, under the stress of the intense heat and internal pressures, is constantly micro-fracturing and re-annealing. The sound is like millions of glass wind chimes being thrusted into a jet engine—a screaming, singing lattice of sharp pings, ringing tones, and sudden cracks.

Despite this overwhelming wall of sound, the environment is static. The temperature never fluctuates. The light never wavers. The deafening noise is a constant, unchanging tone. Nothing grows perceptibly; nothing dies. It is a loud, hot equilibrium stuck in time.

Abundant fauna thrives in this sonic furnace, adapted to the dry heat and silicate ecology. They are multi-legged arthropods, ranging from the size of a thumb to the size of a dinner plate. Their exoskeletons are not chitin, but biologically secreted ceramics and integrated metals—iron pyrite shells that glitter in the red dark, and legs tipped with diamond dust for gripping the glassy terrain. They scuttle everywhere, a flowing carpet of armored life, their metallic movements adding a dry, skittering friction to the ambient roar. They do not eat one another. They climb the crystal trees and use mandible-like tools of hardened stone to scrape away mineral efflorescence that blooms on the mica leaves.

In the center of the space, where the heat is most intense, there is evidence of visitation. A small area has been partially cleared of sharp debris. There is a rough semicircle of stacked basalt slabs—too clumsy to be a wall, perhaps a windbreak against a heat current that no longer flows. Leaning against it are tools crafted with Stone Age simplicity but shaped from difficult materials. A pry bar made from a blackened, heat-hardened femur of some surface behemoth. A hammerstone made of meteorite iron, crudely lashed to a handle of braided copper wire.

They are dusted with sulfur ash, abandoned long ago. Whoever came down here, braving the heat and the noise to harvest the crystal foliage with primitive tools, they did not stay, and they left little mark on the screaming, static eternity of the geode garden.
