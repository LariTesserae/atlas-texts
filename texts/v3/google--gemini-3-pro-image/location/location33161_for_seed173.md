The location is known as Sector 70-G, the "Sunken Foundry." It is a sprawling, submerged industrial complex, resting deep beneath the surface of a frigid northern ocean. There is no natural seabed here; the "ground" is a vast expanse of riveted iron plating, buckled and fused by centuries of pressure and chemical interplay. The "sky" is a ceiling of reinforced concrete buttresses and condensation-slicked girders, hanging heavy in the gloom above.

The entire environment is deeply submerged. The water is heavy, cold (hovering just above freezing), and tastes intensely of brine, rust, and machine oil. Visibility is poor, limited to pools of sickly yellow illumination cast by explosion-proof lamps caged in brass, many of which have failed over the centuries, leaving vast stretches of the facility in absolute darkness. The pressure is immense, a constant, crushing physical presence.

This is a totally constructed space; not a single shard of natural granite or basalt is visible. Every surface is artificial—cast iron, bronze, thick glass, concrete, and decaying polymers. The architecture is brutally functional, a cathedral scale of heavy industry. Gargantuan pipes, wide enough to drive a truck through, weave like petrified arteries through open lattice-work towers. Enormous gears, some the size of Ferris wheels, turn with agonizing slowness in the dark, their movements driven by geothermal vents tapped deep below the facility's foundations.

The space is ancient and heavily eroded, but stable. The corrosion has settled into a permanent state. Metal surfaces have been eaten away to resemble porous bone, covered in inches of hard, mineralized concretions and black, oily sludge. Edges that were once sharp are now rounded and bulbous with accumulated rust. The concrete is spalled, revealing the skeletal rebar beneath, which has bloomed into rusted coral-like formations.

Despite the decay, the facility is deafeningly loud. The water conducts sound with terrifying efficiency. A ceaseless, resonant thrum vibrates through the entire structure—the heartbeat of the ancient geothermal engines still blindly pumping. Every few minutes, a massive hydraulic piston somewhere in the dark fires with a sound like a depth charge, sending shockwaves through the water that rattle teeth. Rushing water roars through intake manifolds, creating powerful, artificial currents that can sweep an unwary traveler into grinding machinery.

Vegetation is almost non-existent. There are no kelp forests or sea grasses. The only plant life is a stubborn, slick black algae and microbial biofilm that coats the warmer pipes near the vents, slippery to the touch and foul-smelling.

Yet, despite the hostility and industrial clamor, animal life is surprisingly abundant. The fauna has adapted to this artificial reef. Massive schools of pale, blind cod navigate the complex pipe mazes by sound and pressure sense, feeding on the thick sludge. Crustaceans with heavy, calcified shells that resemble rusted bolts cling to the moving machinery, scraping away barnacles. Long, oily-skinned eels, thick as a man's thigh, nest inside broken generator casings, striking out at passing fish. In the shadowy upper reaches of the cathedral-like spaces, large, slow-moving cephalopods with skin textured like corroded iron drift between the girders, the apex predators of this rust ecosystem. They hunt amid the grinding gears, perfectly at home in the noise and the cold.
