The first impression is an assault on the senses: a blinding brilliance of light and a deafening, inescapable roar.

You are high up, at an altitude where the air is thin and brisk, biting at exposed skin. The light is intense, a cold, unfiltered solar radiation that glares off billions of wet surfaces, turning the atmosphere into a shimmering diffuser of diamond-dust mist. Visibility is high, stretching across immense, deep valleys, but the brightness forces a squint.

The sound is monolithic. It is a crushing amalgam of natural and artificial thunder. The primary layer is water—millions of tons of it—cascading down sheer cliffs in endless sheets, a landscape defined by vertical rivers. Beneath that aquatic crash is a deeper, vibrating mechanical thrum, a bass frequency that resonates in the chest cavity, originating from unseen industrial titans.

This is a place where geology is brand new, yet instantly conquered by botany. The terrain is raw, seemingly thrust upward only recently in tectonic time—jagged peaks, deep saw-tooth gorges, and unstable cliffs that have not yet been smoothed by eons of wind. But you cannot see the stone. The vegetation parameter is total.

Every square inch of this emerging earth is upholstered in life. It is a vertical wetland. There is no bare ground; substrate is a theoretical concept here. You walk upon yard-thick accumulated layers of sphagnum moss, liverworts, and soaking-wet root mats that bridge chasms in the rock. Trees are stunted by the altitude but grow in dense, gnarled interlocking thickets, their branches shaggy with epiphytes that hold gallons of rainwater. The plants *are* the architecture of the space. To move across the landscape is to climb over, under, and through a cold, dripping sponge of biomass. Water weeps from every leaf, pools in every depression, and runs in clear, freezing veins just beneath the surface of the moss.

Despite the density of the plant life, the scale of the landscape feels sparse and open. The valleys are wide basins of green air. The features—a distant peak, a plunging waterfall, a hanging valley—are separated by miles of treacherous, unoccupied space.

Embedded in this green vastness are rare, colossal marks of industry. They are few and far between—perhaps only two or three structures visible across a fifty-mile vista—but their scale is immense. Brutalist monoliths of weeping concrete and algae-stained steel are grafted directly into the canyon walls, straddling the largest waterfalls. These are not factories of production, but massive energy harvesters, hydroelectric citadels that funnel the screaming water through turbines hidden deep within the rock. They vent plumes of geothermal steam that join the constant cold mist of the waterfalls. They are the source of the mechanical undercurrent in the deafening soundscape.

There are almost no animals. The environment is too loud, too wet, too relentless for much sentient life. The world feels dedicated entirely to the slow, patient processes of geology and the blind, grasping growth of plants. Change happens on a massive scale, but slowly—the gradual uplift of a mountain range, the century-long accumulation of a peat layer. It is a high-energy environment frozen in a slow-motion explosion of water, rock, and green.
