This is a place defined first by its overwhelming volume, and second by its bone-deep cold. It is a broad, low-lying basin, a valley gouged recently—in geological terms—out of dark, fractured bedrock. The scale is regional; standing in the center, the jagged, newly sheered cliffs that define its edges are hazy walls in the distance, dim giants holding back a wider world.

The noise is ceaseless and physical. It is a roaring turbulence that vibrates in the chest cavities of anyone present. It is the sound of massive, pressurized geysers shrieking as they blast steam into freezing air, the grinding groan of unstable hillsides sloughing off tons of saturated earth, and a deeper, underlying thrum that sounds like subterranean machinery fighting itself.

The atmosphere is a freezing sauna. The temperature hovers just above freezing, yet the valley is incredibly lush. The air is so thick with moisture—from the geysers, from the sodden ground, from the incessant, chilling drizzle—that it feels aquatic. Visibility is perpetually low, cut by drifting banks of heavy, cold fog and the eternal twilight gloom. There is no direct sun, only a bruised, heavy grey ceiling of cloud that never breaks.

Because of the constant injection of geothermal heat and moisture into a cold environment, the vegetation is explosive and dense, fighting for space in the dim light. It is a black-green jungle adapted to perpetual November. Monstrous ferns with fronds the size of small boats droop heavy with water. Conifers, squat and incredibly thick-trunked to withstand the shifting ground, form tangled walls of oily needles. Everywhere, moss covers everything in foot-thick, water-logged carpets—chartreuse, deep emerald, and bruised purple mosses that drip constantly. The ground is a chaotic slurry of mud, moss, and sharp, shattered rock fragments that look like they were broken yesterday.

Embedded within this hyper-active wilderness are the remains of something else. They are not buildings in a recognizable sense, but scattered, colossal fragments of a technology that defies current understanding. Jutting from a mudslide might be a hundred-foot shard of material that looks like obsidian but possesses a faint, internal pearlescent pulse. A cliff face might actually be the sheared cross-section of a honeycomb structure made of a metal that feels warm to the touch and chimes melodically when struck by falling rock.

These artifacts are not dormant. They are the source of the valley's turbulence. They are damaged, misfiring engines of planetary scale, half-buried and leaking energies that terrify the local physics. A buried conduit might periodically vent a blast of anti-gravimetric force, causing a slurry of mud and trees to float briefly before crashing down. Another structure might be furiously pulling moisture from the air, creating a localized, perpetual downpour around itself.

The water is everywhere. The rocks weep constantly, trickles running down their faces. The ground squelches. Shallow, braided streams choked with sediment rush towards the lowest points of the valley, often flash-flooding as the landscape rearranges itself.

Life is abundant here, drawn by the strange heat and the furious growth, but it is hidden by the density and the dark. You feel it more than see it. Heavy shapes crash through the underbrush just out of sight. Things with too many legs scuttle over the warm, humming surfaces of the alien ruins. In the brief moments when the roaring geysers quiet, you can hear a chaotic chorus of croaks, shrieks, and guttural calls from creatures fighting for territory in this unstable, freezing Eden. It is a place of raw, violent energy, where ancient, incomprehensible power is slowly dying, taking the landscape down with it in a riot of noise and mud.
