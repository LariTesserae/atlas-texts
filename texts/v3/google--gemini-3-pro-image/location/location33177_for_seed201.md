The light here is aggressive. It is a high, thin light, unfiltered by the thick atmosphere of the lower world. It strikes the retina with a brilliance that makes the eyes water, casting shadows as sharp as obsidian blades. The air is frighteningly thin, so cold it feels searing in the lungs, leaving a metallic taste at the back of the throat.

This place is a high-altitude notch, a jagged wound near the summit of a mountain range that feels recently birthed from the earth. The geology is raw; there are no smoothed river stones or rounded hills here. Everything is shattered rock, cleaved granite slabs, and fields of scree that look unstable, as if a loud shout might bring the mountainside down. The scale is deceptive; it is a relatively small bowl, a hanging valley suspended over abyssal drops, perhaps only a half-mile across, bounded by vertical spires of rock that block the horizon.

Despite the terrifying altitude and the bitter cold, the bowl is shockingly, improbably lush. It is a concentrated explosion of life amidst emptiness. The vast majority of the volume here is just empty, freezing air between soaring rock faces, but wherever there is a ledge, a crevice, or a slight depression, vegetation throngs.

The dampness is a daily cycle. Overnight, everything freezes solid, coated in a rime of glittering diamond dust. As the blinding sun crests the peaks, the heat, though insufficient to warm the air much, is intense enough on surfaces to melt the frost instantly. The bowl begins to weep. Rivulets of meltwater trickle down the black rock faces; mosses, inches thick and colored deep violet and almost black to absorb the intense UV radiation, become sponges, dripping with ice-cold water. Strange, succulent-like dwarfed shrubs cling to the unstable slopes, their leaves waxy and thick, hoarding the transient moisture.

It is a silent world. The wind seems to pass overhead, leaving the air in the bowl still and muffled by the thinness and the damp moss. The sounds that exist are small but travel with crystal clarity: the *tink-tink* of water dripping onto slate, the sudden shift of gravel under hoof.

And there are many hooves. The fauna is abundant, adapted to this harsh, high-energy niche. Herds of thick-chested, shaggy creatures—somewhere between a goat and a muskox, with spiraled horns made of keratin that looks like carved wood—navigate the near-vertical rock faces with impossible grace. They graze silently on the purple mosses. They are watched by predators; sleek, snow-leopard-like hunters with pale, reflective fur that renders them nearly invisible against the frost and sun-bleached rock. The dynamic here is intense but quiet; the hunt happens in slow motion bursts of violence that end quickly, absorbed by the snow. Great birds, vultures with wingspans that fill the narrow sky, circle silently overhead on thermals generated by the sun-baked rock, waiting for the inevitable falls.

Evidence of humanity is scant, swallowed by the scale of the geology. You might find a windbreak constructed from crudely stacked slabs of slate in the lee of a large boulder, just big enough for one person to curl up behind. Inside, perhaps a blackened patch of stone where a small fire of dried moss was burned, and a single tool left behind—a scraper made from a chipped piece of volcanic glass, bound to a bone handle with dried sinew. They come here, perhaps, for the abundant game, braving the altitude and the blinding light, leaving only the faintest scratches on a landscape that feels like it was made yesterday.
