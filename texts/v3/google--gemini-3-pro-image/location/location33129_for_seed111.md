This place does not have a name you could pronounce, but if it could be translated, it might be called the Shuddering Flues.

To arrive here requires an ascent that would break most lungs, climbing higher than clouds, until the air is so thin it feels like breathing needles. Yet, despite the crushing altitude, the immediate environment is claustrophobically small. You are not standing on a peak looking down at the world; you are inside a geological wound.

The Flues are a series of interconnected vertical chimneys and narrow chasms, a dense cluster of rock throats opening to a sky that is rarely visible. The scale is cathedral-like—narrow, vertiginous, and enclosed by walls that shoot upward into gloom. It feels less like a landscape and more like the interior of a colossal, broken machine of stone.

The geology here is terrifyingly young. There are no rounded edges, no slopes softened by eons of wind. The rock—a monolithic, obsidian-dark basalt shot through with veins of iridescent violet mineral—looks as though it cooled only yesterday after being violently thrust from the earth's mantle. Every surface is a blade. Jagged spurs, razor-edged fins, and needle-point spires crowd the space, overlapping and interlocking in a dense, chaotic lattice. To move here is to risk being flensed.

It is perpetually twilight. The clefts are so deep and narrow that direct sunlight never reaches the bottom. The light is a bruised purple-grey, filtered down through kilometers of damp air and trapped cloud matter.

Despite the altitude and the darkness, it is damp. Not from rain—the rain freezes long before it falls this far—but from condensation. The rock sweats. Clouds get snagged on the jagged peaks above and slump down into the Flues, trapped. The cold air (it is always hovering just above freezing) is thick with vapor that clings to the glass-smooth faces of the basalt. Water slicks every surface, gathering in thimble-sized pockets, dripping with agonizing slowness from overhang to overhang, feeding shallow, stagnant pools hidden in the deepest recesses. Everything is slick to the touch, amplifying the danger of the sharp edges.

The vegetation is sparse, alien, and tenacious. It does not grow in soil—there is none—but anchors directly into the micro-fissures of the fresh rock. There are patches of "iron-moss," a coppery, abrasive lichen that seems to feed on the metallic veins in the stone, forming crusts that look like rust. Occasionally, you might see a "shard-bush"—a brittle, skeletal plant made of translucent, silicate stems that shatter if brushed against, surviving on the ambient moisture and mineral drips. They look less like living things and more like crystalline defects in the rock.

The true horror and wonder of the Flues, however, is the fauna. It is overwhelming. The sheer density of life in this inhospitable, razor-edged darkness is deeply unsettling.

The air is never silent. It is a deafening, echoing riot of screeches, clicks, and leathery flapping. The primary denizens are the "crag-bats"—not mammals, but reptile-avian hybrids with six-foot wingspans and dozens of needle teeth. They exist in the millions, clinging to the vertical walls in shifting, squabbling carpets of fur and scale. They nest in every conceivable crevice, their bodies adapted to the sharp rock with hardened underbellies. They do not hunt within the Flues; they launch themselves upward into the jet streams above the mountains to feed on high-altitude aerial plankton, returning to this dark, sharp womb to digest and breed.

Below them, scuttling over the slick, sharp rock, are legions of armored arthropods, things like flattened millipedes with chitinous shells as hard as stone, feeding on the guano rain falling from the bats above and the sparse iron-moss. The walls crawl with life. The air is thick with the smell of ammonia and cold, wet stone.

There is a deep strangeness here, a vibration you feel in your teeth more than hear. The "Shuddering" name comes from the rock itself. The geological processes here are slow, but active. The mountain range is still rising, groaning under immense tectonic pressure. Deep below, magma chambers shift, sending low-frequency tremors up through the basalt flues. You feel the stone vibrate beneath your hands; you hear deep, resonant thrums that seem to reset your heartbeat.

Time moves differently here. The lifecycle of a shard-bush is measured in centuries; the upward thrust of the mountains is a matter of millimeters per decade. Yet, the noise of the fauna is frenetic, immediate. It is a place of chaotic, loud biology thriving within a slow, cold, geological vice. You are surrounded by millions of beating hearts, squeezed into a dark crack at the top of the world, where the rock is sharp enough to cut you just by looking at it.
