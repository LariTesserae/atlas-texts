This is a landscape of immense, weeping clutter defined by a persistent, chilly dampness and a deafening acoustic presence.

You stand in a vast, low-lying basin that stretches toward a dim horizon, a region that feels geologically newborn. The terrain is not composed of rolling hills or flat plains, but is a chaotic splintering of the earth’s crust. It looks as though a massive plate of black volcanic glass and basalt was shattered upward from beneath, creating a dense forest of jagged spires, razor-edged ridges, and precarious stacks of rock that crowd the view. There is very little navigable ground; moving through this space requires constant clambering over slick, angled surfaces.

Water is ubiquitous, but rarely in the form of flowing rivers or calm lakes. Instead, the atmosphere itself is oversaturated. A heavy, cold mist clings to everything, condensing constantly on the dark stone. Every rock face streams with rivulets; every crevice is a deep, still pool of black water reflecting the iron-gray sky. The ground beneath the shattered rock is a sucking mire of volcanic ash and grit, permanently saturated.

There is absolutely no plant life. The vibrant greens of chlorophyll are entirely absent. Yet, the surfaces are not barren. The wet rocks are coated in thick, overlapping layers of non-plant life—leathery membranes in shades of bruised purple and pale, sickly yellow. Bulbous, fungal-like growths, some the size of boulders, bulge from between the stone shards, covered in a slick mucus that traps the ambient moisture. These organisms seem to feed on the minerals leaching from the fresh rock and the geothermal vents that puncture the basin floor.

The sound is overwhelming. It is not the noise of wind or animals, but a geological roar. Countless steam vents hiss violently, ejecting plumes of vapor that add to the perpetual fog. The dripping of water is magnified a thousandfold by the hard, echoing surfaces of the rock labyrinth, creating a ceaseless cacophony of splashes, gurgles, and hollow thuds. It is a loud, churning stillness; the processes creating the noise—the venting heat, the slow drip of water—are gradual, changing the landscape only over centuries, yet their immediate output is deafening.

Scattered throughout this dense, wet chaos are vague signs of artificiality. They are not dwellings or machines, but monoliths made of a slightly different, matte-gray stone that stands out against the glossy volcanic surroundings. These rough pillars, perhaps twice the height of a human, seem half-carved or perhaps partially melted. They bear no inscriptions, only faint, swirling indentations that might be tool marks or merely peculiarities of their formation. They stand isolated, silent sentinels amidst the roaring geology.

Fauna is exceptionally rare. Occasionally, in the corner of an eye, a sluggish movement might be detected near a steam vent—a flattened, many-legged creature, perhaps the size of a dinner plate, segmented like a trilobite but coated in the same slick membrane as the fungal growths, slowly scraping nutrients from the damp rock. They are silent, solitary dwellers in the cold din.

The light is perpetually dim, filtered through thick cloud cover and the uprising steam. There are no shadows, only gradients of gray and black, illuminated by a flat, watery light that makes the slick surfaces shimmer. It is a world of cold water, sharp edges, and endless noise, crowded with life that is not green.
