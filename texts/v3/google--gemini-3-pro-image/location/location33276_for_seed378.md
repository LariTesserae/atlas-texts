This is a high-altitude world of scalded stone and blinding steam, a place where geology acts with the speed of biology.

The landscape is defined by massive, ancient terraces of travertine and sinter, deposited over millennia by geothermal waters flowing from a higher, unseen caldera. These steps are enormous, cliff-like risers of mineral deposition that stretch across the horizon, colored in brilliant, unnatural gradients: shocking ochres, deep hematite reds, electric yellows, and pale, milky blues. There is no true vegetation. The green here is not chlorophyll, but thick, slick mats of extremophile bacteria and archaea that thrive in the boiling runoff, coating the wetter rocks in slimy, vibrant tapestries.

Everything is wet. The air is a suffocating blanket of humidity, perpetually past the saturation point. Visibility is frequently cut short by drifting banks of superheated fog. The ground weeps; water seeps from every fissure in the rock, cascading down the terrace faces in steaming sheets, pooling in shallow basins that simmer under the intense sun. The light is incredibly bright, a harsh, diffused glare scattered by the airborne moisture, making the wet mineral surfaces shine like polished gemstones. It is a sweltering, oppressive heat that presses against the skin like a physical weight.

Built into and upon these mineral steps is a sprawling, sparse metropolis. The architecture is industrial yet strangely integrated, constructed from blocks of the same precipitated stone, making buildings look like natural outgrowths of the terraces. The structures are widely spaced, separated by vast expanses of steaming rock and bubbling pools.

The industry here is hydro-thermal. Great, smooth bore-pipes of ceramic and glass weave across the landscape like arterial veins, tapping into the hottest flows, siphoning boiling mineral slurry into processing cathedrals where heat is exchanged and rare elements are precipitated out.

Despite the scale of the industry and the abundance of life, the overwhelming impression is one of silence. There is no clanking of gears or roar of combustion engines. The technology relies on fluid dynamics and thermal differentials; machinery operates with a low, vibrating hum that is felt more than heard. The dominant sound is a ubiquitous, soft *hissing*—the sound of pressure bleeding off, of water turning to steam, of thousands of gallons flowing over smooth stone. This white noise dampens all other sounds, creating an eerie, hushed atmosphere under the blazing light.

Life, however, is everywhere. The lack of plants has not stopped an explosion of fauna. The bacterial mats support a complex ecosystem of grazing creatures—armored, crustacean-like beings with segmented, opalescent shells that scuttle over the scalding rocks, their multiple limbs adapted for gripping slick surfaces. They are busily harvesting the microbial slime.

Larger, spindly predators, resembling wading birds made of carved bone and heat-resistant chitin, stalk the terraces. They stand perfectly still in the steaming pools, waiting for a grazer to come within range before striking with silent, hydraulic speed. The air, too, has life: translucent, membrane-winged things that drift on the thermal updrafts, filtering airborne microorganisms from the steam clouds. The entire landscape is a bustling, silent engine of heat and water.
