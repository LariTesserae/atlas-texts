The air here presses close, heavy as a wet wool blanket and intensely, surprisingly hot. You are standing in a geological amphitheater, a high-altitude notch scooped out of the mountain's shoulder. It feels secret, a cathedral-sized pocket hidden from the wider world by sheer, jagged walls that tear at the sky.

The elevation is significant; the air is thin, which makes the heat feel sharper, stinging the skin rather than just baking it. But the defining characteristic is moisture. It is not raining, yet everything is drenched. The heat forces the abundant groundwater to burst forth as steam from innumerable fissures in the rock floor. This steam rises, hits the cooler upper air, condenses instantly, and falls back as a perpetual, warm drizzle, coating every surface in a slick sheen.

The terrain is incredibly young. The enclosing cliffs are not rounded by eons of wind, but are violently sharp—shattered obsidian and basalt that looks like it cooled only yesterday. The edges are knife-like, the fractures raw and glassy. The ground is a chaotic jumble of these fallen razors, interspersed with shallow, steaming pools of crystalline water that mirror the fractured sky.

Life here is lush, but strangely distributed. It doesn't carpet the landscape; it erupts in fierce, isolated clusters around the hottest vents. Great, fleshy ferns with leaves the size of doors uncurl in accelerated motion, their fronds a deep, almost blackish green. They drip constantly. Between these dense islands of vegetation lay wide stretches of wet, barren rock. The plants themselves seem influenced by something other than mere biology. Some have veins that glitter with a metallic, coppery sheen; others flower into geometric, crystalline shapes that chime softly when struck by falling droplets.

This is where the "built" environment merges with the natural. Scattered across the basin, half-submerged in the hot mud or fused into the cliff faces, are Artifacts. They are not ruins of buildings, but something more fundamental. Smooth, seamless pylons of a material that is neither stone nor metal—a matte, light-absorbing black substance warm to the touch. They show no wear, no rust, no scratches from the surrounding razor-rock. They simply *are*. Occasionally, a faint, deep vibration emanates from them, barely audible but felt in the teeth. They seem less like constructed objects and more like the exposed circuitry of the mountain itself, pieces of engineering that manipulate gravity or thermodynamics on a localized scale to keep this pocket stable.

The atmosphere is mostly quiet, a hushed reverence broken only by the immediate sounds of the ecosystem: the hiss of escaping steam, the rhythmic *plip-plop* of heavy condensation falling from giant leaves into puddles, and the occasional groan of cooling rock.

Fauna is present but elusive. You might see the oily ripples in a hot pool caused by something swimming just beneath the surface, or catch a blur of iridescent movement—like a dragonfly the size of a hawk—darting between the steam plumes. But the creatures here are wary and few.

The place feels dynamic, unstable beneath the surface stillness. The steam vents shift in intensity; a patch of ferns that flourishes one hour might be scalded brown the next by a sudden surge in ground heat, only for new sprouts to emerge almost instantly in the cooling mud. It is a high-energy womb, a place perpetually being born and destroyed in a cycle accelerated by intense heat and incomprehensible buried machinery.
