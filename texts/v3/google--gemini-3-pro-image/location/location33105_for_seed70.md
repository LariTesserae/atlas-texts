This is a realm defined by suspension. You are not underground, nor are you swimming above a seabed. You are adrift within a colossal, high-altitude aquifer—a massive layer of freshwater floating where clouds should be.

The medium is water, but it feels thicker than terrestrial oceans, a tepid, viscous clarity that holds temperature perfectly. It is bath-warm against the skin, a pervasive, comforting heat that encourages torpor. Because of the altitude and the purity of the water, the light is incredibly bright. Sunlight, unfiltered by atmosphere below the water line, plunges deep into the liquid, refracting into shimmering curtains of electric blues, vivid greens, and pale yellows that dance endlessly across every surface. There is no distinct "up" to break the surface of, only a gradual thinning of the medium miles overhead, and no "down" to rest upon, only an abyssal fade into a pressurized haze miles below.

The structure of this world is provided by stone. Great needle-like spires of rock pierce the aquatic expanse. These are not old, worn mountains; they are young, violent splintering of the planet's crust, thrust upward in geologically recent agony. Their edges are razor-sharp, obsidian-black and basalt-grey shards that have not yet known the smoothing caress of time. They form immense, jagged lattices that extend horizontally and vertically across the field of view, a chaotic scaffolding stretching into the bright distance.

Despite the sharpness of the stone, the overwhelming impression is one of softness and suffocating density due to the vegetation. Life here is hyper-lush. Every available square inch of the rock spires is choked with plant matter, layered meters thick. It is a riot of architectural flora that thrives in perpetual submersion and high light.

Enormous, gelatinous fronds, vaguely resembling kelp but the size of redwood trees, spiral around the stone columns, their translucent leaves pulsing slowly with photosynthetic processes. Massive, bulbous plants anchor themselves to the sharp crags, trailing root systems that form dense, woven hammocks between the spires. These roots are covered in fine, velvet-like mosses in iridescent chartreuse and fuchsia. There are "forests" of soft corals—though here they are purely botanical—that form branching, brain-like mazes you have to push through, their textures slimy and yielding. The density is such that in many places, the rock is completely invisible, encased in a living, breathing quilt of biomass.

The soundscape is profoundly hushed. The water dampens everything to a low-frequency murmur. There is no crashing of waves, no rushing currents; the dynamic of this world is near-stasis. Movement is limited to the agonizingly slow drift of the giant fronds in thermal convection currents so weak they are barely perceptible. The only sounds are the muffled, internal clicks of growing cellulose, or the soft *shhh-shhh* of your own movement displacing the heavy water against the plant surfaces.

Fauna is almost entirely absent, making the quiet even heavier. The lushness is a garden without gardeners. Occasionally, you might glimpse something—a translucent, ribbon-like entity drifting passively, more jellyfish than fish, dissolving into the bright background if you look directly at it. But these ghosts are rare. This world belongs to the silent, warm, endless green.
