This place is a furnace-heart suspended in a high, hollow dark.

There is no horizon here, only the immediate, crushing density of the structure. It is a landscape utterly devoid of water, yet it is a choking, tangled jungle. The paradox resolves when you touch the materials. What appears to be a colossal root system forming the uneven floor is actually iron-hard lignin, fused with conductive metals and ceramic ridges. The "soil" is powdered slag and crystalline dust, scorched dry.

This is an engineered ecology that has long since forgotten its architects, growing cancerous and total. It is a built environment where the architecture is biological, and the biology is industrial.

The air is incredibly hot, a dry, searing heat that feels heavy, like breathing volcanic ash. It tastes of copper dust and ozone. There is almost no visible light. The primary illumination comes from the "plants" themselves—not bioluminescence, but thermal radiation. The massive, pillar-like trunks, some thick as refinery towers, glow with a dull, sullen maroon sullen heat from within. Veins of hotter, orange light pulse beneath translucent, mica-like bark, tracing the flow of non-aqueous coolant fluids—perhaps molten salts or pressurized hydrocarbons—that keep the system alive.

It is terribly cramped. The density is absolute. You cannot walk in a straight line; you must climb, squeeze, and crawl through a chaotic lattice of girders made of calcified vine-flesh and structural thorns the size of scythes. Above, there is no sky, only an interlocking canopy of overlapping, saw-toothed leaves, each one a rigid heat-exchanger plate, black and matte, designed to absorb thermal radiation from the unseen core of the world. They rattle against each other like shards of slate.

Despite the industrial scale of the biology, it is eerily quiet. There are no animal calls, no rustle of wind, for there is no weather here other than heat rising. The sound is a felt thing: a deep, sub-bass thrumming that vibrates through the calcified floor and up through your bones. It is the sound of immense pressure moving through conduits, the groan of tectonic plates of bark shifting against one another.

The "turbulence" here is not atmospheric; it is structural and internal. This ancient system is in a constant state of hyperactive, unstable equilibrium. You might watch as a blister forms on a metallic branch-pipe, swelling rapidly with pressurized gases until it bursts, spraying quick-hardening silicate foam that instantly seals the breach, forming a new, ugly knot in the wood. Entire sections of the canopy might suddenly buckle under the strain of sudden growth spurts, grinding against each other in a shower of sparks and hot dust, reshaping the cramped geography in a violent, silent spasm.

It is high up. Through occasional fissures in the floor—gaps where the woven metallic roots haven't quite sealed—you look down into a limitless drop of hotter, darker air, seeing only distant, dull ruddy glows of lower levels, miles below, repeating the same desperate, dry pattern. This whole region is merely one layer in a hanging garden of forged life, stretching endlessly into the hot dark.
