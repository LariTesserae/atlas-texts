This is a high place, a vast, elevated expanse of baked reality that sits closer to the sun than the ground ought to be. The air here is thin, superheated, and tastes of scorched metal and ancient dust.

It is a world of geology, not biology. There is absolutely no water; the concept seems absurd here. The terrain is composed of sintered rock, vast plains of cracked basalt, and towering mesas of stratified obsidian that gleam like polished bone in the unblinking, standard daylight. The forms are old—incredibly old. Erosion finished its major work aeons ago, leaving behind a settled landscape of hard, resistant shapes that have found an eternal equilibrium against the dry wind.

The scale is immense. Standing on the edge of a caldera-like depression, the view stretches for hundreds of miles, a repeating fractal of burnt orange, deep violet shadows, and the oily sheen of vitrified stone. It feels profoundly empty. There are no structures, no ruins, no artifacts of intelligence. The emptiness between the features is agonizingly vast, an expanse of shimmering heat haze that makes the distant horizons waver and detach from the ground.

Yet, despite the emptiness of the space and the total absence of machines, the overwhelming impression of this place is **industry**.

A deafening, rhythmic roar dominates the environment. It is a sound so loud it vibrates in the marrow, a relentless, grinding thrum like the colossal engines of a planet-sized factory running at full capacity. It is the sound of geology being chewed.

The source of the sound, and the reason for the "industrial" feel, is the fauna. The depressions and canyon floors below the high plateaus are choked with life—an overwhelming, seething blanket of creatures. They are massive, armored arthropods, some the size of haulage trucks, with carapaces of iridescent chitin that look like forged iridium.

They are not merely existing; they are processing the landscape. Countless millions of these metallic behemoths march in slow, unending columns, their multi-jointed legs striking the rock with the uniform clatter of piston rods. Their mandibles, diamond-hard and powered by hydraulic musculature, grind the ancient basalt into dust. They consume the rock for trace minerals and thermal energy, exhaling plumes of sulfurous gas that stain the sky a bruised yellow.

This is the industry: a biological strip-mining operation on a continental scale, conducted with agonizing slowness. The landscape isn't changing perceptibly day-to-day—the dynamic is glacial—but the noise of the process is an immediate, inescapable violence.

The combination of the searing heat, the altitude, and the deafening, repetitive noise creates a reality breakdown for any observer. The sound waves become so dense they seem to warp the air visually. The marching columns of chitinous machines appear to loop, walking out of sync with time, their metal bodies blurring into trails of light and noise. The intense temperatures cause mirages that aren't just optical illusions, but temporary solidity; phantom mountain ranges rise and fall in the middle distance, born of heat and sonic pressure.

The rules here are simple: endure the heat, submit to the noise, and stay out of the path of the great processors. It is a world perfectly adapted to its own hostile parameters, a roaring, empty, living factory where the stones are slowly, methodically eaten alive.
