This place exists as a blister inside the granite skin of an impossibly high mountain peak. It is a hidden pocket environment, entirely enclosed save for a hairline fracture miles above that admits a faint, grey twilight. To enter, one would have to squeeze through miles of treacherous, razor-edged fissures, only to tumble into this claustrophobic geode of a world.

The immediate sensation is an overwhelming, physical wall of sound. It is a ceaseless, thundering resonance, a single, deep tone that never varies in pitch or volume. It is the vibration of stratospheric winds shearing against the mountain’s peak miles overhead, the sound captured and amplified downward through the rock throat until it booms within this hollow chamber like a trapped god. The air itself vibrates with the force of it; you feel the sound in your teeth and bones more than you hear it.

It is freezing here—a dry, absolute cold that preserves everything in stasis. There is no moisture. Not a molecule of water exists in the air or the ground. The concept of "wetness" is alien to this geology.

Yet, the chamber is incredibly, impossibly lush.

Every surface—the floor, the curving walls, the ceiling hidden in gloom—is choked with a riotous density of forms that mimic biology but are born of hard, non-aqueous chemistry. It is a jungle formed in a crucible of heavy metals and rare earths.

Great, sweeping fronds of crystallized copper, oxidized to a stunning, dusty verdigris, unfurl from the walls in overlapping scales. They are razor-sharp and rigid as steel girders. Where a forest floor might be, there is a thick, impassable carpet of needle-like hematite formations, glittering silver-black in the dim light, mimicking moss but hard enough to puncture a boot. Rising from this metallic undergrowth are pillars of spun basalt and pillars of cloudy quartz that branch fractally into a dense canopy that fills the upper reaches of the pocket. These "trees" are massive, interlocking formations, their "leaves" sheets of translucent mica that catch the faint light but never move, for there is no breeze inside the blister.

The density is suffocating. There is no open space, only narrow crawlways between the frozen, mineralized vegetation. You are constantly squeezed by cold stone and sharp metal mimicking life.

Scattered throughout this packed mineral thicket are the fossils of industry. They are not ruins so much as inclusions, absorbed by the geological growth. You might mistake a thick, rusted iron pipe for a tree trunk until you see the rivets. The seized carcass of a massive, track-driven drilling machine forms a hillock in the center, now entirely overgrown by a thicket of bismuth crystals that have bloomed in geometric, iridescent cubes over its engine block. Gears the size of carriage wheels are locked tight, their teeth serving as trellises for brittle, white vines of calcium carbonate.

These machines are ancient, remnants of some forgotten attempt to harvest the riches of this impossible garden. They are dead, cold, and still.

Nothing moves here. There are no insects to crawl on the metal leaves, no beasts to stalk through the copper ferns. The ecology is static. The processes that formed these mineral plants ceased eons ago, leaving a perfect, unchanging tableau. Erosion has finished its work; the forms are settled, balanced, and eternal. It is a loud, frozen photograph of a metallic wilderness, hidden forever in the cold dark of the mountain's heart.
