This world does not have a ground floor. At least, not one that has been seen in living memory.

You are standing on the upper terraces of a continent-spanning industrial superstructure, an aggregation of architecture so vast it has created its own geology. We are high up—the air is thin and persistently chilly—but looking down reveals only abyssal layers of more construction: strata of riveted steel girders, brutalist concrete buttresses, and endless interwoven conduits fading into a sepia-grey mist below.

There is no natural substrate here. Every surface underfoot is metal grating, slick composite decking, or pitted ferro-concrete. Yet, the environment is ferociously, impossibly green.

A relentless, cold dampness saturates everything. It is not quite raining, but the air is so thick with vapor that it feels aquatic. Clouds don't float above this place; they drift *through* it, snagging on spires and rolling down metal canyons like slow avalanches. Because of this perpetual wetness, life has colonized the machine.

The density is claustrophobic. The view to the horizon is open, revealing sector after sector of this sprawling rusted lattice, but the immediate vicinity is packed dense with an interplay of manufacture and botany. Enormous pipes, thick as redwood trunks, are choked by blankets of emerald moss that hang heavy and dripping. Ferns and broad-leafed shade plants sprout from every junction box, their roots prying open service panels to find purchase in pockets of decayed rust and oil-soaked grime.

It is an established landscape, old and settled into a grim equilibrium. The metal is deeply weathered—iron ossified by rust, copper scarred with thick teal verdigris. The concrete is stained dark by centuries of runoff. The vegetation is not new growth; these are ancient root systems that have become structural load-bearing elements, holding crumbling gantries together.

The light is consistently dim, a perpetual, watery twilight. The sun is a rumor hidden behind layers of atmospheric sludge and the towering industrial canopy above. Illumination comes from the diffuse grey sky or feebly flickering electric bulkhead lamps that struggle against the humidity.

Despite the colossal scale of the machinery surrounding you, the primary impression is silence. It’s a heavy, dampened quiet. You might feel a deep, sub-sonic thrumming vibrating through the soles of your feet—the evidence of massive engines turning miles below—but the air itself is still.

This silence, however, masks a violent turbulence. The dynamic nature of this place (2.9) is atmospheric and structural. Sudden, freezing squalls whip through the metal corridors, tearing hunks of saturated moss from the walls and sending them plummeting into the void. The temperature fluctuates wildly with these winds, flashing from merely damp to bone-chilling cold in seconds. And the structure itself is restless; unseen automated systems periodically reroute massive flows of coolant, causing pipes to groan and shudder, or distant, city-sized ventilation shutters slam open, changing the airflow of an entire region instantly.

It is a world almost entirely devoid of animal life. The conditions are too harsh, the space too choked. Perhaps in the deepest, most stagnant sump-pools formed in clogged machinery, some pale, sightless amphibians exist, but up here, you are alone amid the groaning metal and the shivering wet leaves.
