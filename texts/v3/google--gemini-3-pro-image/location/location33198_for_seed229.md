This place exists in the high throat of an ancient, dormant volcano, a hidden caldera lofted far above a surrounding sea of clouds. It is a bowl of weathered rock, smoothed not by water, but by the ceaseless friction of abrasive, dust-laden winds over millennia. The scale is contained, perhaps a mile across, feeling like a vast, roofless cathedral where the geology focuses intensely on a center point.

The defining feature is the complete absence of moisture. There is no dew, no rain, no standing pools. The air is parched, tasting of iron filing and ozone, warm like the breath from an open oven. Yet, against all terrestrial logic, the caldera is choked with a bizarre, lush profusion of what can only be called "vegetation."

These are not water-based plants. They are respiratory structures composed of flexible silicates and cured mineral resins, growing in enormous, spiraling thickets around fissures in the caldera floor. These fissures vent not steam, but varied, intensely heated terrestrial gases—sulfur dioxide, carbon dioxide, and heavier, metallic vapors. The flora feeds on these thermal drafts and chemical compounds.

Towering, hollow stalks, colored in deep rusts, bruised purples, and ochres, rise hundreds of feet into the dim air. They resemble colossal, twisted organ pipes or blackened coral fans, porous and intricate. They do not have leaves, but rather millions of rigid, membrane-like flutes that vibrate as the subterranean gases rush through them.

This creates the overwhelming sound of the landscape. It is deafeningly loud. The caldera is a perpetual orchestra of geothermal pressure. Deep, resonant bass notes thrum through the ground as main vents open, overlaid by the shrieking whistle of gas forcing through narrower tubes, and the dry clatter of the rigid structures buffeted by the turbulent air. The environment is hyper-dynamic; the gas jets are unstable, pulsing chaotically, causing the "forest" to sway and sing in unpredictable, violent bursts.

The light is perpetual twilight, dimmed by the heavy atmospheric gases trapped in the bowl and the constant haze of stirred dust. Through this gloom, fauna is abundant, evolved for the dry, noisy chaos. Leathery, glider-like creatures with four wings ride the unpredictable thermals rising from the vents, snatching aerial plankton—flakes of mineral ash lofted by the drafts. On the ground, heavily armored arthropods, the size of large dogs with multifaceted eyes, scuttle between the bases of the giant gas-stalks, rasping nutrients from the mineral crusts deposited by the cooling vapors.

Despite the density of the life and noise, there is a sense of sparsity in the arrangement. The giant tubes are spaced widely, leaving large expanses of polished rock floor between them where the wind howls unimpeded.

Humanoid presence is here, but minimal and clinging to the edges. Tucked into shallow overhangs on the caldera cliffs are primitive shelters. They are constructed from shattered segments of the silicate flora, lashed together with braided tendons from the glider-creatures. There are no fires—combustion is too dangerous here—but faint glows emanate from baskets filled with bioluminescent minerals scavenged from deep fissures. A few crude tools made of chipped volcanic glass and shaped bone rest near these shelters. The inhabitants are scavengers of this violent energy, harvesting the carcasses of creatures battered by the sudden gas blasts, existing precariously in the loud, warm, utterly dry heart of the mountain.
