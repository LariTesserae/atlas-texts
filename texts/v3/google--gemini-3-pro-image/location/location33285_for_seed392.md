This is a world without a bottom.

The entire visible landscape, stretching to a hazy, mid-range horizon, is a constructed floor. There is no bedrock, no sediment, no natural earth. You stand upon an endless expanse of interlocking terraces made from colossal blocks of rough-hewn stone—porous reds, sweaty greys, and ochres. The architecture is overwhelmingly primitive yet built on a scale that defies simple tools. Every surface bears the brutal, repetitive marks of hand-held chisels and stone hammers. The joints between the massive flags are irregular, stuffed with fibrous mortar that is constantly damp.

It is oppressively hot and saturated with water. The air is thick, a palpable jelly of steam and heat that clings to the lungs. The stone floor itself weeps; water doesn't fall as rain, but rather sweats upward from the porous rock, pooling in shallow, hand-carved depressions and slicking every vertical surface. The atmosphere is so heavy with moisture that distant features blur into a watercolor wash.

Despite the lack of soil, vegetation is violently lush. It feeds directly on the mineral-rich sweat of the architecture. Monstrous ferns with fronds thick as leather straps erupt from fissures in the stone.Vines, thick as a human torso and covered in wet, velvety moss, drape over the edges of the terraces like spilled green guts. Their growth is explosive, almost frantic, yet the landscape feels paralyzed.

This is a place locked in a static, blinding present. The light is uniform and intense, a glaring white-gold illumination that seems to emanate from the entire sky simultaneously, casting no true shadows. There is no sun, no movement of celestial bodies, and therefore no time. It is an eternal, unmoving high noon. The structures, too, feel frozen in a moment of creation.

Scattered sparsely across the terrifyingly open expanse of wet terraces are monolithic edifices. They are Ziggurats and squat towers built of the same weeping stone and immense, rough-cut timbers lashed together with ropes of braided bark. They are distanced far from one another, lonely islands of verticality on the flat sea of stone. Some appear unfinished, surrounded by immense wooden scaffolding and ramps that have been assimilated by the aggressive vines. The stone blocks of these buildings are sharp-edged, looking as if they were quarried yesterday, showing no signs of weather-wear despite the constant moisture. It is a "young" landscape that has always existed.

The silence is profound. The air is too thick to carry much sound. Despite the abundant life, there is no ambient noise—no insect buzz, no bird call, no rustle of wind. The fauna here is plentiful but mute.

Amphibious creatures, sleek and oily, slide silently over the wet flagstones, leaving transient trails in the slime. In the shallow, warm pools that dot the terraces, fish with pale, translucent skin crowd together so densely they form a solid, writhing floor, yet they make no splash. Large, avian reptile-things perch on the scaffolding of the distant towers, their leathery wings spread to catch the motionless heat, statuesque in the blinding glare. They do not move; they do not hunt. In a world without change, there is perhaps no need to eat.

The reality of the place feels tenuous. The geometry of the endless terraces doesn't quite resolve in the human eye. Looking across the expanse, the distant horizon seems to curve gently upward, as if you are standing in the bottom of a shallow, infinite bowl, yet your feet tell you it is flat. The primitive construction implies ancient builders, but the freshness of the stone cuts and the static nature of the world suggest it was brought into existence exactly like this, a complete, motionless tableau of sweat, stone, and silence.
