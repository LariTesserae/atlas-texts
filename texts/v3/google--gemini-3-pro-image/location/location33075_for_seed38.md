This is a place built not upon rock, but upon the countless compacted generations of its own dead. It is a high-altitude plateau of staggering proportions, a continent-sized tableland elevated far above the clouds of the lower world, yet it is not cold. It is perpetually, cloyingly warm.

To stand here is to stand inside the lung of a sleeping giant. The air is so thick with moisture it feels viscous in the throat. Visibility is rarely more than a few dozen yards; a permanent, tepid fog hangs between the massive pillars of the vegetation, blurring the boundaries between solid and air. There is no sky in the traditional sense, only an intricately woven ceiling of leaves, vines, and pale, translucent fronds hundreds of feet above, filters the sun into a uniform, watery jade twilight.

The geology of this place was ground down eons ago. Whatever jagged peaks once scraped the stratosphere have been eroded into gentle, slumped hummocks, now utterly subsumed by the biomass. There is no exposed stone. You walk upon a spongy, springy substrate that is hundreds of feet deep—a peat-like matrix of living roots, decaying wood, and water-logged mosses that have compressed into a pseudo-earth. If you were to dig, you would not find soil, but merely older, blacker, slicker layers of plant matter, all the way down to the bedrock smoothed flat by time.

Water does not flow here; it sweats. Every surface glistens. The massive, fluted trunks of the canopy-trees—some as wide as houses—are covered in secondary gardens of bromeliads, ferns, and strange, bulbous fungi that weep continuous streams of condensation. The ground is a patchwork of shallow, tea-colored pools that sit atop the root mat, rimmed by carnivorous pitcher plants the size of bathtubs. Drop a stone into one of these pools, and it won't hit bottom; it will just slowly sink through layers of suspended organic muck until the forest swallows it.

The ecology is characterized by a suffocating density and a bizarre permeability between types of life. The distinction between plant and animal is blurry. There are "rocks" here that are actually massive, calcified bracket fungi, hard as granite, forming shelves that drip slow-moving slime. There are vines that move with agonizing slowness, taking weeks to tighten around a branch, their surfaces reactive to touch, studded with sensory hairs.

The fauna is abundant but terribly secretive. In this dense, dim world, sight is secondary to touch and smell. Most creatures are slow-moving, soft-bodied, and slick, evolved to squeeze through the endless tangle. You might feel the vibration of something massive passing beneath the root-floor you stand on—a burrowing herbivore the size of a whale, swimming through the peat. The air, though mostly quiet, has a texture of sound: the soft, incessant drip of water on leaves, the wet slither of something retracting into a hollow log, and a very low-frequency thrumming that seems to emanate from the trees themselves, perhaps the sound of millions of gallons of sap being pumped upward against gravity.

Scattered through this endless chlorophyll ocean are the remnants of something else. They are hard to find, as the forest actively digests them. You might stumble upon a rectilinear arrangement of what looks like stone, but upon touching it, you realize it is wood that has been mineralized by the strange chemistry of the groundwater. These were shelters once, low and broad buildings grown from the landscape rather than built upon it, their walls formed by guided root systems that have long since died and petrified. Inside, there are no tools, no metal, only vague depressions in the floor where perhaps people sat, and shelves grown from the walls that now hold only dust and phosphorescent slime molds. They are melancholy spaces, evidence of a people who lived entirely within the biological constraints of this world, and who eventually dissolved back into it.

Nothing happens quickly here. A tree falls, not with a crash, but with a slow groan that lasts for days as it is caught by its neighbors and lowered gently to the forest floor, where its decomposition will take millennia in the anaerobic wetness. The cycles are vast, patient, and inexorable. It is a landscape settled into its final form, a warm, wet, breathing tomb of infinite green.
