The air is not something you breathe here; it is something you negotiate with. Every inhalation is a calculated theft from a miserly atmosphere, a shallow sip of cold needles that barely satisfies the ache in your immense, barrel-shaped chest.

I am suspended between the rock and the void, standing on a bridge made of creaking timbers and thick, braided hair ropes coated in ice. Below my feet, through the gaps in the planks, is a thousand-foot drop into a churning white ocean of freezing fog. I do not look down. My world is restricted to the heavy, grease-stained wool of the person directly in front of me, and the precarious, slick slate beneath my own thick leather boots.

I am a hauler of fuel. Strapped to my back, in a rigid leather frame harness that bites into layers of felt and hardened hide, is a towering bundle of dried dung bricks and gnarled roots, scavenged from the lower slopes yesteryear. The load weighs perhaps two hundred pounds—a moderate burden for one built as I am, short, thick-limbed, with joints like granite carbuncles.

We are moving across the Chasm of the Silent Bell, heading from the outer terraces inward to the deep city. There is no rushing. To rush is to die. We move with the collective, rolling gait of the mountain people, planting our center of gravity low before lifting a foot. The bridge sways sluggishly under our combined weight, a rhythm we all instinctively counter.

The wind here is a vivisectionist, slicing through any gap in my wrappings, seeking the skin. My face, I know, looks like the terrain around me—a map of deep fissures and darkened, leathery ridges around eyes narrowed to permanent slits. Ice crystals clung to the coarse hair of my beard.

Reaching the far side, we step off the swinging bridge onto a terrace carved directly into the mountain flank. The relief is immediate, not because it is warm, but because the wind is blocked by the sheer cliff rising above us. Here, the city swallows us. We enter an alleyway so narrow that the load on my back scrapes the rough stone walls on both sides.

The density is a physical weight. I am pressed on all sides by other bodies—stolid, silent bundles of lanolin-soaked wool and smoked leather. The air is thick, a pungent soup of burning dung, human sweat, and the deep, musky odor of the beasts. It is a foul smell, and it is the smell of life. We share warmth aggressively, shoulders grinding together, a slow-moving river of humanity flowing through stone arteries. No one speaks; breath is too expensive to waste on words. The only sounds are the scuff of boots on sludge, the distant, endless *tap-tap-tap* of a mason’s hammer, and the muffled lowing of the great beasts below.

I make my delivery to a lower level, descending stairs cut so steep they are little more than a ladder of slippery rock. I drop my load near the entrance of a ground-floor stable. The heat hits me first—a thick, moist wave radiating from the massive, shaggy shapes huddled in the gloom. A great ox-goat, a mountain of matted, greasy hair, turns its heavy head slowly and exhales a plume of steam that rolls across the floor. Its eyes are ancient and indifferent. I place a hand on its flank for a moment, stealing a fraction of its immense furnace-heat, before turning back to the ascent.

Climbing back up to a mid-level terrace, I pause, my chest heaving against its constraints. I look outward, past the intricate, staggering layers of the city clinging like barnacles to a whale. The fog has lifted momentarily. Beyond the smoke pall of our hive, the peaks slice the indigo sky. They are terrifyingly silent, vast beyond comprehension, indifferent to the stubborn clot of warmth and noise we have fastened to their side.

I am a creature made of this rock and this cold, built to endure the unendurable, existing in the slow, hard rhythm of stone being worn down by ice. I take a shallow, stinging breath, hitch up my empty harness, and turn back into the press of the crowd.
