The vibration reaches me first. It is not a sound, but a persistent, low-frequency tremor traveling up through the miles of solid basalt beneath me, resonating in the dense, mineralized structures of my legs. It tells me the pressure cycle is nearing its peak.

I am standing on the northern slope of the great vent-field known to my kin as the Throat of the World. It is a place of intense thermal brightness in the otherwise dim, copper-hazed spectrum of my vision. The air here is thick as water, incredibly hot, and saturated with the taste of electrified metal.

I am one of the Curators.

My memory is long, measured not in solar cycles—for we never see the sun—but in the rhythmic exhalations of the planet. I remember when the hump of rock I currently stand upon was sharper, less worn by the ceaseless friction of damp air.

I am massive, gravity-bound, and utterly patient. My body is a hunched accumulation of geology, armored in plates of iron sulfide and heavy silicates that have grown over centuries. If I were to stop moving entirely, I would eventually become indistinguishable from the landscape, just another oxidized boulder sweating condensation. But I do not stop. The machine requires tending.

Currently, my attention is focused on Vent 7-Gamma. The thermal signature of its orifice is sputtering, irregular. The bacterial mats have grown too aggressively during the last low-pressure phase, choking the intricate lattice of mineral deposits where the steam should escape.

I move toward it. The motion is slow, a deliberate shift of tectonic weight. My broad, flat foot plants against the slick rock, finding purchase through sheer friction. The resulting sound—a wet groan of stone against stone—is lost in the ambient thrum of the earth. As I walk, filaments of blue-glowing wire-grass snag against my shins, discharging their static payloads against my conductive hide in harmless, silent sparks.

Reaching the vent's lip, the heat washes over my sensory pits in blinding waves. A slag-back, crunching placidly on the bacteria, senses my thermal mass approaching and lumberingly drags itself out of my path, its shell clattering like loose change.

I lean forward, bracing my tremendous weight on one arm. I raise the other. My hand is not truly a hand, but a spade-like convergence of armored plating, polished smooth by millennia of abrasion. With glacial slowness, I insert this tool into the choked throat of the vent.

I begin to scrape.

It is heavy work. The crystallized minerals and baked biological matter are stubborn. I apply pressure, letting gravity do the work. A large chunk of silicate lattice breaks free, falling into the unseen depths of the shaft. Immediately, a plume of superheated vapor, rich in sulfur, blasts upward past my face. I do not blink; I have no eyes to protect. I merely register the intense spike in temperature and the welcome increase in vibrational flow beneath my feet.

The task is not finished, but the immediate crisis is averted. I pause, letting the steam wash over my oxidized carapace, feeling the subtle electrical currents in the air recharge the latent energy within my mineral structure.

Far to my left, across the sweeping curve of the dark horizon, I feel a tremor that is not geological. It is a sharp, rhythmic pounding in the rock. *Thump. THUMP. Thump.*

It is my kin, signaling from the southern vents. The pattern is simple, old: *Observation confirmed. Flow stabilized.*

I wait for the vapor plume to subside before replying. Then, I lift my heavy fist and strike the basalt slope three times. The impact shudders through my own frame and travels outward through the planetary crust, a seismic acknowledgement sent through the dark, wet rock, connecting me to the only community I have ever known in this endless, quiet industry.
