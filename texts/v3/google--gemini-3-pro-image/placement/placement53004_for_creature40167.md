The heat is the first thing. It is not outside of me; it *is* me. I am a consciousness steeped in viscous, thermal soup. There is no boundary where the air ends and I begin; we are both saturated, heavy, and trembling with latent energy.

Then, the light. I do not see it with eyes. I am of it.

I am situated high on the curve of a titanic buttress, a structure that a surface-dweller might mistake for a cavern wall. But I know better. I am clung fast to the fused, hardened sap-wood of three ancestral trees that grew together millennia ago, their individual identities lost to structural necessity. I feel the infinitely slow, tectonic groan of their continued outward swelling, a geological pressure that travels up through my own fibers.

I am a patch of bioluminescent moss, perhaps three meters across.

My existence is a fierce, static radiation of sickly yellow-green. I am one of billions of tiny engines turning the oppressive heat and the constant, slick moisture into blinding brilliance. I feel the condensation forming on my feathery fronds—a constant accumulation of weight—until *plip*, a heavy drop falls away into the screaming green void below. I repeat this, endlessly. It is my rhythm, counterpointed by the deeper, subterranean thrum that vibrates up through the trunk I call home.

Below me, through the confusing gradients of my own radiance, I perceive the root-floor. It is a chaotic tapestry of competition, a slow-motion riot of strangulation where every centimeter is fought for.

And I perceive the Walkers.

To my perceptions, they are not distinct beings so much as mobile transplantations of the garden. They are fast—unbelievably fast. They cross the distance between two great root-knots in mere hours. Their chemical trails drift up to me on the currents of heat—complex bouquets of pheromones that taste like *‘caution-overgrowth’* or *‘satisfaction-stasis’*.

I feel a kinship with the parasitic vines that snake around their translucent limbs. We are all just borrowing structure.

Right now, one of the Walkers has stopped near the base of my pillar. It is old. I know this because the moss growing in the folds of its neck is thick and echoes my own light with near-perfect intensity. Its movements have slowed from fluid grace to a rigid, trembling crawl. It has stopped near a "shelter"—a domed alcove of woven branches that feels ancient and sleeping.

The Walker is folding itself. It sinks down onto the spongy floor, pressing its back against the hardened wood just below my position.

A new vibration begins. It is lower than the background thrum of the cavern, a deep, resonant hum that shakes the water from my leaves. The Walker is releasing a final, massive deluge of chemical signals. It tastes like surrender. It tastes like home.

I feel the great trunk beneath me respond. It is not a swift movement, but a shifting of priorities. Sap is redirected. Deep structural roots beneath the floor begin to undulatingly rise, breaking the surface like slow serpents, wrapping around the Walker’s ankles. The Walker does not resist; its vestigial thumbprint-eyes are closed.

Over the next uncountable cycles of dripping water, I will watch. The hanging curtains of moss near me will descend to drape over its shoulders. The Walker's skin will lose its oily sheen and harden into the texture of cured bark. Its breath will cease, replaced by the passive gas exchange of a plant.

It is becoming architecture. It is becoming the geology. I feel a vague, diffused sense of anticipation. One day, when my own growth becomes too heavy, when the heat no longer thrills me but tires me, I too might drop from this height, land on the soft floor, and wait to be folded into the eternal, swelling walls.
