The condensation freezes to the brass housing of my left eye before I can wipe it away. Not that wiping helps; the air in Gall-Station Sub-7 is practically aquatic, a freezing mist of atomized sap and hydraulic exhaust.

I am deep inside one of the colossal burls hanging beneath the main canopy ridge, a hollowed-out wooden tumor the size of a cathedral, vibrating so hard my teeth ache at their roots. This is the heart of the harvest.

I am wedged into a crawlspace between a pulsating wall of calcified xylem and the scorching heat of a primary intake pump. The noise here is beyond deafening; it’s a physical pressure that tries to shove you out of existence. The rhythmic *CLANG-HISS, CLANG-HISS* of the sixty-foot pistons drowns out even the deepest booming voice. We don’t speak down here. We signal with sharp jerks of our wrist-lamps or the frantic tapping of wrenches against pipes.

I am a Grafted, third generation, if the records scratched onto the bark walls of the habitation pods are true. My name is carved into the leather strap across my chest, but I mostly identify myself by the aches in my modifications.

My right arm is flesh, thick and pale, covered in scars shining with cold sweat under my waxed canvas jacket. My left arm, from the elbow down, is brass and reclaimed steel. It was taken by a snapping high-tension vine five cycles ago. The replacement is a heavy, clawed hydraulic unit that runs hot against my bicep, powered by a glowing canister of refined Grade-A sap strapped tight against my deltoid. It’s clumsy for fine work, but indispensable for what I’m doing now: holding a patch against a rupturing vein.

A hairline fracture has opened in a main brass artery. The pressure inside is unimaginable. It's spitting a high-velocity needle of hot, raw sap that sizzles against the iron plating of my artificial forearm. The smell is overwhelming—a cloying, coppery sweetness that coats the back of the throat, thick with ozone from the straining machinery.

My cleated boots are dug precariously into a patch of spongy, bioluminescent moss that grows aggressively over the metal grating. I’m leaning my whole weight, both flesh and brass, against the wrench, tightening the bolts on the containment collar.

Every screw turn is a battle. The plant fights back. The wood around the pipe is already swelling, trying to swallow the intrusion. Faster than rust, enzymes in the raw sap are eating at the metal edges of my own prosthesis.

But the real fear isn't the pressure or the corrosion. It’s the floor.

Beneath the frantic mechanical vibration of the pumps, I feel the other movement. The slow, oceanic heave of the lattice breathing. The floor beneath my cleated boots rises imperceptibly over the course of an hour, shifting my center of gravity just enough to throw off my leverage.

I work frantically, sweat freezing in my beard. I keep my brass eye focused tight on the threads of the bolt, magnifying them until they are all I can see. I refuse to look at the wider space of the pumping station. I refuse to look at the pistons, because ten minutes ago, I swear I saw the steam sucking *back into* the release valve, just for a heartbeat.

I am a small, shivering parasite made of meat and metal, desperately trying to keep the blood flowing from a sleeping god, terrified that if I stop turning this wrench, the god might notice me.
