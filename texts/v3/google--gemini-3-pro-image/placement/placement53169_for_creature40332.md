The moisture is the first thing you notice; it is not just around you, but seemingly *of* you. The air is so thick with cool vapor that breathing feels like drinking from a chilled, aerosolized sponge. It smells intensely mineral—the scent of wet slate and cold iron—mixed with the ubiquitous, cloying odor of rancid animal grease used to waterproof skin and wood.

You are crouching on a transit juncture, about halfway up the western wall of the Sliver. This specific spot is where a rickety ladder, fashioned from slick, dark branches lashed with gut-rope, meets a narrow horizontal ledge carved precariously from a basalt outcropping.

You are one of the Clinging Folk, a male in what would be considered middle age elsewhere, but here, where a slip means death, you are simply "seasoned."

Your body feels compact, dense. You are not tall, but your center of gravity feels low and solid. Your skin, exposed at the forearms and neck, is a pale, grayish-white, puckered permanently like a prune from the ceaseless humidity. You run a hand through your hair—it is cropped tight against your skull and heavily matted with bear fat to keep the constant drip-drip-drip of condensation from soaking your scalp.

Looking down at your hands resting on your knees, you see the tools of your survival. They are wide, the fingers splayed, the knuckles prominent. The palms are covered in a thick, yellowish carapace of callus, tough as boiled leather. You flex your toes inside your soft hide boots; you can feel the similar, massive pads on your feet gripping the curved rung of the ladder, sensing the texture of the wood through the leather.

You are wearing the uniform of the chasm: layers of stiff, heavy furs that smell of smoke and the surface world, treated until they repel water like a duck's back. But the most vital pieces are the guards. Around your shins and forearms are rigid cylinders of hardened leather, overlapping like insect chitin, studded with flat pieces of dull basalt. You instinctively brush your armored left forearm against the wall beside you. The rock is black glass, smooth and wet looking, but if you were to press your naked flesh against it and slide, it would fillet you to the bone. The armor clicks softly against the stone.

Below you, through the gaps in the fog, you see the dizzying, chaotic architecture of the hive. Huts of sodden, blackening timber lean drunkenly against one another, stacked ten, twenty deep, bridged by terrifyingly thin ropes of braided fiber. You hear the soft murmurs of people moving below, the creak of wet wood under strain, and the eternal *plip-plip-splat* of water hitting roofs. It is quiet, a hushed existence muffled by mist.

Suddenly, a sound cuts through the damp silence—a deep, resonant *thrummm* from deep within the rock face, like a massive cello string snapping underground.

Instinct takes over. It is the communal reflex.

You freeze. Everyone freezes.

Below you on a rope bridge, two figures carrying baskets immediately stop and drop into a crouch, presenting their armored backs to the sky. Above you, someone hauling a timber beam presses themselves flat against the glassy cliff face, becoming one with the wall.

You do not move. You do not breathe deeply. You stare into the middle distance, listening. You are waiting for the stone rain. The thermal shift deep below might peel off a flake of obsidian the size of a fingernail, or a slab the size of a door.

Silence stretches. The dripping water sounds impossibly loud.

Then, a clatter. Fifty feet up and to your right, a shard of black glass peels away from the overhang. It falls silently through the mist, tumbling end over end. It strikes the pitched, stone-reinforced roof of a dwelling three levels below you with a sharp *CRACK*, shattering into razor-sharp shrapnel that sprays over the edge and down toward the Mire at the canyon floor.

You wait another ten heartbeats. No secondary falls.

A low, clicking sound ripples through the nearby dwellings—the "all clear."

You exhale a plume of vapor. The figures below stand up and resume crossing the bridge. The timber-hauler continues their ascent. You grip the slick rungs of the ladder with your wide, calloused hands, feeling the familiar purchase, and begin your slow, deliberate climb upward to your own damp roost, just another barnacle shifting on the wound of the world.
