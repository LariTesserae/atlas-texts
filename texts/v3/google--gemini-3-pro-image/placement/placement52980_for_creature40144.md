The cold is not something that arrives; it is a permanent condition of the air, a sharp, thin presence that lives inside the lungs. I am sitting on a ledge of dark, sweating slate, my legs dangling over a drop so profound that the canyon floor is lost in a shimmering gray haze.

I am of this place. My breath comes slow and deep, expanding a ribcage built like a hardened barrel. The air is thin, offering its meager oxygen grudgingly, so my rhythm is glacial: a long, pulling inhale that stretches the heavy wool of my chest coverings, a pause held for ten heartbeats, and a slow, steaming exhale that vanishingly briefly fogs the crystalline air. There is no hurry. There has never been hurry.

My skin, where it is exposed on my hands and face, is thick as cured rind, waxy and stained ruddy by the relentless, clear light that knows no seasons. I am wrapped in three layers of shag-goat hide, the smell of old lanolin, smoke, and wet mineral dust a comforting, permanent shroud. I am heavy, grounded, my broad feet splayed against the slick rock, gripping with a leather-soled certainty that does not understand vertigo.

I am a tender of the stone.

In my left hand, calloused to the texture of pumice, I hold a chisel made from the thigh bone of a cavern-bear, hardened in fire and sharpened on sandstone. In my right, a rounded hammer-stone. I am working on a corbel that supports the corner of a terrace holding a cistern, three levels down from my sleeping cave. The slate here has developed a hairline fracture—a wound in the city’s skin caused by the relentless saturation.

*Tap.*

The sound is dull, swallowed almost instantly by the thick cushion of emerald moss that clings to the cliff face above me. A single chip of dark gray stone flakes away. I wait. I watch a bead of moisture gather on the overhang, swelling until gravity claims it. It falls, hitting the new flake with a tiny, wet slap.

I study the grain of the rock. Another tap is needed, but not yet.

I look out across the void. The scale of my home is a pressure against the eyes. Miles away, across the hazy gap, the far wall of the canyon rises like a ragged black curtain draped in green velvet. I can see the faint, pale tracing of a vine suspension bridge, a thread spun by giants across the nothingness. It is motionless in the still air. No one is crossing.

Further down the cliff face to my right, perhaps four hundred yards away, a neighbor is tending their tuber garden. They are a dark, indistinct hump of fur against the glistening green terrace wall. They are moving, but so slowly that if I looked away for a minute and looked back, I would not be sure they had moved at all. We have not spoken in two moons. There is no need. We both know the aqueduct above their garden needs clearing; I sent a nod down-canyon three days ago to the weavers, and they will attend to it when the fiber is cured.

The silence is vast. It is a heavy, damp blanket. Underneath the immediate sound of water dripping from a million stone pores, I feel the vibration in my sternum, the subsonic groan of the great river miles below, carving the canyon deeper in the unseen dark.

I return my attention to the slate. I position the bone chisel. I inhale, filling the massive cavity of my chest, and hold the breath.

*Tap.*

The work is slow. The rock weeps. The city breathes its held breath. I am exactly where I am supposed to be, maintaining the eternal, wet equilibrium.
