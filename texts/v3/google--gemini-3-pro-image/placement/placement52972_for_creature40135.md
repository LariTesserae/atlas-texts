The heat is the first thing I know; it is not something outside of me, but the medium through which I exist. It is a thick, pressing weight that thrums against my outer membranes, a geothermal lullaby that promises energy and expansion.

I am blind, utterly and completely in the conventional sense. The cavernous dark, the jagged basalt, the distant, unreachable ceiling—these mean nothing to me as visual concepts. Yet, I map this cathedral of steam with agonizing precision through touch and thermal gradients. I know where the rock is unyielding and cold, offering only anchor points. I know where the air grows thickest with the delicious, metallic slurry of the bacterial fog.

I find myself at a frontier of sensation, a leading edge of the colossal organism.

I am a growth tip, a thick, blunt finger of pale cellulose pushing upward through the crushing humidity. I am part of the great white silence, a single nerve ending in a sprawling, subterranean body.

Currently, I am wrapped tightly around a textured, vertical hardness. It is not the indifferent stone. It is colder than the air, yet it sings with a specific, rigid resonance that calls to my deepest hunger. It is iron. A massive, rusted girder climbing into the upper gloom.

My existence is a slow-motion embrace. I am slick with condensation, my outer skin possessing the yielding, unpleasant texture of waterlogged flesh, sweating the same moisture that fills the air. I press against the metal. I do not move fast enough to be seen, but the pressure I exert is immense, a hydraulic, hydrostatic squeeze built from water pumped up from the vents far below.

I feel the microscopic allies, the bacterial slime that coats the metal ahead of my advance. They are my external digestion, tasting the ferric bounty before I arrive. They signal that this section of the girder is rich, heavily scabbed with the delicious orange decay.

I nudge forward, perhaps an inch in a week. My pale tendrils find the flakes of rust, worming under them. I secrete my own acids, adding my burning enzymes to the damp heat. I feel the stiff, organized lattice of the iron begin to soften beneath me. It surrenders.

A profound, shivering pleasure runs down the length of my translucent stem as the iron oxide dissolves into a usable slurry. I drink it. The sensation is sharp, electrifying, a hard sweetness that flows into my main vascular channels. If one could see in this near-blackness, they would see the ghostly, bone-colored translucence of my form stain slowly, deeply, with arterial streaks of rust-orange and brown.

I am bleeding the structure into myself.

Below me, far down the complex network of my older, thicker limbs, I feel a vibration in the floor—a low frequency groan. It is one of the engine blocks, finally collapsing in on itself under the weight of my cousins who have smothered it for three centuries. The sound is a dinner bell in the deep.

But I remain focused on my ascent. I am a slow, patient tongue licking the rust from the bones of a dead industry. I will continue to climb this iron trellis, transforming its strength into my own sprawling mass, waiting for the inevitable moment, centuries from now, when the metal finally snaps under my weight and we both tumble into the hot mud below.
