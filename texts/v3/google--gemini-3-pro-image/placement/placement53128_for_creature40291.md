The air enters my wide chest not as a rush, but as a slow, deliberate tide, thin and tasting of cold mineral. I am kneeling on the massive, seamless pavement of a northern terrace, the stone beneath my broad, calloused knees colder than ice, yet familiar as my own skin.

I am one of the curators.

My hands, thick and fissured like the cliff face above, hold a bronze scraping tool. The metal has warmed slightly from the heat of my palm, the only warmth in this shadowed corner of the amphitheater. Before me is a shallow drainage channel carved into the bedrock, designed ages ago to guide the ceaseless weeping of the mountain walls into the communal cisterns. A small accumulation of fine, grey silt has gathered here, threatening the perfect, silent flow.

With a movement so gradual it would be imperceptible to a hurried eye, I lower the bronze blade. I do not scrape; haste is vulgar and wasteful in the thin air. I apply steady, hydraulic pressure, lifting the silt deposit in a single, cohesive motion. Behind the tool, the black ribbon of water immediately resumes its silent slide toward the basin.

I pause, resting on my haunches, conserving energy. The silence of the valley presses against my eardrums, a physical weight. It is broken only by the single, delayed *plink* of a water drop falling into the cistern fifty feet away.

I lean forward slightly over the basin. The water within is absolute stillness, a pool of liquid obsidian. In its surface, the pale, unyielding sky is perfectly captured. Suspended against that reflected sky is my own visage: a broad, pale expanse of thickened skin, textured like weathered bone, with faint traces of grey lichen clinging to the deep folds around my brow. My eyes are two enormous, light-absorbing voids in the reflection, giving nothing back to the world but its own image.

Across the expanse of the terrace, separated by a hundred yards of empty, precisely fitted stone, I sense another presence. Another curator stands by the great retaining wall, their leather mantle stiff with cold oil. They have paused in their inspection of the mortarless joints.

They do not wave or call out. Instead, I feel a low shudder travel through the granite pavement, vibrating up through the soles of my feet and resonating deep within my own barrel chest. It is a seismic thrum, a sound felt in the bone more than heard in the air—an acknowledgment of existence in the vast quiet.

I remain motionless for a long moment, letting the vibration settle. The water flows smoothly again. The stone is secure. The frozen equilibrium is maintained. Slowly, deliberately, I begin to rise, preparing to move my shadow a few feet further along the walkway, toward the next task of endless preservation.
