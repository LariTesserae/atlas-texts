The cold is the first thing, a persistent, weeping chill that has seeped through the layers of oiled bark-fiber on my shoulders and settled into my dense bones. I am standing on a ledge scarcely wide enough for my broad feet, my body pressed flat against a wall of black, sweating basalt.

I am one of the haulers of the middle tier. My name is likely a short, resonant bark that cuts through the wind, something like "Kruk" or "Bael," but names matter less here than grip.

Looking down past my thick, calloused toes—splayed wide and gripping the slick rock like tree roots—there is only gray void filled with roaring mist. Looking up, past the heavy protruding ridge of my brow which shields my eyes from the constant spray, I see the underside of the city. It is a dizzying honeycomb of terraces shored up with massive stone blocks, dark doorways carved like wounds into the cliff, and the terrifying, sagging arcs of vine-and-hide bridges swaying in the updrafts.

My immediate reality is weight and friction. Strapped to my short, muscular back via a rigid ironwood frame is a slab of quarried stone meant for a new lintel three levels up. It weighs perhaps three hundred pounds. My legs, thick and foreshortened, absorb the load with a slight flex.

I am waiting for a signal. The noise is incredible—a deafening stew of howling wind and crashing water—so I am watching the rigger across the chasm. Through a momentary break in the fog, illuminated by a sudden, blinding flash of sunlight that turns the wet cliffs into mirrors, I see him. He is fifty feet away horizontally, but separated by a thousand-foot drop. He is clinging to a pylon using just his knees and one hand. He sees me, and makes a broad, sweeping gesture with his free arm, ending with a downward thrust of his fist.

*The line is secure. Proceed heavy.*

I acknowledge with a slow, deliberate nod that shakes the water from my brow ridge. I turn my attention to the task. Ahead of me is a "staircase"—a generous term for a series of jagged notches hacked into the near-vertical face, slick with emerald algae and flowing water.

I reach up. My hand, disproportionately large with skin like cured rhinoceros hide, slaps against the wet stone. My fingernails, thick black chisels of keratin, hunt for a hairline fracture in the basalt. I find one, jam two nails in, and test the hold. It holds. I shift my weight, my center of gravity low, and swing my right foot up to the next notch. The textured sole of my foot bites into the slime.

As I begin the ascent, the mountain gives a low, shuddering groan beneath me. It’s a deep tectonic vibration that rattles my teeth. A few razor-sharp flakes of rock detach from above and clatter onto my shoulder pads. I do not flinch. I just press my forehead against the cold, wet face of the cliff and wait for the mother-stone to finish settling her bones.

The vibration stops. I exhale a plume of mist into the mist, dig my fingers in harder, and begin the slow, grinding climb upward. The stone is sharp, the water never stops, but I will reach the next terrace. I am built for nothing else.
