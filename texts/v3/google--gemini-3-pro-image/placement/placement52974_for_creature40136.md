The cold is the first thing, always the first thing. It is not a dead cold, but an aggressive, hungry presence that seeks to siphon the heat from the marrow of my bones. But I am running hot, a furnace fueled by frantic metabolism wrapped in oily, dense fur.

I find myself clinging inverted to the underside of a massive, slick root-bridge, fifty feet above the unseen, freezing pools.

I am one of them. I am one of the pale, eyeless gardeners.

My world is entirely devoid of light, and I am grateful for it. Vision would be a frantic distraction here. Instead, the cavern maps itself in my mind through a symphony of echoes and tactile feedback. The air currents, disturbed by the ceaseless dripping of icy water from above, wash over the stiff vibrissae whiskering my domed face, telling me exactly how close the nearest fungal stanchion is. My large, independent ears swivel, filtering out the bass thrum of the water hitting the mossy floor far below, tuning into the ultrasonic *click-clack-skitter* of the blind insects herding aphids along the bridge strands.

My six-fingered hands, tipped with curved claws, are currently buried deep in a patch of fibrous sponge-moss growing on the bridge's underside. I am harvesting. With a precise, practiced twist, I snap the neck of a large, sluggish crustacean that had nestled there, attracted by the moisture. Its chitin crushes wetly. I stow it in a pouch made of woven root-fiber slung across my chest. It will be ground into paste later, shared in the huddle.

A vibration runs through the bridge strands—a sharp, staccato rhythm felt through my palms and soles. Someone is coming, moving fast.

I scent them before the air pressure changes: the thick, familiar musk of damp fur and the sharp, coppery tang of secreted growth-enzymes. Another gardener scuttles past on the upper surface of the bridge. They pause directly above me. I feel the faint drum-tap of their fingers against the root fibers, a coded vibration transmitted through the wood: *Structure-stress. Gourd-four. Need shoring.*

I reply with a quick scrape of my claws against the wood: *Acknowledged. Moving.*

I flip myself over the edge of the bridge with a burst of frantic energy, landing on all fours on the swaying walkway. The cold air bites at my exposed sensory dome as I break into a loping run, my body low to the ground, a mound of white heat blurring against the frozen botanical backdrop.

I race along the braided path, guided by the heat signatures of the living city veins. I cannot see the sickly violet glow that a human might perceive, but I feel the structures as pulsing conduits of slightly warmer sap, distinct against the ambient chill. I brush past hanging curtains of wet moss that phosphoresce with a tactile buzz against my skin.

I arrive at Gourd-Four. It is a colossal dwelling-pod, three stories high, grown from a hardened, bulbous protrusion of a ceiling root. It is groaning. I hear the micro-fractures in its cellulose walls, sounds too high for most ears, screaming under the weight of soaked vegetation.

Three others are already there. We do not speak. We work in a synchronized frenzy of touch and scent. I join them at the base of the gourd, where it meets the main root stem. The air here is thick with the pheromones of urgency.

I press my palms against the living wall. Glands in my six-fingered hands begin to secrete a thick, pungent balm—a chemical trigger passed down through countless generations. I begin to vigorously massage the plant tissue, kneading the balm into the stressed fibers. I feel the plant respond beneath my touch, a sluggish, biological acknowledgement as the cellular structure begins to rapid-harden, drawing calcium from the dripping water to reinforce itself against the strain.

We work until my muscles burn and my internal furnace begins to dim. The groaning stops. The gourd is safe for another cycle.

Exhausted, we retreat inside the gourd through a slit-door trained to remain pliable. Inside, the air is startlingly warm, thick with the steam of fifty other bodies packed together in a dense, furry pile in the center of the hollowed chamber.

I crawl over the others, seeking the center of the heat. Claws gently brush my flank; whiskers twitch against my shoulder. It is a silent, communal affirmation of survival. I burrow deep into the collective warmth, my heartbeat slowing from its frantic pace to match the thrumming rhythm of the hive.

As I drift toward sleep, I reach down to my belt and touch the tool hanging there. It is a pruning hook fashioned from a sharpened thigh bone. The texture is smooth, cold, and comforting. It belonged to my predecessor, whose body now enriches the fungal patch near the southern fissure.

I am safe in the dark, warm heart of the city I help grow, knowing that one day, my own bones will be the tools of my descendants, and my flesh will feed the weeping garden that holds us all in its frozen embrace.
