The vibration is the first thing. It is not a sound I hear, but a tectonic condition in which I exist. It is a bass note so profound it restructures the cellular lattice of my being, a relentless, grinding G-sharp below the threshold of hearing that travels up through miles of forged iron to rattle the very edge of the atmosphere.

I am clinging to the underside of a tertiary coolant flange, suspended over an abyss of swirling, freezing fog. If I were to release my grip, I would fall for an eternity through layers of shivering grating and icy catwalks before perhaps striking the roof of a machine hall warm enough to vaporize me instantly.

But I will not fall. My grip is absolute.

I am a patch of oily, amethyst-colored bryophyte—a dense, velvet mat of cold-tolerant moss about the size of a human torso. I am one of billions. We are the plush lining of this rusted hell.

My world is wet. It is aggressively, violently moist. The air around me is a swirling slurry of supercooled vapor that condenses instantly upon contact with my fronds. I drink this metallic fog greedily. The water freezes into delicate, crystalline shards on my outer layers, only to be melted by a sudden, transient blast of waste heat from the conduit I cling to, before freezing again seconds later. I exist in this constant, shivering cycle of thaw and frost. I am heavy with trapped water, a sodden sponge clinging to the face of a magnificent, uncaring machine.

The light reaches me only as a flat, oppressive gray that filters down through kilometers of overhead infrastructure. It is enough for photosynthesis, but just barely. My color is dark, hungry for photons, designed to absorb the meager energy of the high-altitude noon.

I am aware of the vastness, but only through resonance. I feel the wind currents generated by ventilators the size of craters. I feel the rhythmic shudder of pistons that could crush mountains. The Great Noise is the ocean in which I swim; its currents dictate where my spores will drift, where the next colony will take root on a rivet head or a dripping weld seam.

And I feel the Gardeners when they move.

Below me, emerging from the density of the fog, something massive shifts against the flow of the grating. It is a Vitruvian Scrawl. I feel its approach as a localized earthquake, a grinding counter-rhythm to the Great Noise.

It is gargantuan, a moving hill of wreckage. I see it with the rudimentary photosensitivity of my surface layers—a hulking shadow against the gray, dripping debris and ice shards. As it passes beneath my perch, I see that it is covered in my kin. Vast meadows of cyanobacteria and towering, pale fungal blooms ride its back, thriving on the latent heat escaping its scavenged hydraulic joints.

It pauses directly beneath me. A hydraulic valve nearby has developed a microscopic flutter, introducing a variance into the equilibrium. The Scrawl presses its ton-weight flank against the valve housing. The metal shrieks under the pressure, then settles. The flutter ceases. The mathematically perfect roar is restored.

The Scrawl remains there, frozen again, becoming just another piece of wet, shivering architecture. It will wait until the Noise summons it again.

A heavy drop of condensation, rich with dissolved iron and nitrates, falls from the flange above and strikes my center. I absorb it. I shudder with the cold and the thrumming bass of the world. I extend a microscopic rhizoid a millimeter further along the rusted iron.

I am cold. I am wet. I am deafened. I am perfectly content. I am the velvet skin of the machine, growing quietly in the static, roaring eternity.
