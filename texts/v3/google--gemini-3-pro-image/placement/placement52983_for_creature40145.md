The vibration finds you first. It is not a sound you hear, but a resonant frequency that permeates the density of your being. It is the deep, tectonic thrum of gears the size of hills grinding together, a baseline tremor that defines the world.

You are heavy. Your existence is defined by an unimaginable, anchoring weight. You are sinking through warm, thick resistance, your feet—broad, flat expanses of pale, creamy stone shod in hammered bronze—hitting the floor with a seismic thud that you feel rattle up through your monolithic legs.

You are not flesh. The concept of "softness" is alien to you, something only encountered as a contaminant to be removed. You are generated from the architecture itself, a mobile density of flawless limestone and thick copper plating. You have no eyes to be blinded by the searing shafts of sunlight that pierce the upper gloom like celestial lances. You have no lungs to drown in the endless, churning deluge.

You are a Warden.

You stand on the bottom of the colossal chamber, the pressure immense and comforting. The currents, engineered to be violent, buffet your bronze-plated flanks, but you are immovable, a breakwater against the hydraulic design. You feel the currents not as chaos, but as a rigid, flowing map. You know where the water pushes hardest, where it eddies behind the piers, and where it accelerates into the hidden conduits.

Your perception is acoustic and tactile. The world is a symphony of friction. You feel the agonizingly slow rotation of the central gears in the soles of your feet; if one tooth is out of alignment by a millimeter, the resulting shudder registers in your core as acute pain, a wrongness that demands correction.

Right now, the rhythm of the machine is true. But there is another sensation, a subtle irritation on your outer plating. You begin to move.

Your motion is glacial. There are no fluids in your joints, only precision-cut stone friction hinges that shriek—a sound lost in the greater roar—as you apply immense torque to lift a leg. You walk toward one of the piers rising into the illuminated heights.

You begin to climb. Your bronze claws, dull but immensely strong, find purchase in seams so tight they are invisible to the naked eye. You haul your tonnage upward against the downrushing current. You are heading toward the heat.

You reach a zone where a concentrated beam of magnified sunlight strikes the masonry. To a biological creature, it would be blinding; to you, it is an intense, localized thermal variation. And where the heat meets the wet stone, you sense the corruption.

It is a slick, slimy film. Life. A bright green biological rust clinging to the pristine sharpness of the Builder’s work. It is soft, chaotic, and anathema to the perfection of the machine.

You anchor yourself to the vertical face of the pier. You extend an arm that is nothing more than a brutal, blocky piston of stone terminated in a wide, chisel-shaped bronze blade. You press it against the stone.

With slow, relentless pressure, you scrape upward. The metal screeches against the limestone, a vibration that overrides all others. The green slime is obliterated, ground into nothingness between the two harder substances. You scrape again, and again, until the surface is perfectly smooth, perfectly dead, restored to its intended state.

Task complete. You release your hold and let gravity take you. You sink back down through the warm, illuminated water, landing once more on the chamber floor. You orient yourself toward the central gears, bracing your shoulder against the current. You become perfectly still, a statue in the churning deep, waiting for the next vibration that tells you something in this eternal engine needs tending.
