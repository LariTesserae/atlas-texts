The roar is not something that arrives at ears. I have no ears. The roar is a constant, physical pressure that vibrates through the entirety of my density. It is the thrumming bass note of the tonal currents ripping through the drowned valleys below, a sound so profound it acts as a kind of gravity, holding me rooted to this high, terraced plateau.

I am entirely still. Around me, the crushing indigo gloom is absolute, save for the faint, chilling bioluminescence of the landscape itself. To my left, a towering forest of glass-kelp rises hundreds of feet into the dark. They are rigid, crystalline things. I feel the current pushing through them, causing their silica leaves to chime—a high-frequency vibration that scratches against the deeper thrum of the water.

I have stood in this exact spot since the kelp forest was merely a thicket.

Time here is not measured in heartbeats—I have none—but in the slow accumulation of detritus. I look down at what I am. There is no skin, only a thick, mineralized rind compounded of compressed silicate and stony fragments. I am slick with a coating of viridian algae, and patches of electric-blue moss, identical to the mats carpeting the valley floor miles below, grow in the crevices of my form. I am perfectly camouflaged. To a hurried eye, I am just another erratic boulder dropped by some forgotten, ancient glacier.

But I am aware. My awareness is slow, thick, and cold, much like the water pressing in from all sides.

A hunger stirs within my core. It is not a hunger for flesh or plant. I do not graze the lush gardens around me. The hunger is for resonance.

With an effort that feels like shifting a tectonic plate, I begin to move. It is an agonizingly slow process. I do not swim; I am monstrously heavy. I shift my center of gravity, and the granite bedrock beneath me grinds and fractures under the weight. I take one step. A human might live a significant portion of their life in the time it takes me to cross the hundred yards to the plateau's edge.

I position myself where the current is strongest—an underwater jet stream blasting over the lip of the canyon. I anchor my massive, squat legs and let the water hit me. The sound is deafening, a physical bombardment. The vibration shakes my armored exterior and penetrates deep into my core, where my substance is not rock, but a dense, semi-translucent matter like pressurized glacial ice laced with silicate fibers.

The sound shakes this vitreous interior. It generates a spark, a piezoelectric hum that feeds me. I stand there for weeks, perhaps months, just vibrating, drinking the noise of the drowning world.

While I feed, I gaze at the structures nearby. I am drawn to this high place because of them. Great rough-hewn stone monoliths stand in a silent circle. They are brutish, heavy things, covered in the same algae that covers me. They look like my kin.

Beyond them, a dark mouth opens in the granite cliff—a cave. I remember, with a memory that feels like sediment settling, that warmth used to live there. Fire. Softness. A different kind of noise that wasn't water.

I feel a profound, heavy melancholy. It is the only emotion my geology allows. We are the descendants of those who sought refuge in the caves when the waters rose. We did not leave. The pressure and the cold did not kill us; they transmuted us. They forced us to become less biological and more architectural. We are the stone ghosts of the lost colony, adhering to the places where our ancestors once breathed air.

A shift in the currents above warns me. The grinding screech of the miles-thick ice ceiling overhead changes pitch. A down-welling is coming.

Suddenly, the roaring current around me turns instantly, shockingly violent. A column of super-chilled water, dozens of degrees below normal freezing, slams down from the unseen heights.

It hits me. There is no pain, only an immediate cessation of movement. The water around me flashes into solid ice. I am encased in a pillar of sub-aquatic geology. My slow thoughts are frozen mid-sequence. My feeding stops. I become, for a time, truly inanimate.

I remain frozen for days, perhaps weeks, a statue inside a statue of ice, waiting for the warmer currents to slowly erode my prison. It does not matter. I have nowhere to go. I am a sentinel in a silent garden, listening to the roar of forever.
