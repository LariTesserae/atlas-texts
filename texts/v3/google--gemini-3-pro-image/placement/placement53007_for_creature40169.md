The air is not something I breathe so much as something I wear; a heavy, slick garment of heated moisture that clings to my almost-transparent skin. I am standing, swaying slightly, letting the deep, subterranean thrum of the great canopy trees resonate through the spongy ground and up into my loose, flexible bones.

I am of the Descendants, the pale, slow stalkers of the twilight. I am old now. My movements, always deliberate, have gained a new quality of heavy inevitability.

I raise a hand before my face. In the perpetual, watery jade fog, I cannot count my own fingers. It doesn't matter. I don't need to see them to know their vastness, the webbing between the digits pulsing with blood that runs blue-green beneath the skin like captive river water. I splay them wide, feeling the micro-currents of the air, tasting the drifting spores on my fingertips. The air smells of sweet rot, iron, and the distinct, ozone tang of the lightning-moss flowering miles away.

I am hungry, but not urgently. Urgency does not exist here. I begin to move. My stride is long and loping. My wide, flat feet press into the substrate—that miles-deep mattress of my ancestors' compacted remains. It gives way with a wet sigh, warm water pooling around my ankles before slowly receding as I lift my foot for the next step.

I navigate by nose and vibration. To my left, the sharp, ammonia reek of a Great Slug's recent passing. I feel the residual heat of its neurotoxin trail shimmering in the air and give the area a wide berth. To my right, the deep, resonant *thump-thump-pause* of an arboreal grazer signaling its location in the high branches.

I find what I am looking for on the underside of a fallen, half-digested log: a thick mat of protein-rich slime mold, glowing with a faint, cold green light. I scoop great handfuls of the gelatinous substance into my mouth. It tastes earthy and metallic. I eat until the hollow ache in my midsection subsides to a dull hum.

Later, I drift toward the petrified shelters. I do not know who built them, and I do not care. History is too fast a concept for this world. I seek them only because the mineralized wood of their walls amplifies the forest's vibrations. I enter a low, broad doorway, brushing against a calcified bracket fungus that feels like rough stone. Inside, I find one of the shallow depressions in the floor. I sit.

The silence here is textured. The drip of condensation is louder, wetter. The thrumming of the sap rising in the distant giants beats against my chest cavity. I coat my pale, hairless limbs in the antiseptic fluids I gathered from a pitcher plant earlier, a ritual to keep the ever-present fungi from trying to digest me while I rest.

But today, the rest feels different.

As I sit in the dark, lulled by the warmth and the rhythm of the trees, I feel a heavy settling deep within my core. It is a sensation not of exhaustion, but of finality. My metabolism, always sluggish, is downshifting into a geological timeframe.

I know this feeling. I have smelled it on others before they walked away into the deeper fog. It is time.

I rise, slower than ever, joint by joint. I leave the shelter and walk until I find the base of a canopy tree, its fluted trunk as wide as a mountain. The ground here is particularly deep and soft.

I sit down at the base of the tree, facing the direction where the sun would be if there were a sky. I press my legs firmly into the peat. The warm wetness embraces my thighs.

I take one last deep "breath" of the thick, viscous air, tasting the endless green tomb. Then, I stop moving.

I do not close my eyes, for they see little anyway. I just wait.

Days pass. Or perhaps weeks. The concept of time dissolves into the fog. A small, reactive vine, agonizingly slow, begins to curl around my left wrist. Its sensory hairs tickle my skin, but I do not flinch. The sensation is distant now. My pale skin is beginning to feel tight, losing its slick transparency, thickening into something fibrous and tough.

Below me, deep in the warm muck, I feel the first tentative touches against the soles of my feet. The rootlets of the great tree have found me. They are curious, probing the softening flesh of my heels.

I welcome them. I feel a profound sense of rightness as the boundary between myself and the substrate begins to blur. The vibration of the forest is no longer something I feel *outside* of myself; it is becoming my own pulse.

The lignification spreads up my calves, turning muscle into wood, bone into mineral. I am rooting. I am becoming a fixture in the landscape, another hard knot in the endless green expanse, ready to add my own weight to the sleeping giant beneath us all. It is warm here, and quiet, and I am finally still.
