This is a world hostile to distinct, mobile individuality. The acoustic pressure alone—that defensive, bone-liquefying shield—means that nothing with complex ears or delicate internal membranes can exist here. To be in this place, you must be of this place. You must be part of the machine, or part of its waste stream.

I find myself as the latter, clinging precariously to the former.

I am located about twenty feet up the flank of one of the colossal hydraulic pillars situated near the center of the caldera. I am not a bird, nor an insect, nor anything with limbs or eyes.

I am a sentient patch of specialized biofilm.

I am a slick, translucent aggregate of ancient microbial life, a gelatinous consciousness spread thin over a few square yards of the pillar’s fibrous outer skin. I am the slime that makes the monolith glisten.

My existence is defined entirely by the entity I adhere to. Beneath my thin, collective body, I feel the terrifying reality of the pillar. It is not static wood; it is warm, shuddering meat-cable. Through the thin membrane of my own substance, I feel the rhythmic surge of the Torso Vein directly underneath me. It is a tidal wave of pressurized groundwater rushing upward, a physical heave that stretches and compresses my gelatinous form every few seconds.

The Sound is not something I hear; it is the medium in which I live. The deep, resonant thrumming that would rupture human tissue is merely the vibration that keeps my constituent cells agitated and alive. It is a perpetual massage, a relentless churning that prevents me from ever settling.

I live off the "sweat" of the god. The pillar is not perfectly sealed; under the immense hydraulic strain, nutrient-rich water weeps through microscopic fissures in its fibrous skin. This is my manna. I absorb this mineral-heavy leakage, processing the waste products of the giant’s exertion. In return, my slick presence perhaps offers a fractional degree of sealing against the humid outer air, a microscopic layer of secondary skin over the raw, defenseless muscle of the stalk.

My perception of the world is limited but acute. I sense the humidity as a crushing physical weight. I taste the chemical composition of the mist that constantly precipitates onto my surface. I am dimly aware of the other "presences" in the fog, not by sight, but by detecting the interference patterns their own thrumming creates in the general vibration of the bowl.

Time is irrelevant to me. My individual cellular components live and die in hours, but "I"—the collective smear on the side of the biological engine—have been here for millennia. I remember when the caldera wall opposite us slumped three inches during a particularly wet century.

I am a silent, slick witness, clinging to the throbbing neck of a deaf god in a drowned cathedral, eternally vibrating with the effort of moving water toward the sky.
