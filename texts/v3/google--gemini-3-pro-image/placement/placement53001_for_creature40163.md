This is not a world for soft things.

I am wedged in a junction of petrified capillaries, a space so tight that the fractal patterns of the walls are impressed into my own segmented carapace. Above me—though "above" is merely a relative concept here—a frozen whorl of hardened mucus presses down. Below, a fluted chamber of bone-white carbonate pushes up.

I am where I have been for a very long time. I am inside the calcified lung, and I am also made of it.

My consciousness is not seated in eyes or ears. I have neither. To have eyes here would be unending torture; the omnidirectional, shadowless brilliance of the cold light would be an assault. Instead, my "head" is a smooth, multifaceted dome of polished calcium, a mirror designed to reject the radiance, bouncing it back into the blinding void. I do not see the intricate horror of my surroundings; I am simply bathed in its aggressive pressure.

I am deaf, yet my entire existence is sound. The great geological Roar—that single, unvarying scream of planetary weight crushing down on this hollow anomaly—is the medium through which I perceive reality. It vibrates through the marrow of the dead reef.

I perceive the world through eight needle-sharp points of crystal.

My legs are articulated stalks of chalk, ending in spikes of pure silica. I have driven four of these spikes into the microscopic fissures of the floor, anchoring myself against the eternal vibration. Through these points, the Roar travels up my dry limbs and maps the world in my sensorium.

I "feel" the shape of the tunnel ahead not by seeing it, but by how the Roar changes texture as it bounces off the calcified lattice. A narrowing tube feels denser, the vibration quicker, tighter. An open chamber (barely large enough to rotate my hand-sized body) feels resonant and slightly deeper.

Right now, I feel hunger. It is not a wet, churning hunger of the surface world, but a dry, structural deficit, a need for new silicate minerals to reinforce my brittle hinges.

My objective lies three feet ahead. To cover this distance will take perhaps two cycles of what the surface world calls "days." Speed is irrelevant in a finished tomb.

I prepare to move. It is a process of dry friction. I retract my front-left leg. The crystalline joint grinds—*skritch*—a sound that would set teeth on edge, but here it is instantly swallowed by the Roar. I extend the leg forward, searching for purchase in the swirling filigree of the wall. My silica tip finds a microscopic flute in a petrified polyp. I bear down, testing the grip. It holds.

Then, the agonizingly slow heave. My segmented body, a complex articulation of chalk plates and ceramic hinges, slides forward a millimeter. Dust, fine as flour and sharp as ground glass, puffs out from my joints and settles instantly in the violent dryness. I have no blood to pump, no breath to catch. If I were to fall, I would not bruise; I would shatter like an antique vase.

I creep toward the amethyst fan growing from the ceiling. I can feel its razor edges vibrating in the dark acoustic space of my mind. It is sharp, immobile, and rich in trapped aerial metals. When I reach it, I will anchor myself with six legs and use my flinty mandibles to begin the slow work of chipping away my meal.

Suddenly, a new vibration cuts through the geological drone.

It is close. A sharp, staccato rhythm conducted through the contiguous rock wall.

*Tink. Tink-scrape. Tink.*

I freeze my movement, anchoring all eight points to listen. The signal is rapid, frantic, a secret Morse code hidden beneath the screaming pressure of the earth.

It is another Curator. It is perhaps six inches away, separated from me by a thin, intricate screen of hardened lattice. We will not meet; the tubes we inhabit run parallel here. But we acknowledge the proximity.

I raise one crystalline front leg and strike the wall in response. *Tink-tink.*

We are the dry ghosts tending the bright machine. We are the stillness within the scream. We are moving dust.
