The cold is not something I feel *on* me; it is a fundamental aspect of what I am. I exist in the sub-aquatic emerald gloom, low down, where the light has been filtered through a hundred layers of icy canopy until it is barely light at all, just a bruised, greenish hesitation before total darkness.

I am not one of the Rime-Wardens, those armored ghosts that drift through the upper mists. Nor am I one of the mindless, clicking stone-bugs that scuttle over my surface.

I am a Node.

I am a fist-sized ganglion of sentient fungal matter, deeply embedded in the intersection of three massive structural root-veins. I am part of the city's slow nervous system. My consciousness is not seated in a brain, but spread across a shivering network of mycelial threads that lacily penetrate the surrounding woody tissue and the spongy, semi-frozen ground for twenty feet in any direction.

Where I am, the moisture never truly dries. It weeps from the striated walls above and pools around my base. I taste the metallic tang of the impending snow in the water that soaks me. I feel the air, thin and sharp, as it drafts through the narrow corridor I guard.

My existence is one of profound patience and acute sensation. I do not have eyes, but I know the city through vibration and chemistry.

I feel the deep, resonant *groan*—like a cello bowed too hard—of the massive vine-tower above me contracting in the deepening chill. The sound travels through the wood, vibrating along my threads. I feel the sharp, sudden *crack* of ice flowering somewhere high above, a micro-trauma to the structure that I register and archive.

Most of the time, there is only the silence, thick as wool, absorbed by the billion pores of the moss that blankets everything.

Then, a new vibration arises. It is heavy, deliberate, and slow.

*Thump... crunch... thump... crunch.*

It is a Rime-Warden. I sense its approach long before it arrives. The vibrations tell me it is mature, heavy with ice-armor. The clicking arthropods in my vicinity suddenly go still, their tiny clinging feet freezing to the bark.

The Warden stops directly in front of my node. I am blind, yet I perceive its massive presence. I feel the radiant heat of its inner body struggling against the wall of its frozen outer pelage. I smell the musk of ancient wet fur and the ozone scent of the cold it carries with it.

It rears up. Its heavy, six-fingered claws grip the root-structure just inches from my central core. I feel the immense power in those claws, sharp enough to shred the wood, but they are gentle now. The creature leans in. I feel the rough wetness of its tongue as it licks a protruding spur of fibrous vine just above me.

The chemical exchange is instantaneous. I taste the Warden’s saliva as it seeps into the wood—a complex, coppery enzyme. The plant matter around me reacts immediately to this chemical directive. The spur above begins to twitch on a cellular level, a process initiated that will, over weeks, cause it to recede and harden, widening the passage.

The Warden drops to all fours, the plates of ice on its flanks clattering softly like wind chimes made of bone. It pauses. It knows I am here. I feel a low-frequency rumble from its chest—not quite a sound, but a pressure wave that washes over my sensitive threads. It is an acknowledgment. A gardener greeting the soil.

Then, it continues its slow, ghostly migration into the mist. The arthropods resume their clicking. The emerald gloom settles back into silence. I remain, rooted in the frozen wet, feeling the slow, syrupy pulse of sap in the walls, watching the city grow at the speed of ice forming.
