The air tastes of cold copper and wet earth. I am curled in a hollow pocket of basalt, the stone sweating against my back. It is not uncomfortable; the porous sponge of my skin absorbs the moisture, my metabolism heating the thin layer of trapped water until I am wrapped in a persistent, steamy warmth, a micro-climate against the pervasive chill of the twilight.

I unfold. It is a slow, hydraulic process. My knees unhinge, my spine lengthens, and I press a broad, flat hand against the ceiling of my sleeping niche. The ridged pads on my fingertips meet the slick rock, and I feel the familiar, tacky suction take hold. I don't push myself up; I adhere and pull my body free of the alcove, hanging momentarily by one arm over the drop.

Below me, twenty feet down, is the nearest walkway—a thick, swaying suspension bridge braided from the aerial roots of a monolithic pitcher plant clinging to the shard above. Below that bridge is fifty feet of churning grey mist, and then the jagged peak of another drifting island, darker grey against the fog. Below that is the Void.

I drop.

I land silently on the root-bridge, my multi-jointed legs compressing like springs to absorb the impact, my wide feet gripping the slimy fibers instantly. The bridge bows deeply under my slight weight, swinging in a lazy arc. I do not stiffen. To stiffen is to fall. I ride the sway, my internal gyroscope adjusting to the tilt of the world.

The silence is heavy, almost oppressive, like a velvet blanket pressed against the ears. The dense moss that carpets every vertical surface drinks sound just as it drinks the fog. The only noise is a deep, vibrating groan in my jawbone—the subsonic grinding of the rock shard I stand on rubbing against its neighbor in their slow, endless gravitational waltz.

I begin my traverse through the vertical village. I move with deliberate fluidity, keeping three points of contact on the slick ropes. Around me, the basalt spires rise like black teeth choked in emerald and bruised-purple vegetation. The mist is so thick today that I can only see perhaps thirty feet ahead before the world dissolves into a white smear.

A shadow detaches itself from the fog. It is another Mycel, descending a root-ladder towards me. Their skin is a darker slate-mottle than mine, almost invisible against the wet stone. We do not speak. To break the silence here feels violent. As we pass on the narrow bridge, their hand flashes out in a rapid burst of sign language—*fingers curled, thumb tapping the wrist twice, palm slicing horizontally.*

*“The western anchor-root is thinning. The shard tilts.”*

I respond with a deep, resonant hum in my chest, a vibration they will feel through the bridge fibers more than hear. I tap my own shoulder in acknowledgement. We flow past each other, two quiet shadows in the damp.

My destination is the underside of our current shard. I leave the bridges and climb directly onto the rock face, my suction pads finding purchase on stone slick with ancient algae. I crawl inverted, fifty fathoms of emptiness beneath my spine. I am looking for the chitin-worms that burrow into the structural integrity of the stone.

A glider—a pale, translucent membrane stretching six feet across delicate bone struts—drifts silently past my face, riding a thermal current upward. It regards me with a flat, milky eye before vanishing into the fog.

I reach a fissure in the rock, thick with the tangled, beard-like roots that hold this shattered piece of moon together. I brace my legs, unhook my basalt knife from my moss-woven pouch, and begin to scrape away a patch of parasitic fungus. My large, black eyes gather the dim twilight, turning the grey world into a high-contrast landscape of texture and movement. I am cold, I am wet, I am hanging upside down over an abyss, and I am entirely secure. I am of the mist, and the silence is my home.
