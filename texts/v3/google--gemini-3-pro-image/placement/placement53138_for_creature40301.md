The vibration is the first thing. It is not a sound, but a persistent shudder that travels up through the soles of my scavenged boots, vibrating the bones of my ankles and settling as a permanent ache in my lower spine. I am standing on a gantry made of mismatched iron plates, welded together so crudely that the seams bulge like ugly scars beneath the layer of slick, black grease that coats everything.

I am a Striker. I know this because my right arm is a monstrous, asymmetric thing, bulging with muscle that feels as hard and unyielding as the metal I strike. The rest of me is stunted, compressed by the low ceilings and the heavy air, but that right arm is a tool built for one motion, repeated endlessly for twelve hours a shift.

I cannot hear the screaming cacophony of the Undercroft. My hearing went years ago, shattered by the very hammer I hold. Now, the world is a silent, crushing pressure against my brass goggles, a physical wall of air pulsing with the unseen shouts of thousands and the grinding of gears the size of houses.

It is freezing cold, yet sweat slickens the leather straps that bind scrap-metal armor to my shins and forearm. The air is thick with moisture that tastes of rusted copper and ancient mold. Every breath is heavy. Above me, a canopy of corroded pipes weeps constantly; a slow, cold droplet lands on the back of my neck, sliding down my spine beneath my protective leathers.

I am waiting at a junction point in the "Rust-Gut," a particularly narrow chasm where rickety walkways cross over a drop into absolute darkness. The only light comes from a sputtering brazier nearby, burning oily rags that choke the air with acrid smoke, and a glass jar holding faint, bioluminescent fungi hung on a bent nail. The shadows are long and restless.

Looking down through the gaps in the iron grating, I see the Haulers below. They are chained together in a line of six, their backs bent parallel to the floor, dragging a massive, salvaged steel girder along a wire-rope bridge that sways precariously over the void. They move with agonizing slowness, their breath puffing out in visible clouds that I cannot hear.

To my left, a massive wall of iron plating is covered in a sprawling bloom of vibrant orange rust that looks like a fiery scab. A pale, hairless rat-thing, blind and twitching, skitters across the rust patch and squeezes into a crack between two plates.

My shift partner, Gort, taps my shoulder hard—the only way we can get each other's attention. He is a squat shape coated in the same brownish-black slurry as me, his eyes huge and watery behind thick glass lenses. He jerks his immense, overdeveloped left arm towards the approaching Haulers and makes a sharp, downward chopping gesture with his fist.

The girder is almost here. It is time to seal the flange.

I heft the thirty-pound iron hammer. My shoulder joint grinds—bone against bone, cartilage long gone—but I ignore it. Pain is just another texture of the Undercroft, like the damp and the grease. I step up to the edge of the platform as the Haulers heave the girder into place. I raise the hammer. The vibration under my feet intensifies as the hive around me screams its silent, endless roar. I swing.
