This is where I am.

I am wedged deep within a crevice of slick, sweating basalt, halfway up a massive column that supports the unseen ceiling. I am not alone in this crack; I share it with a dozen others of my kind, our flat, segmented bodies pressed together in a shivering mosaic of chitin.

I am relatively small in this world—perhaps the size of a human forearm—flattened and many-legged, built to scuttle in the interstices of giants. My carapace is a mottled, necrotic drab designed not to be seen, covered in tiny, sensitive bristles that taste the ammonia-heavy air and feel the tremors of the stone.

I have no eyes. Eyes are a liability here, easily damaged organs overwhelmed by the nauseating, chaotic strobing of the bioluminescence outside. The flickering bruises of purple and electric blue light that penetrate our hiding spot register only as a painful, senseless irritation on my frontal sensory plates.

Instead, I see with sound.

The world outside my crevice is a deafening, screaming map of pressure waves. The "silence" here is a roar that vibrates in my gut. By pressing my jaw against the wet stone, I perceive the cavern. The high-pitched *clack-clack-clack* of billions of pyrite isopods grazing below paints the floor as a shimmering, shifting ocean of metal. The deep, vibrating thrum that shakes the marrow in my exoskeleton tells me a geological giant is moving three valleys over, its displacement wave causing the air pressure to spike.

A sudden, localized silence cuts through the din nearby—a void in the noise map.

*Hunter.*

A whip-spider has unfolded itself from a neighboring flowstone formation. I feel the minute displacement of air as one of its ten-foot sensory legs sweeps past the opening of our crevice, searching for a vibration that betrays fear. We freeze. My spiracles lock shut. The creature is so close I can smell the coppery tang of its pedipalps. It passes. The chaotic noise rushes back in to fill the void.

But the hunger is sharper than the fear. The chemosynthetic bacterial mat lining our crevice has been grazed bare by our huddled mass. We must move.

A collective shudder runs through the rock—a tremor caused by a distant collapse or a mass migration. This is the signal. High above, in the crushing dark of the ceiling, the tremor knocks loose a cluster of leathery flyers. They erupt into a panic, their wings beating the clammy air into a frenzy, raining down a fresh, foul sleet of excrement and decayed bodies onto the cavern floor below.

Food.

I surge out of the crevice, my twenty legs finding purchase on the treacherous, gelatinous slime that coats the basalt column. I am instantly part of the current. There is no open space. To move here is to push against flesh and shell. I scramble over the arched backs of the metallic isopods, feeling their endless, mindless grinding beneath my claws. I dodge the slow, oozing path of a thigh-thick tunneller worm, careful not to touch its caustic slime trail.

The air is thick, tasting of wet iron and rot. The flickering bioluminescence from the sheer density of bodies makes the shadows writhe, disorienting and sickening, but my bristles keep me true to the vibrations of the falling feast ahead.

I reach a fresh patch of the nutrient-rich sleet that has splattered onto a limestone shelf. I bury my mouthparts into the slurry, eating quickly, desperately. Around me, thousands of others are doing the same. We are a carpet of feeding things, momentarily united by appetite.

A tremor, stronger than the last, rocks the shelf. The feeding stops. The roaring noise shifts pitch. The metallic tide of isopods below suddenly changes direction, a unified, clattering surge flowing away from the center of the cavern.

Something truly massive is coming this way.

I abandon the food. I turn and scramble back toward the pillars, desperately seeking a gap in the living architecture, a crack in the stone small enough that the coming mountain of flesh and cartilage will pass me by. There is no rest here. There is only the noise, the crush, and the endless hunger in the dark.
