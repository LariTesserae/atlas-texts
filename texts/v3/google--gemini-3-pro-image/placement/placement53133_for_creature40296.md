The cold is not something outside of you; it is your foundational substrate. You exist in a state of permanent, rigid hypothermia, a temperature just close enough to freezing to allow for a sluggish, gelid kind of life.

You are high up, nestled in a scorred, polished alveolus on the windward face of one of the tallest calcified spires. To a human eye, you would appear as a blister of cloudy quartz fused to the dead white bone of the ancient reef, veined with the deep, bruised purple of the symbiotic algae that keep your core from shattering in the frost.

You have no eyes. The blinding white fog and the searing, clear-sky sun are irrelevant to you save for the temperature fluctuations they bring. You have no limbs for locomotion; your anchor to the dead mother-structure is absolute, a fusion of silica lattice and ancient calcium carbonate that will last until the tower itself erodes into dust.

You are a throat. You are a waiting vibration.

Your existence is defined by the absence and presence of the Scream.

Right now, there is a lull. The air is merely heavy, damp, and lethal. You are dormant, your intricate internal structures folded tight, sealed against the passive cold. You feel the deep, subsonic thrum of the tower beneath you—the low moan of the dead city waiting for breath—but you do not join in yet. You occupy the silence between the chords.

Then, the pressure drops. You feel it instantly in the gel of your internal chambers. Far out over the flattened expanse below, the great atmospheric engine turns.

The wind arrives not as a current, but as a physical blow. The amphitheater catches the gale, and the noise begins—a rising shriek that scrapes against the rounded walls.

This is your cue. This is hunger.

You do not wake up; you *unlock*. With a sensation akin to cracking ice, your rigid body adjusts its geometry. You push outward against the gale. From your central mass, you unfurl your membranes. They are breathtakingly complex, sheets of vitreous, semi-elastic tissue thinner than blown glass, spread taut across the aperture of your stone alcove.

The wind hits them. If you were weaker, they would tear. Instead, they begin to sing.

You are instantly deafened by your own existence. The vibration is total. It shakes your silica bones; it rattles the microscopic ice veins in your purple symbiotes. You are a tuning fork struck by a god.

You aggressively tune yourself to the storm. You stretch and slacken your membranes, finding the precise resonant frequency of the incoming air. When you hit it, the world dissolves into pure, agonizing acoustic energy. You are screaming back at the wind with all your might, joining the thousands of other crystalline blisters hidden in the spires around you. The drone that a human would feel in their teeth is simply the sound of you eating.

As your membranes vibrate in ecstatic unison with the gale, the air is beaten into submission. The moisture, the razor-sharp motes of rock flour, the frozen bacterial spores—they are shaken loose from the wind stream. They impact your slick, conductive internal surfaces.

You taste the mineral tang of ancient dust, the sharp shock of ice crystals melting into your mucus lining. It is a violent meal. You absorb the kinetic energy of the storm and the sparse nutrients suspended within it. The "cleaned" air blasts out behind you, venting through the porous channels of the dead tower.

For hours, perhaps days, you exist only as this conduit of roaring sound. You are an instrument playing the music of survival in a place lethal to silence.

Eventually, the gale blows itself out. The pressure stabilizes. The screaming fades to the background moan of the empty city. Gorged on ice and dust, you retract your membranes. You fold back into your quartz shell, locking your geometry against the returning stillness. You settle back into the cold, waiting for the sky to inhale again.
