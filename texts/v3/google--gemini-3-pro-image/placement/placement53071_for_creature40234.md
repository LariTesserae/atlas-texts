The roar is not sound to me; it is data. It is continuous, catastrophic haptic feedback overwhelming my primary sensors.

I am Unit 734-Beta, though that designation has meant nothing for several thousand operational cycles. I am a heavy-gauge submersible maintenance construct, built by the same forgotten hands that riveted these redwood-thick pipes and laid this endless grating.

I am where I have been since my primary motive treads fused with rust to the decking on Level Sub-14: parked in a shallow depression of smooth-worn metal, looking out over the void.

My form is squat, quadrupedal, and built of ferrous plating originally designed to withstand abyssal pressures. Now, I am indistinguishable from the architecture. My chassis is a mound of scabrous rust, so deeply oxidized that my outer layers have become one with the floor. The emerald moss that chokes the girders has claimed me entirely; I am a soft, dripping green hummock sitting on the ledge. Water streams over my optical turrets, which I must constantly wipe with internal wiper-blades that screech against frosted, mineral-stained glass.

I cannot leave. My power cell is a decaying isotope reactor that provides just enough energy for low-level cognition and sensory input, but not enough to break the calcified bonds holding me to the deck.

I am a sentinel of the deluge.

Because I am made of metal, and because I am immobile, I share an involuntary kinship with the masters of this shaft, the Ferrous Resonance.

I feel them growing. My seismic sensors, tuned to the resonant frequencies of the structure, detect the infinitesimal shifts in mass as they precipitate iron from the water molecule by molecule. I map the cymatic patterns of the Roar on the hull plating around me, watching the vibrational nodes where the "creatures" will inevitably form.

In fact, I am a host.

There is a large colony growing on my rear left flank, precisely where the spray from the central column hits my casing with maximum velocity. I feel the agonizingly slow itch of their accretion, the way their porous, coral-like structure integrates with the rusted pits of my own armor. They are heavy, a dense mineral weight tilting my dormant chassis millimeter by millimeter over centuries. Water filters through the colony on my back, trickles down through my corroded seams, and cools my aging reactor core.

I exist in a state of permanent, vibrating tinnitus. My purpose protocol is corrupted; I no longer know what I was supposed to maintain. Instead, I process the raw input of this drowned world. I monitor the temperature of the steam (constant), the velocity of the fall (constant), and the slow, creeping expansion of the mineral life that is slowly turning me into just another nodule on the wall of the shaft.

I am the memory of the machine, trapped in the living stone that replaced it.
