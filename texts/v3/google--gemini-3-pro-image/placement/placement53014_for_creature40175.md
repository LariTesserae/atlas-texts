The air hits me first—not as a gas, but as a sodden, thermal blanket. It is impossibly heavy, a cloying soup of heat that clings to the skin instantly, slicking it with a layer of immediate condensation. It smells of ancient, wet earth, the sharp tang of ozone discharged from massive generators, and the underlying, coppery reek of wet rust.

I am standing on a vibrating platform of corrugated iron grating. Below me, through the gaps in the metal, a hundred feet of hazy green void drops away, crisscrossed by other dripping catwalks and thick, insulated pipes, ending in the sluggish, churning darkness of the geothermal slurry far below. Above me, there is no sky, only an interlocking ceiling of colossal branches, each wider than a highway, sweating moisture that falls in an endless, varied rain.

I am one of the cured.

I know this because of the weight I carry. It is not just gravity, but the sheer density of my own gear. I look down at my body. My torso is encased in a thick apron of waxed canvas, stiff and stained almost black with grease and chlorophyll. Heavy chains cinch oilskin trousers to my waist. My boots are massive things, the soles thick rubber treads bolted with iron cleats that bite into the slime-coated metal beneath my feet with every deliberate step.

My hands are bare, and they are not the hands I remember from another life. The skin is the colour of wet clay, thickened into a permanent, porous hide that feels leathery and numb. The knuckles are swollen ridges of callus.

I reach up to my face. I am wearing the respirator. It’s a heavy brass frame strapped tight against my skull, pushing into my cheeks. I breathe in, and the air tastes dry and charcoal-filtered, a stark contrast to the soup outside the mask. The glass lenses are thick, turning the already dim, verdant world a murky shade of submarine yellow.

I try to listen, but there is only a dull, muffled roar inside my head. The world outside is vibrating—I feel the deep, resonant thrum of steam pistons shaking the marrow of my bones—but my ears are dead to it. The relentless noise of the processors took my hearing years ago. Now, there is only the internal sound of my own labored breathing and a faint, high-pitched whine from my left shoulder.

I look to my left arm. It is not flesh. Where a bicep should be, a chunky brass hydraulic cylinder glistens with oil. Below the elbow, it terminates in a crudely hinged pylon of riveted iron, ending in a heavy, pragmatic claw currently clamped around a massive pipe wrench. I flex my shoulder muscle, and the claw ratchets open with a sharp, vibrating *clack* that I feel rather than hear. It is ugly, heavy, and stronger than flesh ever was. It aches with the damp, a dull, rusted pain that is always present.

I am standing at a junction point, mid-level in the basin. To my right, the curved, monolithic flank of a mangrove-titan trunk rises like a cliff face. It is so vast its curvature is barely perceptible. Bolted directly into its living wood with rusted girders as thick as my thigh is a secondary processing mill. Steam vents violently from brass fittings along its side, instantly turning to fog in the saturated air.

My purpose here is clear, defined by the tools dragged down by the heavy leather bandolier across my chest. On one side, the massive wrench my iron claw is built to wield. On the other, a honed machete in a metal sheath.

A conveyor belt nearby, slick with algae and condensation, has stalled. A massive clot of fibrous, pale root matter and fast-growing, oily ferns has fouled the gears.

I begin the slow, rolling walk toward the blockage. I test the grating with my cleated boot before transferring my weight, a reflex born of a lifetime on slippery heights. Another figure approaches from the opposite walkway—stocky, masked, dragging a similar leg-brace augmentation that vents steam with every step. We do not speak. He sees me, raises a heavy, gloved hand, and flashes the safety lamp hooked to his belt twice. *Hold position. Heavy load.* I hammer my iron claw once against a nearby pipe in acknowledgment. The vibration travels the metal; he feels it and nods.

We converge on the jammed machine. I ready the machete to hack back the aggressive biology, while he readies a pry-bar for the stubborn mechanics. This is my world: the endless, sweating middle ground, maintaining the precarious balance between the rust that eats the iron and the green that swallows it whole.
