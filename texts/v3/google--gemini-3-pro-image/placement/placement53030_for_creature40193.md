The air up here is not merely thin; it is a blade against the lungs, sharp and chemically pure. You take a breath, and the cold bites deep behind your sternum before you exhale a plume of instant fog.

You are a trespasser, a scavenger drawn by rumors of imperishable artifacts hidden above the clouds. You are wrapped in layers of wool and treated canvas, bulky and awkward, your breath wheezing through the filters of heavy goggles meant to protect against the searing ultraviolet.

Where you stand, the world is a study in impossible contrasts. You are precariously balanced on a ridge of shattered basalt, wet and slick as seal skin. Below your boots, the chartreuse moss is ankle-deep, a spongy, luminous carpet that swallows sound. Just ahead, a rhododendron thicket, ancient and gnarled, forms a dripping tunnel of dark leaves.

But your eyes are drawn upward. Thrusting out of that prehistoric green tangle is a spire of milky white ceramic. It is flawless. It stands perhaps sixty feet high, a component of some forgotten atmospheric processor. Despite the slick moisture coating its surface, it gleams with an internal, pearlescent light. It looks as though it was manufactured five minutes ago, dropped from the heavens into this primordial soup.

You reach out a gloved hand to touch it. The material is shockingly cold, colder even than the air. You wipe a smear of emerald algae from its surface, revealing the pristine white beneath. It refuses to corrode. It refuses to acknowledge the passage of time.

The silence presses against your eardrums. It is so profound that the tiny *shrrraack* of your boot sliding on loose scree sounds like a gunshot. You stop moving, afraid to break the stillness again.

As you stand frozen, letting the cold seep through your many layers, the light shifts. The sun, a brutal, unblinking eye in that violent violet sky, moves just enough to catch a reflective surface you hadn’t noticed.

Fifty yards away, standing atop a massive, moss-draped capacitor housing, something flares with painful brilliance.

At first, your brain interprets it as a trick of the light, a particularly intense reflection off a patch of wet chrome. You squint through your tinted lenses. The flare settles down into a shimmer.

It is tall, agonizingly thin, like an Giacometti sculpture carved from glare and ice. You almost cannot see it; its ceramic plating blends with the industrial ruins, and its chrome joints reflect the sky and the foliage with such perfect, painful fidelity that it seems made of empty space. It is agonizingly still.

Then you notice the moss. A thick patch of that electric green grows on its left shoulder, trailing vines down a glass forearm. A small fern has taken root in the crook of its neck.

It is a Vitrified Warden. It is facing away from you, its head tilted slightly back, palms open to the murderous sun. It is feeding on the radiation that would blind you in seconds.

You watch it. Minutes pass. Your own body begins to feel heavy, the cold aerosol mist settling into the fibers of your clothing, adding weight. A droplet of water falls from a branch above you, hitting your helmet with a loud *tink*.

The Warden does not flinch. It does not acknowledge the sound, or your presence. It belongs to a different timescale, one measured in the slow creep of lichen over glass.

A strange lassitude steals over you. The frantic energy that drove you up the mountainside evaporates, replaced by the heavy, hypnotic rhythm of the caldera. Why move? Movement breaks the beautiful, terrible silence. Movement sheds heat.

You look down at your own hand, resting on the ceramic spire. A tiny bead of condensation rolls down your thumb and drops onto the moss below. You realize that to this place, to these wardens, you are not an explorer. You are just another temporary intrusion of biology, warm and wet and fleeting, soon to be quieted by the cold and covered, inevitably, by the green. You stand there, sharing the silence, watching the light eat the machine.
