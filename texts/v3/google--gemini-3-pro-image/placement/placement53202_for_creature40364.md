The air is not something I breathe; it is something I swim through. It is a gelatinous soup of warmth, unbelievably thick with the musk of ten thousand sweating bodies, the sharp tang of insect frass, and the cloying, fermentation-sweet scent of bruised succulents. It vibrates against my sealable ear-slits, a physical pressure wave of a million droning wings that rattles my very ribcage.

I am a runner. I am a russet blur in the amber gloom.

My reality is vertical. I am currently clinging upside down to the underside of a massive, bloated root-beam that forms the ceiling of a mid-level thoroughfare. My toes, splayed wide and tipped with thick, tacky pads, grip the slick surface of the wood with absolute certainty. Gravity is merely a suggestion here; density is the law.

Below me, a river of my kin flows through a tubular corridor made of smoothed adobe and hardened insect saliva. They jostle and squeeze, a mottled current of ochre and bruised purple skin, shimmering with that essential, oily sheen that keeps us from dissolving in the damp. I see the flash of iridescent beetle-mandible knives strapped to wiry torsos, the bulky shapes of woven fiber baskets piled high with excavated clay.

I drop. I land silently, absorbing the impact in a crouch between two hurrying Mold-Farmers dusted white with spore-powder. There is no personal space. To move is to touch. My shoulder bumps a neighbor's arm; I receive a sharp, tactile *tap-tap-squeeze* on my forearm in return—*move right, heavy load coming.* I obey instantly, flattening myself against a wall that is already sweating condensation.

A massive tortoise-insect lumbers past, filling the corridor, its chitinous shell scraping the ceiling. Its multi-faceted eyes are dull, mindlessly obedient to the small handler perched on its back, steering it with prods of a polished bone stick.

I am smaller than they are, faster. I am a connector. My harness, woven from toughest root-sinew, holds three precious bladder-pouches snug against my chest. They contain concentrated fungal nectar from the deep compost chambers, destined for the nursing caverns near the warmth of the upper alcove ceiling.

I begin my ascent. I don't take stairs; I scramble up a chimney fissure where the sandstone has cracked away from the mud architecture. My huge, obsidian eyes soak in the dim light, parsing the gloom into highly defined textures of slick rock and fibrous plant matter. I see the heat signatures of the beings around me, glowing faintly in the infrared.

I squeeze past a bulbous, necrotic-brown cactus growing directly out of the wall, its thorns thick as fingers. I duck under a walkway made of woven vines that sways alarmingly under the weight of a dozen hurrying feet. The higher I go, the louder the insect drone becomes, the buzz of the pollinators serving the hanging gardens.

I burst out onto a wider platform near the top of the enclave. The light here is stronger, a bruised, reddish twilight filtering in from the unseen mouth of the cave. Here, the air is slightly hotter, and the smell of vegetation is intense.

For a fraction of a second, I stop. It is forbidden to stop, but the sight always snags me. I look up.

Hanging from the ceiling are the Water-Wardens. They move slower than the rest of us. Their faces, lit from below by the faint green glow of bioluminescent moss cultivated in their head-baskets, are masks of terrifying focus. One gently massages a swollen root tip, coaxing a single, glittering diamond of water into a gourd. It is the most holy act in the hive.

Below them, the city falls away in a chaotic tumble of organic curves—huts built upon huts, bridged by roots and ropes, alive with the ceaseless, frantic churning of my people. It is a magnificent, screaming, stinking engine of survival.

A sharp whistle cuts through the din—my contact. I shake off the moment of contemplation and jolt forward, diving back into the churning crowd, a single, energized corpuscle rushing through the veins of the stone giant we inhabit. Outside this womb, they say there is only dry wind and death. I do not believe them. There is nothing outside. This noise, this heat, this crush of bodies—this is the only world there is.
