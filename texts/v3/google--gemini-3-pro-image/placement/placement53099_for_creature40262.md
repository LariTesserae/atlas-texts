The heat is not something outside of me; it is the medium through which I exist, as fundamental as water to a fish.

I find myself clinging upside down to the underside of a massive, corroded I-beam, high in the vaulted ceiling of the dynamo chamber. Gravity is a negligible suggestion when you have forty-two hooked legs gripping the pitted texture of bloom-iron.

I am one of the gardeners. I am the chitin-clad metabolism of this buried world.

My reality is not composed of the sickly amber light that humans would rely on. To me, that is merely a faint thermal radiation. Instead, my world is a dense, vibrating tapestry of scent and sound. My antennae, long and infinitely sensitive, wave in the thick air, tasting the copper sulfide rising from below, the sweet, rotting musk of the violet moss patches, and the sharp tang of ozone leaking from a cracked capacitor nearby.

Below me, the great silence of the machines is a roaring soundscape to my segmented ears. I hear the agonizingly slow groan of a four-story dynamo settling millimetres further into its foundation. I hear the wet *splat* of condensation dripping onto a far-off metal plate. Most importantly, I feel the deep, resonant *thrum-thrum-thrum* rising through the iron skeleton of the chamber—the heartbeat of the magma far beneath us, the pulse by which I navigate.

I begin to move. A ripple of coordinated motion passes down my segments. My legs make a rapid, soft *tack-tack-tack* against the wet metal, a sound lost in the hiss of steam. My carapace, thick with accumulated layers of iron oxide and mineral runoff, scrapes silently against a bundle of insulated pipes as I squeeze through a gap barely wide enough for my body. I am perfectly camouflaged, a shard of living rust moving through dead rust.

Hunger drives me downward. I navigate the dizzying maze of catwalks not by sight, but by following the scent gradient of decay. I detect a rich bloom of pale dinner-plate fungi growing on the casing of a stalled turbine, fed by a persistent oily leak.

I descend a vertical bundle of cables, thick as tree trunks. Halfway down, I pause. My chemoreceptors pick up a different scent—the faint, yeasty smell of the pale, soft things that are rooted to the machinery.

I crawl right over one of them. It is jammed into an alcove between two boiler plates, its bulbous, parchment-pale flesh pulsed weakly by vein-like cables. It gives off a pleasant warmth. I feel the shallow vibration of its breathing through my claws. It doesn't stir as I pass. It is just another piece of infrastructure, softer than the iron, but equally still. I pause to scrape a patch of delicious, flaky rust from a valve just above its fused head, my mandibles shearing through the metal before I continue my descent toward the fungal harvest.

I am at home in this sweltering, suffocating pressure. I am a necessary component of this slow-motion disaster. I am the eater of the forgotten world, one tick-tack step at a time.
