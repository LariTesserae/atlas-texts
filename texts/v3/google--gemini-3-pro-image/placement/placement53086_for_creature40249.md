The light is the first thing. It is not a welcoming dawn, but an interrogation. It pierces the thick, gelatinous lenses of my goggles, a searing, freezing white that translates instantly into a sharp ache behind the eyes. I do not blink. Blinking is too fast an action for this world.

I am wedged into a crevice of weeping gneiss, high on the eastern flank of Citadel Four. I have been here for perhaps an hour, or perhaps three days. Time, like sound, is fluid and crushing here.

I am a Custodian.

My awareness is not auditory. The concept of "hearing" is a vestigial data point in my genetic memory, irrelevant now. The world reaches me through the marrow of my squat, dense bones. The air itself is vibrating so violently that my heavy, insulated suit ripples against my skin.

The primary sensation is the deep, tectonic churn of the falling rivers—a constant, grinding pressure against my chest and skull. But beneath that, sharper and more insistent, is the mechanical scream of the turbines housed miles deep in the rock beneath me. I feel the RPM of Generator Unit 7 in my molars. It is slightly erratic—a micro-fracture in a bearing, perhaps, throwing a shudder through the basalt every forty-two seconds. I note this not with alarm, but as a geological fact, another datum to be addressed in the coming months.

I decide to move.

The decision takes time to travel from my slow-firing synapses to my heavy limbs. My suit is soaked, heavy with absorbed water and colonized by slimy green algae that slicks the polymer plates. I am a piece of the landscape trying to tear itself free.

I heave my left boot out of the substrate. It makes a thick, sucking sound that I cannot hear, but I feel the shudder of the release in my ankle. The ground is not solid. It is a yard-deep mattress of frigid sphagnum moss and tangled, water-gorged roots. To step is to sink up to the knee in freezing slime, to trust that the woven fungal mat beneath will hold.

I move with the deliberation of a retreating glacier. One step. Pause. Let the vibration of the cliff face filter through the soles of my boots to map the stability of the path ahead. The mist is so thick it precipitates instantly on my suit, running in rivulets down my arms. The air is thin, painfully cold, and tastes of ozone and wet pulverized rock.

My destination is a rusted iron catwalk grafted onto the sheer face of the dam, a hundred yards to my right. The structure is monumental, a brutalist cliff of stained concrete weeping geothermal steam, defying the unending avalanche of water thundering past it.

Reaching the catwalk takes an eternity of squelching, precarious steps. A slip here is not a quick death; it is a long, battering fall down thousands of feet of vertical wetland before drowning in the churning white cauldrons below.

I reach the metal grating. I clip a heavy bronze carabiner to the safety line. I feel the *clunk* of the metal locking through my palm.

I unship the heavy ceramic pry-bar from my back. My task is simple, endless. An intake vent, ten feet across, is choked with a massive wad of dislodged liverworts and timber thickets that tumbled down from the heights. The biomass is threatening to reduce the flow to the secondary turbines.

I jam the pry-bar into the sodden mass. I leverage my dense, low center of gravity against the slick metal railing. I push.

Nothing happens quickly. I am fighting the weight of the water, the stubbornness of the plant life, and the sheer scale of the blockage. I push for what might be hours, my muscles burning with a cold, slow fire. I am synchronized with the machine; the thrum of the turbine in my jawbone is my metronome.

Eventually, with a shudder felt through the pry-bar, the massive plug of vegetation tears free. It is instantly snatched away by the vertical river, hurtling down into the mist like a discarded rag.

The vibration in my teeth smooths out. The turbine is breathing again.

My task complete, I do not celebrate. I simply stop pushing. I re-stow the tool. I stand on the vibrating catwalk, coated in slime and freezing spray, the blinding light glaring off my wet suit. I am invisible against the moss and concrete, a stubborn epiphyte clinging to the machine I serve. I settle back against the wet concrete wall, letting the roar of the world rattle my bones, and wait for the next geological impulse to move.
