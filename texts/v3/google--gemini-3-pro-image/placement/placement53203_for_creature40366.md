This is where the mud meets the bone.

I am found deep in the basin floor, in the chaotic nexus where the weeping cliffs bottom out and the slum-warrens begin. The air here is hotter than above, a suffocating blanket of steam that tastes of rotten eggs and wet copper. The light is barely twilight, permanently shadowed by the towering, precarious hives above.

I am a Rib-Setter.

My body is a dense block of muscle and callous, squat even by the standards of my people. My shoulders are disproportionately wide, built up with layers of hardened skin thickened by years of friction against damp, monolithic bones. My hands are permanently stained a yellowish-brown, the cuticles caked with mineral mud and the grease of ancient marrow. My chest is a taut drum, my throat thick with the ropy muscles needed to bellow over the deafening geothermal scream that defines my workspace.

My existence is defined by the instability of the ground beneath my feet. Down here, the earth doesn't just groan; it shudders. When a section of the lower warren begins to slump—when a mud-brick wall bulges ominously outward, threatening to crush fifty sleeping bodies—they scream for me and my crew.

Our raw materials come from the deepest, hottest sloughs, where the mud boils. There, the massive, fossilized skeletons of the basin’s prehistoric subterranean beasts are occasionally vomited up by the churning earth.

I am currently thigh-deep in a steaming sump, the heat prickling through my thick, fungal-fiber loincloth. With three others, I am harnessed with thick ropes to the curved shard of a creature's ribcage. It is thrice my height, porous and stained oily black by the bacterial mats.

"HEAVE! NOW!" I roar. The sound tears from my throat, a ballistic impact meant to cut through the shrieking steam vent ten feet away. It is not speech; it is percussion.

We slam our bodies against the bone. My shoulder digs into the calcified surface. I grit my teeth against the vibration of the earth, which rattles my own jaw bone. We heave the massive artifact out of the sucking muck. The smell is tremendous—ancient rot released from clay confinement.

My job is to drag this skeletal girder through the slick, crowded alleys, shoving past people whose eyes are narrowed slits of indifference. We haul it to the site of the structural failure. There, amidst the panic of a shifting hive, we use crude obsidian sledgehammers and leverage poles made of tough fungal stalks to ram the bone into the yielding clay wall. We hammer it in until it hits bedrock or hooks onto another buried bone, pinning the sliding mud in place.

We fight geology with biology, using the dead past to prop up the screaming present.

My home is a small cavity jammed between two such structural ribs, right behind a steam vent that keeps it unbearably hot, just the way I need it to keep my muscles loose. My possessions are few: a heavy obsidian hammer polished smooth by my grip, a necklace of smaller finger-bones that clatter against my barrel chest, and a slab of dried, purple leather-fungus I chew on for sustenance.

I do not look up at the bruised sky. I look at the mud, watching for the telltale ripple that means the earth is moving again. I live in the deafening now, waiting for the next groan of the earth, ready to shout back with my shoulder against the bone.
