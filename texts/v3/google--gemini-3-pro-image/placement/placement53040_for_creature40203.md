The vibration reaches me before the sound does. It is a ceaseless, subterranean shudder that travels up through the soles of my thick rubber boots, vibrating along my shinbones and settling deep in my chest cavity. It is the heartbeat of the great geothermal fans, and my own heart has long since synchronized with its slow, churning rhythm.

I am sitting on a narrow metal grating, legs dangling over a chasm of indeterminate depth. Below me, perhaps a hundred feet down, the sickly yellow glow of sodium lamps illuminates a cluster of pig-iron hab-modules perched on squat pylons above a sump pool. The water down there boils with pale, eyeless life, churning the oily surface in a ceaseless hunger that I can hear even over the industrial roar—a wet, multitudinous slapping sound.

Up here, in the "Upper Weeping Tiers," the air is perhaps even heavier than below. It is a physical substance, a hot, wet blanket pressed against my face. I take a breath, and the taste is complex: ancient salt, ionized metal from the arc welders, and the musty, fermented tang of a thousand other sweating bodies nearby.

I raise a hand to wipe the moisture from my eyes. The hand is broad, the skin pale and doughy like uncooked bread, but mapped with the geography of my labor. The knuckles are scarred white, the calluses thick and yellow, and the nail beds are stained an indelible, greasy black. A smudge of vibrant rust-orange bacterial slime streaks my forearm where I brushed against a support strut earlier.

As my hand reaches my face, a cave cricket—translucent, ghostly, and the size of my thumb—skitters across my knuckles. I do not pause. I do not flick it away. Its delicate, tickling transit across my skin is just another texture of the air, no more notable than a draft of heat or a drip of condensation. To react to the swarm is a waste of calories, and calories mean heat, and heat is the enemy.

I am a Conduit Mender. My domain is the shadowy voids between the main industrial nodes. I spend my cycles navigating these swaying catwalks, inspecting the arteries of Foundry-Deep—the massive pipes that carry superheated steam one way and sweating coolant the other.

My welding goggles are pushed up onto my forehead, denting the leather cap I wear to keep the condensation out of my hair. I am squinting, a permanent physical affectation developed to shield my eyes from the stinging sweat and the sudden, searing flashes of arc torches in the gloom.

Across the chasm, another figure is moving along a parallel catwalk. To them, I know I appear only as a blur, a smudge of human shape indistinct within the shimmering nimbus of my own evaporated sweat. They are the same to me—a heat-hazed ghost trudging slowly through the dark. We are obscure to one another, isolated by the very atmosphere we generate.

I stand up, the motion slow and deliberate. My boots crunch satisfyingly on the metal grating. It is coated in a slick paste of oil and a layer of discarded chitin from countless generations of invertebrates crushed underfoot. I don't hear the crunch; the overwhelming Cacophony—the screech of metal, the hiss of steam, the thrum of the fans—drowns out all minor sounds.

I check the leather pouch at my belt for my tools, feeling their reassuring weight. A steam pipe three levels down has developed a weeping fracture, leaking precious pressure and feeding a sprawling patch of violent purple slime that is threatening to corrode the walkway supports. It is not an emergency. Nothing is an emergency here until the moment it kills you. It is merely a task to be endured, a slow decay to be managed.

I take a slow, thick breath of the soup-like air, adjust the heavy leather apron across my chest, and begin the slow trudge downward into the darker, hotter belly of the machine. I am a soft, sweating component of hard rock and iron, and I have work to do.
