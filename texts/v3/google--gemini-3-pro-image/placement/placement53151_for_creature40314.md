The wind here is not merely moving air; it is a physical abrasion, thin as a knife edge and screaming with cold that instantly numbs any exposed nerve. But I have no nerves exposed.

I am anchored high on the windward flank of what appears to be a traveling mountain.

My world is a deep fissure in a slab of armor plating the size of a cathedral door. I am a mineralized scab, a calcified limpet no larger than a human hand, cemented to the hide of a Mountain-Back. My shell is thick, dull grey, and rimed with the same toxic frost that coats the rest of this hemisphere.

To my left and right, the landscape extends in a terrifyingly vast, curved sweep. The horizon is so distant it seems to bend downwards, a bruised rim of charcoal sky meeting the iron-hard earth. The light never changes. It is always this dim, suffused slate-grey, flattening everything into two dimensions. There is no color here but the hues of oxidation—the scab-red of rusted iron veins in the rock below, the sickly yellow of sulfur dust trapped in the ice crevices, and the dull green patina covering the older segments of the beast I ride.

I know my host is alive not by sound, nor by warmth—it is as cold as the glacier-scraped bedrock it traverses—but by vibration.

A constant, oceanic thrum resonates through the twenty-foot thickness of its carapace and into my own small shell. It is the infrasonic song of the herd. I feel their communication as a pressure in my core, a slow, rhythmic squeezing that tells me of the location of others in the grey distance, and the direction of the mineral vents.

We are moving. The sensation is terrifyingly smooth. The giant’s spongy footpads absorb all impact, so our passage across a field of shattered granite feels like gliding over oil. Below me, hundreds of feet down, I see the slow churning of the earth as the herd passes. There are dozens of them visible, hunched, somber shapes indistinguishable from the geology until they shift their colossal weight. They are a slow-motion avalanche, silent save for the deepest vibrations that shudder through the ground itself.

The air tastes of metal. My feeding filaments, extruded gingerly from beneath my shell, taste the copper dust and lead vapor brought by the freezing fog.

Suddenly, a massive tremor shakes my anchorage. The vibration changes pitch, becoming a grinding, deep-earth shudder. The beast is feeding. Far below my perch, its massive hydraulic jaws have lowered to the ground. I feel the sickening crunch transferred up through its skeleton as it pulverizes a ridge of frozen, metal-plated bedrock. The sound is felt more than heard—a stress fracture propagating through a continent.

I hold tight to my fissure. I will spend my century-long life right here, rasping away at the mineral exfoliations of the giant’s skin, riding the slow, silent tide of geology as it grazes on a poisoned world, under a sky that will never brighten.
