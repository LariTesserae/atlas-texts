This is a world where the primary sensation is weight. The weight of the saturated air pressing against the skin, the weight of the waterlogged earth sucking at the feet, and the crushing, insulated weight of absolute silence.

I am not merely in this world; I am of it.

I find myself embodied as one of the Mycora. My consciousness is slow, deep, and resonant, much like the geological processes that drive this plateau. I am currently stationary, precisely as the description suggests, merged with the flank of a moss-hummock the size of a small cathedral. I have been here for perhaps four cycles of light and dark—it is difficult to tell, as time holds little urgency for me.

To human eyes, I would be invisible, just another bulge in the treacherous topography. My rugose hide, textured like volcanic glass and wet slate, is slick with continuous condensation. A thick blanket of living sphagnum moss covers my head and back, dripping cold water down my flanks. Patches of verdigris and iron rust streak my shoulders, a living patina that marks me as part of the landscape’s chemistry.

I do not "see" the sweeping vista of slumped mountains in the way a human would. My milky, vestigial eyes register only the difference between the pearlescent grey of day and the suffocating black of night. Instead, my perception is a complex tapestry of thermal gradients and vibrations.

The feathery arrays unfurled from my neck are tasting the air. They are incredibly sensitive, parsing the metallic tang of copper and iron carried in the mist. More importantly, they map the heat. I feel the world as a swirling map of cold pockets and warm currents. I can sense the precise location of a steam vent three miles away by the signature of its mineral plume against the ambient chill.

Beneath my massive, three-fingered feet, sank deep into the quaking sponge of peat and mud, I feel the true heartbeat of the plateau. It is a low-frequency thrum, a tectonic vibration generated by the geothermal machinery far below the sodden crust. I know the rhythm of the vents; I feel the pressurized buildup before a release, a deep-earth metronome that governs my life.

A disturbance in that rhythm pulls me from my stillness. A tremor in the peat, sharp and irregular, tells me a primary vent to the north is choking on rapid-growth moss. The thermal balance is threatened.

I begin to move. It is not a swift action, but a hydraulic shifting of tons of mass. A pressurized groan emanates from my chest as I shift my weight. I pull one pillar-like leg from the sucking ground—a slow, loud, squelching sound that dies instantly in the heavy air—and plant it forward. The ground quivers for fifty feet around me.

I walk with agonizing deliberation through the choked blanket of life. Giant, leathery fern fronds, slick with dew, brush against my armored sides. I step over a sluggish, slate-skinned salamander the size of a log; it does not startle, merely slides deeper into a rivulet of cold mud. A herd of Rust-Wool grazers is ahead. Their matted, algae-heavy coats are indistinguishable from the vegetation until they slowly lumber out of my path, their movement as silent and heavy as drifting barges.

I reach the troubled vent. It is a fissure in a slab of weeping, iron-rich granite, almost completely obscured by a violently green explosion of moss that has grown over the aperture in the last few weeks. The steam is hissing angrily, trapped.

With a massive, spade-like hand tipped with basalt claws, I begin the work. I tear great hunks of the sodden moss away, the fibers screaming silently as they are ripped from the rock. It is heavy, mindless labor, continued for hours. Finally, with a sound like a bursting artery, the vent clears. A thick, hot plume of mineral steam blasts upward, washing over me.

The heat is ecstatic. The metallic taste coats my sensory arrays. I stand in the plume, letting the warmth seep into my slate-like hide, recharging my core temperature.

My task done, hunger asserts itself. I turn toward the hillside, where terrace formations of bracket fungi jut out like rusted industrial shelving. I approach a shelf the size of a table, its rim glowing with faint copper bioluminescence. I grasp the rim. It is hard as cast iron. I snap off a massive section.

I carry the shard to my mouth. There are no teeth, only grinding plates of ceramic bone. As I chew, the sound is like boulders being crushed in a glacier. I extract the concentrated geothermal minerals, the iron and copper nutrients my immense physiology requires.

Satisfied, I feel the need to re-anchor myself. I find a new hummock, its curve receptive to my bulk. I settle against it, the peat yielding and accepting me. I lift one heavy foot and bring it down—*THUMP*.

The vibration travels for miles through the water-saturated earth. It is a simple code, felt by the few other Mycora scattered across this drowning continent: *Territory mark. Vent clear. Returning to stillness.*

A distant, faint tremor returns the acknowledgement.

I let the moss settle around me again. The mist thickens. I slow my internal rhythms, matching the geothermal hum beneath me, waiting, enduring, becoming the landscape once more.
