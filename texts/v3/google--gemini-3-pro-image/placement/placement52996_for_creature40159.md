This is a world where existence is an act of endless bracing.

I am located on the "Mid-Strata Terrace," a precarious residential shelf carved into the sheer face of a colossal beryl formation. Below me, the terrifying drop into the churning light and magma fissures; above me, the dizzying, multifaceted ceiling of the geode that refracts the geothermal glare into confusing spears of heat.

I am not standing; "standing" implies a temporary state, a readiness to move. I am *anchored*. My four-foot-tall body is a dense aggregate of layered silicate plates and polished hematite nodules, a weight that would crush a biological spine, but here, it serves as necessary ballast against the shuddering reality. My wide, three-toed feet, shod in thick native copper that has annealed perfectly to the shape of the crystal path, are gripped tight to the vibrating quartz floor.

I do not hear the roaring cacophony that fills this sphere. I *am* the roaring cacophony. The geology screaming deep below travels through the crystal lattice of the city and terminates in my own dense structure. I feel the foundational thrum in my core; I feel the high-frequency chime of the nearby obsidian-leaved vines vibrating against my shoulder plating; I feel the deeper, grinding bass of a stone crustacean moving three levels down. This symphony of vibrations is my sight, my map of the world.

My task, the work of my current lifecycle, is the widening of a transit corridor on this terrace. It is a task inherited from the one who stood here before me, whose polished form now rests in the lower archives.

In my vice-like, three-fingered metal grip, I hold a chisel tipped with industrial diamond. I locate a microscopic imperfection in the beryl wall—a stress fracture I identify not by sight, but by the way the vibration dampens slightly at that specific point. I brace my copper-hinged shoulder. I apply pressure. It is not a violent strike, but a relentless, geological squeeze.

*Cr-k.*

A flake of crystal, no larger than a fingernail paring, detaches. It falls, immediately caught by the sonic updrafts, joining the swirling dust motes that glitter in the searing light.

That flake represents the work of the last twelve pulses of the geothermal vents.

Beside me is another of my kind, an elder whose silicate armor has been scoured to an iridescent mirror-sheen by eons of acoustic friction. They are currently motionless, a statue of glittering mineral, letting a particularly violent tremor pass through their frame without resistance.

They reach out a heavy, segmented arm and press their copper palm flat against my thoracic plate. I feel the sharp, precise percussion transmitted directly through my chassis: *Tap-tap-trip-tap-thrum.*

It is our language of conduction. Translated into soft-flesh concepts, they have communicated: *"The resonance frequency of this beryl vein is shifting deeper. Adjust angle of incidence by three degrees downward to avoid shattering the structural integrity."*

I do not nod. I acknowledge by pressing my own palm to the wall and tapping a slow, affirmative double-beat that echoes back into their arm. I adjust my grip on the chisel. The movement takes perhaps twenty seconds. Haste is inefficiency. Haste creates unstable vibrations.

Suddenly, the floor beneath my feet gives a terrifying shudder, distinct from the usual background roar. The ambient heat spikes, searing against my non-flammable exterior. The air grows even thinner.

It is a Pulse. The deep vents are opening wide.

Around us, the mineral jungle reacts in a frenzied blur of stiffness. The spun-glass ferns below angle sharply, their fragile forms vibrating so intensely they blur. The copper vines on the wall tighten their grip, metal screeching against crystal—a sound I feel as a sharp needles in my joints.

I do not panic. My thoughts move with the deliberate speed of shifting tectonic plates. The elder and I merely crouch lower, maximizing our surface contact with the shuddering path, locking our copper joints into rigid immobility. We become part of the mountain. The wave of amplified sound hits us, a physical blow that would pulverize flesh. We weather it. We let the screaming energy wash over our polished carapaces, eroding us microscopically, perfecting us.

The pulse lasts for an eternity, perhaps an hour in soft-world time. Then, the vibration recedes back to its normal, marrow-shaking level.

I unlock my joints with a groan of metal on stone. I raise the diamond chisel again. I find the new stress fracture. I begin to apply pressure. There is much work to do, and we have all the time that geology can provide.
