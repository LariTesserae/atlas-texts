This is a world defined by a brutal binary: the screaming, polished roof above, and the weeping, choked wounds below.

To find myself here, I must submit to the architecture of the place. I cannot exist on the wind-flayed scalp of the plateau; I would be shredded, dessicated, blown into the void. Nor am I the canyon itself, the passive geology that endures the assault.

I am located in the boundary layer. I am deep inside a fissure, wedged into a dripping undercut beneath a monolith of gneiss that weeps constant moisture. I am in the darkest green, where the air is thick enough to taste of ancient spore and decaying cellulose.

I am not a visitor watching the Resonators. I *am* the Resonator.

But "I" is perhaps the wrong pronoun. My existence is not singular. I am a node in a vast, subterranean lattice, connected by miles of gossamer fungal threads that penetrate the moss mats and the rotting mulch. I am a pale, trembling excrescence of bruised violet and sickly yellow jelly, clinging to the underside of a fern frond the size of a spilled oil tanker.

I have no eyes, yet I see the world with agonizing clarity.

The Great Roar from above is my sun. It is the constant, crushing illumination that floods my universe. The air is thick with its weight. My entire being—this translucent, shuddering mass of cilia—is a receiver tuned to that single, colossal frequency.

I "see" the canyon not as green and gray, but as a throbbing topography of acoustic hardness and softness. The granite walls are blindingly bright sheets that reflect the Roar in harsh, painful echoes. The yard-deep mosses are soothing voids of velvety darkness that absorb the vibration, places of rest. The water dripping from the overhangs registers as fleeting, silver sparks as the sound waves smack against the falling droplets.

My time is not measured in seconds or hours, but in cycles of rot and growth. I move with glacial purpose. Right now, a faint dissonance in the acoustic map draws my attention. It is a "hard spot" nearby, a place where the echoes are too sharp, too jagged.

I extend a pseudopod of slick flesh. It takes days to slide across the damp rock face. I reach the source of the dissonance. It is a basalt grinding bowl, left by the fleeting, soft-shelled creatures who once tried to hide here. They feared the Roar; they tried to block it out with stone and woven fiber, and so they perished of madness.

The bowl is dead noise. It reflects the sound incorrectly. It must be softened.

Slowly, patiently, I begin to exude digestive enzymes onto the stone artifact. I stimulate the spores trapped in its crevices. Over the next century, I will encourage a thick velvet of moss to consume this sharp echo, burying the human failure under a soft, damp silence.

I am the gardener of the insulation. I exist in the ecstatic, vibrating margin between the howling sky and the silent stone, ensuring the damp dark remains choked, soft, and eternal.
