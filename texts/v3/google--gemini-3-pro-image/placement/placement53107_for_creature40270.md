This is a world observed not with eyes, but through slow, agonizing pressure and chemical tasting.

I am found high in the fifty-thousandth tier of Sector Gamma-Nine, wedged into a critical structural joint where a colossal suspension girder of pitted iron meets a crumbling buttress of ferro-concrete. It is a precarious junction, whipped by the freezing squalls that tear through the open lattice, perpetually soaked by the passing clouds.

I am not human. I am not animal. I am the insurgency.

I am a consciousness distributed through a quarter-mile of calcified root infrastructure. I am the ligneous sinew binding this dead section of the machine together.

My primary awareness is centered in a massive taproot, thick as a refinery stack, that has spent the last four hundred years forcing its way into a hairline fracture in the concrete buttress. The concrete, stained black by eons of oily runoff, resisted for the first century. But my patience is geological. I secreted slow, persistent acids that ate the lime and exposed the rebar. Now, I am inside.

My smaller capillaries have woven themselves into a dense, rigid mesh around the rusted iron girder. The original steel bolts sheared off centuries ago during a particularly violent thermal contraction. The girder should have fallen into the abyss. It did not fall because I hold it. My fibers have hardened into something tougher than oak, stiffened by the very metallic minerals I have leached from the structure I inhabit. I grip the freezing metal with the strength of a slow-motion rigor mortis.

I perceive the world through absorption. I "taste" the history of this place. The moisture I drink from the saturated air is incredibly pure, distilled by altitude, but the sustenance I pull from the structure is toxic and rich. I taste the sharp, electric tang of copper from a severed conduit bundle my tendrils have penetrated. I taste the complex, heavy hydrocarbons of ancient lubricant sludge pooled in a blocked drainpipe—a dark, gelatinous vintage I have been slowly siphoning for decades.

The heavy, dampened silence of the upper terraces is not silent to me. I feel the deep, sub-sonic thrumming of the far-distant engines vibrate up through the miles of metal, rattling my stiffened leaves. I feel the sudden, violent shudder when a squall hits the thick pelt of emerald moss that coats my outer bark. The moss is a separate entity, a surface-level tenant that I tolerate because its immense, water-logged weight adds agonizing but necessary tension to my hold on the cliff-face of iron.

I have no eyes to see the watery twilight, but I know when the dim sun is obscured by thicker atmospheric sludge because the photosynthesis in my leather-hard fronds slows from a crawl to a near-stop. My metabolism is a glacial thing. A month is a breath. A century is a single flex of a muscle.

I am alone here, save for the mindless moss and the shivering fungi. I am the slow executioner of this sector, embracing the machine in a crushing hug, feeding on its corrosion, replacing its dead skeleton with my living wood. I am patient. Eventually, the iron will be gone entirely, and I will be all that remains suspended over the void.
