The cold is not something that happens to you here; it is a fundamental property of existence, like gravity or mass. It resides in the dense, pale meat of my own body, a permanent ache in the joints that the thickest layers of scavenged oily wool cannot entirely banish.

I am crouched motionless on a rusted I-beam that juts out over a steaming chasm of machinery, high in the mid-levels of Sector 7. I am built for this squat, precarious leverage—short, dense heavy-boned. If I were tall and slender like the ancestors whispered about in the oldest click-sagas, the constant, sickening sway of the catwalks or the sudden seismic jolts of the "emerging" earth would have pitched me into the grinding gears long ago.

My skin feels leathery and thick, coated in a thin, protective sheen of mineral lubricant I harvested from a weeping turbine housing this morning. Around my shoulders and cowl, I wear a living cloak. The vibrant green moss, painstakingly cultivated on a lattice of wire mesh, drinks the atmospheric damp and traps my body heat far better than any dead fabric. When I stay this still, curled against the frosted iron, I know I look like just another fungal lump on the city’s vast, corroding skeleton.

I am waiting, and I am listening.

There is no sound in the air save for the wet drip of condensation and the immense, muffled silence of the fog. But pressing the calloused palm of my hand against the cold metal beam, I can hear the city talking.

Through the iron bones of the basin comes a complex vibration, felt in the wrist and elbow more than heard in the ear. *Thump-thump-scrape. Pause. Rapid-tap-tap.*

It is a geological report from the deep-dwellers in Sector 9. The message travels for miles through interconnected pipes and girders. It tells me that the obsidian shard pushing up beneath the old smelting complex has stopped moving for now. The immediate danger of collapse has passed.

I take a heavy spanner from my belt and tap a response onto the beam, sending the vibration ringing down into the fog: *Received. Steam pressure stable in upper quad.*

I do not speak aloud. To use one’s voice here is a vulgar waste of energy, barely audible against the sound-eating moss anyway. Worse, it is dangerous.

Below me, perhaps two hundred feet down through the swirling gray vapor, past layers of sagging habitation units hanging like wasp nests, I see movement in a canyon between two gargantuan heat-exchangers. My thermal-sensitive eyes pick out a faint signature—cooler than the venting steam, but warmer than the rime-crusted metal.

It is slate-colored and moves with terrifying, hydraulic slowness. A fog-gaunt. It is actively hunting. It pauses, its shovel-shaped head tilting. It cannot see me through the soup, and it cannot smell me over the pervasive stench of ozone and wet rust. It is listening for vibration. A dropped wrench, a stumble on a loose grating, a shout—that is the dinner bell.

I hold my breath. I slow my heart. I become just another piece of cold, damp infrastructure. The baseline thrum of the city—that deep, resonant groan from underground that never ceases—masks the tiny sounds of my organic existence.

The predator waits, sculpted silence, then flows onward like viscous oil, disappearing behind a curtain of dripping ferns.

I exhale slowly. The chill bites deeper into my lungs. A furry pipe-skulk, slick with moisture, darts past my footing, its claws clicking softly as it chases a translucent crustacean into a puddle of oily runoff. Life—stubborn, quiet, and hungry—continues.

I shift my weight, testing the beam beneath me before rising. A drone swarm is chittering nearby, patching a micro-fracture in a support pillar with gray slurry. We are all just maintaining our precarious hold on this freezing, shifting beast of metal and mud.

I have steam valves to check in Sector 4 before the next upheaval begins. I wrap my living green cloak tighter and begin the slow, silent climb across the web.
