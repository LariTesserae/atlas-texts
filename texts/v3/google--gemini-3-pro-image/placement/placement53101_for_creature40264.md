I am not in the water; I am of the water.

I exist in the crushing, comforting warmth, suspended in the eternal womb of the geode. I am a point of density within the pressure, a slow-moving concretion of iron and cartilage. I am a **Ferro-Kelp Grazier**.

My reality is not visual. Sight is a useless sense in this viscous, mineral-thick soup. I know the concept of "light" only as thermal gradients—the searing heat near the furnace portholes versus the cooler, ambient warmth of the open dark.

Instead, I taste the world.

A thousand feathered sensory barbels wreath my armored head, sifting the heavy liquid. The water is a complex narrative of taste: the sharp tang of dissolved iron from the girders I cling to; the rich, rotten egg smell of sulfur plumes rising from below; the subtle, electric metallic taste that bleeds from high-voltage cables.

Right now, the taste that drives me is the sweet, chemical bloom of purple algae.

I am clinging to the underside of a massive conduction tower. My eight clawed limbs, tipped with hardened iron deposits, puncture a foot-thick layer of bacterial matting to find purchase on the pitted metal beneath. I do not swim. To swim is folly in this density. I climb the endless coral reef of machinery.

I move with glacial deliberation. I shift my weight—tons of mineralized armor plate, crusted heavy with barnacles that are centuries old. The metal structure beneath me groans under the shifted load, a sound dulled instantly by the water, felt more as a vibration in my core than heard.

A current hits me—a slow, upward draft of superheated water. My barbels twitch. The taste of sulfur intensifies, rich with the chemical nutrients I need.

I begin the arduous trek toward the source. I follow a cable thicker than my own body, using the intense electromagnetic field humming within it as a guide-rail through the murk. My passing disturbs the bone-white fronds of chemosynthetic kelp tangled in the wires; they brush against my armored flanks, feeling like slick, wet ghosts.

The journey of a hundred meters takes hours. I exist in a state of near-torpor, my metabolism geared to the geological pace of this drowned world. Below me, around me, and above me, the subsonic thrum of the planet-sized geothermal engines beats its endless rhythm. It is the heartbeat of the organism I live within, a vibration so deep it resonates in the heavy fluid in my joints.

Finally, I reach the prize. A heat exchanger, leaking thermal energy, coated thick with a pulsating mat of gelatinous purple algae.

I anchor myself firmly, digging my rear claws into a corroded flange. I lower my head. My rasping mouthparts, metallic teeth harder than granite, begin to scrape against the heat exchanger. The sound is a low grinding, lost in the planetary thrum. I consume the chemical sludge, my internal furnace igniting to break down the sulfur compounds, turning the industrial waste into sustenance.

As I feed, I sense another presence nearby. It is immense, far larger than I. It is not moving. My chemical sense tastes ancient rust and intense, localized heat.

It is a **Vent Warden**, one of my kin who has succumbed to the final stage of our life cycle. It has settled directly over a ruptured steam pipe, its body grown huge and fused to the architecture. It is no longer just a creature; it has become a living cap for the vent, absorbing the fury of the earth’s core. I feel the immense heat radiating from its calcified shell. It is alive, but silent, dreaming its endless, heavy dream, a permanent fixture of the city’s plumbing.

I finish grazing, leaving a patch of bare, gleaming metal on the heat exchanger that will be overgrown again in a decade. Sated, I do not move on immediately. I remain clamped to the tower, letting the warmth of the water seep through my armor. I am safe in the crushing dark, a single blood cell slowly circulating in the veins of a sleeping giant.
