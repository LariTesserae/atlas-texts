The overwhelm of this place is instantaneous. It is not an environment you enter; it is a medium that absorbs you.

I find myself clinging to the mid-level geology, positioned in the treacherous zone between the thunderous ceiling and the compost floor. I am pressed flat against a vast, curved sheet of ancient obsidian, a glassy flow solidified eons ago that now serves as a weeping vertical highway.

My location is defined by texture and temperature. The rock beneath me is terrifyingly slick, coated in a perpetual biofilm of condensation and microscopic algal slime. It is warm to the touch—not the scorching heat of the deep vents, but the steady, incubated warmth of a fevered body.

The air is thick enough to taste. It is a cloying soup of ammonia from the guano raining down, the musty rot of fungal blooms, and the metallic tang of wet volcanic ash. Every breath is a labor, pulling in the heavy, humid churn of millions of other respirations.

I am utterly blind in the conventional sense. The faint grey phosphorescence mentioned in the description is, to my senses, merely a slightly different flavor of darkness—a vague insubstantiality that offers no useful data.

Therefore, I am a creature built entirely for tactile existence in the wet dark. I am low, segmented, and armored.

I am a grazer of the vertical slick. I am something like an enormous, flattened prehistoric slater or a highly adapted terrestrial crustacean, perhaps the size of a human hand. My carapace is calcified and bumpy, mimicking the rougher patches of basalt to break up my outline, though there are few eyes here to see it.

My world is defined by the fourteen specialized legs that pin me to the precarious wall. Their tips are fitted with microscopic hooks and suction pads that find purchase on the slime-coated glass of the obsidian. To lose my grip is to fall into the churning slush of the floor below, to be consumed by the slime molds or snapped up by a lethargic salamander in a tepid pool.

My primary sense organs are not eyes, but a dense array of sensitive bristles fringing my head and flanks, and two long, whip-like antennae that are constantly in motion. They are my cartographers. They tap-tap-tap against the stone ahead, reading the topography of moss, measuring the depth of water droplets, and, crucially, detecting vibration.

And the vibrations are ceaseless.

Through the stone against my belly, I feel the deep, thrumming bass of the geothermal vents far below. Through the air pressing down on my carapace, I feel the maniacal, high-frequency screeching of the bat-ceiling, a physical pressure that makes my sensitive bristles ache.

I am currently frozen in place. My antennae have detected a sharp, rhythmic disturbance on the rock face nearby: *clack-clack-skitter*.

One of the giant, bone-pale crickets is nearby. It is much faster than I am, and while it primarily grazes on fungi, in this protein-hungry hive, it would not hesitate to crack my shell. I press myself flatter into a shallow depression in the obsidian, allowing the weeping water to flow over my back, waiting for the *clack-clack* vibration to fade.

I am waiting to resume my slow, deliberate climb toward a patch of "daughy" fungal bloom I smell growing on an overhang above. That is my existence here: a slow, gripping migration across slippery heat, navigating by touch and tremor, forever suspended between the screaming tapestry above and the digesting swamp below, consuming the decay of the dark womb.
