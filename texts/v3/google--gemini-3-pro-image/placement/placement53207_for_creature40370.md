The heat is not a thing that arrives; it is a medium in which I am suspended. It presses against my chest, a suffocating, aqueous weight that would crush lungs born to thinner air. But my chest is broad and shallow, rising slowly, infrequently, processing the saturated vapor that passes for atmosphere.

I am standing on a narrow gantry of fused ceramic grating, cantilevered over the edge of the Fourth Terrace. Below me, a drop of three hundred feet vanishes into a churning banks of superheated fog. Through gaps in the mist, I catch flashes of the terrace face—a weeping wall of hematite red and shocking yellow, slick with hyper-thermophilic slime.

I do not need to look down to know the danger. A single misstep on the slick grating, a moment of hurried imbalance, and I would fall into one of the collection basins that simmer at a steady ninety degrees Celsius. We learn to walk before we can walk: slow, deliberate, placing the flat of our textured boots squarely, gripping the microbial slickness. We move with the hydraulic patience of the bone-stalkers that hunt in the pools beneath us.

My skin feels thick today, armored. The mottled patterns of rust and pale blue along my forearms blend perfectly with the mineral deposit buildup on the handrail I am gripping. If I were to stand perfectly still, the membrane-winged things drifting on the thermal updrafts would mistake me for a statuette carved from the riser stone.

The world is blindingly bright. The diffused glare of the sun through the steam cloud would sear unprotected retinas. I flick my nictitating membranes across my eyes—a second, translucent set of lids that dims the world to a manageable, milky luminescence.

There is no sound but the Hiss. It is a white noise so total it feels like silence. It drowns out the thrum of blood in my ears. It is the sound of a million gallons of superheated mineral water turning to steam, the breath of the planet venting pressure.

Because of the Hiss, I experience the world through my feet.

Through the soles of my multilayered bacterial-mat boots, I feel the deep, vibrating hum of the hydro-thermal cathedral behind me. It is a good, steady resonance—the sound of equilibrium. But I am here for a specific tremor.

I reach out with a large hand, calloused thick as leather, and lay it flat against the surface of Arterial Pipe 4-Beta. The ceramic is scorching, hot enough to ignite dry wood instantly, but it merely feels pleasantly warm against my mineralized palm. I close my shielded eyes and concentrate.

I feel the rushing torrent inside the pipe, the slurry of dissolved silica and rare earth metals moving at tremendous pressure. It feels smooth. Laminar.

Then, a twitch against my palm. A minute shudder in the flow dynamics.

*Turbulence upstream.*

I grasp my metal-tipped staff and tap the ceramic pipe three times in rapid succession, then once slowly. The sharp vibrations ring out, traveling along the pipe network far faster than a shout could penetrate the Hiss.

A moment later, a vibration travels back to me through the grating under my feet. Two sharp taps from the gantry below. My partner, unseen in the fog, has received the signal. They will adjust the crystalline intake valves.

I remove my hand from the pipe and wait. The heat presses in. A bead of condensation rolls down my hairless neck and pools beneath the chitin harness across my shoulders. I stand perfectly still, a piece of living geology, waiting for the vibration in the pipe to smooth out, stewarding the scalding blood of the earth.
