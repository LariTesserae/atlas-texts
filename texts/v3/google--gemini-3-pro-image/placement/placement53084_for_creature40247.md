The amber twilight is thick today, heavy with the golden dust of pollen that catches in the dry mesh of my throat. I am suspended four hundred feet above the bowl's shadowed floor, my life resting on the integrity of a braided succulent fiber thick as a man’s thigh.

I am an Umbrella-Walker, and I am a Keeper of the Ways.

My current perch is a rounded protrusion of the ancient, tawny granite, worn smooth as a river stone by time. My calloused, prehensile toes grip the sloping curve with absolute certainty; gravity here is not a fear, but a constant companion I have known since birth. My barrel chest heaves slowly, pulling the thin, warm air deep into my capacity lungs. My skin, the colour of dried terracotta clay and feeling much like vellum, does not sweat. Instead, it absorbs the faint, precious dampness clinging to the silvery hairs of the giant cactus-tree against which I lean.

I am mending a primary artery of the hive—a suspension bridge connecting the lower fungal terraces to the Aviary platforms. The main cable had begun to fray, chewed by the incessant friction of a thousand passing feet and the mandibles of nesting chitin-beetles.

My tools hang from the complex leather harness strapped across my lean torso: a razor-sharp adze chiseled from obsidian, a sewing awl made from the polished thigh-bone of a gliding avian, and coils of fresh, un-cured fiber.

The work is slow. Patience is the marrow of our existence. To rush a splice is to invite death, not just for myself, but for the dozens carrying heavy loads of cured meat who will cross this span tomorrow. I take a segment of the fibrous tendril, threading it through the existing braid with the bone awl, twisting my wrist with a practiced, sibilant click of effort. The smell up here is intense—the intoxicating, musty resin of the pierced succulent timber, mixed with the acrid scent of my own cured-hide clothing.

The hive does not stop for my repairs. The noise is a deafening, constant pressure against my eardrums—the piercing shrieks of the wide-winged gliders swooping through the upper canopy, the chattering of nimble mammals racing along the very rope I am fixing, and the low, booming hum of conversation floating up from the dwellings below.

A group of four haulers approaches my precarious station, laden with bundles of dried purple fruit. There is no room to step aside on the narrow walkway. We perform the necessary dance. I flatten my body against the waxy skin of the giant succulent trunk, my heavy braids—weighted with carved root-beads—clacking against the wood. The lead hauler, a woman whose skin is a deep, dusty umber, steps carefully over my seated form. Her hand, rough and warm, grips my shoulder firmly as she passes—a tactile reassurance, a shared anchor in the void. We exchange no words; the avian shrieks are too loud right now. Instead, she offers a quick, sharp nod and a signing gesture with her free hand: *Strength to the weave.*

I return the sign: *Safe passage.*

They move on, vanishing into the textured shadows of the interlocking branches ahead. I return to my splice. I look down for a moment, past my busy, horny-nailed hands. Through the gaps in the cantilevered stone platforms and the woven leather roofs of houses stacked like fungi, I see the deep purple shadows of the bowl’s floor, where the slow reptiles scrape against the rock. My large, golden-orange eyes penetrate the gloom easily, tracking their sluggish movements.

Then, I look out, past the boundary of our sheltering cliffs. There is only the blinding white expanse of the cloud ocean, encompassing the world below. We are an island in the sky, clinging to the dry, warm shoulder of eternity, weaving our lives from dust and fiber, slowly, carefully, under the amber gaze of the sun trapped in our canopy. I tighten the final knot, test the tension with a sharp tug, and prepare to climb higher.
