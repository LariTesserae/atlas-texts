The cold is the first thing, but it is not a sharp cold; it is a heavy, pervasive embrace that sinks through skin and muscle to the bone. I am lying down. No, I am embedded.

I am half-buried in the plush, waterlogged flank of the western cliff face, perhaps twenty feet above the trembling basin floor. Where I end and the environment begins has become impossible to define. My back is cushioned by a mattress of sphagnum moss feet thick, saturated to the point that I am essentially floating in suspended water. Over my legs and chest, the pale, heavy fronds of the giant ferns act as a blanket, dripping their slow, constant condensation onto what used to be my clothes.

I am no longer a distinct entity; I have become substrate.

My skin has gone soft, macerated by the ceaseless humidity until it has achieved the consistency of wet tissue paper. It is permeable now. I no longer need to drink; the supersaturated air continuously filters into my body through every bloated pore, and the fluids of my own biology seep out to mingle with the slime of the wall.

I cannot see. The darkness is absolute, a thick, velvet bunting pressed against open eyes. But sight is a forgotten luxury here, replaced by an agonizingly acute tactile awareness.

I am a intersection point for the teeming life of the hollow. I feel the cold, slick ribbons of blind salamanders gliding across my ankles, their delicate, clawless toes seeking purchase on my softened flesh. A slug, thick as a log and smelling faintly of ozone and drowned meat, is slowly agonizingly crossing my midsection. Its mucilaginous trail is colder than the air, a heavy, numbing line drawn across my stomach. Around my ears, the soft, chitinous clicking of the giant woodlice is a ceaseless murmur, like the sound of thousands of dry leaves rattling, though nothing here is dry.

I do not move. To move would be to disrupt the perfect, suffocating equilibrium. I have learned the rhythm of the place. I breathe in time with the slow, weeping drip of the rock above me.

And beneath it all, beneath the rustle of the millions of sliding bodies and the *plip-plip* of the falling drops, I feel the Anchor.

It is a vibration in the deep peat, too low to hear but impossible to ignore. It travels up through the spongy ground, through the moss cliffs, and into my spine. It is the slow, seismic thrum of the massive white god in the mire below. I feel its metabolism churning, a deep, geological grinding. Every hour or so, the entire basin shudders—a minute contraction of the moss, a ripple in the mud—as the great thing takes a single, heaving breath.

I am waiting for that breath. I am waiting to slide down the wall a fraction of an inch as the peat compresses. I am a node in the closed loop, slowly dissolving, waiting to eventually become runoff, to filter down into the dark, wet maw of the stasis beneath us all. I am cold, I am wet, and I am perfectly still.
