I am wedged into a fissure of slick, weeping granite, high on the windward face of a colossal stone hump. The air up here is so thin it feels brittle in my lungs, each breath a shallow sip of bitter ice.

I am of the People. My legs are short and thick, built for low centers of gravity on icy slopes. My skin is a pale, waterlogged hide that feels perpetually numb, save for the deep ache in my joints that predicts the shifts in the great machine beneath us. I am wrapped in four layers of woven moss cloth, heavy and stiff with frozen moisture, smelling of deep cellar earth and the musk of my own unwashed body.

I am hunting green.

My basalt scraping-tool, chipped to a precarious edge, bites into the cliff face. I am harvesting a particularly rich vein of emerald moss that has colonized a sheltered overhang. It is grueling work. My fingers, though thick and calloused, are clumsy with cold. I scrape the furry pulp into a woven sack slung across my chest, moving with glacial deliberation. To rush is to sweat; to sweat is to freeze; to freeze is to die.

The silence is total, amplified by the muffled scratching of my tool. There are no birds to watch me, no insects to bite me. There is only the gray stone, the purple-green scrub, and the endless, bruising sky.

Then, the warning comes.

It is not a sound. It is a sickness deep in the fluid of my inner ear, a sudden, horrifying vertigo that tells me down is no longer beneath my feet. My bone marrow throbs, an ancestral alarm bell ringing in the core of me.

The Weather is turning.

I do not panic. Panic is energy wasted. I immediately stop scraping. I jam my basalt tool into a deep crack and hook my thick fingers around a gnarled root of iron-wood that snakes out of the rock face. I flatten my body against the wet stone, becoming one with the cliff, squeezing my eyes shut against the biting wind.

A tremor runs through the world—not a shaking of the ground, but a shudder in the air itself.

I open my eyes just a slit. The gravity has gone soft. The moss scraps I just dislodged do not fall; they drift sideways, tumbling languidly like ash in a thermal updraft. A chunk of rime ice, the size of my head, breaks loose from the overhang above me. Instead of crushing me, it floats outward, hovering at eye level, rotating slowly, glistening with impossible buoyancy in the gray light.

Further down the valley, the groan of the world changes pitch. A million tons of scree and glacier ice are lifting slowly into the air, a silent, monumental levitation. The wind screams, confused by the sudden vacuum.

I hold on. My knuckles turn white against the dark root. I breathe shallowly through my nose, tasting blood and ice. I do not wonder *why* the rocks float. I only focus on the ache in my arms and the friction of my moss-wool against the granite. This is just the mountain stretching its strange muscles. It will pass.

For ten minutes, the valley hangs suspended in this nauseating anti-gravity. Then, with a wet, sickening crunch that I feel in my teeth, reality snaps back.

The hovering ice chunk drops like a hammer, shattering on a ledge feet below me. The suspended glacier down the valley crashes back onto its bed with a boom that knocks the wind out of me, sending tremors through the cliff face that almost shake me loose. The familiar, crushing weight returns to my shoulders.

I exhale a cloud of dense fog. I wait for the vertigo to subside, for my inner ear to stabilize. I wiggle my frozen toes in my moss boots. I am still here.

Slowly, methodically, I pry my basalt tool from the crack. The vein of green is still there, waiting. I resume scraping. The sack must be filled before the true dark comes, and the gods beneath the stone begin their deeper, more terrifying dreams.
