The cold here is not the stripping wind of the surface, the howling void that erases skin. Here, the cold is a heavy, wet blanket that seeks the bone.

I am pressed against the slick, dripping wall of the upper fissure, three hundred feet below the surface world and still fifty feet above the canyon floor. My existence is vertical.

I am one of the Menders. My shoulders are thick layers of muscle stacked tight, built to hold fast against the pull of gravity. My legs are short, stout pistons draped in baggy trousers of scraped hide that have been soaked in fern-wax until they are stiff as wood. My feet, wrapped in felted moss and oiled leather, curve around a narrow lip of icy rock with a grip that feels more like a root system than flesh and bone.

My task today is high work. I am re-greasing the anchor ropes of the highest bridge, a swaying catwalk made of braided leather and rough planks that connects the eastern fungaria terraces to the western dwelling-combs. The air up here is thicker with the impending "day." The moisture freezes instantly on my copper tools, riming the hammered edges in white.

Below me, the city sleeps its waking sleep. It is twilight in the slot. I hear the muffled thrum of life through the stone against my chest—someone grinding dried lichen in a mortar just inches away on the other side of the rock face; the soft, resonant vibration of a mother humming deep in her throat to a bundled infant. The acoustics of the cleft are so intimate that I feel these sounds more than I hear them. The peat smoke rises sluggishly, a constant, earthy incense trapped by the thermal inversion, stinging my nostrils pleasantly.

I finish working tallow into the frozen braid of the rope, my breath clouding thick and freezing to the fur trim of my hood. Then, I stop. We all stop.

It is time.

I press my forehead against the frozen moss of the cliff face, ice crystals crunching against the thick, nerveless skin of my brow ridges. I squeeze my eyes shut until they are mere slits protected by the heavy folds of skin and the soot-stained tattoos that ring them.

Above, the slice of gray sky turns blinding white.

High Noon strikes the canyon like a physical blow. The shaft of sunlight, perfectly aligned with the slot, hammers down. Even through closed eyelids, the glare is searing. I open my eyes just enough to see the world ignite.

The gray ice sheathing the walls suddenly blazes with captured fire. The light refracts, bounces, and multiplies, filling the deep pocket with a brilliance that makes the eyes water instantly. The colors, muted moments before, scream into existence. The mosses around my hands are an electric, vibrant green that seems to hum with energy. The dripping water sparkles like falling diamonds.

The silence breaks, replaced by the low, gravelly murmur of my kin. We move with the light.

Below on the terraces, squat, bulky figures emerge from dark doorways, their moss-woven cloaks blending perfectly with the walls, making them look like moving hillocks of vegetation. They move with a rolling, sure-footed gait, copper knives flashing in the sudden brilliance as they begin the harvest of the pale, meaty fungi that thrive in the constant damp.

Up high, I work faster. The light brings warmth, just enough to make the ice slick and treacherous. I move across the swaying bridge I just mended, checking the planks. The light is a precious, violent gift. It is life, but it is also blinding, so I keep my head down, watching my felted feet grip the rime-slicked wood.

For an hour, we are industrious beetles crawling through a glittering, frozen jewelry box. Then, as quickly as it arrived, the sun passes its zenith. The blinding shaft tilts, strikes the upper lip of the canyon, and vanishes.

Twilight crashes back down upon us. The electric greens fade back to somber olives and bruised purples. The intense glare softens into the familiar gray gloom.

My shift is over. I coil my ropes and begin the slow climb down a series of notches worn smooth by ten thousand feet before mine. I slip into the narrow opening of my family’s dwelling unit, pulling the heavy, fat-sealed hide door closed behind me, shutting out the biting damp.

Inside, the air is thick with the smell of burning peat and ancient bodies. It is small, just a hollow carved from the rock, warmed by a tiny, smoldering brazier. I sit on a mat of woven fiber, my barrel chest heaving slowly in the thick air. I take a piece of dried fungi offered by my mate, our fingers brushing in a quick, silent gesture of greeting.

We eat in the profound quiet, listening to the drip of thawing ice refreezing outside in the eternal twilight. We are waiting, with the patience of the stones themselves, for the light to return tomorrow.
