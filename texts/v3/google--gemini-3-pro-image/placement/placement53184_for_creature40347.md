This is where I am.

My existence is defined by the burning in my forearms and the callous pads on my fingertips, thick as boot leather, which currently grip the slime-slicked hide of a braided vine bridge.

I am a Wood-Borne. I am a creature of suspension.

Right now, I am utterly exposed. I am crossing a gap between two "islands"—immense knots of calcified root systems that dwarf the mountains of legends. The bridge I walk on is alive, a thick wrist of woven creepers that sways drunkenly in the warm, thin updrafts rising from the abyss.

If I look down, past my splayed, prehensile toes that cling to the vine-weave, there is nothing but rolling banks of white cloud unfathomably far below. If I were to fall, I would have time to die of thirst before I hit whatever bottom exists down there. So, I do not look down. My large eyes are fixed intently on the "shore" ahead: a titanic expanse of fluted, ancient bark, covered in weeping moss, that constitutes the next island.

The first thing you must understand about my world is that you cannot hear yourself think. The noise is a physical pressure against my eardrums. The air, thick with humidity and the cloying scent of giant waxy flowers, vibrates. A Bark-Speaker, clinging to the underside of the branch I'm approaching, is screaming. It is a chitinous insect the size of my torso, and its ribbed chest pumps out a piercing, mechanical shriek that rattles my teeth. Beneath that high-frequency assault is the low, thrumming bass of millions of Saw-Wing beetles lumbering through the air like drunken, iridescent plates.

I reach the end of the bridge. My long arms haul my lean frame onto the "solid" ground of the island. It feels like stepping onto a gargantuan sponge. The floor is a foot-thick carpet of vibrant green sphagnum moss growing over the dead, hardened root-bone of the world. It squelches wetly around my ankles with every step. Water is everywhere. It drips from the canopy leaves, which are broad enough to wrap a body in; it runs in rivulets down the grooves of the bark-mountains; it hangs in the air as a perpetual, warm mist. I am never dry. My skin is perpetually coated in a thin sheen of moisture and natural oils that help resist the rot.

I am on a harvesting run. I unclasp a carved bone tool from my belt and approach a thick vein bulging from the wooden flank of a skyscraper-sized trunk. The wood here is alive, unlike the calcified substrate beneath the moss. I swing the tool, chipping away the thick outer bark until the milky, sugary sap begins to ooze. It smells intensely sweet, cutting through the background odor of mildew. I hold a gourd beneath the flow. This sap, boiled down, is our energy. The roasted meat of the Saw-Wing beetles is our muscle.

A shadow passes over me. I freeze, pressing myself flat against the wet bark. An avian reptile, leathery wings stretching twenty feet across, glides silently through the canopy gap. Its rusted-brown skin blends with the damp wood. It is hunting primates in the mid-canopy, but it wouldn't turn down a lone Wood-Borne on an exposed root ridge. It passes. I breathe again, the thin air tasting metallic in my lungs.

I finish filling three gourds, stopper them, and strap them to my back. It's time to return to the cluster.

My home is hanging directly underneath me. I navigate to the edge of the root-cliff, where the moss gives way to a sheer drop into the white void. I find the anchor point—a massive, living tendril we have trained to grow downwards. Gripping it with hands and feet, I slide down into the twilight world beneath the main branch.

Here, glued like mud-dauber nests to the underside of the massive wooden artery, are our homes. They are globular baskets woven from living, pliable branches, anchored tight against the storms. I swing toward the entrance of my kin-group’s basket.

Inside, it is dim and smells of woodsmoke and crowded bodies. The noise of the outside world is muffled to a dull roar here. My kin look up as I enter. We don't speak immediately; we are tired from the constant gravity fight. My sister gestures a greeting—a quick flash of palms—and hands me a chunk of roasted beetle leg.

I sit near the opening of the basket, looking out at the terrifying expanse of green spires floating in the endless sky. I eat the smoky meat, my muscles trembling slightly from the climb. I am small. The world is impossibly huge, growing slowly around me, indifferent to my presence. But my grip is true. For today, that is enough.
