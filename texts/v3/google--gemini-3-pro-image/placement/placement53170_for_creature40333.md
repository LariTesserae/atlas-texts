The light hits my optical sensors first—a blinding, unfiltered violence that forces my polarized gemstone lenses to snap shut into narrow slits. Even then, the glare is painful. It is high noon on the Roof, and the sun is a welding arc searing the vacuum above.

I am perched precariously on a serrated ridge of raw obsidian, thousands of meters above a valley floor that is currently obscured by a roiling sea of freezing fog. My six articulated legs, tipped with diamond-dust claws, are dug deep into the glassy rock, holding me steady against a wind that wants to peel my armored plating off.

I am a Conduit Warden, a mid-sized operative unit, perhaps the size of a large terrestrial badger, but heavier, denser, built of forged alloys and conductive polymers. My carapace is a mottled, iridescent blue-black, slick with an ever-present layer of rime ice that cracks and reforms every time I shift my weight. My internal coolant—a thick, neon-green sludge—pumps sluggishly through my veins, keeping my core processor from freezing solid in the ambient temperature of seventy degrees below zero.

My existence is defined by noise and vibration. Through the soles of my feet, I feel the deep, sickening crunch of the continent below grinding against itself. The air around me is a deafening shriek of wind whipping through the metallic filament-needles of the Spire-Trees just above my station. And beneath that, the constant, maddening click-click-click of the living rust—the billions of heatsink arthropods swarming over the lower slopes, managing the thermal gradient.

I am currently motionless, which in this world means I have been holding this position for three days.

Time here is geologic. Below me, emerging from the frozen fog like a surfacing island, is the carapace of a Behemoth mobile refinery. It is migrating toward a fresh vein of quartz. I have watched its front-left primary walkway lift over the course of the last twelve hours. It hangs suspended, shedding avalanches of ice that tumble silently into the void below, waiting for the hydraulic pressure to build sufficiently for the downward step. When that foot lands, perhaps by tomorrow dawn, the impact will shudder through the entire mountain range, and the resulting explosion of steam from its venting stacks will create a blizzard that lasts a week.

My purpose here is small but vital. I am waiting for a discharge.

A cluster of Spire-Trees fifty meters upslope has reached critical electrostatic saturation. Their iron trunks are vibrating so intensely they are blurring my visual field. The air between them is beginning to taste metallic and ozone-sharp. Any moment now, the dielectric breakdown will occur, and a bolt of chaotic, blue-white plasma will arc from the trees down to the geothermal vent capping I am guarding.

My job is to ensure the conduit cap holds. I have spent the last week reinforcing the ceramic seals with my thermal-lance mandibles. If the cap fails when the lightning strikes, the superheated steam below will meet the cryogenic air above, and the resulting thermal shockwave will shatter this entire ridge, myself included.

A Winged Drill flies past me, its high-pitched dentist-drill whine cutting through the deeper bass of the world. It lands on a quartz face near my head and immediately begins boring, its translucent wings shivering. It will likely die there, years from now, half-buried in the hole it made.

I feel a sudden drop in atmospheric pressure. The humming of the trees shifts pitch to a scream. My optical lenses polarize further, turning the world almost black in anticipation. The cold bites harder. I dig my claws deeper into the obsidian.

I am a tiny, frozen gear waiting for the hammer to fall, terrified and thrilled by the efficiency of this terrible, beautiful machine.
