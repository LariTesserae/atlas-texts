The vibration is the first thing, always. Before sight, before the hot, metallic taste of the air, there is the shaking of the world.

I am crouched tight into a shallow depression on a basalt shelf, about midway up the western cliff face of the caldera. The rock against my chest and belly isn't just warm; it's thrumming. A deep, resonant pulse travels up through the stone, bypassing my useless ears entirely and rattling the marrow of my bones. It feels like the mountain is slowly grinding its own molars together deep beneath me.

My breath is short and shallow. It has to be. I am pulling the parched, overheated air through a thick filtration mask strapped tight across my lower face. It smells of scorched charcoal (the filter medium) and the faint, coppery tang of my own sweat. The mask itches intensely where grit has worked its way under the leather seal, but I do not scratch. To break the seal out here is to invite a lungful of crystalline dust and acidic vapor.

Through the thick, amber lenses of my volcanic glass goggles, the twilight world is reduced to monochrome shades of bruised iron, sulfur-yellow, and deep shadow.

I am one of the Silent Ones.

Ten yards to my left, pressed into a similar crevice, is my scavenging partner, Roul. I can’t see his eyes behind his goggles, but I can read the tension in the line of his shoulders beneath his cured leather haul-suit. He is watching the caldera floor below.

He lifts one gloved hand and makes a rapid series of gestures—a flick of the wrist for *wind shift*, two fingers hooked downward for *pressure drop*. Then he slaps the stone floor with his palm, three rhythmic beats. *Tap-tap-tap.* I feel the sharp percussion through the thin soles of my boots. It is our count-down.

Below us, the massive sonic machine is reaching a crescendo. I cannot hear the shrieking whistles or the deafening roar that I know define this place, but I can see the evidence of the sound pressure. The towering, rust-colored silicate pipes—the "forest" that chokes the bowl—are blurring at the edges. They are vibrating so intensely that they seem to lose solidity, shedding clouds of mineral dust that turn the air opaque.

We are waiting for the violence.

Suddenly, the deep thrumming beneath my ribs changes pitch, becoming sharper, more frantic. A major vent near the center of the caldera blows.

It is magnificent and terrifying. A geyser of superheated, heavier-than-air metallic vapor blasts upward. It strikes a cluster of the tallest purple organ-pipes. They don't burn; they shatter. The structural integrity fails under the sonic and thermal onslaught. Hundreds of feet of crystalline tubing explode outward in a shower of razor-sharp resin shrapnel that scythes across the caldera floor.

I see movement down there. One of the great armored arthropods—a younger one, perhaps only the size of an overturned cart—was too slow in clamping itself down. The blast wave catches it, flipping it onto its back. Before it can right itself, the hail of silicate shrapnel impacts its exposed underbelly. Even from this distance, through the hazy goggles, I see the spurt of iridescent vital fluids, boiling instantly as they hit the hot rock.

Roul looks at me. He signs one word: *Gift.*

We do not speak. We do not hear. We only feel the rhythm of the mountain’s terrible song, and we wait for the moments when it offers us sustenance. I grip my pry-bar, fashioned from a hardened shard of dead flora, and we rise from our shelter, leaning hard into the hot, howling wind, beginning the treacherous descent toward the kill.
