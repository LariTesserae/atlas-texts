The vibration reaches me first. It is not a sound, but a persistent, marrow-deep geological resonance that travels up through the welded soles of my boots, bypassing my ears entirely to rattle my ribcage. It is the groan of the mountain reacting to the parasitic bore-shafts creating a pressure differential deep within its molten gut.

I am standing on Gantry 7-Alpha of the Primary Extraction Tap.

I am high up, even by Tecton standards. The air here is exceptionally thin, a ghostly vapor that offers little sustenance. My barrel chest heaves against the restraint of my ceramic-plated torso armor, a slow, deep bellows action. The respirator grafted to my lower face is cool against my constantly sweating skin, a sleek piece of bio-integrated porcelain that clicks softly with every inhalation, scrubbing the sulfur and concentrating what meager oxygen exists in the steam plumes.

My name—or rather, my designation within the work-flow—is Kael-7, Senior Monitor of Pressure Gradients.

My skin, exposed at the forearms and neck, is the color of wet cement, dusted with a fine, glittering layer of silica ash that has embedded itself in my pores over forty cycles. I am thick with muscle, built not for speed, but for static endurance. To move fast here is to die. Haste brings immediate overheating and hypoxic blackout.

So, I stand perfectly still. I am a statue of ash and white ceramic, leaning against a railing that is almost too hot to touch, even through my insulated gloves.

Below me, a kilometer of vertical chaos drops away. Refinery towers, scabbed over with scaffolding and weeping condensation, hang over the abyss. Insulated pipes thick as redwood trunks weave between them like black pythons. Far, far below, the turbulent ocean of clouds churns, a white lid sealing off a world I have never seen and do not care about.

Above, through the polarized amber lenses of my goggles, the bruised violet sky is streaked with the oily exhaust of the geothermal vents. A large, translucent glider-creature drifts silently past on a thermal updraft, its wings shimmering with moisture. It ignores me; I ignore it. We are both just specialized biology clinging to a hot rock.

My attention is focused on the massive valve wheel in front of me. It is three meters across, forged from dull, heat-pocked alloy. A micro-fracture has been detected in the basalt casing of the borehole beneath us. The magma flow needs to be throttled back by 0.4% to prevent a catastrophic blowout.

I do not speak to my two subordinates standing ten feet away. Speech is exhausting. Instead, I lift my heavy boot and tap the metal grating three times in quick succession, followed by a long pause, then one solid stomp. *Prepare for incremental closure. Sequence initiated.*

They acknowledge with a single, synchronized nod. Their breathing huffs in unison with mine.

I reach out and grip the wheel. The metal radiates a heat that laughs at my insulation. I take a breath that fills my expansive lungs to capacity, holding it, letting the oxygen trickle into my blood. Then, with agonizing slowness, I begin to turn the wheel.

It is a supreme effort of controlled strength. I move it perhaps two degrees of rotation. I stop. I exhale a long plume of processed steam from my respirator vents. I wait for my heart rate, hammering against my ribs, to settle. The sweat on my forehead evaporates instantly, leaving a salty crust beneath my goggles.

We wait for the mountain to respond. The deep vibration under our feet shifts its pitch slightly, a subterranean sigh of relief. The pressure gauge needle flickers down a fraction of a millimeter.

I take another breath. I grip the wheel again. We have another twenty degrees to turn. It will take us the rest of this work-shift.

This is my existence. I am a slow-moving component in a geological machine, bleeding heat from a violent planet, suspended in the silent, suffocating violet haze between the magma and the void. I am Tecton. We endure.
