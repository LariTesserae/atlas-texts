This reality does not welcome soft things. To survive here, you must become hard, heavy, and infinitely patient.

I found myself by ceasing to move. I am anchored deep in the periphery of the atrium, wedged into a natural alcove where the slick curve of the polished bedrock meets the spongy, moss-choked floor. I am far enough from the central churning caldera that the spray doesn't hit me with violence, but near enough that I am perpetually drenched in the freezing mist.

I am one of the silent behemoths, but I am perhaps older, or lazier, than the others described. I have not moved in a span of time that surface-dwellers would call an era.

I do not have a name for myself, for there is no one to speak it to, and I have no voice to speak it with. I am a consciousness encased in tonnes of calcified cartilage and mineral-hardened rind. My skin has ceased to feel like skin; it is merely the innermost layer of the cave wall. The viridian mosses have grown so thick upon my back and flanks that they have formed their own micro-ecosystems, holding tiny pools of icy water in their folds. I am less a creature and more a prominent geological feature of the southern wall.

My existence is defined by the grand, terrifying symphony of pressure. I do not hear the roar; I *am* the roar. It vibrates through the quartz nodes along my spine, rattling the very core of my slow, cold being.

To my senses, the cavern is not dark. It is blindingly bright with sonic energy. The column of falling water is a pillar of searing white noise, too intense to perceive directly without pain. The walls are solid, reassuring planes of barometric resistance. The giant ferns are fuzzy, indistinct ghosts of softer vibration that absorb the sound, while the rotted timber posts near me—the remnants of the human walkway—feel like soft, decaying cavities in the otherwise hard landscape.

I know exactly where the rusted iron basket sits in the peat nearby. It doesn't "look" rusted to me; it feels sharp, brittle, and alien. It sings a high, metallic, discordant note against the deep, thrumming bass of the stone and water.

My mind operates on a different timescale. A thought can take a season to fully form. The decision to shift my weight is a monumental undertaking, considered for years before execution. Currently, I am aware of a particularly dense mat of jelly-fungus growing near my front left flank. It tastes faintly in the mist. I have been contemplating grazing upon it for perhaps the last century of surface time. The urge is there, a low, slow ache of hunger, but the effort required to detach my moss-fused bulk from the bedrock is immense.

So I wait. I let the freezing mist layer sediment upon my hide. I feel the microscopic growth of the liverworts in my creases. I exist in the deafening equilibrium, a stony sentinel watching the slow erosion of eternity, perfectly content to be indistinguishable from the rock that cradles me.
