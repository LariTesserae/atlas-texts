I exist in the intricate, dangerous spaces between the armor plates.

My world is a shifting landscape of jagged textures and ferrous tastes. To an observer floating in the greasy mist above, I would be nothing more than a pale, segmented smudge against the chaotic darkness of the moving terrain. I am one of the grazers, the armored slugs mentioned only in passing, but to me, this existence is total.

My home is the dorsal expanse of an elder Lithic-Gorger. It is a mobile continent of scabbed metal and weeping minerals, a territory I share with dozens of my kin. Beneath my gripping foot—a single, muscular suction pad that runs the length of my thumb-sized body—the ground is slick with the giant’s cold, acidic sweat. It tastes intensely of wet pennies and potent chemistry, a flavor that is the foundational bedrock of my life.

I move with glacial deliberation. Speed is unnecessary here; the food does not run. Currently, I am anchored to the curled lip of what was once a thick boiler plate, now cemented by the Gorger's biological mortar onto its left flank.

My purpose is hunger. The air, thick as cold velvet and tasting of iron filings, brings me scents of blooms ripening on the gigantic shell. I slide forward, my armored segments clicking softly together—a microscopic echo of the grander rattling of my host.

There it is. A rich, vibrant patch of corrosion has taken hold on a jutting shard of impacted slag. It is a beautiful, bright verdigris, textured like brittle sponge. I extend my head from beneath my own modest carapace. My mouthparts are a complex arrangement of file-like radulae, hard enough to etch glass.

I begin to feed.

*Rssshhk. Rssshhk. Rssshhk.*

The sensation is exquisite. The verdigris crumbles against my rasp, releasing a sharp, tangy rush of copper salts and oxidized trace metals. It is a culinary bouquet of decay. I scrape the patch down to the bare, grey slag beneath, ensuring the Gorger’s shell remains relatively free of deep-rooting mineral cancers that might eventually pierce its soft under-flesh. We are the cleaners, the symbiotic janitors of these walking slag-heaps. The giant ignores us utterly; we are beneath its notice, yet essential to its comfort.

Suddenly, my entire world shudders. The vibration is so profound it threatens to break my suction seal. I flatten myself tight against the boiler plate, riding out the tremor.

The Gorger has found a meal.

Below me, tones deep enough to be felt rather than heard begin to emanate from the behemoth's chest cavity. It is the sound of tectonic plates grinding together. The Gorger has pressed its terrible spade-head against a buried bulkhead of riveted steel.

The crunching begins. It is a deafening, screaming sound of metal shearing metal, of iron pyrite mandibles chewing through century-old girders. The violence of it shakes the verdigris dust from my back. The air around the Gorger's maw grows warm with friction, cutting through the perpetual chill of the sump.

I wait, clinging to my shifting patch of home, tasting the sour sweat and the metallic dust settling on my skin. The feast lasts for hours. When the crunching finally stops, it is replaced by the deep, wet internal churn of the Gorger's furnace-gut processing the toxins.

The giant lurches forward again, its dozens of legs finding purchase in the abrasive mud below. The movement induces a slow, rocking motion. A vent near my position opens with a wet gasp, releasing a fresh deluge of oily, iridescent waste fluid down the flank of the shell. The scent is overpowering—concentrated sulfur and heavy metal sludge. It flows past me, renewing the patina of the world.

The tremor settles. The cold returns. I detach myself from the clean spot on the boiler plate and begin the slow slide toward a promising yellow bloom of sulfurous fungi growing on a rusted gear-tooth near the spine. There is always more rust. The garden is always growing.
