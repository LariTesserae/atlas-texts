The world is a bright, horizontal sliver, cut down to bearable size by the narrow slits sawn into the bone mask strapped across my face.

I am crouched within the semi-circle of crude slate I stacked hours ago, knees pressed tight against my expanded chest, locked inside stiff, grease-heavy folds of hide. The wind passes silently overhead, ignoring this deep notch in the mountain, but the cold sinks into this depression like a heavy stone. It is a cold that seeks the marrow. Every breath is a deliberate labor; my massive ribcage heaves against the resistance of my clothing to pull in air that tastes of iron and ice, air so thin it feels like breathing vacuum.

My skin, where it is exposed around the bone goggles, feels tight and leathery, thickened by years of exposure to this searing, unfiltered star. I run a tongue coated in fatty violet paste over cracked lips.

Through the bone-slits, I watch the bowl weep. The sun is high now, its heat intense on the black rock, melting the night’s frost instantly. The silence is broken only by the crystalline *tink-tink-tink* of meltwater dripping into the deep, bruise-colored mosses that pad the ledges around me.

I am stillness. I have learned the patience of the granite shards I sit upon.

Across the void, perhaps three hundred yards away on a sheer rock face, the herd moves. They are shaggy, heavy-headed shapes, their wood-grain horns blending with the shattered geology. They navigate footings I could not dream of holding, grazing on the dripping vegetation. I am not watching them, not yet.

I am watching the shadow below them.

There is a pale flicker against the sun-bleached scree—a movement almost indistinguishable from shifting ice. The phantom cat is there. It flows over the unstable rock like spilled mercury, hunting the same herd I am. I respect it. It belongs here in a way I never will. I watch its approach, calculating the inevitable flush.

My hand, gloved in thick fur, rests on the polished volcanic glass tip of my heavy spear. The obsidian is colder than the air, sharp enough to sever a thought. When the cat makes its move, the herd will scatter. Some will come down, toward the lushness of the lower bowl, toward me.

I have perhaps four hours before the sun dips below the spires and this bowl becomes a tomb of ice again. I need the dense fat of the kill to survive the descent to the thicker air below. But before I shoulder the heavy, bloody pack and begin the treacherous climb down, I thumb the small, smooth piece of black glass tucked into my belt pouch. An offering for the violet moss, payment to the raw, brutal spirit of this high place for letting me breathe its killing air one more day.
