The roar is not something I hear; it is the fluid in which I am suspended. It vibrates in the marrow of my fiber-reinforced bones and rattles the multifaceted lenses of my eyes. To be alive here is to be perpetually shaken.

I am perched high in the canopy zone, where the light filtering from the root-ceiling is brightest, a humid, diffuse white glare that streams through the rising steam. I am currently integrated into a junction point of three major thoroughfares—massive, braided bridges of iron-hard vine and cartilage that sway gently over a two-mile drop into the green-black void.

I am of the "citizens," the root-and-muscle people. My skin is a pale, slick membrane over twisted bundles of organic fiber, tough as cured wood. I have no ears; the concept of hearing is obsolete in a world that screams this loudly. Instead, the soles of my splayed feet and the palms of my four-fingered hands are pressed flat against the textured surface of the bridge junction. Through these contact points, the entire city speaks to me in a complex language of tremors.

I feel the rhythmic, grinding thrum of the Slough-Giants miles below, a bass line that stabilizes the higher frequencies. I feel the sharp, staccato clicking of a thousand Weavers scuttling along the undersides of nearby pods, their tireless mandibles extruding foam that hardens instantly against the damp air. I feel the sudden, whip-crack recoil of a tension vine snapping somewhere in the middle distance, followed immediately by the frantic compensation vibrations of nearby structures shifting their weight.

My role here is Sentinel of the Stress Points.

While my kin are out pruning back the aggressive growth of the tuber-walls with bone machetes, or silently battling for nesting rights in the upper hollows, I remain still. My multifaceted eyes, which wrap around the sides of my skull, allow me to see in almost every direction simultaneously. The view is a dizzying, warped panorama of impossible scales. Massive, bloated city-pods hang like swollen fruit across gulfs of misty air so vast that the translucent predators gliding between them look like dust motes. The depth is terrifying, a vertical ocean of humid nothingness defined only by the colossal organic shapes floating within it.

I am waiting for a specific vibration. It is distinct from the noise of growth or predation. It is a shudder—a low-frequency dissonance that tastes like rot to my tactile sense.

There.

It travels up through the soles of my feet. A sickening, grinding slip. It’s coming from "Sector 7 Down-Root," a district of older, calcified cathedral-tubers hanging a half-mile across the chasm. One of the main anchor-roots is failing, eaten away by a parasitic fungus that the gardeners missed.

I do not speak. I act.

I disengage my hands from the bridge surface. My hooked claws, slick with the ambient moisture, extend from my forearms. With precise, practiced violence, I strike the anchor cable I am standing on. I hammer out a rapid, syncopated rhythm. It is a silent alarm sent singing down the tension lines of the city.

*Thump-thump-scrape-thump.*

The message travels instantly through the wet, taut structure: *Sector 7. Anchor failure. Imminent shearing.*

Almost immediately, the vibrations under my feet change. The background clicking of the Weavers intensifies into a frantic buzz as hundreds of them instantly divert toward the threatened sector, ready to bandage the wound with cartilage foam. I feel the heavier, deeper footfalls of a dozen fellow citizens swinging out of nearby pods, their bone blades ready to hack away weight before the structure tears itself apart.

My duty discharged, I sit back on my haunches, letting the deafening, unending roar of the wet universe wash over me once more, watching through my fractured vision as the city far across the void begins to swarm in a desperate attempt to heal itself.
