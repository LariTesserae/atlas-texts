The pressure is the first thing. It is not just gravity, but a crushing, atmospheric weight that holds me down against the slick, unfinished earth. I am lying half-submerged in a tepid depression, a shallow basin filled with fluid thicker than water, smelling of hot metal and fermentation.

The heat is absolute. It does not radiate from a source; it is the medium in which I exist. It permeates the thick, gelatinous membrane that serves as my skin, blurring the line between my internal fluids and the soup in which I wallow. To breathe is to actively pull this heavy, saturated vapor into spiracles along my flanks, a slow, laborious rhythm that yields just enough oxygen to sustain a torpid consciousness.

I am not one of the Giants, whose booming resonance vibrates through the mud beneath me, shaking my very segmentation. Nor am I part of the frantic, mindless cloud of winged jelly that whines incessantly overhead.

I am something in between; a scavenger of the mid-strata. My body is long, flattened, and segmented, a leathery tube of dark, mottled flesh that mimics the raw clay and bruised purples of the terrain. I possess no hardened shell like the slag-mites—my protection is my slickness, a ceaseless production of mucus that allows me to squeeze through the choking density of this world without tearing my skin on the sharp, cooling mineral shards.

I have eyes, but they are filmed over, capable only of distinguishing the deeper purple gloom from the faint, sickly glow of a nearby fungal tower. I rely instead on the dozens of short, bristled legs lining my underside, which taste the ground as I move, and sensitive heat-pits along what passes for my head.

Right now, I am wedged tight. Above me, the thick, sweating weight of a giant fleshy frond presses down. Below me, the raw, paste-like ground is vibrating. To my left, I feel the slow, agonizing slide of a segmented tube-creature, blind and indifferent, its mucus coating sliding against mine. We are jammed together, flesh against wet flesh, in a paralyzed ecstacy of crowding.

I am hungry, a dull, persistent ache in my gut, but the effort to move is immense. I smell something nearby—the rich, chemical scent of a slag-mite that has died and cracked open, its softer innards exposed to the heat. It is close, perhaps only a few body lengths away through the tangled root-mats.

I begin the arduous process of movement. My many legs ripple in sequence, gripping the slick mud. I have to ease my flattened body out from under the frond, pushing against the unyielding mass of the tube-creature. It takes an eternity to move an inch. The air is so thick it feels like pushing through soft wax. The high-pitched whine of the insects drills into my awareness, a persistent headache of sound.

Suddenly, the ground heaves violently. A few yards away, a massive shadow shifts in the twilight. A Giant is waking to feed.

I freeze, flattening myself deeper into the iridescent microbial slime of my pool. The vibration changes from a monotonous thrum to a catastrophic suction. The water around me recoils, drawn toward a sudden cavernous opening in the mud. I dig my bristles in, holding fast against the current. I feel the frantic scraping of thousands of slag-mites being torn from their grips and sucked into the void.

Then, with a wet, thunderous seal, the cavern closes. The water sloshes back, warm and agitated. The Giant settles. The boom resumes.

I remain motionless for a long time, letting the heat seep back into the spaces where fear momentarily chilled me. The scent of the dead slag-mite is gone, consumed in the catastrophe. It doesn't matter. There will be others. I remain wedged in the warm dark, breathing the heavy air, waiting in the eternal, suffocating twilight.
