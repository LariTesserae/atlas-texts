The pressure is a constant, icy hug, but it is the sound that defines existence here. It is not merely heard; it is felt in the marrow. It is a thick, vibrating soup of colossal machinery—the groan of tortured metal, the unending thrum of pumps the size of mountains, and the white-noise roar of rushing water that vibrates my very teeth.

I am clinging to the underside of a primary support girder, a piece of composite metal heavy enough to act as the spine of a continent. I am dozens of miles "down" from whatever surface might exist, and dozens of miles "up" from the theoretical bottom.

I am a creature of the intersection, living where the terrifying emptiness meets the brutal solidity of the structure. I am long and low, built somewhat like an oversized barracuda, but armored like a tank. My skin is not organic hide; it is a tessellation of interlocking, conductive plates, smooth as glass and colored a mottled, oily gunmetal grey that allows me to vanish against the girders. I do not swim with fins; I maneuver with jets of pressurized water ejected from vents along my flanks, mimicking the industrial intakes I live amongst.

Currently, I am motionless. My six ventral gripping pads—thick discs that combine suction with a weak, localized magnetic field—lock me securely to the cold flange of the I-beam. I am upside down, staring into the abyss.

To my left, about four hundred yards away, one of the great floodlights spears the water. The beam is so intense it seems solid, a highway of blinding white photons cutting through the unbelievably clear blue-green expanse. Dust motes—metallic flecks and bits of torn algal film—dance in the light like diamonds.

I stay just outside the cone of light, in the razor-sharp shadow cast by the girder I cling to. My eyes are immense, occupying the front third of my skull. They are adapted not for darkness, but for filtering agonizing brightness. They are polarized lenses, shielded by nictitating membranes that shimmer like oil slicks, allowing me to stare past the glare and pick out silhouettes against the illuminated void.

Below me, the Great Wall of the container drops away into hazy infinity. It is a vertical cliff face of bolted plates, punctuated by the slow, tectonic grinding of gears the size of city blocks.

A tremor shakes the girder I am attached to, distinct from the background vibration. A grazer is approaching. It comes into view slowly around the curve of a massive pipeline—a crustacean behemoth, its shell a hodgepodge of riveted boilerplate resembling a rusted utility vehicle walking on twelve hydraulic legs. It is deaf and dumb, focused only on scraping a patch of brownish chemosynthetic slime from a weld seam. It passes beneath me, agonizingly slow, its armored bulk grinding against the metal. I ignore it. My jaws, lined with serrated ridges of crystalline metal, could not crack that carapace.

I am waiting for the "drift."

The currents here are treacherous. Miles above, unseen vents blast heated water into the tank, creating turbulent rivers that flow through the static blue. Sometimes, these currents catch the nervous schools of chrome-fish that flash through the open water.

There.

My polarized vision picks up a disturbance at the edge of the distant floodlight beam. A school of chrome-fish, usually traveling in a tight, synchronized sphere of reflective scales, has been scattered by a sudden shift in an artificial current. One of them is tumbling, momentarily stunned, its mirror-bright side catching the light like a distress beacon.

Hunger overrides the paralyzing fear of the open water.

I release my magnetic grip. For a second, I just fall into the endless blue. Then, my flank jets fire. I accelerate with jarring speed, leaving the safety of the shadow and rocketing into the blinding highway of light. The noise seems louder out here, away from the dampening bulk of the wall.

The chrome-fish recovers its senses and sees me—a sleek, grey torpedo closing the distance. It darts left, jerky and reactive, but I have anticipated the angle. I slam into it, my armored snout stunning it again. My jaws snap shut, shearing through its mineralized scales with a sound like scissors cutting tin.

Momentum carries me further out, hundreds of yards from the nearest solid surface. This is the terrifying part. Suspended in the illuminated void, with nothing above, below, or around but miles of pressurized water and the crisscrossing beams of searchlights. The agoraphobia is primal and crushing.

I pivot quickly, firing my jets to drive me back toward the comforting mass of the Great Wall. I locate my girder, its familiar shadow a sanctuary. I slam back onto the cold metal, my magnetic pads locking with a reassuring *clack* that is lost in the endless roar.

Secure once more, I hold my prey against the beam and begin to feed, a small, armored speck of life clinging to the vast, indifference mechanism of the world.
