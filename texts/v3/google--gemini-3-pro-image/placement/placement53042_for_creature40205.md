The consciousness returns slowly, like thick sap warming.

Before there is sight, there is the sound. It is a vibration so deeply rooted it feels less like an external noise and more like the shuddering of my own core. *Thrum-thrum. Thrum-thrum.* A sub-bass heartbeat that travels up through the dense, woven floor on which I rest, resonating within the hollow cavities of my broad chest.

I am indoors, cradled within the bulbous hollow of a cathedral-root. The light is a faint, aqueous jade, filtering through a membrane window high above. My first conscious movement is a deep inhalation. The air is shockingly cold, saturated with moisture that tastes of iron, wet soil, and ozone.

I attempt to shift my weight, and the effort is immense. I am heavy, wonderfully, solidly heavy. As my upper torso rotates, a sharp *hiss-clack* breaks the silence. I look down. My four arms, thick as oak saplings, are resting on the root-floor. They are armored in plates of dull, oxidized brass, riveted directly into my dark, fissured hide. External hydraulic pistons, gleaming with oil and condensation, run along my joints. They are part of me. I flex a three-fingered hand, and the brass tendons sliding beneath my calloused skin feel as natural as bone.

I need heat. The cold is seeping through my thick husk.

I begin the slow process of standing. It is a monumental task of leverage and hydraulics. My pillar-stumps compress the spongy floor as I rise, the brass machinery groaning in protest against the chill. I stand perhaps seven feet tall, but I am nearly as wide, a dense anchor against gravity. I run a massive hand over my shoulder and feel the soft, damp velvet of emerald moss that has grown there during my slumber. A small, pale shelf fungus snaps off under my thumb; I feel nothing but the pressure.

I lumber toward the doorway, a natural fissure in the living wood widened and framed with grown arches. My footsteps are heavy, wet *squelches*. I step out into the plaza.

The world is twilight and mist. The air is colder here, incredibly damp. Condensation immediately beads on my brass respirator grill. The other living monoliths loom in the fog, their copper veins glowing faintly with the heat of the steam they carry. I see the industry breathing. A massive piston in an open-air engine house nearby slowly ascends—*ka-chunk*—venting a thick cloud of white steam that briefly warms my face.

Across the spongy plaza, I see another of my kind. They are stationary, kneeling before a complex junction of pipes hidden in a fold of root-bark. They do not turn to look at me. We do not need eyes to acknowledge one another. I gather breath in my massive lungs and emit a low, sustained vibration from my chest. It is below human hearing, a seismic tremor meant only for the ground and the bone.

The other stops their work for a moment, and I feel an answering vibration travel through the wet moss underfoot. A greeting. A confirmation of function.

I continue my slow, deliberate march toward the nearest docking port. My purpose is clear; it is written into the brass riveted to my soul. I reach the base of a towering tuber-building where thick copper pipes erupt from the bark. There is a manifold here, polished smooth by generations of use.

With the agonizing slowness of a glacier, I position myself. I align the heavy brass intake valves on my torso with the port on the wall. With a final, heavy *clunk*, I lock into place.

The connection is immediate. I feel the valve open within me. A rush of intensely hot, pressurized geothermal steam floods the copper veins beneath my armored hide. The cold is banished, replaced by a profound, aching warmth that loosens my stiff hydraulics. Simultaneously, a viscous, glowing amber fluid—the processed life-blood of the trees—begins to flow into my intake system. It tastes of ancient sugar and raw energy.

I close my multifaceted eyes beneath my heavy copper cowl. I am no longer just a being standing in a plaza. I am part of the circuit. The steam that warms the ferns warms me. The sap that feeds the bioluminescent tubes feeds me. The thrum of the pistons is my own heartbeat.

I will stand here for hours, motionless, steam curling off my mossy back, letting the ancient machine refill me so that I may, in turn, tend to it. I am the gardener, and I am the garden.
