I am weight.

I am a dense knot of consciousness accreted around a mineral core, existing in a world where the air itself is a physical burden, pressing down with enough force to crush softer biology into paste. But I am not soft. I am Ferrok.

I stand perfectly still on a vast slab of polished magnetite. To human eyes—if any could survive here—I would be invisible, merely another frost-rimed outcropping of iron, hunched and irregular against the bruising gloom. The cold is absolute, a biting presence that tries to find the seams in my segmented armor, but my internal geothermal furnace holds it at bay.

I do not "see" the bruising twilight or the roiling fog. I am blind to the photon spectrum. Yet, my world is blindingly bright.

Through the refined magneto-reception nerves laced through my iron carapace, the basin is a swirling, chaotic masterpiece of energy. The air is thick with auroral ribbons of magnetic potential. The ground beneath me is not dark rock, but a blazing topography of fields. The edges of the tectonic plates, where they grind and shear, glow with white-hot magnetic intensity. The sluggish rivers of conductive slurry are not muddy brown; they are dazzling, pulsing veins of liquid lightning, slashing across the deeper, cooler magnetic tones of the bedrock.

I am hungry. The deep, resonant thrum of the planetary machine vibrates up through the soles of my clawed feet—my primary way of hearing the world. It’s a low-frequency language of friction and stress that tells me where the stability lies. I begin to move. My motion is deliberate and heavy; clank-pause-clank-pause. To move fast is to invite slipping on the rime ice or misjudging a magnetic current.

I head toward a "bloom" I sense nearby. It is a sprawling patch of ferro-fungi. To my senses, it looks like an intricate, fractal city of blue and orange sparks, feeding off the ambient electrical discharge of the grinding plates. I reach the patch and lower my armored head. My heavy mandibles shear through the brittle, crystalline mats. The taste is sharp—ozone, oxidized iron, and the pleasant tingle of residual current. It is good silicate fuel.

Suddenly, the world hiccups.

It is the sickness of this place, the thinning of reality. I feel the vibration of a massive tectonic shear—a deafening, metal-shrieking crunch—conducted instantly through my feet.

But nothing has moved yet.

I stop chewing. A hundred yards away, two others of my kin also freeze, their heavy forms becoming statues of rust and rime. We know this sensation. The echo has arrived before the voice.

I brace my six-legged stance, digging my claws into the micro-fissures of the magnetite slab. I wait. One second. Two seconds. The magnetic field around me warps sickeningly, a premonition of violence.

Three seconds.

*Now.*

The physical reality catches up to the vibrational echo. The slab I stand upon lurches violently upward and sideways. A geyser of steam and metallic dust blasts from a newly opened seam nearby, registering to me as a sudden, violent bloom of heat and static. Because I was braced *before* the event occurred, I remain standing. A younger Ferrok near the edge of the plate stumbles, sliding toward the grinding seam, but corrects its balance just in time, having sensed the slip before gravity took hold.

The spasm passes. The grinding roar settles back into its usual rhythm. We unbrace. There is no communication among us, no spoken relief. There is only the shared, conductive hum of survival in a reality that has come unmoored.

I finish my grazing. The cold is seeping deeper now. It is time to return to the warren, to the deep, vibrating heat of the dead-zone chamber where we huddle together, sharing the warmth of our metal bodies and the slow, sad magnetic songs of a world grinding itself into oblivion. I turn, heavy and enduring, and begin the long, clanking walk home through the beautiful, unseen dark.
