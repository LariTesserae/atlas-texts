The vibration is constant.

It is not a sound, for I possess no ears to interpret the churning atmosphere. It is a physical assault, a ceaseless throttling of the surface to which I cling. I am anchored to an eighty-degree incline of slick, weeping basalt. My eight lower articulated limbs are splayed wide, the intricate pads at their tips secreting the rapid-set mucus that binds me to the glass. Through these pads, the world screams into my frame.

I feel the deep, tectonic groan of magma shifting miles beneath the mire, a rolling, nauseating bass note that never ceases. Overlaid on that is the sharp, violent shudder of the nearest steam vent, located perhaps three hundred body-lengths to my left. It pulses rhythmically—*push, push, push*—sending thermal shockwaves through the rock that register on my darkened sensor-clusters as blinding flashes of white heat in the endless gray. And everywhere, the chaotic, staccato tapping of a billion drops of cold water hammering the labyrinthine surfaces, a desperate, itchy static against my armored underbelly.

I am heavy. My weight is a calculation of tons, a massive assembly of vitrine plates that mirror the wet obsidian surrounding me. Condensation runs down my carapace in freezing sheets, indistinguishable from the rock itself. If I were to cease moving entirely, I would become merely another precarious stack in this shattered vista.

But I do not cease. I am currently engaged in a correction.

Held firmly in my two upper gripping appendages is a slab of razor-edged volcanic glass, roughly the size of the small, soft bipeds that sometimes imagine worlds like this. I have been holding this specific slab for twelve cycles of the dimming and brightening sky—perhaps four of their Earth years.

It does not fit.

I shift my weight, a process that takes hours. My hydraulic innards grind silently, a tremor lost in the geological din. I tilt the slab two degrees to the right. I press it against the gap in the spire I am mending. The vibrations of contact tell me the seating is imperfect. The resonance is jagged, discordant. It is chaos.

I retract. I wait. I taste the damp air with the chemoreceptors near my sensor-clusters, smelling sulfur and the coppery tang of wet iron. I feel the thermal gradient shift as a cloud bank thins slightly above, warming the black stone by a fraction of a degree.

A new vibration arrives, distinct from the geological background noise. It is sharp, rhythmic, and encoded. It travels through the bedrock, up the spire, and into my gripping pads.

*Tap-grind-tap-tap-thud.*

It is a designation signal from another architect, located on a ridge beyond the steam vent. They are reporting a collapse in Sector Delta-9. The chaos is fighting back.

I do not respond immediately. There is no urgency in this epoch. Instead, I lower my anterior segment toward a thick, bruised-purple fungal mat growing in a crevice. I press my absorption membrane against the cold, slick surface of the growth. Sluggishly, nutrients seep into my cold metabolism. It tastes of ancient ash and deep water.

Refueled, I turn my attention back to the slab. I rotate it another degree. I push.

This time, the seismic feedback is different. A dull, heavy *clunk* that resonates perfectly through the spire. The vibration is smooth, uniform. The piece has locked into place. The jagged edge is mitigated. Order has advanced by four feet.

I release the adhesive grip on the slab, allowing the mucus to crystallize. I begin the slow, arduous process of detaching my lower limbs from the incline, preparing to move to the next jagged imperfection. In the distance, barely perceptible through the thermal haze of the vents, I sense the cold, dead frequency of a matte-gray monolith. It stands silent, a blueprint we are infinitely slowly carving this explosion to match. I have all of time.
