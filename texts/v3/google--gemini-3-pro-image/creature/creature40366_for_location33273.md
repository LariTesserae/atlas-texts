The people of the Basin are creatures sculpted from wet clay and deafening noise. They are the inevitable biological extension of a landscape that refuses to settle.

To survive the oppressive heat and constant steam, they are nearly naked, clad only in loincloths and harnesses woven from tough, coarse fungal fibers that resist rot. Their skin is tough, thickened and calloused not just on their hands and feet, but over their entire bodies, seemingly cured by the sulfurous steam and constant coating of mineral-rich mud. They range in color from pale, sickly grayish-tan to deep, bruise-colored hues, mirroring the bacterial mats they harvest. Many smear their bodies and faces with the oily black slime found on the wetter rocks, perhaps as protection against insects, or perhaps simply because being clean is an impossible concept here.

Physically, they are squat and dense, possessing a low center of gravity necessary for navigating the slick, shifting mud alleys and climbing the precarious fungal-rope ladders of their cliff-hives. Their hands are broad, fingers thick and strong, tipped with nails worn down to nubs from clawing at rock and earth.

Their most defining features, however, are dictated by the noise. Their chests are enormous, barrel-sized ribcages expanded to house lungs capable of competing with screaming steam vents. Their throats are thick, ropy with permanent muscle tension, and their jaws are heavy. Their faces seem perpetually locked in a grimace of exertion, mouths open, veins bulging at the temples.

They do not speak; they project. Every utterance is a full-throated roar. There is no whisper, no murmur, no nuanced tone in the Basin. Conversation is a series of ballistic vocal impacts. Because hearing is dulled by the relentless geological and industrial din, they rely heavily on exaggerated physical communication. A greeting is a hard slap on the shoulder; agreement is a shoved forearm; attention is gained by grabbing a handful of flesh. They move with aggressive, jerky purpose, a society of people constantly bracing against a sonic gale.

Their eyes are large and pale, adapted to the eternal dimness, often narrowed to slits to keep out the stinging steam. There is a hardness in their gaze, a lack of surprise. Living in a "state of agonizing birth" that never resolves has stripped them of long-term expectation. They are intensely focused on the immediate: the next scrape of edible slime, the structural integrity of the mud-brick wall they are leaning against, the current shouting match.

They are hunters and harvesters of the grotesque. You see them clinging to the weeping cliff faces like lice, using bone scrapers to harvest sheets of purple leather-fungus. Teams of them cling to ropes, wrangling the giant, segmented millipedes with obsidian-tipped spears, hauling the thrashing segments back to the hives to be cooked over smoldering dung fires that choke the narrow alleys with acrid smoke. They wade thigh-deep into the warm mud pools, feeling with calloused feet for the bulbous, grinding amphibians, dragging them out by their slippery limbs.

Their tools and adornments are bone-white and glass-black. Necklaces of millipede mandibles clatter against their broad chests. Knives forged from volcanic glass are strapped to their thighs with strips of amphibian hide. The very structures they inhabit are reinforced with the massive, yellowing ribcages of subterranean beasts unearned from the shifting muck, giving their architecture a skeletal, macabre rigidity.

They are a people trapped in an eternal, deafening present. They are as relentless, as raw, and as unyielding as the screaming earth that birthed them.
