The inhabitants of the Copper Spires are not masters of this domain, but rather its most complex expressions. They are the Cuprum Aeterna, creatures of articulated metal, ceramic bone, and spun mineral fiber, as indigenous to this high-altitude desert as camels are to sand dunes.

They are tall and impossibly slender, echoing the verticality of their homes. A mature adult might stand nine feet tall but weigh less than a human, their bodies a lattice of hollow, sintered struts and porous plating designed to let the high winds pass through them rather than against them. They do not have skin in any terrestrial sense. Their exteriors are composed of interlocking plates of polished bronze and a smooth, non-conductive ceramic that looks like aged ivory. Like the spires themselves, they wear the environment; their joints are dusted with oxidized green, and their sun-facing surfaces hold the deep, reddish luster of heat-treated copper.

They have no faces, for there is little atmosphere to carry sound or scent. Where a head might be is a tapered cowl of smooth, conductive metal that serves as a sensory array. Deeply recessed within this cowl are multi-faceted lenses of polished obsidian, capable of seeing across vast distances not just in the amber spectrum of visible light, but into the infrared. To them, the world is a swirling map of heat signatures: the blazing plumes of geothermal vents, the cool shadows of a canyon overhang, and the warm, pulsating cores of their kin.

Their primary sense, however, is resonance. The bottoms of their four multi-jointed feet are broad, padded with a dense metalloid weave highly sensitive to vibration. In a world where high-frequency sound dies instantly in the thin air, they communicate through the ground. They "speak" by drumming complex, subtle rhythms onto the metal flooring of the spires, sending coded tremors that can be felt through the soles of another's feet miles away along connected girders. A gathering of the Cuprum is silent to the ear, but a cacophony of thrumming information to the touch. They feel the sub-bass hum of the planetary energy taps as a constant background comfort, a heartbeat they have known since birth.

They do not eat or drink. The concept of thirst is alien to them. Instead, they are beings of pure energy maintenance. At the center of their chests, protected by a cage of bronze ribs, pulses a visible core of trapped electrostatic plasma—their life animus. When this core dims, they make slow, deliberate pilgrimages to the exterior conductor arrays. There, they anchor themselves with magnetic grips and unfold conductive vanes from their shoulders like stylized wings, basking in the ambient static charge of the upper atmosphere until their cores glow bright gold once more. For trace minerals needed to repair their ceramic plating, they possess specialized intakes on their palms that can dissolve and absorb the metallic dust that coats every surface.

Their movement is a study in agonizing patience. They operate on the same geological timescale as the machinery they inhabit. To observe one crossing a bridge is like watching the hour hand of a clock; you look away, look back, and realize it has shifted position. They do not run; they flow, their dozens of articulated joints moving in a slow, clicking fluid sequence. This slowness is a survival mechanism, conserving precious energy in a resource-scarce environment and preventing sudden movements that might send them tumbling into the crushing depths below the clouds.

Their society is one of silent custodianship. They are few in number, solitary figures often separated by miles of vertical space. Their entire existence is dedicated to the "Great Alignment"—the endless, infinitesimal maintenance of the spires. They spend centuries slowly tightening a single massive tension bolt that holds two arcologies together, or meticulously clearing dust from a geothermal vent's intake grill with small, specialized brushes integrated into their forelimbs. They do not build new structures; they merely preserve the ancient, calcified corpse of the industrial world they emerged from, tending to the machine-mountain as if it were a sleeping god.

They are unemotional, logical, and deeply connected to the physical reality of their home. To touch a Cuprum is to touch warm, dry metal that hums with contained voltage. They are the ghosts in the machine made manifest, an elegant, anhydrous species that has forgotten the world below the clouds, existing solely in the amber light and the deep, resonating hum of the heights.
