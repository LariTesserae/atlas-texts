The beings that thrive in this suffocating warren are a direct reflection of its immense pressure and ancient confinement. They are humans, but humans sculpted by generations of crushing geology and the ceaseless friction of proximity.

They are physically dense people, compact of build and sturdy of limb. There is no room for lanky frames here; height is a liability in hallways where the smoke pools at five feet. Most adults carry a permanent, habitual stoop, their spines curved to match the sagging timber beams that loom just above their heads. Their movements are distinct, a shuffling, sliding gait developed to navigate slick clay floors and shoulder sideways through narrow gaps without breaking stride. They do not walk through space; they negotiate it.

Their connection to the structure is literal and constant. Their hands are never still, and they are remarkable tools—broad, heavily calloused, and stained a permanent grayish-brown from the mud they constantly patch into crumbling walls. Their fingernails are thick and short, worn down by scratching at clay and gripping rough wood. You will rarely see a clean patch of skin; they are coated in the environment itself—a layered patina of dried sweat, lamp soot, clay dust, and the grease of animal wool. Their complexions underneath the grime are pallid, starved of sunlight, resembling the pale belly of a fish or the roots of plants found deep in soil.

Their sensory world is vastly different from one under an open sky. Their eyes are often narrowed, rheumy and red-rimmed from the perpetual sting of acrid tallow smoke, yet they possess an uncanny ability to read depth in the flickering, deceptive orange shadows. They navigate as much by touch and smell as by sight, their hands running along the familiar, polished contours of walls in the darker stretches. Their hearing is highly selective; they have learned to unconsciously filter out the deafening roar of the hive's baseline noise—the bleating, the grinding, the creaking—to hone in on a specific voice shouting three levels up or the warning crack of an overstressed support beam.

They are clothed in the heavy textures of their world. They wear thick, felted wool from the subterranean sheep, dyed in muted earth tones or left natural and gray. These garments are stiff with age and accumulated grease, patched endlessly with scraps of leather or woven reed matting. They smell intensely of their surroundings: the wet, mineral scent of clay, the rancid tallow they burn, and the warm, ammoniac reek of the livestock they sleep beside.

There is no concept of personal space among them. To live here is to be constantly touching another living thing. They cluster together for warmth and social cohesion, sleeping in heaps amidst the goats and chickens. Children are carried strapped to chests or backs almost constantly until they are old enough to climb the treacherous ladders themselves, learning early to cling like barnacles to their mothers as they squeeze through the crowded shafts.

They are a loud, industrious, and profoundly tactile people. Their conversations are shouted rapidly over the din, accompanied by vigorous gestures necessary in the dim light. They are constantly working—hauling wet mud in baskets, pounding grain in stone mortars that vibrate the floorboards, skinning rats for stew, or weaving baskets from brittle reeds. They are not crushed by the environment; they are the engine that keeps it from collapsing entirely, their collective body heat warming the hive, their breath wetting the walls, their restless energy the only thing keeping the ancient clay tomb alive.
