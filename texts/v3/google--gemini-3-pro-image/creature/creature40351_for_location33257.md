The beings that claim this freezing, dripping expanse are not masters of their domain, but gardeners within it. They are creatures born of cold water and slow accretion, an intelligent extension of the fungal and coral architecture they inhabit.

At first glance, they are nearly invisible against the backdrop of their own city. They are bipedal but move with a hunched, fluid grace, their bodies lean and elongated to navigate the verticality of the spiraling ramps and towering spires. Their skin is their camouflage and their primary adaptation; it is a pale, nacreous grey, textureless and slick with constant moisture, resembling the very film that coats the newly formed obsidian shelves.

Upon closer inspection, their connection to the environment becomes visceral. Their shoulders, backs, and the outer ridges of their long limbs are harrowed and armored with the same bone-white, calcified coral secretions that form the city’s ribs. These natural plates are constantly cracking and reforming as they grow, mimicking the sharp, crystalline renewal of their buildings. From the seams of these natural armor plates, fine filaments of violet and deep-green moss sprout, softening their outlines and aiding in the absorption of the pervasive mist. They do not just live within the ecosystem; they carry it upon their bodies.

Their faces are lean and angular, dominated by eyes that are immense, dark, and lidless. These eyes are multifaceted orbs designed to capture the dim, shifting hues of bioluminescence, reflecting the cerulean pools and the distant fungal chandeliers with a glassy sheen. They have no external noses, only slit-like vents under their jawlines that filter the oxygen from the heavy, saturated air. Their mouths are small, lipless fissures, housing grinding plates suited for scraping the nutrient-dense mosses from rock faces and crushing the softer, edible fungi that grow unchecked.

Their hands and feet are webbed with tough, translucent membranes, splayed wide to grip the slick surfaces. The pads of their digits are spongy and porous, allowing them to adhere to the wet rock through suction and friction. They move with a deliberate, shivering slowness, never rushing, their motions absorbed by the silence. To watch one climb a crystalline spire is to watch a shadow detach itself from the rock face and glide upward against the flow of cascading water.

They are the architects, or perhaps the tenders, of this Grown City. In this hushed world, their labor is silent. They carry tools fashioned from the hardest, oldest cores of the calcified fungus—scrapers and curved hooks. With these, they do not build, but rather guide. They can be seen hovering over newly formed edges where mineral water drips rapidly, using their own saliva—a thick, binding enzyme—to direct the flow, dictating where the new layers of silica and calcium will harden instantly. They prune back the aggressive growth of the structural fungi where it threatens to choke a transit path, and they delicately harvest the glowing spores falling from the ceiling, collecting them in woven pouches made of root-fiber to be used in seeding new light sources closer to their hives.

They are a communal people, driven together by the oppressive cold. In the bulbous, hive-like structures bridging the chasms, they gather in dense clusters, sharing body heat. Their communication is nearly silent, a complex language of touches, subtle shifts in body posture, and the controlled flashing of faint bioluminescent patches located on their chests and palms—a soft, pulsing mimicry of the light that surrounds them. They are the silent watchers in the mist, the slow tenders of the stone garden, existing in a perpetual state of cold, wet symbiosis with the colossal cavern that bore them.
