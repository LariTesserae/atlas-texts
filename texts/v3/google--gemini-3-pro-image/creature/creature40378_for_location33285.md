They are the Anchors, the Weighers, the silent architects of the endless floor.

At first glance, amidst the blinding glare and the water-thickened air, they are indistinguishable from the architecture they occupy. You might mistake one for a slumped buttress of ochre rock, or a forgotten pile of rough-hewn gray slabs draped in aggressively verdant moss. They are colossal, their scale commensurate with the monolithic ziggurats they inhabit, possessing bodies broad and squat, built for enduring an infinite, crushing gravity that does not exist here.

Their hides are rough, incredibly thick, and deeply fissured, mirroring the porous texture of the sweating stone beneath them. The skin tones are the same sweaty reds, mineral-stained greys, and muted yellows of the terraces. Moisture perpetually beads on their vast backs and shoulders, running in rivulets down deep channels in their flesh to pool around their massive, indistinct limbs. The violently lush vegetation does not spare them; thick vines root in the crevices of their armored hide, and velvety moss patches carpet their heads and shoulders, making them seem like geological features rather than biological entities.

They have faces, though they are primitive and blunt, appearing more like unfinished sculptures than evolved features. Heavy brows of cartilage overhang deep-set eyes that are milky and rheumy, protected by thick, translucent nictitating membranes against the searing white-gold light. They rarely blink. They have no need to. Below the eyes, their faces are featureless expanses of wet, leathery skin; they possess no mouths, for in a world of eternal stasis where even the fish need not eat, they have no need to consume.

They are profoundly sedentary. In this world without time, haste is a concept that has never been invented. They sit hunched on the edges of the great terraces, or reclined against the base of the unfinished towers, their weight pressing down on the wet flagstones. When they do move, it is with agonizing slowness, a ponderous shifting of tonnage that requires pushing against the palpable jelly of the atmosphere. A single adjustment of a massive leg might take hours, were there hours to count. They do not walk the endless expanse; they occupy islands of it.

Like everything else in this saturated reality, they are mute. They have no vocal cords, no breath to waste on sound. Yet, they are not disconnected. They communicate through the medium that defines their world: the wet stone. They send low-frequency vibrations—tectonic thrums too deep for the human ear to hear as sound, but powerful enough to be felt in the marrow—rippling through the interlocking terraces. A shift in weight by an Anchor on a distant ziggurat is felt by another miles away across the flat, hazy sea of stone. It is a language of pressure and presence, a silent communion acknowledging that they are here, holding down the floor of a bottomless world, forever squinting into the blinding, motionless noon.
