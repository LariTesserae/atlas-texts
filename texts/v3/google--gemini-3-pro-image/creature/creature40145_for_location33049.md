This environment is sterile of biology, yet it is not uninhabited. It has an immune system.

Living here are the Wardens of the mechanism.

They are not creatures of flesh, for nothing organic could thrive in this deafening, churning deluge of pure force. They are direct extensions of the architecture itself, mobile components detached from the cyclopean walls. They are massive constructs, towering beings hewn from the same creamy, blinding limestone that forms the piers. Their forms are blocky and utilitarian—broad shoulders like lintels, limbs like pillars, jointed without mortar using precision-cut friction hinges that allow movement only through immense application of torque.

Heavily armored in plates of dull, reddish-orange bronze hammered directly onto their stone chassis, they sink to the very bottom of the chamber. Their weight is incomprehensible; they are dense enough to walk steadfastly against the engineered violence of the hydraulic currents that would tear a biological entity apart.

They have no faces, only blank slabs of flawless stone where a head should be, perhaps inset with a simple, heavy bronze grate. They do not need eyes; they navigate the machine by feeling the vibrations in the water, sensing the rhythm of the gears and the flow of the conduits through the soles of their titanic feet.

Their purpose is singular: maintenance.

In this colossal engine room, they are the grease monkeys and the cleaners. You might see one slowly, agonizingly slowly, climbing the side of a massive pier, its bronze claws finding purchase in the microscopically tight seams of the masonry. It reaches a spot where one of those intense shafts of sunlight hits the wall—the only places where the bright green slick of pioneer algae has begun to grow. With a scraping sound that adds a new layer of shrieking friction to the deafening roar of the room, the Warden uses a chisel-like appendage to meticulously scrape the stone clean, eradicating the biological contaminant and restoring the pristine sharpness of the builders' work.

Another might be positioned near the central churning gears. It stands immobile for hours, a statue amidst the chaos, until a slight irregularity in the tectonic grinding of the machinery occurs. Then, it moves, stepping into the current, bracing its bronze shoulder against a ten-story gear to nudge it back into perfect alignment, a task requiring force equal to the water itself.

They are silent beings in a loud world. They make no vocalizations. The only sounds they contribute are the seismic thuds of their footsteps on the chamber floor and the screech of metal on stone as they work. They are utterly indifferent to an observer. They are programmed only to ensure the engine runs for eternity, tending the bronze and stone garden in the warm, illuminated deep. They are the ghosts in the machine, made solid and enduring as mountains.
