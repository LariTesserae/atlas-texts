The beings who claim this vertical city are bounded by the immense constraints of their world; they are creatures hewn by gravity, cold water, and razor stone. They are not merely inhabitants of the gorge; they are its kinetic element, moving where nothing else dares.

Physically, they are a compact people, built not for speed on flat ground, but for torque and tension on a vertical face. Their skeletal structure is dense, their musculature thick and foreshortened, particularly in the shoulders, back, and forearms. They possess a low center of gravity and a simian flexibility in their hips, allowing them to splay their bodies against the cliff face, finding purchase on minute ledges.

Their most defining features, necessitated by the glassy sharpness of the basalt, are their hands and feet. They are disproportionately large, heavy with bone and sinew. The skin on their palms and soles is not merely calloused; it has thickened into a permanent, textured hide, resistant to cuts and capable of gripping slick, weeping rock with uncanny friction. Their finger- and toenails are thick, dark ridges of keratin, shaped almost like chisels, which they use to jam into narrow fissures or chip away small obstructions.

Their skin, where not covered by protective gear, is pale and deeply pores, seemingly adapted to handle perpetual saturation without macerating. Their faces are broad and weathered, eyes set deep beneath heavy, protruding brow ridges that act as natural visors against the constant downward drift of mist and spray. They rarely look fully dry; a sheen of moisture is their natural state.

Their clothing is a testament to their environment, a pragmatic armor against the sharp cold and sharper rocks. They wear layered tunics and breechcloths woven from the tough, fibrous inner bark of the stunted ironwood trees, treated with oils rendered from the fungi they cultivate to make them semi-waterproof. Over this base layer, they strap thick pads of cured, textured leather—likely harvested from creatures that live beyond the gorge or traded for—covering their knees, elbows, and shins to protect against the constant grinding contact with the abrasive stone. They wear no trailing garments that could snag on a jagged outcrop or catch in the wind.

In an environment defined by deafening noise—the wind, the water, the tectonic groan—vocal communication is secondary. They possess deep, resonant voices, capable of projecting low-frequency sounds that cut through the higher-pitched hiss of the waterfalls, used only for short, barking commands or warnings. For complex communication, they rely on an elaborate visual language of broad, exaggerated gestures and stances, readable across the misty chasms by others clinging to distant terraces. Their culture is one of intense visual awareness; they read the shift of a companion’s weight on a rope bridge or the subtle vibration of a loose rock face instantly.

Their daily existence is an endless exercise in vertical logistics. They are master riggers and haulers. Both men and women move with a deliberate, fluid grace on the terrifying staircases, often carrying immense loads of quarried basalt or harvested moss strapped to their backs in woven frames. They traverse the sickeningly swaying vine bridges with a nonchalant, rolling gait, absorbing the oscillations in their powerful legs, heedless of the void beneath them.

They are a people of immense patience and brute perseverance. Just as they slowly chip away at the living rock with bronze and stone to make their homes, they approach life with a grinding fortitude. They do not fear the shuddering of the earth; the low-frequency tectonic shrieks that unnerve rare visitors are the background hum of their existence, the heartbeat of the mother-stone that shelters them. They are as hard, as wet, and as tenacious as the moss clinging to the spray-drenched cliffs.
