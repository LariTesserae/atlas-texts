They are the Cenoa, a people literally shaped by the pressure, the cold, and the wet stone that surrounds them. They do not merely inhabit this high-altitude hive; they are the living, breathing extension of its geology.

Physically, the Cenoa are built for endurance and stability rather than speed. They are generally short of stature, with stocky, compressed frames and a low center of gravity—an evolutionary necessity for a life spent navigating slick, unguarded precipices. Their chests are remarkably deep and broad, housing cavernous lungs adapted to wring sufficient oxygen from the thin, moisture-laden atmosphere. Their limbs are thick with dense muscle, their hands and feet disproportionately large, broad, and calloused, possessing a grip like textured rubber on wet slate.

Their skin is thick and cool to the touch, pale and tinged with the grey-green undertones of the surrounding rock and lichen. It has a unique, slightly porous quality, seemingly impervious to the constant dampness, never wrinkling or softening from exposure, but rather toughening like cured hide. Their hair is universally dark, coarse, and thick, usually worn in tight, waxed braids wrapped close to the skull to prevent it from becoming waterlogged; even so, beads of mist cling to their hair like jewels.

Their eyes are their most striking feature. Adapted to the perpetual diffuse twilight, their pupils are enormous, dominating the iris. They hold a reflective, almost feline quality, catching the scarce grey light. When they look at you, their gaze is unblinking and incredibly patient, serene pools of dark water reflecting the clouds.

They clothe themselves in the only materials the plateau provides in abundance: stone, water-hardened leather from subterranean creatures, and thick, oily wool from hardy beasts kept on lower terraces. They wear countless layers of felted wool, dyed in the muted earth tones of their home—deep rust reds from iron oxide, moss greens, and slate greys. Over these layers, they wear heavy cloaks treated with natural oils and waxes, making the garments hydrophobic; water beads on their shoulders and runs off them just as it runs off the canyon walls. There are no metal clasps; clothes are secured with toggles carved from blackened bone or polished river stones.

The Cenoa move with a deliberate, mesmerizing slowness. To rush here is to die. Every step on a moss-slicked stair or bridge is calculated, a silent compact with gravity. They flow over the stone rather than walk upon it. This deliberate pace contributes to the prevailing silence of the city. They rarely speak above a low murmur; their language is soft, resonant, and vowel-heavy, designed to carry short distances through the damp air without echoing harshly. A shouted word here feels like an act of violence.

Their culture is one of subtraction and maintenance. Their primary tool is the stone chisel, made of harder rock brought up from the deepest chasms. Their lives are dedicated to the slow, endless work of carving their expanding hive, following the natural fissures of the rock, and maintaining the intricate hydrological systems. They do not fight the water; they curate its flow. They spend hours clearing moss from drainage grooves with bone scrapers, ensuring the weeping walls drain into cisterns rather than flooding dwellings. They are master weavers, creating the heavy wool barriers that serve as doors and windows, blocking the wind while allowing the stone to breathe.

They are a people of profound quietude, seemingly unaffected by the bone-deep cold. They hold a deep reverence for the water that both sustains and threatens to erode their world. When they stand still on a terrace, wrapped in their grey wool cloaks, they become nearly invisible against the architecture, just another rain-slicked formation of rock waiting patiently in the mist.
