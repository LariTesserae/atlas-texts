This is a kingdom of the exoskeletal, a sprawling necropolis inhabited solely by the eaters of decay. The things that live here have inherited a world sterilized of soft biology; they are the hardened survivors who found paradise in a chemical sauna.

The primary residents are the Blattodea gargantua—the immense cockroaches. They are the undisputed masters of the vertical surfaces. In the murky twilight, they do not look like individual insects, but rather a shifting, sentient texture coating the corroded metal. Their bodies, the size of hands, are varnished in the same viscous oils that drip from the pipes, making them gleam with an iridescent sheen in the dim light. They are profoundly hydric, thriving in the clinging humidity. They congregate in thick, layered mats around the weeping joints of steam pipes, their long, whip-like antennae forming a restless, hazy forest that tastes the saturated air. Their movement is a ceaseless, rustling current, a flowing mosaic of chitin plates sliding over chitin plates. When they move across the corrugated roofing, the sound is like dry leaves behaving like water, a pervasive, sibilant whisper that underlines the deep thrum of the subterranean pumps.

Patrolling the horizontal realm—the rusted gratings and spalled concrete floors—are the armored hunters. These are massive carabid beetles, fused into shapes of industrial efficiency. Their carapaces are matte black, scarred and pitted like the iron they traverse. They are heavy creatures; their hooked feet clack audibly against the metal catwalks as they hunt. They are the apex predators of this closed loop, stalking the shadows for the sickly, the slow, or the newly molted among the roach-mats. When they catch prey, the crunch of chitin is a sharp, sudden punctuation in the humid silence.

Far below the catwalks, in the lightless sumps and oily drainage canals, lives the blind engine of this ecosystem. The warm, toxic sludge, opaque with metallic salts and organic runoff, is thickened further by the churning of billions of pale, segmented larvae. These are the maggots of the upper world's giants, colossal grubs the thickness of a wrist. They possess no eyes, only ringed mouths that endlessly filter the lukewarm, chemical broth. They writhe in knots just beneath the oily surface, a pale, churning porridge of soft tissue that consumes the deepest filth of the basin, transforming the waste of an idle machine into the fuel for the crawling millions above.

There are no birds to eat them, no mammals to compete with them. There is only the heat, the wet metal, and the endless cycle of eating and being eaten. They are the blood cells in the veins of a dead giant.
