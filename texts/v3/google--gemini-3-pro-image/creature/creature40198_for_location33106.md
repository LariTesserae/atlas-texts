They are few, and they are seldom seen all at once. Like the architecture they inhabit, their presence is sparse across the vast expanse of the chasm, revealed only by slow movement against the static backdrop of black rock and rusted metal.

They are giants, hulking figures that seem less born of flesh and more forged from the very elements of the canyon. Their stature is necessary to operate the oversized controls of the geothermal valves and to haul the heavy gauge chains that secure the suspension bridges. If they were once human, generations of existence in this deafening, dripping fissure have warped them into something functionally distinct.

Their bodies are encased in what appears to be a permanent, integrated armor. It is a crude fusion of repurposed industrial salvage and thick, calloused hide. Plates of pitted iron, scavenged from the very structure around them, are bolted directly onto heavy leather aprons and pauldrons that never seem to come off. Over time, the constant dampness has caused these coverings to rust into a deep, bruised orange, streaked with mineral deposits from the incessant dripping.

Like everything else in the canyon, they are covered in life. The aggressive mosses have found purchase in the seams of their armor and the deep folds of their gear, turning them into walking patches of the vertical garden. Electric-green fuzz carpets their heavy shoulders, and small, stubborn ferns sprout from the joints of their knee-braces. They do not remove this growth; it serves as additional insulation against the pervasive cold and a camouflage against the lichen-dusted basalt.

Their movements are ponderous and heavily deliberate, perfectly synchronized with the geological patience of the machinery they tend. They do not run. They lumber along the groaning catwalks, their footfalls heavy metallic clanks that are entirely swallowed by the thrum of the subsurface pumps. When they work—perhaps turning a massive valve wheel that takes three of them to budge—they apply pressure slowly, over the course of minutes, waiting for the hydraulic assist to catch up.

They are utterly silent beings in a deafening world. They have no need for speech; the roar of the steam vents and the echo of the crushers would render it useless. Instead, they communicate through the conduction of vibration along the metal railings and floor grates, tapping out complex rhythms with heavy wrenches, feeling the message vibrate up through the soles of their thick boots. Their "faces" are rarely visible, obscured by heavy, brass-framed sensory helmets blocked with thick, condensation-fogged glass. Perhaps they are deaf, their ears long since atrophied by the noise, relying entirely on the seismic throbbing of the canyon to orient themselves.

They exist in a state of wet, cold indifference to the chaotic biology around them. The six-legged pipe-scuttlers race over their boots unnoticed; the oily birds roost on the cranes they operate. These giants are merely the largest, slowest moving parts of the machine, the custodians of the steam, tending the deep heat of a broken earth with plodding, rusted devotion.
