They are the Cultivated, and they are difficult to distinguish from the garden that entrained them.

At first glance, amidst the screaming yellow-green radiance and the confusing depths of overlapping leaves, the cavern appears devoid of sentient life. Only the pale, sliding slugs seem to inhabit this sweltering hothouse. But if you remain still, breathing the soupy air until the sweat on your own skin matches the perpetual slickness of the surroundings, movement begins to detach itself from the background.

They are humanoid only in the vaguest sense—a bipedal schema warped and reclaimed by botany. They are tall and incredibly gaunt, stretched thin by the heat and the low gravity of the deep earth. Their skin is not flesh as we know it; it is a pale, translucent membrane, somewhere between wet parchment and the skin of a boiled fruit. It constantly weeps moisture, a necessary adaptation to the supersaturated air, making them shimmer with a oily sheen in the relentless light.

In this blinding environment, sight is redundant. Their eyes are mere vestigial thumbprints on smooth, elongated faces, clouded over with thick, fibrous cataracts that filter the agonizing brightness into manageable gradients of gold and sickly green. Instead of seeing, they feel the heat signatures and the subtle vibrations of growth through sensitive, root-like cilia that fringe their jaws and elongated fingers.

They wear nothing, for the heat is too great, yet they are clothed. Mosses and small, glowing epiphytes have taken root in the folds of their damp skin. Tendrils of parasitic vines snake around their limbs, tapping into their bloodstream, not killing them, but joining them. They are walking trellises, mobile ecosystems that carry the cavern’s biology on their backs.

Their movements are excruciatingly slow. In this oppressive sauna, energy is precious. They drift through the choked pathways with a languid, liquid grace, their splayed, spongy feet making no sound on the root-floor. They do not speak. The silence of the cavern remains unbroken by human vocal cords. Their communication is chemical—a release of spores and pheromones that convey complex emotional states and instructions related to the care of the Garden.

They are the tenders of this fierce equilibrium. You might see one slowly, painstakingly use a hardened fingernail to prune back a monstrously fast-growing creeper that threatens to choke a "pathway," only to immediately graft the cutting onto another surface to ensure no biomass is lost. They are constantly curating the chaos, ensuring the furious growth and furious decay remain locked in their perfect, static dance.

But they are not just the gardeners; they are the crop.

The "abandoned structures," the living domes and fused-branch shelters, are not houses they built and left behind. They are the final stage of their lifecycle.

When a member of the Cultivated grows too old, too heavy with its own internal ecosystem, or too weary of the endless heat, it instinctively seeks out a nexus of structural roots near the swelling walls. It sits, folding its elongated limbs into a meditative posture. Slowly, over cycles of timeless growth, the cavern reclaims it.

The roots of the floor rise up to embrace its legs. The hanging curtains of glowing moss descend to drape over its shoulders. The creature’s metabolism slows to match the geologic thrum of the cave itself. Its individual consciousness dissolves into the collective bio-electric hum of the root network. Its skin hardens into sap-wood, its bones fuse into structural supports, and its flesh becomes the rich, living soil for new generations of glowing foliage.

The "built" things are their ancestors, petrified in living wood, eternally watching over the sweating, silent descendants who slowly tend the choked womb until it is their turn to become part of the architecture.
