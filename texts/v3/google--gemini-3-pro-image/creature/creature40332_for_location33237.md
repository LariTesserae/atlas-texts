The people of the Sliver are creatures sculpted by dampness, verticality, and the ever-present threat of a lacerating edge. They call themselves the "Glass-Walkers" or, more simply, the "Clinging Folk."

Physically, they are a compact people, lean and wiry. Broad shoulders and heavy musculature would be a liability on the swaying rope bridges and narrow ladders; instead, they possess a sinewy, endurance-based strength. Their most striking features are their hands and feet. They are disproportionately large, splayed and dexterous. Decades of gripping slick timber and navigating razor-sharp rock faces have developed pads of callus so thick and yellowish they resemble the hoof-sole of an animal. These natural crampons allow them to traverse wet, sixty-degree slopes of basalt with an unnerving, fluid nonchalance.

Their skin is uniformly pale, a milky, grayish tone born of living perpetually in the chasm’s twilight shadowed by the mists. Because the air is always saturated, their skin rarely completely dries. It has a permanent, semi-translucent quality, slightly wrinkled at the extremities like one who has soaked in a bath for too long. Their hair is universally dark, kept cropped short or tightly braided close to the skull to prevent it from catching on splinters or rock shards, and usually matted with grease to repel the constant dripping.

Their clothing is purely functional, designed as armor against both the cold moisture and the geology. They wear layered hides of surface animals—creatures they must leave the Sliver to hunt in the surrounding basin—which they laboriously treat with rendered animal fats and mineral oils gathered from seepages deep in the chasm. These heavy, stiff garments shed water and smell pungently of rancid grease, woodsmoke, and wet wool. Crucially, they wrap their forearms, shins, and necks in thick bands of boiled leather, hardened to carry protective overlapping slates of dull basalt. These are guards against the casual, accidental brush against a vitreous wall that could otherwise open a vein.

The suffocating density and acoustic deadness of their environment have profoundly shaped their temperament. They are an incredibly quiet people. Shouting is culturally taboo and practically useless in the fog; a loud voice is seen as a sign of dangerous instability. Communication is conducted in low murmurs, subtle clicks of the tongue, and a complex vocabulary of gestures and shifting stances adapted for tight spaces.

They have no concept of personal privacy. In the stacked hovels, three generations might sleep huddled together for warmth in a space no larger than a closet. This closeness has bred a society of intense, almost psychic communal awareness. A shift in weight on a high platform is instinctively counter-balanced by those below. The failure of a single support rope is everyone’s immediate crisis. They move through their crowded vertical world with a synchronized deliberation, a hive mind focused on remaining attached to the wall.

Their existence is defined by a high-stakes patience. When the thermal cracks echo from deep within the earth—the warning shot before an obsidian shard peels loose—the Clinging Folk do not panic. They simply stop. Hundreds of people freeze mid-climb on the ladders, pressing their bodies flat against the nearest overhang or crouching beneath their reinforced roofs, shielding their heads with their armored forearms. They wait in the dripping silence for the "stone rain" to fall, hear the clattering impacts below, and then, with a collective exhalation, resume their slow, ascending lives.
