The things that live here are human, but they have been cured by the environment like leather in a tannery. They are the inevitable fauna of a sweating, thrumming biome, creatures adapted to an existence of relentless heat, noise, and verticality.

They are stocky and broad, bodies shaped by generations of navigating slippery metal grates and hauling sodden biomass. Their skin is the color of wet clay, permanently slick with a mixture of sweat, condensation, and thin machine oil. Because true dryness is impossible, their epidermis has thickened, becoming porous and callous, especially on their hands and feet, to resist constant immersion and the fungal blooms that thrive in the folds of flesh. You rarely see their faces; they are hidden behind heavy, brass-framed respirators that filter the thick ozone and spore-heavy air through canisters of activated charcoal, giving them the appearance of deep-sea divers stranded above water.

Their clothing is functional and heavy, built to resist rot and abrasion: thick aprons of waxed canvas, oilskin trousers cinched with chains, and boots with soles made from tire tread, reinforced with metal cleats to grip the slime-coated walkways.

But the defining characteristic of these people is their integration with the machinery they tend. In a place where the organic and constructed have fused, the inhabitants have followed suit. Accidents among the crushing pistons and grinding gears are frequent, and the medical response is industrial.

Almost every adult bears some mechanical augmentation. A crushed hand is replaced not with a sleek prosthetic, but with a heavy, functional claw of riveted iron and brass, powered by hydraulic lines looped over the shoulder. A leg lost to a steam blowout is substituted with a heavy pylon of timber and steel, tipped with a thick rubber stopper, its joints hissing faintly with pressurized air as they walk. These replacements are not elegant; they are durable, greasy, and loud, requiring the same constant maintenance as the mills themselves.

They move with a deliberate, rolling gait, never rushing, testing every foothold on the slick grating before committing their weight. They are profoundly deaf, their hearing sacrificed long ago to the unending roar of the processors. Communication is a high-speed dialect of hand signals, flashing safety lamps, and rhythmic hammering on pipes with wrenches.

They are not just workers in this place; they are its gardeners and its mechanics simultaneously. A typical inhabitant carries a heavy bandolier holding a massive pipe wrench on one side and a honed machete on the other. Their days are spent in an endless cycle of tightening brass fittings against steam leaks and hacking back the aggressive, fast-growing ferns that threaten to choke the conveyor belts. They grease gears with one hand and scrape moss from junction boxes with the other, existing in the same precarious balance of slow growth and slow corrosion that defines their world.
