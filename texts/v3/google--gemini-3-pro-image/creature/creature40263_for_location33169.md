This place is not dead. It is merely animate in a way biological life finds horrifying.

Something lives in the screaming labyrinth, creatures perfectly adapted to an ecosystem defined by acoustic violence, crushing gravity pockets, and rapid mineral growth. They are the **Lithochordates**—the Stone-Singers.

They are notoriously difficult to see, primarily because they look exactly like the environment they inhabit. A Lithochordate is a squat, massive entity, rarely taller than a human’s knee but often as wide as a small car. Their bodies are not made of flesh, but of segmented plates of the same slick, greenish-black vitrified rock that forms the canyon walls. They are heavy, incredibly dense things, moving with the grinding slowness of a miniature glacier.

Because the gravity here is unreliable, they do not rely on balance. Instead, they possess eight to twelve multi-jointed limbs arranged radially around their central mass. These limbs end not in feet, but in powerful, clamping pedipalps that drive spiked anchors into the wet stone. They don't walk; they ratchet themselves forward, securing three points of contact before releasing a fourth, ensuring they are never torn away by the screaming wind or sudden lateral shifts in gravitational pull.

They have no eyes, for there is nothing here worth seeing in the searing, deceptive light. Instead, their entire carapace serves as a massive, complex tympanic membrane. The constant, deafening roar of the landscape—the grinding tectonic plates, the screaming wind, the thundering subterranean water—is not noise to them. It is illumination. They perceive their surroundings through a high-resolution sonarscape built from the ambient cacophony. The strange geometries and warped perspectives that confuse a human eye are perfectly navigable to them because they "hear" the density of the rock and the curvature of space through vibration.

Their primary food source—their energy currency—is the piezoelectric discharge of the shattering geoflora.

You will rarely see one moving amidst the spires. They prefer the deepest, narrowest slot canyons where the air is thickest with cold moisture. They gather in clusters around rapidly growing patches of the indigo and rust-copper crystalline dendrites. They sit perfectly still, their wet, black bodies glistening like oil, waiting.

When the thermal stress becomes too great and a massive crystal formation explodes in a shower of glassy shrapnel and a high-pitched *PING*, the Lithochordates do not flinch. Instead, fissures open on their armored backs, revealing core tissues made of conductive metallic fibers and pressurized hydraulic fluids. They absorb the acoustic shockwave and the electrical burst generated by the crystal's fracture. The shards rattle harmlessly against their armored hides. To a human observer, it looks like a silent, dark clump of wet boulders suddenly shuddering with pleasure when things break.

They are also the reason for the terrified human artifacts. The Lithochordates communicate.

They possess resonant cavities within their rocky bulk. By grinding internal plates together and modulating the airflow through spiracles along their flanks, they can generate sounds that out-shout the wind. It is a terrifying, ultra-low frequency thrum that you feel in your bone marrow more than you hear. It sounds like the earth groaning in pain.

The crude copper disks with their spiraling lines found in the human huts were not maps of the landscape. They were primitive spectrograms—desperate attempts by terrified explorers to record the complex, vibrating songs of the rock-creatures that surrounded their shelters in the pitch-black, screaming nights. The humans, huddled in their tiny stone igloos, were trying to appease the mountains that walked, the heavy, unseen things that fed on destruction and sang with the voice of an earthquake.
