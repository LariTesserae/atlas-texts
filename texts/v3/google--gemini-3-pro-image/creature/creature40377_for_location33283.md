The inhabitants of this bruised sky-forge are creatures of necessity, molded by the twin anvils of suffocating heat and hypobaric pressure. They are human, or at least they share a common ancestor with those who live on the obscured surface miles below, but generations of existence within the geothermal parasite have diverged their physiology and culture into something distinct, something hardened and slow-burning.

They call themselves the Tecton.

To survive the thin atmosphere, their chests have barrelled outward, housing lungs of extraordinary capacity. Yet, even this is not enough. Every Tecton wears—or rather, is integrated with—a filtration respirator. These are not bulky masks strapped to faces; they are sleek, ceramic-plated devices surgically grafted around the nose and mouth, extending down the throat. They function in near silence, efficiently scrubbing toxins from the volcanic vapors and concentrating the meager oxygen. The soft *huff-huff* of their breathing is the only personal sound they make, a counterpoint to the city's deep geological groan.

Their skin is the color of wet ash, thick and calloused, tough enough to resist incidental contact with scorching pipes. Over time, their pores become impacted with the omnipresent mineral dust, giving them the appearance of living statues carved from the same basalt that forms their foundations. They sweat profusely, a constant slick sheen that streaks the grime on their bodies, but their physiology has adapted to retain electrolytes, making their sweat almost pure water, quickly evaporated by the ambient furnace.

Clothing is minimal but highly specialized. They wear layered garments of woven silica fiber and heat-deflecting ceramic overlapping plates, resembling the carapace of the segmented scavengers outside. This armor is focused on the extremities and the back—protection against radiant heat drafts and falling drops of scalding condensation. The aesthetic is purely functional; there is no adornment, only the dull gray of alloy and the matte white of high-temperature ceramics.

The Tecton move with agonizing deliberation. In the 45°C heat and thin air, haste is death. To run is to court heatstroke or asphyxiation. Their movements are choreographed, efficient, and slow. A worker turning a massive valve wheel does so in incremental shifts, pausing between efforts. A group crossing a precarious gantry bridge moves in a rhythmic lockstep, conserving momentum. This enforced slowness imbues their culture with an aura of profound patience and ritualistic calm.

They are a silent people. The roar of the geothermal vents renders shouting useless, and the thin air makes speech exhausting. They communicate through a complex lexicon of hand signs, subtle body posture shifts, and tapping on metal surfaces—a Morse code of vibrations felt through the soles of their boots, transmitted along the city's metallic skeleton. When they must vocalize, their voices, filtered through their respirators, are gravelly, resonant bass tones that travel well through the dense humidity.

Their labor is the tending of the great machine. They are the midwives to the magma, guiding the slow flows into cooling molds with lances tipped in tungsten. They scale the scaffoldings of the refinery towers, scraping mineral buildup from ventilation louvers. They monitor the deep-bore taps, their eyes—hidden behind polarized goggles that reflect the violent violet sky—fixed on gauges measuring pressures that could tear the mountain apart.

They are masters of geothermal husbandry, possessing an intuitive understanding of heat gradients and pressure differentials. They do not fight the volcano; they siphon its fury with surgical precision.

Their diet is an indistinct paste, synthesized in pressurized vats from recycled biomass and mineral supplements, flavored with sulfur compounds that would choke an outworlder. They have no concept of day or night, only the endless rhythm of work shifts dictated by the fluctuation of steam pressure.

The Tecton do not fear the heat; they respect it as a god that sustains them while constantly trying to kill them. They are a grim, heavy people, their humanity encased in ceramic and callus, living a slow-motion existence balanced precariously above an unseen world, dedicated to the silent, endless extraction of power from the earth's boiling blood.
