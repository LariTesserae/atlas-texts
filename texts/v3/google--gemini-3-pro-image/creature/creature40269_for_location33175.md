This environment does not just house life; it is synthesized from it. The inhabitants are not occupants in the traditional sense, but rather motile extensions of the screaming, growing architecture. They are creatures adapted to a world where solidity is a temporary phase of biological growth, where air is thick enough to swim in, and where silence is extinct.

The most ubiquitous residents are the architects and maintainers of this cacophony. Crawling over every surface of the immense seed-pods and cathedral-tubers are the Weavers. These are multi-limbed arthropods the size of large dogs, their bodies covered in a slick, hydrophobic chitin that gleams in the daylight filtering from above. They are blind—sight is secondary in the constant fog—and navigate the dizzying heights through exquisite sensitivity to vibration along the vine-bridges. Their mandibles constantly extrude a quick-hardening, cartilage-like foam. They are the reason the impossible structures don't collapse; they spend their brief, frantic lives reinforcing stress points, patching tears in the tuber-walls, and weaving new thoroughfares across the yawning voids. Their movements are jerky and incessant, adding a constant, dry clicking to the ambient roar.

In the vast gulfs of humid air between the hanging districts, gravity seems to have lost its grip on the fauna. The "avian" creatures you glimpsed are not birds, but membranous pterodactyl-analogues, their wingspans ranging from two to twenty feet. They are pale, almost translucent, their skin thin enough to absorb oxygen directly from the saturated atmosphere. They do not flap, but glide on the thermal updrafts generated by the decaying heat of the city below. They are silent hunters, drifting like ghosts through the steam, their long, needle-filled jaws snatching smaller prey from the swaying bridges with unerring accuracy despite the warped optical depth.

The source of the booming calls from the dark below are the Slough-Giants. These immensely bloated amphibians inhabit the flooded lower chambers of the deepest hollow tubers. They are practically immobile, huge sacs of mottled, permeable skin that act as living filters for the ceaseless runoff dripping from miles above. Their booming cries are not territorial calls, but sonar pulses, low-frequency blasts used to map the density of the fog and the stability of the structures around them. They are the sessile gods of the understory, their backs covered in parasitic fungi gardens, their mouths wide enough to swallow a Weaver whole.

Yet, there is a dominant, quasi-sapient species here, the "citizens" of this hanging metropolis. They call themselves something that sounds like tearing wet wood. They are humanoid only in the loosest sense—bipedal configurations of twisted, iron-hard root fiber and pale, slick muscle. They possess no ears, having evolved past the need for hearing in the deafening din; instead, their entire bodies act as sensitive tympanic membranes, feeling the specific vibrations of their world through the soles of their feet and their palms.

They inhabit the hollowed-out city-pods, moving with terrifying speed along the swaying cartilage bridges using hooked claws extended from their forelimbs. Their eyes are enormous, multifaceted orbs that wrap around their skulls, adapted to process the bizarre, bent light and the dizzying scales of empty space. They are gardeners of a sort, but violent ones. They farm the giant insects, pruning the aggressive growth of the city with bone machetes to prevent the structures from choking themselves, and engaging in ritualized, silent wars over the prime nesting grounds in the upper canopy.

They do not build; they guide the growth. They do not speak; they vibrate the bridges to communicate across the chasms. They are the nervous system of the city, as frantic, wet, and intensely alive as the screaming architecture they call home.
