The people who inhabit this frozen oasis are a reflection of the cleft they occupy: compact, resilient, and deeply quiet. They are human, perhaps, but altered by countless generations of isolation in this high-pressure, hyper-oxygenated freezer.

They are short of stature, broad-shouldered and barrel-chested, built to conserve heat in a perpetually damp chill. Their movements are deliberate and incredibly sure-footed. Watch one descend a precarious, rime-slicked rope bridge, and you see not fear, but a fluid, rolling gait, their center of gravity kept low. Their feet, wrapped in layers of oiled leather and felted moss, grip the icy rock with prehensile certainty.

Their faces are dominated by the necessities of their environment. To combat the blinding, crystalline glare of the noon sun bouncing off ice walls, their eyes are set deep within heavy, fatty ridges of brow. They are almost always squinted into slits, surrounded by elaborate tattoos inked in soot and copper oxides—dark masks around the eyes designed to absorb glare. Below these blackened ocular bands, their skin is pale, thick, and porous, resembling the texture of the fungi they cultivate.

Their clothing is a marvel of Neolithic adaptation. Fur would rot in the constant damp, so they wear complex layers of treated skins—likely from the rounded, snowy mammals that share the canyon—scraped thin and heavily impregnated with animal fats and waxes rendered from the giant ferns. Over these watertight base layers, they wear bulky, textured tunics and cloaks woven from fibrous mosses and spun lichen, dyed in the same bruised purples and electric greens that carpet their world. The effect is camouflage; when standing still against a canyon wall, a person becomes merely another hummock of dripping vegetation.

They are a people of profound silence. In the acoustic intimacy of the slot canyon, a shout would be an assault. They communicate primarily through a complex system of gestures—quick, subtle movements of thick-fingered hands—and soft, resonant humming that seems to vibrate through the sternum rather than the air. When they do speak, their voices are low, gravelly rumbles, unused to raising above a whisper.

Their days are governed by the vertical shaft of light. During the long hours of twilight shadow, they tend the smoky peat fires in their cramped stone chambers, chip obsidian tools with patient, rhythmic clicks, and hammer copper into bowls and adornments using stone mufflers to deaden the noise.

When high noon strikes and the blinding column of solar fire hammers down, the city comes alive. They emerge onto their narrow ledges, squinting against the weeping radiance. This is when they tend the high terraced gardens of pale, meaty fungi, harvesting them with copper knives. It is when the cisterns are checked and the ropes of the bridges re-tallowed. They move among the dazzling ice and dripping moss like industrious, bulky beetles, unbothered by the cold that would kill an outsider in hours.

There are no children visible, though sometimes a small, bundled shape is strapped to a back, and occasionally, a soft, muted cry echoes fleetingly before being hushed. They seem ageless; the elders look much like the young adults, merely more weathered, their joints thicker, their skin more akin to the ancient leather they wear.

They are the stewards of this impossible garden, existing in a permanent state of frigid preservation. They do not seem to desire escape from their slot in the earth; they are as rooted here as the shaggy lichens hanging from the overhangs, waiting patiently for the daily miracle of the light.
