At first, you see nothing but the cold botany and the slick, rounded bones of the ancient city. The environment seems too hostile, too deafeningly violent for anything sentient. But if you press yourself against a polished alcove, shielding your eyes from the stinging rime and tuning your ears to the specific frequencies of the geological organ, you begin to perceive the tenants of this cathedral.

They are not animals in any terrestrial sense. They are the inheritors of the reef-builders, a distant, highly specialized echo of the colonial organisms that first raised these towers. They do not scurry or fly; they are anchored, sessile things, existing somewhere on the spectrum between geology and cold-weather fungi.

They cluster in the highest, most exposed throats of the porous spires, precisely where the wind shrieks the loudest. To the untrained eye, they look like architectural irregularities—bulbous, translucent excrescences fused to the calcified substrate. They share the same rounded, eroded aesthetic as the city itself, having been shaped by the same abrasive winds. They are pale, the color of rime-ice or clouded quartz, veined with deep purple and viridian streaks where microscopic symbiotic mosses live within their outer tissues, providing insulation.

These creatures are essentially living resonators. Their bodies are formed of a cartilaginous, silica-based lattice, incredibly tough yet flexible. They are hollowed out by complex internal chambers, mirroring the macro-structure of the towers they inhabit.

They feed on the wind itself. Or rather, on what the wind carries.

When the prevailing gale slams into the amphitheater, driving freezing fog and mineral dust into the honeycombed stone, these creatures "wake." They do not open eyes—they have none, having no need for sight in a world of blinding fog and searing sun—but they unfurl.

Hundreds of intricate, vitreous membranes flower outward from their anchored bodies, stretching across the apertures of the stone towers like living stained glass. These membranes are unimaginably sensitive. As the wind howls through the stone flutes, it vibrates these living tissues. The creatures are tuning forks, adjusting their own internal geometry to resonate perfectly with the incoming air current.

This vibration is their method of filtration. The intense acoustic energy shakes the moisture and microscopic particulate matter—ice crystals, rock flour, bacterial spores—out of the rushing air. The nutrients are trapped in sticky, conductive mucus lining their internal chambers, while the "cleaned" air is vented out the back.

The drone you feel in your teeth is not just the passive noise of wind hitting rock; it is the collective thrumming of thousands of these crystalline entities vibrating in ecstatic, deafening unison as they feed. They are the choir in this pocket cathedral, transforming the violent energy of the storm into cold sustenance. Their lives are measured not in heartbeats, but in cycles of vibration, existing entirely within the screaming chords of the wind.
