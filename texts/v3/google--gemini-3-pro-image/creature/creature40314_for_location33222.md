The inhabitants of this high, desolate expanse do not merely live upon the geology; they are continuous with it. They are the animate expression of the plateau’s deep time and industrial chemistry.

The dominant life forms are the vast herds of lithovores, creatures so monumentally scaled that from a distance, in the flattening grey light, they are indistinguishable from the hunched granite shoulders of the terrain. They are known to observers simply as the Mountain-Backs.

A single mature Mountain-Back is the size of a citadel, a low-slung, behemoth organism built to endure an eternity of cold. Their primary adaptation is their exterior plating. They are not covered in skin or fur, but in segmented carapace shields grown from excreted silicates and heavy metals absorbed from their diet. These plates are inches thick, rough-textured, and fissured like ancient bedrock. Over decades of life, their backs accumulate the same toxic patina that coats the landscape—streaks of leaden grey, oxidized copper greens, and pale sulfur yellows. When they rest, hunkered down against the thin wind, they become part of the topography. A traveler could walk right up to the flank of one and believe they were standing before a cliff face, until the "cliff" drew a shuddering, subterranean breath.

Their movement is a study in silent, terrible mass. They possess multiple pairs of thick, columnar legs, ending not in hooves or claws, but in massive, splayed pads of spongy, nerveless tissue that conform to the uneven ground. These pads dampen vibration and spread their immense tonnage so effectively that their passing over fields of loose scree creates only a muted, shifting sigh, instantly swallowed by the thin air. A herd of a thousand Mountain-Backs on the move generates less audible noise than a human footfall on gravel.

Their communication is entirely below the threshold of hearing. The silence of the plateau is maintained even as these giants signal to one another across miles. They possess resonant cavities within their chests—chambers of bone that produce powerful infrasonic pulses. Standing near a herd, one feels a strange, oceanic thrumming in the pit of the stomach and the marrow of the bones, a sensation of pressure rather than sound, as the creatures coordinate their slow migration towards the mineral-rich venting grounds.

Their feeding is a mechanical, industrial process. A Mountain-Back possesses a massive, downward-facing mouth structure that functions like a geological crusher. Instead of teeth, they have opposing plates of high-density bone, ridged and diamond-hard. They slowly lower their massive heads to the ground where the metallic vapors have settled most heavily as rime ice. With slow, hydraulic force, these jaw-plates grind the frozen, toxic earth, pulverizing rock and ice together to ingest the trace minerals and crystallized metals. The sound of their eating is the only noise they make that might be audible—a low, deep *crunch* that vibrates through the ground more than the air, like the stress-fracturing of a deep glacier.

In the margins of this world, smaller life exists, wholly dependent on the conditions created by the geology and the giants. In the deep fissures where the supercooled brine pools, blind, heavily calcified crustaceans, the size of dinner plates, move sluggishly through the slush. Their metabolism is virtually nonexistent; they filter the heavy salts and minerals from the fluid, living for centuries in the dark, freezing sludge.

And on the leeward sides of the greatest boulders, where the metallic lichen crusts cling, microscopic mites with iron-infused shells graze on the purple and rust-colored growths. Their entire life cycle occurs on a single patch of stone, a microcosm of the greater mineral processing taking place across the continent.

Life here is slow, heavy, and silent. It does not flourish; it endures. The creatures of the plateau are the necessary biological machinery of a cold, metal-poisoned world, grinding away at geological speeds under a bruised and sunless sky.
