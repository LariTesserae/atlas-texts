To dwell in this place is to become made of light and moisture. The primary inhabitants of this cathedral-hollow are not creatures of bone and blood, for such density would be an affront to the airy brilliance of the chamber. They are beings of spun silica and captured humidity, immense, elongated shapes that drift through the center of the nave, utilizing the vast, unoccupied expanses.

They are barely substantial, enormous sacks of translucent membrane, akin to jellyfish adapted for a thick, warm atmosphere instead of a sea. Their "flesh" is nothing more than a slightly denser coagulation of the humid air, held together by delicate, vein-like structures made of flexible, organic glass that catch the golden luminosity so perfectly they become almost invisible against the glowing walls. They do not cast shadows, for light permeates them entirely.

Their movement is incidental, a surrender to the environment. They possess no wings or fins to churn the air. Instead, they rely entirely on the subtle thermal convection currents rising from the warmed floor. They rise slowly toward the blazing ceiling, swelling slightly as the heat expands the gases within their membranes, then drift languidly toward the cooler, slick walls, cycling endlessly through the vast volume. To watch them is to watch clouds form and dissolve in super-slow motion.

They are the apex of this silent ecosystem, passive filter-feeders of a gentle sort. As they drift, huge, gossamer-thin folds of their lower bodies trail behind them like veils of shimmering heat haze. These membranes are perpetually sticky with condensation. The shimmering clouds of tiny, spun-glass insects, carried by the same currents, drift into these veils and are trapped. There is no violence in this consumption; the insects are simply absorbed into the creature's watery mass, slowly dissolved over days, their minute mineral components utilized to repair the larger creature's crystalline veins.

These beings have no eyes—in a world without shadow, sight as we know it is redundant. Instead, their entire surface area is a sensory organ tuned to thermal gradients and humidity levels. They perceive their world as a shifting map of warmth and wetness. They have no voices, making no sound heavier than the brush of damp silk against stone.

They are the silent custodians of the light chamber, living calendars whose lifespan measures not in years, but in the geological grinding of the earth miles above. They do not seem to age; they merely grow larger when the humidity rises over millennia, and shrink when it falls. They are eternal recyclers of the bright, wet void, existing in a state of awareness that is vast, slow, and utterly peaceful, feeling the formation of a single water droplet on the ceiling hours before it sings its *plink* into the pool below.
