This realm is inhabited by the architects themselves, a species whose history, biology, and culture are completely inextricable from the sprawling botanical organism they call home. They are the tenders of the lattice, the patient gardeners of a city-state that grows at the speed of wood.

They are beings built for a vertical, wet, and silent existence. Tall and exceedingly slender, their physiology has abandoned heavy musculature in favor of lithe, tensile strength, mirroring the boughs they traverse. Their skin is not hide or scales, but a smooth, semi-permeable membrane that resembles polished, wet driftwood, mottled with patches of greenish-grey that allow them to blend seamlessly with the ubiquitous epiphytic mosses. This skin stays permanently slick, coated in a thin, protective mucus that repels the constant inundation of the warm mists while allowing for cutaneous respiration in the oxygen-rich, humid air.

Their limbs are elongated and multi-jointed, ending not in hands, but in sets of four flexible, spatulate digits tipped with curved, retractable hooks of dense keratin—or perhaps bone derived from ancestors. These wide pads are ridged like gecko feet, providing immense friction on the slick, root-woven roads, while the hooks allow them to secure themselves to the underside of massive branches, hanging comfortably over the misty voids as they work. They move with a deliberate, fluid slowness, flowing from one anchor point to another; to rush here is to invite a fall into the endless "below."

In the perpetual diffusion of light, vision is of secondary importance. Their eyes are large, filmed over with nictitating membranes to keep out the water, sensitive only to shifts in light intensity and shadow. Their primary sense of the world comes from clusters of intricate, branching tendrils that sprout from their cheekbones and brows, resembling pale, fleshy coral. These organs are hyper-sensitive to humidity gradients, airflow patterns, and the minutest vibrations traveling through the wood. They "see" the structural integrity of a bough by the way it hums under tension; they navigate the void by sensing the slightly denser plumes of mist rising from unseen structures below.

They are a people operating on a geological timescale. Their lives are long, and their projects span generations. The guiding of a new structural branch across a hundred-yard gap to form a future bridge is a task commenced by a grandparent, tended by a parent, and completed by a grandchild. They use simple, elegant tools carved from the incredibly dense, cured heartwood of dead sections of the city, or shaped from the bones of the large, algal-furred sloth-creatures that perish and fall into the lattice.

Their society is quiet, communal, and seemingly without hierarchy. Communication is achieved through a complex language of soft, clicking sounds produced in the throat, transmitted effectively through the dense air and amplified by the wood itself, supplemented by the release of complex pheromones that convey emotional states and architectural intent. A disagreement over whether to graft a new living chamber here or induce a controlled rot there is resolved through days of silent, collective contemplation, feeling the stresses of the surrounding structure until a consensus vibration is reached.

They are farmers as much as architects. In hollowed-out knots of ancient wood, they cultivate gardens of bioluminescent fungi that provide dim, pulsing illumination and supplemental food. They carefully tap the massive lignified arteries of the city-tree, harvesting thick, nutrient-rich sap without harming the host structure. They are stewards of the rot as well, guiding saprophytic blooms to digest dead structures, clearing the way for new growth in a perpetual cycle of slow cannibalism and renewal.

They are rarely seen in large groups. Usually, they are encountered alone or in pairs, clinging to the side of a colossal arborsculpture like industrious insects on a redwood, patiently applying tension to a young vine with bone clamps, or gently abrading the bark of two massive limbs to encourage them to fuse. When they rest, they retreat into spherical dwellings grown from woven leaves and sealed with resin, suspended in the intricate webwork of the canopy.

Sometimes, one will stand on the edge of a great root-road bridge, looking out into the boundless, temperate haze. They stand perfectly still, moisture beading on their slick, mottled skin, their facial tendrils gently waving in the muffled air currents, listening to the endless, quiet percussion of dripping water, feeling the slow, deep pulse of the vegetable behemoth they serve.
