What lives here is a singular, sprawling organism of pale cellulose and immense, patient hunger. It is a botanical hegemony that has conquered the dark through a slow-motion strangulation of the space.

This entity is the albino leviathan comprised of the waxy, translucent vines. It is not a collection of individual plants, but a continuous, tangled nervous system that thrives on the geothermal vents below. Its "blood" is the superheated, mineral-rich moisture sucked from the humid air and the slick rock; its "breath" is the oppressive, oxygen-poor steam that fills the cathedral hall.

The inhabitant is a devourer of minerals, but its preferred prey is iron.

The pale vines are thick as hawsers, slick with the same condensation that coats the walls, having the unpleasant, yielding texture of waterlogged flesh. They possess a terrifying, hydrostatic strength. Where they wrap around the ancient basalt, they hold tight enough to fracture existing fissures with the slow pressure of their growth. But their true intimacy is reserved for the ruins of industry.

They have claimed the iron structures entirely. The massive girders are not merely trellises; they are being metabolized. The thick mats of pale roots secrete corrosive enzymes that accelerate oxidation in the damp heat. The metal is not just rusting; it is bleeding its structure into the gripping plants. The vines appear rusted themselves, stained deep orange and brown where they make contact with the girders, actively siphoning the iron oxide into their translucent veins to build their eldritch, bone-colored structures.

The half-buried engine blocks on the floor are the centers of digestion. They are indistinct mounds beneath blankets of fleshy leaves and white root systems, looking like great beasts brought low by a parasite. The flora works its way into every gear tooth and cylinder bore, cracking the casings with hydraulic pressure, slowly turning the hulking machines into soil.

There is a microbial component to this inhabitant as well. The slick sheen on every surface is a biofilm, a thriving bacterial sludge that assists in breaking down the stone and metal, creating the nutrients that the larger vines absorb. The air itself tastes metallic and fungal because it is thick with their spores and exhalations.

The only light in the chamber is the faint, sickly yellow glow emitted by the newest growth—the tender tips of the vines searching for fresh holds in the darkness. It is a ghostly, pulsing bioluminescence that signals the creeping advance of the organism.

It is a silent, blind colossus. It has no eyes to see the gloom, no ears to hear the dripping water, only a vast, tactile awareness of heat and moisture. It occupies the chamber with the absolute stillness of a predator that measures its ambush in centuries. It is simply waiting—waiting for the girders to finally buckle, for the engine blocks to dissolve into mud, and for the geothermal heat to eventually cool, so it can die along with the world it has consumed.
