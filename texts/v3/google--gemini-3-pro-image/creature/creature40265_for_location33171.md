The beings that cling to existence in this frozen archipelago call themselves the **Lithak**. They are creatures sculpted by gravity, cold, and the subterranean rhythm of their world.

At a distance, a Lithak is indistinguishable from the rock face it adheres to. They are squat, heavily built beings, possessing a density of bone and muscle necessary to endure the crushing gravitational pull of the unseen abyss. Their primary orientation is not upright, but pressed flat against the vertical plane. Four powerful, multifaceted limbs sprawl from a central, segmented torso. These limbs do not end in hands or feet, but in complex, articulated gripping pads shod in organic, calcified spurs jagged as crampons. They can drive these spurs into the micro-fissures of the basalt or lock onto thick rime ice with a grip that requires conscious effort to release.

They have no need for eyes in the perpetual gloom. Their heads are low, armored cowls streamlined into their shoulders, protecting a sophisticated sensory array. Where a face might be, there is a cluster of sensitive, feathery antennae that act as chemoreceptors, constantly tasting the brittle air for the metallic tang of a thermal vent or the musk of a shaggy grazer. Beneath these antennae are broad, flat resonance plates, tight against their skulls. These plates serve as their ears, but they hear very little airborne sound; instead, they are attuned to the deep vibration of the rock. They feel the "geological breathing" of the planet, sensing the subterranean thrum in their marrow, knowing intuitively when a vent is about to cycle a pulse of heat.

The hide of a Lithak is a marvel of environmental adaptation. It is a thick, rugose carapace of mineral-infused chitin, dark grey and cracked like cooling lava. Over their lifetime, this carapace becomes a substrate for the environment. It gets coated in layers of rime ice and dusted with windblown spores, eventually growing patches of the same insulating grey-blue wool-moss that covers the cliffs. An elder Lithak is a walking ecosystem, perfectly camouflaged against the frozen walls, moving with agonizing slowness.

Their society, such as it is, is clustered tightly around the geothermal vents. They are the gardeners of these precarious oases. Using tools fashioned from honed basalt and the bones of fallen grazers, they excavate intricate burrow-systems within the meters-thick mats of wool-moss, creating insulated pockets of rank, humid warmth protected from the lethal outer cold.

Their lives are dedicated to maintaining the balance of heat. They scale the treacherous, icy faces surrounding their vents, chipping away excessive ice buildup that threatens to seal the fissures. They tend the towering, bioluminescent fungal chandeliers, harvesting the glowing stalks for sustenance and using the fibrous pulp to patch their moss-homes. It is a life of extreme patience and deliberate movement. A single mistake in gripping means a silent, interminable fall into the lower darkness.

The Lithak do not speak with voices that carry on the thin wind. They communicate through the stone itself. By striking the rock face with their hardened spurs in rhythmic patterns, they send vibrational messages that travel for miles through the interconnected spires—warnings of structural instability, locations of new chemical plumes, or elegies for those claimed by the void. Their culture is quiet, enduring, and deeply fatalistic, moving to the slow, grinding tempo of the geological machinery that sustains them.
