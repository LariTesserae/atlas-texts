What lives here is less a resident and more a symptom of the place, an animate extension of the cold, crushing architecture. They are slow, solitary entities, difficult to distinguish from the environment they inhabit until they move, and even then, movement is glacial and deliberate.

At first glance, they appear to be gargoyles or abstract ornamental carvings left unfinished by the city’s unknowable machinery. They are massive, lumpen things, hunched and heavy. Their hide is not skin, nor scale, but a contiguous, seamless layer of that same matte, charcoal-grey material that forms the towering chasms. It is incredibly dense. Were one to fall, it would not tumble like flesh, but strike the ground with the dead, monumental thud of a dropped anvil.

They are profoundly cold. The penetrating chill that saturates the air seems to intensify around them; they are walking heat-sinks, absorbing any trace warmth remaining in the reality pocket. Because of this, they are perpetually slick with heavy condensation. Water beads on their broad, featureless backs and runs in rivulets down their thick, pillar-like limbs, dripping constantly. To touch them is to risk instant frostbite, your skin adhering to their damp, freezing surfaces.

They have no faces in any recognizably terrestrial sense—no eyes to see the shadowless white light, no mouths to speak into the heavy silence. Instead, their "heads" are broad, sensory surfaces, slightly concave depressions curved downward toward the surface they cling to. These depressions are sensitive to the minute vibrational shifts of the groaning architecture and the warped gravitational flows. They navigate not by sight, but by feeling the deep, subsonic tectonic strain of the city reforming itself.

Their locomotion is a disturbing mimicry of the city's own geological slowness. They do not walk so much as flow in highly viscous spurts. They possess four to six thick appendages that end not in feet, but in broad, flat pads that fuse atomically with whatever surface they are on—wall, floor, or overhang. Gravity means nothing to them; they are just as likely to be found clinging upside down to the underside of a massive buttress as they are hunched on a ledge. To move, one limb detaches with a sound like tearing wet concrete, shifts forward slowly, and re-anchors, before the rest of the dense body ripples sluggishly after it, like a droplet of cold slag.

Their purpose seems tied to the unstable reality of the city. They are often found gathered near the active "seams"—those areas where the grey material is crawling like cooling magma, where staircases are extruding or walls are merging. They cluster around these points of change in a state of deep torpor, their sensory depressions pressed close to the shifting matter.

They are "grazing" on the process of becoming. They feed on the raw, latent energies released when the architecture breaks down and reforms, absorbing the reality-warping radiation that allows floors to become walls. As they feed, the pale, crystalline verdigris moss tends to grow thicker on their stationary flanks, crumbling off in glassy showers when they finally move on weeks later. They are the silent, freezing anchorites of this churning stone world, processing the immense forces that keep the impossible city alive.
