The dominant intelligent life here has been shaped by gravity and the lack of a horizon. They call themselves the Grate-Walkers, though "walking" is rarely what they do.

They are human, perhaps descended from the original builders, but countless generations of vertical existence have warped their physiology. They are short and densely muscled, with a low center of gravity. Their most striking features are their arms, which are disproportionately long, dangling past their knees, ending in hands that are enormous, callused spatulas of flesh. Their fingers are thick and permanently curled, tipped with thick, horny nails almost like talons, adapted for hooking into mesh grating and gripping slick, oily pipes. Their feet are similarly prehensile, broad and flat with splayed toes that can wrap around a girder edge as securely as a clamp.

Their skin is not merely dirty; it is permanently dyed a splotchy, terracotta orange, stained by the ubiquitous rust and the mineral-rich water that drips constantly. They smell of old sweat, metallic tang, and the sweet, earthy odor of crushed foliage. Their eyes are small, deeply set beneath heavy brows to shield them from the intense shafts of sunlight and falling debris, often protected further by goggles fashioned from scavenged glass shards and leather straps.

The Grate-Walkers live in small, tribal clusters, clinging to the stable "islands" formed where several large platforms intersect near slow-moving gears that provide residual warmth. Their shelters are woven nests basketed between beams, constructed from tough, fire-resistant vines, scavenged metal sheeting, and the massive, waxy leaves of the "Iron-eater" plants. They rarely speak; the industrial din makes conversation impossible over more than a foot of distance. Instead, they communicate through a complex language of piercing whistles that cut through the machinery’s thrum, accompanied by rapid, percussive sign language beaten against their own chests or nearby metal surfaces.

Their existence is a perpetual vertical migration. They farm the deep recesses, cultivating bioluminescent fungi that thrive on the hydrocarbon sludge coating the lower machinery. They hunt the giant insects—"plate-bugs"—using spears tipped with sharpened ceramic shards, prizing the creatures' fatty, protein-rich abdomens. Water is collected in tarps strung beneath the weeping pipes, funneling the lukewarm runoff into corroded barrels.

They share this dizzying lattice with a hybridized ecosystem. The primates mentioned—the "Grease-Monkeys"—are competitors for fruit and shiny scavenged items. They are smaller than the Grate-Walkers, with fur slicked down by oil, moving in chaotic troops that fling excrement and rusted bolts at intruders.

The avian life is equally adapted. The "Steam-Divers" are sleek, featherless birds with leathery skin, immune to the scalding plumes rising from cooling vents. They dive through the steam to snatch insects, their calls sounding like metal scraping against a grinding wheel.

Far more dangerous are the things that have evolved to mimic the structure. The "Cable-Snake" is a constrictor immensely thick and slow-moving, its scales perfectly replicating the texture and color of rusted, braided steel wire. It lies dormant for weeks wrapped around functional conduits, waiting for a monkey or an unwary Grate-Walker to use it as a handhold before crushing them with hydraulic pressure.

Down below, in the warmer, thicker haze where the light rarely penetrates, live the scavengers of the deep. Pale, blind things with multi-jointed limbs skitter across the underside of platforms, feeding on the detritus that falls from above—the dropped wrenches, the animal carcasses, the un-caught bodies of those who slipped. The Grate-Walkers speak of the "Deep-Maws" with superstitious dread—immense, sessile creatures said to dwell in the unreachable lower gloom, their open mouths cavernous enough to swallow falling girders, digesting metal and bone alike in the churning darkness.
