The beings that cling to existence here are the "Aurless"—a name not spoken, for there is no speaking here, but felt as a specific, frantic vibration passed from hand to shoulder in the crushes of the slate huts.

They are human-shaped, perhaps distantly descended from humanity, but warped by generations of hyper-oxygenated pressure and acoustic violence. They are short, incredibly dense creatures, built of compacted muscle and heavy bone to withstand the deep-sea crush. Their skin is pale, almost colorless, scarred ragged by razor-edged silicate shrapnel and the burns of cooling metal. It has a thick, blubbery texture, insulated against the paralyzing chill of the high-altitude ocean.

They have no external ears. Where ears would be, there are only smooth, calloused patches of skull. The Roar is too great for hearing; it would burst an eardrum instantly. Instead, the Aurless perceive their world through bone conduction and supreme tactile sensitivity. Their entire bodies are resonance chambers. They *feel* the scream of the universe in their marrow. They can distinguish the shudder of a stabilizing iron spire from the death-rattle of a silicate lattice about to shatter back into void-stuff solely through the vibrations coming up through their calloused feet.

Their eyes are their only connection to the "airy" quality of the place. They are enormous, disproportionate to their skulls, shielded by thick, polarizing nictitating membranes that slide across to cut the agonizing glare of the unfiltered daylight miles above. When the membranes are retracted in dimmer moments, their pupils are blown wide, swallowing the illuminated emptiness, forever scanning the terrifying distances for the flash of new matter birthing.

Their lives are defined by the Rope and the Knot.

They move with a frantic, jerky agility, crab-walking across shivering obsidian faces, their limbs powerful enough to drive slate pitons into microscopic fissures. Around their waists and chests, they wear bulky harnesses woven from the glassy kelp fibers—the same fibers that tether their precarious baskets. They are never unattached. To be untethered is to be swept away into the bright, screaming void between drifts.

Their hands are broad, the fingers stubby and powerful, tips worn smooth by constant friction against raw geology. They are master weavers, master knot-tiers. Their crude technology—the basalt fiber baskets, the stone-tipped spears—are not for hunting the sparse, transparent fauna. They are harvesting tools.

The Aurless wait, crouched on the "stablest" rocks, feeling the chaotic rhythm of the Roar. When a new spasm racks the area, and a globule of molten copper or a shard of super-heated crystal erupts nearby, they do not flee. They scramble *toward* it.

Working in desperate teams, communicating only through rapid slapping motions on each other’s backs or the yank of a safety line, they cast their basalt baskets. They try to snare the raw matter while it is still agonizingly hot, before it fully solidifies or, worse, dissolves back into energy. They leverage their slate spears to pry chunks of cooling ore loose, tossing them into the weighted baskets. It is a harvest of leftovers caught in the milliseconds between creation and destruction.

Their society is one of huddled terror and frantic snatching. In the rare moments when the immediate geological births subside, they retreat to those precarious beehive huts on the ledges. There, packed tightly together to share bodily warmth, they engage in their only culture: tactile storytelling. They drum complex rhythms on each other’s chests and arms—stories of the Great Roar, of spires that lasted a whole day, of comrades lost when their anchor-rock suddenly ceased to exist.

They are a people living in the caldera of a perpetual detonation, armed only with woven grass and chipped stone, forever tying knots against the unraveling of the world.
