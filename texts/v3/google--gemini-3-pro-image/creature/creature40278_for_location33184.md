They are the ghosts of the wet rock, beings perfectly adapted to a world of deafening noise and perpetual twilight. At first glance, they are indistinguishable from the terrain they inhabit. Crouched low against the wind and spray, their bodies are broad, flattened domes of segmented armor, colored the same slick, striated greys and oily blacks as the gneiss and obsidian humps. Water sheets off their backs just as it does the surrounding stone.

They are not bipedal; such a stance would be suicidal on this slick, shifting landscape. Instead, they move on six powerful, stumpy limbs spread wide from their low center of gravity. Each limb ends not in a foot, but in a complex cluster of muscular pads and tough, keratinous gripping claws arranged in a radial pattern. They do not walk so much as adhere and release, adhere and release, moving with a fluid, rolling gait that allows them to traverse vertical, waterfall-drenched chasms as easily as flat ground. When they stop and hunker down, clamping themselves to the stone with hundreds of pounds of suction pressure, they become geologically immovable objects.

In this world of shattering noise, auditory organs as we know them are useless. Their "ears" are the entirety of their underbellies. Sensitive membranes stretched taut across their thoracic segments pick up the deepest subsonic rumblings of the subterranean rivers, allowing them to map the unseen labyrinth beneath them by vibration alone. They know where the rock is thin and liable to collapse, and they can feel the shuddering precursor of a geyser eruption minutes before the steam shrieks free.

Their vision is poor in the watery twilight. Their primary interface with the immediate environment comes from a cluster of highly specialized palps located near their mouths. These appendages are fringed with delicate, neurological filaments that constantly taste the saturated air and tap against the wet stone, searching for the chemical signature of bacterial mats or the vibrational trail of a passing grazer.

Beneath their heavy dorsal armor lies surprising dexterity. Tucked away when moving, they possess a pair of secondary, manipulative limbs. These are thinner, longer arms ending in multi-jointed fingers capable of immense precision. These are the hands that build the domed shelters, selecting river-smoothed stones and rotating them patiently until the friction fit is perfect, creating dry pockets of silence in the roaring world.

They are patient hunters and scavengers. They craft the nets of braided sinew—harvested from the eel-predators—and anchor them in the chipped notches near chasms, waiting for the torrent to deliver stunned prey. More often, they stalk the slow-moving, armored grazers on the bacterial fields. They utilize their own immense suction to anchor themselves beside prey, then use their manipulative arms to leverage underneath the grazer's shell, flipping it over to expose the softer underbelly to their grinding, beak-like mouths.

They are a solitary, quiet species. Communication is physical; a rhythmic tapping on the stone substrate can send vibrational messages over miles of bedrock, a Morse code felt in the bones rather than heard in the air. Their lives are governed by the slow rhythms of geology and the violent outbursts of water. They are the silent watchers on the banks of the roaring chasms, the patient stackers of stone, clinging stubbornly to the wet, hardest bones of the world.
