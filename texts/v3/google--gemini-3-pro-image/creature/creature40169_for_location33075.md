The creatures that inhabit this viscous, jade twilight are mere condensations of the environment itself; they are the warmth and wetness given temporary, gelatinous form. To survive here is to be permeable. To have a rigid boundary between oneself and the crushing humidity would be lethal; skin here is not a shield, but a sieve, constantly exchanging fluids and gases with the cloying air.

There are few eyes in this world. In the perpetual fog, vision is a useless extravagance. Instead, life relies on a hyper-developed sense of touch and chemoreception. The primary inhabitants of the mid-story—the space between the sinking muck and the distant canopy—are massive, slow-moving entities that resemble land-bound jellyfish or shell-less gastropods of nightmare proportions. Their flesh is translucent and slick with mucus, pulsing gently as they glide silently over the root-mat on broad, undulating feet designed to displace their weight over the spongy ground. They trail neurotoxins in their slime, a passive defense mechanism that leaves shimmering, deadly paths through the undergrowth. They feed by simply pressing their bodies against the massive banks of fungi, absorbing nutrients directly through their exterior membranes in a slow, ecstatic digestion.

Higher up, in the massive fluted branches of the canopy trees, live the arboreal "grazers." They are vaguely simian in shape but move with the agonizing, deliberate slowness of a tectonic plate. Their fur has long since been replaced by symbiotic growths of moss and algae; when still, they are perfectly camouflaged as just another lump of wet biomass. They have vestigial eye-sockets covered by pale skin, and their long, specialized fingers end not in claws, but in textured pads sensitive enough to read the chemical history of a leaf with a single touch. They do not vocalize. Communication happens through low-frequency thumping against the resonant wood of the trees, vibrations that travel for miles through the interconnected root systems—a subterranean drum language of warnings and mating calls felt in the gut rather than heard by the ear.

And then, there are the occupants of the petrified shelters.

They are the descendants of the builders, though they have strayed far from whatever human blueprint they once followed. They are tall and terribly thin, their bone structure softened and flexible to navigate the dense tangle. Their skin is the color of skimmed milk, nearly transparent, revealing a sluggish network of blue-green veins beneath. They are hairless, coating themselves daily in the antiseptic slime of certain pitcher plants to ward off fungal infections.

Like everything else here, they are nearly blind. Their faces are smooth, dominated by large, complex nostril slits that twitch constantly, reading the complex bouquet of decay and growth. Their hands are enormous, the fingers webbed with sensory flesh, constantly splayed to feel their way through the fog.

They are a quiet, melancholy people. They wear no clothes, for the heat is absolute, and they carry no tools. They have no need to build; the forest provides shelter in its hollows. They have no need to hunt; the forest offers endless soft fruits, calorie-rich saps, and mats of protein-heavy slime molds that they scoop up with their wide hands.

Their lives are dictated by the slow, grand cycles of the plateau. They move with a languid grace, never hurrying, for there is nowhere to get to before the fog closes in. They gather in the ruins of their ancestors' homes not out of nostalgia—they have no concept of history—but because the petrified root-walls resonate pleasantly with the deep thrumming of the forest's sap-flow. They sit for hours in the dark depressions of the floors, swaying gently, their skin glistening in the faint glow of phosphorescent fungi.

The most profound adaptation of these people is their end. They do not bury their dead, for the ground would just swallow them into the anaerobic muck. Instead, as an elder nears the end of their life, their metabolism slows even further. They choose a spot near a great canopy tree and sit down, pressing their splayed legs firmly into the peat. Over weeks, they stop moving entirely. Their translucent skin begins to thicken and lignify. Rootlets from the ground below locate their feet and begin to weave into their flesh.

They do not die in a human sense; they *root*. The boundary between their biology and the forest's botany dissolves completely. They become one of the grotesque, calcified statues you might mistake for rock, their form preserved as a hard, woody nodule in the landscape, eventually serving as a trellis for the very slow-moving vines that studded their living skin. They return to the substrate, adding one more layer to the miles-deep foundation of compacted ancestors upon which this warm, breathing world is built.
