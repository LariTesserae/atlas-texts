They are the fauna of the thrum, creatures whose very biology—if biology is the word—is tuned to that eternal, grinding bass note. They do not just live within the vibration; they require it. It is the medium through which their internal fluids circulate and their heavy joints articulate. Without the resonant roar of the plateau, they would seize up and calcify into statues of scrap.

They are immense, hulking beings, their scale dictated by the machinery they attend. Standing three or four times the height of a human, they are broad, squat entities composed of a hybrid of materials that mirror the layered architecture around them. Their core structure appears to be carved from the same dark, iron-rich basalt as the caldera foundations, polished smooth by the wet steam. Over this stone skeleton lies an accumulated dermis of salvaged plating—riveted iron scarred with deep orange rust, patches of newer, dull alloys, and sections of ceramic piping that act as external vascular systems.

They are perpetually wet. Condensation beads on their cooler ceramic parts and runs down into the porous iron, staining their bodies with sprawling maps of corrosion. They smell of hot oil, wet ozone, and ancient mineral dust.

They have no faces in any recognizable sense. The diffused, shadowless light and the thick steam render acute vision useless. Instead, their heads are massive, downward-sloping cowls of pitted metal, engineered to shield complex sensor arrays that interpret the world through thermal gradients and sonar-like echoes bouncing off the canyon walls. They "see" the heat blooming from a strained bearing or the micro-fractures in a cooling tower through shifts in the vibratory landscape.

Their movement is glacial. They do not walk so much as shift their immense weight with agonizing deliberation, their footfalls heavy enough to add syncopated percussion to the city’s roar. A single stride takes seconds to initiate and complete. When they work on the machinery—perhaps applying a lubricant sludge from a built-in reservoir to a piston the size of a house, or clearing a blockage in a sluice gate—their motions are tidal. They leverage their incredible mass against the resistance of the gears, a slow, irresistible application of force that matches the lethargic sweep of the cathedral-sized flywheel.

They are silent. They have no need for vocalization in a world deafened by industry. Their communication is subterranean, transmitted through the soles of their feet into the conductive bedrock, a complex language of tremors and thuds intelligible only to those tuned to the frequency of the caldera.

They are the custodians of the momentum, maintenance workers evolved into a priesthood. They move through the swirling steam like archaic armored ghosts, carrying out routines established millennia ago, greasing, patching, and observing the colossal, dying engines with a dedication that has long outlived the memory of why the engines were built in the first place. They are indifferent to the open sky above the fog layer; their entire existence is bound to the warm, wet, vibrating expanse of the foundry floor.
