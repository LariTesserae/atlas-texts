The beings that tend this weeping garden are creatures of intense, distilled vitality, burning brightly against the crushing cold. They are the architects of the gourd-pods and the weavers of the root-bridges, a species for whom geology is a foreign concept and biology is the only reality.

They are humanoid only in the loosest sense—bipedal when necessary, but preferring a four-limbed, scuttling gait along the swaying fiber-ways. They are about the height of a human adolescent, but denser, compacted with muscle and insulating fat. Their bodies are covered in a pelt of incredibly thick, fine fur, colored the pale, creamy white of subterranean fungi. This fur is coated in natural oils, sleek and water-repellent, so the ceaseless drips run off their backs like rain off a duck’s feathers. When they stop moving, which is rarely, they seem to vanish against the pale root-mats, visible only as mounds of steaming warmth in the frigid air.

They are utterly eyeless. Their heads are smooth, elongated domes shaped for pushing through dense tangles. Where a face should be, there is a complex array of sensory organs spread across the skin—highly sensitive patches of nerve endings and cluster-vibrissae, long, stiff whiskers that twitch constantly, mapping the immediate environment through air currents and the slightest vibrations of the walkways. Their ears are large, mobile swivels of cartilage framed by fur, capable of rotating independently to catch the ultrasonic ticks of the blind insects they farm or the groaning stress-fractures of a growing gourd-tower.

Their hands are the engines of their culture. They possess six multi-jointed fingers on each hand, tipped with thick, dark claws that are surprisingly dexterous. These claws can shred tough fibrous pulp or gently manipulate a delicate fungal spore.

They are a silent people. Vocalization is dangerous in a space where sound travels so strangely and predators might be lurking in the dark pools below. Instead, they communicate through a complex language of touch—drumming their fingers on another’s flank, brushing whiskers, and releasing specific pheromones that cut through the prevailing musk of damp fur and chitin.

Their interaction with their bio-engineered city is less like construction and more like midwifery. You might see a group of them clustered around a young, developing dwelling-gourd. They do not hammer or saw; they massage the plant tissues with secretions from glands in their palms, chemical triggers that coax the cellulose to harden in specific shapes or stimulate rapid, ballooning growth to create a hollow chamber. They carry tools of sharpened bone, harvested from their own dead, used to prune back aggressive mosses or tap into the thickest roots to siphon off nutrient-rich sap, which they drink mixed with the icy water.

They are frenetic beings, possessing a metabolism tuned to a fever pitch to combat the subterranean chill. They move in sudden bursts of speed, racing across the swaying bridges with uncanny balance, their pale forms blurring in the weak violet glow of the city veins. Inside the gourd-towers, they huddle in dense piles to share warmth, a collective heartbeat that thrums against the living walls.

They are the masters of this darkened hollow, but they are also its captives. Their lives are short, intense, and entirely cyclical. They grow the city, they eat the city's produce, and when they die of exhaustion or cold, their bodies are ritually interred deep within the root-mat floor, becoming the rich compost that fuels the next generation of towering fungi and weeping ferns. They are the gardeners, and eventually, they become the garden itself.
