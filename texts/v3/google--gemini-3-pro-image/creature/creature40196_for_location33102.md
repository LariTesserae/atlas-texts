The prompt beautifully establishes the aerial ecosystem—the silent membrane-wings filtering the roaring sky. But it leaves open the question of what, if anything, manages to exist on the terrible, polished surface of the earth itself. Given the parameters—the constant hurricane-force wind, the abrasive dust, the blinding UV, and the lack of standing water—terrestrial life here is defined by two imperatives: adhesion and density.

Nothing walks upright. To raise a head above the thin boundary layer of slower air right against the rock is to be decapitated by the wind. Life here is exclusively subterranean or hugging the ground with hysterical strength.

In the deep, flute-like bowls on the leeward side, where the silvery cushions of rock-hard moss grow, live the **Lithos-Grazers**. They resemble prehistoric chitons or highly modified limpets, grown to the size of dinner plates. They do not have shells in the traditional sense; their dorsal surface is a segmented carapace of biologically secreted mineral composite, seemingly grown from the granite itself and polished to the same friction-free sheen as the surrounding landscape by the endless sandblasting.

They are heavy—shockingly dense creatures that cling to the stone with a muscular foot capable of generating thousands of pounds of suction pressure. They move with glacial slowness, scraping the metallic moss from the rock with radulae tipped with diamond-dust. They are blind—eyes are a liability in the searing light and flying grit—and deaf, for sound is meaningless in the monolithic roar. Instead, they perceive their world entirely through seismicity. They feel the deep, vibrating hum of the Eternal Gale against the mountain, and they feel the minute crunching vibrations of their own grazing.

Venturing out of these bowls onto the exposed, wind-hammered ridges is a different kind of creature, the apex predator of this two-dimensional world: the **Gale-Skimmer**.

The Skimmer is built low and wide, a six-legged creature constructed like a flattened tank. It has no skin or fur; it is encased in thick, interlocking plates of keratin and bone, smoothed into a perfect aerodynamic foil. It does not walk so much as glide across the slick rock on spatulate feet lined with microscopic hooks that grip the crystalline structure of the gneiss.

Like the grazers, Gale-Skimmers are blind. They hunt by pressing their heavy, armored chins against the stone, sensing for the specific vibrational signature of a Lithos-Grazer moving in a distant bowl, or the incredibly faint, high-frequency impact of another Skimmer's claws on the rock.

Their primary food source, however, comes from above. They wait on the exposed ridges, hunkered down under the crushing weight of the wind, waiting for tragedy in the aerial world. When one of the great translucent sky-rays dies or is injured in the slipstream, its body eventually falls. If it hits the ground, the wind tumbles it along the smooth rock at hundreds of miles an hour until it smashes into a windward cliff face, shredding into an organic paste that freezes instantly.

The Gale-Skimmers track the vibrations of these impacts. They laboriously crawl against the torrent, gripping the rock with desperate strength, to reach the cliff bases and rasp the frozen organic detritus from the stone before it sublimates back into the hungry air.

Nothing here is soft. Everything is armored, heavy, low, and vibrating with the energy of the ground. It is an ecosystem of slow-moving stones feeding on other stones, forever pressed flat by the hand of the wind.
