This is a people shaped by the slow violence of geology. They call themselves the **Lithos**, a name whispered in the hushed tones required by their immense, echoing home.

Physically, the Lithos are a reflection of the environment they cling to. They are stocky and dense-boned, a physiological response to generations spent hauling slate, turning heavy winch-cranks, and navigating a world with very few flat surfaces. Their center of gravity is low. They do not walk so much as they negotiate the terrain; their gait is a deliberate, testing placement of feet shod in thick leather boots studded with iron hobnails, designed to grip slick quartz and damp slate.

Their skin, where it is visible beneath layers of heavy clothing, is pale, almost translucent in the shadow-zones, veined with blue like the underside of marble. Yet, their faces are often weathered, tanned not by a constant sun, but by the intense, concentrated bursts of UV radiation they endure when crossing the "sun-shafts." This creates a striking contrast: bodies milky as quartz, with faces etched and browned like dried mud. Their eyes are perpetually narrowed, surrounded by a dense network of squinting lines—a necessary adaptation to a world that shifts instantly from crushing darkness to blinding crystalline refraction.

Their clothing is purely utilitarian, a defense against the pervasive cellar-chill and the ceaseless dripping. They wear layers of thick, felted wool—likely traded from surface dwellers, as no grazing animals exist down here—oiled to repel the constant condensation. The fabric is dyed in the drab tones of their world: bruise-colored purples, charcoal greys, and deep mineral browns. Over time, these garments become calcified by the mineral-rich water dripping onto them, stiffening into a sort of soft armor that sparkles dully in the twilight.

Adornment is common, but it is born of the earth. A Lithos is rarely seen without their "sounding-pick"—a small hammer with a head of cold-forged iron on one side and a dense piece of raw amethyst on the other. They use these constantly, tapping the walls of their dwellings or the anchor points of bridges, listening to the pitch of the ring to judge the stress of the growing rock. Their jewelry consists of raw, unpolished chunks of crystal—smoky quartz pendants or bracelets of rough garnet—worn not for sparkle, but as talismans of the specific mineral vein their clan claims heritage to.

Mentally, the Lithos possess a geological patience. In a city where a new room might take a decade to carve, and a neighborhood might be lost to a slow-crushing crystal fissure over a lifetime, they do not rush. They are deeply observant, attuned to the imperceptible rhythms of their world. They know the Groan; they can distinguish the sharp crack of a cooling crystal from the deeper, resonant boom of a tectonic shift. Their language is rich in terms for rock texture, vibration, and the quality of light. They don't say "noon"; they say "when the high pillar strikes the western amethyst wall."

Their society is structured around the stability of rock. Clans are named after the geological features they inhabit—the Crystalline Terrace Folk, the Fissure-Dwellers, the Shaft-Watchers. Social standing is determined by one’s ability to interpret the earth. The "Listeners"—engineers and elders who dictate when a bridge needs slackening or when a tunnel must be abandoned before it collapses—hold the highest authority.

They are a quiet people. In a cavern that amplifies every sound, shouting is taboo. They speak in low murmurs, their voices roughened by the damp, particulate air. Their music is rhythmic and percussive, mimicking the *drip-drip* of water and the *clink* of chisel on stone.

The Lithos are parasites on a titan, and they know it. They exist in a state of reverent anxiety, acutely aware that the vibrant, growing womb that shelters them could, with a shudder of tectonic slowness, become their tomb. They live not just *in* the stone, but *with* it, bending their short, soft lives around the immortal, unyielding growth of the geode.
