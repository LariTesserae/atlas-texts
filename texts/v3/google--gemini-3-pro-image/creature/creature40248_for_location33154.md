What lives here is not born of flesh, for flesh would rot in the wet or shatter in the cold. Nor is it purely machine, for machines possess a logic of construction that these things lack. They are the tertiary effect of this environment, the moving components of the static loop.

They are the Vitruvian Scrawls.

They are immense, shambling accretions of the landscape itself. Imagine a creature coalesced from tangled rebar, hunks of weeping concrete, shorn girders, and thick, shivering mats of blue-black moss. They have no defined shape, no front or back, only a hulking, mountainous center of gravity that shunts itself along the endless industrial gratings. They can be the size of a freight train or the size of a refinery cracker, growing larger as they accumulate more detritus from the chasms.

They are painfully slow, moving with the grinding, tectonic inevitability of glaciers. When they move, they do not step; they drag. Multi-jointed limbs made of rusted conduit bundles and hydraulic pistons reach out, grip the architectural lattice, and haul their sodden bulk forward.

They are profoundly wet creatures. Water cascades off them in continuous sheets, just as it does from the buildings. The freezing mist clings to their iron flanks, building up layers of rime ice that crack and reform with every tortured movement. The lush, cold-tolerant vegetation—the oily purple ferns and house-sized fungal bulbs—grows directly upon their backs and flanks, making them look like walking chunks of the bruised ecosystem.

They have no eyes, for the flat gray light offers nothing worth seeing. Instead, they exist entirely within the realm of the deafening noise. They are sensory organs for vibration. The millions of shivering rivulets on their bodies act as a distributed nervous system, feeling the exact frequency of the planetary roar.

Their purpose is the maintenance of the equilibrium.

In a world of maximum exertion and zero progression, they are the antibodies that prevent change. If one of the skyscraper-sized pistons begins to groan at a slightly different pitch, a Scrawl will laboriously climb the tower to brace it with its own body, adding its weight and mass until the original frequency is restored. If a waterfall cascading from a cooling tower threatens to surge due to an influx of condensation, Scrawls will congregate at the lip, using their moss-choked bodies to act as living sluice gates, ensuring the water falls at the mathematically constant rate required by the stasis.

 They do not eat; they absorb the latent heat of the machinery through direct contact. They do not sleep; they merely pause, becoming indistinguishable from the heaps of industrial wreckage and rampant flora around them, until a deviation in the Great Noise summons them to move again.

They are the silent gardeners of the cacophony, the wet, shivering monstrosities that ensure the loud, frozen eternity never skips a beat.
