The scarcity of animal life here is not an absence, but an adaptation to perfection. In a world without seasons, without predators, and without scarcity of warmth or light, the kinetic energy of existence has unwound into near-absolute stillness. The creatures that inhabit this suspended ocean are few, and they are defined by their magnificent lethargy. They do not swim; they drift. They do not hunt; they absorb.

The most significant inhabitants reside deep within the woven hammocks of bulbous roots and iridescent velvet moss. They are the *Somniaflorae*, or "Sleep-weavers." It is terribly difficult to distinguish them from the architecture they tend. Imagine a creature the size of a human torso, but with no rigid skeleton, shaped roughly like a flattened, six-limbed starfish. Their bodies are composed of a dense, gelatinous cartilage, completely transparent save for a faint, internal lattice of milky-white nutrient veins.

The Sleep-weavers are blind. In this world of searing brightness, sight is unnecessary burden. Instead, their entire epidermis is hypersensitive to the minute shifts in water pressure and the tactile quality of the flora. They spend their lives anchored deep in the mossy webbing between the razor-sharp spires. Their movement is geological in its slowness; to perceive a single limb shifting its grip from one root strand to another would require hours of unwavering observation.

They are the secret gardeners of this realm. From porous pads on their undersides, they secrete a highly viscous, nutrient-rich gel that acts as both fertilizer for the iridescent mosses and a mortar binding the loose root systems to the obsidian shards. They are symbiotic nodes in the vast blanket of greenery, living in a state of perpetual, warm coma, nourished by the very plants they glue together. To touch one is to feel something cold and yielding, like firm agar jelly, which will only very slowly pulse in a mute, passive recoil.

Further out in the open, bright expanses between the kelp-redwoods, drift the entities previously glimpsed only as ghosts. They are the *Lucivora*—the light-eaters.

They are vastly larger than the Sleep-weavers, some reaching wingspans of forty feet or more, yet they possess almost no mass. They are essentially enormous, single-cell membranes, sheets of nearly invisible biological film suspended in the tepid water. They have no organs, no heads, no tails. They are living solar sails.

The Lucivora spend their existence unfurled in the intense, refracted sunlight, their bodies shimmering with the same electric blues and vivid greens as the light shafts around them. They feed directly upon this solar radiation and the dissolved minerals in the ultra-pure water. They possess no means of self-propulsion. They are at the mercy of the agonizingly weak thermal currents, drifting thousands of feet upward over the course of a "day," only to slowly sink again as they drift into cooler shadows.

If you were to approach a Lucivora, you would likely swim right through it without realizing. The membrane is so delicate it would tear apart around your body like wet tissue paper, only to slowly, painfully stitch itself back together over the coming weeks. They make no sound, they have no fear, and they possess no awareness other than the binary states of "light" and "dark." When the light is brightest, they spread wide, becoming almost two-dimensional. In the deeper, hazier regions below, they fold in on themselves like collapsed parachutes, becoming dense, gelatinous lumps that sink until they find warmth again.

Finally, there are the cleaners of the deep. Far below, where the light begins to fade into the pressurized haze and the water grows slightly cooler, the "snow" of dead plant matter—sloughed-off layers of giant kelp and decaying moss—slowly settles.

Here live the *Cyst-sponges*. They are not true sponges, but fungal-animal hybrids that anchor themselves to the naked, razor-sharp obsidian blades where the vegetation fails to take hold. They look like clusters of pale, bloated blisters clinging to the black rock. They are immobile detritivores. As the slow rain of decaying cellulose falls upon them, they secrete enzymes that liquefy the matter on contact, absorbing the nutrients through their porous outer membranes. They are the color of bruised cream, stark against the volcanic black glass, growing silently over centuries, performing the only act of destruction in a world of endless growth: slowly dissolving the dead so the living have room to choke the light once more.
