This is a place where humanity, or what has become of it, does not walk; it climbs.

They are a people shaped by verticality and crushing cold, a species whose entire existence is a precarious cling to the skin of a god. They call themselves the Resonants, perhaps, or simply the Climbers, but their identity is woven into the fibers of the world-plant.

Physically, they are compact and dense, bodies built to conserve heat and keep a low center of gravity against the dizzying drop. They are hunched, rarely standing fully upright, forever bracing against a sudden shift in the wind or a violent shudder of the stalk beneath them. Their limbs are disproportionately long and corded with muscle, joints articulate and capable of extreme angles.

Their hands and feet are their most arresting features. They are huge, splayed things. Fingers and toes are tipped not with simple nails, but with curved, keratinous talons, thick as bone chisels, designed to bite into icy bark and hook over crystalline ridges. The pads of their hands and feet are callous-leather, inches thick, rough as a rasp, allowing them to grip surfaces jagged enough to shred ordinary flesh. They move with a unsettling, spider-like fluidity, transitioning from a crawl to a swing to a dead-hang with unconscious grace.

They are wrapped in layers of insulation harvested from the ecosystem. Their base layer is a tight wrapping of woven fibers derived from the softer, inner cambium of dead secondary branches—a textile that is surprisingly soft but tough as hemp. Over this, they wear heavy, shaggy pelts taken from the six-limbed predators they compete with. These hides are cured in acrid, preserving s sp and smell faintly of pine resin and musk. They wear hoods pulled tight against the howling wind, and over their eyes, they strap goggles carved from the translucent, cartilaginous breastbones of the leather-winged gliders, protecting their vision from the freezing fog while tinting the indigo gloom a sickly yellow.

When they push back their hoods in the relative shelter of a crevice, their faces are lean, skin weathered to the color of dried tobacco leaf and wrinkled by perpetual squinting. Their noses are broad and flat, adapted to warming the thin, freezing air before it hits their lungs. Their eyes are enormous, dominating their faces—dark, reflective pools with pupils that dilate almost full-circle to capture the scant bioluminescence and the dim twilight from above. They rarely look straight ahead; their gaze is in constant, jittery motion, scanning the terrifying vertical distances above and below.

Their culture is one of transient anchors in a shifting world. They are semi-nomadic, moving when the section of the world-stalk they inhabit grows too unstable or when the local resources—sap pockets or fungal blooms—are exhausted.

Their tools are extensions of the plant world. They carry adzes and pry-bars made from fire-hardened splinter-thorns, used to hack away the crystalline ice or lever open plating bark to get at the nutritious, syrup-thick sap flowing beneath. They carry coils of rope braided from vascular fibers, stronger than any steel cable, blackened with frozen resin for grip. At their belts hang bone daggers fashioned from predator femurs, and pouches full of dried bioluminescent fungi which they use as dim, glowing breadcrumbs to mark paths through the chaotic braiding of the stalks.

They are a deeply quiet people in close quarters, having learned that shouting is useless against the roar of the wind and the groaning of the stalks. They communicate largely through a complex sign language of swift hand gestures and subtle shifts in body weight. When they must speak, their voices are low, resonant hums, pitched to travel beneath the ambient noise of the crashing ice cascades.

But their most defining characteristic is their relationship with sound and vibration. They are masters of listening to the organism they inhabit. Before crossing a vast gap on a swaying rope bridge, a Climber will press a palm flat against the "ground," closing their eyes. They are feeling for the deep thrum of the sap pumps, gauging the pressure. They can distinguish the terrifying *crrrr-ack* of structural failure from the harmless growing pains of a new tendril unfurling. They know the rhythm of the world's shifting weight. They live in the brief, breathless intervals between the plant’s seismic adjustments, forever waiting for the next shudder that might send them plunging into the absolute blackness below.
