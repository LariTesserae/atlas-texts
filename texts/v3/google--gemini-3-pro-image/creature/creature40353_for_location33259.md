To exist here is to be utterly consumed by the imperatives of heat and pressure. The things that move through this choking lattice are not distinct from the architecture; they are its mobile expressions, fauna forged from the same industrial genetic code that dictates the flora.

They are slow, deliberate creatures, built low and segmented for navigating tight, crushing spaces. You might mistake one for a tumorous growth on a metallic root until it detaches with a sound like grinding gears. Their carapaces are mosaics of salvaged materials—shards of the heat-exchanger leaves layered over plates of ceramic bone, fused together with rivulets of cooled slag. They are matte-black and mottled with oxidized rust, perfectly camouflaged against the dark, non-glowing elements of the jungle.

They have no eyes, for there is nothing to see. Instead, their faceted, armored heads are studded with pits lined with thermal receptors, allowing them to perceive the world as a complex topography of temperature gradients. To them, the dull maroon trunks are blazing highways, and the pulsing orange veins are rivers of sustenance. They navigate not by sight, but by following the deep, sub-bass vibrations that shudder through the floor, sensing the shifting stresses of the structure long before a buckle occurs.

Their locomotion is a hybrid of mechanics and biology. They move on multiple articulated struts that end in gripping claws made of conductive carbide, designed to puncture the iron-hard lignin for purchase. When they move, you do not hear footsteps; you hear the faint hydraulic hiss of high-temperature fluids shifting in flexible metallic hoses that serve as tendons, and the muffled *clack-clack* of ceramic plates sliding over one another. They do not breathe the scorching air; rather, they have dorsal vents—rows of thin, ribbed radiator fins along their spines—that shimmer with heat haze as they regulate their internal reactors.

These entities are grazers and parasites on the colossal body of the furnace-world. They seek out the glowing veins of non-aqueous coolant beneath the mica-bark. Using diamond-tipped proboscises, they bore into the conduits, latching on like industrial ticks to siphon off a portion of the pressurized molten salts or hydrocarbons. This liquid fire is their blood, their energy source, circulating through them before being returned, slightly cooler, to the system, or vented as waste heat.

They are solitary, territorial things. When two meet in a narrow girder-pass, there is no swift violence. There is a slow, agonizing shoving match, a grinding of armored bodies where the victor is the one whose internal hydraulics can withstand the greater PSI, forcing the other to retract into a crevice.

Occasionally, the system spasms. A blister on a branch-pipe bursts, spraying quick-hardening foam. These creatures are the first to react to the change in vibration. They swarm the breach, not to repair it, but to feast on the raw, exposed energy and the mineral-rich foam before it sets. In these moments, their camouflage fails, and their internal temperatures spike with exertion, making them glow brightly against the dark—scuttling constellations of angry orange light fighting over a fresh wound in the world's hot heart.
