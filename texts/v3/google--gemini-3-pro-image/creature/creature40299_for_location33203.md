The primary residents of this ossified metropolis are not merely inhabitants; they are the living keystones of the dead architecture. They are the Anchorites of the Deep Thrum.

At first glance—if a glance were possible in the crushing gloom—they are indistinguishable from the structure itself. In the vastest naves of the sunken cathedral, where the calcified arches soar miles high, these beings cling to the junction points like monstrous, parasitic tumors of iron and cellulose. They are immense, colossal entities whose scale rivals the architecture they inhabit, often spanning thousands of feet across their flattened, limpet-like bodies.

Their outer carapace is not shell or bone, but a biologically generated mimicry of the city itself. Over millennia, they secrete and adhere layers of the same calcified kelp and hard cellulose that form the surrounding lattice, cementing themselves into the environment. They are craggy, textured mountains of dark, hardened matter, covered in the same sickly-green bioluminescent moss that outlines the rest of the city. You could drift past one of these sleeping leviathans a dozen times, mistaking its vast, gently curving flank for just another sprawling buttress of the ancient plant-growth.

It is only when they feed that they reveal themselves as distinct from the dead city.

They are sessile apex filter-feeders on a planetary scale. They do not hunt the ribbon-eels or chase the clouds of "living snow." Instead, they wait. Positioned at major intersections where the thick, unwilling water is forced through structural bottlenecks, they slowly, glacially, open.

A fissure appears in the craggy facade of what looked like a solid wall. The fissure widens, revealing an interior landscape of terrifying biology. Inside, there is no delicate pink flesh; everything is engineered for crushing pressure. Their interiors are a dense, fibrous lattice of dark purple muscle and cartilage, thick as hawser cables, throbbing with a slowness that borders on geological time.

From this opening, they unfurl vast, translucent sheets of sensory netting—membranes thinner than silk but stronger than woven steel—that drift out into the black water like ghostly auroras, spanning miles. These nets catch everything: the microbial mats, the gelatinous grazers, the unsuspecting eel-things. Once laden, the nets are slowly retracted into the armored maw, the fissure seals, and the digestion process begins, lasting perhaps a century.

But their primary connection to this world is not food; it is sound.

The Anchorites are the living receptors of the eternal tectonic groan. Their entire physiology is evolved to interpret the bass vibration that permeates the city. Their calcified shells are fused directly into the structural lattice, acting as biological tuning forks. The thrumming of the planet’s stress travels through the dead plant-beams and directly into their bodies.

They do not hear this sound; they physically embody it. The vibration keeps their dense fluids flowing in an environment where water wants to be solid. It is their heartbeat, their clock, and their means of perception. They communicate not with light or chemical trails, but by subtly altering their own density to modulate the vibration passing through them—sending ultra-low frequency shifts back into the lattice, a slow-motion language of seismic echoes felt by others of their kind hundreds of miles away across the dark plain. They are the silent, brooding consciousness of the frozen city, dreaming in heavy, monotonous tones generated by the shifting crust of the earth.
