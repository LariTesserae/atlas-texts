This is an ecosystem founded not on sunlight, but on patience and the slow, chemical respiration of the drowning stone itself. The life here is defined by what it lacks: sight, speed, and urgency.

The primary inhabitants, the architects of the living wallpaper, are the **Lithic Tubeworms** and the **Copper-Mantle Bladders**.

The Tubeworms are the foundation layer. Over millennia, they have secreted tubes of dark, iron-rich calcite that are almost indistinguishable from the surrounding eroded basalt. They grow in chaotic, intertwining reefs, cementing themselves over the ancient carvings, turning the faces of forgotten gods into bulbous, stony excrescences. From the tops of these chimneys emerge fans of stiff, leathery feeding tendrils, dark grey and coated in mucus, which comb the tepid water for microscopic bacteria thriving on invisible mineral vents deeper in the structure. Their bioluminescence is the "fading copper" mentioned—a dim, reddish-gold glow located at the base of their feeding fans, pulsing only once every few minutes in a slow, metabolic throb.

Growing atop and between these stony tubes are the Copper-Mantle Bladders. These are soft, sessile organisms, ranging from the size of a fist to a human head. They have no hard shell, only a thick, textured dermis that feels like waterlogged suede. They are sacs of chemosynthetic bacteria, living filters that process the sulfate-heavy water. They are the source of the "deep indigo" light. The light doesn't shine outward; it suffuses their entire gelatinous bodies, making them look like bruised, glowing organs pinned to the walls. When touched, they do not retract quickly, but slowly deflate with a sighing expulsion of water, taking hours to regain their shape.

The open water is thick with **Membrane-Jellies** and **Blind Ribbon-Eels**.

The Membrane-Jellies are unlike their oceanic cousins. They are not transparent or fragile. They are constructed of thick, opaque, proteinaceous gristle, coloured a mottled slate-grey to match the darkness. They range from dinner-plate size to immense, sluggish shield-shapes three feet across. They have no stinging tentacles, only short, stubby oral arms that grapple with the bacterial mats sloughing off the walls. They move with agonizing slowness, a single contraction of their heavy bells propelling them only inches before they drift again. They are utterly silent, bumping against obstacles with the resilient thud of wet rubber.

Navigating between the jellies are the Blind Ribbon-Eels. These creatures are immensely long—some stretching ten feet or more—but incredibly thin, like flattened strips of raw muscle. They are utterly eyeless. Their entire bodies are covered in microscopic sensory cilia that taste the water and feel pressure waves. They move in hypnotic, perpetual undulations, never resting, their countless vestigial fins rippling along their flanks like a breeze over velvet. They are scavengers, their jawless, sucker-like mouths adapted for rasping away dead matter from the tubeworm reefs or consuming dying jellies. They are the source of the constant, silent jostling; they wrap around everything, sliding over your skin with a cool, indifferent friction, tasting you and moving on.

Finally, there are the **Basalt Grazers**, the giants of this submerged world, and the source of the rare chitinous clicks. These are colossal, flattened arthropods, resembling horseshoe crabs crossed with woodlice, growing up to four feet in diameter. Their segmented dorsal plates are so heavily mineralized and encrusted with dormant tubeworms that they look exactly like detached paving stones come to life. They are the gardeners of this crypt. They move with glacial deliberation along the walls and floors, scraping away the accumulated layers of tubeworms and bladders with heavy, shovel-like mandibles. A single Grazer might spend a century clearing one wall of a chamber, leaving behind a track of relatively "fresh," scarred basalt that will take another millennium to be recolonized. When they move, their heavy plates grind against each other and the stone floor, creating that muted, quickly dampened *click* that is the loudest sound in the drowned citadel.

All these creatures share a common physiology dictated by the environment: slow metabolisms, extreme longevity, and a complete lack of fear or aggression. In this warm, crowded dark, there are no predators, only recyclers of the eternal, drifting biomass.
