This is a place hostile to lungs that require open air and limbs that need space to swing. Consequently, the things that live here are not animals in any recognizable sense. They are interstitial creatures, born of the uncomfortable borderland where the aggressive botany meets the stubborn machinery. They do not inhabit the hollow; they infest its seam lines.

The most obvious inhabitants are the **Pipe-Wraiths**. At first glance, they appear to be just another variation of the thick, parasitic vines choking the ironworks. But they are motile, if glacially slow. They are pale, translucent ropes of organized fungal matter, varying in thickness from a finger to a human thigh. They do not grow randomly; they seek out heat and vibration.

You will see them coiled tightly around the functioning steam-pipes, their pale flesh pulsing slowly in time with the subterranean thrumming. They feed on the thermal energy leaking from the corroded metal. Over decades, they have integrated themselves into the machinery. Their outer layers have hardened into a chitinous, heat-resistant bark, while their inner filaments have wormed into rivet holes and flange joints, acting as living gaskets that seal leaks with viscous, fibrous slime. When a valve shrieks with escaping steam, it is often because a Pipe-Wraith has shifted its grip, momentarily breaking the seal it provides. They are the unwitting custodians of the pressure, keeping the ancient boilers from rupturing by absorbing the excess heat and plugging the gaps with their own bodies.

Crawling painstakingly across the squelching root-floor are the **Copper-Slugs**. These are massive gastropods, easily two feet long, flattened so they can squeeze between the tightly woven architectural roots. They have no shells; instead, their backs are armored with a patchwork mosaic of scavenged materials—shards of porcelain insulators, flakes of rust-pitted iron, and bits of oxidized copper wire, all cemented together by a conductive mucus.

They are the eaters of the interface. They slide along the exposed, decaying wires where the rubber insulation has rotted away, feeding on the verdigris and the electrical leakage. As they move, their conductive slime trail occasionally bridges the gap between a live wire and a wet root, causing sudden, brilliant sparks of blue light to sizzle in the chartreuse gloom, momentarily illuminating the undergrowth before the circuit shorts out.

Smaller, yet far more numerous, are the **Rust-Mites**. The air is so thick with them that their collective movement looks like a shimmering, orange-brown heat haze over the metal surfaces. They are near-microscopic chemo-synthetic arthropods that do not breathe oxygen; they breathe corrosion.

They swarm in thick crusts over the "iron tendons" and the sides of the old boilers. They are constantly eating the metal, accelerating its decay, but immediately excreting a binding, cement-like waste product that actually reinforces the structure it’s destroying. They are slowly replacing the original steel with a biogenic concrete of their own making. If a massive gear turns once a week, tearing a scar through the moss, the screech of metal on metal is muffled because the surfaces are coated in millions of these tiny bodies, which act as a sacrificial, dry lubricant, crushed into an oily orange paste by the immense pressure.

Nothing here moves quickly. To move fast is to thrash against the suffocating density of the air and the vegetation. The life here is patient, relentless, and partially metallic. It doesn't hunt; it absorbs. It doesn't run; it grows over.
