The beings who claim this sprawling, photosynthetic underworld call themselves the Arboreals, though they are far removed from any tree that ever saw the sun. They are the stewards of the root-titan, a species evolved not merely to survive in this damp, deafening kiln, but to become an intrinsic part of its industrial biology.

They are tall and powerfully built, though their height is often hunched against the oppressive weight of the atmosphere and the low-hanging vine-conduits. They seem less like flesh and blood and more like animated extensions of the architecture itself. Their "skin" is a mantle of polished, nodular lignum, the same hardened plant matter as the walls, ranging in color from the pale cream of fresh heartwood to the deep, burnished mahogany of old growth. It sweats a constant, slick resin that fills the air with that sweet, musky scent and protects them from the corrosive steam blasts.

They have no hair. Their heads are smooth, elongated domes, flowing down into thick, fibrous necks that merge seamlessly with broad shoulders. Where ears should be, there are instead intricate whorls of sensitive, membrane-thin bark, capable of filtering the deafening roar of the machinery to detect the subtle shifts in vibration that signal a change in sap pressure or a misaligned piston gear.

Their eyes are massive, spanning nearly half their face—orbs of reflective, milky jade designed to amplify the weak bioluminescence of the fungi farms. They rarely blink, staring with an intense, unsettling stillness that belies the frantic energy of their work.

The Arboreals do not walk so much as they flow along the spindly walkways. Their limbs are long, sinewy bundles of muscle that resemble twisted vines, ending in four-digited hands and feet tipped with curved, incredibly dense claws made of darkened keratin. These claws allow them to grip the slick, sweating railings and scale the sheer faces of the groaning wooden pistons with the ease of the skittering mammals beneath them.

They wear very little, for the heat is intense. What clothing they possess is strictly functional, crafted from the discarded elements of their ecosystem. Shoulders and chests are often draped in asymmetrical pauldrons made from the iridescent, amber-colored chitin plates of the giant scavenge-insects, bound together with braided tendrils. These serve as blast shields against sudden ruptures in the steam lines. Around their waists hang utility belts fashioned from cured stalk-leather, holding tools that are grown rather than made: wrenches shaped from naturally hardened ironwood knots, and siphoning tubes made from the severed, muscular esophagi of larger root-parasites.

Because the noise of the refinery is an all-consuming wall of sound, spoken language is non-existent. The Arboreals communicate through a complex, rapid-fire sign language of gestures and touches, supplemented by percussive sounds. They strike their own resonant, woody chests or hammer rhythmically on the hollow railing-pipes to transmit signals across the chasms. A sharp *clack-clack-thrum* against a walkway might order a pressure release; a low, scraping sound against a piston casing might signal a fungal infestation needs clearing.

They are a tireless people, moving with synchronized purpose amidst the chaos. One might see a team of three suspended by vine-ropes over a yawning black void, manually lubricating a massive botanical gear-joint with gobs of harvested amber slime. Another group might be found near the orange flare of the tuber furnaces, their resinous skin gleaming like polished bronze as they shovel fuel into the fire, their movements rhythmic and relentless as the pistons they tend.

They are not just inhabitants; they are the titan’s antibodies and its engineers. They maintain the flow, harvest the energy, and prune the excess growth. In the sickly green half-light, amidst the roar of steam and insect, the Arboreals look less like individuals and more like mobile components of the vast, living machine they serve.
