This is a place where humanity has been sculpted by hypobaric cold and eternal damp. The people here are not merely inhabitants of the cliffs; they are an extension of the geology, as rooted and patient as the gnarled trees that grip the fissures.

Physically, they are built for conservation. They are short of stature but immensely broad. Their bodies are defined by barrel-shaped chests, expanded ribcages housing enormous lungs adapted to wring oxygen from the thin, biting air. This physiology gives them a top-heavy, solid appearance, anchored by thick, short legs and wide, splayed feet whose soles are as tough and insensate as cured leather, gripping the slick slate with preternatural assurance.

Their skin is a testament to the environment. It is thick, porous, and deeply weathered, permanently ruddy from the cold and the high-altitude ultraviolet exposure. It has a distinct, slightly waxy sheen, a natural secretion that repels the ceaseless drizzle, smelling faintly of mineral dust and raw wool. Their faces are broad and flat, with wide nostrils that flare to maximize air intake, and deep-set eyes habitually narrowed against the unrelenting, clear light.

You rarely see their actual shapes, however. They are swaddled in bulky layers of shaggy goat-antelope hides, fur turned inward against the skin, the outer leather smoked and oiled to a dark, waterproof mahogany. These heavy garments obscure gender and age, making every figure seem like a slow-moving boulder of fur and hide. When they stop moving against a dark cliff face, they simply disappear, blending perfectly with the moss and wet stone.

Their movements are the antithesis of haste. In this oxygen-poor environment, rapid exertion is a death sentence. Therefore, they move with a glacial, deliberate fluidity. Every step on a precarious ledge is calculated; every pull on a suspension bridge rope is measured. There is no wasted energy, no sudden gesticulation. Watching them navigate their vertical city is like watching sap flow—slow, relentless, and undeniable.

This physical economy extends to their temperament. They possess a profound, unsettling stillness. Living in a world devoid of seasons or significant weather fluctuations has bred a psyche entirely lacking in urgency. They are masters of a patience that modern minds would find maddening. A stone-carver might spend three days shaping a single corbel for a terrace wall, tapping away with a bone chisel in a rhythm that matches the dripping water. They do not seek to conquer the sheer faces but to integrate with them, painfully slowly, over generations.

Their culture is one of quiet isolation punctuated by necessary communal effort. Because their dwellings are spaced so far apart along the canyon walls, they are comfortable with long stretches of solitude, tending their hanging tuber gardens or mending nets in the misty silence of a cave mouth. Yet, the maintenance of the staggering infrastructure—the colossal vine bridges and the aqueducts—requires complex cooperation. They achieve this with minimal verbal communication.

When they do speak, their voices are startlingly deep, resonating in those massive chests. The language is guttural and hushed, a series of low rumbles and clicks designed to carry short distances without breaking the prevailing quiet. They communicate as much through deliberate pauses and subtle shifts in weight as they do with words. A nod, a slow gesture of a calloused, slate-stained hand, is often sufficient to convey complex instructions regarding structural integrity or water flow.

They are a people held in the same breathless equilibrium as their city. They are not waiting for something to happen; they are maintaining what *is*. They are the human expression of the weeping rock—enduring, saturated, slow, and infinitely resilient.
