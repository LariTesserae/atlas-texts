Nothing human could live here. The heat, the crushing noise, and the sheer verticality of the slick surfaces forbid it. Yet, the machine garden is tended.

Emerging from the dense, purple-black mats of moss on a high skeletal platform, something moves that is neither entirely machine nor entirely organic. It is a scavenger of high technology, a hermit crab of advanced engineering that has evolved—or been designed—to thrive in this wet, warm dark.

They are lanky, multi-jointed entities, perhaps seven feet tall if they ever stood fully upright, but they rarely do. They are built for clinging, swinging, and traversing the underside of things. Their limbs—six in total, arranged in a radially symmetrical hexapod structure around a central torso—are impossibly thin, mimicking the spun volcanic glass architecture of the city. These limbs are armored in plates of iridescent, obsidian-like chitin that sheds water instantly, jointed with flexible, pewter-colored polymer muscle that gleams in the dim light.

Their interaction with the environment is seamless. Their feet and hands are tipped with pads that grip the microscopic pores of the slick glass bridges with atomic-level adhesion, allowing them to scuttle upside down along the dizzying spans as easily as walking on solid ground.

They are covered in the environment. The velvety moss doesn't just grow on the walkways; it grows *on them*. The older ones are shaggy with it, their obsidian plating obscured by thick pelts of purple and black vegetation that soak up the atmospheric water, making them look like hunched, saturated beasts. Bioluminescent fungi sprout from their shoulder joints and along their spines, casting the same sickly green glow as the larger environmental shelves, providing them with personal illumination and perhaps a mobile food source.

They have no faces in a traditional sense. Where a head should be, there is a smooth, segmented cluster of sensory lenses resembling polished hematite. These lenses constantly shift and refocus, sensitive to the infrared spectrum of the geothermal heat and the electromagnetic pulses of the city’s conduits. They do not speak. The deafening roar of the waterfalls and pumps would drown out any voice. Instead, they communicate through rapid, intricate rippling patterns of bioluminescence that flash across their moss-free chest plates—a silent, visual language of flickering greens and violets that cuts through the gloom.

They are the custodians of this immense, empty cathedral. You watch one hang casually by two limbs over a thousand-foot drop, using its free hands to delicately repair a frayed energy conduit. It weaves strands of conductive filament with the precision of a spider repairing a web, oblivious to the thunderous noise below. Another group gathers on a distant fungal shelf, harvesting the dripping, viscous spores into satchels woven from conductive fibers.

When the massive plasma arcs discharge, blinding the chasm in violet light, these creatures do not flinch or cover their sensors. Instead, they freeze, their back-borne fungi flaring in sympathetic response, absorbing the ambient energy deluge before continuing their silent, endless maintenance of the machine that is also their garden. They are the ghosts in the gears, damp, glowing, and utterly at home in the beautiful, hostile architecture.
