The beings that inhabit this sprawling, unstable machine-scape are less residents and more a constituent part of the churning apparatus. They are heavy, slow-moving figures, indistinct in the perpetual gloom, their silhouettes bulky with layers of necessary protection.

They call themselves the Anchorites, though whether this is a title of honor or a grim descriptor of their function is unclear. To survive the biting cold and clinging damp, they are swaddled in thick, felted wools oiled to repel moisture, and rough leathers cured in the acrid smoke of the foundries. Over time, these garments have become impregnated with the rust of the city, turning them the same bleeding orange as the riveted iron beams they traverse. Iridescent moss frequently takes root in the seams of their clothing, making them seem semi-organic extensions of the cliff faces.

Their physiology, or perhaps just their posture, has been warped by the environment. They possess an unsettling center of gravity, a low-slung stoutness developed to counter the vertigo-inducing heights and the unpredictable gravitational anomalies. They do not walk so much as trudge, planting iron-shod boots with deliberate, magnetic heaviness onto the vibrating metal catwalks.

It is rare to see an Anchorite’s face. They wear complex, bulky headgear—part respirator to filter the oily vapor, part sensory deprivation hood. Their eyes are hidden behind thick, multi-layered lenses of smoked glass that perpetually weep with external condensation. These goggles are not meant to help them see clearly; seeing clearly in a place where geometry shifts is a liability. Instead, the lenses fracture vision further, breaking the world into manageable, isolated segments, preventing the mind-shattering realization of the sheer drop beneath or the sight of a neighboring district tilting impossibly on its axis.

They rely less on sight and more on the conduction of sound through bone. The deep tectonic groan of the emerging earth and the rhythmic thrum of the pile drivers are their true north. By pressing a gloved hand against a vibrating girder, an Anchorite can "read" the structural integrity of a bridge or sense an oncoming reality-tremor before vision begins to scramble.

Many are modified. Life expectancy up here is short for the unaugmented. You will see limbs reinforced with external bracing of crude steel, pistons pulsating with steam assisting a bicep in lifting a heavy crate. These are not elegant cybernetics; they are brutal, rivet-and-boiler-plate repairs, often leaking hydraulic fluid that trails behind them like dark blood. Some have rusted grappling hooks integrated into their forearms—a final, desperate contingency against the call of the void.

Their demeanor is one of profound, dissociated numbness. The "fatalistic slow-motion" noted in the workers is a survival mechanism. To acknowledge the impossibility of their existence is to invite madness. When a wrench passes ghost-like through a bolt, an Anchorite does not curse or recoil. They simply pause, breathing evenly through their blackened filters, waiting for the localized flicker in causality to stabilize, before attempting the turn again. They have learned to synchronize their own labor with the stuttering rhythm of a broken reality. They are the ghosts in the machine, heavy and rusting, endlessly turning gears for a purpose they have forgotten, in a world that is trying to shake them off.
