The beings who inhabit this suspended amphitheater are a mournful reflection of the granite itself. They are few, a scattered handful occupying a space meant for hundreds, and their presence is so unobtrusive that one might overlook them entirely against the immense grey backdrop.

They are humanoid but distinctly shaped by the crushing altitude and the cold. They are short and immensely broad, possessing barrel-like chests that house capacious lungs evolved to distill sustenance from the thin air. Their limbs are thick and sturdy, ending in broad hands and feet calloused to the texture of coarse-grit sandpaper, allowing them to grip the freezing rock with absolute certainty.

Their skin is thick, seemingly porous, and pale—a hue somewhere between old bone and glacier ice—weathered into deep fissures and folds that mirror the cracked cliff faces surrounding them. Into these crevices, the environment has encroached; faint washes of green algae and dustings of grey lichen cling to the ridges of their brows and the backs of their hands, making them appear almost mineralized when still. Their eyes are enormous, limpid pools of black, lacking discernible whites or irises, seemingly engineered to absorb the maximum amount of the cool, unyielding light. They reflect the world with the same ripple-less perfection as the obsidian basins on the valley floor.

They clothe themselves in heavy, felted wools derived from some unseen herd, dyed in the muted tones of the valley: moss greens, lichen rusts, and stone greys. Over these layers, they wear thick aprons or mantles of cured leather, oiled against the pervasive dampness, fastened with simple clasps of hammered bronze that have developed a dark green patina.

Their movement is the defining characteristic of their existence. They do not hurry. In this thin air, haste is an extravagance. Every action is deliberate, calculated to conserve energy. They move with a hydraulic slowness, a measured placing of one foot before the other upon the paved walkways and the carved staircases. To watch one cross a wide expanse of glacier-worn rock is to watch the slow creep of a shadow as the sun shifts.

They are a people of profound silence. They rarely vocalize. When they do, it is in sounds so low they seem to emanate from the diaphragm rather than the throat—deep, resonant thrums and vibrations that travel through the stone they stand upon more effectively than through the air. It is a language of seismic tremors, felt in the bones rather than heard by the ear.

Their lives are dedicated to the maintenance of the frozen equilibrium. They are the curators of this stone museum. With infinite patience, they use small bronze trowels to clear silt from the drainage channels, ensuring the black seepage water flows smoothly into the cisterns. They inspect the massive dry-stone walls, searching for microscopic shifts in the granite blocks, tapping them back into perfect alignment with heavy iron hammers wrapped in leather to muffle the sound. They tend the patches of emerald moss as if they were prize gardens, misting them with water drawn from the basins.

There is no laughter here, no weeping, no signs of play or commerce. There are only the slow, cyclical rituals of preservation. They exist in a state of solemn waiting, occupants of a vast, silent monastery built of bedrock, tending a shrine whose original deity has long since departed, leaving them alone with the stone, the cold, and the eternal quiet.
