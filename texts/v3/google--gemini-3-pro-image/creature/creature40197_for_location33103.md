The dominant impression of life here is one of overwhelming, glistening friction. In the absence of sunlight and photosynthesis, the entire biological lattice is powered by geothermal vents, waste heat from the colossal machinery, and the endless cascade of organic slurry.

The street-level scavengers are not merely present; they constitute a shifting, living pavement. The pale isopods, segmented and curved like armored bellows, are the color of bone soaked in dirty water. They move in literal waves over the rusted iron grating, millions of tiny legs creating a localized susurrus that underlies the greater mechanical thrum. Their larger cousins, the dog-sized crabs, are brutish tank-creatures. Their carapaces are not naturally colored, but are stained a deep, oily brownish-black, coated in the same grime that slicks the buildings. They don't just scuttle; they clatter heavy-footedly against the metal walkways, their pincers scarred and chipped from prying open corroded access panels to get at the sludge beneath. They fight over particularly rich veins of runoff with vicious, silent intensity, tearing each other apart in brawls that leave slick smears of blue-black hemolymph on the wet stones, which are immediately consumed by the smaller isopods.

Above them, the leathery aviators claim the humid canopy. They are heat-seekers. They cluster in massive, trembling stalactites directly over the venting smokestacks and the uninsulated bundles of steam piping, their hairless bodies soaking up the thermal exhaust. Their skin is a translucent, vascular gray, stretched taut over brittle finger-bones. With no trees to perch on, they cling to crumbling gargoyles and rusted girders with hooked claws. Their flight is jerky and spasmodic; they drop like stones through the haze to snatch a scavenger from the street, their tooth-filled maws snapping shut before laboriously flapping back up into the warmer strata of the air. Their constant, high-pitched clicking is a sonar net blanketed over the city, guiding them through the eternal fog.

In the deep, open gutter-canals, life is blind and muscular. The eel-things are pure predators of the dark water. They are thick as a man's thigh, segmented like high-tension conduit, and coated in a thick, protective mucus that allows them to glide through water toxic with heavy metals and petroleum byproducts. They have no eyes—eyes are useless in the opaque, iridescent sludge—but rely on rows of sensory pits along their flanks that detect the vibration of a careless crab slipping on the canal edge. When they strike, it is an eruption of oily water and needle-teeth, dragging prey down into the warm, churning depths where huge sub-aquatic impellers slowly turn.

And then, there are the maintainers of the machines.

Emerging from the steam-fogged doorways of the squat buildings are the sentient inhabitants of the plateau. They are bulky figures, broadened not necessarily by muscle, but by layers of protective gear necessary for survival in the weeping city. Their clothing is heavy leather treated with wax and grease until it is stiff as slate, stained the color of soot and rust. Many wear thick, vulcanized rubber aprons and heavy boots with iron cleats to grip the perpetually slime-coated ground.

Their faces are rarely seen uncovered. The air is too thick, too chemically dense for easy breathing. Most wear filtration masks of brass and oiled canvas, with glass eyepieces perpetually fogged with condensation. The skin that is visible—at the neck or wrists—is pasty, blanched like something found under a rock, sweating profusely in the humid warmth. They move with a slow, deliberate plodding gait, synchronized to the rhythmic grinding of the tectonic machinery beneath their feet. They do not seem to speak; perhaps their voices cannot compete with the relentless industrial din. Instead, they communicate with heavy gestures of gloved hands, or solemn nods.

They are the tenders of the great pistons, the oilers of the house-sized flywheels, the cleaners of the clogged grates. They carry heavy wrenches, coiled hoses, and buckets of solvent. They seem less like masters of this metallic world and more like specialized symbiotes, an industrial servitor species evolved—or engineered—to ensure the great, damp mechanism continues its endless, slow churning. They trudge through the masses of scattering isopods without looking down, a higher tier of the same grim, wet ecosystem.
