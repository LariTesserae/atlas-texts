The prompt explicitly states that there are no animals here, for the noise would shatter them. And indeed, biological flesh, with its delicate eardrums and watery cellular structure, could not exist in the Foundry of the Deep Basin. Life here has had to find another way, evolving not from carbon soup, but arising directly from the ferrous substrate and the ceaseless, deafening energy of the churn.

They are the Ferromorphs, the slag-children of the basin.

They are not built; they have accumulated. They are ambulatory conglomerations of the basin’s geology—massive, hulking entities formed from compacted engine blocks, fused gears, and strata of crushed plating that have achieved a crude, grinding sentience. Like the landscape itself, their individual components are unrecognizable, worn smooth by centuries of friction into bulbous, heavy carapaces of polished iron and streaked rust. They look like gargantuan tumbled stones, ranging in size from a delivery truck to a small locomotive, humped and uneven.

They move with agonizing slowness across the slick, uneven floor on multiple, stumpy limbs that are less like legs and more like articulated pillars of magnetized scrap. Their passage is marked by a terrible screeching sound, a harmonious counterpoint to the basin’s roar, as their heavy iron feet slide and grip the slippery plating, kicking up sprays of greasy water and showering sparks that die instantly in the damp air.

They have no eyes, for sight is useless in the steam-choked twilight. Instead, their entire being is a seismic receptor. Deep within their cores, armored by feet of compressed metal, heavy pendulums swing in oil baths, interpreting the tectonic vibrations of the Foundry. They "see" the rhythmic thrum of the building-sized pistons as a landscape of pulsing waves; they feel the sudden, blinding arc flashes as searing spikes of electrical potential on their metallic skin.

They are deaf in the conventional sense, having no need to filter sound when they are creatures born of resonance. They communicate by adding to the din. They slam heavy, hammer-like appendages against their own hollowed-out chest cavities or strike the floor plates in complex percussive codes—a language of clangs and thuds that travels through the ground, readable only by others anchored to the same vibrating foundation.

Their ecology is dependent on the waste energy of the great machines. They are parasites of current and heat. Herds of smaller Ferromorphs cluster around the bases of the towering smokestacks, pressing their bodies against the hot metal to absorb thermal energy, their carapaces steaming in the frigid drizzle. Others, bolder and more heavily armored, lumber toward the blinding blue-white flashes of the distant arc welding phenomena. They bathe in the electrical discharge, siphoning runaway voltage through copper-wire tendrils that sprout from their joints like stiff, conductive hair. They graze on the crystalline rust flowers and spun-copper vines in the deep shadows, grinding the metallic flora between jaws made of interleaved gears.

They are the mindless tenders of this mindless world. Their existence is a closed loop of maintenance that serves no ultimate purpose. A Ferromorph might spend a century slowly migrating to a jammed section of the kilometers-wide conveyor belt, only to spend another century crudely hammering a bent girder back into place before turning around. They weld cracks in the floor with discharged internal heat; they clear sludge from trough intakes. They do not build; they merely drag out the inevitable decay. They are the white blood cells of a dead mechanical god, forever circulating, forever repairing, deaf and blind, grinding onward in the cold, wet dark.
