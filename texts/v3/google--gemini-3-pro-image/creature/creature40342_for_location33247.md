The things that live here do not occupy space so much as they cling to its margins. They are creatures of adhesion, grip, and infinite patience, their entire existence defined by the terrible polarity of the vertical wall versus the empty void.

Primary among them are the architects of those rotting fiber bridges, a people who have become as sodden and stubborn as the moss they live upon. They are short, physically dense beings, built with barrel chests to process the thin, watery air, and limbs thicker than their height would suggest. Their skin has lost whatever pigment it once had, bleached to the translucent grey of a cave fish by perpetual cloud-cover and cold steam. It is hideously wrinkled, permanently prune-like from constant immersion, and coated in a natural, rancid oil that beads the water.

They are silent. Speech is futile against the throbbing roar of the wind tunnel. Instead, they communicate through the stone itself. They carry small hammers made of the hardest, densest river stones, tapping out complex, low-frequency codes directly onto the granite faces—vibrations that can be felt through fingertips and bare soles hundreds of feet away.

To watch them move is to understand vertigo tamed by necessity. They do not climb with agile grace; they ascend with agonizing, terrifying slowness. Their hands and feet are oversized, the pads calloused into a substance resembling rough hoof-horn, fitted with hooked, keratinous nails that can punch through the thick moss mat and find purchase in the slick rock beneath. They traverse the slime-covered humps of the cathedral walls like methodical insects, secured by ropes woven from the tough, rubbery veins of the cliff-side ferns. They are the tenders of the rotting bridges, constantly re-weaving the precarious spans that cross the abysses, their lives depending on fiber knots tied with numb, swollen fingers in a gale.

They share the ledges with the prey they slowly farm. The giant salamanders, sleek and black as crude oil, are herded into the deeper, wetter bowl-depressions, kept penned by low walls of stacked stone. The people eat their jelly-like eggs and harvest the thick slime from their backs, using it to waterproof their own bodies and the fibers of their ropes.

Yet, these moss-people are not the masters of the chimney. They are merely its most stubborn residents. When the "pale flash" occurs in the mist, they flatten themselves against the dripping walls and cease their tapping.

The apex predator here is the Fog-Leopard—a misnomer, for it is reptilian, or something older. It is the creature built for the "empty volume." Its skin is a pebbled, leathery white, almost invisible against the swirling nebulae of mist. It is lean, built of whipcord muscle and hollow bone. It does not climb; it bounds. It has evolved to treat the opposing walls of the fissure not as barriers, but as stepping stones.

It hunts by launching itself across the twenty or thirty-foot gaps of churning air. For a terrifying second, it is a pale missile suspended in the void, before landing with impossible precision on the opposite wall. Its four paws are equipped with retractable clusters of obsidian-sharp spurs that act like ice axes, biting instantly into the moss and geology, arresting its momentum in a shower of water and shredded vegetation. It snaps up the sluggish salamanders, or occasionally a careless moss-farmer, before launching itself back into the updraft to vanish on the other side.

Finally, there are the things that live solely in the chaos of the backward waterfalls. Where the gale hits the descending water with enough force to blow it upward into swirling geysers, strange, semi-translucent organisms thrive. They look like solidified smoke, delicate lattice-works of cartilage that anchor themselves into the very eye of the aerodynamic storm. They are filter-feeders on a massive scale, spreading wide, shimmering nets in the confused air to catch the stunned insects, the torn bits of moss, and the aerial plankton suspended in the upward-raining water. They vibrate intensely in the wind, a shivering, ghostly forest that only exists where the water flows the wrong way, feeding on the confusion of the elements.
