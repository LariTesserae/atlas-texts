This is a world where life has calcified. The inhabitants of this continent-spanning reef are not mere occupants; they are simultaneously its architects, its custodians, and its eventual recycled building material. They are creatures of dry chemistry, their biology a mirror of the volatile mineral circulatory system that feeds the land beneath them.

They are arthropoid in general structure, but their composition is closer to animated geology. Their exoskeletons are not merely chitinous; they are mineral-reinforced composite shells, matte and dusty, colored in the precise burnt umbers, deep ochres, and faded amethysts of the reef they crawl upon. To the untrained eye, a stationary individual is indistinguishable from a protrusion of the structure itself. Their joints do not rely on aqueous lubrication, but rather on smooth, ceramic-like ball-and-socket mechanisms that function with a faint, dry clicking sound—the dominant noise of the landscape.

The population is functionally divided by size and task, a vast, non-hierarchical collective driven by instinctual engineering.

The smallest among them, the size of a human thumb, are the **Masons**. They are multi-legged creatures, low to the substrate, possessing oversized, glandular mouthparts. They swarm over hairline fractures in the spiraling towers, secreting a thick, pungent paste derived from processed minerals. This mortar cures instantly upon contact with the dry heat, sealing the breach with a material harder than the original growth. Their movements are jerky and precise, a ceaseless tarantella of repair.

Intermediate-sized entities, roughly the bulk of a large domestic cat, are the **Harvesters**. They are slower, distinguished by rigid, fan-like structures rising from their dorsal plates. These intricate lattices act as air filters, trapping airborne mineral dust blown across the basin. Periodically, they stop, and specialized forelimbs scrape the accumulated nutrient-dust from their fans toward their intake orifices. They are the energy gatherers of the surface, turning the thin air into solid matter.

The largest inhabitants, heavy, lumbering beasts the size of small carts, are the **Recyclers**. They are built for destruction. Their heads are dominated by massive, hydraulic mandibles made of flint-like biological material. They seek out the oldest, graying sections of the reef—parts of the structure that have ceased active growth. With slow, deliberate power, they crush the calcified tubes and tessellated plates, the sound of grinding stone echoing softly through the tunnels. The resulting rubble is consumed, processed in their extensive, furnace-like guts, and eventually excreted as raw material for the Masons to build anew.

Deep within the smooth-worn tunnels that riddle the superstructure, another form exists—the **Conduits**. Rarely seen on the surface, these are elongated, many-legged creatures, sleek and blind. Their shells are thinner, radiating heat. They run the labyrinthine paths between the surface reef and the scorching magma substrate far below, acting as couriers. They ferry the concentrated volatile compounds from the deep earth up to the living layer, their bodies humming with the same low frequency as the geothermal engines beneath the crust.

There are no leaders here, no queens, no central intelligence directing the work. There is only the collective imperative of the reef. Every click of a joint, every scrape of a mandible, every secretion of mortar is a minute adjustment in the endless, quiet expansion of this dry, living city.
