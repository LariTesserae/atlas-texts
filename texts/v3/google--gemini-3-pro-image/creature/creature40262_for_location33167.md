The true masters of this sweltering, subterranean cathedral are rarely seen, but their presence is woven into the very texture of the decay. They are the primary agents of the slow metabolism consuming this forgotten industry.

They are the chitin-clad inheritors of the void, creatures that have evolved not just to tolerate the oppressive heat and crushing pressure, but to require it. They are multi-segmented arthropods, grown thick and armored, ranging in size from a human forearm to larger, rarer specimens the length of a man’s leg.

Their form is a direct reflection of their habitat. Having evolved in absolute absence of natural light, they are eyeless. Their heads are sleek, heavily plated helms fitted with sprays of sensitive, trembling antennae that taste the chemical soup of the air and map the intricate lattice of pipes via infinitesimal vibrations. Their primary sense, however, is tied to the geothermal thrum. They navigate the complex three-dimensional maze of catwalks and conduits by sensing the pulses of the magma chambers below through their countless hooked legs.

Their carapaces are no longer the pale translucence of their distant surface ancestors. Here, they have incorporated the environment into their very exoskeletons. They consume metal oxides and mineral-rich fungi, and their armor secretes the excess. As a result, they are camouflaged perfectly against the machinery. One might be staring directly at a cluster of rusted valves, only to realize the valves are breathing, shifting slightly as a creature clad in oxidized copper and bloom-iron waits patiently in the heat. Their joints are sealed with a natural, viscous oil that smells faintly of the same petroleum products sweating from the dynamos.

They are the gardeners of this toxic terrarium. They move with a surprisingly fluid, flowing speed along the undersides of wet girders, their many legs making a rapid, soft *ticking* sound against the metal, like cooling engines. They graze upon the great mats of deep-violet moss, keeping the gears clear enough for their own passage. They harvest the pale, dinner-plate fungi, carrying chunks of the fleshy matter into deep crevices between boiler plates to cultivate their own private fungal nurseries in the warmest spots.

Crucially, they accelerate the ruin. Their mandibles are strong enough to shear through corroded iron to get at the softer mineral deposits beneath. As they scuttle, their hard shells scrape away protective layers of paint and grease, exposing fresh metal to the relentless humidity. They are not just living amid the decay; they are actively, industriously eating the machines.

There is another, rarer thing that lives here, though "lives" might be the wrong word.

Deep in the maze, perhaps in the hollowed-out firebox of a dead furnace, one might stumble upon the remnants of the original "humans—or something like them." They are no longer distinct entities. Over centuries of isolation, heat, and strange emanations from the deep earth, they have become sessile organisms, fused to the infrastructure they once tended.

They are pale, bulbous things, their skin having taken on the consistency of wet parchment and fungal flesh. They are clusters of atrophied limbs and fused torsos, rooted into the metal grating by thick, vein-like cables that penetrate their flesh. They are integrated into the dormant machinery, perhaps acting as biological filters or processors in a system long since forgotten. They do not move, save for a shallow, labored breathing that wheezes in time with the hissing steam. Their eyes are sealed over by cataracts of milky tissue. They are the forgotten pilots of this great rusted ship, slowly turning into soil alongside their engines, dreaming feverish, endless dreams in the hot dark while the chitinous scavengers tick-tack over their oblivious heads.
