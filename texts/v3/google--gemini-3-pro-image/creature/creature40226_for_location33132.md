The denizens of Node 7-Kappa are not merely inhabitants of this sunken infrastructure; they are its biological extensions, creatures whose evolution has been warped and guided by the deafening thrum of the pumps and the relentless, crushing warmth. In this warehouse-lit abyss, biology has mimicked engineering.

The foundational life here doesn't just grow on the machinery; it mineralizes it. The violet and green algal mats are thick layers of velvet sludge, home to billions of microscopic rotifers that filter the rust-rich water. Feeding on these mats are the **Rust-Farmers**, the hubcap-sized crustaceans. They are heavy, cumbersome things, their carapaces thickened with absorbed metals until they resemble mobile chunks of corroded bronze. Their asymmetrical claws do indeed resemble hydraulic tools—one oversized, piston-like pincer for crushing mineral deposits, and a smaller, finer manipulator for scraping biofilm. They do not swim; they trudge along pipes and across the curved walls on six stiff legs, their movements jerky and mechanical. The *clack-scrape-clack* of their feeding is a constant percussion underlying the groan of the station. They are the unintended custodians, keeping the heat exchangers from fouling completely.

Where the colossal gears of the flow-regulators turn, churning the water into cavitating froth, live the **Gear-Dace**. These are the sleek, silver fish, but up close, they are scarred and ugly things. Their cranial plating is not bone, but a callus of cartilage and metallic deposits, evolved to withstand the glancing blow of a slow-moving cog. They are adrenaline junkies of necessity, darting into the tolerances between grinding metal teeth to rasp away the richest, most thermally active bacteria. They move in synchronized flashes of scarred chromium, a school of living shrapnel that scatters only when the machinery shifts its rhythm.

Deeper inside the system, where the heat leakage from uninsulated conduits would boil surface life, the **Conduit Eels** thrive. They are long, ribbon-like thermal parasites. Their skin is scaleless and pallid, almost translucent, save for the pulsing stripes of bioluminescence running their length—not soft blues or greens, but harsh, warning yellows and oranges that match the industrial lighting. They are blind, navigating solely by thermal gradients and acoustic vibrations. They braid themselves into writhing knots around leaks in the steam lines, pressing their bodies against the hottest surfaces, digesting the chemical soup leaching from the gaskets. To see a cluster of them unwinding from a pipe cluster is like watching spilt molten copper taking on independent life.

The open water spans are patrolled by the **Rivet-Skinned Barracuda**. It is the apex predator here, a bloated torpedo of muscle armored in heavy, overlapping scales that mimic the pitted texture of the surrounding polymer-concrete. It is an ambush hunter. It drifts motionless in the shadow of a redwood-sized pipe cluster, looking for all the world like a piece of jettisoned debris. Its eyes are large, milky orbs adapted to the strange, constant warehouse glare. When it strikes, it is with shocking speed, a blur of galvanized gray. Its jaws do not just snap; they slam shut with the bone-jarring *crack* of a metal shear, instantly crushing the armored plating of a Rust-Farmer or snapping up a careless Gear-Dace.

And then there are the ghosts in the machine. The **Maintenance Drones**. They are not alive, yet they are integral to the ecosystem. They are bulbous, antique contraptions, coated so thickly in barnacles and algae that their original geometry is lost. They shudder along forgotten guide-rails on sputtering thrusters, performing tasks that ceased to matter centuries ago—polishing a clouded lens, tightening a bolt on a defunct flange. They are the unwilling providers. When a drone engages its rotary scrubbers, it kicks up a dense cloud of nutrient-rich detritus, creating a temporary feeding frenzy where smaller, translucent shrimp-analogs swarm in the warm light like dust motes, only to be picked off by the lurking predators.

Life here is heavy, armored, blind to the sun, and utterly dependent on the eternal, deafening churn of the World-Ocean Circulator. They are the children of pressure and gears, thriving in the forgotten warmth of a planetary engine.
