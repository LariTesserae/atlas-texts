The denizens of this high, harrowing bowl are defined by the patience of stone and the necessities of the thin, biting air. While the shaggy, spiral-horned grazers and the phantom-pale cats are the permanent residents, integrated perfectly into the silent violence of the ecosystem, there is another presence here, transient but deeply marked by the terrain.

They are the hunters who utilize those crude slate windbreaks. They do not live here—no human could survive the nightly freezing of the lungs for long—but they journey up from lower, thicker atmospheres, driven by a need that outweighs the terror of the altitude.

To see one of them is difficult; they are stillness personified. A hunter might be crouched within the crescent of stacked slate, knee to chest, wrapped in layers so thick they appear almost spherical. Their clothing is a testament to the environment: heavy hides of the goat-oxen they pursue, fur turned inward against the skin, the outer leather scraped raw and greased with heavy animal fats to repel the daily meltwater. These garments are stiff, smelling of smoke and rancid tallow, colored the same dusty greys and rusted browns as the shattering granite.

Their physical form has been molded by the ascent. They are short and immensely broad across the chest, their ribcages expanded like barrels to house lungs bulky enough to pull oxygen from the metallic emptiness. Their movement is a study in conservation; every step on the shifting scree is calculated, slow, and deliberate, avoiding the sudden exertion that leads to the terrifying, gasping deficit of breath at this height.

The most striking feature, however, is their faces, or what little can be seen of them. To survive the aggressive, unfiltered brilliance of the sun, they wear goggles carved from the same dense rib-bones used for their tool handles. These bone masks cover the eyes completely, save for two narrow, horizontal slits sawn across the front. These slits cut the blinding glare down to a manageable sliver of landscape, forcing them to scan the terrain by turning their entire heads slowly, like the circling vultures above.

Below the goggles, their skin is a roadmap of exposure. It is burnt to a deep, leathery mahogany by the ultraviolet radiation, thickened and fissured with deep crevasses around the mouth and nose, tough as cured meat. Their lips are often coated in a protective paste made from fat and the pulverized dust of the violet mosses.

They are silent predators in a silent world. They squat for hours, immobile as the boulders they hide behind, watching the slow, graceful movements of the herds on the vertical faces. They also watch the sleek, reflective leopards, respecting them as rivals and markers. Where the leopard stalks, the hunter knows the herd will eventually flush. Their weapons are short-range—heavy spears tipped with the razor-edged volcanic glass found in the scree, and short, stout bows backed with sinew—requiring them to get terrifyingly close.

They are here for the dense, high-energy meat of the grazers, essential for survival in the lower lands, and perhaps for the valuable, shimmering pelts of the predators. But there is something else in their posture, a quiet reverence for the raw, newly birthed geology. When they kill, the act is quick, brutal, and followed by an almost frantic processing of the carcass before the sun dips and the lethal cold returns. Before they begin the treacherous descent with their heavy packs of meat and hide, they might leave a small offering—a sliver of liver, a polished piece of obsidian—tucked into the deep, dripping violet moss of a crevice, a payment to the brutal spirit of the high bowl.
