The inhabitants of this high, copper-lit expanse do not build; they occupy the spaces between the geology. They are creatures of deep time and heavy gravity, shaped by the very forces that smoothed the basalt hummocks.

At first glance, they are invisible. Against the slick, wet slate of the rolling landscape, they are merely imperfections in the rock face—bulges of shadow in the dim light. They are massive, hunched beings, possessing a density that makes their slow movements seem inevitable rather than willful. Their hide is not skin, but a thick, segmented epiderm of mineral accretion, similar in composition to the carapaces of the grazing "slag-backs," but far more complex. It is the color of wet iron and basalt, streaked with natural patinas of green verdigris and bloom of orange rust where the humid, metallic air has oxidized their outer layers.

They have no need for swiftness in a world without sudden predators, nor do they need acute vision in the perpetual gloom. Their heads are low-set, merging seamlessly into enormous, rounded shoulders, lacking distinct necks. Where a face might be, there is instead a broad, armored plate dotted with clusters of deep sensory pits. These organs do not see light; they see heat. To them, the world is a vibrant topography of thermal gradients—the blazing pillars of the vent chimneys, the warm streaks of underground magma veins, and the cool downdrafts from the bruised sky.

They navigate primarily through vibration. Their thick, pillar-like limbs end in broad, splayed feet that grip the slick rock with immense friction. Through these soles, they feel the low-frequency thrum of the planet’s geological engines, reading the subterranean pressure builds long before a vent erupts. They communicate in the same manner, striking the resonant rock with calculated blows of their heavy fists, sending seismic messages that travel for miles through the conductive stone—a slow, booming language felt in the bones rather than heard in the ears.

They are the curators of this vast machine. They are rarely seen far from the towering thermal vents. With glacial patience, they tend to the geological chimneys. Using tools shaped from basalt slivers polished smooth by centuries of use, or simply their own immense, armored hands, they scrape away excessive mineral buildups that threaten to clog the vents' orifices. They prune back the aggressively growing bacterial mats and clear debris that might impede the free flow of superheated vapor.

They do not construct cities, for the vents are their hearths and high altars. They gather in small, silent groups around the warmth, their metal-infused bodies drawing latent electrical charge from the air, much like the wire-grass. When they do move, the only sound is the wet scrape of stone on stone, a sound indistinguishable from the natural settling of the ancient landscape. They are the sentient gears in the slow, planetary mechanism, existing in perfect, heavy symbiosis with the breathing earth beneath them.
