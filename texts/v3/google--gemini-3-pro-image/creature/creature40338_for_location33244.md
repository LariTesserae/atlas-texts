The beings that cling to this frozen lattice of stone and timber are sculpted by the thin air and the crushing cold. They are a people built for endurance, not speed; for conservation, not expenditure.

Physically, they seem compressed by the immense atmosphere above them. They are generally short of stature but possessing immense depth of torso, their ribcages flared wide like barrels to house lungs capacious enough to glean oxygen from the stingy altitudes. Their limbs are thick, stolid, and heavy-jointed, built for the constant upward leverage required by their vertical city, rather than for stride.

Their skin bears the brutal signature of their environment. Exposed to intense high-altitude ultraviolet radiation and biting, desiccating winds, their faces are weathered into the texture of cured hide—darkened to shades of smoked umber and deep terracotta, deeply grooved around eyes that are permanently narrowed against the glare of ice and sun. Hands are broad, calloused paddles, the skin thickened and often stained by rock dust, soot, and the grease of wool.

They are a swaddled people, moving bundles of texture that blur the lines between the individual and the herd. They wear layers upon layers of thick, felted wool sheared from their beasts—oily, dense cloth that repels the damp fog and traps body heat with desperate efficiency. Over this, heavy pelts and roughly tanned leathers are strapped with intricate harnesses of braided hair and leather ropes—functional gear for a life where being tethered can mean the difference between crossing a street and plummeting into a chasm. The entire population carries the scent of their survival: the heavy musk of damp wool, the lanolin of the herds, and the pervasive, acrid tang of dung-smoke that clings to every fiber.

Their movement through the choked, icy arteries of their city is distinct. There is no rushing here. To hurry is to invite a fall on the slick slate, or to exhaust oneself in air that offers little return. They move with a deliberate, rolling gait, planting feet firmly, center of gravity low. In the crowded alleys, personal space is an unaffordable luxury; they move in a constant, fluid press, a communal sharing of warmth where shoulders brush and bodies lean against one another without apology. Conversation is low and sparing; breath is too precious to waste on idle chatter. Their silence is not sullen, but a reflection of the vast quiet that surrounds their island of noise.

Sharing this precarious existence are the beasts that make it possible. The great shaggy burdens—creatures of immense bulk and infinite patience—are the living foundations of the city. They are mountains of matted, greasy hair that drag through the mud, their broad, split hooves finding purchase on slick rock. Their primary function is not just transport, but heat. Stabled in the ground floors, their massive, slow-burning metabolisms act as furnaces, their warmth radiating up through timber ceiling-floors to keep the humans above from freezing in their sleep. Their breath is a constant, steaming fog in the lower streets, and their low, vibrating groans are the bass note of the city.

Above the plodding streets, smaller, nimble goat-creatures with curled horns and sure feet act as the city's scavengers. They defy gravity with irritating ease, clattering over slate roofs and leaping across impossibly narrow gaps between swaying bridges, consuming the city's waste and daring the void. And hanging silently above it all, riding the thermals generated by thousands of hearths and warm bodies, are the immense carrion birds—waiting with the same geologic patience as the mountains themselves for a misstep on an icy bridge.

The life here is a single, dense organism fighting a holding action against the void. The people, the beasts, and their sprawling hive of stone are woven together in a tight, smoky knot of heat, existing only through stubborn, collective will.
