They are the living foam on a stagnant, industrial sea. If the machines are cathedrals of function, these things are the multitudinous, blind worshippers, prostrating themselves not out of reverence, but out of a mindless, chilling necessity.

They are a pale, writhing geography. To look down at the spaces between the monolithic engines is to see the concrete floor seeming to ripple and shift, a hallucination brought on by the damp cold, until you realize it is a solid carpet of segmented bodies. They are the color of rancid fat, of bleached bone soaked in dirty water. They possess the segmented armor of crustaceans, yet it is not hard chitin, but a pliable, leathery tegument, translucent enough in the flat light to show dark, viscous fluids pulsing sullenly beneath the surface.

They have no eyes. In this world of uniform gray light and thick fog, sight is an extravagance. Instead, their blunt, faceless heads are studded with wet sensory bristles and twitching antennae that taste the oily air, forever seeking the metallic tang of sustenance. They possess far too many legs—rows upon rows of pale, scuttling limbs that allow them to flow over obstacles like a slow, deliberate liquid. When they move en masse, the sound is a high-frequency rustling, a billion tiny clicks of semi-rigid shell against wet stone that underpins the booming bass of the pistons like a horrifying murmur.

Their existence is governed by two blind drives: heat and hunger.

In the pervasive chill, they are drawn to the machines as the only source of warmth. They swarm up the oxidized flanks of the cooling towers, their pale bodies stark against the deep orange rust. Around the hissing steam vents, they pile upon one another in shivering, pyramidal mounds, dozens deep, the ones on the bottom crushed and cooked by the ones above, yet still desperately pressing toward the hot metal. They clog grates and overwhelm gangways, a living sludge that must be waded through.

They are the scavengers of an abiotic world. They feed where the machines weep. They cluster around the leaking valves and the cracked sumps, their complex, multifaceted mouthparts—a nightmare of wet palps and rasping surfaces—lapping up the congealed lubricants, the heavy metal runoff, and the nutrient-rich slurry that drips from the gears. They drink the poisonous nectar of the engines, growing fat and slick on industrial waste.

And then, the cycle completes itself. They do not die of old age; they are harvested by the blind mechanism they inhabit. The crushers that seem to jaw at empty air are positioned beneath grates where the creatures inevitably cluster and fall. The conveyors that move "nothing" are slick with the paste of their crushed bodies, carrying the dead biomass in an endless stream toward the furnace hoppers. They are scooped up without resistance, a raw, wet fuel shoveled into the fire to boil the water to make the steam that shrieks from the vents, which in turn warms the survivors in their brief, clicking lives. They are the meat in a metallic grinder, essential solely because they burn.
