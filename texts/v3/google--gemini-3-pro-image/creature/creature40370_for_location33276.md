The inhabitants of the travertine terraces are a tall, gaunt people, shaped by generations of intense heat and crushing humidity. Their physiology has adapted not by fighting the environment, but by becoming permeable to it.

Their skin is unique—thick, almost reptilian, and devoid of hair or sweat glands. It has a cured, mineralized quality, tough enough to withstand brushes with scalding stone or splashes of near-boiling runoff. Their coloration mimics the brilliant landscape they inhabit: mottled patterns of rusty hematite, pale ochre, and streaks of milky blue. When standing still against a riser of mineral deposition, shielded by the drifting steam, they are nearly invisible, appearing as just another natural outgrowth of the geology. Their eyes are protected by heavy, nictitating membranes that slide across to shield against the searing glare of the diffused sun and the constant sting of mineral vapor.

Clothing is minimal and strictly functional, as traditional textiles would rot in days or suffocate the wearer. Instead, they wear articulated harnesses and protective aprons fashioned from the harvested, iridescent chitin of the giant grazing crustaceans, riveted together with ceramic studs. These chitin plates deflect the hottest sprays and shed water instantly. Their feet are wrapped in thick, splayed boots made from layered, cured microbial mats—the only material that provides reliable traction on the slime-slicked lips of the geothermal pools.

In keeping with the overwhelming silence of their world, the people are incredibly quiet. The constant, drowning *hiss* of escaping pressure makes vocal speech difficult over any distance, so they have developed a sophisticated language of gesture and vibration. They communicate through subtle shifts in posture, hand signals, and by rhythmically tapping the ceramic pipes or the resonant stone ground with metal-tipped staves. They "listen" to these vibrations through the soles of their feet, reading the tremors of their peers just as they read the operational hum of their machinery.

They are the custodians of the hydro-thermal cathedrals, and their movements within these vast, steaming spaces are deliberate and unhurried. Rushing would mean slipping, and slipping means death in a boiling pool. They move with a slow, aqueous grace, like wading birds.

You find them perched on narrow gantries above roaring injection wells, their faces obscured by wide, conical hats of spun glass fiber that block the overhead glare. They lay their large, calloused hands upon the throbbing ceramic arterial veins, feeling the flow dynamics, sensing thermal differentials through their fingertips, and making minute adjustments to massive crystalline valves with a patience that mirrors the slow geological growth of their terrace home. They are a solemn, heat-hardened people, existing in a perpetual, sweltering equilibrium with the lethal forces they shepherd.
