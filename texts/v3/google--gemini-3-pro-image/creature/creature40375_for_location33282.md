The sterility is a lie, but it takes hours in the suffocating damp for the eyes to understand why. The absence of twitching whiskers or fluttering wings is not an absence of life; it is merely the absence of blood and bone.

Something *does* live here, generated by the intersection of unending moisture, geothermal heat, and the decaying slurry of iron and oil.

They emerge from the background only when they move, and they move with agonizing slowness, synchronized to the deep, geological rhythm of the great pistons. At rest, they are indistinguishable from the environment. A humped mound of oily rags left on a grate is not rags at all, but a resting creature. A thick, scabrous patch of rust on the side of a pump housing slowly detaches itself and begins a laborious crawl up the warm metal.

They are the custodians of this churning pit, golems born of the industrial detritus. They have no defined shape, shifting their mass as needed to navigate the maze of pipework. Their bodies are composed of a dense, fibrous fungal matte that has incorporated the surroundings into its structure—woven together with threads of copper wire, flakes of oxidized iron, and slicked with heavy grease. They are heavy, sodden things, dragging themselves along on pseudopods made of compacted sludge.

They have no faces, no eyes. Instead, their entire surface seems sensitive to heat and vibration. They congregate where the steam vents hiss loudest, basking in the scalding vapor like reptiles on a sun-warmed rock. When one of the electrical junction boxes spits a blue-white arc, a dozen of these creatures will slowly turn their bulk toward the flash, drawn by the ozone and the sudden surge of energy.

Their purpose is obscure, but they are not idle. You might see one spend an entire day painstakingly clogging a pinhole leak in a steam pipe with a cement made from its own regurgitated mud and rust filings. Another might wrap its body around a seized valve handle, applying slow, relentless hydraulic pressure over the course of a week until the metal groans and yields. They are the reason the machinery is still running; they are a slow-motion immune system, patching the iron body as it decays.

They make no vocal noises. Their communication is felt rather than heard. They tap against the metal catwalks and the sides of the vast pumps, sending complex patterns of vibration through the iron skeletons of the complex—a submerged Morse code that travels instantly through the tangled Gordian knot of pipes, undetectable beneath the overwhelming bass roar of the engines unless your hand is pressed directly against the metal.

They are the children of the steam and the rust, the only things capable of surviving in a world that has drowned everything else. They do not hunt, they do not breed in any recognizable fashion, and they do not sleep. They simply exist, tending the eternal machine, as slow and inexorable as the rust that birthed them.
