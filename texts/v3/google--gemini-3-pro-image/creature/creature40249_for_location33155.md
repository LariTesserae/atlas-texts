The primary inhabitants of this drowning world are not sentient in the way we understand it. They are the kingdom of the absorbent.

The true population here exists on the microscopic and near-microscopic scale, a staggering biomass hidden within the yard-thick carpets of sphagnum and liverworts. This dripping sponge is a universe unto itself, teeming with highly specialized invertebrates: cold-tolerant nematodes, tardigrades of unusual size navigating the freezing interstitial water veins, and rotifers that cluster in the billions in the cups of epiphyte leaves. It is a limnological frenzy occurring in slow motion, unseen beneath the surface layer of radiant green. Fungal networks, vast and sprawling, act as the nervous system of the terrain, their mycelium binding the precarious soil and rock together tighter than any root system, feasting on the relentless decay that accompanies the relentless growth.

Yet, the massive brutalist structures are not entirely abandoned to automata. They require tenders.

Emerging rarely from the weeping concrete facades or navigating the treacherous, moss-slicked paths along the gorge edges are the custodians of this wet machinery. They are few, perhaps numbering in the low hundreds across thousands of square miles, solitary beings adapted to an existence of sensory overload.

They are squat, dense creatures, built low to the ground to maintain a center of gravity against the vertigo of the cliffs and the slickness of the terrain. Their physiology has abandoned traditional hearing entirely; the acoustical assault renders ears a vulnerability. Instead, their skulls are thick, resonant chambers, designed to perceive the world through bone conduction—feeling the deep, vibrating thrum of the turbines in their teeth and spine, sensing the shifting of unstable rock through the soles of their feet.

Their skin is pallid, wrinkled deep with permanent waterlogging, and textured like wet chamois, coated in natural hydrophobic oils that make them gleam in the blinding light. They wear heavy, insulated suits that have become indistinguishable from the environment—patched with rubber and polymer, but heavily colonized by the same algae and slime molds that coat the dams. When they stop moving, they simply cease to exist visually, merging perfectly into the dripping tapestry of green and grey.

They carry no weapons, only heavy, non-sparking tools of bronze and ceramic strapped to their backs. They move with excruciating deliberation. To rush here is to slip, and to slip is to fall for miles. Their movements are synchronized with the slow, grand processes around them; they are patient as glaciers, scaling a turbine housing over the course of a day, cleaning intake grates with the monotonous diligence of an eroding stream.

They do not speak. Communication is impossible in the roar. If they interact at all, it is through slow, broad gestures visible across chasms, or seismic taps transmitted through the bedrock. Their minds, shaped by the constant thrum and the freezing mist, operate on geologic time. They are not masters of this environment, but its most complex, stubborn epiphytes, clinging to the cold, wet skin of a world that screams.
