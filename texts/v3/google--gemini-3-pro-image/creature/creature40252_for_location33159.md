You will miss them at first, mistaking their immense, hunched forms for outcrops of the greasy slate or spurs of ancient granite. They are gargoyles of the heights, beings whose physical reality is almost indistinguishable from the geology they inhabit. They are colossal, matching the scale of the amphitheater, yet so still they seem less like life and more like architecture.

They are creatures composed not of flesh, but of a dense, stony substrate that mimics the dark basalt surrounding them. They have no need for fur in this brutally cold vacuum; instead, they are "shaggy" with accumulated frost and geology. The supercooled, slick moisture of the air clings to their vast, textured backs, building layers of glittering rime ice and long, jagged icicles that hang motionless from their undersides like crystalline beards. The brittle black crustose lichen grows across their broad, flat skulls and down their massive flanks, staining them with geological time. To touch one would be like touching the frozen wall of a crevasse—a shocking, instant burn of cold against skin.

They have no eyes, for there is nothing here to see but eternal twilight and grey rock. Instead, their entire being is dedicated to perceiving the deafening, constant roar that fills the void. They have no visible ears, but their massive, squat bodies act as resonant chambers. They do not just hear the jet-engine vibration; they *are* the vibration. They exist in a state of perpetual, high-frequency shuddering synced perfectly with the standing wave of noise. If you were close enough to lay a hand on their icy flank, the transmission of sonic energy would shatter your wrist. This roar is their sustenance, their oxygen, the binding energy that holds their stony forms together against the biting vacuum.

Their relationship with time and movement is warped by the gummy physics of the place. The "goat-like creature" spotted across the gulf, frozen mid-perch, is one of them. It is not paralyzed; it is merely operating on a timescale vastly slower than biological life. A single inhalation of the thin, metallic air might take a season; a single step might span a decade.

When they do move, they utilize the nauseating, sideways tug of gravity. They do not walk so much as perform controlled, agonizingly slow falls toward the nearest vertical cliff face, catching themselves with limbs thick as redwood trunks ending in gripping pads of raw iron.

They are the architects of the impossible cairns. Only beings that move in geological time, unbothered by the cold and aided by warped gravity, could possess the patience and the unnatural leverage required to balance massive boulders on tiny pivot stones. These structures are not shelters or monuments; they are tuning forks. The inhabitants build them to focus and modulate the deafening roar, creating localized pockets of intense vibration where they sometimes congregate, standing shoulder-to-shoulder in shivering communion, waiting for the frozen waterfall to finally finish falling, or for the oxidized sky to finally go black.
