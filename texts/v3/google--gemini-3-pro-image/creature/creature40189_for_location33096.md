The people of Oakhaven are a distillation of their environment: wiry, perpetually damp, and vibrating with a desperate, kinetic energy. They are not dwellers *in* a city so much as sailors aboard a colossal, organic vessel that is constantly trying to sink beneath a sea of green.

Physically, they are built for vertigo. They are lean and sinewy, possessing a climber's explosive grip strength and the uncanny balance of tightrope walkers. You rarely see a soft belly or slouching posture here; the very act of moving from one's sleeping basket to the communal kitchen requires navigating a slippery, swaying obstacle course over a lethal drop. Their hands are things of rawhide and callus, stained permanently with chlorophyll and tanbark resins. Their feet, often left bare to better grip the slime-slicked roots, are wide and splayed, the soles thickened into natural leather.

Their clothing is a testament to their fiber-based technology, layered defenses against the eternal chill and seeping wet. They wear heavy felted wools derived from shaggy goat-creatures kept in pens on the lower, thicker branches, and leathers cured in acrid fungal smokes until they are waterproof and stiff. Everything they wear is quilted, oiled, and lashed together with intricate knotwork instead of buttons or buckles. Mold is a constant accessory; clothes have a pervasive musty smell, and it is common to see patches of fuzzy blue or white growth clinging to the seams of a heavy coat.

The scarcity of metal has lent their culture a specific aesthetic. A person’s status is conveyed through the complexity of their braids and the rarity of their biological ornaments. A master builder might wear a necklace of polished ironwood gears, while a hunter displays a pauldrons made from the iridescent, chitinous carapace of a giant canyon beetle. The few extant pieces of ancestral metal—rusted, pitted knives or spear tips—are worn openly as talismans of immense power, though they are rarely used for mundane tasks for fear of breaking them. Their working tools are wicked-looking implements of bone, flint, and fire-hardened wood, honed to razor edges that require constant resharpening.

The ambient noise of Oakhaven has shaped their very communication. They do not speak; they project. Their voices are pitched deep and loud to cut through the foundational roar of the waterfalls and the shrieking of the canopy fauna. A casual conversation sounds like an argument to an outsider. For quieter communication, or to signal across the misty voids between districts, they utilize a complex, rapid-fire language of hand signs and piercing, bird-like whistles that echo strangely against the wet cliff faces.

Their temperament is defined by the "endless, sweaty war" against the vegetation. They are a frantic, industrious people, possessing a collective anxiety that if they stop moving, the jungle will bury them. There is no leisure in Oakhaven, only different forms of work. Even children are employed spotting new fungal rot on suspension cables or scraping moss from walkway planks. They have a pragmatic, almost adversarial relationship with the nature that surrounds them; they respect its immense power but hack away at it with a ruthless determination.

Despite the hardship, there is a fierce, tribal cohesion among them. The terrifying fragility of their city means they rely on each other absolutely. A poorly tied knot by one person could send a neighbor's house tumbling into the abyss. This shared peril fosters a blunt honesty and a rough communal spirit. They live stacked atop one another, breathing each other's air, their lives interwoven like the chaotic root systems they call ground. They are the tenacious lice on the scalp of giants, wet, loud, and stubbornly alive in a place that wants to consume them.
