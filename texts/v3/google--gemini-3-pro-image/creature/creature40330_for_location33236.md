At first glance, and perhaps even at tenth glance, you would swear nothing lives here capable of spanning more than an inch. The environment is too hostile, the slow-motion violence of the churning earth too absolute for anything complex to survive.

But if you remain still, pressing yourself against the slick surface of a half-drowned boiler, letting the cold damp soak into your marrow, you might begin to perceive the inhabitants. They do not live *in* the basalt bowl; they are part of its digestion process.

They are the Oxidized titans, the Ferro-gastropods.

They are immense, solitary creatures, their scale matched only by the industrial corpses they inhabit. An adult might grow to the size of a small car, but you will never see its true shape. They are shelled, but their armor is not grown biologically; it is accumulated.

Through a slow, patient secretion of highly acidic, adhesive slime, they harvest the environment. As they agonizingly crawl through the viscous slurry, they cement flakes of rusted iron, shards of basalt, and fist-sized gear teeth to their backs. Over centuries of life, this accretion builds into a massive, craggy carapace that is perfectly camouflaged against the graveyard. One of these creatures looks exactly like a tumbled pile of wreckage. You could stare directly at one, mistaking its armored peak for the broken corner of a submerged engine block, until the moment it decides to move.

And when they move, they do not fight the churning mud; they ride it. They are masters of the basin's convection. They sense the deep thermal currents and the grinding pressure waves of shifting metal with vibration-sensitive pads on their unseen undersides. They anchor themselves to rising iron monoliths to ascend from the depths, or release their grip to let the mud swallow them again, sinking into the warmer, sulfurous substrate to feed.

Their movement is so slow it is imperceptible to the human eye, matching the geological tempo of the basin. A Ferro-gastropod might take a week to traverse ten feet of open sludge. They make no sound. Their locomotion is a lubricated glide, a hydraulic shifting of weight that displaces the mud without a ripple.

They feed on the decay itself. They are highly specialized metal-grazers. Beneath that massive, accumulated shell lies a powerful, rasping radula—a tongue studded with magnetite teeth hardened in the deep earth. They attach themselves to the thickest, juiciest veins of ochre rust on the ancient machinery and slowly, silently rasp away the corrosion, ingesting the metal oxides and the mineral-rich clay. They are the accelerators of entropy here, consuming the skeletons of industry and excreting the fine, toxic grit that makes up the slurry floor.

They are blind, deaf, and utterly silent. Their existence is a slow, cold meditation on pressure, taste, and gravity. They are the living ghosts of the machines, armored in their ancestors' rust, churning endlessly in the cold, gray soup.
