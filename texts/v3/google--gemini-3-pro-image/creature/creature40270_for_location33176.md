If you are alone, then the only true citizens of this infinite upper lattice are botanical. But to call them plants suggests something passive and decorative, utterly unfitting for this brutal altitude. The things that live here are occupying forces, a slow-motion botanical insurgency that has conquered the rust.

The primary resident is the moss. It is not the soft, velvety carpet of a forest floor. Here, saturated by the ceaseless aerosol damp, the moss grows into thick, heavy pelts that resemble the water-logged fur of drowned beasts. It swathes the meter-wide pipes in layer upon layer of emerald and viridian sludge, growing so dense that it muffles the metallic groans of the structure beneath. When the freezing squalls rip through the canyons, these massive hanging mats swing heavily, shedding freezing water in sheets, shuddering like cold flanks.

Where the moss establishes a beachhead, more rigid life follows. The ferns here have adapted to an existence without soil and almost without light. They are grizzled and tough, their fronds dark, waxy, and stiff as cured leather to resist the shredding winds. They do not need the sun; they thrive in the watery grey twilight, their metabolism slowed to a glacial crawl, matching the ancient stillness of the girders.

Their roots are the true marvel of adaptation. Over centuries, they have become capable of digesting the industrial corpse they inhabit. Fine, capillary root systems secrete slow acids that etch into the verdigris, leeching trace minerals from the scarred copper. Thicker, ligneous taproots prize open seams in the ferro-concrete, snaking into the darkness of service conduits to seek out pools of ancient, congealed lubricants and petrochemical sludge. They feed on the toxic residue of the machine’s past lives.

These root systems have become structural. In many places, the original iron rivets have long since rusted away to dust; now, tight knots of calcified root mass clutch the girders together, sinews of wood and fiber fulfilling the architectural duty of steel. The city is being held aloft by the very thing that is slowly devouring it.

In the rare moments of absolute stillness, if you press your ear against a moss-choked bulkhead, you won't hear the chittering of insects or the rustle of rodents. You will only hear the wet, heavy silence of absorption. It is a passive, enduring life, one that has learned to be as cold, as wet, and as hard as the metal lattice it now rules.
