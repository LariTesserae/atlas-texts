They emerge from the viridian gloom not as masters of this element, but as its stubborn, deliberate guests. These are a people sculpted by pressure and cold, their physiology drifted far from their terrestrial ancestors over countless generations of immersion.

Their bodies are thickened and smoothed by the environment. They possess barrel chests, expanded ribcages housing lungs of immense capacity adapted for long, slow intervals between breaths drawn from trapped air pockets or the distant surface. Beneath skins that have been leached of warm tones, appearing a mottled cyan and slate-grey in the filtering light, lies a sleek layer of insulating fat, blurring the definition of muscle but providing essential warmth against the eternal chill. Their skin is not soft; it is toughened, pebbled slightly like the rayskin they utilize, resistant to the constant abrasion of living stone and saltwater.

Their movements in the open water are deceptively languid. To move quickly is to fight the medium, so they swim with an economy of motion, powered by powerful thighs and calves encased in the stiff, widespread fins strapped to their feet. Their arms, however, tell the story of their labor. Their shoulders and backs are densely muscled, disproportionately strong from the endless, repetitive act of driving bone chisels into silicate sponge. Their hands are masses of callus and scar tissue, curved permanently as if gripping a tool handle, the fingernails thick and worn down to the quick.

Their faces are broad and flattened, streamlined against drag. Their eyes are enormous, dominating their features—dark, reflective pools with pupils dilated to the very limbus to capture every photon of the phosphorescent bacterial glow. They have lost the ability to perceive red; their aesthetic sense is tuned instead to textures, patterns of iridescence, and the infinite gradations of green and blue. Hair is impractical here; most keep their heads shaved smooth or cropped very short to prevent fouling in kelp or anemones.

Clothing is purely functional, woven from treated bladder-kelp fibres that resist rot, clinging tightly to the body to avoid drag. Broad utility belts made of cured grouper leather hold their precious tools—the basalt hammers, the prized conch-shell gouges, and the bone pitons—each tethered with braided fiber cords so they are not lost to the crushing depths below.

They are a quiet people. Vocal communication is difficult in a medium that distorts human speech and carries unintended sounds for miles. When they do speak, it is in booming, resonant tones meant to cut through the background crackle of shrimp and fish, or else in close-quarters sign language, their pale hands flashing against the dark jade of their walls.

Their lives are defined by patience. A young adult might begin hollowing out a sleeping alcove for their future child and not finish it until that child is nearly grown. They exist in a state of constant, low-level physical exertion, their metabolisms burning high to keep the shivering cold at bay. They are stoic, slow to anger, and deeply communal within their isolated towers, bound together by the shared reality of the crushing weight that surrounds them and the incessant, rhythmic *thud-scrape-thud* that is the heartbeat of their civilization.
