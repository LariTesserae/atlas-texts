They are the ghosts of the glare, moving silhouettes against the blinding white fire of the caldera. To survive in this crucible of light and killing cold, the human form has vanished entirely beneath layers of desperate insulation.

They are walking mounds of heterogeneous furs—shaggy, matted pelts of lowland beasts brought up from the forgotten green world below, stitched together with coarse sinew and stained by smoke and the grease of the segmented ice-creatures. The fur is stiff with frozen rime on the outside, but near the skin, it reeks of ancient, trapped sweat and the oily unguents they smear on their bodies to hold in heat. There is no definition to their bodies, only bulk, a necessary heaviness that helps ground them against the treacherous, slurring floor of their basin.

Their faces are never seen. To expose skin to the achromatic brilliance of the high-altitude sun is to invite instantaneous frostbite and snowblindness. They wear heavy, conical masks fashioned from cured, rigid hides, hardened like boiled leather. These masks are featureless save for the eye-slits—horizontal gashes no wider than a thumbnail, sometimes further baffled by slivers of smoked quartz or volcanic glass traded up from the lowlands. These narrow apertures reduce the searing world to a manageable, squinting sliver of view. When they turn their heads, the masks present a blank, bestial visage to one another, an anonymous fraternity of survival.

Their movement is peculiar, dictated by the unstable environment. While the bristled crustacean-things around them skitter with frantic energy, the humans move with deliberate, agonizing caution. Their gait is a rolling shuffle, knees permanently bent to lower their center of gravity, booted feet probing the slurry ground for pressurized brine pockets before committing weight. On the precarious ice ledges of their settlement, they cling like barnacles, moving with a slow, fluid tension, relying on spiked bone crampons lashed to their boots and the secure grip of mittened hands coated in abrasive grit.

Silence is their language. In air too thin to support casual chatter, speech is an exhausting luxury. They communicate through a complex shorthand of gestures, shoulder shifts, and the rhythmic tapping of bone chisels against ice. A sharp *clack-clack* might warn of a widening fissure; a low, sweeping gesture might indicate a section of wall destined to collapse by sundown. This silent coordination gives their bustling, crowded settlement the eerie feel of a hive mind, where hundreds of bulky figures work in feverish unison, their collective breath puffing in quick, vanishing clouds of ice fog.

Their lives are defined by a frantic, endless war against the glacier that houses them. They are rarely still. They are forever carving, shoring up, and retreating. With tools of honed mammoth bone and igneous rock, they chisel away at the encroaching walls of their own homes. They are master ice-crafters by necessity, knowing exactly how to shatter a block with a single blow, or how to melt a controlled channel using precious, smoky oil lamps to divert the weeping slush before it freezes into an impassable barrier.

In the tight, suffocating tunnels of the ice-city, personal space does not exist. They squeeze past one another on narrow rope bridges, their fur-clad bodies brushing in the dim, blue-filtered light that penetrates the glacial walls. Yet, there is no intimacy in this proximity, only a shared, crushing burden. Their existence is a perpetual state of emergency. When the deep, subsonic groan of the shifting ice vibrates through their bones, they do not pause in fear; they simply redouble their silent, frantic efforts, appearing less like masters of this high outpost and more like desperate parasites burrowing deeper into the skin of a beast that is slowly, unconsciously trying to freeze them out.
