The things that live here are less inhabitants and more concretions of the environment itself. They are not born; they coalesce. In a world without biology as surface-dwellers know it, life has found a way through geology and industrial waste.

They are the **Ferro-Kelp Lurkers** and the **Conduit Leeches**, though such names imply a taxonomy that doesn't exist here. They are indistinct, massive entities, blurred lines between scavenged machinery, calcified mineral deposits, and tough, fibrous chemosynthetic tissue.

They have no eyes; there is nothing to see but blinding violence. They have no ears; the sonic pressure would rupture them instantly. Instead, their entire bodies act as dense, seismic receptors. They "hear" the world through the shuddering of the bedrock and the screaming vibrations of the machines they cling to. They navigate the crushing density not by sight, but by sensing the heat gradients of venting plasma and the electromagnetic taste of leaking power couplings.

Their forms are squat, heavy, and radially symmetrical, built low to the ground to endure the turbulent currents that would rip apart anything possessing a spine. They look like walking tumors of rust and rock, roughly the size of industrial dumpsters. Their outer shells are not grown, but accumulated—layers of precipitated iron, heavy metals bonded by bacterial glue, and plates of scavenged wreckage fused to their backs like caddisfly larvae made of scrap metal.

They move slowly, deliberately, on multiple thick, hydraulic limbs that are part root system, part pistons. These limbs end not in claws, but in grinding surfaces studded with industrial diamonds and hardened slag, allowing them to chew their way through the dense tangle of pipes and petrified vines. When they move, the sound is a sub-bass grinding that adds its own note to the abyssal cacophony.

They are the parasites and the paradoxical caretakers of the machines.

You will find them clustered around the hottest, most radioactive vents of the sprawling complexes. They latch onto rupture points in the cooling systems with mouths that are mere toothless apertures of heat-resistant gristle. Once attached, they become sessile for weeks or years, siphoning off superheated chemical sludge and raw electricity. Their bodies pulse with the stolen energy, their internal vascular systems glowing with a sickly, Cherenkov-radiation blue that shines dimly through the cracks in their armored hides.

They feed on the toxicity. The crystalline fungi that grow so rapidly on the machinery also crust over these creatures, forming symbiotic, shimmering carapaces that the creatures periodically shed in violent, shuddering convulsions when the crystal growth becomes too heavy.

In this reality of thinning physics, these beings are anchors. They are so dense, so deeply integrated into the metallic soup, that they seem immune to the gravitational glitches and phasing matter. Debris falling "upward" smashes against their hunkered forms; pockets of vacuum collapse around them, merely scouring their iron shells clean.

They have no language, only a shared vibration. When two meet over a prime feeding fissure, they lock their grinding limbs together, a slow-motion contest of hydraulic pressure until one is forced to retreat into the churning darkness. They are the blind, deaf gardeners of this industrial hell, feeding on the waste of creation, ensuring the screams of the machines never truly stop.
