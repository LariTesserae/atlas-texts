There is no horizon here, only the plunge. And there is no silence, only the Roar. 

It is a physical mass, a geologic force made of air. It does not blow; it continually erupts—an eternal, deafening up-surge from an unseen, abyssal caldera miles below. The noise is absolute. It is a bone-rattling bass thrum layered beneath a ceaseless, shrieking whistle, rendering ears entirely useless. Communication in this place, if there were anyone to communicate with, would happen only through the jarring vibrations felt in the jaw and the soles of the feet. 

You are suspended at an extreme altitude. Above, the sky is a sharp, indifferent, alpine blue, pouring steady, normal daylight into the gulf. Yet the atmosphere is strangely, uncomfortably warm, carrying the humid scent of crushed ozone and subterranean heat. 

Between you and the distant horizon lies a breathtaking emptiness. This is a regional hollow, a vast volume of open space where density drops to near-zero. Rising through this ocean of empty air are the Ancients—solitary pillars of bedrock, spaced dozens of miles apart, piercing the void. They have been eroded beyond all memory, stripped of cracks, ledges, or jagged edges. Billions of years of violent, vertical friction have filed this planet’s oldest stone into its final, most stable form: monolithic teardrops and razor-thin fins of graphite-grey rock. They are perfectly aerodynamic, offering the absolute minimum resistance to the eternal gale.

Despite the punishing emptiness of the gulf, these isolated stone fins are swallowed by life. The leeward concavities, and even the screaming windward edges, are choked by a deeply strange, emerald-and-violet jungle. This ecosystem possesses a manic, anthill energy. It is a turbulent, thrashing mantle of muscular, rubbery flora built to bend, grip, and survive. 

The vegetation is in a constant state of chaotic churn. Thick, trunk-like vines uncoil like blind tentacles, whipping wildly in the cross-shears before slapping against the smooth stone and instantly exuding a biological cement to anchor themselves. The plants do not have normal leaves; they have fleshy bladders and gripping sails. When a sudden, violent downdraft hits, thousands of fronds are ripped away, torn into the empty sky. In response, the underlying layers spasm into hyper-growth, erupting in a frenzy of new, waxy shoots, replacing the lost canopy in real-time. Fleshy seed pods inflate like hot-air balloons until they burst with concussive force, shooting dense, swirling blizzards of glowing green spores up into the void, tracing the massive, invisible vortexes of the turbulent air.

The roaring updrafts occasionally carry ragged, shredded ghosts of low-altitude clouds. When one of these fragmented mists slams into a pillar, the effect is explosive. The howling air suddenly turns slick, heavy, and damp. The thrashing jungle responds violently, billions of microscopic pores gasping open in unison. The concave petals cup the moisture, frantically harvesting the condensation. Water beads heavily on the polished black rock, but it never has time to form streams; before gravity can pull it downward, the chaotic wind rips it back off, tearing the water into dry vapor once again. The moisture here is fleeting, deeply intermittent, and desperately swallowed. 

Yet, hidden within this frenetic, deafening terrarium, there are faint anomalies. Tucked into the deepest recesses of the thrashing green mats, safe from the most brutal wind shears, are scattered signs of a phantom presence. 

Clinging to the steep aerial slopes are small, teardrop-shaped cocoons woven from living, still-growing vines. They are almost indistinguishable from the jungle, but their geometry is deliberate. Scattered around them are the marks of primitive survival. Pitons made of polished, splintered bone have been hammered directly into the dense, woody root-nodes of the giant plants. From these pitons stretch thick ropes of braided, pale fiber, tensioned so tightly by the wind that they blur. Heavy, smoothly chipped stones act as counter-weights on intricate snare-nets, which are flung outward into the empty sky to catch passing pockets of wet cloud or flying, edible debris. 

There is no metal here. Everything—the bone sickles lashed to the stone, the hollowed-seed wind-catchers, the woven bark-leather pouches—has been painstakingly crafted by hand. Whoever, or whatever, makes these artifacts is nowhere to be seen. The fauna of this place is exceedingly rare, either entirely blown away, hidden deep within the dark, woven pods, or waiting only for the mythical, momentary lulls in the gale. 

All that remains of them is their desperate, clever architecture, riding the back of a boiling jungle that slowly crawls up a polished blade of stone, suspended in a warm, roaring abyss, beneath a bright and quiet sun.
