The landscape has been worn down to a mirror. 

Eons of patient, ceaseless weathering have planed the bedrock into a vast, undulating pavement of pale limestone and ancient, bleached sandstone. There are no hills left to interrupt the eye, no valleys out of which shadows might rise. The continent has reached its final, stable state—a featureless, near-flattened expanse sitting only inches above sea level, stretching out in a huge, open panorama under the bright dominion of the sun. 

Over this immense, exhausted stone, a continuous sheet of water lies trapped. It is a wet world, but not a deep one. The water over the plain is mostly ankle-deep, spreading for miles in a perfectly level sheen. It gathers in the shallow, smooth-rimmed dimples of the bedrock and flows over imperceptible gradients in a current so gentle it can only be tracked by watching a drifting leaf over the course of an hour. The water is bath-pan warm, heated uniformly by the heavy, humid air and the baking overhead light. 

It is a blinding place. The light beats down from a hazy, washed-out sky, glinting mercilessly off the unending shallows. The boundary between the heavens and the drowned earth is obscured by a constant heat shimmer, a rippling distortion that makes the distant stretches of the plain appear to float or dissolve entirely into white glare. 

Despite the abundance of water and tropical warmth, greenery is remarkably scarce. The deep time of erosion has long since stripped away anything resembling soil; no loam or dirt remains to anchor a root system. Vegetation exists only in stubborn, isolated gestures. Here and there, a solitary, twisted scrub-tree clings to a rare hairline fracture in the stone, its trunk thick and corky, its leaves pale and rigid to guard its moisture. Occasionally, a low clump of wiry sedge establishes itself in a slight depression where a handful of silt has miraculously gathered. But the rock mostly remains bare of higher plants, coated instead in vast, submerged, rust-colored and olive-green mats of algae and cyanobacteria that thrive in the hot, sunlit shallows.

This is a world without forests, without thickets, untouched by the hand of man or the mark of a tool. It is an architecture of pure, flat expanse, defined by sparse, scattered presences. 

Yet, to stand in this sun-blasted, shallow sea is to be engulfed in a deafening wall of sound. 

The air literally vibrates with life. Because the shallow waters act as a massive, sun-warmed incubator, they are spectacularly rich in invertebrate life. Millions of snails graze on the algal mats. Massive swarms of fairy shrimp, water beetles, and insect larvae cloud the warm pools. And feeding upon them is a riotous abundance of fauna. 

The noise is a physical pressure. A continuous, throbbing hum of stridulating insects hangs over the water, a high-pitched drone that never wavers. Beneath this lies the overlapping, guttural rhythms of millions of small amphibians hiding in the shaded undercuts of the rock panes, their churring croaks forming a polyrhythmic baseline that shakes the humid air. Above it all are the shrieks, clicks, and trills of avian life. Flocks of thousands of wading birds—stilt-legged, recurve-beaked, painted in shades of stark white, slate, and pale pink—dot the shallows as far as the eye can see. They sweep their bills through the water, snap at the surface, and occasionally take to the air in sweeping clouds of flapping wings, only to settle again a few yards away. Their calls bounce off the hard bedrock and flat water, carrying clearly across the immense distances.

The sheer density of the sound contrasts wildly with the open visual field. You can see for a dozen miles, easily picking out the lonely silhouettes of birds and the rare, gnarled trees standing stoic against the horizon. 

Nothing happens quickly in this flooded pan. The dynamics of the place operate on a scale of profound slowness. The evaporation and the creeping drift of the water are balanced by gentle, weeping rains that replenish the shallow sea without ever resulting in a flood. The heat is a constant, heavy blanket. The ecosystem is locked in a sprawling, unchanging climax—an endless loop of sun turning to algae, turning to insects, turning to birds, and returning to the water. The rock beneath it all yields to the dampness at a rate of mere millimeters per millennium. It is a landscape that has long forgotten the violence of tectonic uplift or sudden shifts. It rests in the bright, noisy, humid calm of its final form, holding the sky in its endless shallows, vibrating with the loud, patient pulse of endurance.
