The sky is a ceiling of solid rock, yet it is blinding. Miles above, the arched vault of this colossal subterranean basin glows with a furious, shadowless luminescence. It is a firmament of translucent, pale-gold stone pumped full of some unimaginable geothermal light, diffusing down like a frosted incandescent bulb. There is no daytime or night; there is only this eternal, unbroken, sweltering noon.

The heat is immense, a physical weight that presses into the lungs, smelling of pure, green sap and distilled water. To stand in this basin is to stand inside a hermetically sealed hothouse, trapped in a state of suspended animation.

There is no "ground" beneath your feet. The floor, extending for miles across the regional hollow, is composed entirely of living tissue. This is not a forest of bark and wood, but an ocean of gigantic, overlapping embryonic flora—turgid, pale-green, and yellow structures resembling the fleshy sprouts of newly germinated seeds, scaled up to the size of cathedrals. Enormous cotyledons, thick as mattresses, lay over one another in a dense, unbroken lattice. The landscape is a topographical map of soft curves, swollen stems, and vast, cupped petals. 

And everything is completely unblemished. There is no rot here. No dead leaves fall to a forest floor. No edges are browned, no stems cracked by age, no surfaces worn by wind. Every curve of every plant is pristine, glossy, and impossibly smooth, as if the entire biome was willed into existence only seconds ago, frozen at the exact moment of birth. It is an ecosystem without decay, holding its breath in a perpetual, undeveloped infancy. 

Because the temperature never drops and the air never cools, the landscape rests in a flawless equilibrium. There are no thermal gradients to create wind, so the air is utterly motionless. The dampness of the cavern does not arrive as rain; instead, the heavy, static humidity gathers as enormous beads of condensation on the undersides of the great glassy leaves. These droplets cling there, swelling to the size of fists, but rarely fall. When one finally does drop into the vast, clear basins of trapped water pooling in the junctions of the colossal stems, the single, resonant *plop* rings out for miles. It is one of the only sounds. Otherwise, there is only a vast, ringing quiet, pressing against the eardrums like the hum of the light above.

Scattered across the sprawling, green flesh of this world are the quiet signs of hands. They are sparse, swallowed by the sheer scale of the cavern, but entirely untouched by time. High in the crooks of the largest stalks, solitary bowers have been shaped from the living, pale vines, woven into perfect, symmetrical domes. Extending from them are faint, smoothed grooves running along the backs of the great leaves—paths compressed countless ages ago, yet looking as fresh as if they were walked today. 

Here and there, in hollows of pure green, sit perfect circles of pale, unweathered stones. At the center of these scattered rings, polished bone staves or wooden bowls rest, holding solitary drops of unwavering dew. They resemble monastic cells or ceremonial platforms, built without a single tool or shred of metal. Because nothing decays, the intention behind these small, isolated structures is preserved in terrifying clarity, like insects trapped in sap.

Animals are almost nonexistent. You might climb for days across the thick, resilient highways of plant tissue and see nothing moving. Only occasionally do you find life: a massive, glass-winged moth the size of a shield, resting in the center of a giant waxy bloom. It does not flutter. It does not fly. Its iridescent wings are locked open in the blinding light, perfectly still, quietly drinking the heavy dampness straight from the unmoving air. 

Nothing shifts. Nothing fades. The giant leaves endure the blazing light, their surfaces gleaming with trapped sweat, locked in a raw, unending beginning that will never be permitted to end.
