The space is a vertical cleft in a high montane ridge, a narrow canyon where a booming river cuts through the deep earth. But you cannot see the stone canyon walls. The entire gorge is choked, bridged, and bound by an ancient, colossal lattice of living timber. Titanic evergreens, their roots thick as watchtowers, have sought out the opposite sheer faces of the gorge, intertwining in a massive, three-dimensional knot over the abyss. This living scaffolding is an ancient, sprawling hamlet—a settlement carved into and woven from the forest itself.

It is bitterly cool. A perpetual, heavy mist drifts down from the peaks, snagging in the canopy and condensing on every available surface. The air is a frigid, silvered soup, smelling intensely of bruised pine needles, wet animal fur, and crushing dampness. Water weeps from everything. It trails down the deep, grooved bark of the massive trees, beads on the waxy leaves of giant, hanging ferns, and pours in shattered cascades from one level to the next. The settlement is constantly soaked, reliant on the steep verticality to shed the relentless rainfall and heavy fogs into the roaring river below.

Generations of inhabitants have shaped this living architecture into an environment of astonishing, layered density. There is no stone, no metal. The structures are grown, coaxed, and lashed together. Hollowed burls serve as cavernous storehouses. Massive, horizontal roots have been flattened into slick, broad avenues, their surfaces polished to a deep, glossy sheen by centuries of bare feet and clawed paws. Thick, fibrous lianas are braided into suspension spans, while the canopy has been manipulated to form tightly woven, overlapping roofs of living foliage that divert the heaviest water. Dwellings are enclosed pods of woven bark-cloth and densely packed sod, tucked atop every stable branch and tucked under the lee of massive trunk-creases.

Within this enclosed, vertiginous space, the energy is hyperactive and relentlessly chaotic. It is a vertical bazaar fueled by an anthill momentum. Bipedal inhabitants, bundled tightly against the chill in thick, greasy pelts and capes of woven cedar-bark, scramble across slick wooden gantries and vine webs with terrifying speed. There is a frenetic urgency to their movements to maintain warmth and manage the constant decay wrought by the damp. 

The technology holding the chaos together is massive but entirely primitive. Great timber wheels and wooden gears squeal in protest as teams of laborers crank heavy wooden winches, hauling massive nets of scavenged tubers, dripping moss, and raw lumber up from the canyon floor. Huge counterweights made of hollowed logs filled with river water slosh and swing perilously overhead. Masons wielding flaked obsidian adzes and heavy bone mallets hack feverishly at new growth to keep passageways clear, sending showers of green splinters raining down on the levels below.

The sheer volume of fauna is overwhelming. Life is layered atop life until the boundaries blur. Above, in the misty rafters of the weave, deafening flocks of cliff-dwelling birds with dense, iridescent plumage nest in tight colonies, constantly taking off and landing in a flurry of wet shrieks and snapping wings. Down on the root-highways, the inhabitants drive large, shaggy-furred ungulates—creatures with the balance of mountain goats but the bulk of oxen—laden with heavy, woven panniers. These beasts snort cold plumes of steam, their cloven hooves clacking sharply against the wet wood. Scampering scavengers, long-tailed and monkey-like, dart fearlessly between the legs of the laborers, stealing scraps of forage and screeching at one another in territorial disputes. 

The sound within the cleft is a physical pressure. The foundation is the endless, bass-heavy roar of the unseen river below and the constant, percussive drumming of water hitting broad leaves and wooden roofs. Layered above that is a cacophony of work and life: the sharp cracks of wood chopping, the rhythmic, groaning squeal of wet ropes stretching under tension, the braying and roaring of the loaded beasts, and the shouts of hundreds of inhabitants haggling, directing, and arguing over the din. 

When shafts of normal, pale mountain sunlight briefly pierce the cloud cover, they slice down through the canopy in stark, misty beams. The light catches the plunging waterfalls, turning them to flashing silver, and illuminates the vivid, saturated greens of the lichen that clings to every inch of un-walked wood. In these flashes of light, the full cross-section of the settlement is visible—a sheer cliff-face of life, boiling with movement, soaked to the bone, loud, weathered, and fiercely alive in the cold upland air.
