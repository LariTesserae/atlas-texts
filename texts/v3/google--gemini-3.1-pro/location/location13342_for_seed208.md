You feel the landscape in your bones long before you can make sense of it through the gloom. It is a world of overwhelming, concussive noise. A relentless, deafening hum vibrates through the soles of your feet, periodically shattered by deep, percussive booms that hit the chest like physical blows. To speak here is useless; the air itself is already full.

The light is a heavy, bruised grey, entirely diffused by a permanent ceiling of thick, cool mist. Under this twilight, the terrain extends in a vast, open expanse, seemingly limitless. There are no mountains, no hills, no valleys. Three billion years of relentless weather have won their war against the earth’s crust. This is geology in its terminal, finalized state: the entire region has been worn down to a single, continuous, planetary mirror of polished black bedrock. 

Over this slick, frictionless basin, water sits in shallow, miles-wide pans, rarely deeper than a few inches. The air is bitterly cool, and everything drips with a constant, heavy condensation that smells faintly of copper and sulfur. 

Because the bedrock is so perfectly flat, and because the stone is so dense, it acts as a flawless acoustic sounding board. The deafening, grinding drone of the ecosystem propagates for miles. The surface of the shallow water is frantic with it. The deafening noise forces the water into intense, shifting cymatic patterns—intricate geometric grids of standing waves, shivering mandalas, and chaotic, splashing crests that appear and collapse in time with the roaring baritone of the world. 

There is no soil, and there is absolutely nothing green. The bedrock is coated in a thick, slick, translucent biofilm—a chemosynthetic slime that thrives in the cold, wet dimness. Swimming through the acoustic waves of the shallow puddles are countless millions of tiny, transparent arthropods, whipping through the cold water to gorge on the slime. 

But the source of the skull-rattling noise lies miles away across the sparsely populated emptiness. Emerging from the grey fog like creeping mountains are the grazers.

Despite the emptiness of the plains between them, their numbers are staggering. They move in widely spaced herds, vast domed silhouettes dragging themselves across the wet mirror. They are colossal, crustacean-like behemoths, devoid of eyes, clad in overlapping plates of dark, mineralized chitin. Beneath their immense carapaces, diamond-hard rasps and heavy, grinding radulae scrape the microbial slime from the bedrock. The collective scraping of tens of thousands of massive, heavy teeth against the polished stone creates the shrieking, foundational drone of the landscape. As they graze, they communicate. They possess internal acoustic respiratory chambers, and to signal one another, they slam heavy, piston-like forelimbs into their own echoing shells. The resulting *BOOM* travels through the water and the bedrock, blowing the mist apart in momentary rings of atmospheric pressure. 

Here, interspersed within the immense, shivering emptiness of the rock flats, are the faint marks of those who survive on the margins of the herds. 

Scattered miles apart, clustered on slight, imperceptible rises in the stone where the water does not pool, sit primitive, temporary hunting camps. They are impossibly small against the region's scale. The dwellings are low, rounded coracles made by lashing together the shed, curved armor-plates of the giant grazers using braided sinew. To touch one of these shelters is to feel it vibrating like a drum skin. 

Inside the bounds of one abandoned camp, the water on the black stone is stained white with bone-dust. Heavy, blunt hand-tools rest near a depression in the rock: hammers made of fused vertebrae, and thick, jagged spears tipped with the snapped radula-teeth of the behemoths. There is no metal here, nor wood. Only stone, bone, and carapace. In the center of the camp is a dense mat of dried, harvested bacterial slime, rolled tightly like leather, likely used to plug the ears, insulate against the biting chill, or soften the bone-shaking vibrations of a world that never sleeps.

You look out past the crude shelters. The shallow water continually ripples and dances in perfect geometric squares. Miles away, a titan lifts a heavy, armored limb and brings it down. A second later, the concussion hits you, throwing a spray of cold mist into your face, as the ancient, unending machinery of the wet stone world grinds relentlessly on.
