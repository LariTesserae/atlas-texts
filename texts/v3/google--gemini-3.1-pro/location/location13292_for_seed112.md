The first thing that strikes you is not the landscape, but the sheer, concussive violence of the air. The sound is an absolute, physical weight. It is a continent-spanning roar—a deafening, overlapping percussion of shrieking fibers, rhythmic thudding, and the immense, hollow howling of wind through massive flues. You cannot hear yourself breathe; you can barely hear yourself think. 

You are standing on a high, freezing plateau, elevated so close to the planet’s stratosphere that the sky has lost its blue. Above, it is a glaring, bleached optic plain, pouring down blinding, shadow-scorching light. Yet the light brings no warmth. The atmosphere here is thin, biting, and desperately cold, carrying a turbulent, chaotic wind that never settles. The gales change direction with hyperactive violence, snapping from updrafts to shearing crosswinds in seconds, driven by the immense thermodynamics of this world.

Look down at your feet. There is no dirt. There is no rock. There is no sand. The ground is a single, planetary crust of interwoven, pale lignocellulose. The entire surface of the world is a hardened biological substrate. It looks like an ocean of polished, petrified wood, bleached bone-white by the glaring sun and scoured smooth by eons of endless friction. This is an ecosystem completely devoid of water. Not a drop of moisture exists. Instead, the chemistry here is driven by silicon, dry nitrates, electrostatic gradients, and raw kinetic energy. The ground bears the deep, beautiful scars of profound erosion—not by water, but by time, wind, and the relentless scouring of dry, abrasive dust.

The landscape is overwhelmingly vast, stretching in a smooth, frictionless curve toward a razor-sharp horizon. For miles in every direction, the pale wooden floor is empty, swept clean by the screaming gales. The density of presences here is remarkably low, which only makes the entities that *do* exist all the more staggering in their scale. 

Spaced miles apart across the gleaming plain are colossal, towering botanical structures that defy all terrestrial logic. They are not trees; they are living, evolved heavy industry. 

Miles to your left, one of these mega-flora breaks the horizon. It is a staggering vertical complex of pale, fibrous material, resembling a cross between a factory cooling tower and a bucket-wheel excavator. With no water to transport nutrients, the plants here have evolved to harness the chaotic, turbulent wind-state of the plateau. The structure extends vast, rigid sails of silica-laced cellophane—translucent, reinforced bracts the size of city blocks. As the violent wind strikes them, the entire organism rotates. 

It is a biological turbine. Ponderous, groaning, planet-scale wheels of hardened deep-wood spin on colossal axles. The shrieking you hear is the sound of immense wooden gears grinding against one another, transferring torque down into the subterranean layers of the crust. The mega-plant acts as an organic drop-forge, its roots pounding blindly into the carbon-dense substrate beneath the surface, cracking mineral veins and crushing chemical salts into a fine, energetic dust. 

Through hollow, chimney-like stalks reaching hundreds of feet into the blinding sky, the plant exhales its exhaust. Massive plumes of gray silica, inert carbon, and electrostatic ash are violently expelled in pneumatic bursts, drifting away on the chaotic winds. There are no straight lines here, no metal, no rivets, no intellect behind the design. It is completely unbuilt, untouched by conscious hands, yet it perfectly mimics the roaring heart of a mechanized, industrial factory city. 

The environment is in a state of continuous, violent reconfiguration. A sudden localized storm—a shear-gale of hurricane force—slams into a distant turbine-tree. You can clearly see the biological machinery react. Gigantic ligaments of braided, tensile fiber snap taut. The organism reefs its crystalline sails to avoid shattering, opening massive pressure-valves in its trunk to let the gale pass through. The resulting sound is a pneumatic shriek that rattles your teeth.

Despite the desolation, the dry cold, and the horrifying noise, this world is crawling with life. Fauna here is abundant, having evolved in perfect, parasitic synchronicity with the great botanical engines. Because the ambient air is freezing, life congregates where there is heat—and heat here is generated entirely by mechanical friction. 

Swarming around the great spinning axles of the turbine-trees are dense clouds of what look like flying shrapnel. These are "clatter-birds"—avian analogs with bodies of hardened, metallic-feeling chitin. They flutter in the thousands around the roaring wooden joints, basking in the radiant thermal energy of the grinding gears. 

Where the immense axles turn, they are maintained by billions of pale, armored grubs. These creatures are the biological equivalents of grease-monkeys; they feed on the fraying splinters of the spinning wood, digesting the raw cellulose and excreting a dry, incredibly slippery fluoropolymer dust. Their waste lubricates the joints of the mega-flora, preventing the plant from burning itself down through its own friction. 

Larger creatures prowl the open, polished voids between the distant factories. Massive, segmented grazers that look like rolling armadillos of riveted copper and basalt slide across the frictionless floor. They hunt the electrostatic charges left in the wake of the exhaust plumes, their bodies humming and sparking as they devour raw static and dry mineral salts from the air. 

As you stand there, battered by the chaotic wind, the sun climbs higher, intensifying the glare. The grand, spinning towers cast long, stroboscopic shadows across the pale wooden earth. The overlapping rhythms of a dozen distant, miles-high organic factories create an industrial symphony of terrifying proportion—a relentless, deafening, cold, and waterless machine, built by no one, running flawlessly for millions of years.
