The first thing to claim you is the noise. It is absolute, crushing, and completely static—a deafening, planetary-scale roar that vibrates not just in the marrow of your bones, but in the conceptual architecture of your mind. It sounds like the shrieking of a billion panes of glass being bent just to the point of shattering, accompanied by a subterranean bass drone that thickens the very air. Yet, despite the apocalyptic volume, nothing is exploding. Nothing is falling. The violence is entirely geologic, and it is moving at the glacial pace of deep time. 

You are standing at an extreme, impossible altitude, in a place where the atmosphere should have thinned into the vacuum of space. Instead, the sky is a bruised, dim violet-black, heavy and suffocatingly dry. There is no water here. No frost, no vapor, no clouds. The air is desperately cold, a dry chill that pulls the moisture from your skin and leaves your breath invisible against the dusk. 

The ground beneath you is embryonic. This is a landscape caught in the agonizingly slow act of its own birth. The terrain consists of towering, jagged peaks and vast, sweeping valleys, but none of it has ever known the touch of wind or water. The rock is utterly unweathered. It extrudes from the crust in perfect, mathematical formations—limitless expanses of sharp-edged, obsidian-black polyhedrons and raw, unpolished silicate planes. From the vantage point of a towering, knife-edge ridge, you can look out across the vast, open region. Mountains don't slope; they glitch upward in sheer, terraced steps, their angles too precise, too cruel for a lived-in universe. 

And reality here is fraying at the seams.

When you look toward the horizon, the perspective fails. Distant peaks occasionally render in wireframe, glowing with a faint, sick luminescence before filling in with heavier matter. Shadows defy the dim, ambient dusk, falling toward whatever you focus on rather than away from a light source. Space itself stutters. If you turn your head too quickly, a mountain range might briefly superimpose over the sky, creating a dizzying, kaleidoscopic tessellation of dry rock and empty void. Gravity is mostly downward, but it lists lazily to the left, making the sheer cliffs feel like tilted floors.

Despite the desiccation, the crushing noise, and the broken physics, there is life, though nothing breathes. Spaced out across the vast, empty miles of raw geometric stone are isolated but incredibly lush pockets of vegetation. These solitary thickets are wild, sprawling, and ferociously dense. Giant, fern-like structures with fronds the size of cathedral doors cascade over the sharp rocks. But they are not aqueous. They are woven from brittle, metallic fibers and translucent, glass-like capillaries. They possess a deep, impossible magenta hue, shifting to an abyssal green in the shadows. 

These plants do not drink water; they feed on the immense acoustic energy of the landscape. The deafening howl of the world’s tectonic extrusion drives their metabolism. Where the roar is loudest, the thickets grow thickest. They bleed dry, sparkling mineral dust when fractured, and their broad leaves vibrate with kinetic fury, absorbing the brutal soundscape and translating it into slow, sprawling, fractal growth. In the spaces between these lush islands of sonic flora, there is only miles of empty, sharp-angled void-rock. 

There are no animals. Nothing scurries, nothing flies, nothing hunts. The sheer volume of the world would pulverize any fragile, fluid-filled nervous system. 

Yet, there are marks. 

Scattered across this newborn, screaming, non-Euclidean roof of the world are the faint, undeniable signs of hands. On a perfectly flat, extruded plane of hyper-dense basalt, you might find a cairn of stones. But because the laws of physics are actively breaking down, the stones in the cairn do not touch. They hover an inch apart from one another, bound together by crude, hand-woven ropes made of stripped metallic plant fiber. 

Elsewhere, lodged into a fissure where space seems to tear and reveal pure static, there is a spear. Its shaft is a thick, dead stalk of acoustic flora, and its head is knapped from a chunk of conceptual geometry—a hand-axe shaped from a piece of rock that looks like a frozen piece of sky. These built interventions are sparse, separated by days of walking, but they are deeply primitive. Faint etchings scraped into the unweathered stone depict rudimentary spirals and tally marks, though the markings tend to drift slowly across the rock face when you aren't looking directly at them. 

Whoever, or whatever, left these primitive tools did not use them to hunt, for there is no meat here. They used them to pin reality down. The crude stone altars and hand-hewn wedges are driven into the fault lines of the world’s physics, like crude stitches in a bleeding wound, trying to staple the dimensions together using only the logic of the Stone Age. 

The entire landscape exists in a state of suspended agony. The deafening screech of reality ripping, the slow, silent extrusion of perfect geometric rock, the vibrant, dry rustle of the acoustic jungles vibrating in the cold, dim air. It is a world whose creation is breaking its own rules, watched over only by a few scattered, hovering stones, tied together with string, trying to hold the cosmos in place.
