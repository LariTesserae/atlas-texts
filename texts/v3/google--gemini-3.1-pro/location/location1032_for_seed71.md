This is a world that has simply worn out. You stand on what might be the roof of a dying universe—a planetary high-altitude plateau, raised into the thin, freezing altitudes and left to the scouring winds of eternity. Deep time has exercised its ultimate patience here. The concept of a mountain is a forgotten memory; the terrain is an endless, slate-gray floor of near-flattened lithosphere, worn perfectly smooth into vast, slick, oceanic swells of polished bedrock. 

Over this infinite expanse, the air is bone-chillingly cold, yet it hangs heavy with a perpetual, clinging moisture. It never rains in any traditional sense, but the atmosphere constantly weeps. A freezing, low-to-the-ground fog drifts across the stone, weeping heavy condensation onto every available surface. It pools in shallow depressions, freezes into intricate, heavy crusts of rime, and then slowly sublimates back into the mist under the pale, ordinary light of a distant, indifferent sun. 

Across this immense, slick expanse sprawls an impossible city. It blankets the continent, perhaps the entire planet, an urban grid designed by an intelligence that deeply understood complex infrastructure but possessed only bare hands and raw stone. It is a metropolis of ancient, primitive toil: monolithic dolmens, wide avenues paved with unhewn erratic boulders, stepped ziggurats of mortarless basalt, and long, low communal structures roofed with lashings of giant, pale reeds. Yet, the density is agonizingly sparse. A single cobblestone plaza might be five miles wide. A stone-circle temple, bearing the crude strike-marks of flint chisels, might sit in total isolation, separated from its nearest neighboring structure by hours of walking across the damp, empty bedrock. 

And the city is not a ruin. It is violently, ceaselessly active. 

Here, the deep-time erosion that flattened the mountains has begun to wear away the laws of physics themselves. Reality is threadbare, frayed, and unspooling. Without a single living builder, a massive dry-stone wall will suddenly, silently dismantle itself. Three-ton boulders drift through the freezing fog like tethered balloons, tumbling across the sky to reassemble into a crude aqueduct three miles away. A vast, empty avenue delineated by standing stones suddenly folds at a sheer 90-degree angle, snapping the horizon into the sky so that a neighborhood of cobblestone huts looms directly overhead, defying gravity. 

Despite this frantic, continuous rearrangement of the world, there are no animals. No heartbeats, no insects humming, no birds navigating the fractured airspace. Consequently, the silence is absolute and surreal. The tearing of a spatial dimension sounds only like frost cracking on a frozen puddle. When a massive stone column stutters in a localized temporal loop—dragging its own image across the plaza in a glitching, shuddering smear of ghostly architecture—it does so with barely a whisper. The only sounds are the slow, sluggish drips of freezing water and the faint hiss of displaced air.

Into this cold, breaking, silent vastness comes the vegetation. In the isolated pockets where gravity pools like water, or where time loops continuously enough to offer shelter, the flora is explosively, impossibly lush. Life has not completely surrendered; it has simply discarded the rulebook, forming violently vibrant oases separated by miles of gray emptiness.

Massive, fleshy succulents with bruised-purple leaves the size of boat sails anchor themselves to the shifting megaliths, swollen and heavy with sub-zero sap. Thick, dripping mats of bioluminescent cyan moss cascade over the primitive masonry, somehow thriving in the freezing, crying mists. There are groves of immense, glass-like frost-ferns that lock into the rapid, dynamic cycles of the landscape. They grow and die in time-lapse speeds: their fronds unspooling in seconds to a towering, verdant green, capturing the wet mist, instantly freezing solid, and shattering into iridescent dust to seed the next generation. 

Because space is torn, the biology adapts to the tears. A grove of pale, fat-trunked trees might grow horizontally out of an invisible rift in the air, their roots reaching into a fourth dimension to draw out warmth and moisture, while their heavy, dripping red canopies drape over the empty stone streets below. Thick, fibrous lianas weave themselves through crude stone window-frames and across optical illusions, attempting to stitch the fraying fabric of the universe back together with brute, silent, botanical force. 

It is a landscape of staggering juxtaposition. It is a wildly dynamic ecosystem of creeping frost, shattered dimensions, and endlessly migrating megaliths, woven through with overflowing, alien lushness—all holding its breath in the freezing quiet, playing out the final, glitching heartbeat of a physical world.
