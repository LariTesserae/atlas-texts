The absolute first sensation is the crushing, omnipresent geometry of the noise. It is a drone so massive, so structurally profound, that it replaces the silence it murdered with a new kind of physical medium. The sound is an endless, multi-octave roar—like the groaning of tectonic plates sliding against one another, interlaced with the deafening rush of a hurricane. Yet, there is no wind. The heavy cold air is perfectly, rigidly still. The roar does not fluctuate. It has no crest, no lull, no rhythm. It is a steady-state cosmic scream, an eternal chord vibrating the marrow in your bones, locked at peak amplitude.

You are standing at an altitude so extreme the word "sky" has lost its terrestrial meaning. Above and all around is an infinite, shadowless vault of bruised, slate-grey twilight. There is no sun, no moon, no gradient of light to imply a horizon. The illumination is weak but pervasive, a dim, flat glow that seems to seep outward from the very fabric of the void. 

You stand upon the summit of a stone monolith. Below you, sheer cliffs drop away into mist-choked emptiness. Across vast, echoing gulfs, other pillars rise from the deep. They are colossal, isolated islands of dense dark rock, spaced miles apart. Or, at least, they *appear* miles away. Depth perception here is profoundly broken, a casualty of reality slowly unspooling itself. You look at a neighboring spire far across the abyss—and as you take a step, the intervening distance instantly folds. Suddenly, that distant monolith is looming just an arm’s length away, its cold surface almost brushing your nose. If you blink and shift your weight, it snaps back to the horizon. Space here is crumpled like discarded paper, allowing miles and inches to exist in the same overlapping coordinate.

The physical geology of the rock tells a story of unbothered, immense age. The plateau beneath your boots is domed and immaculately smooth, weathered down over eons into frictionless curves. But it was not wind or rain that eroded it. The stone appears worn down by the sheer abrasive friction of the roaring space itself. It has reached a state of ultimate, established balance. Nothing can be subtracted from it anymore.

Despite the biting chill—a temperature hovering just a fraction of a degree above freezing—the environment is suffocatingly damp. The unmoving air is a suspension of heavy moisture, clinging to clothes and skin like an icy sheen. The slate-dark stone weeps. Condensation forms continuously on the rock, welling up into fat, shivering drops. 

But gravity surrendered its sovereignty here a long time ago. When the droplets grow too heavy, they do not fall downward. They detach and drift slowly upward, or slide horizontally in sluggish, chaotic vectors, catching the dim light like floating quicksilver. Where the water manages to pool in the shallow indentations of the eroded rock, the surface of the liquid does not ripple; it is shattered into intricate, fractal standing waves by the immense acoustic pressure of the drone. The waves hold their shapes perfectly—frozen spikes and ridges of liquid water that never crest or collapse, locked in unvarying acoustic levitation.

In the shallowest, roughest fissures of the stone, biology makes a stubborn, muted stand. Sparse patches of something living cling to the rock—a brittle, wire-like growth resembling coarse iron wool or blackened moss. It feeds directly off the floating dampness, absorbing the hovering droplets through osmosis. The moss does not sway, nor does it tremble. It is rigid, caught in stasis, a biological afterthought holding desperately onto the edge of existence.

As you wander across the slick, domed summit, faint ghosts of intent reveal themselves. Someone, or something, was here, perhaps millions of years ago. Shallow, smooth depressions march in sequence across the rock—footprints left by unrecorded pilgrimages, slowly sanded down to vague impressions. Near the sheer drop of the monolith's edge sits a primitive cairn: five rounded stones stacked atop one another. 

Yet, as you pull close, you see the reality of their arrangement. The stones do not touch. They hover a fraction of an inch above each other, suspended in the chill air, preserving the shape of an altar while completely ignoring the forces that should topple them. Past the cairn, leading outward, a small staircase has been roughly hewn into the rock. It climbs to the edge of the abyss and simply continues into the void—six stone steps, entirely unmoored, hanging over sheer emptiness. They lead to nowhere, a staircase into the pale, roaring mist, abandoned in mid-thought by physics.

Movement catches your eye, a majestic disruption in the empty grey expanse. Drifting through the vast chasm between the pillars is a creature. It resembles a deep-sea siphonophore, a colossal, translucent entity made of pale, pulsing bells and long, ghostly ribbons of trailing membrane. It swims through the heavy, loud air with agonizing slowness. It is entirely deaf to the roar, entirely blind to the impossible geometry. As its trajectory brings it toward the side of your monolith, it makes no attempt to veer away. It simply glides into the dark stone and phases through it, its bioluminescent internal organs glowing dimly beneath the surface of the rock, before slipping out the other side. As it leaves, it deposits a fresh slick of freezing moisture on the cliff face, dragging a wake of floating silver droplets into the void.

You remain on the plateau, surrounded by the deafening, eternal grind. Slowly, the profound melancholy of this high-altitude archipelago sinks in: nothing here will ever change. The roar will never cease. The dim light will never fade into night or brighten into morning. The water will endlessly drift in its erratic orbits, the hovering stones will never fall, and the void will forever hold its breath. This is a landscape perfectly balanced on the breaking point, a frozen equilibrium of ruined physics and cold mist, shouting a beautiful, terrifying note into the emptiness forever.
