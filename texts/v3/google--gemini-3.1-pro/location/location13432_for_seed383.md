The descent into the basin offers no darkness; instead, it opens into an ocean of blinding, shadowless glare. Deep below the planet’s crust, the Great Alabaster Hollow stretches for miles, an immense cavern whose ceiling is a continuous, unbroken vein of violently luminous fluorite. Powered by geothermal heat from unseen depths, the ceiling burns with a cool, harsh, fluorescent whiteness. It washes the world below in perpetual, unblinking day.

There is no green here, nor any memory of it. It is a strictly mineral world. The cavern floor spans out like a vast sea of ancient milk, composed of chalk and porous limestone. Eons of time have acted upon this rock, weathering away any sharp edges, stalagmites, or jagged ravines. What remains is a deeply aged, near-flattened landscape of smooth, undulating pale hummocks, worn down like bars of soap left too long in water. 

The air is distinctly cool, carrying a sharp chill that bites at the skin. Condensation is the only weather. Moisture from the porous stone evaporates in the glowing light, rises, hits the cool ceiling, and gathers into heavy, swollen drops. It falls as an intermittent, lazy mist, leaving the chalky ground permanently damp. In the shallowest depressions, mirror-like pools of perfectly clear water collect, reflecting the blinding ceiling above.

Across this vast, sparse expanse of pale, damp emptiness, there are sudden, hyper-dense explosions of civilization. 

They are settlements—sprawling, layered, and jarringly primitive. From a distance, they look like porous coral reefs or the towering mounds of termites, rising hundreds of feet into the brilliantly lit air. They are constructed entirely without metal or forged materials; the buildings are woven from the pale fibers of giant fungi, mortared with damp chalk-slurry, and framed with the massive shed carapaces of subterranean beasts. 

But these cities are not static. They are in a state of violent, chaotic turbulence. 

If you stand near a settlement, the sheer energy of it is overwhelming. The population is a frantic, swarming mass of long-limbed, pale-skinned builders and their abundant beasts of burden—giant, velvet-fuzzed arthropods with dozens of segmented legs, and wide, flat-headed amphibians that pull sledges of raw white stone. It is a wildly hyperactive bazaar of motion. Plumb lines swing erratically. Thousands of workers dart across swaying suspension bridges spun from insect silk, passing carved pumice bricks from hand to hand in a blur of cooperative speed. Scaffolding is thrown up in minutes, bound by thick sinew, only to be dismantled just as quickly. The ground here—a damp, ancient dust—is highly unstable, behaving like a slow-flowing liquid. Left alone, a structure would sink within hours. To survive, the settlement must perpetually rebuild itself. Walls are torn down on the sinking side of a tower and frantically rebuilt on the rising side. Entire districts are deconstructed and moved a hundred yards across the damp plains in sheer, frenzied bursts of manual labor.

Yet, despite the manic, anthill energy of a hundred thousand beings smashing stone, pulling hoists, and swarming over their primitive metropolis, the landscape possesses an eerie, heavy weirdness: it is absolutely, impossibly quiet. 

The ancient, porous rock of the cavern floor acts as an acoustic black hole, swallowing vibration. Furthermore, the damp air seems too thick to carry sound, and the creatures themselves possess no vocal cords. The giant arthropods glide over the wet chalk on pads of sound-dampening fluff. When a block of stone falls from a height and strikes the mud, it does not produce a crash, but a muffled, distant *thud*, like a heartbeat heard underwater. 

The chaos is a silent film. You watch two builders fiercely argue, their communication entirely visual—rapid, complex flashes of bioluminescent nodes on their throats and wrists, hands waving in a fury of sign language. A beast of burden rears up, mandibles snapping in agitation, yet the only sound is the quiet rustle of its velvet joints. Pulley wheels spun from dried bone squeak at the volume of a mouse’s breath. 

To look upon this world is to stand in a cold, blindingly bright void. Vast stretches of wet, empty chalk isolate these frantic hubs. But within the hubs, life is a turbulent, silent storm of early-tool survival, an infinite cycle of tearing down and building up, illuminated by a glowing stone sky that has watched the labor for millions of years.
