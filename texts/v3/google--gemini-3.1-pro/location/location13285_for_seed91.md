Sound is a physical medium here. It does not just fall upon the ear; it occupies the hollows of the chest, rattles the teeth, and drives out all other thought. It is an immense, unyielding roar—a deafening mechanical thunder born from scattered industrial processors that churn in the dim reaches of this subterranean expanse. 

The environment is a gargantuan underground rift, a newly formed tectonic tear deep below the surface. The geology is raw. Where the rock is visible, it is violently sharp, bearing entirely unweathered fracture lines and sheer, knife-edged geometries that speak to a recent, catastrophic parting of the earth. But you rarely see the stone. 

Instead, the entirety of the space has been consumed and claimed by engineered vegetation. The plants serve as both the architecture and the substrate of this world. There is no soil, only a miles-wide, densely woven mat of pale, cold-hardy roots, thick as iron cables, spanning the abysses and slinging between the sheer cavern walls. The floor is a rolling, spongey topography of fibrous gray-green mosses and woody, frost-bitten tubers. Wide, empty plains of this biological floor stretch out in stark emptiness. The cavern is incredibly vast—a subterranean valley dwarfing the human scale—yet it feels desolate and sparsely populated. There are no forests here, no varying heights of flora, just this creeping, all-encompassing vegetative crust bridging the dark.

Miles apart, interrupting the sprawling, empty tundra of roots, sit the sources of the noise. Gargantuan atmospheric engines—scattered remnants of some scaled industrial endeavor—are bolted directly into the jagged basalt walls. They are the size of city blocks, their brutalist steel housings sweating in the cold. Enormous turbine blades spin endlessly, driving subterranean wind through the cavern. Weak, bruised-yellow halogen floodlights ring these processing stations, bleeding into the gloom. The light drops off quickly, leaving the vast stretches between the machines in a perpetual, freezing twilight.

The temperature hovers permanently at the threshold of freezing, locked in a bitter cold. The immense columns of air propelled by the distant engines carry a clammy, intermittent moisture. When the internal weather shifts, heavy condensation fogs the cavern. Freezing mist rolls across the empty plains of roots, leaving millions of tiny water droplets shimmering on the pale vegetative fibers and beading on the rusted iron of the distant machinery. The dampness never amounts to a true rain, but it keeps the world perpetually slick, chilling the bone and feeding the relentless, creeping biomat.

Life besides the structural flora is vanishingly rare. The sheer volume of the noise prohibits complex ecosystems; animals rely on hearing to communicate and survive. Occasionally, low to the ground, pale and heavily armored arthropods the size of a fist can be seen moving sluggishly through the damp, scrubbing moss. They are deaf, feeling only the mechanical vibration of the world through their hooked claws, moving with agonizing slowness.

Everything here operates on that same glacial timescale. Despite the violent, deafening roar of the turbines and the rushing of the cold, damp air, the environment is fundamentally static. The raw, sharp rock will take millennia to dull. The industrial engines are built for century-long cycles, automated and unchanging in their brutal rhythm. And the deep-cold vegetation grows by fractions of an inch a year, patiently, blindly swallowing the raw stone and the scattered steel into a singular, shivering surface at the bottom of the world.
