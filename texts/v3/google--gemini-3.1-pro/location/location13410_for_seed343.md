The bedrock fell away fifty million million years ago, but the mountains remain. 

Long before the world settled into its current geologic age, an ancient mountain range of soft, porous stone was overtaken by a colossal, creeping forest. Over eons, the vegetation sent deep bore-roots down into the karst and limestone, dissolving the mineral, eating the very mass of the peaks. The wind eventually scoured away the remaining dust, leaving only the botanical skeleton behind. What stands now is a mountain range entirely fabricated from petrified-yet-living plant matter—a sprawling, continent-scale lattice of titanic roots, trunks, and vines holding the exact shape of a vanished earth.

Because the rock is gone, the world is profoundly hollow. You can stand on a branch the width of a six-lane highway and look outward through a labyrinth of twisting wooden arches, seeing for miles *through* the interior of the peaks. The density of physical matter is staggeringly low. Space here is mostly composed of empty air and dizzying, mile-deep vertical drops. You are at an extreme elevation, far above the cloud-layers of the lowlands, suspended in a wireframe geography. 

Yet, it is surprisingly warm. Colossal thermal updrafts rise from the unseen valleys miles below, funneled through the hollow wooden canyons like heat through a chimney. These thermals carry high humidity, which undergoes a rapid transformation when it strikes the cooler, high-altitude shadows of the giant timber. There is no weather here in the traditional sense, no pounding rain, but a constant, heavy dampness. Ghostly wisps of cloud snag and tear against the bark, leaving the ancient wood slick with condensation. Every crevice harbors a dark, humid pocket. Massive, bowl-shaped epiphytes—the size of houses—cling to the sheer vertical faces of the primary roots, their overlapping leaves acting as catch-basins for the perpetual morning dew, creating tiny, isolated ponds suspended in the otherwise dry, open air.

But the most overwhelming physical presence in this landscape is not the wood, nor the empty scale. It is the sound. 

It is an environment devoid of silence. The acoustic chamber of the hollow mountains amplifies a constant, concussive roar of biological activity. The air is heavily populated, an oceanic reef of the sky teeming with an incomprehensible volume of fauna. Millions of leathery, multi-winged gliders ride the thermals in sweeping, chaotic flocks that resemble black rivers flowing upward. Swarms of heavy-bodied insects, each the size of a fist, move in dense, snapping clouds that vibrate the air with a deep, bass thrum. Ribbon-tailed predators dive from the upper canopies, their feathers screaming against the wind as they hunt. The combined friction of a billion beating wings, territorial shrieks, clicking mandibles, and the rushing wind roaring through the wooden flutes of the mountain creates a continuous, deafening pressure. It is a noise so absolute it vibrates in your teeth. 

Normal, bright sunlight filters down through the upper strata of the canopy, striking the white, bone-worn curves of the central root-pillars and casting dramatic, hard-edged shadows into the hazy gulfs below. In the shafts of light, the ecosystem is a frenzy of motion. Predators unhook their claws from the wooden ceilings and drop into the void, catching the warm updrafts to soar upward and ambush their prey from underneath. The sky is a kinetic tapestry of eating, fleeing, and mating, dictated purely by the rhythm of the rising heat. 

Scattered within this deafening, vertical immensity are the faint, precarious marks of human habitation. Sound is useless for communication here; survival relies entirely on the visual. Tucked into the damp, sheltered junctions on the underside of the mega-vines are small, isolated structures. They are woven hanging-pods, constructed from braided plant fibers and spider-silk, lashed securely to the ancient bark to hide from the apex flyers. They look like the nests of weaver birds, dwarfed by the scale of the wood. 

The technology of whoever lives here is entirely drawn from the surroundings—there is no stone, and therefore no metal. Leading away from these hanging villages are dizzying pathways marked only by shallow, deeply worn footholds carved directly into the petrified bark by generations of primitive bone chisels. Occasionally, you might find a frayed climbing rope made of spun vine dangling over a thousand-foot drop, or a discarded hunting harpoon fashioned from the sharpened, lightweight femur of a chasm-glider. These frail artifacts are the only evidence of a people who have adapted to a life entirely off the ground, clinging like moss to the bones of an eaten mountain, living out their days in a warm, roaring void.
