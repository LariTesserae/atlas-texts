The noise arrives long before the eye can make sense of the gloom. It is a deafening, percussive symphony of raw geology being bent to an unseen will. The air in this vast, sunken basin is dominated by the agonizing grinding of stone on stone, the sharp, concussive *crack* of immense mallets, and the endless, plunging echoes of dropped weights. It is impossible to hear yourself breathe.

The light is scarce, limited to sputtering, angry smudges of orange fire from fat-lamps hung on rusted iron hooks. In this deep twilight, the scale of the valley gradually reveals itself—an interior region stretching for miles, yet entirely devoid of natural geology. Not a single sloping hill, untouched boulder, or organic cavern wall remains. Every square inch of this world has been quarried, cut, mixed, or fired, then restacked into an unimaginably tight labyrinth of architecture. 

It is a canyon of raw, emerging masonry. Staggering ziggurats of damp, black basalt crowd against towering, precarious scaffolding made of oversized, fossilized ribs and thick cables of braided sinew. There is no wood here, no green, no trace of a leaf or a root. This is a kingdom of pure mineral and simple chemistry, an endless, overlapping favela of unbaked clay, knapped flint, and crude brickwork. Archways intersect other archways; staircases spiral abruptly into the bellies of massive, unfinished domes. The structures are so densely packed they seem to be fighting for oxygen, leaning heavily upon one another, separated by chasms no wider than a shoulder's breadth.

The air is cool, hanging around fifty degrees, enough to turn every exhaled breath into a momentary plume of pale fog. It is a wet cold. Intermittent sheets of condensation rain down from the unseen, lightless ceiling miles above. The moisture glazes the newly sheared faces of obsidian and slickens the half-dried mortar. This water is precious, and the architecture is designed to capture it. Crude, shallow aqueducts carved from pumice line the edge of every roof and causeway, gathering the drips and feeding them into larger, centralized ceramic pipes. 

The water eventually finds the immense waterwheels. You can hear them plunging and churning through the darkness—massive, primitive engines built from slabs of slate and stretched amphibian hide. They catch the channeled waterfalls and begin to turn, driving a chaotic, valley-wide system of primitive automation. The turning wheels rotate colossal stone drums, winding ropes of spun glass and heavy hide. These primitive windlasses drag multi-ton limestone blocks up steep ramps of crushed gravel. 

Everything is in motion. The landscape is embryonic, caught in a violent spasm of genesis. There is no erosion here, no smoothing of edges by time. Every corner of granite is razor-sharp. The mortar is still wet, exuding the harsh, chalky smell of quicklime. Piles of fresh debris constantly cascade down the flanks of half-built towers. The architecture is shifting, too—entire city blocks are built atop beds of rolling stone spheres. As thousands of tons of new rock are added to one side of a plaza, the change in weight causes the underlying floor to groan, tilt, and slide the adjacent structures into new, interlocking configurations. The city solves itself like a blind, crushing puzzle. 

Through the dense, soot-stained fog and the spray of the water sluices, you occasionally catch sight of the inhabitants. Life is rare in the damp cold, but it is immense. In the shadows of a fresh archway, wiping wet slate-dust from its face, stands one of the builders. It is a hulking, pale biped, twice the height of an elephant, its flesh the color and texture of raw dough. It wears a heavy apron of uncured leather, tools of bronze and immense, chipped flint hanging from a belt of iron links. 

These solitary titans are sparse, scattered by the miles, but they work with a frantic, unceasing rhythm. They do not speak. They only hoist, carve, stack, and bind. Guided by some incomprehensible blueprint written into the architecture itself, they swing massive counterweighted hammers with their bare hands, driving limestone wedges into fault lines to cleave new blocks. When a pale builder fits a massive keystone into a vault, the loud, final *thud* reverberates through the valley, instantly answered by the chiming of fifty chisels far away, compensating for the new distribution of weight. 

Occasional scavengers navigate this active, dangerous world—fluttering things that resemble giant, translucent moths, lacking eyes, feeding on the rich calcium paste of the drying mortar. They cast frantic, twitching shadows against the wet bricks as they pass the flickering fat-lamps.

Slowly, the environment overwhelms the senses. The constant rain of condensation splashing against raw terracotta; the sudden, terrifying lurch of a bridge settling into a new gear-tooth of the city; the echoing boom of a falling monolithic slab; the cold, harsh smell of pulverized flint. It is a world forever being born, violently constructed by primitive hands, a sprawling machine of wet stone without end, without purpose, and without rest.
