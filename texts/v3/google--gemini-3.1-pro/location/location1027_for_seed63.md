High inside the lightless pinnacle of a stratospheric spire, there is a vault of baking heat. The air here is aggressively dry, thin from the altitude, and superheated by geothermal veins radiating from the deep rock beneath. There is absolutely no water. To inhale is to draw in a breath of hot ash, ozone, and the alkaline tang of dry, metallic dust. 

At first, a visitor in this absolute darkness might think they have entered a lifeless, empty chimney. There is almost no sound. Against the vast, suffocating heat, the silence feels heavy, broken only by a continuous, muffled whispering—like the slipping of dunes in a desert wind, or a thousand bolts of heavy silk being dragged across stone. 

But this space is not empty. It is saturated. 

The floor is not rock; it is alive. The walls are not stone; they are paved in moving bodies. In this cathedral-sized chamber, life has found a strange, waterless foothold, and it has proliferated until it filled every available inch. Millions of organisms inhabit the vault, packed so densely that they form a continuous, undulating surface. 

They are long, segmented creatures, ranging in size from a finger to heavy, serpentine leviathans as thick as a torso. They have no eyes and lack the wet, glistening wetness of terrestrial biology. Instead, their carapaces are formed of desiccated silicates, carbon, and sulfur, completely covered in an incredibly dense, ash-grey micro-fleece. This velvet-like insulation serves a dual purpose: it traps the ambient geological heat from the spire, and it deadens sound. When millions of these creatures writhe, climb, and slide over one another, the friction of their fleece produces only that soft, ghostly hush. 

The ecosystem operates as a biological convection current. The vault experiences immense heat from an open fissure in the floor, but the rarefied, high-altitude air from vents near the ceiling is freezing. To manage this gradient, the animals are in perpetual, flowing motion. The mass feeds on raw thermal energy and dry minerals at the bottom, absorbing heat into their dense, metallic flesh until they are nearly baking. Then, they begin to climb. They surge up the walls in a tightly knitted carpet, passing their stored heat to those above them, seeking the cooler heights. As they reach the vault's apex, having expended their thermal charge to digest mineral dust, they cool, detach, and cascade back down the center of the chamber in a slow-motion avalanche of soft, dry bodies. 

This silent, boiling loop never stops. It is a mesmerizing, blind predator-prey system as well. Amidst the slow upward climb of the smaller segments, the massive, sluggish elders wait. Because there is no liquid to spill, predation is bloodless and quiet. An elder simply opens an ossified, heat-radiating maw and engulfs a dozen smaller kin. The prey is broken down in dry chemical furnaces over days, leaving behind nothing but completely hollowed-out, brittle husks.

These husks are the building blocks of the vault’s strange landscape. Discarded by the thousands, they are pushed and packed into the crevices of the chamber. Over uncounted millennia, some primitive instinct in the swarm has driven them to collect these shells and bind them with a secreted, heat-curing resin. High on the walls, out of the main thoroughfares, they have sculpted massive, jutting balconies—crude but intricate architectural ledges that resemble the inside of a wasp nest forged from iron and ash. These suspended nurseries are where the creatures molt, shedding their outer layers to grow, creating even more husks to build with. 

The underlying rock of the chamber reveals the immense age of this cycle. Wherever the swarm's flesh touches the basalt walls, the stone has been polished to a seamless, glass-like finish by constant, soft friction. It is a long-established world, worn into absolute balance. 

Light does not exist here naturally, though occasionally, the overwhelming density of dry bodies rubbing against one another generates massive static charges. Without warning, a branching spiderweb of blue electrostatic lightning will crackle across the surface of the swarm, illuminating the chamber for a fraction of a millisecond. 

In that frozen flash, the true scale of the cathedral is revealed: a dizzying vertical shaft of polished obsidian, entirely choked by a crawling, faceless tide. Then the spark dies, plunging the vault back into darkness, leaving only the tremendous heat, the static taste of ozone, and the endless, velvet whisper of the unending climb.
