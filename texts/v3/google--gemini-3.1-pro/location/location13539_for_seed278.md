The high plateau curves away into a dusk that never fully deepens into night. This is a continental roof, thrust so high into the atmosphere that the air is thin and bitterly cold. The terrain stretches out in an unbroken, oceanic vastness, entirely carved by ancient, forgotten glaciations. Every ridge has been sanded into sweeping, tired curves; every valley is a shallow, gentle depression that might take three weeks to cross. The earth itself feels entirely settled, locked into a physical equilibrium that has not suffered a sudden geologic shock in a billion years. 

There is a permanent chill here. The pale, watery light filters down through a continuous, high-altitude overcast, casting no shadows. It is a dim, bruised illumination that renders the landscape in shades of slate, iron-oxide red, and frost-pale gray. 

Across the endlessly worn bedrock, water exists as a phantom. It does not flow; it settles. Condensation clings to the smoothed stones, freezing into delicate, fern-like structures of rime in the colder hours, and weaning into a heavy, suspended dampness when the temperature marginally rises. This meager, atmospheric moisture sustains the only vegetation: sparse, isolated webs of black, wire-like lichens and dull, metallic mosses that grow in the rock-fissures. They form widely scattered patches, leaving vast swaths of the plateau utterly bare.

Yet, this desolate stillness is an illusion of distance. The landscape is not empty. It teems with an overwhelming, planet-scale ecology, spread out like an armada across the endless plains.

They are immense, lumbering silhouettes, spaced far apart—sometimes a half-mile between individuals—but so unimaginably numerous that their dark shapes speckle the horizon until they blur into the curvature of the earth. They are living megastructures, animals evolved into engines. Biology here has bypassed flesh to embrace heavy industry, doing so without a single constructed factory or forged tool. 

These beasts stand as tall as hills, encased in carapaces of heavily oxidized, naturally extruded iron and thick, silicate slag. They are built like massive, ambulatory refineries. From their backs, ossified vents—hollow spires of bone and calcified minerals—rise into the cold air. Deep within their cavernous abdomens, slow-burning chemical furnaces digest the iron-rich lichens and sulfurous rocks they scrape from the earth. You can see the dull, cherry-red glow of internal combustion pulsing faintly through the thick, translucent joints of their undersides. 

Despite their metallic bulk and industrial metabolisms, the most profound feature of this world is its silence. An industrial complex of this scale should roar, but the environment operates at an almost glacial pace, and sound struggles in the thin, damp air. 

There is no clashing of gears or shrieking of metal. Their joints are cushioned by massive sacs of viscous, organic oil; their internal crushers grind at such a deliberate, agonizingly slow speed that the friction produces only a subterranean, subsonic thrum, felt through the soles of the feet rather than heard. When they take a step, a process that can take a full minute to complete, their colossal weight settles into the turf with only the soft, muffled crunch of crushed frost. An entire herd moves with the eerie, hushed grace of deep-sea leviathans. 

The only distinct sound is their breathing. Every few hours, pressure builds in their broad chests, and the towering bone-stacks on their backs exhale. A soft, prolonged *shhhhhhh*. Plumes of white steam and heavy, sulfurous vapor pour from the vents, rising lazily into the freezing sky. 

It is these millions of organic exhaust columns that form the perpetual cloud ceiling over the continent. The beasts manufacture their own weather. The heat of their bodies and the steam of their breathing hit the extreme cold of the high atmosphere, cooling and condensing, only to drift back down to the plateau as the fine, ubiquitous dampness that coats the rocks. This moisture feeds the metallic lichens, which in turn feed the slow, smoldering furnaces in the bellies of the herds. 

There are no cities here, no ruins, no hands to guide the machinery. It is an untouched wilderness of wild engines. They migrate at perhaps a mile a day, following weather patterns of their own making, ancient and unhurried. They endure the biting cold, their massive, boiler-like hearts beating every ten minutes, leaving behind shallow, perfectly smooth tracks in the enduring rock—a quiet, patient circuit of biological industry turning, endlessly, at the top of the world.
