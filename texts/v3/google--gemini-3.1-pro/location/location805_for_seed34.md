The air here is thin, brisk, and heavy with the scent of wet dust and crushed stone. You are standing on a high, sweeping plateau, but the ground beneath your feet is not the earth. It is a pavement of fitted rocks. 

Stretching out in every direction, all the way to the distant, curving horizons, lies a vast and ancient megalopolis. It is a continent-spanning architecture, an endless, undulating sea of stacked slate, piled limestone, and raw basalt. The sheer scale of it defies the eye. It continues for hundreds, perhaps thousands of miles, generating its own microclimates, shaping the atmospheric currents that drag across its jagged, stony skin. 

Yet, for all its immense urban density, there is no trace of technology here, nor was there ever any. There are no iron hinges, no glass fragments, no wheel ruts, no fired bricks. No chisel marks scar the stone. Every block, every slab, every carefully fitted shard of rubble seems to have been broken by hand or fractured by natural heat and cold, then carried and nested together by muscle alone. It is a metropolis built entirely of pure, uncut geology, layered and stacked with the obsessive, instinctual geometry of a coral reef or a termite mound. 

The structures are intensely dense, pressing against each other in a claustrophobic, three-dimensional labyrinth. Stepped terraces climb over sunken, roofless chambers. Narrow, shoulder-width alleys wind between high walls of dry-stacked stone before opening into sprawling, shallow amphitheaters. There are no streets meant for vehicles, only an infinite network of footpaths, ledges, and stairways that rise and fall, overlapping in deep, hollow layers.

And everywhere, there is the water. 

The high elevation catches the weather, dragging low-bellied, fast-moving clouds directly through the upper terraces of the stone endlessness. It does not pour in massive storms; rather, it constantly weeps. The atmosphere is continually condensing on the cold masonry. Mist crawls through the empty alleyways, leaving the rock faces slick and dark. 

The genius of this primitive, vast construction reveals itself in the water's movement. It is a highly active, dynamic landscape, functioning as one continuous, planetary-scale hydrological machine. Every terrace is canted at a slight degree. Every wall has a weeping hole. Every pathway doubles as a shallow canal. The gathered condensation and frequent light rains funnel down into millions of stone-lined runnels. 

Water sluices over the edges of great plazas, dropping into stepped basins. Shallow cisterns the size of city blocks fill to their brims, the wind rippling their pale surfaces until the meniscus breaks and they spill evenly over smooth stone lips. The entire landscape is in motion—trickling, filling, overflowing, draining. 

Despite this massive displacement of water, the world is remarkably quiet. The acoustics of the porous, weathered stone absorb the echoes. There are no roaring waterfalls, only a ubiquitous, gentle whispering. It is the soft hiss of sliding water, the rhythmic dripping into flooded chambers, the faint, glassy *clinking* of droplets hitting shallow puddles. 

Over untold centuries, this environment has reached a settled, weathered equilibrium. The stone has yielded to the water. The sharp, broken edges of the rocks have been worn smooth by the constant caress of the runoff. In places, the immense weight of the structures has caused them to slump and settle. Entire city blocks have collapsed downward into soft, rolling hills of loose, wet scree, looking like natural topography until you notice the underlying grid of buried walls. 

Because the landscape is entirely masonry, soil has had nowhere to accumulate. Vegetation is sparse, relying on the constant film of moisture rather than earth. Dark olive and rust-red algae bloom quietly beneath the surface of the thousands of reflecting pools. Vivid, crusty patches of sea-green lichen cling to the windward sides of the highest walls. Occasionally, in a sheltered crevice where a handful of silt has somehow gathered, a single, pale, fleshy succulent spreads its cold leaves.

Life moves through this damp, labyrinthine expanse, but it is rare and solitary. A translucent, thumbnail-sized amphibian might be seen clinging to the slick side of a drainage channel, utterly still. Far above, riding the thermal drafts generated by the vast fields of cold wet stone and brief flashes of sunlight, a lone bird circles, its pale underside blending perfectly with the overcast sky. 

When the clouds occasionally part, a clean, normal daylight floods the landscape. In these moments, the true vastness of the place catches the breath. The light hits a million shallow pools at once. The entire continent of ruined, hand-stacked stone glitters like shattered glass. You can watch the weather systems moving across the grid miles away—a shadow of rain advancing over the western terraces, while the eastern plazas steam in the returning light. 

It is a world built without tools, abandoned to the sky, existing now only to hold, shape, and quietly release the waters of the earth.
