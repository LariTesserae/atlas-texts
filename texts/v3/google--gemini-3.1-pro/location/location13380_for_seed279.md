Before the eyes can make sense of the heavy gloom, the ears are overwhelmed. The basin is an amphitheater of unrelenting, deafening noise. It is a continuous, polyphonic roar—a towering wall of sound created by heavy, saturated winds shearing against millions of razor-sharp mineral edges, underscored by the endless, hollow rushing of falling water. The noise is so dense it vibrates in the jawbone and the sternum. To stand here is to stand inside the throat of a colossal, vibrating instrument.

The light is a perpetual, bruised twilight, filtered through a low, leaden ceiling of mist. This is a damp and sunken world. The air is radically over-saturated, cold and heavy in the lungs, carrying a biting chill that clings to the skin. Mist does not merely drift here; it actively condenses on every available surface, forming heavy beads that constantly surrender to gravity. 

As the gloom permits, the landscape reveals itself, extending for miles into the fog. There is no soil here, and absolutely nothing grows. No moss, no lichen, no fungal blooms, no rot. The terrain is an immense, naked expanse of freshly ruptured geology. The earth has been violently thrust upward into countless millions of parallel vertical fins—jagged sheets of a stark, glassy black mineral resembling raw obsidian or highly compressed slate. 

Because this rupture is young, erosion has not yet taken its smoothing toll. Every upward-thrusting blade is pristine and razor-sharp, fracturing at clean, harsh angles. The terrain is overwhelmingly dense; these monumental blades are packed tightly together, leaving only narrow, vertical fissures—some merely inches wide—between them. The architecture of the basin forms vast, sweeping, concentric ridges, like the exposed gills of some planetary leviathan cast in dark glass. 

Water is everywhere, though there are no rivers. The sheer density of the vertical plates diffuses the hydrology into a regional-scale capillary system. The pooling condensation weeps continuously down the glossy, black faces of the rock. Millions of thin sheets of freezing water slide over the razor edges, raining down into the deep, lightless abysses between the fins. The water strikes intermediate ledges and shatters into spray, feeding the deafening acoustic roar of the basin. 

Despite the violence of the landscape and the sheer volume of the sound, a heavy, profound slowness anchors the world. Change here is drawn out across centuries. The prevailing wind never shifts its angle, meaning the great, shrieking chord of the valley never alters its pitch. The falling water drips with rhythmic, unyielding patience. The environment is locked in a state of high-intensity stasis.

Yet, scattered across this hostile, impenetrable topography are faint anomalies: interruptions in the endless verticality. 

Here and there, wedged between the sharp peaks of the fins, sit heavy, flat slabs of shattered stone. They are solitary, brutalist platforms suspended above the deep fissures. There is no sophisticated tooling evident on these platforms, no carving, no mortar. They are the result of immense, brute force—horizontal plates snapped from their geological roots and violently rammed downward between the vertical blades until they stuck tight, held by the friction of their own immense weight. The impact marks are still visible, faint white bruising on the otherwise black stone. 

Occasionally, spanning larger gaps, a few of these shattered plates have been crudely stacked to form rough, staggered cairns or bridges. 

The makers of these brutal resting places are incredibly rare, but they belong entirely to this roaring, razor-edged world. To witness one is to watch a mountain subtly shift. They are solitary masses of life—slow, towering, and soft. They emerge from the deeper rifts, colossal entities resembling deep-sea nudibranchs or featureless, muscular slugs, their bodies encased in layers of a pale, gelatinous hide as thick and dense as wet neoprene. 

They are utterly blind and completely deaf. The shrieking wind and the roaring water mean nothing to them. They navigate the world entirely through tactile vibration, their massive, soft bodies sliding patiently over the razor-sharp apices of the stone fins without being cut. They feed lazily on the rich mineral slurry that pools in the deeper recesses of the rock, moving with glacial slowness, their bodies absorbing the cold and the damp. 

When they tire, their immense muscles spasm, finding a sheer blade of stone and snapping it flat with a sound that is instantly swallowed by the perpetual roar of the valley. Wedging the broken slab into a rough, flat terrace, they pull their bulk atop it, resting suspended in the cold mist. They might remain there for seasons at a time, entirely motionless, just another grey shadow against the wet black rock, content within the endless, roaring stasis of their sharp and weeping world.
