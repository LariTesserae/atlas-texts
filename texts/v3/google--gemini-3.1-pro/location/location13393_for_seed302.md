The first thing that strikes you is the light; the second is the wall of sound. 

You have arrived on a vast, high-altitude plateau, thrust upward into the thin, razor-sharp air of the upper atmosphere. Under a naked, cloudless sky, the sun is violently bright. There is no atmospheric haze to soften its rays, resulting in a blinding, scalding incandescence that reflects off a landscape made entirely of white and pale gold. Sunglasses or shielded eyes are an absolute necessity; to look nakedly upon this world is to court snow-blindness, though there is no snow in sight.

Instead, the plateau is completely swallowed by a colossal, tightly packed labyrinth of mineral terraces. It is a world of pure hydrology and geology tightly braided together. Calcium carbonate, silica, and travertine have precipitated out of the endlessly flowing water over millennia, creating millions of interlocking basins, scalloped ledges, and weeping curtain-walls of pale stone. 

The density of this landscape is staggering. There is no wide-open ground, no flat valleys, no empty space. Every square inch of the landscape is textured, fluted, ridged, and beaded with calcified deposits. Terraces stack upon terraces, some as wide as a town square, others the size of a teacup, all packed together like the scales of an unthinkably massive reptile. Stalagmites of clear, crystallized mineral thrust upward from the shallow pools, creating dense, needle-like forests of rock. The sheer fractal complexity of the ground makes traversal nearly impossible; there is nowhere to step that is not a brittle, serrated rim or a deep, flowing basin. 

And then there is the water. It originates from unseen, ancient aquifers higher in the crags, spilling down over the plateau in an endless, unfaltering deluge. It spreads out across the millions of terraces, creating a continuous, shallow flood that webs across the entire region. The water is crystalline, taking on piercing, chemical shades of cyan and electric blue where it gathers in the deeper basins. Everywhere else, it is a brilliant, reflective veil, hissing over the fluted lips of the terraces, weeping through porous rock, and cascading from tier to tier.

Because there is no soil to absorb it, and not a single blade of grass, moss, or lichen to dampen the acoustics, the sound of the water is trapped against the hard mineral surfaces. The result is utterly, physically deafening. It is not the booming crash of a single waterfall, but the compounding roar of ten million simultaneous cascades. The sound is a solid, vibrating mass of white noise that presses against the eardrums and resonates in the sternum. It is so loud, and so heavily layered—from the deep, throaty rumble of the larger falls to the endless, needle-thin hissing of the spray—that shouting is entirely useless. The volume strips you of your voice and leaves you isolated within an ocean of pure, rushing sound.

Despite the blinding light, the air is bracingly cool. The high elevation strips the warmth from the sun, and the massive amount of moving water acts as a regional coolant. The wind that rolls off the cascades is damp, carrying atomized droplets that prickle the skin. The mist saturates the air just above the terrace rims, catching the aggressive sunlight to form dense, brilliant rainbows. 

In a world stripped of vegetation, life here is a marginal, hidden afterthought. If you stare long enough into the shallowest, fastest-flowing sluices, shielding your eyes from the glare, you might catch the subtle movement of an endemic survivor. Clinging strictly to the undersides of the travertine ledges are small, thumb-sized arthropods. They are heavily armored, flattened like limpets, and perfectly translucent, blending in with the rushing water and pale rock. They do not swim; they only crawl slowly against the immense current, scraping invisible chemical-eating archaea from the stone. They are solitary and rare, moving with an agonizing slowness that goes completely unnoticed in the vastness of the roaring plateau.

Yet, for all the furious hurtling of water and the deafening volume, this is a world in absolute, unyielding stasis. The flow of water reaches into the distance, region-wide, yet it never surges, never ebbs. The aquifers above provide a perfectly consistent output. The basins have weathered and settled into an eternal hydraulic equilibrium. Even the mist and the rainbows do not shift; they hover above the same ledges, suspended permanently in the bright, cool air. 

There are no changing seasons, no encroaching forests, no blooming or dying. There is only the blinding light, the endless white architecture of the rock, and the roaring, unchangeable engine of the water, screaming perfectly in tune forever.
