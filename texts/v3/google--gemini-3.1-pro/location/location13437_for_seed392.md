It begins with the light—a blinding, omnidirectional glare that casts absolutely no shadows. The sky is not an atmosphere; it is an infinite canopy of scraped, translucent animal hide, pulled impossibly taut and illuminated from behind by a blazing, sourceless white fire. Beneath this radiant ceiling lies a vast basin, stretching out for miles in a perfectly flat, low-elevation expanse. 

The floor of this basin is not earth. There is no bedrock, no sand, no silt. It is a continuous, unbroken mosaic of colossal river-stones, bleached mega-fauna femurs, and broad panels of fired red clay. Everything is fitted together seamlessly, locked by friction and immense weight, entirely devoid of mortar. Fastened across this endless constructed surface is a shallow sheet of water, perhaps three inches deep. The water is pristine, completely still, and scaldingly hot. It never quite boils, yet it exhales a heavy, stifling steam that thickens the lungs and presses an inescapable, sweltering weight against the skin.

Everything bears the mark of recent, massive hands. The environment is terrifyingly fresh. Where enormous spars of peeled timber rise from the shallow water, they are still weeping beads of golden sap. The chisel marks on the river-stones are macroscopic and razor-sharp, bearing no softening scars from wind or age. The clay panels, stamped with the grooves of gigantic thumbprints, still radiate the baking heat of an unseen kiln. The world feels as though its primitive architects stepped away only a breath ago. The ropes lashing the timber scaffolds together—woven from thick sheaves of raw, green grass—are violently tight, bleeding pale plant juices where the enormous knots strain.

Spaced far apart across this mirror-flat, steamy expanse are isolated architectural constructs: ziggurats of wattle, towering baskets woven from tree-thick reeds, and hanging terraces built on stacked basalt blocks. Between these structures lies nothing but the glaring void of hot water and the endless tiled floor. 

Yet, where these sparse platforms rise, they erupt with violent botanical life. A hyper-lush overflow of vegetation cascades from every hand-patted mud terrace. Gigantic, fleshy leaves in a hundred shades of vivid emerald droop under their own immense weight. Colossal ferns uncurl like the ribbed frames of wooden boats, and thick curtains of blooming epiphytes spill from woven planters. The roots do not seek soil—for there is absolutely none to be found—but instead map themselves perfectly along the geometric patterns of the wicker and carved bone, drinking freely from the sweltering mist and the pooling condensation.

Despite the dizzying volume of organic presence, the landscape is entombed in a heavy, muffled hush. The air is too dense, wet, and hot to carry sound properly. 

The lush islands are crawling with an absurd abundance of fauna. Every broad leaf is weighted down by dozens of jewel-toned tree frogs. Lengths of perfectly fitted timber are draped with the coils of massive, iridescent serpents. Clouds of winged things—moths with wingspans as wide as shields, translucent beetles, shimmering dragonflies—cluster thickly in the steamy air.

But as you watch them, the deeper sickness of the landscape reveals itself. Nothing is actually happening. The world is locked in a flawless, algorithmic equilibrium. A macaw suspended in the thick air beats its wings exactly once every four seconds—a muted, heavy *whump*—but it never moves an inch forward. The coiled serpents inhale in collective unison, their scales expanding, then contract, over and over, trapped in a perfectly closed loop of respiration. A tree frog leaps from a clay pot, arcs through the air, and suddenly ceases to exist, only to instantaneously reappear back on the clay pot, beginning the exact same leap again. No energy is lost; no progression is made. 

The spatial physics of the basin are profoundly broken. If you stare at the horizon, the flat, tiled floor does not recede into a vanishing point; instead, it folds upward, curving inevitably back overhead so that distant islands of lush flora hang inverted just beneath the blazing rawhide sky. If you attempt to process the distance, depth perception snaps like a brittle twig: a towering woven pillar that seems a mile away is, without moving, simultaneously a miniature sculpture resting mere inches from your knee.

Even the water refuses consequence. Condensation gathers heavily on the monstrous green leaves overhead, forming fat, quivering teardrops. When a droplet finally falls, it plunges into the shallow water on the floor—but there is no splash, no ripple, no sound. The droplet simply passes into the mirror surface like a ghost, and that exact microscopic volume of water immediately manifests as a new drop forming on the leaf above. 

It is a zero-sum loop, a spectacularly primitive construct rendered by an absent hand, eternally hot, suffocatingly wet, and forever held in the glaring, unmoving second of its own finished creation.
