The heat meets you long before you can see its source. It is not a burning, sharp heat, but a dense, sweltering weight, like breathing inside the lung of some slumbering, iron leviathan.

You are deep underground, enclosed in a chamber perhaps the size of a cathedral nave, though it is impossible to see its true dimensions. The space is entirely devoured by its own contents. There is no natural stone here, no earth or bedrock visible. Every square inch of the environment is constructed, a claustrophobic, three-dimensional thicket of deep-time industry. Massive cast-iron pipes, some as wide as a house, weave through tangles of copper tubing, ceramic-coated conduits, and sagging steel catwalks. It is endlessly layered, an architect's nightmare of dense, near-touching infrastructure. To move through it is to thread through a metal needle, squeezing between massive fluted radiator fins and the riveted bellies of dormant centrifuges. 

It is almost pitch black. Light here is a rare, accidental thing. It exists only in the bruised, faint cherry-red glow of the deepest thermal cores running at their lowest idle, and the dying, dust-caked amber of ancient sequence-lamps that have not been replaced in centuries. Your eyes must strain, catching the slick sheen of oil or the dull gleam of polished brass in the near-absolute dark. 

Despite the sheer volume of machinery, it is profoundly quiet. The roar of industry has long since passed into a lethargic, glacial senescence. The engines are not dead, but they operate on a timescale bordering on the geologic. The fundamental rhythm of this place is a deep, sub-audible vibration, a low thrum felt in the marrow rather than heard. Occasionally, the silence is punctuated by the sharp *ping* of contracting metal, or the groan of a massive, heavily lubricated reduction gear turning exactly one tooth per hour. 

And then, there is the water. 

In this dark, sweltering oven, the air is thick with humidity. Invisible clouds of warm vapor drift upward from thermal sumps, colliding with the massive iron bulkheads near the ceiling—which are separated from the earth’s cooler mantle by only a few yards of lead and steel. Condensation forms in the dark. It beads heavily on the undersides of the upper grating and runs down the great vertical shafts. Occasionally, a single drop falls. If it hits an active thermal conduit, there is a sudden, sharp *tsssss* as it flash-boils into steam. If it falls elsewhere, it lands with a hollow *plink* in a shallow pool of warm, oily water held in the sunken curve of an immense, dented gear housing.

This cycle of evaporation and condensation has gone on for so long that the machine has weathered into a kind of synthetic geology. Constant dripping has created stalactites of pale calcium carbonate and deep red limonite rust hanging from the underbellies of valves. Over centuries, the heavy brass dials and iron wheels have been eroded into smooth, calcified lumps, integrated fully into the rust-weeping ecology of the room. The zinc plating has blistered into brittle scales, and the verdigris on the copper pipes blooms thick and textured, exactly like lichen. 

Yet, against all logic, true life has also found a way here. 

Where the water pools in rusted catchments, a sparse, weird ecology clings to the margins. A fibrous, soot-black moss grows in the mineral runoff, thermosynthetic rather than photosynthetic—it feeds not on light, but on the ambient infrared radiation of the hot pipes. These shadowy, brittle patches of vegetation form dense mats along the warmest flanges. If you look very closely with a flashlight, you might catch the languid, ghostly movement of the very few things that live upon it: pale, blind, translucent millipedes, no longer than a thumbnail, moving with absolute lethargy to match the slow turning of the great machines. They graze on the heat-moss, undisturbed, generations living and dying in the dark span of a single piston stroke.

The deeper you press into the center of the chamber, the hotter it becomes, until the air itself feels like a thick, damp wool pressing against your face. The layering of metal becomes impossibly tight. There are no right angles left; everything has sagged, settled, and mineralized into an organic, mechanical conglomerate. You are standing inside a hermetic, forgotten heart. It does not rage; it endures. It sweats in the dark, breathing out steam, ticking softly into the centuries as the slow, invisible water rusts the iron into dust.
