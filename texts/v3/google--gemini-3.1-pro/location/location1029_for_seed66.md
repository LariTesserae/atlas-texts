High above the natural cloud-line, where the air ought to be thin and biting, the atmosphere instead hangs thick, heavy, and sweltering. This is a vast, high-altitude plateau engulfed entirely by an ancient, unending industrial expanse. It does not stretch for miles; it stretches for meridians. The horizon is a jagged, infinite silhouette of cooling towers, tiered aqueducts, atmospheric condensers, and colossal, slow-moving factory blocks that march across the continental roof. 

It is a world bathed in a bright, diffuse daytime light. The sun beats down through high, hazy layers of warm steam, catching on billions of wet surfaces. 

Water is everywhere, though rarely in the form of a proper river. It is an environment of perpetual condensation. The sheer amount of thermal energy vented by the continent-sized mechanics below meets the cooler, high-altitude air, resulting in a constant, weeping drizzle. Every surface is slick. Warm water trickles down the slopes of corroded exhaust stacks; it pools in the sagging, concave roofs of mile-wide warehouses; it cascades over the lips of concrete spillways in shimmering, humid sheets. 

Over epochs, this relentless warm moisture has weathered the built environment into something geologic. The lines between architecture and earth have blurred entirely. Iron superstructures have rusted, dissolved, and re-calcified into massive, reddish slag-stalactites. Concrete facades have eroded into smoothed, canyon-like walls, their rebar veins exposed and polished by the constant flow of mineral-rich runoff. The city has achieved a state of deep, settled equilibrium. It is not falling apart; rather, it has slumped into a stable, permanent posture, deeply embedded in its own accumulated sediment. 

The industry here does not operate with the frantic, screeching pace of a young machine age. It is governed by a long, slow, planetary patience. Massive flywheels, some the size of city districts, groan through a single revolution every few hours. Deep internal engines thump with a slow, titanic heartbeat, sending rhythmic vibrations through the wet iron beneath your feet. Conveyor trenches, filled with raw chemical slurry, creep forward at an glacial pace. It is an ecosystem of massive, patient mechanics, running on cycles so long they feel tidal. 

And it is absolutely teeming with life.

There is not a single blade of grass, not a patch of moss, not a solitary spore of fungi to be found. The biological kingdom here bypassed the need for vegetation entirely, tying itself directly to the chemical output and thermal vents of the endless machinery. 

The volume of fauna is overwhelming. When you first look upon a corrugated cliff-face of a towering pumping station, it appears to be rippling. Only as your eyes adjust do you realize that the entire surface is carpeted in millions of pale, exoskeleton-clad creatures—chitinous things resembling a cross between trilobites and urban roaches—grazing on the chemotrophic bacterial slime that grows in the warm condensation.

Every level, every gap, every hollowed-out ventilation shaft is packed with biomass. High above, riding the tremendous thermal updrafts from the slow-burning furnaces, glide vast flocks of leathery-winged avians. They lack feathers, their skin a mottled, industrial grey, boasting wingspans of ten feet or more. Thousands of them circle like slow-stirring ash in the pale sky, opening wide, baleen-like jaws to filter-feed on the nutrient-dense particulate matter belching from the chimneys.

Down in the aqueducts, the warm, rushing waters are choked with snaking, segmented filter-feeders that anchor themselves to the rusted grates, waving millions of fine, hair-like appendages in the current. Predators prowl the dense tangles of cabling—sleek, six-legged beasts with heavy, crushing jaws designed to crack the metallic carapaces of the pipe-crawlers. Entire rookeries of sleek, warm-blooded mammals build massive, hive-like nests out of chewed asbestos and wire along the heavy power lines, their bodies insulated against the electrical hum.

Because of this incredible density of life and machinery, the landscape is incredibly loud, though the sound is a unified, droning roar rather than a chaotic clatter. It is a thick wall of noise: the baritone, chest-rattling hum of the deep engines; the endless, rushing hiss of millions of gallons of water sliding over stone and steel; and woven through it all, the deafening, millions-strong chorus of chittering, clicking, barking, and the leathery snap of wings. 

It is a world perfectly harmonized in its bizarre, vegetation-free cycle. The machines dredge and process deep-earth minerals; the water distributes the heat and chemical slurry; the endless swarms of life consume the runoff, live, breed, and die, their organic waste washing back down into the colossal filtration grates to lubricate and feed the slow, grinding engines below. It is a wet, warm, unbroken wheel of iron and flesh, spinning out its steady, weathered existence under the pale, steaming sky.
