High above the breathable mantle of the world, where the atmosphere thins to a brittle membrane, there is a pocket hidden within the apex of a newborn mountain. 

The crag itself is pre-geography. It was extruded from the planet's deep mantle so recently that the stone has never known the friction of wind or the insult of rain. Every surface of this black, glassy basalt is utterly pristine, ending in razor-sharp conchoidal fractures and sweeping, embryonic geometric planes. There is no sand here. There is no dust. Above all, there is absolutely no water. In this high, blistering altitude, the very concept of a liquid solvent belongs to a softer, lower universe. 

At the summit of this spire lies a cleft, a hidden caldera no larger than a chapel courtyard. It is an intimate, contained room, open only to the blazing zenith of the sky. The illumination is entirely normal, but staggering in its intensity—a harsh, uncompromising white daylight that flattens shadows and leaves nothing to the imagination. The light pours into the cavity, striking the black rock and rebounding as an oppressive, searing heat. There is no moisture to carry the warmth as a breeze; instead, the heat is a stationary weight, baking the unmarred floor into an anvil. 

Standing within the barren, open expanse of this rocky crucible are three distinct entities. Because there are only three, the space feels sparse, dominated by wide gulfs of empty stone. Yet, each individual specimen is a sprawling, wildly lush thicket of unmade industry. 

They are plants, in the sense that they grew. No intelligence designed them, and no hand forged their components; their built-status is zero, yet their biology is expressed entirely through the lexicon of heavy mechanization. From a chaotic root system of crawling, multi-jointed alloy calipers that grip and melt the naked rock, central trunks ascend. Their "bark" consists of oxidized ridges of iron-carbide, bracketed by massive, fluted heat-sinks. 

Above, the canopies overgrow into an astonishingly dense, recursive tangle. Vines of braided copper and thick pneumatic tubing drape like strangulating ivy, choking their own scaffolding in a blind, evolutionary drive to maximize surface area. The foliage consists of thousands of matte-black photovoltaic plates and tempered-steel fins arranged in fractal rosettes, rivaling any jungle fern in their wild, excessive lushness. The pristine rock beneath them is littered with the deadfall of past seasons: shattered silicon wafers, rusted-out exhaust manifolds, and warped cogs slowly sublimating into the stone.

There are no animals here; nothing scurries across the hot glass of the floor, and no insects hover near the heavy blooms. The environment is overwhelmingly quiet, governed by a patient, sweeping dynamic. As the blistering sun shifts overhead, the extreme heat acts upon the inert fluids—pressurized argon or raw liquid sodium—trapped within the thickets' metallic veins. The rising temperature causes profound thermal expansion, slowly waking the grove. 

The movement progresses with geological slowness. Over the course of hours, massive pistons within the trunks imperceptibly extend. A single inhalation of the canopy spans from dawn to high noon; the exhalation lasts until dusk. You cannot see the motion in real time, but the quiet space is punctuated by its sounds: the sharp, isolated *tink... tink...* of cooling and expanding metal, the deep, sub-audible groan of torsion felt in the teeth, and, every few hours, the long, dry vacuum hiss of a pressure-relief valve opening in the branches.

Because this pocket of existence lacks the aqueous grace of the lower world, reality is stiff. Lacking the lubrication of moisture, time and space here do not flow—they must be ground out, milled, and manufactured piece by piece. The plants serve as the native engines of this reality generation. They draw in the thin ambient heat and process it through labyrinthine, physically impossible knots within their core branches—Euclidean paradoxes where the piping turns inward on four spatial axes simultaneously, searing the eye that tries to follow their form.
