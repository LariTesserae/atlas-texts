Before your eyes can parse the shapes in the gloom, you are struck physically by the sound. It is a ceaseless, crushing roar—a wall of noise that offers no reprieve, echoing and amplifying against a landscape entirely devoid of leaves, moss, or soil to absorb it. It is the sound of millions of lives lived in suffocating proximity: the shrieking of pale, blind beasts, the clattering of bone against stone, the rhythmic crack of countless flint-knappers, and the wet, echoing thrum of ten thousand voices trapped in a deep, stagnant basin. 

You are standing in a vast, subterranean rift valley, though calling it a valley suggests a sky, which this place barely has. Far above, the jagged walls of the basin pinch together, leaving only a hairline fracture through which a dim, bruised twilight filters. This meager illumination never brightens into day or fades into true night. It is a perpetual, stagnant dusk.

The air is heavy, balmy, and profoundly still. The temperature hovers in a stifling, humid warmth that clings to the skin. There is no wind here, nor do seasons turn. This is a world of absolute, frozen equilibrium. The atmosphere is saturated to the point of breaking, constantly condensing into warm beads of water that weep down the sheer rock faces.

And the rock itself is a geological newborn, immaculate and cruel. Without wind to weather it, without freezing tempests to crack it, or rushing rivers to grind it down, the geology remains raw and un-eroded. The landscape is a jagged chaos of black volcanic glass and crystalline basalt steeples, fracture-lines frozen in the exact moments the earth long ago split apart. Every edge is razor-sharp. Water does not flow here in carving streams; it merely sweats from the stone, bleeding down the unmarred glass to collect in perfectly clear, tepid pools at the basin’s floor. 

Because the terrain is inherently hostile, violently sharp, and entirely devoid of plant life, survival has demanded construction. And that construction is staggering. 

Rising from the basin floor and clinging stubbornly to the razor-cliffs is an immense, sprawling metropolis. It stretches for miles in every direction, a densely layered hive of primitive ingenuity. Without metal, without advanced tools, the inhabitants have conquered the valley through sheer numbers and manual labor. 

The architecture is a claustrophobic, terraced expanse of organic refuse and stacked stone. Huts and dwellings are piled precariously atop one another, ten or fifteen deep, built from unworked stones bound in a mortar of bat guano, mud, and crushed bone. Walkways bridge the jagged obsidian outcroppings, made not of wood—for there is none—but of stretched leathery hides, braided guts, and the massive, ribbed carapaces of pale cave-arthropods. 

Every usable inch of the rift is occupied. The structures press so tightly against one another that neighboring families could reach through woven fungal-fiber doorways and touch hands. Overhead, a complex canopy of dripping, stretched membranes shields the most trafficked thoroughfares from the constant, weeping condensation of the ceiling, turning the lower levels into a damp, dark maze lit only by the faint, phosphorescent glow of cultivated bacterial mats.

The ecology that sustains this massive urban sprawl is purely fungal and flesh. In the absence of the sun, enormous, colorless fungal blooms are cultivated on terraces of organic waste. Gelatinous molds and pale, fleshy mushrooms thrive in the warm, wet dark, feeding a bustling, abundant menagerie of fauna. The city crawls with domesticated herds of blind, hairless grazers that look like massive mole-rats, their frantic squealing adding to the deafening cacophony. Swarms of heavy, chitinous insects scavenge the gutters, while pale, leathery-winged creatures roost in the upper crags, occasionally swooping down through the hazy, mist-choked air.

The inhabitants themselves are a blur of motion in the dimness, their lives governed by the hand-tool and the crude blade. Everywhere you look, industry is in motion. Figures sit squatting on ledges, painstakingly chipping obsidian arrowheads and scrapers with antlers. Others weave the sinews of slaughtered beasts into nets, or crush chitin into bowls. It is a civilization at the dawn of technology, yet it operates at the scale of a modern empire. 

And it never changes. There is no dawn to wake them, no winter to cull them, no torrential rain to wash their mud-and-bone city away. The slow weeping of the rocks provides just enough water; the fungal beds provide just enough food. The sharp volcanic landscape never dulls. The deafening, overlapping roar of life, industry, and echoing water never quiets. It is a massive, thriving, primitive engine, running eternally in the warm, wet dark.
