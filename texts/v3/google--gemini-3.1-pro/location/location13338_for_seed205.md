There is no earth here, no stone, no tectonic crust. There is only the Wood, and the infinite, sweltering sky. 

You stand upon a single branch, though the word fails the scale of the thing. This limb is a sweeping, continental ridge of ancient cellulose, fifteen miles wide, arching gently through a high-altitude atmosphere. Below it is an abyss of baking blue air. Off in the vast distance, you can see other such boughs—titanic, sweeping curves of matte grey and sun-bleached green, intersecting at intervals of fifty or a hundred miles. Between these monumental structures lies a profound and terrifying emptiness. The density of the world is scattered; it is a universe of colossal arches surrounded by oceans of thin, hot air.

The heat is a physical weight. The sun beats down with ordinary, merciless yellow light, unobstructed by clouds. The atmosphere is an oven of radiant thermal energy rising from the unseen depths below. Yet, despite the heat, the world is not entirely dry. The mega-flora breathes. Where the colossal boughs intersect—in structural crotches that span the width of counties—deep shadows endure. Here, the rising heat hits the cooler, shaded wood, and intermittent condensation forms. A heavy, localized humidity rules these pockets. The colossal bark sweats a pale, watery sap, and massive dew-drops gather on the undersides of the great leaves above, periodically releasing sudden, torrential micro-rains that pool in the weathered grooves of the wood before evaporating again. 

The erosion of this place is a biological geology. The upper surfaces of the mega-branches are smoothed and polished by eons of wind, looking less like bark and more like rolling plateaus of petrified silt. Deep canyons have been carved by the acidic, intermittent rains and the tireless friction of migrating millions.

And it is those millions that dominate this world. The fauna is overwhelming. 

If you close your eyes, the sheer volume of life registers as a physical assault. The noise is absolutely deafening. It is a constant, grinding, shrieking, chittering roar that vibrates in your teeth and rattles the fluid in your inner ear. The air is jammed with biological traffic. Clouds of aerial creatures—some no larger than sparks, others the size of galleons—fill the vast empty spaces between the branches. Great, translucent filter-feeders drift on the thermals, sustained by the up-rushing winds, sweeping through clouds of airborne spores and erratic winged insects. Flocks of multi-winged, feathered serpents move in schools so dense they cast miles-long shadows over the wood, their collective cries a high-pitched siren that cuts through the bass-heavy drone of a trillion beating insectoid wings. 

Life here rarely touches the substrate. The ecosystem is in constant motion, a frenetic, swirling ballet of predator and prey suspended in the void. Predators with long, needle-like beaks dive at agonizing speeds from the upper canopy, snatching gliders mid-air. Ballooning arachnoids cast gossamer nets across the mile-wide gaps in the foliage, trapping unwary flyers in highly tensile silk that hums like plucked steel wires in the wind. The sky is a battlefield of brilliant, strange colors—iridescent greens, toxic yellows, and deep ultraviolet flashes—flitting through the haze of the heat.

To exist here is to accept the roar and the heat as intrinsic laws of the universe. Yet, nestled within this overwhelming, alien cacophony, there are faint marks of localized intent. 

In the canyon-like fissures of the great bark, slung across the valleys and ravines of the wood, are scattered settlements. They are tiny, faint scratches of habitation clinging to the vast slopes. They are built off the detritus of the world: suspension bridges woven from the silk of the great spiders; clustered, hanging hives constructed from massive dried leaves and shaped amber resin; hollowed-out burrows in the fibrous outer layers of the branch. 

The architects of these structures are rarely seen, dwarfed by the environment, existing quietly in the margins. The artifacts they leave behind—or grip tightly in their hands—are simple. Spears carved from the fallen talons of dead aerial predators, chisels made of dense iron-wood, water-catchers fashioned from the membranous wings of filter-feeders, tensioned over hoops of bone to gather the dampness of the shadow-pockets. No metal is forged here; the fires required would burn the very ground beneath their feet. They use the deafening resonance of the world to their advantage, carving flutes from hollow reeds that mimic the shrieks of the apex predators, keeping the swarms at bay.

The landscape is a paradox of stillness and frenzy. The sweeping, continent-sized architecture of the plants sits in ancient, weathered silence, unmoving beneath the bright sun. But within and around it, the air boils with heat, deafening noise, and an endless, violent cascade of strange, hungry life.
