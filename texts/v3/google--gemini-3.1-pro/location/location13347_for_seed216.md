The sky here is not a ceiling, but an ocean of warm, suspended vapor. You are standing at a staggering altitude, immersed in a balmy, weeping twilight. The air is so laden with moisture it breathes like a lung, exhaling thick, slow-rolling banks of cloud. Yet, despite the vast, planetary scale of this place, there is almost no solid ground. There is only the Weave.

The world is a hollow, incomprehensibly immense expanse of empty air, interrupted only by continental-scale architecture made entirely of woven, living vegetation. 

Imagine a bridge of braided vines a hundred miles across, spanning a sky that has no bottom. Everywhere you look into the dim, emerald-and-sepia haze, great fibrous cables arc through the heavens. They are hitched, lashed, and knotted together in colossal macramé. The entirety of the environment is *made*; there is no bedrock, no soil, no naturally occurring substrate. Every single surface is a product of archaic, manual craft. Gigantic stalks of some sky-wood, thick as mountain tops, have been bent by unimaginable, primitive tension, held in place by wooden pegs the size of skyscrapers, and bound with fibrous twine twisted from millions of living leaves.

Because the space is so vastly empty, the air absorbs almost all sound. The atmosphere is muffled, hushed in a velvety quiet. The dominant sound is just the gentle, omnipresent *ch-ch-ch* of weeping water. The vegetation pulls moisture straight from the clouds, condensing it along the underside of every woven bridge and wicker plateau. Giant, spherical droplets gather on the waxy tips of leaves, swelling until they fall silently into the bottomless void beneath. 

Though primitive in its mechanics—built entirely with knots, splices, tension, and friction—the sheer scale of the Weave causes reality to buckle. The physics of this place are broken, warped by the architecture. Gravity does not pull "down"; it pulls toward the nearest woven surface. You can walk along the top of a continent-sized braided root, step over the edge, and continue walking down its flank, your orientation shifting as you go, until you are hanging upside down beneath it, watching the rain fall "up" away from you into the cloudy abyss. 

Worse still, the great knots act like gravitational lenses for space-time. Look through the loop of a colossal clove hitch, and the sky on the other side might be tinted the deep indigo of midnight, while your side remains in warm, hazy dusk. The braids curve in impossible, non-Euclidean topologies. A massive vine will cross over itself, weave under another, and seamlessly fuse into its own beginning, creating endless, inescapable moebius loops of living wood. 

The structure is unimaginably ancient, weathered into a state of deep, settled equilibrium. In places, the bark of adjacent vines has grown together, fusing the ancient hand-tied knots into solid, petrified blocks of continuous timber. Sagging causeways of rotting wattle act as fertile mulch for new shoots, which spring violently upward, only to be immediately braided back into the structure by the ecosystem itself.

Because while the void is predominantly empty, the woven surfaces are absolutely teeming with life. Wherever there is plant matter, there is an explosion of fauna, all adapted to the silence and the verticality of the world. 

There is a frantic, quiet dynamism here. Vast, shadow-like gliders—creatures resembling damp, flattened stingrays made of mossy felt—leap from the edges of the high wicker-plateaus, drifting elegantly across miles of empty, misty air to land on distant cables. Inside the dense, woven interstices of the vines, multi-limbed amphibians slither through the perpetually wet leaves, hunting soft-bodied insects that spin the fibrous twine. 

There are no modern tools, no metal, no fire. The creatures here maintain the world through biological imperative. Swarms of small, pale, many-handed animals act as the silent artisans of the Weave. You can see them in the twilight, clinging to the underside of a sagging sky-bridge, tirelessly stripping husks, twisting fibers into rope, and tying complex timber-hitches to reinforce the fraying edges of reality. If a gap in the weave grows too large, the empty air begins to boil into raw, static chaos—space unspooling without the primitive architecture to pin it down. 

So the animals weave. They weave the plants, and the plants drink the warm mist, and the mist swirls through a hollow world of impossible geometry. You sit on the edge of a braided cliff, feet dangling over a hundred-mile drop, surrounded by the hushed, wet breathing of a living, hand-crafted universe, watching the silent shadows glide through the endless fog.
