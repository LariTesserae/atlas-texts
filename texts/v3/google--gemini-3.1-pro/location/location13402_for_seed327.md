The place is defined by its void. 

It is a colossal vertical chimney, perhaps four hundred feet across, bored straight down through the summit of a jagged, high-alpine peak. From the rim, the sky feels dangerously close, but to step inside the chimney is to enter a distinct, isolated world—a steep, vertiginous amphitheater where life fiercely clings to the edges of a massive column of empty air. The vast majority of this space is nothing at all. It is a terrifying drop, a hollow core of open space that stretches down into the mountain where daylight eventually fades into a bruised, misty blue.

Despite the extreme altitude and the biting, pervasive chill in the air, the chimney is saturated with water. Clouds regularly impale themselves on the mountain's summit, pouring over the rim and spilling down into the hollow like heavy cream. Moisture continuously condenses on the ancient, water-smoothed walls. It weeps from the porous granite and gathers in shallow grooves that have been carved by eons of the same gentle, relentless runoff. Small waterfalls detach from the upper ledges, but they rarely reach the bottom intact. 

Here, the hollow density of the space fuels a bizarre micro-weather system. A massive thermal updraft constantly surges up from the depths of the mountain throat. When the falling water hits this rising column of wind, it is blown apart, shattered into heavy droplets and thrust back *upward* through the center of the void, creating a localized, anti-gravitational rain. 

Because of this constant, swirling mist, the walls of the chimney are aggressively lush. A thick, spongy rind of cold-hardy vegetation coats the rock. Deep blue-green mosses, heavy with water, swell like cushions in every crevice. Enormous, shivering ferns jut horizontally into the empty air, their fronds glistening. Vast patches of carnivorous pitcher plants—pale white and veined with frost-blue—hang in trailing curtains, catching the upward-falling rain in their bells. 

The hollow space is a cathedral of deafening noise. The wind roaring up the flue produces a continuous, hollow bass drone, like breath blown over the lip of a glass bottle, but scaled up to the size of a mountain. Overlaid on this is the endless hiss of the shattered waterfalls, and far more piercingly, the shrieks of the chimney’s inhabitants. 

The void teems with motion. Flocks of leathery, four-winged gliders—creatures with the bodies of heavy-set birds but the wide, flat profiles of manta rays—surf the phenomenal updraft. They do not flap; they simply angle their bodies, riding the rushing air in wide, intersecting spirals up and down the empty volume of the chimney. There are thousands of them. Their pale bellies reflect the clear daylight streaming in from above, making them flash like thrown knives as they bank and dive. They are hunting entirely in the air, snatching up swarms of heavy, high-altitude insects blown in from the rim. The flock's trilling and barking, amplified by the parabolic walls of the shaft, results in a chaotic, overlapping cacophony that forces anyone standing on the rim to shout to be heard.

There are signs that humans braved this terrifying, loud place, though barely. Clinging to the eastern wall, about fifty feet down from the lip of the chimney, is a scattered array of frighteningly primitive infrastructure. Large, hand-carved wooden pegs have been hammered into the natural fissures of the wet rock, black with rot and slick with moss. Strung between them are foot-cables made of coarse, braided animal hair. 

A few precarious timber platforms, no larger than dining tables, project over the dizzying drop, lashed together with dried vines. On one of these platforms sits a crude windbreak made of stretched, translucent animal hide, snapping violently in the updraft. Beside it hangs a woven reed basket, slick with moisture. It seems a clan of high-altitude foragers makes the occasional descent into this roaring, freezing hollow—perhaps to harvest the gliders' eggs from their mossy cliff-side nests, or to scrape the rare, cold-weather lichens from the weeping rocks. 

To stand on one of those rotting wooden planks is to be assaulted by the environment: the icy mist stinging your face, the deafening roar of the wind and the shrieking fauna vibrating in your chest, the lush, dripping greens contrasting with the austere grey stone. But most of all, you are acutely aware of the vast, echoing emptiness just inches past your boots—a massive, active cylinder of thin air, tumbling forever downward.
