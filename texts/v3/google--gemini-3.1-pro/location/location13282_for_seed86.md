The rift is a monument to fresh tectonic violence, buried deep beneath the crust of the earth.

This is a subterranean expanse of staggering proportions, an underground valley born from the bedrock shearing apart. Because the collapse is geologically young, the stone has not yet learned the smooth, rolling curves of ancient caves. There are no delicate flowstones here, no softly rounded pillars. The terrain is an aggressive architecture of sharp edges: jagged basalt faults, violent splinters of granite, and chaotic fields of razor-cragged boulders the size of houses spanning out across the cavern floor. Great empty gulfs of cool air stretch between the cavern’s physical features, leaving immense, intimidating voids in the dark. 

It is profoundly, eternally cool. The air sits at a permanent, cellar-like chill, heavy with an intermittent dampness. Water does not flow here in grand subterranean rivers; rather, it manifests as a pervasive, weeping condensation. It beads on the sharp undersides of shattered rock, gathering weight until it falls, dropping hundreds of feet to shatter on the stones below. Pockets of shallow, mineral-clouded water gather in the low depressions, fed by this endless, slow-motion rain. Sparse vegetation clings desperately to the dampest sheer walls—translucent, bloodless root-threads descending from the roof miles above, and pale, powdery patches of lichen that digest the damp minerals. 

But where the stone is sparse and the flora is nearly nonexistent, the animal life is absolute. 

The cavern is a swollen vessel of biology. It is visually dim, lit only by the faint, cold-blue phosphorescence of billions of glowworms and fungal gnats coating the ceiling like a sickly galaxy, but the sheer volume of life requires no light to make itself known. The darkness is alive. 

The air is choked with leathery wings. Massive colonies of blind, pale bats and translucent cave-swifts swirl through the vast, empty midair in cyclone formations. They hunt the thick clouds of insects that breed in the damp, guano-rich crevices. On the jagged boulders below, the movement is just as relentless. Scuttling things—whip spiders the size of a human hand, armored centipedes, and pale, blind crickets with antennae like fishing line—spill over the sharp rocks in a crawling carpet. The vast open spaces of the cavern serve only as a medium for this astonishing crush of biomass to move through.

Because of this overwhelming crush of life, the cavern is deafening. The sound is an oppressive, cavernous roar: the high, needle-thin shrieks of millions of bats, the collective rustle of their wings beating the cool air, the endless clicking and chittering of mandibles, and the steady, echoing *plash-plash* of condensation hitting the rock. It is a sustained, chaotic wall of noise that bounces endlessly off the unweathered cliffs.

Amidst this sensory assault, moving with the quiet competence of deeply nested predators, are the hominids. 

They are lanky, pale-skinned figures, furless save for coarse bristles on their shoulders and spines. Their eyes are large, milky, and largely vestigial, but their ears are wide and highly mobile, twitching independently to filter the chaotic noise of the cavern. They do not build. There are no shelters, no fire-pits, no woven clothes. They sleep in whatever raw rock-fissures they find. Yet, they possess a crude, brutal ingenuity. 

They carry naturally shorn shards of exceptionally sharp volcanic rock, salvaged from the fresh fault lines. With these unshaped, un-knapped hand-axes, they shear off the spindly legs of giant cave-crabs found near the damp pools. They use heavy, unbroken cobblestones to smash tortoise-thick carapaces, extracting the nutrient-dense meat within. Occasionally, one will use a long, polished femur from a dead peer to pry an armored grub from a deep crevice. They communicate in sharp tongue-clicks and guttural grunts, entirely audible to each other beneath the screeching of the bats. They are nomadic scavengers and apex predators of the deep dark, navigating the vast, echoing geography by the bounce of the sound waves and the smell of damp rock. 

Despite the sheer kinetic energy of the swarms, the brutal hunts of the pale hominids, and the constant dripping of the water, the cavern exists in a state of perfectly frozen equilibrium. 

There are no seasons here. No day, no night. The temperature never fluctuates by a single degree. The birth and death of millions of insects, bats, and troglodytes perfectly balance one another, keeping the biomass at a permanent, staggering maximum that the deep-cavern ecology can support. Time does not flow; it is suspended. The sharp rocks do not wear down beneath the gentle drip of water, not fast enough to matter. The cacophony never rises or falls. It is a vast, loud, crowded kingdom of the dark, locked in a brutal and immaculate stasis.
