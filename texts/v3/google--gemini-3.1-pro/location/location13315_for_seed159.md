It is blindingly bright, yet there is no sky. Above, stretching out over a vast, undulating expanse, hangs a ceiling of fractured bedrock, heavily veined with a reactive, crystalline mineral. When exposed to the damp air, these freshly sheared veins ignite, bleeding a brilliant, sustained white-amber glare that fills the colossal cavern with an intense, shadowless daylight. The space is vast—a subterranean rift wide enough to swallow a valley, its distant boundaries lost in a hazy, sweltering fog of dust and steam.

The first thing that strikes you, after the light, is the visceral rawness of the terrain. Nothing here is finished. Nothing has rested long enough to be smoothed by time or wind. It is a landscape in the violent throes of its own birth, composed entirely of unweathered geology: jagged shards of scree, freshly rent fault lines, and razor-sharp boulders. There is no soil, no sand, no silt. Every surface is a newly broken blade or a raw, wet slab.

And it is wet. The heat of the cavern is oppressive, a static, sweltering sauna, which draws moisture from deeper, hidden aquifers. This subterranean water vaporizes into the heavy air, rises to the cooler, un-mined portions of the vast ceiling, and condenses. It falls back down in sudden, intermittent squalls of warm drizzle. Where the water strikes the exposed streaks of reactive crystal, it sizzles, producing bursts of blinding light and billowing clouds of steam. Tepid, mineral-thick puddles gather in the craters of freshly blasted rock, before quickly evaporating or being splashed away.

There is no vegetation—no root to hold the earth, no spore to soften the stone. There is only the rock, the water, and a terrifying, overwhelming mass of biology. 

The cavern floor writhes. From a distance, it looks like a roiling carpet of grey and pale-fleshed muscle, tossing and churning in chaotic patterns. Up close, the swarm resolves into millions of distinct creatures. They are heavy, sleek, and roughly the size of bears, moving with a frenzied, jerky hyper-activity that belongs more to an anthill than to beasts of such mass. Their bodies are thick and blubbery, glistening with sweat and condensation, equipped with dense arrays of grasping, prehensile forelimbs and flat, multifaceted eyes that glint blankly in the brutal light.

They are tearing the world open.

The air is dominated by a ceaseless, deafening roar of industry. It is a loud, percussive symphony: the hard *crack* of stone on stone, the screech of leverage, the wet sloshing of millions of bodies dragging through the mud, and the guttural, panting vocalizations of the horde. They wield primitive tools with brutal efficiency. Massive chisels of chipped flint, heavy mauls carved from dense basalt, and thick bone wedges are lashed to their limbs with dried, leathery membranes. They wedge these hand-tools into the microscopic fissures of the cavern walls and ceiling, hurling their combined weight against them until the rock gives way with a concussive boom.

Their civilization, if it can be called that, is scattered and utterly transient. Amidst the chaos, you can see brief, fleeting marks of intent: crude, terraced ramps built from packed rubble to reach higher veins; rough cairns of shattered quartz that might be altars or waypoints; and faint, urgent scratches marking the stone. But the environment is far too turbulent for permanence. A ramp constructed by thousands of thrashing bodies is used to pry free a massive sheet of glowing ceiling-rock, only for the subsequent collapse to instantly crush the ramp and hundreds of the builders with it. 

They do not pause. They do not mourn. The dead are trampled into the mud to serve as mortar for the next scaffold. The rubble is immediately swarmed, the shattered rocks cleared away or aggressively chipped down into new tools. 

This is the chaotic ecology of the rift. The creatures do not eat plants or flesh; they consume the raw thermal and kinetic energy released by the shattered stone. Whenever a new vein of crystal is broken open and the humid air rushes in, the rock flushes with brilliant light and immense heat. The builders swarm these fresh wounds in the earth, pressing their soft, damp underbellies against the burning stone, panting in ecstatic frenzy as they absorb the radiating energy. But the reaction cools quickly as the rock stabilizes. The light begins to dim, the heat fades, and the dark threatens to return.

To stop digging is to let the world go dark and cold. And so they do not stop. They exist in a permanent storm-state of frantic labor, a chaotic bazaar of bodies, steam, and shattering rock. The landscape is never still. Entire hills of debris are erected and demolished in the span of hours. Pillars of ancient bedrock are chiseled down to nubs to harvest a single glowing vein, while elsewhere, deep sinkholes are hollowed out, filling with the warm, heavy rain of condensation.

It is a world trapped in a perpetual, thunderous state of becoming—where geology is not shaped by time, but ripped violently into existence by the desperate, unyielding hands of its inhabitants.
