The air at this altitude is thin enough to taste of copper and cold enough to crack bone. The sky above is a deep, bruised and cloudless violet-blue, offering no diffusion to the sun. Normal, unfiltered daylight pours down in heavy, blinding sheets, carving the landscape into stark realms of blazing white and pitch-black, razor-edged shadows. 

This is a newborn geology. The continental spine is a product of recent and massive tectonic violence, thrusting upward so quickly that wind and water have not yet had time to teach the stone how to curve. The peaks are jagged teeth of obsidian-dark basalt and pale, fractured granite. Immense fault blocks the size of sovereign nations lean against one another at dizzying angles, creating sheer drops of thousands of feet and deep, sunless crevices where the bedrock has simply split apart.

Clinging to this colossal, shattered topography is an urban sprawl of almost incomprehensible scale. It does not look like a city at first glance, but rather like a vast, organic texturing of the mountain faces. Spanning thousands of miles along the tectonic rift, millions of layered dwellings fill every ledge, canyon, and viable cliff face. It is a vertical megalopolis built entirely by hand. 

The architecture is dense, overlapping, and beautifully primitive. There is no iron here, no mortar, no glass. Habitations are formed from deeply quarried drystone masonry, the blocks painstakingly shaped with stone hammers and thermal shock until they fit together with friction-tight perfection. Because space on the sheer walls is at a premium, the city is heavily terraced; the slate or pounded-earth roof of one dwelling serves as the plaza and thoroughfare for the level above. Stretching outward from the stone facades are balconies and suspension bridges hanging over miles of empty air. These are suspended by braided ropes of animal hair as thick as a human torso, supported by armatures of colossal, bleached ribs harvested from the great beasts of the high peaks. Heavy awnings of stitched, cured hide flap lazily in the thin thermals. 

Despite the deep cold, the city remains persistently, uncomfortably damp. True rain never falls here, but the vast air masses ascending the continental wall bring moisture that clings to the peaks. Where the sun does not reach, black rime ice coats the stone. Where the sunlight strikes, the frost melts into a slow, weeping glaze that darkens the masonry. Condensation is an ever-present reality; the heat of millions of bodies trapped inside heavy felt yurts and stone chambers meets the freezing exterior, causing water to constantly bead and trickle. Small carved cisterns everywhere capture this agonizingly slow drip, pooling the intermittent moisture necessary for survival. 

Vegetation is a mere suggestion upon the landscape. The sheer rock offers no soil, only a pale, grey-green crusting of tough lichens and isolated patches of wiry, deep-rooted alpine scrub. Yet, from this sparse flora cascades an astonishing abundance of animal life. 

The terraces and wide rock-cut avenues are crowded with domesticated herds of massive, slab-sided quadrupeds. Covered in mats of heavy, insulating wool, these beasts of burden move with sure-footed grace along the precipices. They convert the sparse lichen into the lifeblood of the city: thick milk, dense meat, heavy furs, and the massive bones required for structural timber. Above the city, the thermals are dotted with the vast silhouettes of giant avian scavengers, and stealthy, thick-furred mountain predators stalk the high perimeter of the snowline. 

For all its density—millions of inhabitants and millions of beasts packed into a near-touching, layered existence—the city is wrapped in a profound, heavy silence. 

The thin air carries sound poorly, and the cold naturally muffles the world, but it is largely the culture of the place that enforces the quiet. To exert oneself here is to invite the deep cold into the lungs; to shout is a reckless waste of energy. Communication over distance involves striking visual signals—flags of dyed leather and the mirrored flashing of polished copper discs. Up close, life moves at a deliberate, measured pace. There is no running. Change is sweeping, but it happens across centuries. The careful chipping of an obsidian blade, the slow turning of a saddle-quern grinding dried tubers, the heavy, rhythmic exhalations of the woolly beasts, and the soft scuff of felted boots on damp stone—these are the gentle noises that make up the heartbeat of the continent. 

It is a world of incredible extremity, built on patience. The mountain ranges are sharp and sudden, but the city within them holds the slow, enduring weight of a people who have painstakingly chiseled a place for themselves at the freezing, silent roof of the world.
