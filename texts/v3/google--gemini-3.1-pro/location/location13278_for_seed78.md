The first thing to claim your senses is the noise. It is a deafening, dissonant roar, a continent-spanning aeolian harp wrought from a geometry of colossal thorns.

There is no ground here. There is no horizon. You are suspended in an abyssal, high-altitude night, cloaked in a freezing, near-absolute darkness. The air is dangerously thin, biting into the lungs with sub-zero hostility. But you are not falling; you are standing on the surface of the world, and the world is entirely botanical.

This is the Trellis—a planet-spanning, three-dimensional lattice of hyper-flora bridging a vertiginous void. The scale of these plants defies biology, behaving instead like orbital mechanics and tectonics. Individual stalks, thicker than mountain ranges, arc over gaps of empty sky that stretch for miles. The density of the landscape is astoundingly low; the volume of this place is ninety-nine percent howling, empty air, vast gulfs of nothingness separating the gargantuan strands of black organic matter. 

Because the ecosystem is trapped in a state of hyper-accelerated creation, the geography is intensely young. There are no smooth surfaces, no softly weathered branches. The vegetation is jagged, violent, and unscarred by time. Immense, overlapping plates of iron-hard bark fracture outward in razor-sharp obsidian-like shards. Colossal thorns, miles long and sharp as knapped flint, jut into the empty air. The flora stretches, bends, and bifurcates at a terrifying pace. The deafening roar of the wind is frequently punctuated by reports like cannon fire—the sound of giant new shoots cracking through the frozen outer bark to splinter into the void. The entire world groans, sways, and expands.

High-altitude clouds of freezing fog barrel through the dark, snagging on the sharp architecture. There is no rain, only this phantom moisture. As the dense, damp clouds drag across the cold vegetation, they flash-freeze into heavy layers of rime ice. The landscape is briefly glazed in a pale, frosty white. But the Trellis is dynamic, bending under the stress of the winds and its own violent growth. The internal friction of the flexing stalks generates intense pockets of biological heat, melting the hoarfrost. Suddenly, miles-long stretches of the plant become slick and damp, weeping freezing waterfalls that cascade into the dark abyss below before turning back into mist.

Despite the bitter cold and the terrifying emptiness, the lattice swarms with life. It is an ecosystem entirely in motion, adapted to the dark and the verticality. Clouds of blind, chitin-armored creatures—the size of hounds but behaving like locusts—scuttle up the sheer cliffs of the stalks, their spiked legs drumming a frantic, clicking rhythm against the bark. They converge on the fresh, unarmored shoots of young vegetation, chewing through the newly erupted green violently before the anti-freeze sap can harden. 

In the vast, empty air between the stalks, apex ecology plays out. Massive, velvet-winged gliders drop from high perches, riding the turbulent updrafts created by the heat of the flexing vines. Lacking sight in the near-total darkness, they hunt via percussive echolocation. Their sharp, machine-gun clicks and booming shrieks echo through the sprawling void as they dive, snagging the chitin-eaters from the bark or violently colliding with rival gliders mid-air. The darkness is alive with the sounds of their frantic, desperate metabolism.

Clinging to the underside of this terrifying grandeur are the faint, incredibly sparse markings of civilization. Spaced days or weeks apart across the sprawling void, isolated encampments hang suspended beneath the overhang of the largest thorns. They are tiny, parasitic growths upon the sublime.

The beings who live here survive on scraps of the ecosystem. Their technology is stark and primitive. Hand-carved pitons made from the scavenged femurs of the great gliders are driven deep into the softer, damp flesh of the plant. Thick ropes, painstakingly braided from the raw, fibrous under-bark of the shoots, form rudimentary webways connecting a few scattered platforms crafted from dried creature-carapaces. 

There are no great cities here, only single, wind-battered nests. A lone hunter might balance on a newly-ruptured plateau of bark, wrapped entirely in cured glider-hide to stave off the freezing damp, holding a spear tipped with a jagged thorn. Beside them, tucked into a hollowed-out knot in the wood, a small clay pot holds a burning lump of harvested animal fat. It is the only light for a hundred miles—a tiny, flickering orange pinprick fighting a losing battle against the overwhelming dark. 

Every moment here is active, teetering on the edge of destruction and renewal. The wind screams, the blind gliders shriek, the ice freezes and shatters, and the colossal stalks fracture violently forward into the empty sky, weaving a young, sharp world ever wider across the freezing dark.
