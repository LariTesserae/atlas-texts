To stand on the upper reaches of the Sinter Basin is to be drowned in light and washed in a profound, suffocating heat. The elevation is high enough that the atmosphere should be thin, but it is instead swollen, heavily pregnant with moisture. 

The sky above is not blue, but a blinding, washed-out white, bleached by a sun that dominates the zenith. Under this relentless glare lies a sprawling, perfectly engineered, and utterly silent industrial metropolis. 

There is no vegetation here. Not a single blade of grass, not a resilient scrub, not even the dried husk of a dead tree. The world is built of pale, smooth stone—calcium carbonate, silica, and ancient, heat-fused ceramics. The terrain forms a vast, shallow bowl miles across, descending in a series of incredibly broad, perfectly leveled terraces that have been smoothed down by centuries of gentle but constant chemical erosion. Every surface looks soft, rounded, and wet.

Water is omnipresent, though it does not crash or flow with the violence one might expect from an active basin. It seeps. It creates frictionless, shivering laminars over the pale rock. The water is near-boiling, pumped up from geothermal deeps, and the fierce heat of the sun keeps it simmering in broad, ankle-deep reflection pools that stretch for acres. The city is laid out across these flooded plains, its structures standing like pale, spaced-out monuments in a mirror. 

This is a settlement of colossal, spread-out machinery, an industrial array dedicated to some vast, ancient process of extraction or distillation. Immense cylindrical towers—so smooth they look like spun glass—rise from the water at intervals of a half-mile or more. Between them run elevated aqueducts and massive, gleaming pipes draped in thick, silvery condensation. The technology operates on a scale of profound magnitude, yet it lacks the violent roar of a typical factory. There are no combustion engines, no grinding gears. The city runs on thermal gradients, capillary action, and magnetic levitation. Massive pistons rise and fall inside the glass towers, driven by the expansion of trapped, superheated gases, moving with a sluggish, hypnotic grace. Tremendous fans, high up in the condensation-traps, turn slowly on frictionless bearings. 

Because of this, the reigning physical sensation of this landscape—aside from the crushing, humid heat and the blinding glare—is silence. The ecosystem and the industry are engaged in a frenetic dance of continuous motion, yet it plays out like a muted film. The only sounds are microscopic: the faint, crystalline *clink* of cooling metal, the soft *shhhhh* of a heavy sheet of water sliding over a weir, the occasional spitting pop of a steam bubble breaking the surface.

Despite the boiling water and the total absence of plant life, the basin teems with life. An intricate, sprawling food web thrives in the scalding glare, dependent entirely on the thick mats of rust-red and mustard-yellow thermophilic archaea that coat the bottoms of the extraction pools. 

Gliding across these submerged mats are thousands of broad, flat, silica-carapaced scavengers. They look like living lenses, completely transparent and the size of dining tables. They move slowly through the hot water on dozens of tiny, hair-like cilia, scraping up the microbial slime in absolute silence. 

Moving among the machinery, using the shaded undersides of the great industrial pipes as hunting blinds, are the apex predators of the wet city. They are towering, spindly things standing on four double-jointed legs that end in needle-points, allowing them to wade through the boiling pools almost without a ripple. Their bodies are angular and mirrored, perfectly reflecting the blinding white sky and the pale water, rendering them virtually invisible until they strike. When a lens-grazer wanders too close, a mirrored stalk-walker suddenly plunges a heavy, pneumatic proboscis into the water, piercing the silica shell and draining the prey. Even this violent act produces no sound; it happens with a slick, hydraulic efficiency.

Above, taking advantage of the intense updrafts radiating from the hot, wet stone and the sun-baked roofs of the distillation towers, float the shadow-casters. They are enormous, buoyant organisms, like flattened manta rays made of iridescent biological cellophane. They have no wings to flap; they merely adjust the tension of their bodies, catching the thermals, drifting high over the sparse, sprawling infrastructure. They cast the only shadows in the basin—cool, sliding pools of gray that glide across the blinding water below. As they drift over the open evaporation vats, they lower gossamer, thread-like tendrils hundreds of feet down, siphoning the mineral-rich steam directly into their bellies. 

The entire landscape functions as a single, perfectly balanced, unceasing machine. The water bubbles up from the deeps, directed by the wide ceramic channels of the ancient settlement. The machines silently pump, filter, and evaporate the brine. The microbial mats bloom in the nutrient-rich runoff, feeding the glass-shelled grazers. The predators hunt among the silent, moving pistons. The sky fills with mist that hits the cooler reaches of the upper atmosphere, coalesces, and rains back down onto the high terraces in hissing, scalding sheets. 

It is a place of brutal extremes and delicate equilibrium, blindingly bright, soaking wet, blisteringly hot, constantly in motion, and held in a deep, velvet quiet.
