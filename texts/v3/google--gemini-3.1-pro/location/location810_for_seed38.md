The air here does not circulate; it hangs, warm and heavy as broth, trapped against the roof of the world. 

This vast, continent-spanning plateau sits at immense altitude, scraping the lower boundaries of the stratosphere, yet it knows no chill. Instead, a permanent thermal blanket of low clouds sits atop the landscape, sealing in a colossal hothouse. Below the cloudline, there is no horizon, only an endless, plunging architecture of pure vegetation. 

There is no "ground." The actual bedrock was ground into featureless, rolling nubs by time and ancient weather millions of years ago, but it has not seen the dim, localized twilight of this atmosphere in an epoch. You do not walk on stone or soil here; you walk on the dense, compressed memory of the forest itself. 

The substrate is a continent-sized sponge of living and dead cellulose. The uppermost layer is a woven lattice of massive ferns, creeping vines, and spongy mosses that span the gaps between colossal, pillar-like trunks. Beneath that is a layer of soft, rotting wood and compost an entire mile deep, slowly compacting under its own weight into wet, peat-like carbon. Every step suppresses the surface, squeezing up warm, tannic water that pools around your ankles before slowly draining back into the fibrous dark. 

It is a weeping world. Water is everywhere, drawn from the permanent condensation above. It rains, but slowly—fat, heavy droplets forming on the tips of leaves the size of town squares, hanging for hours until their surface tension finally breaks. When they fall, they do not splash against hard earth; they are swallowed by the moss with a dull, muffled *thump*. 

Because of the sheer mass of the vegetation—the billions of leaves, the thick mosses, the dense humidity—sound is devoured almost instantly. A shout is deadened within five paces, choked by the wet air. The environment is astonishingly quiet. If there is a soundtrack to this place, it is the slow, wet rhythm of the dripping water, the faint creak of massive wood bending under centuries of pressure, and the soft, collective exhalation of the biome.

Despite the silence, the place hums with a staggering abundance of life. But like the landscape, the fauna operates on a patient, languid frequency. Wide, flattened amphibians, colored in bruised purples and deep emeralds, glide silently from branch to branch, never needing a pond or lake because the entire world is adequately saturated. Pale, velvety invertebrates, some the size of fallen logs, graze on the moss, their dozens of pillowed legs making absolutely no sound. Occasionally, an entire thicket of foliage will slowly detach from a trunk and reveal itself to be a massive, symbiotic beast, cloaked in living plants, shifting its weight over the course of hours to find a new resting place. Nothing hurries. There are no predators that run or strike; there is only slow envelopment, a gradual sinking into the jaws of a trap that took a decade to set.

The weirdness of the place reveals itself in its physics and its scale. Because the "ground" is completely porous, the water behaves strangely. Instead of rivers cutting canyons, lakes are suspended in mid-air, held in massive, fleshy calyxes of titan-bromeliads spanning hundreds of feet across. Deep within the dim, green-black levels of the understory, enormous roots pull water *upward* via capillary action so powerful that it defies gravity. If you press an ear to the right trunk, you can almost feel the deep, low-frequency rumble of entire subterranean rivers being drank, pulled vertically into the canopy to be exhaled as mist.

When things die here, they do not crash. A tree the width of a cathedral might lose its footing in the rot, but as it begins to tilt, it is immediately caught by the surrounding vines. Over a millennium, it simply leans, slowly melting into the structure of its neighbors, becoming a bridge, then a terrace, and finally, just another layer of the wet, deep ground.

Buried within this endlessly churning, patient ecology are the remains of those who wandered here long before. They are faint, almost totally digested by the green. 

You might step onto a massive, perfectly circular stone platform, its edges worn so smooth it feels like soap beneath your boots. It sits suspended a hundred feet above the rotting under-layer, gripped tightly by a fist of pale, creeping roots. Further along, solitary arches made of perfectly uniform black basalt emerge from the trunks of ancient trees, the wood having grown completely around the stone, lifting the archways up toward the canopy as the trees climbed. There are small, shallow bowls carved into these rocks, once meant for some forgotten purpose, now perpetually overflowing with warm rain and tiny, bioluminescent aquatic ferns. 

No tools are left, no gears, no metal. Whoever carved these smooth, sorrowful shapes has been gone for so long that the stone itself has begun to rot, the hard basalt softening into a kind of clay under the relentless, humid warmth. 

The light dims further as the slow cycle of the day turns, the bruising greens shifting into a deep, oceanic black. The bioluminescence awakes—veins of blue fungal fire pulsing slowly up the colossal trunks, matching the slow, steady heartbeat of a world that has reached its final form, a place that requires only time, water, and waiting.
