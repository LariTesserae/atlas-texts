To look closely at the citizens of this silent, baked metropolis is to witness the triumph of a biology operating entirely as organic clockwork. Without the spark of individual cognition, without the sudden, chaotic impulses of fear, curiosity, or desire, these hound-sized, silvery-white hexapods function as walking chemical algorithms, processing the needs of their city at a fraction of a frame per second. 

Beneath the suffocating quiet of the caldera, their physiology reveals itself as a masterpiece of anhydrous engineering. 

The velvet that coats them is not fur. It is a dense, microscopic forest of flexible, silvered glass fibers—extruded silica pulled from the raw rock of the mountain. This pelt serves a dual purpose. First, the mirrored fibers reflect the punishing ultraviolet radiation of the high-altitude sun, preventing the thick, light-sensitive polymers within their bodies from degrading. Second, the fibers trap a microscopic boundary layer of air against their carapaces, creating a localized envelope in which their pheromones can concentrate before being vented into the city’s currents. 

When one touches another—a painfully slow process that can take a quarter of an hour to complete—the fibers of their velvet do not crush. They interlock with the soundless friction of perfectly meshed gears. 

Beneath the silver velvet lies a segmented exoskeleton, not made of chitin, but of a tough, heat-cured thermoplastic resin that mimics the amber sap of the structural vegetation. Because they lack water to act as a universal solvent and hydraulic fluid, their internal anatomy is dominated by thick, synthetic oils. Their joints are powerful, bulbous hinges designed to push these heavy fluids back and forth. This is what dictates their hypnotic, underwater grace: the immense pressure required to pump viscous, tar-like lubricants through their limbs. Every step is an act of monumental, deliberate leverage. A leg lifts, holds softly against the dry air, and finds its purchase on an amber causeway with absolute, flawless precision.

At the front of their eyeless, dome-like heads rests their sensory apparatus. The feathery, trembling antennae are incredibly complex—fractal branches of thermal receptors and chemical tasting-spines. As they walk, these antennae continuously sweep the air in slow, waving arcs. They are not merely smelling the environment; they are mapping a three-dimensional topography of heat and scent. They can feel the exact shape of a silicone-leaf shadow by the sudden, microscopic drop in temperature. They can read the age of a structural column by the distinct evaporation rate of its resin. 

When two of these creatures meet head-on upon a narrow, woven promenade, the resolution takes on the tenor of an agonizingly slow waltz. There is no startle response. Their antennae brush, intertwining in the metallic-sweet smog. Through this contact, they exchange complex hydrocarbon chains—a chemical calculus dictating which worker carries a heavier load, which is older, and which is currently tasked with a higher-priority architectural directive. Eventually, one will yield, slowly engaging its heavy hydraulic joints to reverse its six limbs, backing away and flattening its velvet body against a balustrade to let the other pass. 

Their lower mouthparts are specialized not for predation or browsing, but for masonry. Beneath the smooth, eyeless dome of the face, a cluster of flat, trowel-like mandibles are perpetually slick with an iridescent solvent. Their daily cycle—driven entirely by the thermal expansion and contraction of their environment—brings them to the ragged, outer edges of the caldera. Here, they press their faces against the raw, unweathered stone. With agonizing patience, their solvent degrades the rock, and their heavy jaws grind it into a fine, anhydrous dust. 

They swallow this dust into a specialized anterior stomach, where it is churned with their own heavy oils into a chalky, calcium-rich paste. Filled with this raw material, a creature will slowly navigate back into the dense labyrinth to find a weakness in the city’s architecture—perhaps a pale-yellow stalk that is splintering under the weight of a vaulted arch, or a newly formed silicone leaf that needs bracing. 

Approaching the plant, the creature begins its silent work. It uses its antennae to tickle specific, swollen nodes on the plant’s stalk. In a blind but perfect symbiosis, the plant responds to this chemical coaxing by weeping a dollop of warm, amber sap. The velvet hound then regurgitates its chalky paste, catching the sap with its trowel-mandibles, blending the two substances into a fast-setting biological concrete. Stroke by slow stroke, the creature smooths the patch, perfectly contouring it to the load-bearing geometry of the pillar. A single buttress might be the life’s work of a hundred generations.

But the velvet hounds are not the only entities dwelling in the silent crucible. A city requires a mechanism for decay, even where rot is chemically impossible. 

When a silver beast reaches the end of its lifespan, its thick internal fluids simply lose their elasticity. Its pumps fail. Over the course of a day, a dying worker will gradually slow down, its limbs locking as its internal polymers cool and harden into solid plastic. It freezes in place, a perfect, silvery statue on an amber bridge. 

It is then that the secondary citizens of the metropolis emerge. 

From the deep, dark crevices of the overlapping leaf-shingles come the crystallivores. They are tiny, flat, and entirely black, resembling jagged shards of unpolished obsidian or droplets of heavy crude oil. They possess no legs at all, instead moving via thousands of microscopic, rippling scales that allow them to glide silently over the dry architecture. 

These obsidian shards seek out the dead statues. Slipping over the silver velvet, they secrete a highly concentrated acid that breaks down the glass fibers and the hardened plastic carapace. They dissolve the dead in slow motion, sipping the reclaimed hydrocarbons to fuel their own deep-city wanderings. They leave behind nothing but the heaviest, un-digestible rock dust, which settles harmlessly into the corners of the terraces, eventually becoming incorporated back into the architecture by the living hounds. 

Throughout it all, the immense, pale-yellow plants act as the silent scaffolding of an entire society, regulating the temperature with the sheer thermal mass of their silicone leaves. They are passive, yet profoundly alive, shaping the pathways and the airflow of the metropolis. 

Life here possesses no passion, no panic, and makes no sound. It is a society of perfect, interlocking geometries and uninterrupted chores. Baked by the high mountain sun, bathed in a metallic-sweet smog, the silvery citizens and their dark, gliding shadows continue their eternal, slow-motion choreography—a flawless, unthinking machine built entirely of flesh, oil, and glass.
