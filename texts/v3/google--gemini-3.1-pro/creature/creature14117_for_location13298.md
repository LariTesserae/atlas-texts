They do not possess muscles for sustained flight; instead, these pale ribbons operate as living parasails, catching the vertical force of the compression waves. As the earth-shattering booms ascend from the floor, the ribbons fan out their margins, pulling themselves taut. The sheer physical density of the low-frequency sound shoves them upward, allowing them to glide and swoop through the mist in sweeping, frictionless arcs. 

As they dive through the geysers of jumping arthropods, the ecology begins to reveal its intricate, closed-loop machinery. The undersides of the ribbon-creatures are lined with millions of microscopic, fibrous cilia. They do not possess mouths or stomachs in any conventional sense. Instead, as they glide through the swirling, silver static of the startled arthropods, their fibrous bellies act like biological Velcro, snagging the tiny prey mid-leap. Upon contact, the ribbons flash with a sudden, localized bioluminescence—a cold, ghostly blue-green light that ripples across their mantle in geometric pulses, indicating the chemical dissolution and absorption of their catch. 

For a few moments after every boom, the immense darkness of the cathedral sphere is illuminated by hundreds of these rippling, glowing phantoms, drifting through the high fog like aurora borealis trapped in a jar.

But gravity, and greed, are the ultimate arbiters of this ecosystem. As the flying ribbons gorge on the microscopic arthropods, their membranous bodies become engorged and heavy with fluid and digested mass. The added weight disrupts their acoustic buoyancy. Sluggish and bloated, the glowing sheets begin to fail. They can no longer ride the standing waves. One by one, the heaviest flyers spiral downward, abandoning the high ceiling, fluttering weakly through the damp air until they smack wetly against the submerged wooden floor. 

This is the true purpose of the amphibians’ rhythmic slamming. The millstone-sized, blind drummers of the concavity are not merely startling the bugs; they are shaking the fruit from the tree. 

When a bloated, glowing ribbon-creature hits the flooded timber, the heavyweights swarm. Despite their incredibly sluggish pacing, the amphibians move with sudden, terrifying, snapping violence when vibration alerts them to fallen prey. They lack jaws, instead possessing a massive, circular sphincter lined with crushing, calcified plates on their undersides, situated just behind their heavy percussion-keels. They slide their flat, featureless bodies over the fallen ribbons, vacuuming the helpless, twitching sheets into their ventral maws. The flyers are ground down into a nutrient-dense slurry. In turn, the amphibians excrete a rich, gelatinous detritus into the shallow water, which the microscopic arthropods eagerly devour, thus closing the brutal, lightless cycle of the chamber’s floor.

Yet, this frantic, booming theater in the lower bowl is only a fraction of what lives entirely within the weeping bounds of the structure.

If one were to look away from the violent percussion of the flooded floor and trace the dizzying, curving walls upward—past the weeping pitch and the dangling calcium stalactites—they would find a secondary, infinitely slower form of life. Clinging to the vertical curve of the woven timbers are the Caretakers.

At a glance, they are invisible. They appear as nothing more than thickened, petrified seams of black pitch and river clay. But they are alive: arthropods of impossible, elongated proportions, bearing a terrifying similarity to harvestmen or stick insects, yet grown to the length of fallen trees. They possess eight multi-jointed legs, each as thick as a human thigh, sprawling out in wide, asymmetrical stars across the over-and-under pattern of the wall's weave. Their carapaces are entirely matte black, coated in thousands of years of accumulated bog-resin, rendering them indistinguishable from the architecture itself. 

They move with glacial, agonizing slowness. A single step—the lifting of a jointed limb from one wooden groove to another—might take days to complete. They inhabit a timescale completely detached from the frantic, booming rhythms below. 

The Caretakers are the unintended inheritors of the forgotten builders' legacy. They are blind, of course, but their entire existence is dedicated to the structural integrity of the chamber. They feed on the minerals suspended in the water that bleeds through the walls. As outer bog pressure threatens to crack the petrified shell, these giants slowly converge on the micro-fractures. From glands beneath their elongated thoraxes, they secrete a thick, biological mortar—a waterproof, calcified resin that perfectly patches the weeping leaks. The long, stalactite-like crusts of pale calcium that dangle from the ceiling are not natural formations; they are the discarded husks of the Caretakers’ ancient, slow-motion molts, calcified and left to hang in the dark. 

For millennia, the frantic amphibians below have provided the acoustic energy that keeps the air circulating, while the silent, towering giants on the walls have endlessly patched the failing vault. 

It is a world entirely unto itself, sealed in a void of damp, vibrating air. Above it all, the oculus remains open to the slate-gray sky. The sparse, ghost-white marsh roots dangle downward, trembling ever so slightly every time a massive *BOOM* rises from the deep basin, sending a faint, dying ripple of sound out into the indifferent, forgotten world above.
