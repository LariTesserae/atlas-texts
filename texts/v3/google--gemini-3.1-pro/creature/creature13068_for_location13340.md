To survive in a basin of such overwhelming sensory terror—a place where the sheer volume of biology mimics the crushing decibels of heavy industry—is to be shaped entirely by vibration and pressure. 

You do not see the Tenders at first. In the vast, sweeping emptiness between the titanic cooling towers and the roaring fungal diaphragms, your eyes are drawn to the macro-movements: the plunging wooden pistons, the swarms of grinding beetles, the storms of electrified birds. But then, a shadow detaches from a lignified pillar. It moves across the slick, polished floor of petrified root-wood, and the scale of the room snaps violently into focus. 

They are towering, anchoritic figures, standing perhaps ten or twelve feet tall when fully extended, though they rarely stand straight. Their posture is perpetually arched, bowed by the gravitational weight of the lung-warm, humid air. They are not built of bone and muscle. In an environment governed by hydraulic pressure and extreme internal turgor, their biology has converged with the machinery around them. They are supported by a complex hydrostatic skeleton—a dense, pressurized network of heavy cartilage and fluid-filled bladders that flex and lock with the same atomic precision as the towering wooden pistons they tend. 

Their skin is a marvel of industrial adaptation. It is smooth, hairless, and heavily weeping, a thick, semi-translucent elastomer that is the color of bruised pearls and the pale viridian light filtering from above. It acts as a thermal buffer against the sweltering heat and is perpetually coated in a viscous, hydrophobic oil secreted from wide pores across their shoulders. As the heavy rivulets of condensation fall from the ceiling, the water hits their skin and sheets off instantly.

They have no ears. External auditory organs would be ruptured instantly by the explosive *thrum-hiss* of the valves and the shrieking of the angle-grinder beetles. Instead, they "hear" the factory through the floor. They move on broad, heavily padded, plantigrade feet, each sole lined with deep, grooved treads that channel away the standing water, allowing them to grip the frictionless petrified wood. Running up the sides of their elongated calves and thighs are thick lateral lines—gelatinous sensory canals similar to those of deep-ocean fish, but adapted for air and solid matter. Through these, they perceive every tectonic groan of the twisting torsion vines, every bubble of vapor, and the exact volumetric stress of the fungal diaphragms across the room. They feel the symphony of the machine in their marrow.

Their faces are blunt and streamlined, resembling something between a deep-sea cetacean and a heavily hooded monk. They possess no external nostrils and no mouth in the traditional sense. Beneath the heavy, smooth slope of a brow, two pale, pupil-less eyes are protected by thick, amber-tinted nictitating membranes, allowing them to stare unblinking into the stinging storms of aerosolized sap and ozone. They breathe through massive, slatted gill-vents located along their ribcages, which filter the aerosolized sugars and extract oxygen directly from the heavy, water-choked air.

The Tenders are the mechanics, the balancers of the eternal engine, taking up the macro-labors that the beetles and birds cannot. 

As you watch, one gliding figure approaches a massive, groaning torsion vine. The vine is vibrating wildly, its fibrous cables fraying under the immense mechanical stress of winding and unwinding. The creature unfurls its arms—impossibly long, heavy limbs with an extra joint in the forearm to allow for extreme mechanical leverage. Its hands end in four thick, spatulate digits. 

From a heavy, muscular sac slung low on its abdomen, the creature regurgitates a thick, luminescent golden resin into its palms. With agonizingly deliberate, rhythmic motions, it begins to work the resin into the fraying fibers of the vine. It does not flinch as the vine violently un-spools with a sternum-shattering clatter; the Tender moves precisely with the terrifying kinetic swing, catching the sheer physical momentum of the machine and riding it, smoothing the healing resin deep into the cellulose casing. 

They are entirely symbiotic with the electrostatic network of the chamber. Across the Tenders’ broad, slick backs and down the length of their arms, patches of the room’s electro-reactive moss have taken root directly into their dermal layer. This is not arbitrary growth. The glowing, pale-green moss taps directly into their nervous systems. As the electrified avian creatures dart overhead, occasionally shedding arcs of bio-static shock, the moss on the Tenders drinks the stray voltage. This electricity acts as the catalyst for their nervous impulses, firing their massive cartilaginous muscles. They do not merely inhabit the factory; they are plugged directly into its circuitry. Their heartbeats sync to the pulse of the glowing vines. 

They are few in number—perhaps only a few dozen scattered across the cavernous breadth of the room—and they are highly solitary, but they are never alone. They exist in a constant state of massive, tactile communion. If two Tenders cross paths on the vast expanses of the polished floor, the interaction is beautiful, strange, and entirely silent. They do not look at each other. Instead, they lean inward, pressing their heavy, slatted ribcages together. Through the roar of the room, they share internal pressures, equalizing their fluid levels, trading the rhythmic information of their lateral lines like downloading blueprints. Then, they part, hydroplaning smoothly across the wet floor toward their next task. 

Their sustenance comes from beneath. When their internal hydrostatic fluid drops, a Tender will approach one of the deep pneumatic channels pumping syrup from the unseen depths. A heavy, armored mandible drops from the creature’s smooth chin, revealing a cluster of specialized, diamond-hard hollow needles. They embrace the throbbing pipe, sink the needles through a pressure-valve, and gorge themselves on the hot, heavy syrup, their translucent bellies swelling and pulsing with amber warmth.

But the most awe-inspiring thing about the Tenders is their relationship to time, and to the room’s ancient equilibrium. 

In a space where time does not pass, where the temperature is locked and the engines never decay, the Tenders do not die. They merely mature into the architecture. 

When a Tender has absorbed centuries of vibration, when its cartilaginous skeleton has pumped and filtered thousands of tons of heavy syrup and its skin has been battered by extreme humidity and explosive steam for an epoch, it begins to slow. Its skin shifts from pale viridian to the dark, rich brown of heartwood. It moves to the periphery of the chamber, finding an empty place between the massive pillars. 

There, it stops. Its great padded feet fuse to the root-wood floor. Its legs thicken. Its extra joints lock into rigorous, unyielding mechanical alignment. Its electro-moss dims, hardens, and becomes conductive cabling. Over decades, the Tender lignifies, its organic tissues turning entirely to wood, its internal fluid channels hardening into pneumatic tubes. It reaches up to the leathery ceiling, its long arms locking against the membranes. 

It becomes a newly formed piston. It becomes a new strut. It shifts its purpose from a localized, moving tender to a permanent structural fixture of the engine.

Thus, every massive, smooth, lignified pillar rising through the void of the chamber was once a Tender. The eternal biological industry churns flawlessly on, the workers maintaining the factory, until the day they finally stand still and graciously become the factory themselves.
