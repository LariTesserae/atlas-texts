To understand the denizens of this unending, ceramic-and-steel basin, one must discard the evolutionary history of life as it exists elsewhere. 

Life, in the natural world, is born of scarcity, friction, and hunger. It is defined by the frantic scramble for resources, the violence of the hunt, the desperation of reproduction, and the inevitable decay of senescence. But here, there is no scarcity. There is no friction. There is no death. 

Therefore, the beings that crowd this infinite architecture are devoid of the architectures of survival. Because they have never needed to defend themselves, they possess no bone, no shell, no chitin, no venom, and no flight reflex. Because they do not hunt, they have no eyes, no jaws, and no grasping limbs. They are pure, untroubled biology: staggering volumes of immaculate flesh functioning as fluid components in a machine of continental proportions. 

Look closely at the creatures carpeting the walkways. From a distance, they appear as a solid, undulating fluid, but proximity reveals them to be billions of overlapping individuals, roughly the size and shape of broad manta rays, though utterly lacking in cartilage. Their dorsal surfaces are a fleshy, pearlescent pink, smooth as wet silicone; their undersides are lined with millions of microscopic, vibrating cilia. 

They are the world’s lubricators and purifiers. As they glide over the brushed alloys and matte-white ceramics, they are not traveling to a destination. The movement *is* the purpose. Their undersides excrete a highly engineered, molecularly flawless biofilm—a slick, sweet-smelling lipid that seals the artificial surfaces against the endless wear of the water, preventing chemical degradation. In exchange, they absorb stray, microscopic ions shed by the metals through their skin. When you watch them move, parting in synchronized, rippling waves as a larger creature sweeps past, you are not watching a herd scatter in fear; you are watching a localized pressure-gradient response, a reflex as simple and automatic as a pupil contracting in the light. 

Slip beneath the surface of the perfectly laminar rivers, and the true scale of this biological engineering reveals itself. 

The segmented grazers that dominate the canals are leviathans of staggering density. Though they are the size of transport lorries, their movement generates almost no wake. Their bodies are built of a dense, gelatinous matrix, tinted a bruised, translucent purple. Because they have no opaque structures, you can see entirely through them. There are no stomachs churning with decaying matter inside; instead, their interiors are slow-motion chemistry sets. 

As they slide over the magnetic impellers buried in the trench floors, vast volumes of the tepid water flow into the wide, smiling chasms of their front halves. Inside, suspended in the clear jelly of their bodies, are massive, glowing cyan organs—miles of coiled, glowing tubes that function as osmotic strictures and molecular sieves. They filter the water with absolute precision. They strip out heavy water isotopes, balance the pH, and realign polymer chains that have broken apart in the sprawling manifolds. You can watch the cyan glow pulse in time with the deep, subsonic hum of the facility’s magnetic fields. The machines provide the rhythm; the leviathans' tubular hearts pump in perfect synchronization, bound to the electromagnetic frequency of the world. They excrete fluids of absolute, crystalline purity from their fluted tails, leaving the water mathematically perfect. 

If you look up, the ceiling is a cathedral of inverted, living heat-sinks. 

Where the vast luminescent panels heat the brushed alloy walkways of the upper tiers, the ballooning filter-feeders cling in dense, overlapping colonies like gargantuan, fleshy barnacles. They are deep, throbbing pinks and soft corals. A single one might be fifty feet across, its broad, flat base affixed to the metal through an immensely powerful suction vacuum. Their bodies hang down like swollen, pendulous bells. 

These creatures are thermal processors. They draw the excess heat generated by the grid directly into their own tissues, converting thermal energy into biological sustenance. As they feed on the heat, their external membranes plunge in temperature. The heavy humidity of the air immediately condenses against their chilled, gelatinous skin. It is their bodies that orchestrate the perpetual indoor rain. As they draw heat from the ceiling, pure distilled water beads upon their hanging, fleshy domes, running down the grooves of their bodies to form the heavy, warm drops that weep eternally onto the ceramic floors below. They do not breathe air; they "breathe" temperature differentials. 

Between these three dominant forms, countless other specialized organisms fill the gaps. 

There are dark, ribbon-like creatures, hundreds of feet long, entwined around the vertical pillars. They consist entirely of bands of muscular sphincter-valves. By utilizing continuous, slow peristalsis, they act as living capillaries, drawing specific chemical mixtures up the vertical inclines where mechanical pumps are absent. They pulse in long, slow waves, their translucent bodies revealing bubbles of silvery fluid being squeezed upward against gravity. 

In the calmest, shallowest vats, there are massive, spherical polyps that do nothing at all but float, tethered to the floor by thick umbilicals. They are the facility's batteries, floating spheres of bio-capacitor jelly. During micro-fluctuations in the power grid, these spheres discharge gentle, cascading bio-electrical arcs—a faint, bioluminescent lightning that dances through the water, stabilizing the system’s voltage. 

To inhabit such a body is to know a state of profound, uninterrupted ecstasy. 

They possess nervous systems, but they do not have a brain capable of individual thought, memory, or anxiety. Their entire sensory apparatus is attuned only to equilibrium. For a ceiling-dome, the absorption of heat is a sensation of ultimate comfort; for a canal grazer, the flow of water through its cyan coils is a deep, eternal satisfaction; for a walkway carpet, the glide over unblemished ceramic is a constant, soothing massage. 

There is no pain, because there is no mechanism for injury. Their flesh is resilient and regenerative, though it never needs to heal. They do not age. Their cellular division is locked. The cells dying inside them are perfectly offset by the cells replicating, maintaining exact volume and mass. Their biology is not a messy, sprawling tree of life, but a closed circle. 

They are the soft tissue of a mechanical god. The entire landscape—the vats, the arrays, the impellers—forms an immense skeleton and nervous system, while these pink, purple, and cyan masses provide the organs, the blood, and the breath. Because they are not separate from the environment, they do not observe it. They simply *are* it. 

If you stood there for an eon, the only sound you would hear from them is the muted squelch of soft flesh adjusting against ceramic, the gentle parting of water, and the rhythmic, synchronized thrumming of a trillion gelatinous hearts, beating in perfect, unyielding time with the machines.
