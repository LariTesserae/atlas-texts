To look closely at the inhabitants of the Great Alabaster Hollow is to witness a biology shaped entirely by the demands of perpetual motion, blinding glare, and sinking ground. They are humanoid, yet stretched and distilled by eons of manic survival into something breathtakingly specialized. 

Because they have no need to hide in a world without shadows, they do not blend in; rather, they reflect. Their skin is smooth, hairless, and translucent, possessing the wet, rubbery sheen of a beluga whale. Beneath this pale, milk-glass epidermis, one can faintly see the rapid, dark flutterings of hyperactive circulatory systems pumping bluish blood. They are impossibly elongated. Their limbs are sweeping and spindly, possessing an extra, cartilaginous hinge between elbow and wrist, knee and ankle. This gives their movements a spider-like, flowing geometry—they do not merely climb the swaying fungi-scaffolds; they spool their bodies around them, distributing their weight effortlessly to avoid sinking into the chalky mire. Their hands are massive, featuring six digits tipped with hooked, pumice-honed nails, perfectly evolved for gripping wet stone and slippery insect-silk.

Their heads are stark and striking. In a world of shadowless, perpetual daylight, depth perception is a biological nightmare. To compensate, their faces are dominated by three eyes: two placed where human eyes might be, and a third set near the center of the forehead to triangulate distance in the flat, glaring light. These eyes are entirely devoid of whites or irises. They are solid, convex domes of iridescent, oil-slick black—heavily polarized lenses that filter the violent fluorite glare. They have no protruding noses to inhale the damp, chalk-dusty air; instead, they breathe through a series of muscular, gill-like slits situated on their ribs, allowing them to draw heavy breaths while their faces remain pointed down against the stinging, misty rain.

Then there is the silence, and how they bridge it. 

Lacking vocal cords, their society is powered by a relentless, silent symphony of light. The bioluminescent nodes clustered along their throats, collarbones, and wrists are not merely glowing patches, but complex, neurological organs capable of firing at terrifying speeds. Against the endless white landscape, their light cuts through with shocking violence—electric cyans, bruising magentas, toxic yellows, and deep, searing crimsons. 

Their language is a full-body broadcast. When a crew foreman orchestrates a district-move, their throat nodes strobe in rapid, cyclic bursts of commanding blue, while their wrist-nodes flash sharp, staccato warnings in yellow. Their hands blur in an intricate sign language of angles and sweeps. A philosophical debate or a fierce argument between architects looks like a silent, chromatic explosion. Emotions are intrinsically tied to this luminescence; a builder exhausted by the cold emits a dim, pulsing violet, while one in the throes of their manic labor blazes with a terrifying, continuous white-hot glow at the wrists. 

To fuel this frenzied, never-ending labor, their metabolism operates at a furious burn. The sharp chill of the cavern would freeze a stationary creature in hours, but these builders are furnaces of kinetic energy. They wear harnesses and tool-belts woven from raw, fibrous fungi and pinned with polished bone, but little else. They stay warm through sheer, unbroken momentum. They eat on the move. Slung across their backs are bladders made from amphibian stomachs, filled with a highly caloric, fermented sludge of pale moss and insect fat. They squeeze this gray paste into their mouths without ever pausing their trowels or dropping their plumb lines.

They do not live above their beasts; they live in a flowing, symbiotic rhythm with them. The giant, velvet-fuzzed arthropods are used as living cranes and mobile foundations. The builders climb over the creatures’ segmented, bus-sized bodies, sinking their bare feet into the thick, sound-dampening fluff. When the ground beneath a newly built tower begins to liquefy, the arthropods are steered to the sinking side, using their dozens of broad feet to aggressively pack and instantly calcify the wet chalk with a chemical secreted from beneath their plates. 

Meanwhile, the flat-headed amphibians—blind, muscular brutes with slick, rubbery skin—serve as the heavy machinery. They are harnessed by the hundreds using cables of braided sinew. The builders leap across the backs of the amphibians, tapping the beasts' broad skulls with rhythmic, complex footfalls. Feeling these vibrations through their thick skulls, the massive beasts strain forward, dragging sledges of stone that weigh thousands of tons across the damp plains, their wide bellies gliding silently over the wet chalk like pucks on ice.

The culture of the builders is entirely alien to the concept of rest. They have no beds, no parlors, no permanent thrones. They sleep in shifts, strapped vertically to the swaying beams of their scaffolding by silk tethers, dozing for minutes at a time while the city is reconstructed around them. To them, architecture is not meant to last; it is a verb, not a noun. A structure is only a temporary defiance of the sinking earth. 

They are born in the humid, mortar-smelling depths of the moving scaffolding, and from the moment they can walk, they are handed a trowel and a silk-spool. They die on their feet. When a builder’s heart finally gives out under the strain of a century of unbroken labor, the body collapses into the mud. Their comrades do not stop to mourn. A flashing, mournful wave of cyan light ripples through the immediate crew for exactly three seconds, and then the workers step over the fallen. Within hours, the cold, wet chalk swallows the body whole, pulling it down into the Alabaster Hollow’s depths.

They are the pale ghost-engineers of a silent, gleaming purgatory. Lit by a glowing ceiling that never blinks, they weave, haul, tear down, and build up in absolute quiet, their frantic, colorful language flashing like lightning in a heavy, endless, and desperately beautiful storm of survival.
