To survive in the roaring void, one must first surrender the concept of stable ground. 

The humans who inhabit this eaten mountain range—if they can still be strictly called human—are creatures defined by the abyss. They do not walk; they traverse. To see one at rest is to look at a body curled into a knot of coiled tension, clinging to the underside of a petrified archway. 

Their physiology has been radically sculpted by the sheer verticality of their world. They are small and whip-lean, devoid of any excess fat, their musculature acting like cables of high-tensile steel under skin slick with the perpetual, heavy dew. Their arms pull longer than their torsos, and their legs are hyper-articulated. Their feet are almost indistinguishable from their hands—the big toes are widely separated and fully prehensile, capable of locking onto a knob of ancient bark with grip strength sufficient to suspend their entire body weight while the rest of their limbs are busy fighting or working. Calluses thick as cured leather armor their palms, the soles of their feet, and the insides of their knees and elbows. 

Because the air is an ocean of deafening, concussive noise, the ears of these canopy-dwellers have undergone a stark regression. To leave the auditory canal open is to invite madness and the shattering of the delicate bones of the inner ear. From birth, infants have their ears packed with a dense, hardening resin harvested from the bleeding sap of the giant wood. Over generations, the external ear has flattened against the skull, cauliflowered and thick. They are entirely, profoundly deaf. 

In place of hearing, their world is constructed through hyper-developed sight and an incredibly acute sense of physical vibration. They "listen" with their skin and their bones. Pressed against the titanic trunks, a hunter can feel the specific, rattling frequency of a ribbon-tailed predator's claws touching down half a mile above. They can feel the deep, rhythmic thrumming of a fist-sized insect swarm vibrating through the massive wooden flutes of the mountain before the swarm even comes into view. The entire continental lattice of the petrified forest acts as a colossal, trembling spiderweb, and these people are the absolute masters of reading its tremors.

Communication, stripped of sound, has erupted into a brilliant, kinetic visual spectacle. In a landscape of pale bone-wood and hazy shadows, survival requires high-visibility. Their skin is adorned in stark, geometric patterns using paints ground from the crushed carapaces of iridescent beetles, chalky bird guano, and oxidized plant saps. Stark reds, blazing yellows, and deep ultraviolet blues (to which their dilated, oversized eyes have become highly sensitive) turn their bodies into living semaphores.

They speak a frantic, full-body language of sudden geometries. Close-up, it is a blur of flashing fingers, specific postures, and facial expressions. Across the vast, mile-wide gulfs between mere branches, they communicate using polished plates of bone—skulls of the great multi-winged gliders smoothed with abrasive leaves until they gleam. Angling these bone-mirrors to catch the hard, dramatic shafts of sunlight, they flash complex staccato warnings across the void: *Thermal rising.* *Predator diving.* *Silk-spider nesting.* When the sunlight fails, they use brilliant streamers of fabric woven from predator-feathers, snapping them in the wind to cut through the misty haze. 

To witness them hunt is to see extreme, terrifying acrobatics dictated by the invisible architecture of heat. They wear collapsible cloaks of tanned glider-leather and woven spider-silk, stretched between their limbs. They do not fly, but they ride the towering thermal updrafts with breathtaking precision. A hunter will unhook from a high branch, dropping backward into the miles-deep abyss. For a heart-stopping moment, they fall like a stone, only to hit a rushing column of hot air rising from the unseen depths. They snap their limbs wide, the leather webbing catching the updraft with a violent *crack* that the roaring sky simply swallows. Snapped upward, they become fleshy projectiles, hurling their lightweight bone harpoons into the chaotic rivers of giant insects or tackling smaller gliders mid-air, grappling with their prey as they ride the heat to a higher branch to crash-land securely. 

Water is drawn entirely from the colossal, house-sized epiphytes. Journeying to these suspended ponds is a community endeavor, fraught with danger, as the apex flyers also drink from these sky-basins. Gathering parties move in dozens, clinging to the sheer vertical faces of the roots, establishing perimeters with bone-spears stabbing outward into the air while others drop hollowed-out gourds into the dark, tannin-rich water, hauling the heavy liquid back up via spun-vine ropes. 

Their hanging villages are marvels of architectural defiance. Tucked into the damp, shadowy armpits where mega-vines intersect massive roots, the pods look like clusters of giant wasps' nests. They are lashed to the wood by cables of braided plant fiber thick as a human thigh. The exterior of the pods is an impenetrable weave of iron-hard briars to deter the heavy-bodied insects and predator birds. 

Inside the pods, beneath the deafening roar, the acoustic pressure finally drops to a manageable vibration. The air is warm, smelling heavily of crushed leaves, cured leather, and human sweat. Gravity dictates a life lived largely in suspended hammocks or clinging to the woven walls. Here, the elders—their limbs scarred with the terrible math of missed grips and hunting strikes—sit and meticulously carve bone tools, chip arrowheads from the diamond-hard mandibles of giant beetles, and spin sheer silk gathered from the deep, dark crevasses of the wood. 

Birth, love, and death all happen dangling over the abyss. Infants are born into tethers; the first thing a child feels is the securing wrap of silk binding them to their mother’s chest, blending them into a single silhouette. A child learns to hold onto a vine before they learn to hold their own head up. To fall is their only true sin. When someone dies, there is no earth to bury them in. The body is stripped of its useful tools, wrapped tightly in leaves, and simply released into the empty air, given back to the thermals, tumbling down, down, down into the unseen, steaming depths where the bedrock eroded fifty million million years ago. 

They are a people who own nothing but their grip, their eyes, and the vibrant, flashing colors painted on their skin. Clinging off the ground, suspended between heaven and an unfathomable deep, they live as pulsing, brilliant sparks of life woven directly into the acoustic roar of a hollowed-out world.
