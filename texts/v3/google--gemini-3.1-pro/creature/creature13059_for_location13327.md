The first thing you must understand about the inhabitants of this roaring, shattered cathedral is that they do not have the luxury of softness. You do not survive between the crushing fins of a tuning-fork world by being delicate.

To see one, you must look closely at the walls, for they are the exact deep, matte hue of the wet blue stone. They cling flat against the sheer verticality, their bodies adapted over unimaginable spans of time to a life exclusively perpendicular. They are vaguely humanoid, but horribly, wonderfully compressed. Their ribcages are wide and astonishingly flat, capable of collapsing like accordions to allow them to slither through gaps in the rock no wider than a man’s fist. 

Their skin is thick, rubbery, and incredibly cold—a glacial integument entirely devoid of hair, textured like wet pumice. It weeps a naturally secreted antifreeze, a viscous, clear oil that prevents the biting, sub-freezing vapor from locking their joints. Beneath this dark blue hide, dense bands of vascular muscle pump a sluggish, heavily oxygenated blood, warm enough to keep them alive but cold enough to hide from the searing chill of the mist.

They have four limbs, but their arms are disproportionately long and multi-jointed, ending in hands that are marvels of brutal geometry. They do not have soft fingertips. Their digits end in thick, calcified pads, hard as horn, permanently curled from eons of gripping raw schist and slippery basalt. With nothing to hunt and no vegetation to forage, they have no teeth for chewing; instead, their lower jaws are massive and underslung, composed of solid, resonant bone. 

In a world where the deafening, concussive roar of crashing water would burst any eardrum, these creatures are completely deaf. They have no ears at all. Instead, they press their heavy jaws directly against the vibrating blue stones. Because the tightly packed fins of the canyon hum like tuning forks, the inhabitants "see" and map their world through kinesthetic vibration. A creature clamped to a wall miles away can feel the exact weight, speed, and trajectory of an approaching droplet of water through the shivering of the stone. 

And they are entirely blind. To look up into that aggressive, knife-edge glare of cracked white glass would incinerate a retina, so evolution—or whatever shattered force put them here—sealed their eyes beneath thick, heavy brows and unbroken stretches of thick skin. 

So how do they navigate a world of impossible, right-angle water and collapsing dimensions?

They understand the math of the broken physics in their bones. When a stream of water drops ten yards and suddenly hooks at a ninety-degree angle across empty space, these creatures do not fight it. They leap blindly from the rock face, curling their flat bodies into tight wedges, and allow the gravity-warped current to catch them. They ride the upward-spiraling orbital rings of water with the casual, terrifying grace of a spider surfing its own web. 

But their true mastery of the canyon lies in their exploitation of its glitches. 

Because the reality here stutters, they have learned to use the spatial collapses for transit. A creature will stand perfectly still on a precipice, its jaw pressed to the rock, sensing the localized buildup of atmospheric pressure. It waits for the physical space to violently fold. When a high peak miles away suddenly elasticates and violently snaps forward to sit flush against the foreground, the creature simply steps across the gap. A microsecond later, reality snaps back, taking the creature miles away with it in the deafening thunderclap of displaced air. To the outside observer, they appear to teleport through explosive shocks of raw atmosphere.

Yet, they are desperately, agonizingly primitive. 

Their civilization consists only of stone striking stone. They possess massive, crude axes chipped from heavy slabs of deep-canyon slate. Because there is no wood, no fiber, and no sinew in this completely sterile environment, they assemble their tools using friction and the anomalous gravity of the water itself. A stone axe head is bound to a stone handle by plunging the joint into a stream of perpetually orbiting water; the liquid forms a high-pressure, zero-gravity collar around the stones, locking them tightly together forever.

And with these primitive tools, they hack their catchment basins. They chisel the aqueducts. But what do they consume in a world with no calories, no moss, no meat? 

They drink the anomalies.

The inhabitants survive by harvesting the friction of overlapping dimensions. When the alien water pools in their hand-hewn bowls, becoming a lens that bleeds shifting realities, an inhabitant will kneel at the edge. Cupping its thick, heavily-scarred hands, it lifts the fractured water to its mouth. When it drinks, it swallows the kinetic energy of a collapsing distance, the raw, buzzing caloric output of displaced space. 

Watch one after it drinks from a basin of stuttering water: for several minutes, the creature’s silhouette begins to violently glitch. Its physical location blurs. It exists slightly in front of itself, slightly behind itself, vibrating with the exact same harmonic frequency as the canyon walls, bathed in the warmth of a consumed alternate timeline. 

This drinking of dimensions is also how they propagate. In a sterile world, there is no biological reproduction. When an elder reaches the end of its impossibly long lifespan, its blue skin cracked and its internal antifreeze running dry, it climbs to the highest, most perfectly chiseled catchment basin. It waits until the seething water creates a perfect, lens-like window into another iteration of the canyon. It stares into the reflection of a parallel reality. 

Then, the elder plunges its arms into the liquid, reaches through the skin of the other dimension, grasps its own reflection by the shoulders, and violently hauls its parallel self into this world. 

The new arrival emerges gasping, slick with multidimensional vapor, identical to the elder yet completely refreshed. Sometimes, the pull is imperfect. Sometimes the new arrival steps out of the water with its elbows bending the wrong way, or completely flattened in the wrong axis, a geometric misfire of the shattered sky. The "glitched" ones are simply given axes and sent to the lower levels to hack new channels.

Together, this localized hive of blind, deaf, reality-warping stone-masons constantly builds. Their crude aqueducts spanning the voids have a singular, fanatical purpose. They are desperately trying to corral all the impossible, defiant water in the canyon, steering the screaming torrents into the darkest, deepest fissures of the stone. 

They are funneling the water to the very roots of the canyon, driven by a deeply embedded, primitive intuition. They believe that if they can pour enough heavy, multidimensional water into the core of the broken machine beneath them, they can drown the engine. They work in darkness, fueled by stuttering temporal anomalies, hacking with crude stones, hoping to one day cool the violent friction of the world, mend the cracked, searing sky, and finally bring silence to their roaring home.
