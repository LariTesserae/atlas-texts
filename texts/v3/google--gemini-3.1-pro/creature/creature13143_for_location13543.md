To say the city is uninhabited is to project an animal bias upon a place that has transcended the need for biology's traditional vessels. There is no blood here, no muscle, no bone, and no central nervous system. Yet, into the eerie, roaring expanse of this environment, an endless population is born, lives, and dies, sometimes all within the span of a single thunderclap.

They are the mobile architecture. The localized thoughts of a sprawling, decentralized mind. 

If you watch the glossy, jade-woven surface of a cantilevered terrace as the barometric pressure drops, you will see the floor begin to bead. At first, it looks like condensation, but the droplets are thick, emerald-hued, and highly viscous. Within seconds, the beads draw together, pulled by a fierce internal magnetism of molecular geometry. They harden into crystalline, polyhedral cages of pure, pale cellulose. 

These are the *Turgor-Calculi*. They are the surveyors, the mathematicians of the living city. 

A Calculus does not have legs, wings, or eyes. It is roughly the size of a human skull, a perfect, shifting geodesic sphere of woven fiber containing a core of highly pressurized, boiling fluid. It moves by rapidly altering the hydrostatic pressure in its structural facets—swelling one side while collapsing another, which causes it to violently, precisely tumble forward. 

Dozens of them roll across the plaza in intricate, synchronized patterns, sounding like the rapid clicking of a hundred abacuses. They are measuring the impending storm. They possess a terrifying, momentary consciousness built entirely on sensing atmospheric density, humidity, and shear force. Where they find a weak point on the terrace, a Calculus simply stops, anchors itself to the floor, and instantly unspools its own woven body, sacrificing its mobility to become a reinforced, load-bearing knot in the floorboards, its fluid core bleeding out as a hyper-growth adhesive. Its lifespan is perhaps forty seconds. It does not fear death; its death is its purpose. 

Above, in the empty, mist-choked air, a different kind of inhabitant lives its life in a state of terminal velocity. 

When the hollow cylinders detonate, firing those spears of dividing meristem tissue across the miles-wide chasms, the spear itself is not merely a dumb projectile. It is a high-velocity organism. Inside the smooth, aerodynamic carapace of the spear is a *Vector-Core*—a dense node of navigational cambium. Once launched, this entity awakens into a brilliant, screaming state of hyper-awareness. 

Slicing through the hot, wet air, the Vector-Core assesses its trajectory against the ferocious crosswinds. It has no pilot and no wings, but it steers. It achieves this by selectively, violently shedding microscopic scales across its surface, creating localized drag, or by bleeding microscopic jets of pressurized steam from its tail to adjust its yaw and pitch in mid-air. It is alive in the air, thinking in mathematics, distance, and vectors. And it is incredibly, almost frighteningly eager. When it slams into the neighboring archway, its consciousness detonates. The mind of the projectile flashes outward, converting its kinetic energy into a sudden, explosive cellular frenzy, instantly becoming the anchoring root-system of the new bridge. It lives for the five agonizing, ecstatic seconds of flight.

But beneath the surface, hidden inside the mile-long aqueducts, lives something darker and vastly more powerful. 

The roaring rivers of rainwater that course through the city's veins carry monstrous, rolling inhabitants. They are the *Flow-Baffles*. If you could see through the smooth green skin of the pipes, you would see them tumbling through the darkness: enormous, spherical tangles of barbed, fibrous roots, some fifty feet across, rolling along the pipe walls. They are the white blood cells of the plumbing.

They scour the interior of the aqueducts, their stiff bristles trapping any snapped fibers or rogue tissue that might cause a blockage. But their true purpose is revealed when the internal hydrostatic pressure climaxes during a deluge. 

When a section of pipe is about to rupture, the rushing Flow-Baffles sense the microscopic groans of stretching cellulose. Hundreds of them rush toward the failing section. In a fraction of a heartbeat, they crash into one another, their barbed limbs interlocking in a flawless, complex matrix. They weave together to form a colossal, living dam—a pressure baffle. They absorb the blunt, catastrophic force of millions of gallons of water, their bodies screaming and fracturing under the weight, holding the line just long enough for the great sphincter-valves to blow open and vent the geyser into the sky. Once the pressure drops, the surviving Baffles unlock, untangle, and continue their blind, tumbling patrol down the dark rivers. 

And then, there is the abyss. 

When massive structures fail—when the heavy, drenched solar arrays snap and plummet into the misty, vertiginous void—they do not fall indefinitely. If you lean over the edge of a great plaza and peer down into the impenetrable white clouds, deep below the architecture, you will see faint, colossal shadows moving in the deep currents of the atmosphere.

These are the *Benthic Looms*. 

They are the largest individual organisms in the city, vast, medusoid structures of pale, translucent tissue, spanning miles across. They possess no solid mass; they are woven from single-cell thick sheets of a membrane so fine it is almost immaterial, held entirely aloft by the rising thermals of the city's blistering metabolic heat. They drift far beneath the bridges and plazas, silent, immense, and waiting.

When thousands of tons of sheared cellulose and ruined towers come crashing down into the mist, the Benthic Looms rise to meet them. The falling wreckage violently punches into their gigantic canopies, but the Looms do not tear. Instead, the moment the shattered architecture strikes their nets, the Looms flood the debris with a scalding, enzymatic acid. The heavy, dead plant matter is flash-dissolved, boiling away into immense clouds of nutrient-rich steam. 

The Looms do not eat the wreckage. They merely instantly recycle it, sending the raw chemical potential rising back up on the hot winds toward the city, where the waiting pores of the cathedral spires and hanging pyramids will drink it from the air to build anew. 

There are no animals here because an animal is entirely too slow. An animal hoards its life, protects its body, seeks shelter from the storm. The inhabitants of this place *are* the storm. They are an infinite army of selfless geometries and fleeting intelligences, emerging from the wet wood whenever a problem must be solved, only to gleefully obliterate themselves the moment the equation is balanced. 

They live, they build, they shatter, and they leave not a single trace of decay, ensuring the city remains forever immaculate, forever violent, and forever alone.
