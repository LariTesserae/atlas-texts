To understand the things that live here, you must first let go of the idea of the individual. In this stifling, eternal dusk, isolation is the only true impossibility. 

When your eyes finally adjust to the bruised twilight and the phosphorescent smear of the bacterial mats, the blur of motion resolves into a living, breathing tapestry of flesh. They are everywhere. They are a humanoid species, though to call them "men" or "people" feels utterly inadequate for a biology so violently shaped by this specific, razor-edged basin.

At first glance, they seem to be made of the very shadows and condensation that coat the valley. They are hairless, for fur would be a death sentence in the sweltering, unshifting humidity. Their skin is smooth, utterly devoid of pores that might weep sweat—the environment does all the sweating for them. Instead, their flesh is sleek and rubbery, slick with the ambient moisture. They are pale, but not the ghostly white of typical cave-dwellers; their skin is almost violently translucent. Beneath the surface, dense networks of thick, violet and deep blue veins pulse, giving their bodies a bruised, plum-colored hue that shifts and ripples as they move, camouflaging them perfectly in the perpetual violet twilight. 

They are gaunt, but not starved. Their bodies are masterpieces of tensile strength and extreme, wiry musculature. To survive a world composed entirely of vertical drops and razor-sharp volcanic glass, their anatomy has compressed into a coiled, spring-like density. Their arms are disproportionately long and multi-jointed, ending in massive, splayed hands with six digits. 

It is their points of contact with the world that are perhaps the most striking. The soles of their feet, the palms of their hands, their knees, and the ridges of their spines are not soft flesh. Evolution has armored them against the immaculate, cruel rock. These contact points are capped with thick, yellowish, horn-like callouses, dense enough to blunt the edge of fresh obsidian. They do not walk so much as they flow, moving on two legs or four with equal ease, their hardened foot-pads gripping the sheer, sweating basalt without slipping, never suffering a cut from the splintered glass. 

When one turns its face toward you, the sheer alien terror of its adaptations becomes clear. Their heads are domed and heavy, balancing on thick, muscular necks designed to absorb the constant shock of heavy labor. They have no noses—only paired, muscular slits that flare horizontally to drink in the thick, spore-choked air. 

Their eyes are magnificent and terrifying. They are absolutely massive, taking up the upper half of the skull, and completely lidless. In this soaking humidity, an eye has no need to blink to stay moist. They have no whites, no irises; they are deep, vitreous orbs of pure, liquid blackness. Within those dark spheres, you can see the faint, mirrored reflections of the glowing fungal terraces, drinking in every available photon of the meager light.

But it is the ears—or the lack of them—that explains how they survive the ceaseless, crushing roar of their own civilization. They possess no external auditory architecture. Outer ears would have been deafened and destroyed eons ago by the shrieking beasts and the cracking of stone. Instead, the sides of their heads are smooth, marked only by faint indentations. Their hearing is entirely internal and heavily muffled, tied less to the perception of airborne sound and more to the reception of physical vibration. They feel the roar of their world through the marrow of their bones. The acoustic pressure of the valley is a tactile map to them; they effortlessly navigate the claustrophobic maze because they can "feel" the shape of the cavern in the rebounding echoes of ten thousand flint-knappers.

And they are the source of the wet, echoing thrum that underpins the canyon's noise. Beneath their jaws, their throats are loose and baggy. As they work, these immense vocal sacs inflate, pulsing with a deep, resonant purple hue. They do not speak in words or syllables. They communicate through a continuous, overlapping song of low-frequency vibrations and wet, guttural hums. Millions of them, humming and thrumming in unison, creating a physical wall of pressure that coordinates the swarm without a single instruction ever being shouted.

To watch them in their suffocating proximity is to watch a miracle of fluid dynamics. In a city where neighbors can reach through doorways and touch hands, there is no concept of personal space. Strangers, kin, and children crawl over and under one another in a state of constant, friction-less contact. They slip past each other on the narrow, leathery bridges with the practiced grace of schooling fish. Hands casually brace against the hips or shoulders of passersby. Infants, clinging to their mothers’ backs with terrifying strength, are gently patted or fed scraps of gelatinous mold by anyone who happens to walk past. Their society is an unbroken chain of touch. 

Look closely at the figures squatting on the ledges. You see that their labor is not a chore; it is their baseline state of being. A flint-knapper sits carving obsidian with a heavy piece of antler. He is not looking at his hands. His massive black eyes are scanning the hazy, mist-choked air for roosting predators, while his calloused fingers move in a blur of terrifying precision. *Crack. Crack. Crack.* The rhythm is encoded into his nervous system. Beside him, three others are butchering a massive, blind mole-rat with glass blades, their hands moving in perfect synchrony, passing strips of wet meat and pulling the warm sinews tight with their teeth. Their teeth are broad and flat in the back for chewing rubbery fungal stems, but the front rows are needle-sharp, interlocking like a serrated trap to strip meat from bone.

They wear no clothing, for modesty is a fiction and the heat is absolute, but they are not unadorned. Across their chests and down their long limbs, they carry the marks of their lives. Using the very obsidian they carve, they cut intricate, geometric scarification patterns into their flesh, rubbing the wounds with the luminescent spores of the bacterial mats. The resulting scars heal into pale, glowing lines. In the dimness, the hierarchy of the metropolis is visible through these glowing tattoos—some subtly marked, others entirely illuminated like living constellations moving through the damp dark. 

Because there is no dawn, no night, and no changing of the seasons, they do not sleep as surface creatures do. There is no communal rest. Instead, they enter brief, localized trances. You see them standing in the corners of their stacked huts, or clinging vertically to a basalt steeple, simply locking their joints in place. Their dark eyes remain open, staring blankly, while their throat-sacs deflate. They freeze like this for roughly an hour, unaffected by the deafening roar of the industry grinding on mere inches from their faces, before snapping back into furious, kinetic motion to relieve a worker at the bone-grinding bowls.

They are a people entirely devoid of idleness, devoid of anxiety about a future or a past. They do not dream of escaping the rift. They are an engine made of flesh, perfectly calibrated to the brutal, wet, jagged reality of the deep rock. In the warm, dripping darkness, amidst a cacophony that would drive a human mad in an hour, they are perfectly sustained, perfectly adapted, and eternally at peace.
