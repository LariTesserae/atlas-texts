To understand what lives in this drowned, high-altitude cathedral, you must first stop looking for faces. There are no eyes here to meet yours, no jaws, no grasping limbs, no soft biological grace. The things that occupy this vertical sea of blinding blue-white light are expressions of sheer, unthinking geometry and geologic necessity. 

If you drift close to the raw, un-weathered rock—careful to avoid the lethal, jagged edges of exposed quartz—you begin to resolve the smallest moving components of the machine.

They are the cable-weavers. Up close, they do not look entirely organic. They are blind, heavily armored arthropods, the size of a human fist, but shaped like industrial calipers or soldering irons. Their carapaces are a dull, oxidized green, naturally plated in verdigris. They swarm by the millions over the sheer walls, moving with a terrifying, jerky uniformity. These creatures do not eat flesh or stone; they drink from the highly acidic, heavy-metal seepages in the rock. Inside their segmented abdomens, specialized hyper-thermal glands strip the copper from the water, smelt it using the ambient electrical heat, and extrude it. 

Watch them work. A dozen of these verdigris-spiders cluster around a microscopic fissure in the mountain's quartz. Slowly, agonizingly slowly in the freezing water, they raise their abdomen-spinnerets. Drawn out between them are perfectly uniform, glittering strands of pure biology—living, highly conductive copper wire. They braid these strands together using their forelegs, weaving thick, heavy cables that span across the rockface. They are mindless maintenance drones, driven entirely by electromagnetic instinct. When a natural current in the rock seeks a ground, the weavers sense the resistance and build a bridge to ease the flow. If a cable snaps—sending a localized shockwave and a burst of boiling bubbles into the freezing water—a thousand weavers will swarm the terrifying heat, sacrificing themselves to fuse the break with the copper in their own bodies.

Follow the thick, silken copper cables they leave behind. The wires climb the walls, anchoring into the sides of the colossal, barrel-shaped "pistons."

These tower-creatures are the lungs and stomachs of the caldera. Stacked fifty feet high, an individual segment of the piston-column is the size of a cement mixer. Their exterior armor is made of thick, interlocking plates of pyrite—fool’s gold—giving the columns a dim, heavy, metallic luster that flashes brilliantly when the sun strikes them through the ice lens above. 

To float beside one is to realize your own terrifying fragility. The water around them is violently turbulent, thick with the pull of their mechanism. Between the heavy armor plates are thick, exposed bands of dark gray meat. This is electro-reactive muscle. It is so dense it resembles vulcanized rubber. These creatures are deaf, blind, and motionless—until the electrical charge fed to them by the weavers reaches a critical, agonizing threshold. 

When the voltage peaks, the gray muscle violently spasms. It is a terrifying, instantaneous contraction of thousands of tons of biomass. The *THUD* that punches through the water feels as if an anvil has been dropped squarely on your chest. The water inside their cavernous hollow cores is instantly pressurized and purged through exhaust vents, stripping away heavy metals and leaving behind a concentrated plume of acidic, oxidized waste. As the electrical charge grounds out, the column slowly, lazily relaxes, taking another twenty seconds to draw the newly metallic water back into its hollows, expanding with the inexorable, creeping power of a hydraulic lift.

But the most mesmerizing, hypnotic horror lies in the "turbines." 

Fed by the massive currents flowing from the pistons and the copper veins, these disc-shaped entities jut from the walls like the spinning blades of monumental jet engines. They are ten feet in diameter, their exterior shells calcified into heavy, spiraling ridges of bone-white silica. 

They are anchored deeply into the rock face by a rigid, living axle—a stalk of solid bio-mineral that fuses directly with the mountain. They do not paddle. They do not swim. Their ridged shells catch the immense magnetic fields looping through the chamber. Powered by the raw electromotive force, the entire outer disc rotates around the stationary axle. 

It is this rotation that generates the continuous, ear-splitting shriek. The friction of the spinning bone-shell against the stationary axle creates an eternal screech of grinding rock and shell, audible for miles through the solid mountain. But they turn so slowly. Under the crushing thousands of pounds of high-pressure water, a turbine takes a full, agonizing minute to complete a single rotation. Yet, the torque they produce is infinite. If a boulder fell from the icy roof and became wedged in the ridges of a turbine, it would not stop. The boulder would simply be pulverized into dust, ground to nothing by the slow, unyielding rotation of the creature’s feeding cycle. They simply spin, churning the metal-rich water, generating localized vortexes that draw microscopic silica flakes directly into their porous, spinning flesh.

For a long while, as you hang suspended in the blinding, shadowless light, deafened by the grinding, thunderous factory of the walls, you might think this is all there is. A static engine attached to the mountain.

But the ecosystem has birthed an apex. 

You do not see it at first, because the blinding white light hides it. It lives entirely unmoored in the open, central column of the water. 

You feel it approaching by the sudden change in the water's taste—a heavy, metallic tang of ozone and battery acid floods your mouth. The hairs on your arms, despite the crushing cold, stand on end. The water begins to hum with a frequency that shakes the fluid in your eyeballs.

From the depths below, a shape drifts upward. It spans fifty feet from wingtip to wingtip. It is utterly translucent, gelatinous, and flat, shaped somewhat like an earthly manta ray, but with trailing ribbons of flesh that extend dozens of yards beneath it. Through its clear, glassy body, you can see its nervous system: a beautiful, fractal web of pure, biological silver and gold wire, branching outward into its wings like the veins of a delicate leaf.

It is a Voltaic Ray—a creature that feeds on lightning.

It drifts with impossible, agonizing slowness, riding the invisible columns of magnetism generated by the grinding turbines. It flutters, floating upward toward the ice-lens roof. 

On the sheer rock walls beside you, the electrical pressure is compounding. A localized vein of iron has built up a massive, unsustained charge. The water begins to boil locally against the rock wall. Blue-white corona arcs begin to spit angrily from the stone. The whine builds, rising over the thudding of the pistons and the shrieking of the turbines.

The Voltaic Ray senses the buildup. It slowly, deliberately positions its massive, kite-like body directly in the empty water between the charged iron vein and a copper grounding point on the opposite wall. It waits.

*CRACK.*

A thick, violent arc of superheated blue plasma tears through the freezing water, bridging the fifty-foot gap. 

But it does not hit the water. It hits the beast.

The ray catches the lightning strike across its vast, translucent wingspan. In a fraction of a second, the creature absorbs millions of volts. The silver and gold wiring inside its gelatinous body flares with incandescent, blinding brilliance. For three sustained seconds, the creature becomes a living sun, burning so brightly it eclipses the light from the ice above. Its silhouette is seared into your retinas. Every vein, every filament of its internal circuitry turns a screaming, glowing white as it gorges on the pure geological electricity. 

Then, the arc dies. 

The light fades from the creature's internal wiring, dimming back to a dull, satisfied amber glow. Utterly unharmed, and moving with the same glacial, unbothered slowness, the colossal kite tilts its translucent wings and begins to drift further down into the deafening blue depths, searching for the next storm. 

Around it, the pistons heave. The giant turbines grind. The weavers stitch the burned rock. The immense, living machine of the mountain simply continues to run.
