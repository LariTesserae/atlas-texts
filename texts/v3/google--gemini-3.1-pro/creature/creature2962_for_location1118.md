Because this world has only just been born, the things that live here are not yet entirely certain they are alive. They are preliminary sketches of biology, hesitant drafts written in the margins of a mathematical god’s ledger. 

To find them, you must adjust how you perceive intent. They do not hunt, they do not flee, and they do not vocalize. They are expressions of the arcology’s environmental pressures—living manifestations of gravity, humidity, and light.

In the violently lush, violently angled forest-ledges, the first of these inhabitants forms. They are the **Ephemerals**, creatures born from the localized temporal fields where the jungle ages at a terrifying speed. If you watch a thicket of the bioluminescent silver-blue moss, you will see it begin to tighten, knotting itself around a fallen branch or an exposed root of colossal vine. Over the course of twenty minutes, the vegetation weaves itself into a free-moving, semi-solid mass. 

They resemble vaguely radial beasts—like great, slow-moving spiders made entirely of wet ferns, glowing moss, and damp bark. They do not possess organs, faces, or central nervous systems; their intelligence is decentralized, a botanical instinct driven by the magnetic currents of the architecture. An Ephemeral will detach itself from the forest wall, walking upon six or eight articulated limbs of woven vine, and begin its brief life's work. It moves through the canopy, violently pruning the hyper-growth, gathering the heavy, waxy leaves that block the pathways of the smaller hard-light bridges. They are the jungle’s immune system and its gardeners, ensuring the vegetation does not tear the delicate architectural geometries apart. 

But their lives are brutally bound to the temporal fields that birthed them. After an hour of slow, deliberate labor, the Ephemeral steps to the edge of an abyss. Here, the temporal slipstream causes its botanical body to instantly run out of time. Its vines brown and wither in seconds; its silver light dims. With a final, silent shudder, the creature crumbles into a dry, iridescent cloud of pollen and dead flakes, cascading over the edge into the bruised-plum twilight. A life entirely lived, served, and surrendered in ninety minutes.

Out in the vast gulfs of empty air, drifting between the sheer black faces of the colossal spires, live the creatures hinted at by the ripples in the dampness. These are the **Aërial Polyps**, the deep-sea jellyfish of the open sky. 

As they emerge from the unseen, lower-dimensional folds of the city, their anatomy becomes clear. They are massive—some the size of cathedrals—yet intensely fragile. Their bodies are constructed of a naturally occurring, flexible hard-light geometry, resembling immensely complex origami folded from glass. Draped over these geometric skeletons are undulating veils of living mucus, specialized transparent tissues that drink directly from the thick, wet air. 

They have no means of propulsion. Instead, they ride the localized gravity-bends. You can see one approaching when the intermittent veils of falling mist suddenly warp in mid-air, curving to strike the creature's translucent canopy. As the chilled condensation hits their warm bodies, it creates a faint, secondary mist around them. They feed on the falling iridescent dust of the dead Ephemerals and the fractal pollen expelled by the rapid-blooming flowers. 

When the city shifts—when millions of tons of black matter slide into temporary waveforms to reconfigure the skyline—the Polyps react. They do not avoid the phasing buildings. Instead, they drift directly through the temporary un-matter, their translucent bodies glowing a sudden, searing violet as they absorb the frictional cosmic forces. It is the only moment they seem to express pleasure, their long, trailing tendrils shivering with pure, gathered energy. 

Yet, there is a third ecology, hidden in plain sight, living intimately with the black geometry itself. They are the **Silicates**, or perhaps more accurately, the *Shadows-in-Residence*. 

The pristine, matte-black architecture of the spires devours light, but if you stare long enough at the sheer vertical miles of smooth walls, you will see geometric shapes sliding along their surfaces. They look like two-dimensional shadows, long and angular, moving independently of any light source. They are flat, crystalline networks of dark matter that exist only on the skin of the buildings. 

They move with the deliberate slowness of hour hands on a clock. They have no thickness, no mass that can be touched by the humid air. They are flat entities, feeding on the static electricity generated by the condensing mist and the electromagnetic hum of the city’s unseen power source. As two of these shadow-beings cross paths on a wall, their two-dimensional geometries interlock, forming breathtaking, momentary mandalas of absolute darkness before they slide apart, having exchanged whatever slow, silent data they pass between them. 

All of these entities—the dying botanical walkers, the hard-light jellyfish, the two-dimensional shadows—ignore each other completely. They occupy totally different spectrums of reality within the same sunken basin. 

They do not mate, they do not fight, and they do not make a sound, save for the wet snap of branches or the hiss of steam. They are a profoundly lonely ecosystem. They wander the echoing, warm geometry as phantoms of a newly birthed world, keeping the architecture pristine, balancing the encroaching flora, and drifting through the bruised-plum twilight. They are waiting. Built instinctively by the city itself, they exist to prepare the habitat, keeping the machine perfectly tuned, waiting for the day when the true masters of this colossal lattice awake, arrive, or finally evolve.
