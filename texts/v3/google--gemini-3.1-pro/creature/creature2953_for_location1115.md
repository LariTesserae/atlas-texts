To live in the deafening, saturated bowl of the gorge is to be entirely subsumed by it. The inhabitants of this place—if one can still call them human, after generations of evolutionary drift in this isolated terrarium—do not occupy the settlement so much as they grow out of it. 

You do not notice them at first. They are perfectly camouflaged against the wet, weeping architecture, moving with the same inexorable, ponderous rhythms as the antique machinery. When one finally steps away from a tarred wooden stanchion or lowers themselves from a living canopy of banyan roots, it is like watching a piece of the engine detach and walk.

Their bodies are short, immensely broad, and built entirely for sustained, slow-twitch torque. In a place of unbearable heat and 100% humidity, sweating is a biological impossibility; instead, their skin has adapted to act as a porous surface for condensation. They are cool and slick to the touch, their flesh a thick, boiled-leather hide that repels scalding drops of escaping steam and resists the abrasive friction of canvas drive-belts. In color, they are a map of their environment. While their natural tone might once have been pale, they are irrevocably dyed by their labor: stained maroon to the elbows by stabilized rust, their shins blackened by marsh sediment and grease, their broad shoulders and scalps dusted with the brilliant green of flourishing algal blooms that take root in the damp creases of their necks. 

Because the air in the gorge is relentlessly pulverized by the roar of the macerators and the shuddering thrum of the steam-donkeys, the concept of airborne sound is obsolete. Over centuries, their outer ears have sealed over, leaving only thick, calloused ridges of cartilage flat against their skulls. They are entirely deaf to the air, but incredibly sensitive to the solid world. They communicate through the bones of the gorge. Standing barefoot on the vibrating iron gantries and slick wooden sluices, they read the complex, overlapping frequencies of the facility through the soles of their broad, splayed feet. A shifting tone in the floorboards tells them a boiler is over-pressurized; a stutter in the heavy thud of the flywheel signals a slackening drive-belt. To speak to one another, they press their foreheads against the resonant iron pipes or the dense, water-logged trunks of the hardwoods, tapping deep, low-frequency rhythms with their heavy knuckles. 

In this wet, olive-tinted twilight, they wear very little—mostly aprons and harnesses woven from the living, fibrous bark of the surrounding canopy, slathered in black machine oil to prevent rot. Their eyes are large, with wide, dark irises that capture the scattered, diffuse light, protected by a thick, translucent secondary eyelid that frequently blinks across the sclera to clear away condensation and engine soot. 

Their labor is a continuous, moving meditation. There is absolutely no rushing in the gorge; to sprint in such heavy, sweltering air would be to drown from the inside out. Instead, they move with the infinite patience of the deep earth. When a massive, algae-furred valve must be turned, three or four of the inhabitants will silently gather around it. They do not heave or strain with sudden bursts of energy. They simply grasp the rusted wheel, plant their wide feet, and gradually apply the full, immense weight of their dense frames. They lean into the iron together, matching the slow, pulsing rhythm of the steam pistons, until the sheer, relentless torque of their bodies overcomes the friction of the valve. It is an act of pure, unstoppable biology pressing against unstoppable mechanics. 

Their lives are an exercise in ultimate maintenance. They do not build new machines; the engine is already complete. Their singular purpose, stretching from cradle to grave, is to maintain the profound equilibrium between the crushing iron and the crushing green. 

You will see elder inhabitants—their skin deeply furrowed and entirely stained the color of wet walnut—suspended high in the canopy webs. They are the Adjusters. Tied to the roots with heavy hemp ropes, they spend years, sometimes decades, loosening a single set of heavy iron brackets by fractions of a millimeter. They work with heavy, greased wrenches, turning a rusty nut a quarter-inch every month, giving the living bark of the host tree just enough room to expand and grow without shattering the cast-iron struts it supports. It is a lifecycle devoted to accommodating a tree's slow swell. 

They subside seamlessly into the ecosystem. Their diet consists exclusively of the facility's output: the warm, nutrient-dense, starchy paste rendered by the macerators from the giant ferns, scooped out of the sluices with carved wooden bowls. When they are injured—a limb crushed by a slow-moving gear or scaled by a burst pipe—they do not sever and amputate. They pack the wounds with a dark, medicinal mulch of engine grease and fungal spores, allowing the body to slowly encapsulate the trauma, resulting in massive, knotty burls of scar tissue that make them look even more like the ancient trees they tend. 

When they sleep, they do not seek quiet or solitude, for there is none. They simply lash themselves to the warmest, thickest pipes with lengths of insulated vine, draping their broad bodies over the shuddering metal. Lulled by the heavy, rhythmic heartbeat of the steam-donkeys, they rise and fall with the breath of the machinery, perfectly content, heavily asleep in the perpetual weeping wetness of the valley, just another living component saturated in oil, water, and time.
