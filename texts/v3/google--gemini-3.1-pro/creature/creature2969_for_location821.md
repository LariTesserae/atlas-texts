To look upon the inhabitants of this drowned megalith is to first abandon the frantic, twitching rhythms of blood, muscle, and bone. In a boundless space utterly bereft of fauna, the very definition of a "creature" must be expanded, stretched until it matches the glacial pacing and heavy, hydraulic majesty of the abyss. Life here does not scurry, hunt, or flee. It swells. It reaches. It slowly and relentlessly envelops. 

If you were to observe this world through the lens of human time, it would appear as a static, glowing diorama. But if you were to speed up the centuries, watching the abyssal floor in extreme time-lapse, the silent, eerie peace of the submerged jungle would reveal itself as a theater of terrifying, writhing warfare—a pure, unadulterated botanical violence.

The reigning titans of this ecosystem are the colossal, succulent kelp-forms that anchor themselves in the seams of the cyclopean stones. Though they possess no minds, they operate with a brutal, osmotic logic. Their bodies are marvels of pressurized engineering, built to withstand the crushing weight of the ocean above. The trunks are less like plant stems and more like massive, muscular pillars of pressurized fluid, wrapped in layers of vulcanized, leathery bark. 

Because they have no eyes, they "see" the world through excruciatingly sensitive chemical gradients and the subtle, tectonic shifts of water pressure. They know where the mineral-rich thermal seeps bleed through the ancient masonry, and they vie for these anchor-holds with ruthless patience. A single "battle" over a nutrient-rich fissure might last thousands of years. Two neighboring giants will slowly angle their massive, redwood-thick trunks toward one another, their sprawling holdfasts—blind, fleshy talons the size of city blocks—grinding together in the dark. They crush each other millimeter by millimeter, seeking to sever their rival’s circulatory flow. When the immense pressures finally cause a stalk to yield, it does not snap; it lets out an earth-shaking, sub-audible moan that travels for hundreds of miles through the dense water, a sound of slow-motion surrender as the loser’s trunk is bent toward the featureless pavement to rot.

Through the veins of these giants flows the icy-blue bioluminescence. It is not merely ambient light, but the visible pulse of their sluggish metabolism. The fluid is a dense, antifreeze-like sap, heavily laden with localized isotopes drawn from the deep rock. Because the sap moves so slowly, the light does not flicker; it travels in long, ghostly bands up the towering trunks, charting a rhythmic ascent that can take a month to reach the canopy. To look at the forest from a distance is to watch a silent, localized galaxy inhaling and exhaling in deep, majestic breaths.

In the canopy above, the immense, leathery fronds are engaged in their own silent struggles. Without fish or microscopic grazers to nibble at their edges, the fronds grow to monstrous proportions—acres of rubbery, layered tissue. Here, a different kind of life dwells: the Stranglers. These are pale, parasitic algae that drift lazily through the water until they bump into the canopy. Once hooked, they anchor into the fleshy fronds and begin a hunt that spans centuries. Seeking out the blue bioluminescent veins of the host, the Strangler slowly weaves a suffocating lattice of thick, white tendrils, wrapping the giant kelp in an embrace that tightens over generations. As the parasite feeds, it bleeds its host's light, glowing with a stolen, icy-blue radiance while the titan beneath it turns a necrotic, lifeless black. 

Down on the melted ramps and bloated ziggurats of the megalithic floor lies the true connective tissue of this world: the deep-water fungi. They manifest as the bruised, purple mats of velvet that blanket the architecture. While the towering succulents war over thermal seeps, the purple moss is quietly consuming the very unyielding bedrock of the artificial world.

The moss is a saprophytic network, a planetary-scale colony functioning as an intricate, chemical brain. The fungal threads produce caustic, heavy enzymes—syrups that are denser than the ocean itself—which pool in the millions of eroded joints. These enzymes painstakingly break down the silicates of the billion-ton blocks, dissolving the unbuildable megaliths one molecule at a time. The dull purple glow of the mats flares and dims in slow-moving chemical waves as nutrients are sheared from the ancient stones and distributed across the network. A "thought" or a transfer of resources across this mycelial grid might take fifty years to cross a single sunken plaza.

When an uprooted titan finally falls, crashing to the paving stones in extreme slow motion, it is the purple velvet that rises to meet it. The fungal mats balloon upward, sensing the immense feast of raw biological material. Over centuries, the moss aggressively carpets the fallen trunk, injecting it with digesting mycelia. The corpse of the succulent is slowly turned to purple snow, dissolving into the crushing water, leaving absolutely nothing behind. 

Reproduction in this lifeless ocean is perhaps its most ethereal marvel. Without birds or swimming beasts to disperse seeds, the flora relies solely on the planetary current. From the blackened underbellies of the largest, oldest fronds, colossal spore-pods occasionally detach. They are the size of submarines, fleshy, neutrally buoyant cysts that glow with a faint, ghostly luminescence. Once severed from their parent, they become the absolute nomads of the deep. Dragged by a current that takes centuries to complete a rotation, these silent, glowing behemoths drift over the ruined terraces like visiting spirits. They may float in the frozen dark for fifty thousand years before gently bumping against an empty seam in a distant continent of paving stones, where they finally rupture, spilling heavy, anchor-seeking tendrils into the crack to begin the cycle anew.

There is no malice in this world, nor is there joy or terror. There is only the overwhelming, unyielding pressure of existence, met by life-forms that have molded themselves perfectly to the weight and silence of their reality. They are blind deities of turgor and time, entwined in a lush, luminous, and terrifyingly patient struggle, slowly digesting the greatest architectural triumph of a forgotten epoch in the cold, black belly of the sea.
