To perceive the inhabitants of this place, you must first forget the tempo of the upper world. If you look for the twitch of a muscle, the darting of an eye, or the sudden evasion of a startled beast, you will see only dead copper and weeping rust. You must let your eyes adjust to the bruised red gloom, and then, you must wait. You must watch a single cluster of calcified dials for hours, perhaps days, until you realize that the heavy, limonite-crusted shadow beneath it has shifted. 

It is only then that the architecture begins to softly detach from itself.

They are the *Anointers*. If they once had another name, a taxonomy forged by whatever terrestrial minds built this colossal engine chamber, it has boiled away into the humid centuries. 

At first glance, an Anointer appears to be nothing more than a localized accumulation of the room's synthetic geology. They are sprawling, asymmetrical masses of blistering zinc scales, draped in the ragged, soot-black thermal moss that grows on the warmest pipes. They camouflage perfectly within the rusted, sagging infrastructure. But watch a mass closely, and the illusion of inanimate machinery breaks down. 

A heavy, jointed limb pulls away from a brass flange. It moves with agonizing, glacial deliberation, taking perhaps forty minutes to complete a single grasping motion. The limb is vaguely humanoid, possessing a shoulder, an elbow, and a hand, but the proportions are horrific and dictated entirely by the cramped geometry of the pipes. Some Anointers have arms that stretch eight feet long, multi-jointed and thin as cables, adapted for reaching deep into the spinning guts of the reduction gears. Others are squat, hulking things, shaped like oil drums, their torsos molded by a lifetime of wedging themselves between fluted radiator fins. 

Their flesh is a terrifying, miraculous compromise between biology and metallurgy. They have no skin left, at least none that would recognize the sun. They are covered in a carapace of living verdigris and oxidized iron flakes. Where their joints bend, you can catch the faint, wet shine of translucent, gelatinous tissue, heavily lubricated by the ambient oil and sweat of the chamber. 

They have no faces—not anymore. Centuries passed in absolute darkness have smoothed their skulls into featureless domes of pale calcium carbonate, built up from the ceaseless dripping of the ceiling above. Where eyes might once have been, there are deep, smooth grooves, highly sensitive thermal pits that allow them to "see" the world in brilliant, radiating gradients of ambient heat. To an Anointer, the pitch-black cathedral of the engine is a blinding, beautiful tapestry of searing white pipes, cool violet drafts, and the warm, pulsing amber of the oil pools. 

They live in a profound, quiet symbiosis with the machine. 

An Anointer's metabolism is almost nonexistent, tethered to the slow turning of the great reduction gears. They draw their primary energy not from consuming matter, but by pressing their bodies flat against the thermal conduits, basking in the intense infrared radiation like lizards on a sun-baked rock. You will often find them draped over the largest steam pipes, spread-eagled, entirely motionless, absorbing the heat into their marrow while the blind, translucent millipedes lazily graze on the moss growing upon their backs. 

Yet, they do move. Their existence is dictated by a deeply ingrained, almost holy compulsion to maintain the hermetic heart, even if they no longer understand why. 

Deep in the sweltering, damp wool of the chamber, you might witness their sacred duties. An Anointer will spend an entire week navigating a fifty-foot stretch of catwalk to reach a sunken housing where condensation has pooled. With agonizing care, it will cup its long, multi-jointed fingers, gathering the warm, oily water. It will then carry this precious fluid upward, climbing the vertical shafts with silent, sticky grips, until it reaches a massive, groaning ball-bearing screaming for lubrication. The Anointer will let the oil drip from its hands, perfectly timing the release to coat a single, revolving cylinder. 

They do not fix the machine; the machine is far beyond repair. They merely soothe it. They scrape away the heaviest, pipe-choking rust with hardened, calcified fingernails. They chew the deep-time calcium deposits off crucial release valves, digesting the minerals to add to their own armored bodies. They live to ease the friction of the deep earth.

Because there are no predators here, and because they consume so little energy, the Anointers are immortal in any practical sense. But they are not immune to the slow accretion of the environment. 

As an Anointer ages—living through millennia measured only by the dripping of condensation—it gathers more and more minerals to its body. The crust of rust and calcium grows thicker. Its joints become stiff. The effort required to pull its limbs free of the surrounding verdigris becomes monumental. 

Eventually, an Anointer will feel the final weight of its own body. When this happens, it does not die. Instead, it embarks on its final, slowing pilgrimage. It will seek out a structural weakness in the labyrinth—a sagging crossbeam, a fractured load-bearing iron stanchion, or a perilous gap between two deep sequence-lamps. 

The Anointer will wedge itself securely into the space. It will wrap its elongated limbs around the trembling pipes, lock its joints, and simply stop moving. Forever. 

Over the ensuing decades, the constant drip of mineral-heavy water coats the stationary Anointer. The silica and calcium build upon its back. The damp iron bleeds rust over its shoulders, and the thermosynthetic moss blooms entirely over its face. The Anointer becomes structural. It calcifies into a permanent, load-bearing pillar of flesh, iron, and stone, literally holding the collapsing cathedral together with the rigid strength of its forgotten bones. 

If you press deep beneath the tangled copper thickets, past the dripping sumps and down toward the lowest, hottest idle of the dying thermal cores, you will see them. The ancient ones. Rows upon rows of original Anointers, fused entirely into the bulkheads, their massive, humped shapes acting as buttresses for the machinery, keeping the crushing weight of the earth at bay. 

And at the very center—in the bruising cherry-red glow where the heat is so absolute it feels like a physical weight upon the chest—something else resides. 

It is the oldest of them, the first to descend, or perhaps it arose spontaneously from the friction of the chamber. It is massive—no longer humanoid, but a sprawling, fleshy root-system of pulsing conduits, weeping zinc, and heavy, moss-draped lungs that span a section of the floor the size of a city block. It is so thoroughly integrated with the deepest thermal core that it acts as a living heat sink. The great subterranean engine breathes its blistering exhaust into this central entity, and the entity absorbs it, dispersing the lethal temperatures into its vast, sprawling mass, allowing the machinery to run without melting to slag.

It does not think. It does not scheme. It merely breathes. 

*Inhale.* The groan of the iron leviathan. 
*Exhale.* A cloud of invisible, humid vapor rising to sweat against the ceiling.

This is what lives in the dark beneath the world. A gentle, ruined priesthood of rust and bone. They wait in the absolute heat, moving one millimeter an hour, anointing the gears, becoming the walls, and dreaming in the slow, sub-audible hum of an engine that refuses to die.
