To look up from the weeping, fern-choked base of the towers into the dizzying tangle of the rusted arcologies is to look at what appears to be a graveyard. At first glance, the cantilevered balconies and deep-shadowed catwalks seem utterly still, abandoned to the relentless drip of condensation and the roosts of the pale Rift-Kites. 

But if you watch the upper tiers closely, allowing your eyes to adjust to the blinding glare bouncing off the basalt canyon walls and the dark, recessed shadows of the iron bulkheads, the rust itself seems to detach, shift, and re-settle. 

People live here. But they are a people who have been entirely metabolized by the rhythm of the rift. 

In an environment defined by heavy, wet cold and a two-minute cycle of deafening acoustic violence, speed is not a virtue; speed is a fatal error. A sudden movement on a rust-slicked beam suspended a thousand feet above a basalt floor means a silent, tumbling death. Over the centuries since the engines were first sparked, the inhabitants of the condenser towers have unlearned the frantic, twitchy urgency of typical humanity. They move with an almost hypnotic, oceanic grace. They are practitioners of absolute, deliberate patience.

Physically, the condenser-folk are stout, heavy-boned, and thickly muscled, their bodies shaped by a lifetime of hauling themselves up and down vertical metal topographies. They are bundled heavily against the artificial winter of the condensers, wrapped in thick, layered coats made from the cured, multi-jointed canvas-skin of fallen Rift-Kites, waterproofed with a waxy, pale grease rendered from the amphibious grazers below. Because of this pale, slick coating, the people look remarkably like the wildlife around them—glistening, gray-white shapes slowly navigating the weeping architecture. 

To cope with the relentless sun-glare that fires upward off the crystalline rock of the canyon floor, they wear wide goggles cut from darkly smoked glass, framed in hammered copper. Beneath these visors, their faces are pale, smeared with streaks of black grease to break up the reflection of the light.

But their most profound adaptation is to the sound. Eardrums cannot survive the chest-cracking *THOOM* of the pneumatic exhaust. Over generations, the people of the towers have surrendered their native hearing entirely. They are deaf to the air, but incredibly, intimately attuned to the iron. 

They communicate not through shouted words, which would be snatched away by the machinery or drowned in the roar, but through vibration. Every inhabitant wears clusters of heavy iron rings on their fingers, and knuckles capped in molded lead. As they cling to the girders and catwalks, they converse by striking the metal scaffolding. During the interminable, grinding buildup of the flywheels—the quiet phase of the two-minute cycle—the arcologies ring with their language. It is a complex, syncopated code of taps, scrapes, and heavy reverberations that travels perfectly through the conductive skeleton of the tower. A mother miles up in the rusted canopy can tap a sequence onto a structural rib, and her child gathering moss near the dripping base will feel it through the soles of their bare, calloused feet. 

Their lives are inextricably woven into the wet, hanging ecology of the arcologies. They cultivate the strangling carpets of silver moss, using it as both insulation for their hanging hab-blocks and a spongy substrate into which they plant deep-shade tubers. They maintain hanging gardens of the fleshy broadleaf vines across the warm exhaust pipes, harvesting the thick, starchy pods that grow there in the dark, heavy wet. 

The pale, canvas-winged Kites do not fear them. Because the humans move with the exact same glacial, patient pacing as the beasts below and the thermals above, the kites merely blink large, glassy eyes as heavily bundled human scavengers slowly edge through their nesting grounds to harvest discarded feathers, guano for fertilizer, and the occasional massive, chalky egg. 

They are a maintenance society turned monastic order. They do not know who built the machines, nor why they must keep the air chilled. They only know that if the great engines stop, the water will stop falling. If the water stops falling, the moss will dry, the giant oxen will die, the kites will fly out over the horizon, and the harsh, baking glare of the sunrock will turn their home into an oven. Therefore, the engine is life. 

Every day, roped together with braided cables of dried vine and scavenged wire, maintenance crews slowly descend into the roaring bellies of the towers. They carry heavy buckets of viscous, black lubricant. They time their movements to the two-minute breath of the leviathan. 

*Climb. Position. Wait.* 

Deep inside the dark, the pressure builds. The air turns dense. 

*CRACK-THOOM.* 

The canyon shakes. The sound wave hits them, vibrating their teeth, rattling the smoked glass in their copper goggles, and atomizing the rain right off their waxy coats. And right as the miles of cooling chains begin their agonizing, squealing ascent, the workers step forward into the danger zone. They reach out and thickly paint the moving iron with grease. They work fluidly, feeling the deep tectonic chime fading, knowing exactly how many seconds they have until the next great intake of breath, when the chains will snap taut and pull with enough force to sever a heavy steel beam. 

Before the two minutes are up, they step back into the alcoves. They are perfectly safe, because they are perfectly synchronized.

There is no desire to leave. The sheer, unweathered basalt walls of the rift are insurmountable, and the vast, high-exposure plaza between the five towers is viewed as a terrifying, dry desert, crossed only when absolute necessity demands navigating the ancient, tree-trunk power cables that link their islands. They are content here in the dripping, booming shade. 

They live, they farm, they love, and they die in a perpetual rhythm. Slow, glistening figures suspended between sharp rock and brilliant sky, tapping out out their stories against the iron, taking a full two minutes to draw a single, measured breath between the thunders of the world.
