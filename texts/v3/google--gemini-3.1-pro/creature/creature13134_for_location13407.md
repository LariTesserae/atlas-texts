Because flesh and blood are incompatible with the ambient violence of the caldera, biology was forced to innovate. There are no creatures of bone and breath here. Instead, the Sump is inhabited by the *Scions*—ambulatory, botanical constructs born directly from the terrifying synthesis of the flora and the great machines.

They do not hatch, nor are they gestated in wombs. They manifest as massive, weeping burls on the sides of the pale, tree-trunk roots that coil around the deepest geothermal pipes. For months, these burls swell, gorging themselves on the leaked heavy lubricants and superheated steam of the brutalist keeps. When the pressure within them equalizes with the crushing atmosphere of the caldera, the burls violently burst. From the shell of bruised-purple bark and steaming resin, a Scion drops onto the rusted steel grating below, heavy and wet.

It is vaguely anthropomorphic out of pure mechanical necessity—the walkways, valves, and ladders of the ironclad keeps demand a localized, bipedal geometry to be navigated. But a Scion is a chaotic, asymmetrical knot of pure industrial botany. Its "skeleton" is a tightly braided cable of pale, hyper-dense root-wood, flexible as weeping willow but tough enough to withstand a hammer blow. Rather than muscles, its frame is wrapped in thick, fibrous tendrils—the same living gaskets that seal the machinery—which contract and release with the audible torque of vulcanized rubber. 

A Scion wears no carapace but the foliage of the Sump. Those gigantic, waxy leaves of deep cyans and oily blacks overlap across heavily strained joints, forming a sliding, armored skin that constantly drips with condensation. They have no faces, for they have no need to perceive light in the suffocating twilight. Instead, their "heads" are bulbous, porous knots of highly sensitive, spongy fronds. These sensory crowns "see" exclusively through thermal gradients and barometric shifts. To a Scion, the dark, atomized air is vividly illuminated by the blinding white heat of the great engines, the cold black ribbons of the marsh below, and the sharp, scarlet blooms of a cracking pressure valve.

They swallow the boiling exhaust just as the plants do, drawing the smog into deep, internal heat-exchangers. Their veins pump a vital fluid that is half-sap, half-distilled mechanical coolant, maintaining their internal pressures against the crushing atmosphere. When a Scion is punctured or crushed, it does not bleed red; it sprays a scalding, translucent liquid that smells sharply of ozone, ozone, and pine.

The Scions exist as the active white blood cells of the caldera. The terrifying equilibrium of the Sump is their only native religion, and they are its tireless monks. 

When an iron keep sounds the shriek of a failing boiler, tearing the murky air with white-hot steam, the Scions emerge from the dark crevices of the plant life. They do not run; they move with a heavy, deliberate, flat-footed thudding across the sagging gantries, their clawed, root-like feet locking perfectly into the serrated steel grating so the violent thermals do not blow them into the abyss. 

They use no tools. They *are* the tools. 

Upon reaching a ruptured brass joint, a Scion will plunge its own appendages directly into the jet of scalding steam. It is an act of gruesome, beautiful synergy. The hyper-accelerated cellular growth of the Sump's flora takes over; as the Scion jams its fibrous fingers into the ruptured threading, the immense heat causes the plant-matter to violently expand, petrify, and seal. Sometimes, a Scion will purposefully sever its own arm to wedge the dense root mass into a busted drive-shaft housing, replacing worn gears with its own rapidly calcifying timber. There is no pain in this, only purpose. Gorging on the heat of the repair, a new appendage bursts from the Scion’s oily stump within hours, unfurling in aggressive, fleshy spasms until it tightens into fresh, muscular vines.

Though they are blind, faceless, and born of weeds, they communicate. Beneath the colossal cacophony of the three isolated keeps—beneath the *THUD-SLAM* of the pistons—a secondary, rhythmic dialogue echoes through the canyon. The Scions drum against the hollow steel pipes with heavy, wooden knuckles. It is a rapid, resonant code of deep-bass thumps, transferring diagnostic data from the depths of one pumping station, across the miles of swaying chains and black water, to the remote colonies maintaining the other two. It makes the very air pulse with a low, tribal heartbeat, inextricably layered beneath the mechanical roar.

Yet, like the ancient machines they tend, the Scions are subject to wear. Over decades of absorbing toxic, industrialized rain, drinking raw coolant, and patching superheated valves, their internal capillaries begin to choke. They calcify. Their pale inner wood turns dense and blackened with mineralized scale, and their movements stiffen. 

When a Scion realizes it has become inefficient—when the heavy oil no longer cycles through its vines—it performs its final duty. It detaches from the great machines, walks down the rusted bulkheads, and wades out into the shallow, black-water marsh. 

It stands dead center in the vast, echoing emptiness between the pumping stations, enveloped in the eternal twilight, and simply stops moving. Slowly, the thick, fleshy leaves unfurl one last time, exposing the Scion’s core to the chemical rain. The sterile, highly astringent black water begins to dissolve the bio-matter. Within days, the organics break down entirely, leaving behind only the pure, refined metals, lubricants, and rare minerals the Scion had absorbed over its lifetime, dissolving back into the black swamp—ready to be drawn up by the deepest geothermal pumps, flash-boiled, and circulated through the metal veins of the leviathans once more. 

Nothing in the Sump dies. It is only swallowed, and recycled, in the deafening, blistering dark.
