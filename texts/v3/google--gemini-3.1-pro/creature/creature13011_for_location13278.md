To understand the beings who cling to the underside of the Trellis, you must first conceptually strip away the heavy, sour-smelling hides of the velvet-gliders they wear. Beneath the cured leather and dense, insulating pelts, their anatomy is a breathless testament to tensile leverage and starvation.

They are elongated, wiry things. Their limbs are distended, making up seventy percent of their body mass, wrapping around their torsos like tightly coiled ropes of braided steel. They do not have the bulky musculature of ground-dwelling brutes; heavy muscles consume too much oxygen, and the razor-thin air of the Trellis accommodates no excess. Instead, they are built of dense connective tissue, ligaments, and tendons that can lock their joints into agonizing, rigid geometries, allowing them to hang motionless from steep overhangs for hours, even days, without fatiguing. 

Their hands and feet are symmetrical—both feature elongated, double-jointed digits ending in thick, curved spurs of black keratin. These spurs are not claws for slashing, but living pitons. With a calculated strike, they can punch these natural hooks right through the flash-frozen rime ice and into the softer cambium of the stalks. 

In a world enveloped in absolute dark, eyes are an unaffordable metabolic luxury. Their faces are startlingly flat and featureless in the upper half. Where eyes might once have been, thick deposits of thermal fat smoothly bridge the forehead and cheekbones, protecting the brain from the sub-zero wind. Instead of seeing, they perceive the world through absolute acoustic and tactile mastery. 

Their snouts are broad and heavily corrugated with internal baffles to rapidly warm the freezing air before it hits their massive, deep-barreled lungs. But their primary sensory organs are their hands, and the fine, translucent hairs that cover their pale skin. When you see a lone hunter standing upon a cracked plateau of fresh bark, they are not looking out into the void. They are *feeling* it. They press their palms flat against the vibrating wood, feeling the microscopic tremors of the chitin-hounds scurrying a mile below. They turn their chins into the howling gale, the hair on their necks reading the turbulent eddies of wind to sense the silent, velvet-winged gliders dropping from the dark. 

The roar of the wind makes spoken language impossible. They do not have vocal cords meant for speech; their throats only house specialized air-sacs that can produce a sound akin to the cracking of frozen timber. Their true language is tactile and percussive. In the rare instances a family unit inhabits a shared nest—huddled tight in a hollowed-out knot, breathing each other’s recycled warmth—they converse by drumming complex rhythms on each other's ribs and spines. Across longer distances, they send messages by striking their bone-pitons against the iron-hard bark in harmonic frequencies that travel through the inner sap conduits of the flora, carrying for miles beneath the roaring wind.

To live in the Trellis is to accept that there is no concept of a map. The geography of their world is violently birthed and destroyed every day. Therefore, their minds operate entirely in the present tense. They possess no written history, no deep ancestral memory, and no concept of future permanence. They are intensely, fiercely alive in the *now*.

They survive not by dominating the violent ecosystem, but by slipping quietly through its gears. They hunt the chitin-hounds by waiting for the immense stalks to bend under the wind. They know that the internal friction will generate a burst of biological heat, melting the hoarfrost. They smear the sap of these weeping vines over their hides, masking their scent and thermal signature. When a chitin-hound scuttles past, drumming its frantic rhythm, the hunter strikes not with strength, but precision—driving a jagged thorn-spear through the gaps in the creature's armor, directly into its central ganglia. They drink the hound's anti-freeze hemolymph while it is still warm, a vital communion to keep their own blood from seizing in the cold.

Apex predators like the giant gliders are rarely hunted directly; they are scavenged or tricked. A hunter might harvest a clump of bioluminescent moss, tie it to a weighted bundle of chitin, and hurl it into an updraft. When two rival gliders dive blind toward the acoustic and thermal anomaly, they crash in a terrifying tangle of shrieking velvet and shattered bone. The hunter simply descends the vines to harvest the dead, claiming femur-bones for pitons, hide for warmth, and pure white fat for the lamps.

Ah, the fat-lamps. 

For all their feral adaptations and savage existence, the fat-lamp is the soul of their civilization. It is housed in clay harvested from the deep roots of dying shoots, shaped by blind, freezing hands. In a world of howling, absolute dark, these tiny, sputtering orange flames serve no practical purpose for navigation or hunting. They are entirely spiritual. 

The inhabitants of the Trellis are solitary by necessity, separated by miles of lethal, empty air. But deep in the unending night, a solitary dweller will light their lamp and place it carefully at the edge of their precipice. The minuscule light cannot push back the darkness—it illuminates nothing more than an arm's reach of scarred bark. 

But it can be seen from afar. 

Another hunter, huddled on a frozen thorn a week's climb away, might catch that singular, flickering pinprick through a break in the freezing fog. Seeing that light brings no rescue. It brings no warmth. But it is an anchor. It says, *I am here. The void has not taken me yet. The wind has not won.* When one sees a distant light, they light their own. Periodically, if the fog clears, a constellation of four or five desperate, impossibly small stars will glow across the abyss of the Trellis—the silent, glowing chorus of an impossibly resilient people.

Eventually, the flora will shift. A cannon-crack will ring out, a new shoot will burst violently from the wood, and a nest will be utterly obliterated, sent plunging down into the endless, bottomless dark. The dweller knows this. They expect this. They do not mourn the fall, just as they do not mourn the dark.

Instead, they pick up their ropes, they secure their bone-pitons, and they climb. They climb upward, into the howling wind, hunting the warmth of the flexing vines, carrying the clay pot of their lamp like a sacred seed, eternally re-weaving their lives into the jagged edge of a growing world.
