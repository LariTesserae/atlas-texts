To survive in a world where gravity and exposure are absolute monarchs, the inhabitants of this vertical megalopolis have become creatures of extraordinary density. 

Seen from a distance, moving along a sunlit terrace, they appear almost boulder-like—squat, broad, and wrapped in voluminous layers of wool and hide. But up close, stripped of their heavy outer felts within the dim warmth of a yurt, their physiology reveals a breathtaking, brutal adaptation to the roof of the world.

They are bipedal, but built with a low center of gravity. Their legs are thickly muscled and slightly bowed, designed not for speed, but for absolute, immovable stability on a precipice. They possess massive, barrel-shaped chests that dominate their torsos, housing lungs immense enough to draw whatever trace oxygen exists in the thin, copper-tasting air. Breathing, for them, is a conscious, rhythmic art. They do not pant. They take deep, oceanic inhalations through wide, flat noses lined with dense, frost-catching bristles, warming the air before it ever reaches their blood.

Their skin is the texture of the drystone they relentlessly quarry: heavily calloused, micro-fissured, and deeply pigmented to defend against the merciless ultraviolet bombardment. Across their shoulders, backs, and the outer lengths of their arms, their skin is almost slate-dark, absorbing whatever heat the searing sunlight provides. Their faces are shielded by heavy, overhanging brow ridges and thick, epicanthic folds of skin that narrow their eyes to dark, glimmering slits, protecting them from the blinding glare of the snow and the stark white rock. 

Their hands are wonders of biological engineering. They are disproportionately large, with thick, blunt fingers ending in broad, flat nails that resemble natural chisels. These hands have memorized the geometry of mountains; they can feel the microscopic grain of a granite block, sensing exactly where to strike it with thermal shock to achieve a perfect, friction-tight split. Their grip strength is terrifying, capable of arresting a fall over a thousand-foot drop with only three fingers wedged into a hairline fracture.

Because the cold steals energy so rapidly, they are a people of agonizingly slow, fluid motion. Every step is calculated. Every turn of the head is deliberate. When they walk the vast, rib-bone suspension bridges, they adopt a rolling, flat-footed gait, their felt-wrapped toes splaying slightly to grip the rough stone or braided hair beneath them. They do not fight the mountain; they flow across it like slow-moving mortar.

Culturally, they are as quiet as the stone that houses them. Since shouting is a waste of heat and breath, they have developed a highly complex, deeply expressive sign language. Their massive hands move through the air in graceful, sweeping arcs, communicating paragraphs of information through the tilt of a wrist or the specific splay of fingers. To watch two elders "speaking" across a courtyard is to witness a silent ballet of heavy limbs. 

When vocalization is absolutely necessary, it does not come from the throat. It comes from deep within their massive chests—a resonant, low-frequency hum, similar to the purr of a great cat or the rumbling of shifting tectonic plates. Families vibrate these low tones deep into the night, pressing their chests together for warmth, sharing the physical sensation of sound rather than projecting it into the void.

Their relationship with the giant, woolly quadrupeds is less like husbandry and more like a profound, codependent symbiosis. They smell deeply of the beasts—of lanolin, sweet dried milk, and heavy musk. In the freezing shade of the deep canyons, it is not uncommon to see a herder sleeping pressed entirely against the flank of a resting beast, sharing its thermal envelope. They harvest the wool without shearing the animals bare, combing out the winter undercoat to felt into ultra-dense tunics and heavy boots. 

Draped across their chests and woven into their deeply braided, coarse black hair are the copper discs used for communication. Polished with ash and fat until they gleam like true mirrors, these discs are their only real permitted vanity. In alternating flashes of sun and shadow, a precisely angled tilt of a chest-plate can send a beam of blinding light miles across the canyon, signaling the approach of a snowstorm or massive flock of scavenger birds.

They are intimately tied to the cycle of water. They do not gulp fluids; their bodies cannot afford to sweat them out. Instead, they sip. An individual might spend an hour slowly consuming a small cup of meltwater. Intriguingly, they are their own water source. Inside their sealed stone chambers, the immense heat generated by their bodies and the huge beasts they share space with meets the freezing stone. When the weeping condensation beads and trickles into the carved cisterns, they are essentially drinking the purified exhalations of their own families. It is a closed, sacred loop of moisture.

For an individual to die here is merely another phase of the geology. They do not bury their dead—there is no soil to accept them, and the rock is too precious to waste on tombs. Instead, they practice an ancient, practical sky-rite. The deceased are carried to the highest, most exposed fault blocks, beyond the reach of the shadows. There, under the heaviest sheets of blazing blue daylight, they are offered to the thermals and the giant, shadowed silhouettes of the avian scavengers. 

Once the bones are picked clean and bleached by the sun, the people ascend again, retrieving the skull and femurs to carve into new tools, new tent-pegs, new needles. Nothing is wasted. 

They are a people devoid of panic, stripped of hurry, and hardened to an unfathomable degree. In their silent, unyielding patience, they are not just the inhabitants of the mountain; they are the mountain's own consciousness, waking up slowly in the freezing, thin air.
