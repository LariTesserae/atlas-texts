To perceive what lives here, you must first abandon the assumption that life requires motion, or that biology is necessarily wet. In a place of permanent geological exhaustion, life, too, has surrendered the frantic, wet impulses of the surface world. It has distilled itself into a pure, enduring patience. 

Trace the curved, unbroken sandstone wall upward from the pedestal where the handaxe rests. Follow the smooth, ancient concavity high into the overhanging shadow, up to where the ceiling arches inward to form a blind, inverted dome. 

At first, there is nothing to see. The darkness there is dense, pooling like heavy liquid in the hollow. But if you stare into that pitch long enough, the geometry of the negative space begins to subtly unravel. What appears to be a natural fracture in the bedrock—a shallow, jagged seam tracing the circumference of the dome—gradually reveals itself to be a fringe of interlocking limbs.

Stretched across the crest of the ceiling, perfectly flush with the scalloped stone, is a creature of astonishing breadth. It spans nearly a dozen feet across, yet it is no thicker than a piece of tanned leather. It is flattened against the rock convexity, pressed so tightly into the stone’s contours that its body is indistinguishable from the sandstone’s surface. 

It has no color. It is the exact hue of the smothering shadow, its exterior composed of a dry, tessellated carapace that mimics the porous texture of the surrounding rock. It does not hang; it adheres. Six vast, multi-jointed limbs fan outward from a central, disc-like torso. The legs are bent at sharp, agonizing angles, their joints locked, pinning the creature to the ceiling in a pose of absolute rigidity. 

This is an entity forged by the kiln. It is a thermovore, a deep-time scavenger of radiating heat. 

It possesses no heart to beat, no lungs to draw the stagnant air. Such mechanisms would require too much energy, too much friction. Instead, a slow, viscous fluid—heavy with heavy metals and dissolved silica—creeps through its vascular system, driven entirely by the ambient thermal expansion of the chamber. As the walls release their hoarded, furious heat from the day, the creature’s wide, flat belly simply absorbs it. It drinks the temperature. The hotter the suffocating void becomes, the closer to "awake" the creature gets, though its wakefulness is entirely paralyzed. 

It has no eyes; the total darkness rendered them obsolete epochs ago. In their place, along the forward edge of its flattened mantle, droops a curtain of microscopic, gossamer filaments. These pale threads hang no more than an inch from its face. They are the most sensitive thermal receptors on the planet. Through them, the creature "sees" the room in bands of radiating energy. It feels the agonizingly slow *tink* of the sandstone cooling. It maps the topography of the chamber through the microscopic shifts in the heat gradient.

It sees the handaxe below. It remembers the shape that left it.

Thousands of years ago, the creature’s filaments sensed a chaotic, blinding burst of wet, furious heat stumbling into the hollow. It was the wanderer. Through its thermal sensors, the creature watched the blindingly hot silhouette of the hominid lean against the pedestal. It felt the frantic rhythm of their panicked breathing. It felt the sharp, sudden flares of friction as the chert core was struck, flaking away the jagged shards. 

The creature did not descend. It did not hunt. Hunting belongs to the restless world above. It simply waited. 

It watched from the ceiling as the wanderer fell still. It watched over days, then weeks, as the oppressive, baked air slowly wicked the moisture from the primate's body. It watched the heat signature of the human’s internal organs slowly fade, equalizing with the oven-hot air of the chamber. 

Only when the wanderer was entirely still, when the wetness was entirely gone, did the creature finally unlock its joints. Moving slower than the minute hand of a clock, it descended the scalloped wall. Over the course of a month, it draped its massive, dry mantle over the desiccated remains. It did not consume flesh; it simply secreted a localized enzyme that broke down the complex organic compounds, absorbing the latent salts, the calcium, the final, lingering traces of moisture. 

When it was finished, nothing remained of the wanderer but the handaxe and a scattering of flour-fine, bone-dry silt that blew away into the dust pan of the floor, leaving no footprint behind.

Now, the heavy, warm dark holds the chamber once more. The creature is back on the ceiling, spread wide. Its limbs are locked. Its papery husk is indistinguishable from the rock. It is full. 

It feeds on the silence. It feeds on the heat radiating from the walls. It does not move, it does not alter, and it will wait for the next blind burst of warmth to stumble into the dark, even if it takes another ten thousand years.
