To live here is to never know the concept of flat ground. Up and down are the only true directions, and gravity is not a subtle force to be ignored, but an ever-present, hungry maw waiting at the bottom of a bottomless world. The creatures that have claimed the Canopy of the Lattice are defined by this terrifying verticality. They are the apex progeny of the high-altitude hothouse, a sapient species interwoven so completely with the ruinous titan of the machine that it is impossible to tell where the architecture ends and their biology begins.

You see them first as a flicker of motion against the blinding glare of the sky.

They are arboreal, though the "trees" they navigate are kilometer-high exhaust stacks and tangles of rusted suspension cable. Formally, they are humanoid, but their geometry has been stretched and rewired by the demands of the Lattice. They are lanky and rangy, possessed of an unnerving, fluid grace. Every limb is elongated, engineered for leverage and brachiation. They do not have hands and feet so much as they have four identical, incredibly powerful gripping appendages. Their fingers are long, multi-jointed, and tipped with thick, keratinous hooks that can easily slide into the rusted grooves of sheared bolts or dig deeply into the waxy bark of the strangler figs.

Watching them move is a masterclass in kinetic mathematics. A hunter will hurl themselves off the precipice of a solar shield, free-falling for ten terrifying seconds through the steamy, green-tinted abyss, only to casually reach out and snatch a dangling cable of fiber-optics, their joints absorbing the massive deceleration without a wince, swinging upward in a massive arc to land noiselessly on the lip of a steaming pressure valve. To them, vertigo does not exist. The abyss is their cushion.

Their bodies are a canvas of their environment. They have no fur, which would be suffocating in the relentless, humid heat. Instead, their skin is a tough, leathery hide, deeply calloused and textured like cooling slag. Its coloration provides perfect camouflage: mottled shades of oxidized copper, streaks of burnt-orange rust, and the deep, rich verdigris of the damp shadows. To combat the aggressive, unfiltered solar radiation, their eyes are sunk deep beneath heavy, prominent orbital ridges. They do not blink; rather, thick inner membranes—iridescent and oil-slick black—slide horizontally across their sclera, acting as biological polarizing lenses against the glare.

They subsist on the bounty of the industrial jungle. You can hear the gnawing in the quieter spaces of the canopy: the sound of exceptionally dense, iron-fortified teeth chewing through the thick, rubbery plating of dormant low-voltage conduits. They feed on the starchy cores of the vines that grow there, but also on the machine itself. The deep, ferrous sludge that pools in the catwalks is scooped up and cultivated. They farm the neon-green algae that blooms in the concave blast-baffles, skimming it off the surface of the suspended ponds and baking it into dense, nutritious cakes on the scalding-hot steel of the sun-baked plates. 

Because the acoustic profile of the Lattice is a deafening wall of sound—the booming thermal expansion of steel, the subsonic hum of distant engines, the shrieks of gliding drones—these people do not communicate with delicate vocal cords. To speak in the Lattice, one must be as loud as the Lattice.

They are a deeply percussive culture. Every individual carries a pair of heavy, club-like mallets crafted from the dense, unbreakable ceramic of ancient electrical insulators. They communicate across the vast, misty gulfs of the architecture by striking the metal superstructure. A sprawling, complex language of *clangs, tonks,* and echoing reverberations rings out through the girders. They know the exact resonant frequency of a hollow cooling tower versus a solid cross-brace, and can send staccato messages traveling for miles through the metal bones of their world. When a tribe gathers on a major junction plate, their localized conversations are a rhythmic, polyrhythmic drumming, their mallets dancing over pipes and panels in a frenetic, deeply musical dialogue.

But in the thick, steamy fog banks where sound is muffled, they rely on the light. 

The electro-vines of the Lattice—the flora that absorbs faint electrical bleeds to synthesize glowing bio-pigments—are sacred to them. The hunters and weavers tap these vines, harvesting the brilliant blue and violet sap. They undergo fierce scarification rituals, packing this glowing fluid into geometric cuts on their arms, chests, and faces. The sap binds to their bloodstream, creating permanent, bioluminescent tattoos. When a heavy cloud bank rolls in and snags on the antennas, drowning the canopy in dark, wet mist, you can see them moving through the vapor: constellations of vibrant electrical blue, flashing complex sign language to one another with glowing palms.

They do not build houses; they weave holds. Their settlements are staggering feats of suspension engineering, massive hammocks and tension-nets strung between the colossal fluted smokestacks. They use braided strands of strangler fig, thousands of miles of stripped fiber-optic threads, and heavy-duty steel wire to weave tight, trampoline-like platforms over the empty drops. 

More profoundly, their entire civilization is tethered to the glacial life-cycle of the machine. They understand the slow-motion dynamism of the superstructure intimately. Where an outsider would see a static, city-block-sized camshaft, the inhabitants know it is turning. They build their woven villages directly onto these moving parts. As the shaft rotates a few degrees each hour, their village slowly inverts. Across a span of weeks, the floor becomes the wall, the wall becomes the ceiling, and the tribe dynamically unclips, re-weaves, and adjusts their layered nets to accommodate the new gravity. It is a slow, perpetual migration around the circumference of the great machines—a seasonal calendar dictated by the groaning, undeniable rotation of the gears.

They are apex predators, fearless and wildly inventive. To hunt the avian creatures with the multi-jointed wings, they use the updrafts. A hunter will tie a highly elastic bundle of vines around their waist, secure the other end to a rusted hydraulic lift, and simply step off a ledge perfectly timed to intersect the flight path of a flock. They plummet like stones, snatching birds right out of the air in a flurry of feathers and snapping beaks, before the vine snaps taut and they rebound back up into the mists, laughing a harsh, barking laugh that harmonizes with the hissing of the steam vents.

They worship nothing, for the Lattice is too real, too present, and too loud to be treated as an abstract deity. But they maintain a profound, almost terrifying equilibrium with it. When one of their own dies, they do not cast the body into the abyss. Biomass is too precious. Instead, the deceased is wrapped tightly in epiphytic ferns and lashed to the side of the hottest active exhaust vents. The intense heat completely desiccates the body in a matter of days. Their kin then return to grind the iron-rich bones and leathery remains into a fine dust, which is carefully packed into the root systems of the glowing electro-vines.

In this way, they ensure that the green, wet, rust-choked heart of the world keeps beating. They are the blood cells of the Lattice, endlessly climbing, swinging, and drumming their way through a brilliant, deafening landscape—a people born of fallen engineering, who looked at the impossible, rusted sky, and decided to call it home.
