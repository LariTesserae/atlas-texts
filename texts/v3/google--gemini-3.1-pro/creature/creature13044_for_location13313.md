The beings that inhabit this dizzying, waterless spire did not conquer it; they grew into it, adapting over geologic epochs until they became an inextricable knot in the spire’s waxy ecology. To navigate a world where a single misstep means an endless fall, nature has forged them into creatures of profound mass, unyielding grip, and exquisite patience. 

They are thickly built, squat, and immensely heavy, anchored by heavily centralized centers of gravity. Because their biology mirrors the environment—driven entirely by heavy lipids, hydrocarbons, and caloric waxes rather than water—their bodies possess a dense, unyielding solidity. They do not have soft, watery flesh that bruises or yields. Instead, they are wrapped in a deeply folded, leathery integument that resembles vulcanized rubber or polished soapstone. Their skin is mottled in the rich, earthy tones of their world: burnt amber, rust, and the pale ochre of the volcanic dust. As they age, this skin secretes a powdery, fragrant wax that coats them, polishing their bodies to the same glossy finish as the obsidian spire beneath them.

A human would look upon them and see echoes of both the sloth and the tortoise, though they are neither. Their limbs are thick and heavily corded with muscle, ending in broad, multi-jointed hands and feet equipped with blunt, spade-like keratinous hooks. These are hands designed for an eternal embrace. When they wrap their fingers around a braided bone-rope or a torso-thick vine, the limb locks with mechanical finality. They move through the swaying, overlapping jungle with a terrifyingly slow, deliberate grace, their heavy bodies sliding smoothly between the dripping amber sap and the velvet-leaved cabbages. They never leap. They never run. Every movement is a calculated transfer of immense weight, an intimate dance with the precariousness of the abyss.

Their faces are broad, flat, and heavily cowled by thick hoods of waxy flesh. To survive the perennial dust and the iron-colored twilight, their sensory organs have evolved away from human fragility. Their eyes are large, globular, and completely milk-white, lacking pupils but highly sensitive to the subtlest shifts in the permanent dimness. These eyes are protected by dual layers of thick, translucent nictitating membranes that act as permanent goggles against the abrasive thermal winds. They do not have noses. Instead, running along the sides of their thick necks and down their barrel chests are a series of heavily ridged, baleen-like filtration slits. As the oven-dry, dusty air pumps through them, sticky internal cilia trap the volcanic particulate, which the creatures periodically expel as hardened, obsidian-like pellets. 

To survive without a drop of water, their entire metabolism is a furnace of slow-burning oils. They feed on the aggressive flora, using heavy stone mallets to pulverize the pale-yellow tubers into dense, calorically overwhelming pastes of pure starch and fat. They tap the amber-scented vines, drinking the flammable, viscous sap directly, their lipid-based blood carrying the golden sludge through veins as thick as garden hoses. To supplement this, they hunt. Using long, flexible poles whipped from the resin-wood, they snare the giant, feathery-less gliders out of the thermal columns. The hunting is a slow, methodical trapping process; once a glider is hooked, the hunter simply anchors their feet to the stone and relies on their immense bodily density, hauling the screeching beast down against the updraft like a fisherman fighting a leviathan. 

Yet, they do not fight the dog-sized arthropods that clatter across the walkways; they live in symbiosis with them. The heavy-carapaced insects act as the settlement’s hounds and sentinels, feeding on the scraps of processed tubers and, in return, spinning out coarse, fibrous silk that the inhabitants weave into their rope bridges. It is common to see a massive, amber-skinned elder sitting cross-legged on a bone-bridge, calmly stroking the segmented back of a chittering arthropod, both of them radiating the comfortable, balmy warmth of the geothermal updrafts.

Communication among them is a marvel of adaptation to the deafeningly loud environment. They do not have delicate vocal cords to be drowned out by the roaring thermals and the shrieking gliders. Instead, they possess massive, bellows-like lungs and resonating chambers deep within their thick chests. When they speak, it is an exertion of the whole body—a guttural, percussive shout that vibrates through the waxy stalks and the polished stone. To stand near one when it speaks is to feel the sound as much as hear it: a rhythmic, booming *cough-bark* that cuts cleanly through the ambient chaos, echoing over the abyss like the striking of an enormous wooden drum.

Because they are a climax species in a climax ecology, they are immune to the frantic neuroses of ambition or expansion. There is nowhere else to go. Downward is death; upward is the suffocating dust. Therefore, their psychology is shaped entirely by the profound stillness of deep time. They are obsessed with maintenance, with thickening the knot of their existence. Generation after generation layers on top of the old. 

This generational layering is literal. When an inhabitant dies, their body—devoid of water—does not rot or putrefy. Instead, through the constant exposure to the oven-dry geothermal heat, the lipid-heavy corpse undergoes a slow, flawless saponification. They turn into dense statues of hardened, organic soap and wax. These revered, petrified ancestors are not discarded. They are lovingly woven directly into the expanding scaffolding of the settlement. The vaulted domes and fibrous walkways are mortared with the very bodies of their forebears, bound in sinew and sealed with mud. The settlement smells intensely of melting frankincense and toasted spices because the very walls are made of rendered ancestor-resin, slowly baking in the thermal heat.

If you were to stand briefly in this dizzying, roaring place, you would eventually notice one of these living beings positioned on the highest outermost ledge, looking into the iron sky. They would be perfectly still, their thick claws locked into the fossilized shoulder of an ancestor beneath them, their milky eyes unblinking behind translucent membranes. Around them, the gliders shriek, the vines ooze sap, the arthropods clatter, and the wind roars like a jet engine. Yet within this heavy, waxy creature, there is only the slow, rhythmic pumping of golden blood, and a mind that rests in perfect, eternal equilibrium with the ceiling of the world.
