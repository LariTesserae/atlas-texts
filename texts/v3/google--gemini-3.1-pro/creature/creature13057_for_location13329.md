To live in the deep-ward is to become an extension of its creeping, viscous heartbeat. Over the generations, the denizens of this tectonic splinter have been fundamentally sculpted by the dark, the weight of the air, and the relentless, slow-motion engine of the void. 

First, you notice their bodies. The people here are elongated and preternaturally lean, yet possessed of a dense, hydraulic musculature built entirely for isometric endurance rather than sudden force. Because the air is a thick, alkaline soup, their chests have expanded into deep, barrel-shaped vaults. Every breath they take is a conscious, diaphragmatic draw—a slow, deliberate inflation and deflation that makes their ribcages rise and fall like the bellows of a slumbering forge. 

Their skin is pallid, having never known the ultraviolet kiss of a sun, but it does not look frail. It has taken on the texture of wet, oiled canvas. Coated in a perpetual sheen of sweat, condensation, and the ambient botanical oils in the air, their flesh gleams dully in the bruised-violet light of the giant shelf-fungi. The damp, unbleached cottons they wear cling tightly to them, the fabric heavily stained with green copper verdigris, iron oxides, and the pitch-black of the narcotic resin they harvest. 

Then there are the heat-scars. The promptings of their hybrid environment are mapped directly onto their flesh. These are not the chaotic, jagged scars of accidents; they are smooth, geometric burns, the negative spaces of the machinery they tend. When a valve must be held closed against a surge of superheated thermal pressure, a worker might press their bare shoulder against the heated brass to gain leverage, taking the brand of the wheel’s spokes into their skin. Over a lifetime, a deep-warden’s arms, back, and torso become a sprawling, raised canvas of gears, rivet-heads, and pipe-flanges—a tactile résumé of their devotion to the great, slow engine.

Their faces are hauntingly serene, locked in the profound lethargy the environment demands. To look into their eyes is to look into the void itself. Years of surviving in near-complete darkness have caused their pupils to permanently dilate, swallowing the iris so that their eyes appear as liquid black pools, occasionally catching the cherry-red glow of the abyss or flashing with a ghostly purple luminescence. Their lips and teeth carry a faint, permanent obsidian stain—the physical toll of consuming the heavy, psychoactive sap that serves as the foundation of their diet.

This resin binds them to the terrarium. Ingesting it fuels their bodies, but it also sedates their nervous systems, aligning their internal clocks with the creeping, lunar biorhythms of the giant tubers. They live in a waking delta-state, a shared, hallucinogenic calm. Fear, panic, and anxiety have been chemically and culturally bred out of them; adrenaline is a lethal liability on a slippery catwalk spanning a bottomless, superheated drop. 

When you watch a deep-warden work, the mind struggles to process their movement. It is a kind of industrial Tai Chi. A worker scaling a sheer lattice of black vines and riveted copper does so with the hypnotic, calculated grace of a chameleon. You watch a hand reach upward—the fingers uncurling, drifting through the humid fog, finding a rusted iron rung, and wrapping around it. The single motion might take twenty seconds. They do not climb so much as they slowly distribute their weight against gravity. When they harvest the plants, drawing bronze scrapers down the sides of the swollen root-stalks to bleed the black sap, the swing of their arm is so continuous, so painfully patient, that it feels less like labor and more like a religious rite. 

Because the air is too heavy to waste on shouting, and the psychoactive pollen makes rapid thought difficult, their society is bound by vast oceans of silence. Their sign language is a marvel of environmental adaptation. It relies on large, sweeping motions of the arms and torso that can be read through the mist across the cavern. But it is more than just hand shapes; it is a fully tactile vocabulary. They communicate through the slow vibrations of the city itself. A worker might press their palm against a calcified root-stalk and tap a slow, rhythmic heartbeat against the wood. Hundreds of yards away, inside a hollowed-out boiler shell, another worker feels the vibration in their spine, understanding immediately that the sap pressure in sector four has crested. 

There is no concept of private property or individual ambition in the deep-ward. Survival is too precarious, the space too tight. Inside the massive, verdigris-stained pressure vats that have been drained and hollowed out for housing, they sleep in communal heaps. Suspended in hammocks woven from the muscular vines, their bodies overlap, sharing warmth during the unpredictable thermal drafts. In sleep, they look like chrysalises—silent, breathing clusters of humanity tucked into the iron and botanical crotches of the city.

There are no children running or playing; an infant born into the deep-ward is kept swaddled against a parent’s chest, lulled by the subsonic groans of straining brass, taught from the first days of life not to cry, lest they waste the precious alkaline air. They learn to watch. They learn to move slow. 

As they age, the deep-wardens do not retire; they merely slow down further. When a worker’s hair has grown long and matted with decades of black resin, and their body can no longer sustain the isometric agony of the catwalks, they take on the final task: the tending of the deepest roots, near the abyssal drop. Here, the heat is most punishing, the violet light dimmest. When they finally stop moving entirely, folding their legs beneath them on a damp iron grate, there is no mourning. 

In a stubbornly binary ecosystem, nothing is wasted. The ghostly shelf-fungi detect the cessation of their slow, measured breath. Violet-glowing spores settle onto the unbleached cotton. The muscular vines creep forward a fraction of an inch each day. In the suffocating, humid quiet, the line between infrastructure, biome, and biology dissolves completely, as the quiet curators are gently, inexorably folded back into the heavy soil of the god's sleeping lung.
