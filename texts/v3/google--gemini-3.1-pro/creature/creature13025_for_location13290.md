To trace the humming cables upward is to transition from a world of fluid crushing weight into a world of violent, tearing shear. 

As you follow the massive woven strands of rime-kelp up through the dark, the dense, syrupy ocean begins to atomize. It does not transition peacefully. The water tears itself apart, frothing into a perpetual, sideways-driving sleet, and then into a horizontal hurricane of heavy, breathable rain. Here, in the catastrophic boundary layer between the super-cooled ocean and the atmospheric oblivion above, the sky is a permanent, roaring white-out. Updrafts scream with enough force to rip flesh from bone; this is a place where gravity seems to have failed, defeated by the absolute upward fury of the planetary weather system.

And here, at the bitter end of those mile-long tethers, are the great, groaning islands of the Storm-Weavers.

They are not landmasses. They are gargantuan, artificial rafts—floating continents, some of them a dozen miles across, suspended mid-air in the permanent cyclone. They are woven entirely from the black, rubbery trunks of the rime-kelp, interlaced over thousands of years into massive, fibrous mats. To provide buoyancy against the little gravity that remains, these mats are under-strung with millions of the pale, gelatinous fungal blooms harvested from the deeps. When brought to this altitude, the spores expand in the lower pressure, ballooning into colossal, gas-filled lungs the size of cathedrals, straining upward against the kelp-wood decking.  

For the raft to survive the updraft, it must be anchored to the valley floor miles below. For the raft to not be dragged under by the weight of the anchors, it must trap the lifting-gases of the fungus. The existence of these islands is a precarious, violently contested zero-sum game between the sky and the abyss.

And the players of that game—the makers of the rafts, the carvers of the stones—are everywhere.

They are massive, moving through the blinding deluge with a slow, terrifying, unbothered grace. A single inhabitant is roughly the size of a bull elephant, but built low to the deck, a sprawling, hexagonal mass of muscle, blubber, and chitin. They have six limbs: two immense, pillar-like rear legs ending in retractable, hooked cleats that lock into the fibrous wood of the raft, preventing them from being blown into the sky; and four muscular arms. The lower two arms are enormous, heavily calcified, and end in broad, spade-like claws—these are the hands that swung the heavy basalt chisels, the hands that gouged the monoliths from the caldera’s rim. The upper two arms are longer, multi-jointed, and startlingly dexterous, ending in thick, leathery digits designed for grappling, pulling, and braiding the massive kelp cables.

Their bodies are sloped and aerodynamic, encased in a thick, slate-grey hide that feels like wet neoprene but is hard as horn. To survive the freezing storm, they do not wear clothing; instead, they harvest the anti-freeze resin from the rime-kelp. They continuously smear this thick, glowing-blue sap over every inch of their bodies. In the shrieking, white-out darkness of the storm, they look like glowing blue ghosts, phantoms of bioluminescent muscle moving rhythmically through the mist.

They possess no eyes. In the blinding spray of the storm and the absolute dark of the ocean below, vision is a useless liability. Instead, their heads are flat, sloping domes, ringed entirely by dense crowns of highly sensitive, feathery cilia. These tendrils act as barometers, anemometers, and hydrophones. 

Because they are blind, they live entirely in a world of vibration and tension. This is why the valley ropes are pulled so taut. The tethers are not just anchors; they are the sensory nervous system of the entire flotilla. The Weavers press their specialized upper hands against the massive cables, feeling the deep, booming bass-notes vibrating from the abyss miles below. Through the hum of the rope, they can "see" everything: the shifting of the sub-aquatic jet streams, the structural integrity of the stone anchor, the approach of a thermocline, the health of the kelp forests below. 

Their society is an endless, exhaustive choir of maintenance. The storm endlessly frays the raft. The Weavers never stop working. You watch them moving in perfectly synchronized teams of twenty or thirty, hauling dripping, redwood-sized logs of kelp up from the watery deep to reinforce the deck, singing to one another not through sound—which would be lost in the roaring wind—but by drumming their heavy claws against the hollow wood of the raft. The entire island shudders with their rhythmic, percussive communication. 

They are peaceful, herbivorous creatures, feeding entirely on the rich, protein-dense spores of the fungal lungs that buoy their homes. They have no natural predators; nothing else could survive here. Their only enemy is the wind, and the relentless upward suction of the sky.

But their existence demands an agonizing toll. 

As you watch, you witness the genesis of the stone anchors you saw on the dark floor miles below. A massive cable of newly braided kelp has been woven. At the edge of the raft, where immense, jagged shards of igneous rock have been dragged from the caldera’s outer rim, a solitary Weaver sits. 

It has spent perhaps years carving this monolith with its lower claws, boring the crude eyelet through the center. Now, it ties the final knot of the glowing black kelp-fiber through the stone. 

The anchors cannot simply be dropped; the chaotic updrafts would toss them like pebbles, tangling the ropes and tearing the raft apart. They must be piloted down, through the violent thermal tornadoes, past the jet streams, into the freezing dead-zone.

The solitary Weaver locks its four massive arms around the stone. Its companions gather, drumming a deafening, mournful rhythm against the deck. The Weaver does not hesitate. It shifts its weight, and drags the monolith off the edge of the raft.

They plummet. The Weaver and the stone vanish almost instantly into the churning foam, dropping miles through the vertical gradient, fighting the violent currents with sheer mass and the hydrodynamic bulk of its resin-coated body. It is a one-way trip. The Weaver will guide the stone to a flat, scoured plain on the abyss floor. It will ensure the anchor takes a firm hold. 

And then, trapped miles deep in the hyper-dense, freezing dark, unable to swim back up against the colossal pressure and the downward-rushing helices of water, it will perish, its body swept away into the dark to feed the fungi that will eventually float up to sustain its kin. 

Its sacrifice ensures the tether is taut.

Back on the floating island, the remaining Weavers place their hands on the newly dropped cable. High above the abyssal plain, they feel the sudden, violent jerk as the stone strikes bedrock miles below. The rope pulls painfully tight. It begins to vibrate. 

A new, deep bass note joins the roaring symphony of the city. The Weavers feel the music of their lost companion singing up the line, and they turn back to the storm, their blue resin glowing, perfectly anchored against the sky.
