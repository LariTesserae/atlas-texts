To find the architects—the tenders of this boundless, wind-swept machinery—you must first surrender your biological expectations of time. If you scan the ten-mile expanses of satin bedrock with a restless eye, you will see only stone, shadow, and the grazing lithic herds. 

But if you stop, if you sit upon the sun-warmed ochre dust and watch a single horizon for an entire solar week, the landscape begins to resolve. What you took to be a slender, uniquely upright spur of oxidized copper between two dunes is not a rock at all. It is a stepping foot.

They are the Anchor-folk. Or at least, that is the concept that closest approximates their nature. They are beings born not from a primordial soup, but from a dry, energetic crystallization. They are the ultimate, refined iteration of the metropolis itself, forged from the very polymers, noble gases, and heavy metals harvested by the colossal nodes above. 

Their bodies are towering and attenuated, often reaching fifteen or twenty feet in height, yet they possess a fragile, aerodynamic grace. They do not have skin. Their forms are encased in overlapping, smooth plates of chalky-white ceramics and dull, burnt-orange silicates. Eons of abrasive, high-altitude wind have sculpted them into shapes of perfect fluid dynamics; there are no hard edges on an Anchor-person, only sweeping curves and teardrop slopes that allow the prevailing gales to part around them without a whisper. 

Like the great turbines of the processing plants, they possess no physical joints to grind or wear down in the absolute dryness. Their limbs—long, multi-jointed appendages of fused glass and iron—are held together by localized magnetic vacuums. When an Anchor-person moves, their shin does not scrape against their knee; it hovers a millimeter away, suspended in a permanent, frictionless magnetic lock. Because of this, their movements are not just agonizingly slow; they are flawlessly, mathematically smooth. 

A single stride across the bedrock takes three of our days. To watch them walk is to watch the creeping hand of a cosmic clock. They lift a foot, the magnetic tensors in their crystalline thighs whining at a pitch only the arthropods can hear, and shift their center of gravity with the patience of shifting tectonic plates. 

Because they move so deliberately, the lithic ecosystem treats them as mobile geography. As an Anchor-person walks, thousands of the tiny, iron-rich crystal-scuttlers swarm up their legs and across their backs. The arthropods meticulously clean the the beings of accumulated precipitant dust, their piezoelectric mandibles flashing with microscopic sparks of gratitude. It is a symbiotic grooming protocol that has lasted for epochs. An Anchor-person in motion shimmers with a rippling, glittering coat of these tiny cleaners, making them look as if they are wearing cloaks of crawling jewels. 

They do not have faces, for there is nothing in this thin-aired world to look at that requires soft, wet eyes. Instead, their heads are flat, elongated cowls of smooth obsidian, ringed with bands of highly sensitive, exposed silica. Through these bands, they perceive the world not as shapes and colors, but as a vast, overlapping topography of thermal gradients, magnetic fields, and static charges. They "see" the heat radiating from the bedrock. They "see" the immense columns of warm air rising from the factory vents. They "see" the exact structural integrity of the deep stone beneath their feet.

It is this vision that dictates their purpose. The Anchor-folk are the tuners of the metropolis.

The factories operate automatically, but the extraction of noble gases and the compounding of polymers create massive variances in continental pressure and planetary resonance. If left unchecked, the deep, subsonic pulsing of the machines would eventually shatter the bedrock. The Anchor-folk exist to balance the frequency. 

As they walk the vast empty spans between the architectural nodes, they are feeling the deep, resting heartbeat of the machinery through the magnetic soles of their feet. When an Anchor-person finds a fault line, or an area where the industrial resonance is building to a dangerous harmonic, they stop. They sink to their knees—a process that takes a full afternoon—and place their fractal, bismuth-threaded hands flat against the warm stone. 

Then, they begin to sing. 

It is not a song of breath and vocal cords. It is a subsonic broadcast, generated by the rapid friction of internal polymer flywheels within their chests. The vibration travels down their arms and into the bedrock, precisely phase-matching and nullifying the dangerous industrial tremors. To stand near them when they do this is to feel the marrow in your bones hum, and to watch the dust around them organize itself into perfect, geometric cymatic patterns. 

They are deeply social, though their interactions belong to the realm of deep time. When two Anchor-folk meet in the vast emptiness between the monolithic plants, they do not speak. They slowly raise their arms and interlock their fractal hands. Once joined, they become a single, closed circuit. 

This communion is a data-transfer, an equalization of memory, structural diagnostics, and electrostatic charge. They will stand locked together in the unblinking, profound indigo daylight for months at a time. During this embrace, they generate tremendous amounts of static data-heat. Sensing this, the great glass-winged aero-grazers descend from the chimneys. The massive, translucent rays will slowly circle the two joined figures, their conductive silk wings brushing the tops of the Anchor-folk's obsidian cowls, bleeding off the excess static charge safely into the thin air, glowing with beautiful, pulsing veins of blue electricity. 

There is no malice here. There is no violence, no starvation, no decay. The Anchor-folk do not die; when a body eventually accretes too much heavy radiation or suffers an unrepairable magnetic collapse, the individual simply stops moving, locking their joints in a posture of eternal communion with the stone. Over millennia, the abrasive wind sands them down, returning their chalky whites and burnt oranges back into the rolling bedrock from which they were conceived. 

They are a people perfectly married to their environment. They require nothing but the radiant heat of the ground, the slow turn of the sky, and the deep, eternal pulse of the machines they tend. They are the living embodiment of the Roof—patient, immense, beautifully eroded, and profoundly at peace.
