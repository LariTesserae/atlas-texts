To understand the inhabitants of this place, one must first discard the concept of a "population." They are not a society, not a community in any vibrant, bustling sense of the word. They are, rather, the biological extension of the city’s limestone equilibrium. There are perhaps twenty of them in total, though it is difficult to be certain; they are so perfectly attuned to the pacing of the shadows that they rarely appear in the same courtyard together, distributing themselves through the vertical labyrinth with a quiet, magnetic repulsion.

Physically, the inhabitants are a reflection of the pale, exhausted geology that holds them. They are unmistakably human, but time and altitude have pared them down to minimal, efficient armatures of bone, sinew, and exceptionally tough skin. They are universally lean, lacking entirely the soft insulation of fat. Their complexions mirror the stone—a chalky, dust-rubbed pallor that holds no red flush even in the biting cold. When the crisp, high-altitude sun strikes their faces, their skin seems almost porous, absorbing the light rather than reflecting it. 

Their eyes are the only dark things about them, echoing the still, black water of the cisterns. These eyes are deep-set, heavily lashed against the glare of the sky, and they blink with an incredibly slow, reptilian deliberation. It is a gaze devoid of curiosity, ambition, or anxiety. It is the gaze of a creature that has memorised its entire universe.

The raw, undyed wool and woven grass they wear are not merely clothing; they are heirlooms, perhaps centuries old, meticulously maintained and passed laterally from one ascetic to another over generations. The wool is thick and hangs heavy, smelling faintly of the cold damp and the rust-orange lichen. The grass woven into their mantles and sandals is harvested from the rare, stunted sedge tufts—a process that takes decades to gather enough material for a single pair of soles. Because of this, their movements have evolved to protect everything they wear. They do not stride; they glide, placing their feet flatly upon the time-softened curves of the stairs to minimise friction. The leather-soled scrape that occasionally breaks the silence is the sound of a deliberately measured step, a foot testing the stone before committing weight to it.

The daily life of these inhabitants consists entirely of maintenance—not as a chore, but as an act of profound, moving meditation. They are the caretakers of a perfection achieved a thousand years ago. There is no new construction, no redesigning of courtyards, no carving of new rooms. Their labor is entirely devoted to stasis. 

In the mornings, figures emerge with stiff, hand-bound brooms of alpine scrub. They sweep the wide communal plazas with sweeping, rhythmic arcs, brushing away the microscopic accumulation of mineral dust and the occasional dried husk of a pale moth. They trace the mortar-less joints of the dry-stacked walls with calloused fingertips, blindly reading the structural integrity of the stone, ensuring the microscopic shifts of temperature have not unbalanced the flawless masonry.

Their relationship with the weeping wall at the rear of the alcove is their most vital ritual. They tend to the hand-rubbed grooves that catch the cliff’s sweat. With small, polished slivers of bone, they scrape away any rogue lichen or mineral buildup that might divert the slow trickle of condensation. When they drink, they do so from small, hollowed-out halves of dried gourds, dipping exactly to the surface of the dark cisterns so as not to disturb the sediment at the bottom. 

They eat very little. Their slow metabolic rate is sustained by a sparse, ascetic diet: a thick, bitter paste ground from the woody roots of the dwarf shrubs, supplemented by a medicinal tea steeped from the sea-green lichen. It is a diet that would starve a lowlander, but here, in the thin, suspended air, it is precisely enough to keep a human heart beating at a slow, echoing rhythm.

Linguistically, they are practically extinct. Speech requires expelled air, energy, and the disruption of the cavern’s immense quiet—all things they instinctively conserve. When two inhabitants pass in a narrow, roofless alley, they do not greet one another. There is occasionally a slight dipping of the chin, or the shifting of a shoulder to allow passage, but mostly they navigate around each other like fish in a dark current. If complex communication is necessary—to point out a hairline fracture in a water-groove, or to signal the approach of a high-altitude storm—they use a vocabulary of minute hand gestures and subtle, almost imperceptible clicks of the tongue behind closed teeth. 

The most astonishing thing about these people is their complete lack of temporal anxiety. They have no apparent fear of death, nor any evident drive toward reproduction. If there are children among them, they are kept entirely out of sight, perhaps nurtured in the deepest, warmest chambers of the honeycomb until they have learned the absolute discipline of the elders. When death does come to one of them—most likely from an organ finally slowing to a gentle, permanent halt—there is no weeping. The body is quietly carried to the deepest, most inaccessible crevices of the limestone scallop, where the cold, dry air simply halts decay, turning the flesh to parchment, allowing the departed to become just another dry-stacked element of the city.

Those who remain do not mourn; they simply recalibrate. The sweepers slightly widen their arcs. The water-watchers tend another groove. They are the living embodiment of the city's absolute finality. Caught in the high, cold light, clad in their wool and grass, they simply wait within the perfection of their hollowed cliff—not waiting for an event, nor for rescue, nor for the end of the world, but merely inhabiting the deep, unbroken quiet of a present tense that will never end.
