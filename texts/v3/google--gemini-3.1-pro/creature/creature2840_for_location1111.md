To view one of the velvet masons in isolation—magnified from its coarse-sand scale up to the size of a human hand—is to behold a creature that is less an animal and more a highly pressurized, kinetic jewel of biology. 

Beneath the dense cover of their signature silver pelt, their bodies are segmented, pallid, and translucent, driven not by a skeleton but by hydrostatic pressure. They pump warm fluid through complex internal valving to move. They possess an eight-limbed radial symmetry: six multi-jointed, skeletal legs designed for spanning distances, grasping vascular plant-fibers, and moving with terrifying agility in three dimensions; and two specialized, heavily muscled frontal arms that end in delicate, three-fingered manipulator digits. 

Their heads are entirely eyeless, presenting only a smooth, pale wedge of chitin designed to deflect the perpetual freezing sleet of the chamber. But springing from the lower edges of this smooth plate is a chaotic, exquisite array of sensory equipment. They possess feathery olfactory palps, stiff vibrissae (whiskers) that gauge humidity and barometric pressure, and highly articulated mandibles that close not like shears, but like the sliding puzzle-pieces of a geometric lock. 

Yet, you almost never see this pale, fragile anatomy. They are entirely enshrouded in their "velvet." 

This pelt is a marvel of evolutionary engineering. It is a dense, plush forest of microscopic, hydrophobic setae (hairs) that traps a micro-layer of insulating atmosphere against their bodies. In the pitch-black void, if you were to introduce a single point of light, a mason would look like a shivering, six-legged drop of mercury. The silvery hairs repel the ambient dampness; when the freezing sap-globules or the sleet of their own body-heat falls upon them, the moisture simply beads and rolls off their backs, leaving their core temperature intact. 

They need this heat. They run extremely hot. They are tiny, ravenous furnaces burning pure, raw starch. 

Their entire existence is a frantic, perpetual metabolism. Because the host tuber is constantly trying to heal over the city, the masons cannot stop working even for a second. If they were to sleep, the plant’s cellular walls would expand, crush their geometric plazas, and entomb them in rigid, freezing xylem within a matter of days. Therefore, the masons do not sleep. They operate in a state of rolling, continuous wakefulness, a species-wide frenzy of chewing, chiseling, and swallowing. 

Their diet is the city itself. As they use their resin-tipped adzes to carve away the swelling plant tissue in mathematically perfect, razor-thin sheets, they immediately pass the resulting starch into their mandibles. They extract the high-yield sugars to fuel their body heat and muscle spasms. The indigestible cellulose is mixed with their own glandular secretions to exude a tough, weather-resistant resin. This is the material they use to mold their tiny baskets, their scaffolding knots, and the replacement blades for their tools. Nothing is wasted. 

Because they are entirely blind, their society is bound together by a consciousness rooted purely in chemistry and vibration. 

Their metropolis of ziggurats and hexagonal plazas is not just a settlement; it is a massive, three-dimensional tactile internet. The gossamer suspension causeways spun from vascular fibers are pulled to specific, mathematically tuned tensions. Millions of velvet masons hang from these fibers at any given moment. By tapping their resin tools against the cables, they send sub-audible, resonant waves cascading across the cavern. 

A mason on the western wall can feel the exact location of a structural failing on the eastern wall—miles away on their scale—by the specific frequency of the shudder in her six gripping feet. 

When they move, they flow. They do not walk so much as they ripple over one another, a shimmering, liquid carpet of silver velvet shifting across the blinding white starch of the tuber. When two masons pass each other on a narrow vascular thread, they do not pause, but their facial palps blur together for a fraction of a second, an intense, high-speed kiss. In that microsecond, they exchange a massive dump of chemical data: *I am hungry. The sap is freezing thicker in Sector 4. The southern ziggurat requires more structural tension. The plant is healing too fast over here.* 

It is a true hive, but not a mindless one. There are no queens, no distinct castes of soldiers or workers. They are a pure egalitarian swarm of engineers, entirely united by the terror of the closing walls. 

Their reproduction happens on the run. Deep within the core honeycomb of the ziggurats, where the residual heat of billions of bodies makes the air distinctly balmy, masons will pause their carving just long enough to affix a single, translucent, pearl-like egg to the wall. Within hours, the egg hatches a miniature, fully formed mason, its silver velvet already thick. It is immediately given a tiny resin adze by a passing adult, and it joins the line, adding its tiny *tick* to the roaring, silent rhythm of the city. 

The mortality rate is staggering. The cold takes them. The massive, plummeting globes of freezing sap crush them against the walkways. exhaustion simply stops their high-speed hearts. 

When a mason dies, there is no mourning, nor is there any waste. The colony cannot afford the buildup of corpses, nor the spatial blockages they would cause. Within seconds of a worker going still, its neighbors will swarm it. They strip the dead of their valuable resin tools. Then, they simply push the motionless, silver-furred body off the ledge. 

If one could see into the vast, empty gulfs of the central chamber, they would witness a perpetual, quiet snowfall of the dead. Thousands of tiny, velvet corpses constantly drifting down through the dark, humid air, plummeting into the abyssal black lake at the bottom of the root-chamber, where they sink into the jagged ice and heavy sap, preserving the pristine, impossible geometry of the living city above.
