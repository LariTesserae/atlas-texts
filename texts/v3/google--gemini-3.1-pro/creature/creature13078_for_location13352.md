To look upon them as they emerge from the peripheral darkness is to realize they are not invaders of this brilliant void, but an extension of its geology. 

They arrive from narrow, lightless fissures near the cavern’s upper rim, slipping into the blinding pearlescent glare with the slow, deliberate fluidity of melting wax. There is no urgency to their arrival, for gravity and time operate differently upon them. They are a spindly, arachnid people, their limbs elongated and double-jointed, wrapped in lean, densely coiled muscle. In the humid, incubated warmth of the subterranean greenhouse, they wear almost nothing. Their skin is smooth, hairless, and dusted with a fine layer of powdered calcite, rendering them a chalky, spectral white that perfectly camouflages them against the illuminated limestone walls. 

You first notice their eyes, or rather, the absence of them. To survive the blistering, shadowless glare of the crystal ceiling, they wear tightly fitted visors—smooth, curved plates carved from the yellowed skulls of some unseen tunneling beast. These visors are tied tight against their heads with fibrous root-twine. Across the center of the bone, a single, agonizingly thin horizontal slit has been carved. It restricts the overwhelming radiance to a single, manageable ribbon of light, reducing the dizzying void to a flat plane of focus.

Below the bone visors, their mouths tell the story of their symbiosis with the cavern. Their lips and chins are stained a vibrant, bruised violet. Before making the descent, they harvest the flat, star-shaped rosettes that cling to the wet walls, mashing the spongy, fungal flesh against their teeth and letting the juice run down their throats. The violet sap is a powerful metabolic depressant. It slows their heartbeats to a crawling rhythm, cools their blood, and brings their breathing down to shallow, imperceptible sips. It forces them into a state of zen-like lethargy, allowing them to mirror the agonizing slowness of the cavern’s drifting ecology. 

Watching one of them prepare for a harvest is a study in impossible stillness. 

A hunter selects a hunting ledge, testing the slick stone with toes that are prehensile, the knuckles thick and gnarled. From a woven pouch at their waist, they withdraw the tiny, razor-sharp flakes of knapped obsidian. With the precision of a watchmaker, the hunter finds micro-fissures in the sheer wall—cracks too fine for a human eye to see—and presses the obsidian shards deep into the rock. 

Stepping out over the glowing abyss, thousands of feet of empty air below them, they trust their weight entirely to these glass-sharp slivers. Their soles are padded with a thick, leathery callus, completely impervious to the razor edge of the stone. They hang cantilevered over the void, supported only by three toes on a sliver of glass and the flat palm of one hand pressed flush against the smooth, wet limestone. The moisture does not make them slip; they seem to grip the water itself, their skin conforming to the undulating ripples of the established erosion.

Then, they wait. 

For hours, they become architecture. They do not twitch. They do not swat at the heavy droplets of condensation that plummet past their shoulders. They simply stare out through the hairline slit in their bone visors, a pale ghost hanging off a luminous wall. In one hand, they loosely grip the throwing stick of polished yellow bone, the coiled root-net hooked deftly onto its curved end.

Out in the void, a floating polyp drifts within range. The creature is torso-sized, a translucent gas-bladder trailing gossamer skirts, moving on a gentle updraft. It is blind and deaf, but acutely sensitive to changes in air pressure. If the hunter were to lunge, the bow wave of displaced air from their body would send the polyp bobbing safely out of reach.

So, the strike must be a paradox: violently fast, yet possessing minimal displacement.

When the moment comes, the hunter’s arm snaps forward with the mechanical, whip-like tension of a released spring. The yellowed bone stick acts as a lever, amplifying the snap of the wrist. The coiled net rockets outward. The tiny, polished stones weighted at its edges pull it squarely open in mid-air. 

*Clack.*

It is the loudest sound in the cavern—the heavy little stones colliding and tangling around the buoyant mass of the polyp. The creature’s gas-bladder is instantly crushed in the embrace of the roots. The buoyant ghost suddenly becomes dead weight. 

Before the heavy mass can pull the hunter off the wall, their violet-stained mouth opens, exhaling a long, silent breath as they brace their prehensile toes against the obsidian shard. The muscles in their gaunt back flare into sharp relief. Hand over hand, smoothly, rhythmically, pulling the cord against the heavy, humid air, they reel the catch in. 

The pale, moth-gecko creatures that weave through the air flutter frantically away from the disturbance, their leathery wings rustling faintly, fleeing the sudden violence. 

Once the collapsed polyp is hauled onto the ledge, the hunter does not celebrate. They work with mechanical reverence, bleeding the remaining buoyant gas from the creature's bladder so it will not fight them on the ascent. The translucent, gossamer skirts are carefully folded into a pouch to be woven later; the dense, nutritious core of the organism is bundled tight. 

Their work done, they do not linger. The cavern is not a home; it is too vast, too bright, too sacred in its quiet. Gathering their tightly packed harvest, they pry only the lowest obsidian flakes from the wall, leaving the uppermost and the throwing stick behind on the ledge—a marker, a promise of return, a heavy tool left where gravity makes it most logical to leave it. 

Slowly, fluid as melting wax drawing backward up the wall, the chalk-white figures scale the smooth heights. They slip back into the dark, jagged fissures near the ceiling, retreating into the cool, shadowed earth where they belong. Behind them, the subterranean cathedral remains unchanged: brilliantly white, suffocatingly warm, and perfectly empty, save for the slow drift of the ghosts, and the relentless, patient *plink* of water falling into the light.
