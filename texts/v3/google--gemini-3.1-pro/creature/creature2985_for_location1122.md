To witness the inhabitants of the Settlement-Engine is to realize that in a perfectly tuned ecology, there is no line between the machine, the product, and the operator. They are not merely living *upon* the grand, swooping boles that span the limestone basin; they are the regulating consciousness of the plant-factory, as vital to its mechanics as the fungal gears and pneumatic vines.

At first glance, moving in the bruised purple and mint-green shadows of the canopy, they might be mistaken for humans who have been peeled and submerged in cool water for a thousand years. But as they step into the sharp shafts of daylight, their true, hyper-adapted nature reveals itself.

They are tall, incredibly attenuated, and entirely hairless—any fur or hair in this permanently damp, cellar-cool environment would quickly become a vector for rot or unwanted moss. Instead, their skin is a pale, opalescent grey, thick and slightly rubbery to the touch. It is inherently hydrophobic. When the constant, drifting mist from the cooling-leaves settles upon their shoulders and backs, the water simply beads like quicksilver and rolls away, keeping them perfectly dry and insulated against the chill. 

Their anatomy is a masterclass in arboreal engineering, designed for a life suspended over an empty basin. They possess long, double-jointed limbs and curved, elongated torsos, allowing them to stretch across the vast, sparse gulfs between the scaffolding with terrifying ease. Their hands and feet are massive, equipped with extra digits that end in broad, spatulate pads rather than nails. These pads mimic the texture of a tree frog's toe, granting them absolute traction on wet, polished wood, or the slick, curved sides of the high-pressure clarification vats. 

They do not wear clothing in any traditional sense—fabric has no place here. Rather, they wear *living* utility. Fitted over their chests and waists are tight, corseted harnesses grown from woven cambium and green saplings, grafted directly into the uppermost layers of their skin. From these organic belts dangle specialized, calcified hooks. Some carry sharp-edged shards of silica-rich bark used to precisely slice into vascular pipes; others carry hollowed gourds for measuring fluid weight. 

Because the Settlement-Engine is so profoundly quiet, functioning on hushed pneumatics and hydraulic swells, the inhabitants are completely mute. They do not possess external ears—which would only catch on dense thickets of machinery—nor vocal cords. Rather, the sides of their heads feature flush, tympanic membranes, highly sensitive to low-frequency vibration. 

Their society communicates entirely through tactile resonance. To speak, an inhabitant presses the flattened palm of their hand, or the flat of their jaw, against one of the great municipal branches. By contracting specialized muscles in their throats and chests, they send deep, rhythmic thrums through the living wood. The entire settlement acts as a vast, localized tuning fork. A Tender on a lower residential node can feel the precise, coded vibration of a supervisor on the central processing deck miles above them. This gives their society an eerie, monastic coordination. A dozen individuals will suddenly step away from their tasks, in perfect unison, responding to a deep-wood vibration that the open air never carries out loud.

Their eyes are perhaps their most arresting feature. Lacking whites or distinct irises, their eyes are solid, luminous orbs the color of polished moonstone. They do not see the world as a human would; they do not need to read small print or track fast-moving prey. Instead, their vision is tuned to internal fluid dynamics and botanical health. They can "see" the buildup of hydraulic turgor pressure inside the leathery bladder-gourds. They can perceive the milky sap running behind the translucent plant-glass as a bright, glowing current, instantly identifying a clot, a rupture, or an inefficiency in the flow.

Their days—and their entire culture—are given over to an industrial choreography of maintenance. You can see them crawling slowly over the massive wheel-like fungal shelf-growths, using their own body weight to gently press down on the breathing exhaust-flowers, assisting the rhythm of the machinery. You can see them hanging upside down by their knees from the great spanning routes, using their silica-knives to scrape away excess lichen that might foul a slow-turning, living gear. 

They sleep in the hollowed-out nodes, curling into tight, interlocking circles for warmth. These small, wood-walled quarters contain no art, no decoration, no surplus. They do not cook. There is only one source of sustenance in the basin. At dawn and dusk, the inhabitants gather around deep, chalice-like indentations carved into the primary structural roots. Here, a specific valve is opened, and the heavy, pale, milky sap from the factory floors is allowed to bleed into the grooves. 

The inhabitants kneel and drink the sap. It is dense with synthetic proteins, sugars, and lubricating lipids generated by the botanical machinery. The sap sustains them, repairs their tissues, and gives their skin its pale sheen. They ingest the product of the factory, and in turn, their labor ensures the factory survives. It is an absolute, closed-loop symbiosis. 

The most profound expression of their total integration with the Settlement-Engine comes at the end of their lifecycle. When an inhabitant grows too old to balance on the spans, or is irreparably injured by a sudden, violent burst of pneumatic pressure, they do not face a traditional death. There is no soil for burial, and burning would introduce smoke and chaos to the pristine air.

Instead, the dying are carried to the outer edges of the settlement, where the newest, youngest boles are just beginning to reach across the void of the limestone basin. The individual lies down along the growing edge of the branch. The community uses fresh, living vines to tightly bind the elder in place. Over the course of weeks, the rapidly expanding plant matter engulfs the body. The inhabitant’s skin seamlessly merges with the bark; their bones, dense from a lifetime of sap-drinking, become structural rebar for the new growth. 

They do not just live in the infrastructure. Decades later, as a great, pale-barked bole holds a massive processing floor above the abyss, the tensile strength keeping it suspended is the petrified skeletal framework of their ancestors, carrying the weight of their living descendants, turning the slow, quiet gears forever.
