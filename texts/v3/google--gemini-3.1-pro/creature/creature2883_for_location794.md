To understand what lives in this blazing, inverted terrarium, you must stop looking at the ground and look instead into the bellies of the suspended lakes. 

The closer you observe the floating, quivering spheres of water, the more you realize they are not just reservoirs; they are the true substrate of the ecosystem, entire suspended oceans the size of living rooms. Suspended within their cool, high-pressure cores are swirling galaxies of microscopic life—translucent, geometric planktons that mimic the sharpness of the obsidian below. They are intricate cages of biological silica, thriving on the punishing cerulean sunlight, converting raw light into dense, sugary oils. When the light hits a globe just right, the water goes suddenly opalescent, revealing currents of gold and pink spun by trillions of these spinning, microscopic glass prisms. 

This living soup feeds the gliders. The semi-transparent, manta-like creatures you noticed earlier are the *skaters* of this world. Up close, they are unimaginably delicate, less like flesh and more like animated membranes of spun sugar and gelatin. They possess no eyes; they navigate entirely by sensing the localized gravity gradients and thermal updrafts. They ride the hushed *shhhhh* of the rising steam. When one reaches the edge of a water sphere, it doesn’t splash inward. Instead, its hydrophilic skin merges seamlessly with the water’s surface tension. Once inside, they beat their ghostly wings in slow, undulating rhythms, mouthless and gill-less, simply absorbing the sweet silica-plankton through the pores of their skin like a sponge soaking in dye. 

They are fleeting, fragile things. Because they are mostly water themselves, straying too low into the oven-like heat of the bedrock is a death sentence. If a glider misses an updraft and sinks into the sweltering dead zones, it desiccates in mid-air. Its wings shrivel, its transparent body flashes opaque white, and it disintegrates into a fine, iridescent dust before it even touches the stone floor. This dust acts as the pollen of the high canopy, fertilizing the massive, fleshy ferns before the invisible gravity fields sweep it back into the sky.

But beneath the cool, lazy sanctuary of the floating oceans, down on the brutal, sun-baked floor where temperatures can roast unprotected flesh in minutes, life has taken a much harder, violent path.

Clinging tightly to the porous walls of the raw basalt are creatures that look like nothing more than shards of broken rock. They are lithic isopods, armored heavily in pure carbon and iron leached from the newly born geology. For ninety-nine percent of their lives, they are utterly motionless, locked in a state of suspended animation to survive the blistering heat. They do not eat; they do not breathe. They wait. 

They are waiting for a rupture. 

When a giant fern drinks too greedily, or the wind shifts, and a massive water sphere breaks, dropping hundreds of gallons of water like a falling house, the environment momentarily snaps. The massive percussive *slap* of water hitting the superheated obsidian sends a titanic explosion of blinding white steam outward. 

In this sudden, violent release of kinetic thermal energy, the ground-dwellers wake. Superheated steam is their trigger. The moment the boiling white fog washes over the rock face, the isopods unclasp from the stone. Propelled by the sudden, localized rush of moisture and the precipitous drop in temperature, they enter a frenzied, hyper-accelerated state. In the five to ten seconds it takes for the steam to dissipate and the unseen pylons to pull the moisture back to the sky, these creatures live entire lifetimes. They scurry frantically across the wet stone, mating, shedding their heavy carapaces, and laying microscopic eggs in the micro-fissures of the obsidian. As the floor flashes dry and the brutal heat violently returns, the survivors instantly snap their fresh armor shut, locking their joints, freezing once more into inert slivers of basalt to await the next catastrophic downpour.

Yet, holding this entire polarized ecosystem together—bridging the frozen patience of the ground and the fluid dream of the sky—is one final presence. 

You might not register them at first, because your eye expects a creature to have a center, a mass. But these beings are mostly negative space. They are the shepherds of the basin, tall and impossibly spindly, moving with the slow, deliberate grace of wading herons. 

Their legs are like threads of spun black glass, sometimes fifteen feet long, terminating in pinpoint tips that minimize their physical contact with the scalding bedrock. Instead of a solid torso, their upper bodies flare out into wide, dome-like canopies made of a thin, translucent membrane—like the bell of a jellyfish or a living parachute. 

They survive by weaponizing the environment’s extreme mechanics. They position themselves directly over the hissing vents of steam rising from the rock. The hot vapor catches inside their blooming, membranous torsos, ballooning outward and generating tremendous lift. It renders these towering creatures virtually weightless. They do not walk so much as they drift on tiptoe, taking long, bounding, lunar strides across the jagged plateau, suspended by the hot air trapped within their own bodies.

They are the caretakers of the engineered system. A symbiotic extension of whatever intelligence left the white pylons behind. As they stride through the canopy, their long, mantis-like forelimbs delicately groom the giant coral-ferns. When a fern grows too aggressively and threatens the delicate surface tension of a water globe, the shepherds gracefully slice away the errant fronds. 

Between feedings, they congregate near the matte-white ceramic slivers. Their heads—tight clusters of pale, frost-white sensory tendrils—reach out and brush against the seamless artifacts. They are the only things in this basin capable of touching the pylons. When they do, wrapping their long limbs around the floating ceramics to cool their internal temperatures, the pylons hum in response. A deep, resonant cello-vibration hums through the rock, momentarily aligning the frantic buzzing of the flora's chlorophyll and the swirling plankton in the sky. 

They are creatures of profound melancholy and perfect adaptation. They have no predators. They do not build, and they do not speak. They simply maintain the garden in an endless, quiet devotion to the physics they inherited, wading through the shimmering, sweltering air, their ghostly parasol-bodies rising and falling with the breath of the mountain, waiting for the next drop to fall.
