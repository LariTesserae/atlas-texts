To survive a world that digests stone, one cannot remain rigid. Whatever sharp-minded, tool-bearing engineers carved the basalt arches millions of years ago realized, ultimately, that resistance was a failure of imagination. To endure the eternal hothouse, they had to surrender their hard edges. They had to forget the forge, the wheel, and the word. 

They had to become softer than the rot.

If you look closely into the dim, bruised-green canopy—past the creeping vines and the fat, suspended drops of water—you might see a shadow detach itself from a colossal trunk. It takes an hour for the movement to complete. This is one of them. They are the inheritors of the canopy, the descendants of the carvers, though they are no longer anything that could be called human. 

They are impossibly elongated, stretched by a million generations of gravity-free brachiating in the dense, fibrous mid-levels. Their limbs are not so much arms and legs as they are tensile ribbons of muscle and cartilage. Because there is no true ground to stand upon, they do not possess calcified, heavy skeletons. A dense skeleton would drag them down into the crushing, mile-deep peat. Instead, their internal architecture is entirely cartilaginous, flexible as green willow, allowing their joints to bend fluidly in any direction. When they move across the woven lattice of ferns, they splay themselves wide, distributing what little weight they have so perfectly that the spongy moss does not even sigh beneath them. 

They are hairless, their skin a mottled, velvety tapestry of deep emeralds, charcoal greys, and weeping purples. It is highly permeable, kept perpetually slick by the humid broth of the air. In fact, they do not have noses, nor do they heave their chests to breathe; they absorb the trapped, oxygen-rich moisture directly through their skin via osmosis. This lends a profound, deathly stillness to their bodies. A solitary individual might hang suspended, inverted from a titan-branch by three long, multi-jointed fingers, perfectly motionless for days, looking entirely like a rotting liana vine.

Their faces possess an eerie, flattened beauty, shaped by the muffling silence of the world. They have no vocal cords, no external ears to catch sounds that would only die in the wet air anyway. Where ears once were, there are broad, fan-like tympanic crests that flare along their jawlines. These crests are incredibly sensitive to low-frequency vibrations. When they need to listen, they press their chests and jaws against the massive trunks. Through the weeping wood, they can "hear" the rumble of the capillary rivers being pulled skyward, the shifting weight of the colossal beasts, and the distant, rhythmic thrumming of others of their kind.

Because the air kills sound, their language is woven from light. At the base of their long throats and down the inside of their forearms, they host the same blue bioluminescent fungi that pulse along the deep-woods trunks. They control the blood flow to their skin to feed or starve the fungi in a fraction of a second. When two of them meet in the sprawling dark, they communicate in excruciatingly slow, hypnotic displays of flashing azure veins—a language of sweeping light-patterns that might take half a day to articulate a single thought. It is a poetry of the patient. 

They do not hunt. To bite, to tear, to chew—these are frantic, caloric actions of a lesser epoch. They are tenders of the suspended lakes. With slow, languorous grace, they dip their impossibly long arms into the titan-bromeliad pools, drawing up the gelatinous, nutrient-rich egg sacs of the flattened amphibians, or harvesting dense, floating mats of aquatic ferns. They ingest their food whole, their jaws unhinging slightly, letting their slow, powerful internal acids do the work over weeks.

Despite their physical devolution into arboreal spirits, a ghostly imprint of their ancestry remains, locked deep in the unlit corridors of their biology. They are inexplicably drawn to the rotting basalt ruins. 

When the light shifts into the deep, oceanic black of night, they gather entirely by instinct at the perfect, circular stone platforms. They do not know *why* they congregate here. They do not comprehend architecture. But in a world devoid of straight lines and hard angles, the soap-smooth basalt triggers a dormant reverence. 

Dozens of them will slowly glide out from the bromeliad thickets and drape themselves over the black stone arches, their long bodies interweaving like a living, breathing knot of kelp. They lower their faces to the shallow, carved bowls overflowing with warm rain, drinking from the very basins their ancestors chiseled when they still possessed the terrifying burden of ambition. They will rest together on the stone platforms in a vast, silent tangle of limbs, their bodies pulsing in unison with soft, synchronized waves of blue bioluminescence, creating a radiant, breathing mandala in the dark. 

And when they reach the end of their immensely long lives, they do not fall to the forest floor to rot. 

Old age in this species is a gradual process of rooting. An elder will find a crook between two colossal boughs, weave their limbs securely around the wood, and simply stop moving. Their heart rate slows to a beat per day. The living moss of the forest begins to creep over their damp skin. Their cartilaginous bones fuse with the surrounding cellulose, their flesh slowly converting to peat and sap. Over decades, their closed eyes are covered by pale lichen, and their blue bioluminescent veins bleed into the tree itself, passing their light into the host trunk. 

They die exactly as they lived: waiting, melting, and quietly agreeing to become the architecture of the weeping world.
