The illusion of abandonment shatters only if you have the patience to look away from the spectacular, violent birth of the obsidian mountains, and instead focus on the pale anomalies—the crude granite ruins. 

You assumed the architect was absent. They are not. They are simply indistinguishable from their architecture, until they begin to move. 

From the shadow of a fractured ring of stone, a shape slowly detaches itself. It does not step; it shifts. At first, it looks like a trick of the starlight—a jagged column of pale granite, identical to the ruins around it, sliding infinitesimally across the black glass of the plains. But as it crosses over a deep, glowing fissure, the upwelling vermillion light reveals the profound, alien anatomy of the plateau's natives.

They are massive, towering figures, asymmetric and jagged, entirely devoid of biological softness. They look like shattered stalagmites or walking shards of a destroyed temple, composed of that same heavy, pale stone that litters the landscape. They possess no eyes, no discernible faces, and no traditional limbs. Instead, their bodies are conglomerations of sharp, interlocking mineral plates, constantly grating against one another. 

What animates them is the brief, beautiful chemo-synthetic life that clings to the fresh magma. The amber and rust-colored silica-ferns you observed on the glowing vents are not just isolated flora; they are the nervous system, the musculature, and the blood of these entities. 

Deep, glowing veins of this fern-like lattice snake between the heavy granite slabs of their bodies, holding the pale stones together in a shimmering, fractal web. When the entities are flush with extreme heat, the amber web glows blindingly bright, and the creature moves with a startling, staccato swiftness. Because the air is terribly thin, they do not walk with a fluid gait; their movement is piezoelectric. The quartz in their bodies rapidly expands and contracts as the thermal energy courses through their silica nerves. They move in frame-skipping jerks, snapping from one pose to another with a chorus of delicate, glassy *clinks* and deep, sub-aural thrums that vibrate up through the soles of your boots.

They are heat-seekers, bound to a terrifyingly fragile metabolism. To survive, they must hunt the chaotic, unpredictable ruptures of the crust. 

You watch as a distant section of the black plain heaves and turns cherry-red. Instantly, several of the pale entities converge on the new extrusion. They do not flee the world-shattering violence; they hurl themselves into it. As the molten rock presses upward, the creatures wade into the blinding heat. They plunge their pale, fractured appendages directly into the liquid stone, gorging themselves on the raw thermal radiation and the fresh sulfur. The amber lattices holding them together bloom violently, thickening and pulsing as they absorb the energy of the earth’s open wounds.

But they are trapped in a tragic, ceaseless race against the cooling of the world. 

The heat here fades as quickly as it surges. As a lava flow crusts over and turns to black obsidian, the profound cold of the void above begins to win. You watch as one of the entities, caught too far from a new fissure, begins to slow. Its movements, once sharp and electric, become agonizingly sluggish. The amber lattice stitching its body together begins to lose its vibrant fire, shifting to a dull rust, then to a sickly, translucent white. 

In its dying moments, the creature does not panic. It drags itself toward a flat, seemingly stable expanse of black basalt. It locks its limbs into a rigid, tripodal stance, bracing itself against the earth. As the last of the heat flees its body, the silica-ferns linking its plates disintegrate into dry ash. Without the animating lattice, the creature is frozen forever. It becomes solid, unmoving, and deeply pale.

It becomes a ruin.

The realization hits you with the weight of the crushing stars above: they are not bringing the pale granite to build the plinths and arches. They *are* the granite. The solitary monoliths, the rough-hewn tripods, and the broken rings scattered across the savage landscape—these are the calcified remains of their dead. 

And the living do not leave them be. 

As another fissure seals shut and the tectonic storm lulls for a precious minute, two glowing, heat-flushed entities approach the newly frozen corpse of their kin. Moving with brutal, synchronized efficiency, they wedge their sharp appendages beneath a massive chunk of their fallen companion. There is a deep, bone-rattling grind of stone on stone. Trembling with thermal exertion, they lift the corpse. 

You realize they are building. They are deliberately stacking the dead. They arrange the rigid, petrified bodies of their ancestors into heavy rings and towering arches across the tectonic fault lines. It is an act of desperate, lithic defiance. In a world that is constantly unmaking itself, that refuses to be smooth or still, these creatures use the only permanent thing they have—their own corpses—to weigh down the crust. They are trying to pin the thrashing earth in place. They are trying to build anchors in a tempest.

It is a hopelessly futile endeavor. Even as the two entities lock the new piece of stone into the top of a pale archway, the ground beneath them begins to tremor. A new rift unzips, violently bright and terrifyingly silent. The black crust breaks, and the archway they have just sacrificed their kin to complete begins to tilt toward the glowing maw.

The living entities do not mourn, nor do they give up. Re-energized by the blast of terrible heat rising from the new chasm, their amber veins flare bright orange. They turn away from the collapsing arch and snap into sudden, jagged motion, sprinting across the razor-sharp sea of black glass, racing toward the next eruption, driven to survive just long enough to freeze, to die, and to become the next anchor in a world that will not hold still.
