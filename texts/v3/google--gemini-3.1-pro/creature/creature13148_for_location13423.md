To see them, you must first stop looking for people and instead watch the geometry of the vines. Because for a long time, the inhabitants are invisible—perfectly camouflaged against the crimson and grey stalks, entirely subsumed by the amber dust. 

When they finally resolve into focus, it is through sheer kinetic energy. They move with an unsettling, fluid verticality, entirely divorced from the horizontal logic of plains-dwellers. They do not walk; they brachiate, pivot, and flow. Their limbs are proportionally longer than a flat-world human’s, their joints unnaturally supple, allowing hips to splay wide and shoulders to rotate in near-complete circles. They maneuver through the three-dimensional tangle like spiders, their weight in constant, calculated suspension. 

Physically, they are a testament to the brutal efficiency of their environment. They are deeply sinewy, stripped of all subcutaneous fat, looking as though they were carved from the very vines they inhabit. Their underlying skin is a mottled maroon and ashen grey, though it is permanently caked in the thick, fragrant dust of the hollow, turning them into moving statues of terracotta and gold. Because there is no water to spare, they do not sweat. To survive the sweltering heat, they possess networks of enlarged blood vessels close to the surface of their skin, which flush deep blue and violet when they are working hard, radiating thermal heat into the moving air. 

Their extremities are hyper-adapted. The "callouses" on their hands and feet are not merely hardened skin, but thick, ridged pads that offer immense friction against the impossibly smooth stone and the waxed bark of the flora. Their toes are long and sharply prehensile, capable of gripping a liana as firmly as a hand, allowing them to hang inverted for hours while harvesting. Their fingernails have evolved into thick, curved hooks of keratin, stained green and amber from resin and insect blood.

Because the air is a choking soup of dust, moth-scales, and caustic pollen, their faces are largely hidden. Moth-silk veils, woven so tightly they shimmer like spun glass, wrap around their heads and necks. These veils are not just practical; they are the primary canvas for personal identity. Intricate patterns are embroidered into the silk using dyes extracted from crushed beetles—vivid ochres, deep blacks, and startling, iridescent greens. 

Beneath the veils, their faces are striking. Their eyes are enormous, taking up a vast portion of their narrow skulls, with irises of liquid gold and pupils that dilate into wide ovals to capture the dim, particulate twilight. To minimize moisture loss, their mouths are narrow, and they do not breathe through their noses—at least, not in the traditional sense. Their nasal passages are highly convoluted, trapping exiting moisture before it can be exhaled. When they do peel back their veils to eat, they reveal teeth that are sharp, serrated, and interlocking, evolved for the dual purpose of piercing the leathery skins of vines and cracking the tough, chitinous armor of the hollow’s fauna. Their lips and tongues are often stained a ghostly, milky pale by a lifetime of drinking plant sap, intermixed with the yellowish tinge of insect hemolymph. 

In a space this densely packed, this impossibly loud, their culture is a masterpiece of extreme choreography and hyper-empathy. There is absolutely no privacy in the cavern. They live clustered in spherical, suspended nests, sleeping in tangled piles of warm, dry bodies to conserve space and comfort one another. Their society functions like a super-organism. They move through the narrow, swaying catwalks without colliding, anticipating one another’s shifts in weight through the vibrations of the vines. 

Spoken communication is almost entirely useless over the deafening rustle and clatter of the ecosystem, so they have developed a dual language. The first is a complex sign language, executed with blurring speed using their long fingers. The second is structural: they treat the entire settlement as an instrument. By striking, plucking, and dragging bone tools across specific, taut lianas, they send vibrational rhythms throughout the cavern. A low, rolling thrum might signal a hunting party returning with a haul of pale lizards, while a sharp, staccato metallic buzzing—created by bowing a dried beetle carapace—signals a structural failure in the woven bridges. When they do vocalize, it is in high, piercing whistles that cut perfectly through the ambient, low-frequency roar of the insects.

Their lives run on the same dizzying, hyper-drive biological clock as the rest of the hollow. Maturation is incredibly rapid. Children are bound to their parents' chests in silk harnesses until they can climb, which they do by the time they are a year old. They reproduce frequently, a necessity in an environment where a single slip, a severed liana, or a bite from a venomous centipede means instant death. An individual of thirty years is considered an ancient, venerable elder.

They are apex predators, but they are devoid of arrogance. They know they are integrated into a closed loop, and their relationship with death reflects the brutal mathematics of their world. Nothing is wasted. When a member of the settlement dies, there is no mourning period of decay, nor is there flame to spare for cremation. Instead, the deceased is honored through recycling. The body is stripped of its tools and silks, carried to the lowest, darkest reaches of the town, and reverently dropped into the heavy webbing of the lower canopy. There, the massive scavenger swarms and bloated beetle grubs consume the remains in a matter of hours. The grubs will grow fat on the ancestors, and the next day, the harvest teams will tap those same grubs for their watery hemolymph to sustain the living. 

To live among them is to be constantly touched, constantly moving, and constantly consuming. They are a feverish, beautiful people, smelling of sweet sap and raw ammonia, their eyes shining in the amber dark. They are not trapped in the hollow; they are the heart of its frantic machinery, beating in perfect, frenetic time with the wings of the moths and the slow, bloody pulse of the crimson vines.
