To understand the life here, you must let your skin become your eyes. The creatures of this cathedral do not cast shadows; they cast pressure wakes, sonic shadows, and thermal blooms. 

The vast, hollow gulf in the center of the cavern is the domain of the hyper-kinetic. If you stand utterly still, you can feel the frantic, displaced wind of their passing. These are the swarmers—a species of chiropteran-like gliders that exist in a state of perpetual, frenzied calculus. If you were to catch one mid-air—which would be nearly impossible, as they would sense the disruption of your heartbeat from ten yards away—you would find an animal that is entirely wing and ear. 

They possess no eyes, not even vestigial indentations. Their heads are concave, fleshy dishes composed of tightly ribbed cartilage, acting as perfect parabolic receivers. At the center of this dish sits an organ that vibrates with terrifying speed, firing out the strident clicks and trills that tear through the humid air. Their bodies are no larger than a clenched fist, covered in a velvet down that absorbs excess moisture, preventing the heavy condensation from grounding them. Their wings, immense and leathery, are webbed with a network of superficial veins pumped full of hot blood, allowing them to ride the thermal updrafts created by the ancient tech-anchors far below.

They never land. Their skeletal structure has over thousands of millennia lost the density required to support their own weight on solid ground. They mate in mid-air, a brief and violent tangle of clicking ribs and fluttering silk. They give birth on the wing. They even drink in flight, their jaws snapping open to catch the heavy, warm drops plummeting from the ceiling. They feed on the thick cloud of nutrient-dense pollen—a biological snow exhaled by the fleshy, sprawling vines at the cavern’s edges. They are the fast-beating heart of the ecosystem, living frantic lives of high metabolism and constant noise, burning out in a matter of months before tumbling, exhausted and dead, into the thick loam below.

And waiting in that loam, deep within the tangled, pulsating vines, are the architects of the deep, booming bellows. 

You feel them before you hear them. The lowest threshold of their calls vibrates through the worn stone and up through the soles of your feet. These heavy-bodied amphibians are the apex ambush predators of the void. They are immense—some the size of small automobiles—but they are mostly empty space. 

They are effectively giant, fleshy bellows hidden beneath a dermal layer of warty, moisture-trapping skin. Lacking legs, they drag themselves through the hydraulic vines using thick, muscular pseudopods. They possess a terrifying sensory organ: an external network of lateral-line pits tracing down their backs, capable of detecting the finest barometric shifts in the air. They know exactly where a swarmer is based on the air it displaces. 

Their strategy is deeply tied to the ancient machinery half-buried in the rock. The amphibians congregate around the gravity anomalies. Because the machines warp the falling water into slow-motion spirals, pooling the heavy droplets into suspended, floating spheres of water against the ceiling of the anomaly, the swarmers often swoop close to the nodes to drink from these easy, hovering liquid caches. 

The amphibians simply wait beneath them. When a swarmer dips into the gravity well, the giant amphibian unhinges its cavernous mouth. It does not bite; it forcefully expands its massive, pneumatic lungs. The sudden vacuum sucks the heavy air, the floating water, and the screeching swarmer downward into a toothless, crushing gullet. The booming bellow you feel in your ribs is the aftermath—the amphibian venting the excess air back out across specialized vocal folds, a belch of triumph that doubles as a territorial warning, shaking the condensation from the ceiling and keeping the cycle in motion.

Yet, beyond the frantic wings and the booming lungs, there is a third tier of life here, one that operates on the agonizingly slow timeline of the machines themselves. 

If you reach your hands deep into the thickest part of the vegetation, right where the meaty vines crush against the heat-radiating obelisks, your fingers might brush against something that feels at first like the smooth, worn granite of the cavern wall. But it is warm. And, very faintly, it pulses.

These are the Lithic-Graziers. In an ecosystem of extremes, they are the quiet constants. They look like nothing more than massive, heavily armored slugs, their chitinous shells polished to a frictionless sheen by eons of dripping water. They are the only creatures permitted to touch the machines directly. 

They possess a biology that borders on the impossible. They are heavily fortified against the extreme gravitational shearing and thermal radiation of the tech-nodes. In fact, they feed on it. Their mouths are rasps of iron-infused cartilage, and they slowly, methodically chew their way through the massive, hydraulic vines that threaten to smother the ancient artifacts. They are the caretakers of the machines, pruning the aggressive vegetation. 

Without the Graziers, the vines would eventually choke the nodes, suffocating the heat, and the ecosystem would freeze to death. Without the vines, the Graziers would starve, and the swarmers would have no pollen to eat. Without the swarmers, the amphibians would perish. It is a perfect, unbroken loop.

But the Graziers live in a state of near-statis. Their metabolism is so achingly slow that to them, the frantic, echolocating swarmers above are just a continuous, invisible vibration—a static hum in the background of reality. A Grazier might take three human lifetimes to move around the circumference of a single gravity-node. They live for thousands of years, aging and weathering exactly like the rock beneath them. When they finally die, they do not decay; their heavily mineralized bodies simply calcify, becoming new, smooth stone at the base of the machines.

This is the life of the absolute dark. A deafening, frantic sky; a heavy, waiting periphery; and an impossibly ancient, painfully slow beneath. They do not know of stars, or the freezing alpine wind just beyond their stone sky, or the concept of light. They only know the heat, the wet, the song of the acoustic void, and the deep, steady pulse of the sleeping god that keeps them warm.
