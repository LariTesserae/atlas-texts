To exist in a place that is actively dissolving, you must be undeniably, oppressively heavy. 

The denizens of this fractured metropolis are born into weight. They do not have a name for themselves, for naming requires a linear continuity of history, and here, memory is as sheared and unrendered as the atmosphere. They are simply the Grafters. They are the biological counterweight to the void.

At first glance, it is difficult to distinguish a Grafter from the machinery they tend. They lumber through the bruised twilight enveloped in gargantuan, pressurized diving suits designed not for an ocean of water, but for an ocean of failing physics. Their armor is forged from thick sheets of raw cast iron, vulcanized rubber, and brass, heavily riveted and insulated with layers of compacted fungal matter. Their boots are weighted with dense, radioactive slag to ensure the mountain's capricious gravity never mistakes them for a passing cloud or a suspended droplet of water. 

Because the landscape is drenched and vibrantly overgrown, so too are the Grafters. The humid vitality of the localized jungles does not stop at the edges of their suits. Spores root perfectly into the porous rust of their armor. A Grafter who has been stationed on a cooling tower for a few months becomes a walking terrarium. Mantles of thick, velvet moss drape over their broad shoulders. Broad, cyan-glowing ferns sprout from their exhaust valves, trailing behind them like bioluminescent plumage. In the deep gloom of the swinging catwalks, you can track their movements by the shifting pinpricks of glowing vegetation growing upon their backs. The giant, luminescent hound-moths often mistake the workers for moving gardens, landing softly on their iron helmets to rest, their wings folding over the Grafters’ visors like pale, trembling hands.

Beneath the iron and the moss, their biology has been profoundly rewritten by the extreme elevation and the smothering damp. They are massive, slow-blooded creatures. Their skin is heavily ridged, slate-grey, and porous, resembling the newborn basalt that pushes from the mountain’s core. They do not have lungs as a lowlander would understand them; instead, their chests house dense, fibrous gills adapted to breathe the liquid air. They inhale the suspended mist and weep the excess out through joints in their suits. 

Their life cycle is intertwined with the bizarre micro-climates of the suspension-cables. Because reality fractures the deepest at the edges of the factory-spires, the Grafters do not build nurseries. Instead, they utilize the trembling, levitating spheres of condensation that defy gravity to pool in mid-air. Inside these floating, jellyfish-like bubbles of water, the Grafters deposit heavy, metallic eggs. If you walk along the swaying tramways and look out into the abyss, you will see hundreds of these suspended water-globes glowing from within with a soft, cyan light, each containing a dark, curled, tadpole-like infant slowly gestating in the amniotic damp, safe from the gravity that breaks below them.

But the most striking thing about the Grafters is not their rusted shells, nor their mossy plumage, but their relationship with time and space. 

Operating in a metropolis where the physical laws are casual and prone to clipping requires a psychology of absolute, terrifying stoicism. A Grafter cannot afford the luxury of panic or sudden movement. Because the world occurs out of sync, their labor is a staggering choreography of prophetic muscle memory. 

You might observe a Grafter on a jutting spire, holding a white-hot rivet in the empty air with heavy tongs. They will stand completely motionless for six minutes. Then, a massive steel beam will clip into existence exactly where the rivet is hovering. Three minutes later, the arm of a coworker—just an arm, severed by a spatial tear from a body standing yards away—will manifest in the space beside them, wielding a pneumatic hammer. The hammer strikes the rivet in total silence. Ten minutes later, long after the work is done and both Grafters have lumbered off to the next pylon, the deafening *crack* of the impact will finally shatter the air. 

They work across staggering timelines, communicating in a slow, physical sign language that anticipates delays. They know instinctively where a walkway will be when a glitch heals. If a Grafter is walking across a courtyard and steps into a "dead pixel"—one of those terrifying, unrendered boxes of grey nothingness—they simply cease to exist for a moment. They do not thrash or scream. They hang in the void, a paused schematic of a person. When the mist rushes back and the geometry re-renders, the Grafter finishes the exact step they began, their boot hitting the wet stone, their mind unbothered by the minute or hour they just spent outside of reality. 

They do not dream, and they do not create art. Art is an abstraction, and abstraction is a luxury for places that are solidly tethered. Their sole, driving philosophy is *maintenance*. They are fully aware that the city is a stapler trying to bind a shredded sky. They revere the sluggish, tectonic industry not as a god, but as a bodily organ. 

When a massive, submarine-sized biological dreadnought drifts too close to a churning rotor, a squad of Grafters will slowly, methodically step off the edge of a cliff. They fall in agonizing slow-motion through the gelatinous atmosphere, their slag-heavy boots dragging them toward the beast. Landing on its ribbed hide, they use long, rusted shepherd’s crooks to gently prod the colossal grazer away from the machinery, guiding it back into the safe, empty voids, before attaching their pneumatic winch-lines to a cable and allowing themselves to be hauled, over the course of hours, back up into the brutalist canopy. 

There is a strange, tragic peace to their existence. They are prisoners of the glitch, but they are also its masters. When a Grafter grows too old, when the dampness finally rots the inner gears of their suit and their heavy bones succumb to the tectonic groan of the world, they do not die in a traditional sense. They simply stop moving. 

They find an empty ledge on the razor-sharp basalt, sit facing the bruised, permanent twilight, and power down their internal heat. Within hours, the velvet moss overtakes them. Within weeks, the cyan ferns bloom wildly from their eye-holes and exhaust pipes. They become structural. Their iron suits weld to the rock through rust and time, their mass adding one more heavy, immovable anchor to the breaking mountain. They become part of the very stonework that the next generation of slow, rusted giants will bolt their metal to—an eternal, stubborn weight holding down a world that desperately wants to float away.
