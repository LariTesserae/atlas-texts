Awareness does not arrive all at once in a place like this. It accretes. It gathers, like the weeping condensation along the curved, dizzying walls. 

At first, I am only the sensation of sound. 

I am a deep, oceanic resonance—a terrifying, rhythmic shudder that ripples through a membrane of wood and pitch. *Thrummmmm.* The violence of the floor below is distant, yet it travels intimately through the tightly woven timbers of the sky-curving dome. I feel the percussion vibrating in the marrow of things, long before I know I have a body to feel it with.

Slowly, out of the cellar-dark and the eternal, hanging mist, my edges begin to define themselves. I find my shape in the negative space of the architecture. My belly is pressed against a colossal groove where one petrified oak beam crosses beneath another. I am anchored. Splayed outward in a wide, asymmetrical star, I possess limbs. Eight of them. They are heavy, cold, and immensely thick, coated in the same matte-black resin as the cathedral I cling to. I am indistinguishable from the wall. I am woven into the weave.

I find myself halfway up the curving expanse of the chamber, a Caretaker awakened from a long, static slumber. 

I do not possess eyes, but my sensory pits are bathed in a staggering wealth of data. Time, for me, is not the frantic, snapping violence of the flooded basin below. My consciousness operates on a geological scale, stretched thin and long. Because I experience the world at such an agonizingly slow rate, the frenetic ecology of the floor smears into something beautiful. I do not hear the amphibians' rhythmic slamming as singular, earth-shattering *BOOMS*. To my drawn-out perception, the impacts blur closely together, forming a continuous, droning hum—a low, soothing lullaby that continuously massages my heavy carapace. 

And the ribbon-creatures, those tragic, fluttering things that ride the shockwaves and flash in the dark? Because they hunt in their hundreds, living out their brief, gorging lives in mere fractions of my time, I do not see individual flyers. I see only a steady, undulating ocean of spectral blue-green light, a glowing lake of bioluminescence suspended in the lower fog. To my slow mind, the bottom of the world is a peaceful, humming aurora.

I do not concern myself with the floor. The wall is my universe.

A sharp, metallic tang cuts through the heavy moisture of the air. It is the scent of deep bog-water, rich entirely in iron, tannins, and dissolved peat. My thorax hums with instinct. The outer hydrostatic pressure of the marsh has stressed a seam in the ancient woodwork. A micro-fracture is weeping. If left unattended, the pressurized water will widen the crack, bringing the crushing weight of the bog down upon the hollow sphere.

I prepare to move. 

The lifting of my right foreleg is an epic undertaking. It is a slow-motion un-suctioning of tarsal claws from the petrified clay, a quiet wrenching of multi-jointed hinges. It takes hours to raise the limb a few inches into the damp mist; it takes days to shift my vast, elongated weight upward, guiding my limb to the next grooved foothold in the over-and-under pattern. I move like a shadow sliding across a sundial. 

As I inch through the eternal twilight, my body feels heavy. Centuries of drinking the mineral-rich runoff have thickened my shell. Before I can heal the wall, I must heal myself. I pause my ascent. Deep within, a hydraulic shift occurs. Slowly, my carapace splits along the dorsal ridge. Over the course of a long, humid season, I step forward out of my own skin, leaving the heavy, mineralized shell clinging to the steep curve of the wood. The empty husk weeps with excess calcium, its fluids dripping down to form another pale, jagged stalactite in the dark, a monument to my passing.

Newly soft, pliable, and glistening in the dim, slate-gray light filtering from far above, I finally drag my mouthparts over the weeping fissure. From the glands beneath my thorax, I extrude the warm, thick biological mortar. I pack the waterproof, calcified resin deep into the crack, smoothing it perfectly against the petrified wood. The scent of raw bog-water slowly fades. The seal holds. The integrity of the void is maintained. 

Normally, the instinct would be to settle back into the weave, to become invisible once more and wait ten, fifty, or a hundred years for the next fracture. 

But I am drawn upward. 

Perhaps it is the draft—the faint, alien pull of windless, open air escaping through the jagged oculus at the apex of the dome. Or perhaps my new, soft shell is more sensitive to the temperature changes of the twilight. Over decades, I pull my colossal, tree-trunk length higher and higher up the vertical curve. The drumming hum from below grows fainter. The eternal mist thins. 

Eventually, the petrified ceiling ends, abruptly breaking off into jagged splinters. I drag my front limbs over the lip and pull myself to the very edge of the oculus.

There is a world above. I cannot see it, but I taste a terrible, endless emptiness—an expanse with no walls to patch, no acoustic pressure, no limits. It is freezing, indifferent, and impossibly dry. It terrifies me. 

But dangling right in front of my sensory bristles, hanging completely still in the void, is a ghost-white root. 

It has breached my chamber, reaching down blindly from the unseen marsh above. It does not smell like pitch, or mud, or calcite. It smells like living, thirsty cellulose. As another massive, vibrating wave rises from the unseen floor below, the root trembles, kissing the side of my face.

We are both blind. We belong to two entirely different time scales, two different ecologies, separated by millennia and a barrier of petrified ancient labor. But we are both reaching.

I do not retreat. I lift my heavy, multi-jointed forelegs and gently encircle the trembling phantom root. I do not snap it. I do not eat it. I pull it firmly against the splintered edge of the broken timber weave. 

My glands begin to swell. The forgotten hands that bent this great cathedral are gone, but their compulsion—to form, to seal, to build—echos in my blood. Slowly, I extrude a line of wet, biological mortar, tacking the living root to the ancient, dead wood. I pull another dangling root closer, and then another, crossing them over and under in a relentless rhythm.

For the first time in ten thousand years, I am not merely patching a leak. Finding myself between the chaotic violence of the deep and the silent indifference of the sky, I become the architect. I will take all the time I need. I am weaving a new ceiling out of the encroaching world.
