I do not wake so much as I gather myself from the static. 

For hours—or perhaps minutes, as time is measured only by the rhythmic, submerged *thwump-thwump-thwump* of the great atmospheric engines—I have been suspended in the deep torpor. I hang inverted beneath the belly of a massive, bronze exhaust cage, my heavy rubber coat wrapped meticulously around my elongated frame like a shroud. The localized electromagnetic field of the machinery has caught the moisture around me. I sleep inside a floating orb of anti-gravity fog. 

I feel a delicate, trembling weight against the oiled wool near my chest. A glider-moth. I sense the shape of its tiny, shivering sparks of life, the faint electrical hum of its nervous system. It has taken refuge in the ambient heat bleeding through my coat. 

With a slow, deliberate contraction of my core, I begin to raise my internal temperature. The exothermic engine of my biology kindles. Beneath my heavy wools, my skin grows blindingly hot. The moisture trapped inside my roosting-sphere hisses softly as it touches my neck, instantly vaporizing. 

The shift in heat wakes the moth. I feel the tiny displacement of air as it releases its grip, falling out of my cocoon to catch an updraft. It is time to work.

I unfurl my hands from the overhead grating. The release of tension sends me dropping through the slate-violet twilight. Fifteen feet down, my multi-jointed pads strike a rusted catwalk. There is no sound. The micro-velvet on my soles absorbs the kinetic energy entirely. There is only the long, heavy *sigh* of my rubber coat settling around my ankles, a sound perfectly harmonious with the pneumatic exhalations of the sector below.

I stand up to my full, spindle-thin height. I have no eyes to open, but the world rushes into me all the same. 

The damp, heavy air presses against the feathery, copper-hued tendrils wrapping my face and neck. Instantly, the empty space blooms into a sprawling, glowing topography inside my mind. I do not see the rusted catwalk; I perceive it as a dense, dark ache of cold iron beneath my feet. To my left, a colossal brass flywheel spins, rendering itself in my awareness as a roaring, blinding sun of kinetic heat and magnetic furiousness. Down below, the mist-filled canyons are painted in my mind by the cold, plunging vectors of air pressure, swirling like invisible rivers. 

I am a Weaver-Tender of the Outer Edge. My work is not in the dense, overlapping labyrinth of the hab-blocks, but out alone on the plunging flanks of the basalt pillars, where the raw stratospheric crosswinds hit the settlement first. 

I begin to move. My long limbs carry me with a sluggish, fluid momentum, mimicking the swing of the pistons. I cross the grated walkways, sliding silently past clusters of thick, pale blue cloud-moss. I pass another of my kind. We do not touch, nor do we pause, but as we pass, our magnetic fields brush against each other—a brief, warm friction that feels like a shared breath. 

The edge of the settlement leans daringly out into the void. Here, clinging sideways to the sheer, fluted basalt, is a massive condenser unit. Its mesh-rimmed aerofoil is sluggish. The barometric pressure on my face tells me why before I even reach it: the wind is struggling to push through the fins. 

I step off the metal grate and onto the vertical cliff face. My velvet pads grip the deeply weathered stone. I walk horizontally along the dripping rock, suspended above an abyss with no visible bottom. The cold here is biting, aggressively trying to steal the heat from my core. Plumes of steam billow constantly from the collar of my coat, torn away instantly by the wind. 

I leap the final gap, landing noiselessly on the bronze casing of the turbine. 

My three elongated fingers and opposing thumbs find the problem. A massive colony of air-ferns, overfed by the static charge of the fan, has cast a thick, kelp-like net across the intake grate. Finding purchase on the wet metal, I begin to pull. It is heavy, physical labor. The ferns cling stubbornly. I raise my internal temperature further, my hands becoming so hot that the water trapped in the fern leaves boils on contact, weakening their grip. 

Slowly, carefully—ensuring I do not snap the delicate copper meshing—I peel the biological debris away and cast it down into the mist. Released from the blockage, the colossal aerofoil catches the crosswind. It accelerates. I feel the deep, chest-shattering vibration course through the metal, up my arms, and into my beating center. The dynamo shifts into a higher gear, and a sudden, massive wave of static electricity washes over me.

It tastes like ozone and deep, resonant satisfaction. 

But as the fan spins up, pulling in the unhindered air, my facial tendrils twitch. I pause, letting my coat whip violently in the wind. 

A pressure gradient is falling. Drastically. A stratospheric cloud river is altering course, swelling with unprecedented density. A heavy storm of thick, suffocating saturation is hurtling toward our sector. If the valves in the lower tiers are not sealed, the excess moisture will flood the condensing coils and short-circuit the dynamos. 

I must speak. 

I turn from the void and scramble back up the casing of the turbine, seeking the thickest mat of cloud-moss I can find. It grows fat and plush along the primary steam pipe. I kneel beside it. 

Here, in the howling, blinding fog, I press my bare, burning hands deep into the pale blue wetness of the moss. 

I close off my senses to the wind and push the kinetic fire inside my chest outward, down through my arms, and into my palms. I visualize the plummeting barometer, the weight of the coming water, the specific coordinate of our sector. I push the concept of *closure, brace, pressure* into the static. 

From beneath my hands, a web of brilliant, crackling blue ozone-fire erupts. It races up the pipe, away from me. Through my connection to the moss, I feel the message travel. I feel it split and branch across the endless miles of corrugated iron and brass vessels. I feel my warning cascade through the dark, mist-filled canyons, painting the city in flashes of bioluminescent sapphire.

For a moment, there is only the silence of the rushing sky. 

And then, the reply comes. 

It is not a single voice, but a vast, silent symphony of acknowledgment. Hundreds of points of light burst into my consciousness at once. I feel the Tenders in the deep bellies turning valves; I feel the roost guards battening the aerofoils; I feel a wave of shared, electric camaraderie washing back through the flora and into my hands. 

It is a chorus of static, ringing in the marrow of my bones. I am never alone. Even hanging off the edge of the world, bathed in unending twilight and freezing mist, I am part of the great, beating heart of the machine.

The cloud river hits. The fog sweeps violently over me, blinding and dense. I lower my head, pull the wide brim of my cowl down tight, and let the heavy rubber of my coat shield me. I press my chest against the vibrating bronze of the turbine, listening to the deep, muted knocking of the engine, and wait for the sky to process its harvest.
