The first thing that emerges from the dark is the grip. 

There is no concept of waking up unattached. To release the fingers in sleep is to invite the Drop, and so even in the deepest, dream-heavy slumber, my knuckles are locked. Six joints per digit, six digits per limb, twenty-four points of absolute, unyielding tension wrapped around a slick, resin-coated spar of ironwood. 

I open my eyes. The golden grid of the sun-shaft fractures through the hazy dust, snapping my amber pupils to pinpricks. The air is already hot, smelling of ammonia from the grey birds above and the sharp, coppery tang of crushed beetles from below. A bead of condensed water drops from the great terra-cotta ring high overhead, falling for twenty seconds before entirely splashing across my snout. I lick it away. It tastes of clay, shed feathers, and sweat.

I am not a hunter. I am no longer swift enough to hurl myself into the empty air, tethered only by my waist, to snatch a purple beetle from the swarm. Nor am I a gatherer, lowering gourds to the weeping pipes. 

I am a Listener. A keeper of the weave. 

I hang inverted, my spine entirely relaxed, letting gravity pull my auburn fur toward the Abyss. My left fore-limb is anchored to the wood; my right is pressed flat against the colossal, steam-bent timber that forms this sector's rib. With my ghost-fingers—the extra, hyper-articulated digits on the outer edges of my paws—I stroke the wood, feeling for the pulse of the cylinder.

To inhabit this body is to be an instrument of vibration. The air itself is thick enough to chew, but it is the solid matter that speaks to me. 

*Thwump.* 

A hunter has landed on an ancestor-membrane four levels up. The vibration travels through the braided sinew, into the ironwood rib, and into my palm. It is a healthy sound. Taut, elastic, secure. 

*Clack-clatter-clack.* 

A flock of heavy-beaked birds scrambles up the wickerwork to my left. Their vestigial thumb-claws scrape the clay. I blink away the dusty down they kick into the humid air, unbothered. They are necessary. They eat the pale centipedes that would otherwise inject their necrotizing venom into our young. 

But then, beneath the shrieks and the percussive drumming of the metropolis, I feel a dissonance. 

It is micro-tremor, barely a sigh in the architecture, but to my padded fingertips, it screams. A groan of yielding fiber. A slackening.

My phantom-weaving—the unconscious twitching of my hands in empty air—ceases immediately. My muscles bunch, turning instantly from soft, hanging mass to whipcord tension. In three liquid motions, I uncoil from my sleeping spar and launch myself outward. 

I do not look at where I am going; I look at where I will be. I catch a bundle of bundled rattan, swing my momentum in a pendulum arc, release, and plummet through twenty feet of empty, misty air. The Abyss howls below me—a terrifying, throat-less dark. Fear is a cold spike in my belly, but it is a fear honed into absolute precision. 

My hind feet slam into a jutting woven hammock. *Thwump.* I ricochet sideways, catching a vertical hawser woven from auburn fur. Hand over hand, a blur of twenty-four fingers, I scramble downward into the shadowed underbelly of a terra-cotta ring, where the indoor rain spits and pools. 

Here, the rot is strong. The dampness gathers pale, stringy fungi in the crevices. I follow the discordant vibration, pressing my ear to the struts. 

I find it. 

It is a primary tension line, anchoring one of the great leather floor-webs to a main wooden rib. It is thick as my thigh, made of braided, cured sinew. And it is fraying. 

Water has wicked into a micro-fissure in the ironwood above, feeding a patch of black rot. The rot has eaten into the knot of the sinew. Three of the great braided strands have snapped. The line is slowly unraveling. If it gives, a quarter of the floor-web will collapse, plunging dozens of nursing mothers and pups into the Drop. 

I inch out along the trembling line. Below me, the Abyss yields nothing but the faint, iridescent violet flashes of the fist-sized beetles, churning in the deep waste. 

I wrap my legs around the fraying line, securing myself entirely with my lower twelve digits. My hands are free. I run my fingers over the snapped strands. 

The texture of the cured hide is familiar. Taught, slick, carrying the faint, chemical bite of beetle acid used in the tanning process. I close my eyes and feel the ridges of the twisted muscle. 

I know this sinew. 

It is my sire. 

He took the Drop into the Tanner's nets three molt-seasons ago, his lungs filled with the damp-rot. I remember the Tanners chewing his muscles flat, pulling them taut over the steam vents. Now, he holds the floor. He is the architecture. And he is failing. 

"I have you," I click, my throat producing a chattering, percussive vibration that echoes into the damp wood. 

From the woven pouch strapped to my chest, I draw my tools. A heavy-eyed needle carved from the hollow leg-bone of a slate-grey bird. A spool of fresh, fine-spun fur. I reach down and unhook the scavenged flint shears bound to my waist. 

The work is frenetic, desperate. Time is measured in the agonizing creaks of the remaining strands. I clamp my powerful jaws onto the slack end of my sire's broken sinew and pull back with the entire weight of my torso, pulling the tension taut. My neck muscles scream. 

With my primary fingers, I align the broken edges. With my ghost-fingers, I thread the bone needle. Six joints bending in impossible, fluid geometries. Down, up, through, pull. Down, up, through, pull. 

I stitch my sire back together. 

I spit a wad of chewed fungal paste into the splice, using my thumbs to mash the living mycelium into the dead tissue and the rotting wood, forcing it to bind, to bridge the gap. I wrap the fresh fur-cord around the repair, pulling it so tight it cuts into my own calloused pads, drawing a bead of bright red blood. I press my bleeding pad into the knot. Our blood, separated by three molt-seasons and a chemical curing process, mingles in the weave. 

I release my jaws. I ease my weight off the line. 

I press my ear to it. 

The dull, horrific groan is gone. The line hums—a high, singing tension that resonates perfectly with the great ironwood rib. The drumbeat of the cylinder is restored. 

I hang limp beneath the terra-cotta ring, my chest heaving, the perpetual rain spattering against my slick fur. I look out into the cathedral of the interior. 

Above, a cloud of slate-grey birds dives screaming through the golden sun-shaft, intercepting a rising swarm of purple beetles. The heavy *CRACK* of chitin echoing against wood reaches me as hunters feast. The air is deafening, chaotic, oppressive, and perfect. 

I lift my hands. My fingers ache from the tension, yet they are already moving on their own. Thumb, index, hook, anchor, ghost. They undulating in the humid air, phantom-weaving, continually practicing for the next tear, the next fray. 

I am a prisoner of this suspended world, yet I am entirely of it. I know that one day, my amber eyes will cloud. My hyper-articulated joints will stiffen with rot and age. I will miss a grip, or I will politely crawl down to the heavy rains of the lower rings and surrender my breath to the Tanners. 

When that day comes, I will not fear the dark below. I will surrender my fur for the hawsers and my skin for the great webs. I will become the gravity, the tension, and the tactile reality of the weave. 

Until then, I grip, and I listen.
