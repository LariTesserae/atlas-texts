I do not wake up, because in this world, sleep is not a retreat from the senses; it is merely a shift in resonance. 

I come to full awareness suspended halfway up the flank of a rusted iron monolith, miles from the basin floor. My wide, splayed hands—ridged with thousands of micro-fissures—are locked effortlessly onto the weeping, oxidized metal. I have spent the dark hours here, anchored like a barnacle. 

The first sensation of morning is not light, but a shift in the density of the air. The hazy, unchanging sky begins to warm, and the atmospheric steam thickens. The external, spongy gills draped over my broad shoulders flare and undulate, drawing in the scalding moisture. I take a "breath," and my dense, fluid-filled chest expands. There is no hollow rattle of air in lungs, no fragility of breath. There is only the slow, hydraulic cycling of vapor into oxygen, a heavy, rhythmic pulse that drives my thick blood.

And then, there is the texture of the world. 

If a human were placed in my body, they would think they were being crushed to death. The noise—the roaring, grinding frequency of the atmospheric condensers—is a physical wall. But to me, this crushing force is the architecture of my existence. I do not hear the roar; I inhabit it. 

I release my grip with my left hand and lean backward. I do not fall. The acoustic pressure billowing from the great turning turbine above is so dense, so violent, that I can literally recline against it. The invisible waves of sound hold my heavy, cartilaginous frame like a hammock. 

I am a Tender. I am composed of thick, incompressible waters and heavy cartilage. I wear a thick, living mantle of rust-colored thermal algae across my back and shoulders, which is currently drinking in the heavy morning condensation, cooling the skin beneath it.

My task today is written in the tremors of the metal. 

Through the pads of my feet, pressed flat against the iron, I can feel a dissonance. Miles above me, the great turbine is rotating at the speed of a creeping shadow, coaxing water from the sky. But there is a stutter in its rhythm—a micro-fracture somewhere in the casing, leaking a discordant vibration into the monolith’s superstructure. If left alone for a century, that vibration would propagate, and the iron would shatter. 

I begin my descent. I do not climb down so much as I slide, a deliberate, agonizingly slow flow of muscle and mass, moving with the patient grace of geology. Fighting the sonic pressure would tear my muscles, so I move *with* it, riding the down-beats of the deep bass grinding. 

Hours pass. Time here is not measured in minutes, but in tasks. 

I reach the smooth, pale sandstone of the basin floor. It is slick with the perpetual, creeping sheen of weeping water. The horizon stretches out in massive, undulating arcs of stone and scattered, titanic machines. 

To my left, across a shallow, glassy valley, I spot another disruption in the weeping moisture. It is another Tender, draped in streaks of iridescent green slime. We are hundreds of yards apart, but through the wet stone, their footfalls register in my kneecaps—a heavy, dragging, three-count rhythm. *Identification.* It is a Tender I have known for three hundred of my years. 

We alter our trajectories, wading through the warm, shallow pools that gather in the basin’s depressions, until we meet.

We do not wave. We brace our squat, dense bodies against the solid wall of sound pressing against our backs, and we lean into one another.

I press the crown of my pale, hairless forehead against theirs. I raise my massive hands and interlace my fingers with theirs. Our palms lock. In the roaring, weeping eternity of the basin, we create a quiet, closed circuit.

I flex a dense cluster of muscle deep in my chest, striking my breastbone. The vibration travels up my spine, through my skull, and directly into theirs. 

*Thrum-pulse... thrum-thrum... slide.*

It is not language. It is raw, unmediated experience. In that single, drawn-out pulse, I pour out the sensation of my morning. I give them the exact temperature of the iron I slept on; I give them the dissonant, shivering fracture in the condenser above; I give them the feeling of the heavy moisture sliding through my shoulder-gills. 

They answer. Their muscles flex, sending a deep, resonant wave through our interlaced fingers. 

They share the sensation of extracting thick, sludge-like lubricants from a sinkhole. They share the deep, contented fatigue in their lower spine. They share a profound, static joy—the quiet ecstasy of the eternal rhythm remaining unbroken. 

For ten minutes, we stand locked together, breathing the steam, two statues conversing in earthquakes beneath the screaming sky. We are sharing our lives. I hold their exhaustion; they hold my purpose. 

When we part, we are recalibrated. We know we are not alone. 

I leave them and make my way toward the deep, flooded culverts carved into the base of the nearest monolith. The ambient light dims as the smooth stone curves downward into a bowl-like depression. The water here is deep, warm, and highly acidic, overflowing from the condenser's catch-basins.

I wade in until the water reaches my jaw. The thermal algae on my back blooms in the heat. 

Deep in the glassy pool, a shape shifts. It is one of the scavengers, a gelatinous beast the size of a transport truck, its semi-translucent body glowing faintly in the dimness. It has no eyes, no face, only a vast, rippling mantle of fluid-filled flesh. 

It ignores my approach, continuing to graze blindly on a toxic mat of buildup near a drainage pipe. I wade closer, respectfully, smoothly, creating no sudden wakes. I reach out and lay both of my wide hands flat against its quivering, jelly-like flank.

The creature’s internal workings are a symphony of sub-bass vibrations. I can feel the slow, monumental grind of its digestion, the oceanic shifting of its fluids. I stroke its side in a specific, rhythmic pattern—a physical lullaby, mimicking the deep frequency of the earth beneath the machines. 

In response to the soothing friction, the beast’s skin tightens, and it secretes a thick, pearlescent glob of heat-resistant mucus from a specialized pore. I gather the heavy, sticky substance in my hands, shaping it into a dense ball. 

I press my forehead briefly against the beast’s soft flank—a silent gesture of gratitude, though I know it only feels the pressure—and wade back out into the hazy daylight. 

I turn back toward the vast, rusted monolith. I will spend the next three days climbing back up, fighting the sonic wall, until I find the micro-fracture. I will pack this pearlescent mucus into the crack, sealing it, smoothing the vibration of the great machine back into perfect harmony. 

This is my life. It is slow. It is hot. It is deafeningly quiet in my mind, yet louder than a dying star against my skin. 

I do not long for green forests or crisp breezes; those things are fragile, fleeting illusions of a thinner world. I belong to the weight. I belong to the friction. 

I know that one day, perhaps two centuries from now, the fluid in my chest will begin to thicken. My heavy joints will stiffen. On that day, I will not climb the iron. I will seek out a glassy pool, much like the one I just left. I will lie back in the acidic, weeping water, and I will let the rust-algae consume me. I will feel the vibrations of my world slowly dissolve my cartilaginous bones, breaking me down until I am pulled up into the very sky, only to be rained back down by the machines I spent an eternity tending.

Until then, I press my wide hands to the wet metal. I lean into the roar. I begin to climb.
