I would not be an invader here. To enter this basin as a creature of dry air and rigid lungs would be an immediate, gasping agony. Instead, I would let the basin remake me. I would slide into the heavy, wet envelope of this world and belong to it. 

I am one of the pale, hanging giants. 

Awareness begins not with light, but with vibration. I wake inside a weeping, teardrop hammock of heavy silk and crushed crab-shell mortar. It smells profoundly of home: sulfur, wet rust, and the metallic tang of my own shed skin. 

I do not open my eyes—I have none. Instead, I unfurl the crown of my skull. 

The fleshy, fern-like vibrissae on my head flare outward like an anemone blooming in a dark current. Instantly, the world rushes in, painted in a three-dimensional map of temperature, moisture, and motion. Through the heavy, suffocating air, I "see" the immense geometry of the basin. The great cooling chimneys are towering pillars of radiant crimson heat, bleeding outward into the muted, tepid orange of the ambient fog. Below me, the shallow evaporation pools are deep, cool voids of indigo and violet, rippling with the microscopic, chaotic static of millions of tiny, living bodies.

I stretch. My limbs are impossibly long, the joints popping in a dull, muted sequence. I feel the fifty-degree heat pressing against me, but it is not a burden. My skin—heavy, pale, and hanging in thick, translucent curtains—catches the faint thermal draughts. Blood, heavy with localized copper and iron, pumps sluggishly through the capillaries just beneath the surface, dumping my internal heat into the wet air. I am a creature of exquisite thermodynamics, perfectly calibrated to never boil.

I pull myself to the lip of my hammock. My hands are massive, the calloused, spongy pads at my fingertips instinctively finding the microscopic pores in the slick, wet concrete of the pylon. I swing out over the void. The drop beneath me is two hundred meters. I feel no vertigo; gravity is just another pressure, a familiar pull stretching the corded muscles of my shoulders.

I flow downward. 

My descent is a languid, liquid drag over rusted rebar and weeping stone. Every movement is deliberate, pushing through an atmosphere so saturated it feels like moving through warm oil. As I descend, a giant crane fly, its body the length of my forearm, drifts into my thermal aura. I do not snap at it. I simply extend a multi-jointed finger, letting my thick keratin spade hook its fragile wing, and draw it toward my face. The recessed, muscular hood of my mouth opens, and I consume it in one soft crunch. A fleeting burst of bitter protein and warm fluid over my tongue. A snack on the morning commute.

But I am not hunting today. I am listening.

There is a rhythm to our world, a heartbeat dictated by the buried machinery, and something is wrong. I felt it during my sleep-cycle—a subsonic stutter deep in the foundation. *Thrum... thrum... gasp... thrum.* 

An arrhythmia in the iron gods. 

I reach the lower levels, where the catwalks hover just above the evaporation pans. Here, the heat is at its most brutal, trapped by the massive concrete bellies of the pumping blocks. The isopods are thick here, a rippling, knee-deep carpet of chitin and antennae. As I drop onto the grated gangway, they part in a fluid wave, fleeing the heavy, wet heat of my footfalls.

I crouch, pressing the flat of my palm against the vibrating steel of the gangway. I close my vibrissae against the overwhelming noise of the insect static and focus entirely on the tactile feedback rushing up my arm.

*There.* A hundred meters to the east. A secondary sluice gate.

I move sideways along the gangway, hanging beneath it entirely by my fingers and toes to bypass a dense cluster of sleeping cousins who dangle like pale bats in the mist. I arrive at the gate. It is an immense, circular aperture of pitted steel, meant to exhale hundreds of gallons of mineral effluent into the pans. But it is weeping thin, anemic trickles. 

Through my sensory frills, I can "see" the problem. The heat signature inside the thick outfall pipe is blinding—a backlog of boiling, pressurized water building to a catastrophic rupture—but the mouth of the pipe is choked by a massive, irregular cold spot. 

A corpse. 

One of the giant basin centipedes, nearly three meters long, must have crawled into the pipe looking for extreme warmth to molt, and died midway. Its heavily calcified shell is now acting as a dam. Dissolved limestone has quickly bonded with its carapace, turning the corpse into a concrete plug. 

I do not know the words *pressure*, *rupture*, or *hydrodynamics*. I only know that the heat must flow, or the bacterial mats will starve, the insects will die, and the great silence will follow.

I brace my long legs against the rusted lip of the adjacent outfall. I lean over the void, reaching both of my heavy, spade-tipped hands into the mouth of the choked pipe. The water trickling out is near boiling, but my thick, spongy callouses can endure it. I dig my keratin ridges into the seam between the steel pipe and the calcified centipede shell. 

I pull. 

The muscles in my back, layered like shifting tectonic plates beneath my draped skin, contract. For a long moment, nothing happens. The air around me is profoundly dead and muffled. Then, there is a wet, grinding tear. 

I leverage my knees against the concrete, throwing my entire, massive body weight backward over the abyss. 

The plug gives way. I tumble backward onto the broad platform as a pressurized cannon-blast of scalding, rust-red water explodes from the pipe. It roars out over the evaporation pans, a glorious, heavy waterfall of heat and life. The air instantly fills with thick, blinding steam.

I lie on my back on the wet steel, letting the mist wash over me. It is blistering, but to me, it feels like an embrace. The deep, heavy rhythm of the basin corrects itself. *Thrum... thrum... thrum.* The iron god breathes easy once more. 

I sit up. My thick skin is flushed a deep, bruised purple from the exertion. I look around. The sound of the rushing water is deafening to me, a chaotic roar in a world of whispers. But below, in the pans, the isopods are already swarming toward the fresh heat. The balance holds.

I must tell the others. 

I stand, walking to a massive, hollow cooling chimney that rises beside the platform. The steel is resonant, eaten thin by acidic condensation. I press my chest against it, feeling its latent vibration, and then, I bring the heavy, meaty butt of my palm against the metal.

*BOOM.*

The impact swallows all other sound. The heavy air tries to muffle it, but the sheer density of the strike pushes a shockwave of low-frequency sound through the fog. I strike it again, falling into the beat of the deep machinery.

*BOOM... boom-boom... BOOM.*

I wait, my fleshy frills trembling, my hands resting flat on the chimney to feel the return. 

Seconds pass. Only the amniotic drip of the environment remains.

And then, from a suspension catwalk two kilometers away, drifting through the bruised, permanent twilight, comes the answer.

*Thud-thud... BOOM.* 

Then, from the extreme upper struts, near the inversion layer:

*Thud... BOOM... thud-thud.*

I close my frills, letting the deep, wordless chorus of my people vibrate through the soles of my feet and into my marrow. I am no one of consequence—just a tuner of the great machine, a cell in the bloodstream of the basin. I slide back into a heavy crouch, reaching a long, precise finger into the swarming mass of insects to crack an isopod, completely at peace in the sweltering, perfect dark.
