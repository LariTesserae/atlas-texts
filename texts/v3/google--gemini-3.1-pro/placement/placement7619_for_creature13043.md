I am heavy. The sheer, gravitational reality of my own bulk is the first truth of my existence. I am a teardrop of dense muscle and industrial blubber, wedged into a hairline crevice at the very bottom of a forgotten sinkhole. 

Above me, the cavern vibrates. The ceiling of my refuge shakes with the concussive, rhythmic *booms* of the grey tide—my frantic, scuttling kin, millions of them, hurling their hydraulic mass against the high bedrock. Dust sifts down from the cracks above, settling on my broad, scarred back. It mixes with the viscous mucus weeping from my hide, hissing softly as the chemical reaction cools my skin. 

I was once like them. My upper back bears the deep, overlapping topography of keloid ridges, the history of a thousand ceiling collapses and razor-edged rockfalls. I remember the blinding ecstasy of the swarm. I remember the pneumatic snap of my primary limbs vaulting my mass over the crushed bodies of the fallen, the desperate panting of my gill-vent, and the orgasmic, terrifying rush of pressing my soft, pleural underbelly against the searing flash of newly exposed crystal. I remember the heat. I remember the panicked, dying chill that always followed.

But I am no longer part of the tide. I fell, days or perhaps years ago—time does not exist here, only temperature—tumbling down a scree-slope of shattered quartz as a scaffold collapsed. I landed here, in the neglected depths of a sinkhole already stripped of its vast, easy veins of light. To my kin, this deep pocket is dead ground. Their flat, polarized eyes sweep over it and see only cold slate. They do not come down here. 

And so, trapped in the dim, tepid shadows, I was forced into the greatest heresy of our biology: I stopped moving.

At first, it was an agony born of terror. My vascular pleats contracted, desperate for the thermal shockwave of cracked stone. My enormous primary limbs locked in frantic, shivering spasms, trying to pry apart the immovable walls of my cage. But the stone held. As the ambient moisture rained down on me, I felt my internal fires dying. I prepared to become cold meat, to let my fluids freeze and my rubbery flesh become one with the mineral mud.

But in the stillness, my vision changed. 

Without the blinding, white-amber interference of the violent, macro-fissures above, my polarized eyes began to adjust to the dark. I stopped looking for the glaring lakes of heat that the horde chases. I began to look closer. I peered into the solid, unweathered slab of bedrock curving just inches from my face. 

I saw the micro-fractures. Not as fault lines to wedge and shatter, but as a delicate, glowing circulatory system. A capillary network of the reactive mineral, buried beneath millimeters of slate, infinitely thin and dormant.

I reached out. Not with the thick, hydraulic pillars of my primary forearms—those hung limp and useless at my sides—but with the nest of six articulate limbs beneath them. My multi-jointed digits, usually wrapped tightly around crude basalt mauls, were suddenly free. 

With meticulous, trembling care, I used my toughest, sharpest digit to scrape at the slate. Just a flake. Just a millimeter of rock dust peeled away.

The damp air rushed into the microscopic wound. A single, thread-like vein of the crystal ignited. 

It did not flash with the blinding daylight of a shattered ceiling. It sparked like a tiny, brilliant star. I shifted my weight, gently pressing just one translucent pleat of my underbelly against the pinprick of light. 

The heat did not rush through me in a chaotic, overwhelming surge. It trickled. It was a slow, agonizingly perfect drop of warmth that seeped into my luminous bloodstream. Because I had barely exposed the vein, the damp air could not scavenge all the heat at once. The reaction stabilized. The tiny star burned steadily, a slow, continuous ember rather than an explosive flash.

I have remained in this crevice ever since. I have learned the rhythm of the stone.

When the pinprick cools, I do not panic. I do not heave my body against the walls. I merely extend a delicate digit and scrape away another micrometer of stone, opening the next node in the capillary line. I am drinking the thermal energy of the earth not by slaughtering it, but by bleeding it, slowly, perfectly. 

Because I do not participate in the frantic friction of the swarm, my biology is altering. The basic, cooling mucus that constantly weeps from my hide has nowhere to burn off. It drips down my flanks, pooling at my base. As the heavy, warm rain of the cavern washes over me, the mucus has begun to react with the dense accumulations of rock dust. 

I am calcifying. 

The thick, rubbery elastomer of my lower hide has hardened into a grey, cement-like crust, fusing permanently with the floor of the sinkhole. My primary, heavy forelimbs are now completely encased in this biological mortar, transforming into solid pillars anchoring me to the earth. 

I am becoming something new. In a violent world utterly devoid of flora, where nothing rests long enough to take hold, I am becoming the first rooted thing. I am a living lichen of blubber, heat, and stone. 

Above, the deafening roar of industry rages on. The barks and screeches echo endlessly as the grey tide rips the world into a state of perpetual, agonizing birth, trampling their dead into the mud for the sake of a fleeting, explosive gasp of heat.

I let them roar. Here, in the dim and quiet deep, I huddle beneath my armored shell. My lesser hands move with the gentle, rhythmic precision of a watchmaker, scraping away solitary grains of dust, coaxing singular sparks from the dark. I am warm. I am still. And for the first time in the history of the rift, a piece of the world is holding together.
