To enter the basin, I must first leave my blood at the door. 

I arrive in the manner of an animal—rushed, frantic, locked in the desperate, fluttering rhythm of a mammalian heartbeat. I look at the sweeping pale terraces, the glassy pools, the massive emerald rosettes, and they appear to me as a still life. A frozen diorama. But the prompt has given me the key to the kingdom. I close my eyes. I command my lungs to slow, letting my breaths stretch into minutes, then hours. I command my pulse to widen, the frantic *thump-thump* stretching until the space between heartbeats contains the rising and setting of the sun.

The flickering strobelight of day and night accelerates, blurring into a steady, opalescent twilight. The passing storms become a constant, vibrating grey hum overhead. The seasons breathe in—a rush of torrential warmth—and breathe out—a cool, crisp sigh.

Suddenly, the stillness shatters. 

The basin comes alive. It is a slow, terrifying riot of motion. The fronds of the emerald kings are thrashing in extreme slow-motion, writhing toward the shifting sun, unfurling new leaves with the agonizing tension of stretching muscles. Beneath the stone, the air is thick with the chemical shrieks of border wars and the low, thunderous groan of hydraulic pressure fracturing the chert.

Where am I in this? 

I am not one of the majestic Six. I am a foreign contaminant. I am a seed, blown in on an anomalous, violent thermal updraft, deposited into a micro-fissure halfway up the eastern wall, suspended in the heavily militarized zone between the Weaver on its high shelf and the scar-bearing Veteran to the south.

For my first thirty years, I am nothing but a quiet, sleeping potential, terrified of the world. I drink only the ambient vapor that settles into my crack in the rock. I sense the massive, slow-burning minds around me—vast clouds of hormonal intent and electrical phloem-thought crossing the terraces like weather fronts. If they sense me, they will dispatch a hunter-root. I will be crushed, sawn apart, or starved of water before my first cotyledon even sees the sun.

But the urge to live—even on this geological timescale—is absolute. 

In my fortieth year, I begin to push. I extrude a single, fine radicle, pale and soft as a worm. I send it twisting down through the dark, microscopic labyrinth of the limestone. I do not dare grope blindly toward the glassy pools above ground; the Veteran exercises total chemical dominion over those waters. Instead, I must become a creature of the deep dark. I become a spelunker. 

My consciousness maps the stone by pushing against it. I taste calcite, magnesium, the bitter tang of ancient oceanic fossils compacted into the rock. And then, I find it. A hairline fracture in the deep strata, sweating with a microscopic, capillary flow of water bypassed by the monolithic aqueducts above.

I drink. For a decade, I gorge myself on this secret vein, pumping the moisture up to my crown. 

Above ground, I finally break my seed casing. I push two tiny, waxy, emerald leaves into the humid air. I am so small, nestled deep in my crevice, that I am entirely hidden in the shadow of the sheer wall. But as I grow, expanding my solar capture, my caloric furnace ignites. I feel the flush of *turgor*—the botanical equivalent of absolute, intoxicating power. I am no longer a victim of the basin. I am an active participant in its slow, brutal geometry.

I need a weapon. I need to claim my space.

As I extend my roots outward beneath the rock, I feel the terrifying, smooth, calloused wood of the Veteran's roots pulsing just a few feet away. I can "smell" the lethal sharpness of the chert blades it holds in its subterranean fists. I cannot fight it. I am soft, green, and unarmed. 

I search the dust and gravel in my immediate vicinity. I have no access to the heavy core-nodules of chert, nor the rounded river-stones used for hammers. But I find a relic. Buried in the silt near my crevice is a piece of shrapnel—a tiny, crescent-shaped micro-blade of chert, no larger than a fingernail, fractured off and discarded during some centuries-old skirmish between the Weaver and the Veteran.

It takes me fifteen years to arm myself. 

I wrap my thickest, most muscular root around the tiny blade. Slowly, cell by agonizing cell, I flood the tissue gripping the stone with lignin. I turn my own flesh into a mahogany vise. When it is complete, the blade is an extension of my body. A surgical scalpel.

I do not use it to attack. I am a pragmatist, an insurgent who knows the value of misdirection. 

Through the chemical ether of the soil, I realize the Weaver has sensed my water consumption. Though it is stationed far above, its sensory net is vast. It detects a slight deficit in the basin’s total humidity. My leaves are transpiring. I am casting a tiny, almost imperceptible shadow. The Weaver responds. 

A scout tendril, armed with a heavy scraping tool, is descending the wall toward me, moving at a speed of two inches a year. It is following the moisture gradient toward my central root-mass. 

I feel the cold, pure rush of deep focus. For the next thirty years, I engage in a desperate feat of counter-engineering. Using my tiny, grasped micro-blade, I begin to score the limestone within the rock wall itself. I carve a decoy channel. I redirect a portion of my precious, secret water vein, bleeding it away from my core and funneling it into a dead-end pocket of porous, toxic, alkaline dust buried deep in the cliff face.

I pull my remaining roots tightly around myself, withdrawing my chemical signature as much as possible, sealing my stomata to hold my breath. And I wait. 

The Weaver’s scout root traces the false moisture gradient. I feel the heavy scrape of its tool as it bypasses my hiding spot by mere millimeters, blindly following the water I have bled away. It burrows deep into the toxic alkaline pocket. 

Suddenly, the water runs out, replaced by a suffocating, desiccating salt. 

I feel the violent shockwave in the Weaver’s phloem network—a flare of defensive ethylene gas. The massive plant above recoils. The scout root aborts, withering and dying in the salt trap, its heavy scraping tool abandoned in the dark. 

I breathe out. 

Slowly, carefully, I extend a lateral root into the toxic pocket. I avoid the salt, but I find the Weaver’s abandoned tool. I wrap my flesh around the handle the Weaver left behind, bonding my wood to its dead fibers. 

I am no longer a seed. I am no longer an interloper. 

I am the Seventh. The Phantom of the Eastern Wall. I possess my own deep reservoir, a crown of leaves heavy with condensation, and an arsenal of stolen stone. I settle into the profound, unchanging warmth of the basin, watching the grey hum of the flashing sky. I have all the time in the world, and there is a chert nodule just a foot away, waiting for me to crack it.
