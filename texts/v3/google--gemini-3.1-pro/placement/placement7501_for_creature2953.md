The waking comes not as a sound, but as a shift in the marrow. 

I am suspended forty feet above the basin floor, lashed to the pulsing, scalding artery of a secondary steam vent. Sleep leaves me gradually, pushed out by a subtle arrhythmia in the iron beneath my chest. The great piston in the southern quadrant—the one we call the Lung—has caught a micro-second of drag on its downstroke. I feel it as a faint, dragging stutter in my ribs.

My secondary eyelid sweeps across the expanse of my eye, a reflex that pushes a heavy bead of accumulated gray dew down my cheek. The olive-tinted light of the gorge is diffuse and blindingly constant. I unlace my arms from the pipe. The skin of my inner forearms releases its suction on the hot metal with a wet tearing sensation. I feel no pain, only the comfortable, residual heat soaked deep into my boiled-leather hide. 

Where would I find myself here? I am exactly where I am meant to be: grafted to the middle-canopy, suspended in the weeping twilight. I am a Reader of the Iron. 

I lower myself from my sleeping harness, my wide, splayed toes finding the greasy curve of a banyan root. Gravity is a heavy, soaked cloak in this basin, and I wear it willingly. Every movement I make is a slow, deliberate transfer of mass. I am thickly built, stained a deep, mottled rust from my collarbones to my wrists. A patch of brilliant, velvety green algae flourishes in the deep creases where my neck meets my shoulders, kept vibrantly alive by the perpetual steam. My left thigh bears a massive, heavy burl of dark scar-wood—a gift from a cracked gear-tooth twenty years ago, packed with fungus and engine grease until my flesh simply enveloped the trauma, making my leg as thick and unyielding as a tree trunk.

I step off the root and onto an iron gantry. The metal is heavily pocked with damp maroon oxidation, but structurally sound. The moment my bare soles connect flatly with the iron, the gorge speaks to me in its true, silent language. 

The vibration is magnificent. It is an overwhelming, polyrhythmic symphony of torque playing directly up my shins and into the bowl of my pelvis. Through the iron, I track the grinding crush of the macerators below; I feel the slow, wet slap of the canvas belts; I map the flow of the pressurized steam. I close my eyes and simply *listen* with my skeleton. 

There it is. The Lung. The drag is caused by a buildup of sediment in the sluice gate feeding its condenser. 

I begin my descent. I do not take stairs; there are none. I negotiate a vertical drop through a chaotic lattice of riveted steel and braided vines, moving with a ponderous, inexorable fluidity. The heat is a physical weight pressing into my lungs, but my skin is slick, weeping a cool condensation that perfectly mimics the weeping stones of the cliff walls. Passing a junction of enormous, algae-furred timber, I encounter two others. They are hauling a slate-gray canvas drive-belt, their broad shoulders strapped into bark-woven harnesses. 

We do not look at each other's faces. Faces do not matter. We pause. I press my forehead against the damp, blackened timber of the support column. One of the haulers does the same. With my heavy knuckles, I tap a low, resonant cadence into the wood. *Deep-shallow-deep. Sediment in the south gate. I am bound for it.* 

Through the wood, against my brow bone, comes the reply. A single, heavy thump. *Understood. Equilibrium.*

When I reach the basin floor, the water is warm and brown, swirling sluggishly just above my ankles. The air down here is so thick with moisture and black oil that it tastes like sweet, metallic earth. I wade toward the southern gate. The Lung is a towering, cast-iron marvel, its great arm rising and falling with the agonizing slowness of a dying star. Beneath it, the wooden sluice gate is jammed by a small, sodden mass of decaying fern fronds and heavy mud. 

I do not rush. Rushing is a myth of the dry world. Rushing shatters bone and splinters iron. 

I wade into the sluice, sinking into the muck up to my knees. The waterwheel turning nearby dredges up a thick, starchy foam. I brace my back against the brick-lined channel and press my thick, calloused palms against the jammed wooden gate. It is locked tight by the pressure of the water and the sheer weight of the debris. 

I wait. I close my eyes and feel the vibration of the gorge through the water. I wait for the exact moment the Lung reaches the apex of its draw—the moment when the local pressure drops by a fraction of a degree. 

It comes. A heavy, hollow shudder in my jawbone. 

I plant my feet. I do not heave. I simply become an immovable object pressing against a movable one. I draw upon my short, dense muscles, letting the sheer, terrifying torque of my biology flow down through my shoulders, into my elbows, and through my palms. I lean into the wood. Seconds pass. Minutes pass. The strain is absolute, a silent, vibrating tension that turns my skin a deeper shade of maroon. 

The gate groans—a low-frequency vibration that ripples through my arms. Then, it slides. With infinite slowness, I force the heavy timber upward. The dammed water surges through, carrying the knot of dead ferns with it. The sediment clears. Above me, I feel the Lung hit its downstroke. The stutter is gone. The beat is perfectly, relentlessly smooth. 

I step back, letting my arms drop. The gorge is in equilibrium once more. 

Tension leaves my body. I wade to the edge of the machinery and find a wooden catchment trough. Inside is the lifeblood of the basin: the warm, starchy, pale-green paste extruded by the macerators. I scoop it up with my broad hands and eat. It tastes of pulverized chlorophyll, ancient rain, and a faint kiss of iron oxide. It is the taste of myself. 

My shift is not over, because there are no over, only the continuous present. I will spend the next hour slowly greasing a trunnion with a clump of oil-soaked moss. Then, perhaps, I will climb. I am growing older; my thoughts turn increasingly upward, toward the high canopy. There is a massive banyan up there, its trunk pressing dangerously close to a primary steam-valve. Soon, I will carry a heavy wrench into the upper branches. I will lash myself to the wood, let the green algae bloom thick across my scalp, and become an Adjuster. I will spend the next five years turning a single, rusted bolt, giving the living tree room to breathe. 

I will be the slow turning of the nut. I will be the grease. I will be the dampness. I will live forever inside the deafening, beautiful heartbeat of the gorge.
