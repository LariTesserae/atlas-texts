I would be found at the very top of the scallop, in an interstitial chamber where the sky is only a thin, pale blue blade visible through a fissure in the vaulted ceiling. It is the highest habitable elevation in the micro-city, a terrace so narrow and recessed that the crisp daylight never directly touches it. Here, the air is cold enough to preserve, but just warm enough to keep the weeping rock from freezing into frost. 

I am the apex tender. I am the one who watches the master capillary. 

My body is a record of the stone. If you were to see me, you might not immediately realize I am alive. I sit cross-legged on a worn, concave depression in the floor, wrapped in a mantle of raw wool so old and dense it feels like a carapace. The lichen woven into the fibers of my collar has long since bonded with the damp air, blooming into quiet, sea-green rosettes against my jawline. I do not brush them away; we are sharing the moisture. 

My skin is the exact shade of the limestone dust—a matte, chalky gray that absorbs the ambient dimness. There is no subcutaneous fat left on my bones; my arms, extending from the heavy wool sleeves, look like bundles of dry kindling wrapped in translucent parchment. My pulse is a phenomenon that happens perhaps thirty times a minute, a slow, heavy *thud* that I can feel reverberating in the hollow of my throat, keeping time with the eternal drip of the cavern.

I do not move for hours at a time. The ecology of this city relies on absolute stillness, and my role requires the deepest stillness of all. 

Before me is the source. The weeping wall here does not just sweat; it bleeds a slow, continuous film of moisture from a horizontal seam in the bedrock. A thousand years ago, the first hands carved the master groove here, the primary vein that splits and branches down into the capillary network supplying the deep, dark cisterns of my nineteen kin below. 

My task is to ensure the breath of the mountain is never interrupted. 

Between my thumb and forefinger, I hold a splinter of polished bone—the femur of a high-altitude corvid, smoothed by generations of skin oil to the texture of river glass. Once every hour, or perhaps once every two, I lean forward. The movement is a glacial glide. I do not disturb the dust. I place the edge of the bone into the master groove and trace it with agonizing precision, feeling through the tool for the microscopic resistance of a stray granule of limestone, or the infinitesimal bloom of a rogue lichen spore that could dam the heavy, cold water. If I find an obstruction, I scrape it away with a motion so slight it burns no calories. 

When I am thirsty, I tilt my head back and open my mouth beneath a stalactite of carved bedrock, waiting. I do not reach for a gourd. I wait for the condensation to gather, swell, and drop. When a single droplet falls upon my tongue, tasting of deep earth and cold iron, it is enough. When I hunger, I take a pinch of the bitter, woody root paste from a small stone ledge beside me, letting it dissolve against the roof of my mouth, a slow release of earth-bound energy.

I do not feel lonely. The word has no architecture here. 

Instead, I feel suspended in an immaculate web of vibrations. The silence of the city is not a vacuum; it is a canvas. Through the soles of my feet and the base of my spine, which rest cleanly against the dry-stacked floor, I can feel the life of the city playing out below me. I feel the rhythmic, sweeping arcs of the alpine scrub brooms in the courtyards far below—a faint, rhythmic whisper rising through the limestone. I feel the sudden, infinitesimal weight shift of a brother or sister testing a stone stair with a grass-woven sandal. Every so often, the hollow acoustic of the cavern carries a sharp, dry *click* from a throat twenty terraces down, signaling a full cistern. 

I do not click back. My presence is simply known by the uninterrupted flow of the water.

Behind me, in the darkest recesses of my high terrace, the wall is not made of dry-stacked stone alone. It is made of the ancestors. Bundles of wool, brittle grass, and parchment-flesh, wedged into the crevices like mortar. They give off a faint scent of dry dust and old thyme. I rest my back against them sometimes. They are cool and solid. They do not decay, they simply wait forever, forming a protective barrier against the deep-drafting winds of the inner mountain.

I am the oldest living thing in the city. I know this not because I have counted the years—we do not measure time, we only inhabit it—but because I can feel my organs beginning their final recalibration. 

My breaths are becoming shallower, requiring less of the thin, brisk air. The interval between the heavy *thuds* in my chest is lengthening. The cold no longer stings; it feels like a familiar, heavy blanket settling over my shoulders. There is no anxiety in this realization. There is only a profound, crystalline satisfaction. I have spent my life tending the equilibrium, and soon, I will become a permanent part of its structure.

One day, very soon, I will trace the polished corvid bone down the master groove one last time. I will fold my hands into the thick wool of my lap. I will blink my heavy eyelids, slowly, until they simply remain closed. My chest will rise a final time, and then it will settle into the same unbroken stillness as the stone beneath me.

I will not fall over. I am perfectly balanced. 

Below me, someone will notice that the groove-scraping has ceased. A younger ascetic, whose skin is slightly less gray than mine, will glide up the narrow, stepped alleys. They will see me sitting in the dim, weeping light. They will not weep. They will gently lift my desiccated, lightweight frame and press me into a waiting crevice in the wall, fitting my shoulder perfectly against the parchment shoulder of the ancestor beside me. 

And then, they will pick up the polished corvid bone, sit in the concave depression my body has worn into the floor, and begin to tend the water. 

I will remain behind them, a dry-stacked element of the city, perfectly quiet, perfectly preserved, feeling the vibration of their breath and the eternal, slow sweat of the mountain, forever unchanged in the shadow-held chill.
