I would not be found in the canopy. The canopy is too thin, too close to the permanent gray ceiling of the sky, too distracted by the immediate arrival of the heavy rain. 

If I exist in this world, I have surrendered to gravity, drifting down through the emerald twilight, past the suspended lakes and the grazing invertebrates, until I reach the deep understory. I would be found in the transitional darkness—the pressure zone—where the living architecture of the roots begins to plunge into the mile-deep sponge of compressed, ancient rot. 

Here, the air is so saturated it is practically a liquid, rich and dark as steeped tea.

I am one of the elongated descendants, yes, but I am very, very old. I am in the final, decades-long exhalation of my life. I am undergoing the Rooting. 

I do not look like a distinct creature anymore. To a wandering observer, I am merely a knot in the colossal, striated trunk of a titan-fern. My immensely long, multi-jointed limbs are no longer slung across branches; they are braided tight against the weeping wood, locked in a permanent, loving embrace with the pillar that supports my corner of the sky. 

The environment has begun to digest me, and I am allowing it. It does not hurt. Pain is a sharp, frantic mechanism, a warning system for beings that need to run. We do not run. The slow merging feels entirely like relief—like the unlacing of a corset I did not know I was wearing for a thousand years. My soft, cartilaginous ribs have parted slightly, willingly, to allow a thick, pale liana vine to thread itself through my thorax. Spongy, water-logged moss has blanketed my back and shoulders, acting as an insulating sheath. Lichen, the color of bruised plums, has crept over my face and sealed my eyes shut. 

I do not need eyes. Sight requires distance, and there is no distance left between me and the world. 

Instead, I experience the world through my tympanic crests. Pressed flush against the wet bark of the titan-fern, my jawbone acts as a geological seismograph. I do not just hear the environment; I am entirely submerged in its vibrations. Deep inside the trunk I cling to, I feel the massive, anti-gravitational roar of the capillary rivers being pulled upward from the soaking peat, a soundless, thrumming earthquake of water rushing toward the canopy. It vibrates through the wood, through my crests, and rattles the remaining jelly of my marrow. It is a terrifying, magnificent sensation, and it is my entire universe.

But I am not just passively melting. I have a function. 

As the sharp, ambitious minds of our ancient ancestors softened, they lost the ability to write, to carve, to encode data in hard stone. But consciousness does not simply evaporate; it changes its medium. As my body fuses with the tree, the blue, bioluminescent fungi that once coated my throat and arms have bypassed my skin entirely. They have rooted into the vascular cambium of the tree itself. 

I am becoming a neural node in the ancient, weeping wood. 

Because I sit at the junction between the living canopy and the dead, compressing peat, I act as a lens. The tannic water churning up from the deep ground is not just wet; it is saturated with the chemical memory of millions of years of fallen leaves, dead beasts, dissolved basalt ruins, and the dissolved bodies of my ancestors. As the tree drinks this memory-water, it flows through my slowing, permeable body. 

I filter the memory of the world. 

When the deep oceanic black of the cycle settles over the forest, I orchestrate the light. I no longer flash poetry on my own skin. Instead, drawing on my slowing heartbeat—now thumping perhaps twice a week—I send slow, colossal waves of blue fungal fire pulsing up the entire circumference of my host tree. I am broadcasting the dreams of the rotting basalt, the quiet history of the peat, the slow enveloping tragedies of the deep rot. I paint stories that take a month to tell, written in azure light stretching two hundred feet high.

Sometimes, a younger one comes. A descendant still fluid, still untethered, gliding down from the titan-bromeliads. They will drift out of the gloom and press their velvety, emerald skin against the trunk below me. They will flare their tympanic crests and lay their long, tensile arms against the bark to feel the light and the hum. 

They are coming to read the archive. 

We will sit like that for an entire season. I will push a thought, a feeling, a memory of a perfectly smooth stone basin, out through the glowing veins of the wood. They will absorb it through their skin, shivering slightly in the warm, heavy air, and then, slowly, they will pulse a response back from their chest—a gentle, glowing blue flicker that simply means: *I understand. We are still here.*

Eventually, even my heartbeat will cease entirely. The moss will swallow the last outline of my skull. My cartilaginous bones will dissolve into liquid sap. But my consciousness will not snap out. It will simply disperse, widening until I am the capillary water, the damp hum of the wood, and the blue light sliding up the bark into the endless, dripping dark. 

I would be the archivist who surrendered the archive, happy, at last, to just be the shelf.
