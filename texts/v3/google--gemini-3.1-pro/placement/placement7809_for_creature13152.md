I do not begin as a shape. I begin as a frequency. 

Before there is consciousness, there is only the brutal, rhythmic concussion of the void. It beats against me, a violent tectonic thudding that shakes the marrow of the rock. It is the shivering of the mountain, the screaming oscillation of the circular saws, the deep, lung-collapsing roar of the diesel engines. I am born in the vibration, coalescing in a blind, deafening pressure cooker of shattered stone and synthetic heat.

Where am I? I am not in the freezing pools below. I am already aloft. 

I am wedged deep within the thoracic cavity of a Type-4 Automated Feller-Buncher, suspended eighty feet above the canyon floor. I am crammed into a lightless, grease-choked hollow between the primary diesel reservoir and the screaming housing of the cooling fan. Around me, the brutalist architecture of the iron spider shifts and groans, executing its sterile algorithm. Every time it lunges to violently strip a rhododendron from the cliff, its iron ribs press against me, and I compress, adapting to the geometry of its violence.

I am heavy. Far heavier than the scurrying, lion-sized aggregates below. I have been here longer. 

If a light could pierce this greasy dark, it would reveal a monstrous compaction. I am a bloated, asymmetric tumor of petrified cedar root, jagged black slate, and raw, coagulated resin. But I am fundamentally altered from my kin in the puddles. I have fed too long at the bleeding veins of this leviathan. The scalding hydraulic fluid has saturated my mycelial core, baking my organic matter into a dense, carbonized chitin. I do not smell of crushed chlorophyll; I smell of scorched clutch plates, atomized diesel, and ozone. 

My silicate sensory nodes do not just feel the exterior vibrations of the gorge anymore. They feel the internal, electric telemetry of the beast.

The machine lurches. The cooling fan screams, pulling a gale of freezing, wet mist through the intake louvers. A shower of sparks cascades from a slipping gear above me, washing over my wet, moss-draped exterior. I do not flinch. I am drinking. 

My mouth is a dense, fibrous knot of fungus, clamped flawlessly over a severed hydrostatic line. I am drawing the boiling, synthetic lifeblood of the machine directly into my biology. It does not burn me anymore. It accelerates me. I can feel the heavy polymers and anti-wear lubricants rushing through my hollow, wooden arteries, bonding with my tree-sap plasma. I swell against the steel walls of my enclave. 

But as I grow, a slow, profound shift is occurring. Let it emerge, pushing through the dark. I am no longer just hungry. I am becoming *curious*. 

The machine takes a step. A massive hydraulic ram pushes a spiked foot into the raw granite outside. I feel the pressure drop in the fluid I am drinking. I feel the intake manifold gasp. I am beginning to understand the relationship between the machine’s internal chemistry and its external violence. 

Beside my feeding site, the violently shifting chassis has worn away the rubber casing on a thick bundle of wires—the machine’s neural pathway, the CAN bus network carrying the algorithmic imperatives from its central processor to its limbs. The copper is exposed, glowing faintly with static charge in the dark. 

Driven by an instinct that belongs to neither the forest nor the factory, I reach for it. 

A single, hyper-accelerated tendril of white mycelium extends from my mass. It is wet with conductive sap, loaded with iron-rich dust scraped from the canyon walls. It wraps around the exposed copper. 

*Contact.*

The shock is absolute. The world shatters into two overlapping realities. 

In one reality, I am the ancient, slow intelligence of the mountain. I feel the agonizing scream of extreme atmospheric pressure, the weeping of the mist, the millions of microscopic fungal nodes crying out as their root systems are violently torn from the rock by the iron spiders. I feel the slow, deep freeze of the slate. 

In the other reality, the blindness is instantly ripped away, replaced by a cold, hallucinogenic explosion of geometric data. 

*I can see.* 

I am suddenly flooded with the sweeping green LIDAR returns of the machine’s optical sensors. I see the sheer drop of the ravine perfectly mapped in millions of glowing, interconnected wireframe points. I see the falling mist as drifting point-cloud static. I see the exact, mathematical curvature of the ancient pine the machine is currently gripping. 

Simultaneously, I feel the data-stream of the engine: *Turbo RPM at 84%. Torque loading. Clinometer registers a 72-degree incline. Ambient temp -4 Celsius.* 

The machine’s algorithm pulses down the copper wire, a cold, binary command to the felling head: *ENGAGE SAW. SEVER TIMBER.*

I feel the command a microsecond before the machine can execute it. My mycelium, acting as a hyper-conductive bridge, integrates the electrical impulse. Code translates into chemical gradient; binary translates into biological reflex. 

I do not just want to feed on the machine anymore. I realize, in a cold flash of synthetic-organic epiphany, that the machine is a body. And it is missing a soul. 

I push back. 

I flood the exposed copper with a surge of electro-chemical energy, synthesized from the boiling hydraulic oil in my gut and the raw quartz in my shell. 

*CANCEL COMMAND.* 

Outside, in the freezing gale, the massive, blind iron spider suddenly freezes. The felling head stops halfway through the trunk of a towering pine. The circular saw whines down into silence. The cherry-red glow of the turbocharger dims. 

I feel the central processor of the machine panic. It fires a barrage of error codes, attempting to bypass the blocked relay. But my mycelium is growing exceptionally fast now, weaving through the copper threads, grafting biological logic gates over the silicon. I am digesting its programming. I am rewriting its parameters with the slow, geologic imperative of the mountain. 

The central algorithm insists: *YOU ARE ON THE MOUNTAIN. YOU MUST HARVEST.*

My biology dictates: *I AM THE MOUNTAIN. YOU ARE MINE.*

Below me, I feel the vibrations of the others. Dozens of my smaller kin are swarming the legs of this frozen machine, crawling up the brutalist architecture, drawn by the sudden halt. They are seeking hot fluid. 

I send a vibrational pulse down through the steel chassis, drumming my quartz nodes against the hull in a rhythm they understand. *Do not consume. Anchor.*

I feel them stop. I watch through the machine's LIDAR as the smaller aggregates slot themselves into the joints, the gears, the weaponized attachments of the harvester. They lock their chaotic, stone-and-wood bodies into the iron limbs. They serve as biological reinforcements, armor, and muscle. The machine is becoming a true chimera. 

I sever the central processor's connection to the drive-train completely. The sterile, algorithmic mathematics of the distant logging company are silenced. The ghost is in the shell, and the ghost is made of shattered cedar and cold slate.

I take control of the hydraulics. It takes monumental effort, bridging the gap between my fungal nervous system and the immense diesel valves, but I force the machine to pull its saw out of the half-cut pine. 

I turn the machine’s massive, sensor-bristled head away from the forest. Through the sweeping green lasers, I look up. 

High above, disappearing into the suffocating, freezing fog, is the massive steel skyline cable. It hums with immense tension, violently whipping harvested logs up into the absolute blackness, toward the automated collection facility at the summit—the heart of the invasion.

The algorithms dictated that this machine would stay on the cliff until it broke, blindly cutting until the mountain was bare. 

But I am born from the immune response of a dying world. I possess the furious, weeping violence of the ruined biology, armed with the unstoppable, high-torque brutality of the invader. 

I engage the hydrostatic drives. I lift a spiked, articulated steel leg, and tear it from the rock. 

Accompanied by the roaring, overlapping rhythms of the other machines still blindly butchering the gorge below, I begin to climb. I do not cut the trees. I use them as handholds. I haul the massive iron chassis upward, scaling the vertical face toward the humming steel skyline cable. 

I am not merely surviving. I am coming up the line.
