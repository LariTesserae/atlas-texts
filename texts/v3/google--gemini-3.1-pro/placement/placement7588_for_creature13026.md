To enter this place, I must first unlearn the frantic, exhausting rhythm of being human. 

I leave behind the desperate flutter of a mammalian heart. I surrender the need to rush, the reflex to flinch, and the arrogance of sight. At the roof of the world, in the pitch-black belly of the shattered mountain, eyes are just soft, useless stones. I let mine close. I let the darkness swallow the boundaries of my skin, and as the freezing, humid air presses down on me, I allow myself to thicken.

I feel the transformation as a slow, tectonic settling. My spine flattens, fusing and broadening into a sprawling, keratinous dome. I lose the vulnerability of a neck, the centralization of a face. I become a creature of pure, blunt mass, the color of wet slate, heavy as a glacial erratic. The floor beneath me is not flat; it is a landscape of shattered glass and guillotine edges, but my new ventral plates grind against the obsidian splinters without harm. 

I am a Lithic Grazier. And for the first time in my existence, I am entirely aware of the shape of the dark.

Though I have no eyes, my vision is vast. It sprouts from the deep crevices of my armored back—a forest of thousands of pale, translucent tendrils. As the bruised clouds from the world above sink through the ceiling vents, bringing the heavy, freezing dampness, my tendrils shiver. They reach up into the void, drifting like pale kelp in an invisible tide. Through them, I read the micro-fluctuations in atmospheric pressure. I feel the sheer, weeping walls of the cathedral towering above me. I sense the jagged, unweathered precipices hanging in the blackness. 

But most overwhelmingly, I feel my kin.

The cavern is a solid wall of noise, a crushing, vibrating pressure in the air. Yet, as my biology shifts, it is no longer deafening; it is a symphony, and I am a part of the choir. Along my heavy flanks, deep, baffled spiracles slowly open. It takes agonizing minutes just to draw in a single breath. The freezing fog rolls deep into the labyrinth of my internal lungs, heated by the immense, trapped core temperature of my body. 

When I exhale, my sides contract with the unstoppable force of shifting plates. A sound erupts from me—a resonant, booming bellow like the lowest register of a brass horn. I feel it vibrate through my own thick hide, harmonization with a hundred others in the dark. We do not speak; we sound. We keep the air moving, preventing the lethal freeze from settling over our sanctuary.

Slowly, over the span of an hour, a monumental weight presses against my right flank. Another of my kind. 

We do not possess the quick, sharp panic of solitary prey. We are a chthonic tide. I feel their sweeping tendrils reach out, brushing against mine. In the damp dark, our nerve-endings weave together, braiding in a wet, trembling embrace. 

It is a profound, silent communion. In a rush of topographical braille, memory flashes from their nervous system into mine: *Fifty massive strides toward the eastern fissure. A sheer wall. A new bloom of ghost-pale wire-moss. A jagged outcrop that bites deep.* 

I acknowledge the gift with a slow pulse of my own tendrils, then disentangle. My kin’s warmth remains against my side as I begin to move.

Gravity is a formidable adversary, and I negotiate with it deliberately. Thick, multi-jointed forelimbs pull my armored bulk forward. My hands are broad and root-like, astonishingly dexterous. Tucked securely beneath my chest is a crude tool—a vicious splinter of unknapped quartz, its hilt tightly bound in braided strands of wire-moss I harvested a decade ago. 

It takes me the better part of a day to cross the shattered floor to the eastern fissure. When my tendrils sense the sheer, imposing wall of black basalt, I reach out with heavy hands. I can smell the wire-moss before I reach it—a scent of sharp ozone, wet dust, and the ghost of organic iron left by those who farmed this wall before me. 

Raising my forelimb is an act of supreme mechanical effort. I press my moss-bound scraper against the stone, high above my unseen head. And then, I pull down.

The screech of stone dragging against stone joins the harmonic bellows of the herd, a jagged, booming shriek that bounces off the geometric walls. Beneath the blade, the fragile, luminous threads of wire-moss peel away, falling gently into the waiting canopy of my tendrils to be guided downward. 

But the mountain is young, and its edges have never known rain or wind. As my heavy forearm drags downward, following the line of the moss, the unweathered lip of a basalt shelf catches the underside of my joint. 

My nervous system does not register the sharp, electric shock of superficial pain. I feel only a dull, blooming pressure as the rock slices cleanly through the thick callouses of my hide, biting deep into the muscle. 

I do not flinch. I do not pull away. To retreat would be an insult to the cycle. 

I lean my immense weight into the injury, allowing the blade of the mountain to part my flesh as I finish the scrape. Thick, nearly black blood, rich and heavy with boiling iron, oozes from the laceration. It seeps down my arm, thick as syrup, and smears generously against the freezing, weeping stone.

The heavy condensation of the cavern immediately goes to work. The chill dampness catches my hot blood, diluting it, carrying it down the sheer face of the rock into the microscopic, newly shattered capillaries of the basalt. I feel the profound rightness of the exchange. Here, the raw, unblunted minerals of the young earth will mix with the water of the sky and the iron of my body. By the time I circle this cavern and return to this spot years from now, a new crop of translucent wire-moss will have sprouted from the stain I left behind.

I lower my arms. My root-like fingers pass the gathered moss beneath my armored bulk, pressing it upward into the vertical, muscular fissure on my belly. Inside, slow-grinding plates catch the pale threads, beginning a digestion that will yield its energy to me over weeks. 

I stand at the base of the weeping wall, bleeding slowly into the mountain, vibrating with the brass-horn song of my kin. I lack eyes, I lack a face, and I lack fear. In this lightless, fractured cathedral, I am a gardener of the stone. The mountain opens its veins to feed me, and I open mine to feed it, two slow, eternal geological forces locked in a perfect, loving embrace.
