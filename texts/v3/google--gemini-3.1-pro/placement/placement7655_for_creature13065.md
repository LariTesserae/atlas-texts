I do not wake, because I do not sleep. I merely coalesce. 

My awareness begins as a pooling sensation, a gathering of density among the rusted rungs of fifty-story service grating. The air is terribly heavy, tasting of ozone and wet iron, and the freezing drizzle slides through me as if I am made of the rain itself. 

I am a ghost of the weeping canyon. I am draped in a cowl of deep green, hyper-dense moss that I harvested centuries—or perhaps minutes—ago. To the unseeing world around me, I am just a bulge in the overgrowth, a trick of the dripping mist. But beneath the suffocating canopy of my moss, I am pouring mercury. 

I flow downward, elongating into a thin, gelatinous ribbon to slip through the gaps in the grating. I do not have a spine to dictate my posture, nor lungs to heave in the thinning, high-altitude air. My biology is an osmotic slush, saturated with antifreeze proteins that render the raw chill of this atmospheric cellar not merely tolerable, but intimately comforting. 

Where my face should be, there is a shield of pitted, rusted iron—the scavenged cowling of a long-dead ventilation fan. Embedded within the gelatinous flesh behind this mask are the shattered prisms of drone optics. Through them, I do not see the bruised gray sky. I see the world in beautiful, swirling gradients of magnetic flux and thermal bleeding. 

In my vision, the valley is a silent, raging aurora.

I glide along the sheer face of a ferrocrete cooling tower, my multi-jointed fingers finding purchase in the microscopic pores of the metal. Far below me, the silence is physical. I sense a massive, distant *whump*—a tremor that travels up the spire and vibrates through the copper wires I have woven through my own flesh. A heavy-load track has fallen. The structural collapse reads as a sudden, violent bloom of thermal heat in my lower periphery. 

I feel the pull of the friction. The equilibrium requires me. 

As I slide down the corrugated slopes, moving with acoustic absolute zero, I observe the frantic hush of the battle around me. To my right, a colossal creeping vine, thick as a mainmast, is slowly, violently constricting a cluster of hydraulic exhaust valves. I can see the heat of the stress—the metal glowing aggressively crimson in my thermal sight, bending under a torsion that would snap bones. The automated defense reacts. Six structural repair booms swoop down on magnetic rails, moving like hunting spiders. Their shears open, and they sever the vine.

The silence is absolute, but the visual violence is staggering. 

The machine bleeds first. The crushed valves rupture, weeping thick, luminescent blue synthetic oil. It cascades down the ferrocrete in a silent waterfall. A moment later, the severed vine violently recoils, spewing its hyper-oxygenated, neon-green sap into the freezing mist. 

I arrive at the wound. 

I extend a translucent limb, stripping back the heavy flap of wet fern I wear like a sleeve. I press my palm against the weeping metal, then against the raw, shuddering stump of the alien vegetation. I drink the tragedy of their conflict. 

The fluids osmose directly through my porous membrane. Deep within the gelatinous matrix of my body, the blue oil and green sap helix together. They emulsify. I watch the chemical reaction bloom inside my own chest, an eruption of vivid, iridescent aquamarine that races through the scrapped fiber-optic cables I use as a nervous system. I am suddenly illuminated from within, a liquid-neon geometry cloaked in rust and wet moss. I feel a sluggish, creeping euphoria. I am fueled.

The repair booms are already spinning their arc-welders, throwing soft, blue, silent sparks across the gloom, sealing the ruptured valves. If they succeed completely, the vine will die, its root system will putrefy, and the ferrocrete bulkhead it was anchoring will destabilize. 

The machine is winning too easily here.

I flow into the shadow of the primary gantry crane. To the sensor arrays of the booms, I am a pocket of ambient water, registering at the exact same temperature as the mist. I reach out. With pinpoint precision, my long, pliant fingers locate the main magnetic coupling of the repair array. I intentionally cross the torn copper wires near my wrists, dumping the newly acquired aquamarine energy into a localized electrostatic pulse. 

*Pop.* 

Just a spark, tiny and silent. The magnetic lock fails. The massive boom silently, heavily slides off its rail, plummeting into the gray abyss below. The remaining drones instantly retreat to recalculate the failure. The stump of the vine, spared from total eradication, begins to pulse with aggressive regrowth.

The balance holds. The apocalypse continues.

I turn away, retreating into the shadow of an exhaust pipe the size of a cathedral. As I step onto a dripping catwalk, another shape pours itself out from a tangle of high-tension cables. 

It is one of my kin. 

They wear the carapace of a deactivated welding drone as a mask, the glass eye-slits glowing with their own internal, aquamarine slush. They are tall, fluid, imposing, dripping with the eternal condensation. 

We do not speak, for the world demands our silence. We step toward each other. I raise my long, glowing hand, and they raise theirs. Our fingers intertwine.

The contact is an electric shock of pure knowing. Through the conductive plant-veins and copper wiring embedded in our skin, a compressed packet of telemetry flashes between us. In a fraction of a millisecond, I download their journey. I see a terrace three miles above us, where a sudden bloom of dark, spongy moss threatens to suffocate the atmospheric vents. If the vents choke, the fog clears. If the fog clears, the valley dry-rots. 

They have brought me a map of a crisis. 

I squeeze their hand, sending back a pulse of bioluminescence—an acknowledgment, a promise. We break contact. They dissolve downward to take my place near the cooling tower, and I turn my face upward, toward the high-altitude fade.

I begin the long, silent climb. 

I am in the margins, the weeping gaps, the cold crevices of this boiling antheap. I do not mourn the metal, and I do not love the leaf. I am the child of their friction, the keeper of their stalemate. I will climb for a century if I must, a streak of liquid light moving through the shadows, ensuring that this world lives forever in the perfect, silent agony of its own breaking.
