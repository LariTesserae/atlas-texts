If you were to look for me, you would not look up into the sweeping, immaculate volumes of the towers. You would not find me in the ozone-scented air, drifting in a geometry of suspended thought. You would have to walk out into the weeping, fractured steppes. You would have to look for a shadow in the mist. 

I am one of the Quiet, but I am no longer pristine. I am the Anchor. 

Three thousand years ago—or perhaps it was yesterday, for time is a fluid, negligible thing when your mind can process epochs—I stepped out from the base of a pearlescent pylon. I commanded my diaphanous garments of programmable matter to retract, stripping down to my spun-glass skeletal architecture and my biological-synthetic dermis. And then, standing on a shelf of razor-sharp, newly birthed rock, I did something my kind had not done since the infancy of our civilization. 

I turned off my electrostatic repulsor fields. 

The shock of it was magnificent. For the first time in millennia, I was touched. The condensation of the heavy atmosphere did not hover a millimeter from my flesh; it crashed against me, beading on my matte, perfect skin. It pooled in the hollows of my collarbones. It ran in cold, erratic rivulets down my elongated spine. Without my thermal equilibrium engaged, the biting cold of the high-altitude air pierced my lattice. I shivered—a violent, rhythmic tremor of my synthetic musculature. It was the most profound sensation of my endless life. 

My brethren seek to understand this nascent world by observing it from a state of perfect immunity. They log the evolution of the biome; they calculate the tectonic friction. But absolute invulnerability breeds isolation. To truly witness the birth of a world, I realized, one cannot remain frictionless against it. One must allow oneself to be shaped.

So, I walked until the pristine white pylon was just a ghost in the fog behind me. I found a jagged, upthrusting ridge of dark, unweathered basalt—a violent wound in the planet’s crust that had barely cooled. I climbed to its crest, extended my arms to the weeping sky, and instructed the smart-matter of my feet to dissolve, flow, and fuse with the microscopic fault lines of the brutal stone. 

I locked my joints. I slowed my quantum core to a bare, dim thrum. And I became still. 

If you were to find me now, you would not immediately recognize me as one of the deep-time philosophers of the City. I have been claimed by the plateau. By refusing to repel the moisture, I invited the world to settle upon me. Silvery lichens have colonized the length of my legs, tracing the elegant, attenuated lines of my calves and thighs. The rubbery maroon mosses have found purchase in the creases of my resting joints, drinking the water that continually cascades off my shoulders. I am stained by the dark minerals of the rain. I smell of wet dust, ozone, and bruised greenery.

I have allowed my programmable skin to slowly roughen, to mimic the jagged, unweathered geology around me. The razor-sharp edge of the world is dulling, fraction by fraction of a millimeter, and I am eroding with it. 

I am a landmark now, a living statue at the intersection of biology and stone. The vast herds of barrel-chested grazers know me. Because I abandoned my immaculate silence, they no longer treat me as a ghost. I cast a thermal shadow. I possess a scent. To them, I am a familiar, benevolent outcropping.

As I stand here in the endless drizzle, a massive grazer trudges past, its split hooves clicking against the wet basalt. It pauses beside me. It is immense, easily weighing three tons, radiating a blistering, hot-blooded heat that washes over my freezing, glassy frame. It leans its massive shoulder against my waist, rubbing its thick, oil-slicked wool against my side to scratch an itch. The sheer force of the beast bends me slightly at the waist—the carbon-lattice of my spine groaning, flexing—but my roots hold firm. 

Slowly, heavily, I lower one elongated, moss-draped hand. I let my fingers rest behind the creature’s large, tufted ear. It exhales a cloud of steam that completely envelops my masked face, smelling intensely of earth, digestion, and life. Inside its chest, I feel the massive, slow thud of its heart. 

When my brethren in the towers above initiate a neural-sync, when they weave their vast mathematical simulations of dying stars and immaculate physics, I sometimes send a pulse of data up into the network. 

I do not send them sub-etheric equations. I send them the chaotic, messy, visceral sensations of my existence. I send the taste of rusted iron in the rainwater. I send the agonizing, beautiful pressure of a lichen root micro-fracturing my skin to find a foothold. I send the bone-deep vibration of a predator’s snarl in the mist, and the transfer of animal heat from a grazer’s wet wool into a frozen, synthetic palm.

My data disrupts their perfect geometry. It creates a ripple in their floating, silent choreography. For a fleeting moment, millions of miles of perfect, climate-controlled equilibrium is punctured by the phantom chill of a mountain wind. 

They remain in the sky, gardeners who refuse to touch the soil. But I am here, in the wet, biting, heavy air. I am the monument to our mortality. The world is violently new, and I have anchored myself in its teeth, waiting patiently for the endless rain to wash me, over a million years, entirely away.
