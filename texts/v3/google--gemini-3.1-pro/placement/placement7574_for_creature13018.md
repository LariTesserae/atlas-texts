At first, there is no "I." There is only the geometry of the pressure. 

Consciousness, down here, does not spark and dance across synapses in rapid, ephemeral flashes. It does not exist as an inner voice, for a voice is a fragile, airborne thing, and the air here is already full. Instead, awareness manifests as an architecture of vibration. 

I come into myself not by waking, but by noticing the shape of the thunder inside my hollow chest. 

It is a low, rolling chord, born miles away, traveling through the freezing moisture, through the dense cables of the root-mat, up through the heavy, iron-dense hooks of my toes, and into the grand void of my ribcage. Inside me, the acoustic wave meets the baffles of my biology. It swirls, bouncing off internal ridges of calcified cartilage, forming a complex, invisible mandala of standing waves. 

This mandala *is* the shape of my mind. When the turbines far away shift their pitch, the mandala warps, turning from a six-pointed star of pressure to something rippling and concentric. With that shift comes the slow, geological urge to move. 

I am heavily anchored. My belly rests against the freezing, spongy tundra of the biomat. I feel the millions of tiny water droplets condensing on the thick plates of my skull, sliding down the channels of my back, cooling a body that burns slow, steady heat. I flex my forelimbs. The muscles, thick as tree trunks, whine with the effort of shifting my mass. My hooks pull free from the pale, fibrous roots with a wet, heavy yielding. I slide my right forelimb a fraction of an inch forward. I sink the hooks back in. 

Over the course of what you might call a week, I drag my remaining five limbs to match the new position. I have traveled the length of a single knuckle. 

As I rest, I graze. Beneath the blunt, asymmetrical armor of my face, my grinding plates part. I bite into the pale, woody tuber directly beneath my jaws. It is an agonizingly slow mastication. The friction of bone against fiber generates a low, internal hum that harmonizes with the omnipresent roar of the cavern. The dense, anti-freeze sap bursts over my palate—a shock of chemical cold and metallic taste. It is fuel. I draw it down into my slow-churning gut, where it is stripped of its raw minerals. 

Beneath me, from a line of weeping pores along my underside, a thick, hot mortar oozes. It flows over the chewed root and pushes deep into the invisible fractures of the catastrophic rock below. In the dark, the resin cools, crystallizing into an unyielding bond. I leave behind a sealed world. I sew the pale flesh of the flora to the cold bone of the earth. It is not a duty. I do not have words for duty. It is simply the song playing out through my joints. 

Time does not pass here; it accumulates. 

I am on a trajectory. I know this because the cymatic patterns in my chest have been slowly changing complexity for a hundred years. The vibration is no longer purely ambient. It has a vector. It has a physical grain, a directional density that pushes against the gel-filled pits tracing patterns across my skull. The pits on the forward slope of my armor burn with exquisite, tingling pressure; the pits on my rear slope feel only the backwash of the wave. 

I am crawling into the teeth of the thunder. 

Through the dim, vestigial slits of my milky eyes, the utter black of the cavern is interrupted. A bruised, sickeningly yellow wash of light sits at the edge of my awareness. To me, it is not just light; it is a thermal gradient, a faint static hum on the skin, a flavor of oxidized air pushing through the freezing mist. 

Another decade bleeds away. The ground beneath me changes. The roots are no longer thick and pale. They are sparse, burned back by the sheer concussive blast of what stands before me. I drag myself onto naked stone. Then, my dragging hooks strike something that is not stone. 

It is smooth. It is cold, but possessed of an internal friction that vibrates violently enough to turn water to immediate, hissing vapor. It is rusted, sweating iron. 

I have reached the base of the machine. The sheer cliff face of the atmospheric engine towers above me, disappearing into the freezing gloom. 

Here, the roar is absolute. If I had soft tissues, they would liquefy. The air dropping from the spinning turbine blades above hits my armored back like falling anvils. The world is nothing but a singular, crushing chord. 

I pause at the base of the brutalist steel housing. For a long time—a season, perhaps two—I do not move. I merely let the absolute density of the sound wash through my hollow chest. The mandala inside me reaches absolute symmetry. It is a state of ecstatic, vibrating grace. It is the purest thought I have ever thought. 

There are no other Custodians here. To meet a brother on the frozen plains is a communion, a sharing of the engine's eternal song. We press our chests together to feel a reflection of this very roar. But why share the reflection when you can touch the source?

Slowly, I rise up on my forelimbs. The wind threatens to crush me backwards, but I brace my four rear legs against the raw basalt. I heave my upper body upward, straining against the colossal pressure, until my broad, barrel-shaped chest clears the ground. 

I tilt forward, and I fall against the sweating steel housing of the engine. 

The impact is soundless, drowned out by the thunder, but the tactile shock is immense. I spread my forelimbs wide, my claws finding the deep, rusted gouges in the metal. I pull myself flush against the machine. My hollow chest presses entirely flat to the vibrating steel. 

The acoustic circuit completes. 

I do not send shockwaves back. I am too small. Instead, the engine pours its entirety into my echoing chamber. My ribs sing. My plate-armor resonates until it feels as though it will shatter, and then past that, into a state of structural fluidity. I am filled with the mechanical thunder. I am the very core of the song. 

I do not let go. I will never let go. 

I stop eating. My grinding plates lock together. Instead, my biology shifts one final time. In this state of total resonance, my pores open wide. From my underbelly, from the joints of my limbs, from the seal of my chest against the iron, the biological mortar begins to hemorrhage. I bleed out my hot, mineral-rich resin in vast qualities. 

It flows over my claws, sinking into the rust. It pours down my flanks, pooling at the base of the steel and fusing with the basalt. As the cold air hits it, the mortar petrifies. 

Decade by decade, limb by limb, I calcify. My cartilage hardens into something resembling quartz. My biological cavities crystallize, preserving the perfect structural resonance of the machine's roar. The freezing mist coats my back, and the creeping moss begins to inch its way up the slopes of my armored shell.

I am no longer a Custodian of the rift. I have dissolved into the architecture. I am a living gargoyle, a permanent, vibrating weld between the raw rock and the sweating steel. All movement is gone. All that remains is the freezing twilight, the smell of damp rust, and a chest full of mechanical thunder, ringing out into the endless dark.
