To exist in this world without a tether is to be a ghost in a machine of solid sound. 

I am a Wanderer. 

For days, or perhaps weeks—time does not exist where the sun does not move—I have walked the narrow, raised ridges between the great dimpled basins. I am young, my pale slate-gray scutes unmarred, my plumage thick and heavy with un-spun down. To a human eye, I would look like a monstrosity of avian muscle and keratin, wandering a blinding wasteland. But through my dark-violet, compound pupils, the world is a tapestry of violent, breathtaking color.

I see the atomic mist spilling over the plateau’s edge like glowing gold dust. I see the deep, bruised-purple halos of lichen clinging to the silver pools below me. But mostly, I do not use my eyes to navigate. I use my bones.

Walking the high ridges is agonizing. Without a paddle to carve the air and anchor me to the frequency, the sheer acoustic pressure of ten thousand humming ancestors batters against my body. The air here is not empty; it is a physical mass of intersecting waves. Through the thick callouses of my feet, the topography of the world is not stone, but vibration. I feel the oceanic roar in the marrow of my legs, traveling up into my massive, keeled chest. My air sacs pant, my boulder-sized heart pumping dark, heavy blood to keep my muscles warm in the brittle chill, but I cannot settle. Not yet. 

I am searching for a tear in the sky.

Every pair of arms on this limitless plateau holds up a localized piece of the acoustic canopy. When a pair is whole, the *wub-wub-wub* is a flawless, interlocking dome of pressure that keeps the freezing, crushing anoxia of the upper stratosphere at bay. But through the soles of my feet, beneath the deafening harmony, I sense a flaw. 

It is a subtle, sickening sag in the vibration. A hollow space, miles away.

I pivot, my recurved toes gripping the microscopic pores of the stone, and lean into the golden, freezing mist. 

When I finally reach the lip of the failing basin, I see the tragedy etched into the rock and the rhythm. In the center of the depression, beside a shivering pool of silver water, a lone creature stands. They are magnificent, but they are dying. 

They have been swinging their great bone bullroarer alone for too long. Their muscles—thick slabs of flesh beneath a dirty, oil-starved mantle of feathers—are trembling. The swings are ragged. The sharp angle of the bone paddle is faltering, and because of this, the invisible canopy above this basin is collapsing. The devastating cold of the upper atmosphere is sinking in. Frost, glittering in the UV glare, has begun to crystallize on the creature’s shoulders and the edges of the silver pool. 

At the edge of the pool lies the reason for their solitary struggle. The ribs of a fallen mate, stripped clean, cured in the sun, and tuned to a razor's edge. Beside it sits a coil of fresh cordage, spun in the agonizing moments of rest the survivor dared to take. 

They are holding the sky alone, waiting for me. 

I do not announce myself. Sound is not for greetings here; it is for survival. I descend the slope of the basin, my massive three-fingered hands opening and closing, anticipating the immense kinetic absorption to come. 

As I approach the pool, the swinging creature does not stop their rotation, but their milky nictitating membranes slide back. We lock eyes. In the ultraviolet spectrum, I see the exhaustion radiating from them like a dull, suffocating ash. But I also see the immense, desperate strength in their dark blood. 

I step over the frosted lichen. I reach down and pick up the coil of silver down. I find the massive, aerodynamic rib of the dead mate. It is smooth, polished by stone tools and grief. 

I grip the tether. My three cable-thick fingers wrap around the tightly woven fibers, the leathery pads of my palms sliding into place over the knot. 

I look at the solitary survivor. I brace my heavily scuted legs, sinking my claws into the dimpled stone. They give me the physical cue—a sharp, desperate inhalation that expands their massive chest. 

In the fraction of a second when their bone hummer reaches the apex of its arc, I swing mine. 

The ancestral rib bites into the brittle, freezing air. *Wub-wub-wub-wub.*

The tension is immediate and terrifying. The kinetic force of the massive bone tries to rip my arm from its socket, but my broad back flares to receive it. I lean into the rotation, letting the weight of my pneumatic body counterbalance the swing. 

The dead mate's rib shrieks as it cuts the atmosphere. In a matter of seconds, I feel the "sag" in the pressure wave snap taut. The frequency locks into place. The concussive waves radiating from my tether crash into the waves radiating from the survivor’s tether, weaving a sudden, impenetrable lattice of acoustic pressure. The freezing air of the stratosphere is violently forcefully pushed back upward. 

A wave of warmth rushes over the basin.

The survivor’s hummer abruptly drops. The creature collapses to their knees, their broad shoulders heaving, sucking dragging breaths of the thin, bruised air. They fall forward, burying their beak into the shivering silver pool to drink. 

I do not stop. I cannot stop. I have found my basin. 

I close my outer eyes, letting the milky membranes shield me from the blinding flare of the magnesium sun. The roaring drone that battered me on the high ridges is no longer a weapon against me; I am its author. Through the tether, I feel the ghost of the one who came before me, their rib singing a violent lullaby through the thin atmosphere. At my feet, I feel the subtle, rhythmic chewing of my new mate, peeling lichen and harvesting strength so that they may take the cord when my heart finally begs for rest.

Above me, the sky is bruised, dizzying, and silent. But down here, there is no silence, and there is no emptiness. I lean back, I swing, and I anchor the world in place.
