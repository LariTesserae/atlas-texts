The warmth is heavy, damp, and smells of fermentation and old blood. 

I wake buried deep in the dreadlocks of *Baelo*, the old herd-sire. His massive lungs function as bellows against my own broad, barrel chest, breathing in a slow, rhythmic counterpoint to my own exhalations. It is the darkest part of the morning cycle, the air beyond Baelo’s shaggy bulk a freezing, silvered soup that stings the lungs. I untangle my short, thick legs from the beast's coarse, steaming fur. My skin, mottled cedar-red and deeply creased at the knees and elbows, immediately tightens in the chill. I run a thick hand over my shoulders, stimulating the waxy sebum to coat my hide, throwing my heavy cape of woven cedar-straw over my head.

Baelo snorts, a plume of hot steam rolling over my face. I scratch him briefly behind his ear, my thick fingers slick with his musk. Then, I turn toward the abyss.

I am not a Beast-ward, though the freezing nights require us all to share their living hearths. I am not an Arbor-wright, though my forearms are scarred from clearing splinters. I am a *Strand-caller*. I am the nervous system of the gorge. 

I step out of the herd’s sod-packed shelter onto a massive, polished root-highway. My horizontal, glacial-blue eyes snap open to their widest aperture. The world is a sheer, vertical slate of grays and wet, deep greens, framed in the staggering peripheral vision my goat-like pupils provide. I don't look down; I don't need to. I feel the abyss pulling at my heavy, low-slung hips. It is a constant, muscular argument, and my body knows all the retorts. 

I drop into my hunched, spring-loaded gait. My heavily fissured foot-pads slap the glossy wood. The elongated, keratin-hooked toes on my front feet bite into the microscopic grooves of the ancient timber. Step, reach, vault. Step, reach, vault. 

The chaotic din of the waking settlement washes over me. The deep, bass-heavy roar of the unseen river below vibrating up through the soles of my feet. A flock of iridescent, sickle-winged cliff-birds explodes from a hanging colony above, showering my cedar-cape in icy condensation and a few prized, violet feathers. I snatch one from the air without breaking my vault, tucking it into my cheek for later trading. 

A midnight-blue monkey-rat darts across my path, screeching its territorial nonsense. I hiss a burst of air through my teeth, and it scrambles up a liana, its spindly fingers clutching a plump, writhing wood-grub. I let it go. We need the thieves. 

I reach my station at the nexus of the Third Western Span—a dizzying intersection of living roots, braided lianas, and groaning wooden gantries hanging over the void. The mist is profound here, weeping from the giant ferns attached to the canopy above. I grab a taut, wrist-thick vine that serves as a suspension line. My hands are permanently curved from this work, callouses thick as horn. 

I close my eyes. I stop listening with my ears, tuning out the squealing gear-works of the Winch-kin hauling dripping tubers two levels down. 

I listen with my hands.

Through the slick, wet fiber of the liana, the life of the gorge speaks to me in vibrations. I feel the deep, steady thrum of an ox convoy trudging along the lower tier. I feel the frantic, staccato hacking of obsidian adzes far out on the eastern wall. 

Then, I feel it. A brittle, high-frequency shudder trembling through the line. A sick twitch. It is coming from a blind curve on a gantry twenty spans above me. Tension where there should be slack. Dead weight where there should be flex.

I throw my head back and force air up from my dense lungs. I press my tongue to the roof of my mouth and issue a piercing, bird-like whistle that slices straight through the roar of the water and the grinding of wooden wheels. I follow it instantly by beating my fists against the hollowed drum-trunk beside me—*crack-thump-thump, crack-thump-thump*. 
*Overgrowth. Stress point. Fraying line.*

I don't wait for the Arbor-wrights to answer. I launch myself into the vertical. 

I leap from the root-highway to a dangling net of fibrous creepers. Gravity tears at me, but my hooked toes catch the weave perfectly. I scramble upward, a creature born to cling. Water pours over my brow-ridge, cascading past my flat nose. I taste the bruised pine needles in the runoff. I use my teeth as a third hand, biting onto a stabilizing root while my hands reach for higher purchase, my thick lips slipping easily over the rough bark.

When I pull myself onto the upper gantry, the problem is obvious. The titan trees never sleep. A monstrous new shoot, thick as my torso, has erupted from a massive burl in the night. In its violent thrust toward the mist, it has coiled around a primary suspension liana holding a cluster of living-pods. The liana is screaming, pulling tight, individual fibers snapping with sounds like pistol shots. 

Two Arbor-wrights swing down from the canopy moments later. They are immense, their ash-gray skin glazed in sticky, fragrant green sap. They don't waste time looking at me. One unslings a heavy bone mallet; the other readies a jagged obsidian adze.

But they cannot hack the shoot while it is under this much tension; the recoil of the severed wood would whip-crack the liana and send the living-pods plummeting into the abyss.

I throw myself forward. I wrap my heavy, barrel torso around the taut liana, locking my arms and legs around the massive new shoot. I dig my keratinous toes deep into the soft, green wood of the invasive growth. I bite down on the liana itself, my jaw locking. 

I become the anchor. I am the bridge between the stress and the release.

I thump my chest with one free hand. *Go.*

The adze falls. Wood chips explode against my face, bouncing off my thick brow-ridge. The Arbor-wrights work with terrifying, frantic violence. The tree bleeds thick, green sap over my hands and chest, mixing with the rainwater and the waxy sebum of my own skin.

*Crack.* 

The shoot gives way. The stored kinetic energy is monumental. It tries to buck me off into the empty air, into the mist and the roaring river below. My shoulders scream, a dull fire blooming in joints already swollen from years of the damp. I feel the terrifying drag of gravity try to peel me off the wood. 

But I am heavy. I am short. I am a creature of the abyss edge. I squeeze tighter, grunting through my nose, my hooked toes tearing tracts through the sapwood. 

Slowly, the violent shuddering dampens. The snapped shoot slumps safely onto a broad gantry. The suspension liana relaxes into a gentle, swaying hum. I release my jaws, spitting out a mouthful of bitter cedar dust, and uncoil my tired muscles. 

The Arbor-wrights nod once—a sharp jerk of their chins—and immediately begin clearing the heavy debris. There is no time for thanks in the damp.

I pull myself up onto a flat, moss-slicked branch and sit, my legs dangling casually over a drop that would take a falling stone a long time to hit bottom. I am breathing hard, white steam pluming from my flat nostrils. The musky smell of my own exertion cuts through the frigid mist. My skin aches. My hands throb. 

And then, it happens. 

The heavy, silvered soup above thins. The relentless drumming of the rain slows to a hesitant patter. An impossibly bright, stark beam of pale mountain sunlight pierces the cloud cover, slicing down through the cleft. 

It hits the plunging waterfall across the gorge, shattering the water into flashing, blinding silver. It touches the lichen coating the un-walked wood in front of me, turning it a vivid, electric, saturated green. 

The entire settlement pauses. 

Below me, the squeal of the winches dies. The hacking of the adzes stops. The deep grunts of the ox-herds fall silent. Up and down the dizzying, sheer cliff-face of life, hundreds of my people stop. 

I tilt my broad face upward, letting the beam of light wash over my wet skin. I close my rectangular pupils. The warmth is fragile, fleeting, barely enough to penetrate the grease of my cape, but it is glorious. 

In this moment of golden silence over the roaring river, I feel the immense, sprawling knot of the gorge breathing beneath me. The roots, the vines, the beasts, the water, and the sap. It is a violent, beautiful argument. And as the light lingers on my face, I know with absolute certainty that I have already won it today.
