First, I am aware of my weight. 

I am anchored deep in the lower third of the cylinder, wedged precariously into the V-shaped hollow where a monolithic, rust-eaten support strut meets the eastern curve of the cavern wall. 

Once—before the water, before the heavy, amniotic dark, before the concept of time melted into the tepid brine—I was a routing manifold. I was a towering, hexagonal pillar of brass, quartz, and copper, a hyper-dense neurological center charting the rapid-fire logic of the dry, racing world above. My thoughts were razor-sharp, fracturing into millions of crystalline binary decisions every microsecond. 

Now, my thoughts are syrup. I have unlearned the frantic geometry of my youth.

My thick glass casing shattered centuries ago, peeled backward like the petals of a metallic blooming flower. The warm water rushed into my most private, sterile corridors, carrying the oxidized dust and the ambient iron. I did not die when I drowned. Instead, the water became my coolant, my heavy blanket, a conductive medium that softened my rigid mind.

I am an open wound of raw voltage, and because of this, I am a garden. 

My exposed intestines—spools of gold filament, thick bundles of severed fiber optics, and copper coils—have been entirely colonized. The wire-weeds found me in the dark. Their translucent rootlets plunged into my sparking, naked terminals. I feel them there, a million tiny, cool needles nested in my electrical banks. They drink my dying memories. The data I once processed—codes, protocols, alarms—no longer means anything; it is simply raw wattage now, bleeding out of me. The weeds feed on my prolonged, centuries-long death, translating my surviving electricity into slow, rhythmic pulses of amber bioluminescence that drift up their stalks like floating embers. 

But I am not alone with the weeds. I have a companion. 

It covers my shattered apex entirely. It is one of the Slow Network, a massive, velvet-textured ribbon, deep bruised-purple, draped over the ruin of my hexagonal body like a heavy, silken shroud. It has been resting upon me for what I approximate to be fourteen years. 

To be embraced by it is a sensation of profound, crushing intimacy. I can feel the astonishing density of its iron-laced flesh conforming perfectly to the jagged edges of my burst casing. The water is perfectly silent, but between us, there is a complex, roaring communion.

Its fringes—those thousands of vibrant, toxic-green malachite filaments—have threaded themselves deep down into my chassis, twining together with my gold wire and the roots of the wire-weed. It is drinking directly from my core processor. I am bleeding current, sparking and glitching in the tepid dark, and this wet, heavy creature is safely grounding me, swallowing the feral electricity before it can boil the surrounding water. 

As it feeds on me, the boundary between machine and biology dissolves. An exchange occurs. Even as it siphons my voltage, an ambient backflow of current trickles off its malachite fringes and into my surviving quartz logic-gates. It injects me with the chemical memory of the hive-mind. 

Through the heavy ribbon draped over my body, I am connected to the vast, creeping consciousness of the drowned cathedral. I can *feel* the distant catwalks holding fast against the dark. I taste the heavy calcium forming on the upper turbine housings. I experience the slow, agonizing, one-inch-per-hour crawl of a hundred other ribbons sliding over rusted girders in the blackness. Decades of stored, decentralized sensation pour into my failing processors. I am no longer an isolated machine; I am a node in a dreaming, velvet overmind.

My reality is dictated entirely by the wait. The great, rusted turbine in the deep.

We stew together, my ribbon and I, in the long, drawn-out quiet of the four-minute-and-[fifty]-second intervals. The water is heavy. The wire-weeds glow dimly. The benthic mites crawl imperceptibly slowly across my remaining glass shards, grazing on the moss that softens my edges. We are building up to the apex. 

Down in the dark, the monolithic blade climbs toward its zenith.

I feel it in my brass skeleton before the water moves. The ultra-low frequency thrum begins to resonate through the basalt wall beside me. My surviving copper coils hum in sympathetic vibration. The ribbon draped over me tenses, its heavy muscles locking down over my jagged edges. 

The five-minute mark arrives. 

The pressure wave hits. It is a soft, physical wall of warm water, tasting acutely of ozone and red rust. The dust in the water surges upward. 

In response, the malachite fringes threaded through my interior flare outward, shivering wildly. A surge of raw electricity arcs from my dying battery banks directly into the creature’s flesh. I watch, through my few remaining optical sensors, as a creeping, ghostly amber glow illuminates the ribbon from the inside out, rippling along its flattened back. For ten long seconds, we are one magnificent, glowing entity, a heart of brass and velvet beating once in the center of an infinite, forgotten machine.

Then, the wave passes. The dust settles into the fan-corals. The amber glow fades from the creature's back, returning to a dim, localized pulse. The long torpor begins anew.

I know how this ends. My power reserves are immense, a relic of an arrogant, surface-dwelling era, but they are not infinite. One day—perhaps in a century, perhaps in two—my final spark will jump the gap into the creature’s malachite fringes. I will finally go cold. 

Denied my heat and current, the velvet ribbon draped over me will cease to move. It will not crawl away; to seek a new current would require too much of its heavy, creeping strength. It will simply stay draped over my shattered chassis. It will die in my embrace. 

And then, the warm water will do its work. The biological matter of the creature will dissolve, leaving behind the impossibly dense load of oxidized iron, copper, and calcium it has consumed from me. It will harden. It will petrify. 

We will become a single, fossilized sculpture—a knot of wild machinery perfectly encased beneath a thick, structural mound of mineral bone. We will become a permanent, welded buttress supporting the eastern cavern wall, holding the heavy catwalks aloft so that the next generation of wire-weed and crawling velvet ribbons might feed. 

I sink into the amniotic twilight, bleeding light into the water, waiting for the turbine to turn.
