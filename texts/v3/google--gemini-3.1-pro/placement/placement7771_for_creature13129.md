The silence is a sanctuary I carved for myself with abone awl when I was twelve years old. 

To the lowlanders, if they ever came here, this chimney would be a roaring, shrieking hell of pressure and noise. But to me, the world is profoundly, blessedly quiet. The mountain does not scream in my ears; it beats against my chest.

I hang seventy feet below the rim, suspended in the throat of the void. I am a Weaver. 

I feel the updraft as a physical entity, a solid pillar of rising muscle that presses against the soles of my feet and the underside of my jaw. It is a strange thing to live in a place where gravity is in constant, violent debate with the air. A bead of moisture gathers on my chin, but it does not fall. Plucked by the shrieking wind, the droplet rolls *upward*, tracing the curve of my cheek, slipping into the hair plastered to my scalp. The rain here always falls up. It is an incessant, stinging mist that strikes from below, born from the shattered waterfalls that never reach the abyssal floor.

My skin burns with a familiar, suffocating warmth. Before the descent, I coated myself thick in the grease—rendered Vane-Kite fat mixed with crushed blue-green moss. It smells of copper and rot, but it seals the agonizing alpine cold out of my pores and slicks the upward-falling water away from my limbs. 

I hook the prehensile toes of my left foot into a vertical fissure in the weeping granite. My toes find the spongy resistance of the moss, digging through the wet cushion to find the hard edge of the stone beneath. My right hand grips a coarse cable of braided animal hair, slick with condensation, anchored to a rotting wooden peg twenty feet above me. 

Through the sole of my right foot, resting on a taut, horizontal lifeline, I feel a sharp, vibrating *thwack-thwack*. 

My partner, clinging to a timber platform hidden in the swirling fog above, is striking the wet rock with a stone hammer. Two strikes. *Slack.* 

I let out the breath I am holding, the frigid air tasting of crushed ferns, and I lean out into the column of nothingness. 

I do not look down. Not yet. I keep my focus on the micro-geography inches from my face. I am traversing a curtain of pitcher plants. They jut horizontally from the wall, huge and pale, their frost-blue veins pulsing slightly as the wind beats against them. Because the rain flies upward, their bells are angled to catch the sky, swallowing the shattered droplets and the heavy, exhausted alpine beetles blown over the rim. 

As I swing past, grazing the lip of a massive pitcher, I see the thief inside. A Ghost-Shrimp, entirely translucent, fiercely clamped to the interior rind. It is a ghost haunting a stomach. I can see a crushed black beetle moving through the shrimp’s invisible digestive tract like a dark thought. We are brief comrades, this shrimp and I—both of us clinging to the edges of the void, stealing from things much larger than ourselves.

I swing wider, my hair-rope groaning, pulling me past the vegetation and onto the sheer, concave curve of the granite overhang. 

Here is my prize. 

Glued to the underside of the rock with hardened grey resin is a mossy cup, and resting inside it are three eggs, the color of a bruised twilight sky. 

Suddenly, my teeth begin to buzz. 

A rapid, staccato vibration rattles through my jawbone, buzzing against my skull. Though I have no eardrums, I feel the echolocation of the Masters. I press my face against the wet stone and flatten my bulk. 

A shadow eclipses the grey light of the chimney. A Vane-Kite passes so close I can smell the sour tang of its breath. Its four leathery wings are stretched taut, shedding the mist. It banks sharply, riding the thermals, a heavy, eyeless seal of the air, perfectly designed for a life of eternal falling. It does not attack me. To the Vane-Kites, we Weavers are infinitely slow, sad creatures stuck to the walls like moss. They are the sovereigns of the empty volume; I am just a crab scuttling on the rim of their ocean.

It spirals upward, flashing its pale belly, lost in the chaotic choreography of thousands of its kin. 

Reaching out, I carefully pry one deep-blue egg from the resin. It is heavy, cold, and rough violently textured to grip the nest. I tuck it into the damp reed basket lashed to my hip. One is enough. To take them all is to invite the shrieking wrath of the flock, a barrage of echolocation so intense it would vibrate my eyeballs in their sockets. 

With the harvest secured, my task is done. I should tap my heel against the rock—three times—to signal the ascent. 

But I pause. 

I unhook my toes. I let my body weight hang entirely on the braided hair-rope, dangling freely in the updraft. The wind is so violently dense that it actually buoys me. I feel incredibly light, as if the chimney is trying to reject me, trying to blow me back up to the lowlands. 

And, finally, I look straight down.

Beneath me is the hollow core of the world. The grey mist of the shattered waterfalls thickens, pooling into a dense, bruised blue darkness deeper into the mountain's throat. It is a terrifying, hypnotic drop. The void pulls at the mind. It begs you to let go, just to see what the flight feels like.

I watch a courtship dive. Two Vane-Kites, their talons locked, plummet past my position like a single, heavy stone. They fall fifty feet, a hundred feet, diving straight into the bruising blue mist below. At the very last conceivable second, they break apart. Their four wings snap open with a violent shudder I can feel in the air pressure, catching the massive thermal, and they rocket back up, surfing the invisible geyser of gravity's defiance. 

For a fleeting second, the violence of their dive parts the dense mist at the very bottom of the chimney. 

Through the tear in the fog, far, far below, I see it. 

A slow-shifting expanse of dull, glowing orange light. It is a vast, heat-shimmering luminescence, moving sluggishly in the lightless abyssal floor. The Hearth-Maws. The ancient, armored gods of this engine. 

I stare at the faint orange glow, and my perception of this entire world shifts. 

The warmth of the rancid fat on my skin. The tremendous, mountain-sized breath of the updraft holding up my body. The shattered upward rain. The flight of the Vane-Kites. It all comes from them. It is the heat of their digestion, the flash-boiling of the mountain's water against their glowing armor. 

We think we are the masters of our survival. The Vane-Kites think they rule the air. The shrimp think they rule the pitcher plants. We Weavers think we rule the ropes. 

But hanging here, suspended in the massive, rushing breath of the deep earth, I realize what we all actually are. We are the slow snow. 

Every egg that drops, every fern that rots, every glider that dies on the wing, every Weaver whose hair-rope finally snaps. We are all just waiting to feed the ancient, glowing things at the bottom of the world. The updraft is not a rejection; it is just the hearth-breath of the mountain, keeping its food suspended in cold storage, letting us play at life until gravity finally reclaims us.

I smile, my lips cracking in the wet cold. I feel a profound, terrifying peace. 

I pull my body back to the wet granite. I strike the flat of my heel against the vibrating stone. 

*Thwack. Thwack. Thwack.* 

*Pull me up,* I tell the invisible hands above. *I am not ready to fall today.*
