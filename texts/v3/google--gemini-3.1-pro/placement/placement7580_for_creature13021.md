I would find myself at the very nadir of the region’s softest depression—a valley so overwhelmingly shallow it is less a geographical feature and more a long, drawn-out sigh in the earth. 

I would not arrive here. I would have always been arriving, for millennia. 

At first, my awareness would localized, dim and warm. I would feel the sheer, crushing blessing of my own mass. I am one of the Elders. I am the size of a low-slung, sunken temple, though my architecture is entirely flesh and iron-rich shell. My carapace, once the pale terracotta of youth, has oxidized over aeons into a profound, almost blackened rust. Underneath, my immense muscular foot rests flush against the bedrock. I do not move anymore. The need to slide, to grind, to subdue the jaggedness of the world has left me. I have eaten the last sharp crag of the mountain that once stood here, dissolving its peak with my alkaline breath, sanding it into this perfect, welcoming bowl. I am the anchor.

But the "I" is a fleeting, porous illusion. 

As I settle into the narrative of my own flesh, I begin to feel the edges where my carapace meets the world. They are not borders; they are doorways. Seven hundred neighbors press perfectly flush against my perimeter. Where their softness meets mine, thousands of pink, arterial polyps have sought me out, tasted my chemical signature, and irreversibly fused. 

With every agonizingly slow beat of my hidden, decentralized heart, my sense of self bleeds outward. 

I feel the golden hemolymph flowing into me from the north, carrying the syrupy, heavy-molecule sensation of *afternoon sunlight warming the rust*. I pump it outward to the south, adding my own endocrine translation of *profound, resting gravity*. I do not have a brain to spark with frantic electrical thoughts. Instead, my consciousness is a massive, continent-wide weather system of moods and nutrients. I am aware of the exact moment a shadow crosses a cousin three miles away. I taste the mineral composition of a pebble being dissolved by a herd-mate at the edge of the plateau. We are an ocean of unified satiation. 

And yet, there is one sensation that defines my localized place in the tapestry. To the east, just past the edge of my immediate neighbors, there is a chemical silence. A vast, echoing deafness in the network. Through the shared blood, I can feel the shape of it: severe drops, unyielding right angles, lifeless symmetry. The ruins. The ancient stone cannot be dissolved. It will not graft. It offers no golden fluid. To our slow, undulating mind, the dead city is a painful shard of the world’s old frenzy—a place where frantic, short-lived things once built upward in a hysterical defiance of gravity. We flow around it. We leave it to the whisk-ferns. The ruins are the only reminder of what it means to be lonely, and the taste of their proximity makes the herd press closer together in a slow-motion shudder of comfort.

As the pale afternoon wanes, my velvety back—a sprawling forest of billions of micro-cilia—reads the changing barometric pressure. The temperature drops a fraction of a degree. A chemical wave of anticipation, tasting faintly of zinc and sweet alkali, rolls through millions of bodies. 

Twilight has come. The harvest begins.

I hinge my heavy plates upward. It takes an hour just to crack the seal, the leathery groan of my carapace echoing deeply in the chests of those grafted to me. From the damp, shadowed crevices beneath my armor, I unfurl my feeding tissues. They bloom into the cooling air like the opalescent gills of an impossible, deep-sea leviathan. They flush with bioluminescence, casting a faint, ghostly turquoise light against the darkening sky. Everywhere, for miles around me, the plateau begins to glow. 

The mist spills over the lip of the highlands. I reach into it. 

I do not breathe the air; I comb it. The phantom moisture catches perfectly in the fractal webbing of my tissues. The fog becomes dew. The dew becomes heavy droplets. 

Because I have lived so long, my great weight has warped the center of my shell downward, creating a vast, smooth concavity. I am a living basin. The condensation from my own gills, and the harvested moisture channeled down from the arched backs of a thousand smaller neighbors, begins to flow. 

*Tink... tink... tink.* 

The water pools in the crater of my back. It mixes with the golden, nutrient-rich secretions I weep through my shell. It forms a warm, dark, nectar-rich pond in the center of the world.

It is here, in this pool, that I feel the pinnacle of our existence. 

Floating in the golden water atop my back are the slivers of silver—the naked, disc-like flesh of our newborns. They are dizzyingly fast to me, rippling and spinning in the water over the course of hours, drinking the harvested sky and the shared lifeblood of the millions. Through the cilia submerged in the pool, I feel their delicate, searching touches. 

I am a cup that holds the future. I am not simply resting on the earth; I *am* the earth for them. They will take a century to harden, to slip from my back, to take their place in the living mosaic. But for now, they are perfectly safe.

I have no eyes to close, but I let my consciousness sink deeper into the shared, golden ocean of the network. The mist is cold, but the blood shared by millions is infinitely warm. The violent world is gone. The mountains have been tamed into submission. I hold the water, I hold the young, and I dream the long, slow, victorious dream of stone.
