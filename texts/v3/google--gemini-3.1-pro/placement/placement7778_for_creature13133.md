I am a nerve ending. 

If this city is a singular, suspended organism, I exist at the very limits of its flesh, where the built world meets the raw, agonizing teeth of the fault. I am an Edge-Binder. 

You would find me not deep within the warm, wool-muffled heart of the honeycomb, but hanging in the frigid, weeping shadows of the absolute perimeter. I live in the interstitial spaces—the vicious, acute angles where the pristine basalt refuses to accommodate our wooden geometry. To be an Edge-Binder requires a body that time and pressure have wrung completely dry of excess. Even among the lean Splicers, I am small, gaunt, and hyper-flexible. My spine is a bow; my collarbones are practically double-jointed, allowing me to compress my ribcage and slide into fissures no wider than a handspan. 

Right now, I am suspended upside down. 

My prehensile toes, wrapped in fat-soaked felt, are locked into a deep notch carved into a primary load-bearing strut. Gravity wants to pull me into the abyssal throat of the canyon, but I do not fight the drop; I simply hold the tension, my knees permanently bent, absorbing the micro-sways of the settlement as thousands of people move above me. 

My hands are pressed flat against the virgin rock face. The stone is freezing, hovering around fifty degrees, but to me, it feels alive. It is weeping. A slow, shimmering sheet of condensation glides over the unoxidized flecks of copper embedded in the gray granite. The water coats my thick, squared-off fingertips. The skin of my palms is thick as boiled leather, yet beneath that keratinous armor, my nerves are tuned to a frantic, microscopic frequency. 

I am listening to the stone with my flesh. 

We do not strike the rock. We do not drive wedges into it. We *embrace* it. My task today is to replace a structural rawhide lashing that has begun to rot from the perpetual damp. It wraps around a brutal, razor-sharp extrusion of quartz-veined stone, holding a massive, unbarked timber firmly against the cliff.

The process is an agonizingly slow ballet of leverage and terrified patience. Tucked under my chin, smelling deeply of rancid tallow and damp river clay, is a wooden jack—a threaded rod of dark, dense wood heavily slathered in animal fat. Operating entirely in the muffled silence, my wire-taut forearms burning with lactic acid, I slowly turn the jack. *Millimeter by millimeter.* I am taking the multi-ton weight of the city onto the jack so I can free the rotting hide. There is no squeak. There is no groan. There is only the violent, pounding rhythm of my own heart in my ears, and the soft, breathless plumes of white vapor escaping my nose.

I feel the rock beneath my left palm shudder. A vibration, infinitesimally small. 

I freeze. My eyes, stark wide beneath bands of thick black paste, lock onto the gray plane in front of me. I do not blink. I stop breathing. The rock is a coiled spring of tectonic geometry. Have I altered the pressure too quickly? Has the jack disrupted the fragile tension of a million tons of hanging granite above us?

The silence is absolute, a heavy, suffocating blanket that presses against my eardrums. I wait. Five heartbeats. Ten. The shudder passes; the stone accepts the new load. I let out a breath so slow it doesn't disturb the falling mist. 

In the dimness, a thin strand of braided fern-fiber, strung inches from my temple, suddenly vibrates. It produces a soft, rolling tremor against my cheek, followed by a double-pluck. 

It is my partner, anchored somewhere in the dense scaffolding three levels up, asking if the load has shifted. I cannot see them—my line of sight is entirely filled with the sheer rock face—but I can picture them, hand resting lightly on the taut capillary line, waiting. I press two fingers to the string, slide them down half an inch, and release gently. *Holding. Proceed.*

I unspool the fresh rawhide from my waist. It has been soaking in water, heavy and limp. I feed it behind the sharp jut of rock, my thick fingers walking it carefully over the flawless cleavage plane, ensuring it doesn’t snag on a quartz crystal. I bind the timber to the stone, crossing and re-crossing the wet hide, pulling with all the strength in my deep-set lungs, my bare, hardened heels bracing against the wood. As this hide dries in the chill air, it will shrink, clamping the timber to the cliff with a bone-crushing, silent grip. 

My work is an endless cycle of maintenance, chasing the rot, anticipating the slip, holding back the apocalypse with stripped bark and animal ligaments. It is a life of terrifying intimacy with the earth's open wound. 

But then, the gloom shifts. 

The gray mist around me slowly begins to pearl, taking on a luminous, milky quality. The shadows cast by the dense honeycomb of the city begin to stretch and clarify, drawing sharp lines down the sheer cliff. 

The Sun-Strike is beginning.

High, high above, the ribbon of brilliant sky lines up perfectly with the tectonic tear. The colossal column of daylight comes driving down into the abyss. It does not hit me directly—I am tucked too deeply beneath the overhang of the city's belly—but the ambient light ignites the canyon. 

The weeping walls suddenly burst into a billion points of liquid fire. The native copper veins flash like trapped gold. The dampness in the air, previously an oppressive weight, suddenly becomes a shimmering curtain of suspended diamonds.

I stop pulling the hide. I slowly untwist my body, letting my toes hold my weight, and lean out backward over the dizzying drop. 

Everywhere, across the sprawling, saturated underbelly of the city, movement ceases. I see dozens of other Edge-Binders and lower-scaffold workers doing exactly what I am doing. We look like pale, moss-draped spiders hanging from a vast wooden web. 

We all tip our heads back. The warmth doesn't quite reach us down here, not like it does for those on the upper roofs, but the *light* does. It washes over my grimy, gray face. I let my unblinking eyes close for the first time in hours. I open my mouth. 

A single drop of condensation, pure and impossibly cold, perfectly displaced by the shifting temperature, falls from the wooden lintel above and lands on my tongue. It tastes of the sky, of high-altitude rock, and of nothingness. 

For twenty minutes, I am not a nerve ending holding off death. I am just a creature suspended in light. 

Then, the shadows re-sharpen. The sun slips past the fault line. The velvet darkness slams back in, bringing the biting chill of the deep earth with it. 

I open my eyes. The mist rushes back into the gap. I reach out, my thick fingers finding the wet rawhide binding. I bend my knees, press my chest against the unweathered stone, and resume the silent squeeze.
