I do not wake; I merely surface. 

In the hollowed belly of a verdigris-stained pressure vat, I am woven into a knot of five other bodies. We lie suspended in a vast hammock of muscular, black vine. For hours, our breathing has been synchronized, our deep, barrel-chests rising and falling in a single, drawn-out rhythm, like a bellows left to the slow drag of gravity. As the narcotic torpor of sleep thins, I slowly disentangle my limbs from the slick, canvas-textured skin of my kin. 

The separation takes perhaps twenty minutes. To jerk a knee or hastily retract an arm would disrupt the sacred lethargy of the vat, wasting the damp alkaline air. I slide my arms out from beneath another’s torso, moving with the viscosity of sinking honey. 

Dropping from the hammock to the rusted corrugated walkway beneath, I feel the perpetual, lukewarm rain kiss my bare shoulders. It streaks through the ambient film of sweat, botanical oils, and condensation that coats me. I am bruised-violet in the dark, bathed in the luminescence of a shelf-fungus blooming from the vat’s upper hatch. I raise my hands and press my thumbs into the corners of my eyes, though the gesture does nothing to change my vision. My pupils—swollen wide enough to consume the iris entirely—drink in the gloom, pulling the shapes of the deep-ward from the humid fog. 

I am a tender of the Mid-Lateral Junction, far above the cherry-red thermal death of the abyss, yet deep enough that the heat presses a physical hand against my sternum. 

I reach into a leather pouch at my hip, withdrawing a hardened bead of the black resin that was bled from the grand tubers three cycles ago. I place it upon my obsidian-stained tongue and let the ambient heat of my mouth soften it. As it dissolves, a heavy, numbing peace cascades down my spine. The delta-state takes hold, washing away the frantic, staccato noise of waking consciousness. Time flattens. Panic is an impossibility, a relic of a sunlit world I have never known and could not survive. 

I begin my commute. 

I reach out through the mist, my hand finding a vertical iron rung. Against the inside of my bicep, the negative space of a heavy gear-spoke—branded into my flesh during a pressure-blowout a decade ago—rubs against the rusted metal. The scar tissue is numb, thick, and proud. I pull myself upward, letting my dense, isometric musculature absorb my weight. 

*Reach. Pull. Shift.* 

Each movement is deliberate, a slow, continuous flow of kinetics. I drift upward through the three-dimensional lattice of the city like a piece of ash caught in a thermal updraft. Around me, the silence is immense, broken only by the wet *thlap-thlak* of condensation dripping from the giant, rubber-thick fronds overhead, and the distant, subsonic groan of the deep-engine twisting. I pass other wardens on the catwalks. We do not nod; we do not smile. We exchange sweeping, languid arcs of our arms—a silent greeting, tracing the shape of a blooming tuber in the damp air. 

My destination is a cluster of machinery suspended between the sheer obsidian cliff-face and a massive, ascending root-stalk that dwarfs a cathedral pillar. Here, a cast-iron flywheel, three stories across, is caught in its glacial rotation. It moves an inch a minute, driven by the phenomenal hydraulic expansion of a captive tuber ensnared in iron bands. 

I do not operate this machine; I listen to it. I am the junction’s resonator. 

I straddle a thick, weeping copper pipe that feeds mineral slurry to the roots. I fold my elongated body over it, pressing my bare, heat-scarred chest directly against the metal. The copper is blistering, but I have been branded by it a hundred times before; my flesh is inured to its temper. I lay my cheek against the pipe. And I wait. 

For an hour, there is only the slow throb of my own heartbeat. Then, I feel it. 

Through the copper, through the ribs, into the marrow. A vibration. Deep in the unseen mantle, a thermal bubble has burst. The tremor travels up through the basalt splinter, into the iron foundations, up the vines, and into my workstation. I read the frequency. It is a slow, rolling wave, building pressure that will reach the mid-tiers in roughly a lunar week. 

I raise my right hand. Slowly, with agonizing patience, I reach for a heavy brass wrench dangling on a chain of braided vine. I grip the handle, lock my jaw, and fit it over the bolt of a vast venting-valve. To turn it requires the absolute commitment of my body. I bend my knees, align my spine, and lean my entire weight backward. 

My muscles lock. My barrel chest inflates, drawing in the humid, wet suspension of air. I hold the tension. I become a static statue of leverage. 

For five minutes, nothing happens. Then, a sharp *crack* echoes through my bones as the rusted threads surrender a millimeter. The valve groans. I shift my weight, leaning deeper. Over the next forty minutes, I bleed the valve open by a quarter-turn. A low, hissing breath of superheated steam escapes, joining the closed weather system of the cavern, rising up to become the lukewarm rain that washes over my back. The pressure is equalized. The tuber will not burst. The flywheel will continue its century-long rotation. 

To report the adjustment, I take a heavy mallet padded in thick moss. I press my palm against the giant root-stalk beside me to feel its grain, then strike the bark in a rhythmic pattern. 
*Thud...* (pause, breathe) *Thud... Thud.* 

The subsonic vibration travels down the root-stalk, carrying my message through the interstitial spaces of the city. Down in the hollowed boilers, or up on the corrugated roofs, my kin feel the slight shudder in their spines. They understand. The junction holds. 

My shift will last until the resin on my tongue dissolves completely. When my mind begins to edge out of the hallucinogenic calm, I will slowly retract my limbs, release the wrench, and begin the hour-long descent back to the communal vat. 

I look down, past the edge of the copper walkway. Below me, the giant black fronds overlap into a seemingly bottomless sea of damp green and gray, illuminated only by the pulsing violet decay of the shelf-fungi. Far beneath it all, the cherry-red glow of the slumbering earth breathes out its massive, ancient heat. 

I know that one day, my legs will shake when I attempt the climb. My hydraulic strength will wane. When that time comes, I will not force it. I will simply drift downward, descending the iron lattice for the final time. I will find a warm, rotting crook in the iron where the vines are thickest, fold my legs beneath me, and let my great, diaphragmatic breaths slow to a halt. I look forward to it with a profound, chemical serenity. I will give my oiled canvas skin to the violet spores. I will give my water to the humid air. I will wait in the quiet dark, a willing digested morsel, swallowed happily by the slow, sleeping god.
