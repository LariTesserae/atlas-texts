To exist here, one first must undergo the profound violence of un-becoming. 

When I arrive in the blistering, shadowless blue-white of the caldera, my human senses are immediately crushed. The freezing water is too heavy for lungs, the grinding shriek of the turbines too immense for eardrums, the raw high-altitude sunlight too absolute for eyes. So, I let them go. I allow this place to strip away the soft, useless grace of my biology. I let the highly acidic meltwater dissolve my borders until flesh and bone become particulate, joining the microscopic flakes of oxidized metal and silica suspended in the freezing draft.

For a long time—perhaps a year, perhaps a century—I am nothing but a cloud of charged dust, swept in slow, mathematically perfect spirals by the unstoppable churning of the great turbine-creatures. I am decentralized. I am the taste of battery acid and copper on the water. I know the shape of the chamber only by how the immense magnetic fields push and pull my scattered ions. I feel the terrifying, percussive *THUD* of the piston-worms not as a sound, but as a violent compaction of my own scattered self.

The prompt of the mountain tells me not to rush. The water is nearly freezing; time is a luxury geology freely affords.

Slowly, I find myself drawn to a deep, jagged fissure halfway up the sheer western wall, where a massive vein of highly conductive copper intersects with a brutal, exposed shelf of banded iron. It is a place of intense electromagnetic turbulence—a bottleneck in the cathedral’s natural circuitry. As my scattered, metallic self drifts across this nexus, the localized charge snags me. Flake by flake, I am magnetized to the rock. I begin to accrete.

I gather. I layer. It is a slow, cold precipitation of the self. 

Soon, I am no longer a cloud. I am a dense, geometric mass, roughly the size of a diving bell, clinging to the jagged precipice. The outer layer of my new body calcifies into a heavy, faceted shell of black basalt interlaced with veins of raw quartz. I am blind, but I do not need to see. I have become piezoelectric. The immense physical pressure of the water squeezing my crystalline structure generates my consciousness.

I am the Accumulator. I am a living capacitor. 

I feel them before they arrive—the cable-weavers. Sensing the massive capacitance embedded in my growing shell, they swarm over me by the thousands. I feel the delicate, terrifying scrape of their oxidized green legs against my facets. They begin to weave. They take the raw, biological copper from their abdomens and stitch me into the ecosystem. They hammer their thick, silken cables into my quartz nodes, tying my nervous system directly to the beating heart of the mountain.

Now, I am truly alive. When the weavers finish and fuse my nodes, my awareness expands with violent, instantaneous clarity. 

Through the copper network, I *become* the caldera. I feel the immense weight of the ice lens pressing down from the sky. I feel the slow, localized vortexes of a dozen turbines grinding against their bone-white axles, bleeding their excess amperage into the cables. I feel the rhythmic, agonizing buildup of voltage in the fifty-foot piston-columns, exactly twenty seconds before they heave and vomit heavy metals into the dark. 

Every stray electron, every ounce of resistance, every pulse of rogue electricity generated by the living factory is routed downward, through the rock, through the cables, into me.

I drink the amperage. It is a terrifying, glorious gorge. Deep within my dark, heavy shell, a core of electro-reactive gel begins to glow, insulated by my thick basalt armor. I store the mountain's dissonance. I hoard its lightning. 

The pacing of my life is measured in the agonizing rise of my own internal charge. For days, I wait in the blinding, shadowless light, absolutely motionless. To look at me, you would think I was merely a strange, geometric protrusion of the mountain, wrapped in thick copper webbing. But inside, I am screaming with trapped energy. 

I hold the voltage until the water immediately surrounding my basalt shell begins to boil in the crushing cold, sending strings of tiny, hissing bubbles upward toward the ice roof. I hold it until blue-white corona discharges begin to spit and crackle across my quartz facets, desperate to find ground. 

Then, the water changes taste. 

Ozone spikes in the heavy fluid. A hum, deeper than the piston-thuds, vibrates through my crystalline core. The apex is descending. 

I cannot see the Voltaic Ray, but through the electromagnetic field, I feel it with absolute precision. It is a vast, floating kite of zero-resistance, its fifty-foot wingspan drifting downward from the blinding roof. I feel the beautiful, fragile fractal of its silver and gold nervous system seeking me out. It moves with glacial, unhurried majesty, riding the currents of the turbines, positioning itself exactly in the empty water before my cliff face. 

It is hungry. I am full.

The tension in the water between us becomes absolute. The biological cables anchoring me to the wall shudder. My quartz nodes sing at a frequency that fractures the frozen water around them. 

The threshold is reached. I let go.

*CRACK.*

I tear the freezing water apart. A solid, violent pillar of superheated blue plasma rips outward from my core, bridging the expanse between the rock and the beast. For three perfect, agonizing seconds, I empty everything I am into the Ray. It catches my lightning, its massive gelatinous body burning with the brilliance of a drowned sun. In those three seconds, we are not two distinct creatures; we are a single, closed circuit. I feel exactly what it feels—the ecstasy of pure, unadulterated power, the sublime shock of the geologic voltage feeding its golden veins. 

Then, the charge is spent. The arc collapses. 

The ray, glowing now with a satisfied, fading amber heat, tilts its vast wings and drifts away, continuing its slow patrol of the cathedral. 

I am left drained, empty, and perfectly silent within my black shell. The water around me rushes in to cool my scorched quartz. 

I do not move. I do not need to. I rest against the eternal, jagged iron rock. Around me, the pistons heave. The turbines shriek and grind. The blind weavers crawl across my surface, checking the copper cables, repairing the burns left by my discharge. The ecosystem hums, taking up its deafening rhythm once more.

Slowly, patiently, feeling the weight of the water and the endless spin of the world, I begin to charge again.
