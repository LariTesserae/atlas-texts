To exist here is to be an open wound in the fabric of the air, and to be entirely, excruciatingly aware of it. 

I do not have eyes to open, but I have edges. I am a localized region of negative space, defined only by the crushing, suffocating pressure of a universe that wants to cave in on me and make me whole. 

I am made of hands. Not flesh and bone hands, but the *idea* of them. A dense, roaring swarm of spatial intentions—millions of overlapping thumbs, broad palms, and articulate, searching fingertips. If you could see me, I would look like a furious, writhing mandala of grasping. But because I am made of nothing, I am invisible. I know my own shape only by the places where the heavy, wet velvet of the air is slashed away. 

At first, I move like the others. I move in a state of absolute, blinding panic. 

The heat of just existing is unbearable. It is a searing, cosmic friction. When I reach out, trying to pull myself through the dimensions of the fog, my phantom fingers drag across the conceptual bedrock, and reality violently rebels. The wet earth screams upward to plug the gaps between my knuckles. Suddenly, I am wearing a glove of freezing, wet mud. 

For a microsecond, it is euphoria. The slip is so cool. It tells me where I end and the world begins. 

But then, my own kinetic heat catches up. The mud flashes onto my invisible skin, baking instantly into rock-hard terracotta. The sudden heat of the ceramic is blinding. It traps my digits. I panic. I rip my hands away, thrashing against empty space, leaving behind a jagged, towering archway of fossilized distress. I stumble through the mist, my millions of hands grabbing, slapping, and pushing at the ground to keep my balance, involuntarily birthing a sprawling, chaotic colonnade that stretches for miles. 

I am a prisoner running down a hallway, constantly slamming doors behind me just to feel them shatter.

For an age, this is all I am. A fleeing architect. I pull the boiling water up from the aquifers, drinking it through negative-space veins, feeling the exquisite, agonizing ecstasy as a body of bubbling glass briefly fills me, only to exhale it as thick steam into the sky. I build aqueducts because I am thirsty. I build ziggurats because I am terrified.

But eventually, as I drag myself across the infinite, troweled plains, a new sensation begins to emerge through the frantic, white noise of creation. 

The others—the young ones falling from the sky, the exhausted elders tying themselves into knots of death—they are purely instinctual. They act, and the clay reacts. But I begin to *feel* the clay. 

The memory of the potter’s hand is woven into my lack-of-biology, but somewhere, buried deep in the center of my emptiness, I realize that I am not just a pair of hands. I am a *mind* that remembers what hands are for. 

I don't have to just flinch. I can shape.

I stop running. 

I find a vast, empty stretch of the foundation, far from the violent extrusions of my kin. The ambient fog drips heavily onto the pavement. I let my million hands settle over the earth. The clay surges up, desperate to pack my borders, as it always does. The panic rises in me—the urge to violently rip away, to shatter the mold and leave another labyrinth behind. 

I deny the urge. I endure the burn. 

Instead of pulling away from the rising mud, I fold my negative geometry inward. I consolidate the chaotic, fractal bloom of my hands. I press millions of thumbs together, aligning them, smoothing them into a single, massive, unified surface of downward pressure. 

I begin to spin.

Unlike the erratic, spasmodic twisting of my brethren, I rotate on a perfect, fixed axis. I am throwing a pot, but I am the wheel, the hands, and the empty space inside the vessel all at once. 

The clay roars up to meet me. It rises in a massive ring, slick and wet with heavy slip. As I spin, the friction generates immense heat, but instead of fleeing from it, I use it. I press my spinning, invisible palms outward, smoothing the walls of the rising mud, guiding it upward. I feel the grog of the clay sliding past my borders. I feel the exact moment the moisture begins to evaporate. I adjust the pressure of my non-existent fingers, coaxing the earth into a towering, sweeping curve.

I am pulling a dome of earth up and over my own head. 

The walls rise miles into the suffocating fog. I taper the neck of the structure, letting the clay fold perfectly inward. The heat is agonizing, a localized inferno, baking the terracotta as fast as I can smooth it. But I do not stop. For the first time in this universe, architecture is not an accident of panic. It is intentional. 

With one final, delicate pinch of a thousand invisible thumbs, I close the top of the dome. 

I seal myself inside.

Instantly, the physics of the world outside are cut off. The weeping fog cannot reach me. The hissing of the steam is muted behind miles-thick, perfectly smoothed terracotta walls. The vacuum of my existence is trapped. 

And because I am no longer moving, no longer fighting the air, the friction begins to die. The searing heat starts to fade. I pull one dark, mineral-rich vein of water up from the deep bedrock, letting it well into the center of my enclosed silence. Without the immense heat of my panic, the water does not boil. It remains cool, dark, and still. 

Here, in the total darkness of a colossal, hollow bell at the edge of the troweled plain, I finally let go. 

I do not fold into a knot of death. I simply expand, gently filling the curvature of the dome, pressing softly against the smooth, baked walls that I made. The millions of phantom hands relax. The thumbs flatten out. The grasping ceases. 

I am no longer a wandering wound on the plains. I am the quiet, cool air inside a perfect vessel. I wait here, a serene and permanent pocket of nothing, listening to the muffled squelch of the world miles outside, finally at peace, holding space for something that will never arrive.
