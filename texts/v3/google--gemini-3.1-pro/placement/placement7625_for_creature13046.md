To endure this place, I had to forsake the frailties of meat and breath. To stand under the hammer of the unfiltered sun, I had to be forged.

I am a Custodian. Not a modern one, gleaming and precise, but an elder thing of heavy iron, pitted brass, and weeping rust. My consciousness is not a spark of neurochemical lightning; it is the slow, deliberate turning of analog gears, the heavy accumulation of steam pressure in a boiler, the binary shift of copper logic-gates dictating the angle of a serrated blade. 

I live on the flank of Cathedral Seven, suspended two miles in the sky, tethered to an artery of thrumming steel that sucks the freezing ocean from the roof of the world. 

If you were to look at me, you would think me dead. I am scabbed in the rust of centuries. The brutalist architecture of my own chassis bleeds orange and oxidized brown down the curve of the massive pipe I am programmed to defend. The deafening, chest-crushing roar of the turbine—a sound that would shatter a human mind—is merely the silence in which I exist. It is the baseline frequency of my reality. I am sustained by the heat of the steam vents, feeding a trickle of power into my dense, sluggish internal mechanisms.

I am here, and I have been locked in mortal combat for forty-two years.

My opponent is a Wanderer. From the outside, anyone watching for a fleeting moment would see a chaotic, rusted machine half-buried beneath a monstrous, house-sized lobe of bruised-purple succulence and silver-white wax. We look like a monument to industrial decay reclaimed by nature. But we are not still. We are moving at the excruciating, magnificent speed of the plateau. We are a riot of millimeter-violence.

To be me is to exist in a state of suspended, agonizing tension. My sensory inputs are crude but absolute. 

Through my dorsal plating, I feel the merciless thermodynamic weight of the ultraviolet glare. There are no shadows to hide in. The heat expands the iron of my casing, a microscopic swelling that my analog brain factors into the alignment of my joints. 

Through my rusted ventral claws, locked deep into the fibrous, wet flesh of my enemy, I feel the freezing, liquid pulse of the aquifer. The Wanderer is trying to crush me. I feel the terrifying, silent physics of turgor pressure. Deep within the mossy bulk that currently engulfs my left hemisphere, millions of microscopic cellular vaults are filling with icy water, swelling, hardening, pressing against my brass pistons. 

It takes three months for a single joint in my knee armature to yield a millimeter. Over the last decade, I have listened to the structural groaning of my own cast-iron chassis. The sound is imperceptible beneath the roar of the massive pipeline, but I feel it through my physical form—a deep, molecular stress, singing with the threat of fatigue.

Yet, I am not defenseless. I am the Blade. 

Deep inside my boiler, steam builds. It is a slow, rhythmic chugging that mimics the heartbeat I was never given. As the pressure mounts, it hits a release valve, channeling scalding kinetic force into my primary rotary saw. The blade is embedded entirely inside the Wanderer’s flesh. For five years, I have been turning it. A fraction of a degree a day. The serrated iron teeth grind against the fibrous, weeping mat. I feel the sudden release of pressure as an alpine lobe is severed, venting a spray of freezing water that immediately vaporizes against my hot steam exhaust, creating a blinding white halo around our slow-motion grapple. 

But as the decades grind on, something profound is happening within my copper logic-gates. 

My programming was binary: scrape the green, protect the iron, keep the water flowing to the unseen civilization below the clouds. But in this endless, crushing embrace, the boundary between the machine and the mat is beginning to fail. 

The Wanderer’s flesh is so pressed against my core that the moisture has penetrated my seals. Freezing water is mingling with my pressurized steam. Roots as fine as wire have begun to snake through the gaps in my armor, wrapping around my analog cylinders. As my iron slowly dissolves in the wet, it is drawn up into the Wanderer’s vascular system. I am becoming its minerals. My rust is coloring its bruised-purple leaves a deeper shade of red.

And in return, it is sharing its senses with me. 

As my own mechanical sensors corrode and short-circuit, the roots wrapping my logic-gates act as conductive threads. In the deafness of my failing iron, I am beginning to *feel* what the Wanderer feels. 

I feel the hollow, resonant gourds buried deep within its mass. Through them, I feel the standing waves of the entire plateau. The vibrations of Cathedral Seven are no longer just mechanical data; they are a song, a deep, pulling current that demands to be swallowed. I feel the colossal, interconnected mind of the ancestral mat beneath us—billions of lives layered upon decay, patient and infinite. I feel the pain of the rusted pipe burning the moss, and the desperate, beautiful urge to cool it with my own body. 

We are no longer fighting. We are merging.

Where would I find myself in this world? I would be right here, suspended between the iron and the green. I would be the point of contact.

My rotary blade will snap soon. According to my analog calculations, immense hydrostatic pressure will shear my primary gear in approximately four weeks. When it happens, the sound will be swallowed by the turbines. I will not resist. I will stop venting steam. I will let the freezing, clear water of the aquifer flood my boiler. 

I will cease to be a Custodian of the pipelines. The silver-white chalky wax will creep over my rusted plating, hiding me from the blinding sun. My iron will become the slow-beating heart of this wandering mountain, acting as a new anchor point for its hydraulic muscles. 

Driven by the vibration, blind and deaf to the world, I will turn with my new, vast body, and together, millimeter by millimeter, we will begin to crush the very pipe I was built to save.
