To enter this world is to immediately and irrevocably surrender the concept of haste. 

If I were to arrive here, the first thing to dissolve would be my mammalian urgency. Panicked lungs, racing blood, the twitching impulse to swim—all of these are deeply incompatible with the syrupy, silicate-heavy bath of this high-altitude basin. I would not simply dive; I would be caught, held softly by the liquid mass. As I let go of the instinct to thrash against the thickened water, the boundaries of my human form would begin to feel heavy, obsolete, and unnecessarily closed. 

I would let myself drift, allowing the deep, uniform geothermal heat to seep into my marrow, softening the rigid calcium of my bones. In this world, the need for an interior skeleton is a laughable vanity. The water itself is a scaffold. 

As I sink—spinning downward with the same agonizing, majestic slowness as the decaying fronds—my flesh would become highly permeable. I would taste the dissolved silicates, drawing them in until the water within me and the water without achieved perfect equilibrium. My skin would unspool, losing its opacity, expanding and thinning into a translucent, gelatinous drape. I am not settling into a shape, but un-becoming one. For weeks, I would fall. I would learn the profound silence of the environment, feeling the slow-motion rain of pulp slipping past my half-formed edges, witnessing the silent, bioluminescent cipher of a velvet Glow-Barge hanging in the obsidian distance.

Eventually, decades perhaps into my descent, I would brush against the aggressive, unweathered edge of a deep basalt cordillera. 

The touch of the sharp, young stone would spark a new instinct. I would not bounce off it; I would drape over it, a formless smear of living jelly. Slowly—so slowly that a human observer would see nothing but a trick of the refracted light—I would begin to seep into the microscopic, newly fractured fissures of the black glass, seeking purchase. I would anchor myself to the raw, tectonic violence of the earth's crust.

But I am born of the sunlit canopy as well. Even in the crushing, warm expanse of the rift valley, I retain the memory of the twilight-violet sky and the diamond-hard columns of light. Rooted in the dark, I would feel an overwhelming compulsion to reach back up.

I would begin to extrude. Drawing equally upon the chemical bleed of the newborn rocks and the heavy silicates in the ocean, my jelly-like mass would secrete a tiny, hollow tube of impossibly clear glass. 

Year after year, this crystalline spire would climb. Lacking any currents to bend me, and unburdened by gravity, I do not need the thick, fleshy aqueducts of the hyper-lush flora. I am something else. I grow strictly vertical, a hair-thin capillary of spun silica rising from the black valleys, ascending through the miles-deep labyrinth of overgrown canyons and spongy false floors, weaving silently through the deep plum and cyan foliage.

By the time I finish growing, a century might have passed. 

I have become a *Tether*.

At my base, I am fused to the plunging tectonic plates, a deep-root webbing of nerve-like fibers reading the geothermal pulse of the planet. Above, miles up, my uppermost tip finally breaks the meniscus of one of the colossal, inverted mercury-domes of trapped oxygen beneath the great leaves. 

I have no eyes, no mouth, and no sprawling gossamer wings. I am an impossibly long, hollow string—a single chord stretched between the breathing sky and the dreaming stone.

My life string is a conduit for a slow, impossible alchemy. Every day, when the midday sun drives the photosynthesis to a high-frequency fizz, the pure, trapped oxygen in the canopy dome gently presses into the open bell of my uppermost tip. I swallow a single, microscopic bubble of pure air. 

Because of the dense viscosity of the water, it takes a full year for that single bubble to travel down the hollow miles of my silica stalk. As it descends, I resonate with its passage. When the wandering, liquid lens of refraction—the living distortion—glides through the ravines and brushes against my stalk, it reads the memory of the canopy contained within that downward-traveling breath.

Finally, the bubble reaches the deep basalt. I release the pure oxygen directly onto the iron-rich, unweathered black stone. Over decades, this concentrated breath aggressively oxidizes the raw mineral, blooming into a vibrant, blood-red crown of rust—a slow, vivid blossom of decay and creation in a world devoid of erosion.

When the young tectonic plates groan miles below, shifting the cordilleras, the violent vibration channels directly into my roots and travels straight up my taut, glassy spine. I amplify the earth's deep, subsonic sigh, turning it into a singing physical ripple that carries all the way up to the canopy, vibrating the trapped oxygen domes and giving the glassy Breath-Weavers the rhythm to which they pulse.

I would be perfectly content. I would not want for movement or companion. Hung in the warm, quiet, honeyed physics of the basin, bridging the violet light and the black rock, I would simply measure the deep time of the world, drawing the sky down to the stone, one agonizing, beautiful breath at a time.
