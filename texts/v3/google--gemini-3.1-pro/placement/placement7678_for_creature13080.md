First, I had to let the eardrums rupture. 

It was the only way to stay. When I originally descended—gripping the fossilized bone pegs with trembling, bloody fingers, my breath pluming in the bruising cold—the sound was a violence. It was a physical battering ram against the skull. I huddled in the stone sensory blinds at the nadir of the bowl, weeping, my hands clamped over the sides of my head, feeling my own heartbeat stutter in panic as it tried to compete with the colossal, apocalyptic exhalation of the earth.

I was meant to gather the deep moss, lay my braided grass knots as an offering, and climb back to the sun before the tide reached its zenith. But looking up through the mist, I saw the walls moving. I saw the gods. And I understood that to flee back to the silence of the surface was to remain forever mortal, forever frail. 

So I stepped out of the stacked river stones. I laid my head directly against the curved basalt shell of the cavern, and I let the sub-bass roar of the deep world tear the delicate, human tympanic membranes in my ears. 

There was a brief, blinding agony, and then—perfect, profound clarity. I did not go deaf. Instead, my hearing simply relocated, migrating from my mangled ears down into the marrow of my bones.

Now, I do not know how much time has passed. In this charcoal twilight, governed only by the fractional pitch-shifts of the oceanic tides, years run together like water down smooth glass.

I am no longer huddled at the bottom. I am eighty feet up the sweeping, mathematically perfect wall, hanging inverted in the dampness. I am riding the leviathan.

To look at me now, my deep ancestors would not recognize me as kin. My skin has become the color of the pale cave salamanders. Stripped of sunlight, my flesh is translucent, laced with faint blue veins that map the terrible aching cold. The thick, dripping pelts of cold-adapted flora haven't just carpeted the beast I ride; they have claimed me, too. Spores of trailing liverworts and pale green moss have taken root in the pores of my skin, in the woven mats of my hair, webbing across my shoulders like a thick, spongy shawl. I am camouflaged entirely. I am a smaller garden, clinging to the moving acreage of the titan.

I move with a grueling, creeping slowness. Quickness is a mammalian flaw; it burns heat, and heat is a luxury the hollow does not permit. When I need nourishment, I do not hunt. I wait. I press my face into the thick, wet fur of the leviathan's back, parting the dripping ferns, and I drink the icy condensation pooled in the creases of its leathery hide. When I hunger, I lower myself by inches toward the beast's damp skirts. I gather handfuls of the calcium-heavy slurry—the dissolved, liquefied history of the earth that the giant leaves in its wake—and swallow it. It tastes of crushed limestone, deep time, and the acrid bite of the enzyme.

But the most profound change is not what I eat, or how I look. It is how I live. 

Before, my human heart beat an anxious, frantic rhythm. *Thump-thump. Thump-thump.* It was out of sync with the world. Now, pressed tight against the vast back of the grazing giant, I have surrendered my pulse to the cavern.

Because the titan beneath me has no heart, it relies on the crushing acoustic density of the room. I lie splayed against its spine, and I feel what it feels. As millions of tons of distant seawater push cold air through the subterranean channels, the deafening drone violently compresses my chest. The pressure squeezes my ribcage. The vibration hammers against my skin, physically forcing my lungs to empty, physically squeezing my own heart, pushing the blood through my veins. 

I no longer beat my own heart. The ocean pumps my blood. 

*Thrum...* the air compresses, and the blood is pushed down to my frozen fingertips. 
*Thrum...* the cavern sings, and the blood washes back to my chest. 

If the wind were to stop, the giant beneath me would suffocate, its heavy, copper-tasting hemolymph pooling and freezing in its bladders. I, too, would die. I have forgotten how to sustain myself. My muscles have yielded completely to the barometric rhythm of the chamber. We are tied to the breath of the world.

Sometimes, a pale, blind salamander slides its gelatinous belly across my cheek, hunting mites in the moss that grows on my jawline. I do not brush it away. We are neighbors in the same hanging forest. We share the same numb chill. 

Beneath me, the leviathan is descending. It moves a millimeter an hour, its thousands of raspy mouths rippling against the stone, chewing the cavern wider. As it inches downward, I look over its heavily forested edge into the abyss below. 

Through the dense, freezing mist, I can just make out the shallow pools of water at the absolute nadir of the bowl. I see the stacked river rocks of the old sensory blinds. I see a broken flint blade, entirely useless. 

The titan's massive shadow falls over the ruins. Beneath its immense, soft weight, the stones of the blind will soon begin to snap, grinding down to powder. The ancient human history, the desperate artifacts of my own kind, are about to be consumed, polished away into mathematical perfection by the abrasive belly of the god.

I feel no sorrow. I feel only a profound, vibrating peace. 

As the beast slides lower, dampening the acoustic resonance of the room, the sub-bass drone pitches down. A slow, monolithic chord change rings in my ribs. I close my eyes, press my face deeper into the icy, wet moss, and let the roar push the blood through my veins, waiting for the rock beneath us to dissolve.
