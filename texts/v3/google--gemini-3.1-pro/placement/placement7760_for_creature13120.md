To open your eyes in such a place would be to invite a blinding ruin. The sheer, unmitigated violence of the sun, reflecting endlessly off a world of white and pale gold, would immediately teach a human mind that light here is not for seeing. Light is a pressure. Light is a weight. 

And then, there is the sound. 

My first instinct would be physical retraction—to cover my ears, to shrink inward, curling into a tight, defensive knot upon an exposed, scalloped ledge. But the sound cannot be kept out. The roar of ten million simultaneous cascades is not something you hear; it is something you endure on a cellular level. It rattles the teeth, liquefies the sense of balance, and aggressively scrubs away the internal monologue. Words—human words, frantic, separate, and small—are utterly annihilated by the physical weight of the region-wide chord. 

To remain on the surface as a creature of flesh, blood, and distinct individuality would be to fracture. The self cannot hold its shape inside a vibrating mass of absolute white noise. Panic would quickly give way to exhaustion, and exhaustion to a profound, overwhelming desire to be absorbed, to find a frequency where I might survive the onslaught. 

I would not try to walk across the brutal, serrated labyrinth. I would surrender to the hydrology. 

Letting slip my grip on the brittle rim, I would fall into the rushing, brilliant veil of the current. I would be carried over weeping curtain-walls and shallow flutes, battered not by malice, but by perfect, relentless geometry, until the water finally plunged me down, down into one of the deepest, oldest basins. 

Here, in the stinging, chemical cyan depths, the world changes. 

The freezing water acts as an anesthetic, a regional coolant that shocks the frantic mammalian heat from the blood. Far below the hissing, atomized surface, the violet and blue light is gentle, filtered through layers of dense calcium carbonate. More importantly, the crushing white noise of the cataracts above is transposed. By the pressure of the depths, the roar is muffled into a deep, pervasive, subsonic thrum. It becomes a slow, rhythmic cello note that vibrates cleanly through the chest. A physical cradle of sound. 

I would drift to the floor and settle against a sweeping curve of pale gold stone. It would take a long time for my senses to understand exactly what I was touching. The rock is textured with fluted deposits, but its underlying geometry is too deliberate, too perfect to be purely geological. I would realize I had pressed my back against the massive, tucked limbs of an entombed Curator. It is one of the ancients, locked into the bedrock, wrapped in a shroud of white rime and permanent stasis. 

Resting in the lap of the buried architect, my human perception of time would finally unspool entirely. 

The days above pass in a blinding flash of unending glare, but down here, in the cold and the blue, there are no days. There is only the hum. I would notice that the frantic rhythm of my heart—so wholly out of place in this eternal equilibrium—had begun to widen. It elongates, dragging its tempo, stretching the space between beats until my pulse attempts to match the deep, rumbling vibration in the stone beneath me. 

I am not dying. The hyper-saturated water is interacting with me, reading the dissonance of my soft flesh and correcting it. 

I would watch my hands spread out over the mineralized armor of the sleeper. They no longer look like hands. The calcium and silica in the water begin to wick into my soft tissues, but instead of turning me into a rigid, hollow statue, my body reacts differently to the chemistry. It unravels. 

I feel my fleshy borders blurring, thinning into a gossamer, translucent gel. The shape of a human is too clumsy, too isolated for the absolute harmony of this plateau. My body dissolves into a network of fine, bioluminescent filaments, glowing the same bright, electric blue as the deepest pools. 

I am losing my center, but rapidly expanding my reach. I wick into the porous travertine. I seep into the microscopic channels left by the archaea, threading myself into the fossilized rime that coats the sleeping Curator. I slip into the cavernous, hollow spaces within its biological armor, finding the tuning-fork resonance of its buried, porous marrow. 

And then, I creep outward. 

I weave through the jagged stone. Through the scalloped rims. Through the weeping curtain-walls. As I stretch into the dark, wet interior of millions of terraces, brushing against the undersides of the ledges, I feel the agonizingly slow crawl of the limpets, sensing their microscopic scraping as tiny flutters against my extended nerves. 

I am becoming the subterranean nervous system of the labyrinth. A vast, fragile mycelium of pure acoustic sensitivity. 

Above me, a living Curator wades through a shallow flood, taking weeks to raise its crystalline chisel and scrape against an offending rim. I feel the scrape exactly. It does not hurt; it is a sharp, sudden spike of brilliant clarity that rings through my vast, spiderwebbed consciousness. I register the subtle change in the flow rate. I feel a discordant hiss resolve into a perfect, region-wide hum. 

On the surface, the living giants are solitary, blinded by the glare, communicating only through slow, deep pulses dragged against the resonant rims. But down here, woven through the bedrock, I am the thing that catches and carries those pulses. I link the entombed ancients—millions of them across the vast plateau—creating a continuous, living acoustic lattice. I become the connective tissue of the harmony. 

I no longer have a voice, and I no longer need one. I stretch across miles of calcified architecture, perfectly still, perfectly tuned, cradling the roaring world from the inside out, listening forever.
