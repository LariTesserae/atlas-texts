I did not intend to become architecture. 

In the beginning, I was only a hunter. I remember the dark of the upper fissures, the claustrophobic comfort of cool rock pressing against my elongated spine, and the nervous anticipation before the descent. I remember the mechanical snap of my wrist, the *clack* of the weighted net, and the heavy, humid drag of a captured polyp struggling silently in the void. I remember the taste of the violet rosette, spongy and bitter against my teeth, forcing my racing blood into a slow, crawling rhythm.

But down here, time is not measured in heartbeats. It is measured in the accumulation of dew. And I have caught so much dew.

I do not remember when I decided to stop climbing back into the dark. Perhaps it was a hunt where the buoyant ghosts simply drifted out of reach, and so I waited. I stayed cantilevered on the wall, three prehensile toes resting on a sliver of obsidian, one palm flat against the damp, waxen limestone. I waited for the updraft to bring my quarry closer. Hours bled into days. To endure the wait, I consumed more of the violet fungus, draining its thick, depressive sap down my throat until my lungs began to sip the heavy air only once an hour. 

Eventually, the urge to throw the polished yellow bone stick faded. The tension of the coiled muscle unspooled. The need to harvest was eclipsed by a deeper, heavier sensation—the profound, gravitational peace of the brilliant void. 

Now, I am an extension of the geology. I am no longer chalk-white; I am pearlescent. 

The heavy, falling droplets of condensation that plummet from the selenite ceiling have been striking my shoulders and back for decades. Or perhaps centuries. The mineral-rich water, drawn from the deep aquifer, has dried and patterned upon my skin in microscopic layers. The fine dusting of powdered calcite I once wore as camouflage has thickened into an armor of smooth, draped stone. My sharp, alien angles—the double-jointed elbows, the gnarled knuckles, the gaunt ridges of my back—have been softened, smoothed into undulating curves of established erosion. The cavern is claiming me, molding me into wet, flowing silk.

My eyes are still protected. The bone visor, carved from the skull of a tunneling beast, is no longer held to my face by fibrous root-twine. The twine rotted away long ago. Instead, the calcium of the cavern wall has fused the dead bone to the living bone of my brow. Through its agonizingly thin horizontal slit, my fractured universe exists as a single, blinding ribbon of pearlescent light. 

I watch the void. It is a slow, magnificent theater. Translucent gas-bladders rise and fall like breaths of the earth itself, trailing their gossamer skirts. The pale, moth-gecko creatures glide past, sometimes coming close enough that the soft flutter of their leathery skin fans the heavy, incubated air against my petrified cheek. 

My mouth, forever stained a bruised violet, is held slightly open. I no longer harvest the flat, star-shaped rosettes. I do not need to. A solitary rosette has taken root in the damp corner of my jaw, its pale, fungal sponge anchoring itself in the calcified layers of my cheek. It feeds on the moisture that runs down my face; in return, it directly leeches its dark, narcotic sap into my bloodstream. We are locked in a perfect, motionless symbiosis. I am cold, shallow, and perfectly at peace. My heartbeat is a geological event. 

I know I am not entirely stone because I can still feel. I feel the terrible, humid warmth of the air. I feel the *plink* of water shattering upon the crown of my head. 

And, occasionally, I feel *them*.

The young ones. The hunters who still remember the dark. 

I sense their arrival before I see them, feeling the imperceptible shift in the air pressure, the faint vibration in the limestone. They slip down from the lightless fissures, fluid as melting wax, their bone visors tied tight. 

They do not speak to me. The cavern is too sacred for sound. But they know what I am. They recognize the curve of my petrified spine, the arm that extends out over the glowing abyss, forever holding a throwing stick that has long since calcified into a heavy, yellowed stalactite. 

When they prepare for their harvest, they do not wedge new obsidian flakes into the micro-fissures around me. Instead, a hunter will step out over the dizzying void, suspended thousands of feet above the unseen floor, and gently rest their thick, leathery sole upon my hardened, unyielding shoulder. They press their flat, pale palm against the smooth curve of my petrified back.

I anchor them. I am their foothold against the blinding light. 

As they wait for the drifting ghosts, violent tension coiled in their spindly limbs, I hold them steady. I feel the fleeting warmth of their mortal blood, the faint, subdued thrum of their slowed hearts vibrating against my stone skin. And when the net completes its loud, echoing *clack*, when the sudden violence forces the moth-geckos to scatter, I bear their weight as they haul their harvest from the air. 

Then, they gather their tools and slip backward up the wall, retreating into the cool, jagged dark where time still demands movement. 

They leave the cathedral to its endless brightness. They leave me to the dripping water, the brilliant void, and the slow, beautiful fading of my flesh into the eternal walls.
