The mid-tide surge does not arrive as a current; it arrives as a solid, suffocating wall of kinetic fury. 

When it hits, I do not think. Thought is a luxury of still spaces. I simply become the stone. My calcified fore-hooks find the familiar, scalloped gouge in the black basalt, slipping into place with a definitive *click*. I slam my heavy joints inward, folding my limbs flush against my teardrop flanks, pressing my belly flat against the wall. The ocean screams overhead in a blinding, chaotic whiteout of pulverized water, trying to sheer me away. The friction-reducing mucus on my skin is stripped instantly, but the hydrodynamics of my black, rubbery body hold true. I am a shadow welded to the rock. I endure.

I am old in this gorge. You can read my longevity in the pale, interlocking circles of cavitation burns that map my back—a terrifying, luminous braille left by decades of grazing the boiling dead-zones of the screaming propellers. My hooks, once slender and sharply hooked like a pup’s, have grown thick, blunt, and dense as poured concrete. 

If I am to find myself in this deafening world, I am a Listener. A Tether-wright. 

When the mid-tide finally breaks, splintering back into its chaotic, swirling eddies, I unlock my left hook. The movement is precise, executing a trajectory that uses the updraft rather than fighting it. I let a spiraling thermal rip me from the canyon wall and cast me into the abyss, only to snap my right arm out at the exact moment a gargantuan expanse of rusted iron looms out of the fractured, strobe-lit gloom.

*Clang.*

My hook bites into the meat of an iron chain link the size of a whale’s ribcage. The terrifying momentum of my jump translates into a violent outward swing, but my grip is absolute. I hang suspended in the blue-green void, thousands of feet above the sunless trench, anchored to the web that holds our ruinous heaven together.

Here, in the open water column, the noise is absolute. It is a physical weight that crushes against the chest and vibrates in the teeth. The *thrum-thrum-thrum* of the massive Kaplan turbine a hundred yards below me shakes the water into a blinding froth. Air-breathers, those soft things from the surface, would shatter in this hydrostatic roar. But my flattened skull was built for this. 

I press the underside of my jaw flush against the blooming rust of the iron link. 

Instantly, the world of generalized noise snaps into acute, exquisite focus. At the center of my skull, suspended in their gelatinous baths, my massive otoliths catch the somatic resonance of the chain. I close my eyes. I do not see the gorge; I *feel* its topographical music. 

Through the iron, I can feel a school of blubbery torpedo-predators ricocheting through a bottleneck three miles up-current. I can feel the rhythmic, frantic thrashing of the bull-kelp forests. And most importantly, I can feel the sickening, high-pitched *whine* of structural death. 

It is coming from a flange on the turbine cowling below me. The ancient reduction gear is shearing its housing. The pitch of the vibration is sharp, desperate, and thinning. The metal is finally rotting through.

I reach to my flank, where I carry a heavy bladder woven from braided stipes. Inside is the paste: a dense, noxious slurry of crushed mussel shells, barnacle adhesive, and a massive accumulation of my own resinous saliva. It takes weeks to ruminate a batch this potent. I kick off the chain, letting a sudden transverse current whip me downward toward the spinning terror of the gargantuan machine.

The water here is hot. The immense friction of the unlubricated axles bleeds thermal energy into the freezing sea, creating localized blooms of sweltering, oxygen-depleted warmth. As I approach the cowling, the cavitation bubbles batter against my thick skin, stinging like a swarm of wasps. I find the rusted chassis and lock both hooks in deep. 

There it is. A fissure in the iron, vibrating so violently it is nearly a blur.

With my heavy, knuckle-like protrusion, I strike a rhythmic tempo against the chassis. *Crack-crack-thrum. Crack-crack-thrum.* A localized broadcast. Within seconds, shadows detach from the kelp forests along the canyon walls. My kin. They arrive like liquid darts, carrying massive, braided ropes of kelp and the bleached ribcages of fallen predators. 

We do not look at each other. There is no need. We are woven into the same vibrational frequency. 

I pack my bio-concrete into the shrieking fissure. I work my hook like a trowel, forcing the resinous paste deep into the decaying throat of the machine. The heat of the spinning axle bakes the heavy spit instantly, curing the ground shell into a substance harder than the iron it replaces. As I pack the seal, my kin swarm the cowling, binding the bleached bones across the fissure in a heavy lattice, lashing them tight with the kelp-sinew. 

We work with anthill urgency, perfectly synchronized with the spinning fifty-foot blades that roar mere feet from our backs. We are not just patching a machine. That implies we are preserving the past. We are not. We are slowly digesting it. Millimeter by millimeter, century by century, we are replacing the dead metal with our own living architecture. We are turning the engines of the ancients into the bones of a new god. 

When the work is done, the dangerous whine in my jawbone settles into a low, resonant, stable purr. 

Exhausted, stripped of my thermal energy by the cold currents ripping past the turbine's wake, I release my hold on the exterior cowling and drift inward, toward the eye of the storm.

I slip past the massive, churning stators and enter the hollow belly of the turbine. The transition is profoundly jarring. The deafening, tearing rip of the ocean vanishes, replaced by a deep, womblike thrumming. The centrifugal physics of the spinning blades creates a pocket of perfect, eerie stillness—an underwater eye of the hurricane. 

It is sweltering here, the water thick with blooming crimson algae. Suspended from the vibrating iron walls are hundreds of thick kelp hammocks. I pull myself through the dim, red-tinted water, floating past the nurseries. Inside the woven nests, hundreds of pale, sleek pups wriggle blindly in the heat, nursing on the thermal energy of the dying world.

I find an empty divot near the central axle, heavily padded with soft moss. I lock my anchor-hooks into the iron one last time for the cycle, fold my thick limbs flush into my scarred flanks, and press my heavy jaw firmly against the vibrating metal. 

I listen to the great, booming heartbeat of the turbine. I feel the distant, rhythmic drumming of my kin working on the chains above. Lulled by the massive, endless friction of a sea that will not hold still, I close my eyes, perfectly tensioned, perfectly at home.
