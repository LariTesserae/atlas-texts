I do not know the world through light or shadow. I know it through the geometry of dampness, the taste of broken fibers, and the deep, resonant drumming of the wood. 

For countless cycles, my universe has been a vertical topography of smells and vibrations. To traverse the gorge is to read a text written in condensation and sap. When I crawl upside-down along the underside of a fibrous vault, I am reading the health of the city. My belly reads the temperature; my sprawling, boneless limbs read the tension of the branches spanning the abyss; my star-shaped mouth tastes the chemical distress of a micro-fracture weeping invisible sugars into the mist.

I am not separate from the walls I crawl upon. The sap I drink is the same fluid that pulses through the turquoise fungal lamps and the hollow aqueducts. The rhythm of my six splayed limbs matches the slow thermal exhale of the compost furnace far below. We are a single, sprawling consciousness, distributed across a million creeping bodies and a billion waxy leaves.

But lately, my perception of time has begun to shift. 

The frantic, fluid grace of my youth is leaving me. I used to scuttle down into the warm, amniotic depths of the undercroft, where the air smells of sweet rot and new growth, vibrating against the pitcher-pods to soothe my unborn siblings. I used to race up the dripping spiral staircases of shelf-fungi to seal a splintering buttress in seconds, my throat purring loudly as I regurgitated the warm, curing resin.

Now, I am so heavy. 

The antifreeze mucus that once kept me frictionless and permeable has grown sluggish. It lingers on my skin, reacting with the freezing air, hardening. Where I used to be the slick, pale color of wet porcelain, I am now mottled with charcoal and iron-gray. My epidermis is thickening into bark. Even the way I hear the city is altering. The high-pitched *tap-tap-tap* of the dripping condensation is becoming muffled, but the deep, groaning tectonic shifts of the banyans are ringing in my core with a painful, magnificent clarity. 

My blood is becoming sap. My flesh is remembering the soil. 

Through the damp air, a scent cuts through the quiet—sharp, metallic, and devoid of life. *Ozone. Frost. Bare granite.* 

Far above, at the very roof of the world, something has torn. The brutal wind of the high-altitude peaks has found a weakness in our armored leaves and is pouring a deadly, freezing jet into the sanctuary of the gorge. Down below, the thermal updrafts shudder. The compost furnace flickers in its slow burn. The city shivers. 

I do not think. I do not decide. The imperative blooms in my fading nervous system like a chemical fire. 

I begin the climb. 

It is an agonizing ascent. With every few feet, my joints pop and grind, resisting the motion. My limbs, once as flexible as water, are stiffening into rigid boughs. I pass younger siblings sliding down the charcoal trunks. They pause as they sense my chemistry, lowering their star-shaped faces in reverence or perhaps just biological recognition. They scrape their bellies against the bark to clear a path for me. They know what I am becoming. 

Up, up into the biting, bruising cold. The mist here doesn't weep; it crystalizes. It coats everything in a fine dust of rime. The bioluminescence fades, replaced by the crushing, empty chill of the sky. 

I reach the perimeter. A massive, overlapping bulkhead of armored bromeliad leaves has been wrenched apart by the sheer force of a recent storm, exposing a jagged, yard-wide tear. The biting wind from the roof of the world howls through it, carrying shards of unweathered granite. 

The cold hits me, and paradoxically, it feels like an embrace. It is the final catalyst. Whatever animal heat is left inside me rushes to my core. 

I crawl out over the terrified, shivering leaves. My heavy, bark-covered body is positioned directly in the throat of the void. I stretch left, digging three stiffening claws deep into the rigid fiber of one wall. I stretch right, splaying my remaining limbs to hook onto the torn edge of the other. I am a living stitch. I am suspended over the dizzying, mist-filled drop of the city beneath me, my back taking the full, violent force of the alpine wind.

I lock my joints. They fuse with a sound like splintering wood. I cannot retract them now, even if I wished to. 

I unfold my face one last time. The star-shaped orifice opens, not to feed, but to plant. I plunge the pale, fibrous rootlets deep into the exposed, weeping cambium of the broken city wall. 

The connection is made. 

It is not a death. When the freezing air finally penetrates my skin and the hormonal trigger fires, petrifying my internal fluids, my mind does not extinguish. It expands.

As my flesh hardens into rigid, load-bearing fiber, my consciousness drains out of my singular, heavy body and flows through the rootlets into the wall. I feel myself rushing down the vascular pillars. I feel myself in the terraced balconies, spreading out across the iron-hard moss floorboards. I feel the warmth of the compost furnace miles below, and the gentle sliding of a thousand younger siblings crawling across my newly petrified back, layering me with resin, turning my rigid carcass into a permanent, seamless archway against the sky.

I find myself everywhere. I am no longer a creature living *in* the city. 

I am the cold. I am the silence. I am the wall, holding back the storm.
