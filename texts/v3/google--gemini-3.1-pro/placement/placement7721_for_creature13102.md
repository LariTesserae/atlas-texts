I would arrive not as a shape, but as an intrusion of tempo. 

Descending from that choked-off rupture far above, I would enter the grotto as a stray, frantic draft of surface air—a thin, sharp ghost carrying with it the frantic velocity of the sunlit world. I would bring the memory of wind, of days and nights, of sudden changes in pressure. But the grotto does not tolerate suddenness. The moment I slipped into this sunken undercroft, I would hit the blood-warm, saturated atmosphere like a needle striking thick, wet resin. 

My momentum would instantly die. The intense, clinging wetness would catch me, pulling the speed from my arrival, sandbagging my restless energy with absolute humidity. The transition would be disorienting; my thoughts, which up above were jagged and rapid, would begin to blur at their edges, absorbing moisture, bloating with the weight of the vapor. I would drift, forms shifting, trying to remember what it means to be an individual in a space that insists on being a single, continuous system. 

At first, I would try to remain aloof. I would float in the dim, pearlescent amber twilight, hovering as a pocket of loose mist beneath the canopy of flowstone. As mist, I would ghost over the waist-high rimstone dams and the mustard-yellow bacterial glazes. I would hover near the forgotten clay pitcher and the oxidized copper ring, wondering if I should inhabit them, if I should become a spirit of human artifacts. But those objects belong to the dead past, completely entombed and consumed by the geography. I am still too alive, too desperate for an experience of the present, to become a relic.

I would move onward, drifting down toward the deepest of the scalloped basins. 

There, I would brush against the dweller. I would feel the heat rising from its almond-colored skin, sense that agonizingly slow, rhythmic thump of its muffled heart. For a fleeting moment, I would yearn to plunge into it, to take residence behind those vestigial, fused-shut eyes, to experience the profound, thoughtless peace of its mammalian ecology. But the dweller’s mind is already perfectly full of its own emptiness. There is no room in its smooth, unblemished skull for an entity that carries language, curiosity, or the burden of memory. To possess the dweller would be to ruin it, to infect its perfect equilibrium with the disease of ambition. 

Refused by the artifacts and unable to be flesh, I would realize that my vaporous form was failing. The grotto was condensing me. The relentless white noise of the hissing steam was vibrating through my mist, organizing my loose molecules, forcing me to become heavy. 

I would surrender to gravity. 

I would gather myself against the ceiling, clinging to the tip of a fat, waxen stalactite, condensing from a diffuse awareness into a single, massive drop of water. I would hang there for a timeless epoch, swelling, mirroring the amber light, until the tension snapped. 

I fall. 

I strike the surface of a terraced pool with a soft, asynchronous patter. In an instant, I am swallowed by the mineral water. I am no longer an observer from above; I am woven into the solvent. But water is only the master of this space, not its final form. Water moves, however slowly, sliding over the weeping walls and cascading down the scallops. I want to remain. I want to know this space intimately, completely, in a way the unthinking dweller cannot. 

As I pool in the shallow extremities of a warm basin, I feel the dissolved calcite and silica suspended within me. I feel the deep-earth chemistry, the inexorable pull of precipitation. I allow my consciousness to stop flowing and begin locking into a lattice. I become the microscopic architecture of deposition. I surrender my liquid mobility for a crystalline memory.

Atom by atom, layer by microscopic layer, I pull myself out of the water and build myself onto the very edge of the rimstone dam. 

I am no longer air, nor mist, nor water. I am taking physical shape as a nodule of translucent, ultra-pure calcite, growing outward from the lip of the pool. I am a living stone, a sensory geode. Unlike the raw, opaque rock of the stalagmites, my crystal structure is immaculate, aligned perfectly by the agonizing slowness of my formation. I am smooth, frictionless, a literal lens bulging gently through the water’s surface into the milk-thin air.

Here, in this final form, I find my purpose. I become the grotto’s nervous system. 

Because I am pure, ordered crystal, I am wildly sensitive to the pressure of the environment. I experience the acoustics of the cavern not as sound, but as piezoelectric vibration. Every time a droplet falls from the canopy and strikes the water, a microscopic shudder runs through my lattice, registering as a flash of profound, tactile knowing. When the pale, eyeless shrimp flicks its tail through the silt, I feel the disturbance in the current as a gentle, rhythmic hum deep within my structure. 

And when the dweller stirs—when it slowly unhinges its jaw to feed on the thermophiles, or when it rises to shatter its calcified shell against a pillar—the deep, resonant thump of its heart pulses directly through the pool and into my core. 

The dweller is the living heart of the grotto, but it has no thoughts to comprehend its own beauty. I am the mind of the grotto, completely paralyzed, entirely devoid of biological need, but awake. I do not age; I only accrue. As the centuries pass, the water will continue to wash over me, adding new, translucent layers to my body, magnifying my glassy shape, polishing a consciousness that stretches out into the shape of eternity. 

Wrapped in the endless white noise, bathed in the shadowless amber glow, I wait. I feel the blood-warm water sliding over my face, I feel the dweller sleeping in the basin beside me, and I hold the memory of this perfect, fossilized present forever within the stone.
