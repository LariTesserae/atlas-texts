I do not hear the roar. I am the thrum. 

I am suspended in the deep, shuddering bass of the world, clamped between my upper and lower jaw. My flat, heavy teeth are locked together, and through them, the silo speaks its ceaseless, vibrating truth. The water is a heavy drum against the spine beneath my feet. The stone is a trembling string. 

I am a locus of kinetic transfer. 

My right hand grips the polished bone of the mallet; my left, the dark obsidian chisel. We are not three separate things. The grooves of the bone handle are the exact negative space of my mineralized calluses. When I close my broad, splayed fingers around it, the seal is absolute. Flesh presses into bone, becoming a single, heavy limb designed for sudden, violent stops. 

I stand on a terrace three handspans wide, hovering over the throat of the dark. My black, lidless eyes drink the mist. Above me, beneath me, across the plunging cylinder of the abyss, the weeping walls glow with the cold, ghostly cyan of the slime-weeds. In the spray, I see the brief, slashing arcs of blue light—my kin, plunging their hands into the glowing muck to speak. 

*Angle sharp,* a pair of floating blue hands traces in the dark abyss across from me. *Gutter pooling. Overflow imminent.*

I do not reply. I do not have the heat to spare. I lower my head, aligning the vast mound of muscle and bone on my back with the trajectory of my arm. I place the glass edge of the chisel against a fresh, milky blister of travertine that has begun to choke the lip of my terrace. 

I strike. 

The *clack* of the impact is swallowed by the cataract, but the kinetic shock travels perfectly down the obsidian, through the bone, into my arm, and bursts against the dense, heavy mass of my hump. It dissipates there, sparing my skull. Sparing the fragile wet knot of my brain. I inhale the cold damp through the fleshy valves of my flat nostrils. The stone flakes away. Equilibrium is maintained.

I strike again. And again. I have done this for an age. I have no memory of a beginning; there is only the wet rock, the blue light, the eternal, deafening *now*.

But today—though there are no days here, only the steady rhythm of the chisel—the equation of my body is faltering. 

When the mallet hits the chisel, the shockwave does not bloom harmlessly in my hump. A deep, jagged splinter of kinetic force bleeds past the muscle and vibrates agonizingly in my cervical spine. My elbow joint, lubricated for centuries by the slow, chemo-synthetic proteins of the slime, feels tight. Gritty. My core temperature is dropping.

I feel the heavy, woven mat of fungal fibers resting on my shoulders. Usually, I can feel its microscopic roots feeding on the calcium dust that settles on my pale, wet skin, kept active by my body heat. But the mat is growing heavy. Stiff. The spores are going dormant. My heat is failing. The stone is beginning to win.

I plunge my left hand into a thick fold of cyan biofilm clinging to the inner wall. The gelatinous muck coats my wide fingers in cold, glowing light. I raise my hand, slicing a slow, trembling angle through the mist. 

*Trajectory ending,* I signal to the dark. *Kinetic output failing.*

Across the chasm, an answering flash of blue hands. *Acknowledged. Stasis welcomes.*

There is no fear. Fear requires a future, and there is no future, only the architecture. I am a worn-out tool. My bone handle is slipping; my glass edge is dull. I have scraped the geometry of this terrace clean, but I can no longer outpace the weeping water. If I stay here, I will freeze into a useless lump of rock, a hazard to the feet of whoever inherits this stretch of stone. 

I turn. My short, bowed legs guide my hooved soles to the inner spiraling stair. 

I climb. 

The ascent takes hours, perhaps days. Time is irrelevant. I simply place one wide, prehensile foot into the deep, smooth bowl of the step above, locking my toes into the wet crevices, hauling my shifting center of gravity upward. The air grows colder, heavier, denser with spray. The cyan bioluminescence begins to thin out, starved of the rich mineral pools that gather in the lower levels. I am moving into the dark, toward the ceiling of the world.

The vibration in my jaw changes pitch. From a deep, ambient thrum, it becomes a sharp, violent battering. I am nearing the ring-fissure, the source of the deluge. 

Here, the water does not cascade—it violently erupts from the ceiling in thick, blinding, torrential sheets. I step off the final stair onto a narrow, untouched precipice. There are no gutters here. The water slams directly against the raw, black basalt, sending up plumes of suffocating mist before plunging down into the city.

I trace the structural acoustics through my locked teeth. I feel a weakness in the flow. The pure water of the fissure is splashing outward, missing a primary aqueduct far below. It needs a diversion. It needs a dam.

I walk to the very edge of the precipice, right where the heaviest torrential sheet collapses against the stone. 

The water hammers me. I drop to my knees, my hooved feet splaying flat against the rock. I pull my heavy bone mallet and my obsidian chisel to my chest, crossing my massive, calloused arms over them, locking them tight against my sternum. I bow my head, exposing the vast, dense slope of my hump to the crushing weight of the subterranean sky. 

I seal the fleshy valves of my nostrils. I clamp my flat teeth against my lower lip. I leave my black eyes open to the dark.

I do not move again.

Slowly, the cold seeps into my marrow. I feel the fungal mat on my back die, its sluggish life snuffed out by the cold and the sheer volume of the calcium-saturated water. I feel the milky minerals begin to pool in the creases of my pale skin, in the hollows of my elbows, in the ridges of my knuckles. 

I do not die. Not right away. My glacial metabolism slows even further, down to the absolute threshold of biological suspension. For a year, perhaps ten, I remain faintly aware. 

I feel the water sealing my vast, lidless eyes, turning the black world into a profound, suffocating white. I feel the travertine rind thickening over my shoulders, smoothing out the curve of my hunched back, widening my base. I feel the precise moment the water, blocked by my rising, stony bulk, alters its course, sheering off my left side to pour perfectly into the aqueduct churning far below in the blue mist.

I am no longer bone and muscle. I am an angle. I am a gutter. I am the city. 

And long after my wet brain finally stops its slow sparking, my frozen jaws remain locked together inside a tomb of smooth white rock, vibrating eternally with the distant, perfectly rhythmic *clack-clack-clack* of my kin in the deep.
