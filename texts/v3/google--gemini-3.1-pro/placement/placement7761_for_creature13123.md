The grain of the stone speaks to my fingers before my eyes even register its color. 

I am suspended three thousand feet above a sunless, jagged abyss. I do not look down. There is no need; the gravity of the void is a constant pressure against the soles of my felted boots, a familiar weight pulling at the edges of my heavy, woolen layers. I am wedge-anchored to the sheer face of pale, fractured granite on the eastern fault block. Slowly, deliberately, I shift my weight. My thick, bowed legs absorb the movement, pressing my center of gravity into the rock. 

I am a Weaver of the Void. It is my task to tend the great suspension bridges that stitch our sundered world together.

I inhale. The air is violently cold, carrying the sharp, metallic tang of copper. I pull it in through my wide, flattened nose, feeling the dense, frost-rimmed bristles within my nostrils capture the chill, baffling the air until it is warm enough to enter my massive lungs. My barrel chest expands, stretching the tight weave of my undertunic. I do not pant. I take a breath that lasts a minute, filling myself with the thin, precious oxygen, and then I let it out in a slow, controlled exhalation that forms a brief, glittering cloud of ice crystals before the wind shreds it.

Above me, the sky is a bruising, lidless violet. The sun beats down in sheets of unmitigated fury, and I tilt my shoulders to let it strike my bare arms. My slatedark skin welcomes the radiation, drinking the heat, storing it in the thick, micro-fissured flesh. Behind the heavy overhang of my brow, my eyes are narrowed to slits, perfectly shielded from the blinding glare of the ice-fields above. 

With my three middle fingers—blunt, calloused, tipped with flat nails as hard as horn—I grip a natural hairline fracture in the rock face. The strength in my hand is absolute. I could hang my entire bulk from this single point if the wind decided to wake. With my other hand, I pull a massive, braided rope of beast-hair toward the anchor point. The rope is as thick as my own torso, heavy with lanolin and the sheer density of a hundred fleeces spun together. It is the new primary tension line for the lower terrace bridge.

Beside me, sharing the narrow, perilous ledge, is Khol. He is a beast of the high peaks, slab-sided and immense, a walking mountain of matted grey wool and placid endurance. He stands with his cloven hooves perfectly placed on the scant rock. We smell identical—a deep, musky blend of sweet milk, dried sweat, and earth. When the sun slips behind a jagged spire of obsidian basalt and plunges us into instantaneous, freezing black shadow, I do not rush. Rushing is death. I simply lean my broad back against Khol’s massive flank. He shifts, accepting my weight, and his furnace-like internal heat bleeds through my garments, wrapping me in a shared thermal envelope. I reach up and bury my hand in his thick neck-fleece, feeling the slow, rhythmic thunder of his heart. 

*Patience.* It is the only law the mountain recognizes. 

From the wide strap across my chest, I unhook a carving tool. It is made from a human thigh bone, polished to a dull sheen by generations of sweat and fat. Perhaps it belonged to my grandfather; perhaps to a Weaver from three centuries ago. It does not matter. The mountain took their flesh via the great avian scavengers that even now circle as dark flecks against the violet sky, but we kept their strength. 

Using the femur, I pry open the tight weave of the beast-hair rope, slow as a glacier, until I create a gap wide enough to insert the armature. I reach into the leather harness strapped to Khol’s back and pull out a bleached, curving rib of a scavenger-bird, longer than my arm. I slide it into the braided gap, securing the joint with friction and the agonizing tension of the bridge. It slides into place with a hollow *thunk* that I feel in my bones rather than hear in the thin air. 

Suddenly, a brilliant, stabbing flash of light strikes the pale rock just above my head. 

I pause. I turn my head, an inch at a time, looking across the vastness of the tectonic rift. Two miles away, on a terrace carved seamlessly into the facing cliff, a pinprick of blazing light flashes again. It is a Watcher from the western block.

I tilt my heavy body. Hanging flush against my chest is my copper disc, polished this morning with ash and beast-fat. I calculate the angle of the sun, the drop of the canyon, and the position of the distant terrace. With a subtle heave of my chest, I angle the copper, catching the violent sunlight and throwing a single, blinding beam back across the abyss. *I see you.* 

Through the clear, cold air, I watch the distant figure. Though they are no larger than a grain of sand, I can see the slow, sweeping arc of their massive arms against the dark stone. It is our silent language, written on the sheer drops. 

*Fingers splayed, sweeping down. Fists touching, pulling apart.* 
*The shadow extends. The wind wakes.* 

I lift my free arm, my anchor hand never leaving the cliff, and reply. 
*Wrist bent backward, palm flat. Hand over chest.* 
*The span holds. I return to the stone.*

My work here is done for the cycle. I pat Khol’s flank—two heavy thumps. He turns with agonizing grace on a ledge barely wide enough for a human foot, his innate geometry perfectly aligned with the precipice. I follow him, rolling onto the flats of my feet, my toes splaying wide inside my boots to grip the drystone path. We walk together, a slow, boulder-like procession tracing the vertical facade of our world, heading inward.

We cross into the deep canyons, into the shade, where the rime ice coats the stone in black armor. We approach the entrance to my dwelling, an arched opening of friction-fitted granite, draped with a heavy awning of stitched hide. 

I pull the hide aside. The heat inside is immediate, thick, and suffocatingly sweet. 

Within the dim, slate-roofed chamber, my family waits. There are five of us, and three smaller woolly calves, all sharing the same space. I strip off my outer felts, exposing my deep-pigmented skin to the damp warmth. The walls of the chamber are weeping. The heat of our bodies and the beasts clashes with the freezing mountain rock behind the masonry, causing condensation to bead and trickle in agonizingly slow drops. 

I walk to the carved stone cistern in the corner. I pick up a small, hollowed horn. I dip it into the shallow pool of our collected breath, the purified water of our shared existence. I raise it to my lips and take a tiny sip. It tastes of stone, of wool, of life. I will spend the next hour finishing this single cup.

I sit on a pile of furs, my low center of gravity rooting me to the floor. My mate, broad-shouldered and heavy-browed, sits beside me. She does not speak. There is no need to shatter the sacred quiet. Instead, she leans her immense weight against my side. 

Deep within my barrel chest, I begin to hum. It is a low, tectonic vibration, a resonance that rumbles through my ribs and transfers directly into hers. She closes her slit-like eyes and harmonizes, a deep, purring vibration that gently rattles the copper disc on my chest. The calves shift their weight, lulled by the physical sensation of the sound rolling along the floor. 

Outside, the mountain is cracked, freezing, and merciless. But inside, anchored by bone, stone, and the patient blood of our kind, we are warm. We are the beating heart of the rock.
