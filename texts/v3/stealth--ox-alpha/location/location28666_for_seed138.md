You hear the town before anything else: a slow, enormous heartbeat, two strokes to the second, carried through the fog as much through the ground as the air. Then the heat arrives, pressing outward through the mist like a hand through cloth. Then, at last, the light — a diffuse brown-gold glow suspended in the fog ahead, sourceless, the way a shaded lamp looks through a bedsheet.

This is the rim of the basin. It is not far. Nothing here is far. From the crest of the Bluff you could throw a stone into the fog on any side and hit the boundary of the world; the whole place — flats, lagoon, town, engine house — fits inside a half-kilometer ring of steep, clean-cut slopes, and you can walk its full circumference in under half an hour. Below you, the basin opens like a held breath.

The first thing to understand about the ground is that it is new. Not young the way a meadow is young — new the way a quarry floor is new. The Bluff itself is a cut face, and the marks of the buckets and blades that shaped it are still crisp along its whole height, every tooth-stripe legible, as if the machines finished yesterday and will return tomorrow. Down on the flats, a wheelbarrow rut from the founding crosses a stretch of grey mud, both edges sharp as letterforms. Mudcracks tile the drier shelves in perfect polygons, their curled edges raised and intact, never crumbled to dust, never trampled smooth. The old men remember the dredges working here. The ground does not remember it. The ground refuses to remember anything further.

Because nothing here ages. Hold that thought; it will explain most of what follows.

Water is everywhere, and it behaves strangely for somewhere so hot. It rains, technically, all the time — but it is not weather. The air is so saturated that it wrings itself against every cool surface: wires, rails, roof tin, the hairs of your forearms. A fine, constant drizzle falls from the fog ceiling itself, warm as spit, and the cut faces of the Bluff weep in a hundred thread-thin streams that glint as they fall. Seep lines cross the flats in silver braids. At the center, lowest of all, lies the lagoon — broad, shallow, steaming faintly, bathwater-warm, its margin marked on every wall and piling by a stain exactly one shade darker than the stone or timber above it. Ask anyone: the stain never climbs. It never retreats. In forty years it has not moved the width of a fingernail.

The town stands between the lagoon and the Bluff on boardwalk streets raised on timber piles, and it is small — three streets long, two deep, laid out in a sober little grid with the gaps between districts left deliberately empty, quiet margins of cracked mud and rust-red spoil ridges where nothing stands and little moves. The houses are timber and corrugated iron, neat, low, their roofs beaded all over with condensation that trembles and lets go, trembles and lets go. Laundry strung between the lampposts dries in minutes in the furnace-air. Every window has shutters, and the shutters matter, because there is no dawn here to wake anyone. The fog canopy thickens overhead into a low brown ceiling that never parts. There is no night either. The town keeps time the way a sailor keeps a heading: by bell and whistle alone, six and twelve and six, and the whole population turns over in its beds to the sound of the shift whistle as faithfully as farm folk turn to a cockcrow.

The heart of everything is Number One Engine House, and you will hear it in your sleep. Two beam engines stroke inside it, vast and unhurried, and their purpose is simple and total: the basin floor sits below the level of the surrounding country, below the water table, below — everyone says, though no one has seen it — the sea itself. Without the pumps, the town drowns in an afternoon. With them, the water sits exactly where it sits, forever. The engines draw from the sumps and throw the lagoon's overflow up a tailrace cut through the Bluff notch, and their stroke has not varied by a single beat in living memory. Inside, brass gauges stand pinned at their readings, needles motionless; the chief engineer taps each one anyway every morning with a knuckle, twice, out of a ritual that stopped being necessary before he was born. Dynamos hum along one wall, and their current feeds the carbon-arc lamps over the great doors, which burn a hard blue-white and make doubled shadows of everything that walks past. Elsewhere the light is softer: oil-lamp amber in windows, furnace orange breathing through the clinker doors, and everywhere else the flat, kind dimness of the fog, which turns the whole basin into a room lit for evening regardless of the hour.

The heat comes from below. Boreholes breathe at intervals across the flats — capped pipes exhaling slow white columns of steam, each one wrapped in its own tower of midges. The ground is warm through boot soles everywhere, hot enough near the breathers that bare skin objects. This, the pump-men explain, is why the land emerged at all: new ground, still cooling from its making, pushed up wet and steaming into the fog belt a lifetime ago, and the town followed the warmth. What the fires take from the coal trucks, the fog takes back; no soot greys the roofs, no ash heap grows behind the boiler house, and the stokers shrug when you ask where it all goes. Nothing accumulates here. Nothing wears. The ironwork shows the same bright rust-paint it wore at painting; the timber shows the same grey. Even decay keeps books and balances them.

Life crowds into this warm dimness more thickly than you'd expect from so spare a place. The vegetation never gets anywhere — that's the truth of it. An algal felt coats the shallows in olive and rust-red mats; a fringe of reeds stands ankle-deep along the warm seeps and has stood, at exactly that height and no taller, since the first photograph of the basin; a film of green moss follows the drip-lines under every eave and never thickens past a coat of paint. Pioneer plants, frozen mid-pioneer. But the animals treat the basin as a banquet. Swallows ride fixed altitude-bands under the fog ceiling, sorting midges out of the air. Black-winged stilts stalk the settling ponds on red-candle legs. Eels pack the tailrace so densely that children lean off the sluice gate and pull them out by hand for the asking, and the frogs around the lagoon margin chorus in strict alternation, half answering half, at a volume that never swells and never fades. Rats commute the pipe-runs at stated hours like small commuters. On the Bluff face after the evening bell, glowworms stitch the cut marks of the dredges with faint cold constellations. Ask the registrar: the counts hold. However many chicks fledge, that many old birds fail; whatever spawns, something spawns less elsewhere. The ledger of the living stays the same thickness as the ledger of the dead.

And the people are part of that arithmetic whether they like it or not. Christenings and burials trade places month for month; the young grow tall and take the road through the Bluff notch, and newcomers arrive to replace them, and the town neither swells nor shrinks. The surveyor measures the crack in the old cistern every spring. Eleven centimeters, every spring, and he writes it down with the resigned care of a man recording the tide. Production figures, coal consumption, the depth of the sumps, the lean of the smoke column off Number One's stack — eleven degrees toward the southeast, every hour of every year — all of it holds, and the townsfolk have organized themselves around this fact with the practical serenity of people living on a floodplain. They keep excellent clocks. They maintain what needs no maintaining. They are, in a sense, the only moving parts the place allows itself, and they seem quietly proud of it.

So the rules, such as they are, can be counted on one hand. The boundary holds — the fog wall stands where it stands, and beyond it the ordinary world resumes its habits of weather and change. Heat rises from beneath; water rises to its stained line; the pumps stroke; nothing deepens, nothing dulls, nothing gathers dust. The land stays at the instant of its making the way a struck bell stays, for one bright moment, entirely itself.

When you climb back to the notch to leave, the heartbeat sound follows you partway up and then hands you over to silence — not because the town has quieted, but because distance is the only thing that diminishes it. Look back once, from the top. The lamplight swimming in the fog, the arc-lamps' blue star at the engine house doors, the reeds, the steaming breathers, and above it all the smoke column leaning its eleven degrees, patient as handwriting.

It will be leaning exactly so when someone you have not met comes through the fog years from now, stops on this same crest, and feels the heat reach out through the mist to take them by the wrist. The pumps will be holding the sea out. The moment will be holding its shape. Sough keeps what it has, and gives back nothing to time — except, at the end of every shift, the sound of its own heart, two strokes to the second, going nowhere at all.
