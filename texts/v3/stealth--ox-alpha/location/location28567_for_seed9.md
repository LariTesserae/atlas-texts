# The Summit Works

There is one way up: a single cable line that rises out of the cloud deck and vanishes into it again, and if you ride it you pass through forty seconds of white silence before the gondola breaks through into ordinary daylight and there it is — not a mountain range, not a wilderness, but one mountain. A single object. You could walk its entire rim in under an hour, and people do, along a parapetted path of fresh-cut stone, while below and all around the cloud sea turns slowly, hiding whatever holds this place aloft.

The first thing you notice is that the rock is young.

Not eroded-young, not glacier-scoured young — *new* young. The cliffs bear their mold-lines still. Strata lie in clean courses like stacked slate straight from a kiln, seams crisp, corners unrounded, surfaces glittering with minerals that have never once been rained on until recently and haven't yet learned to dull. There is no lichen. There has been no time for lichen. In shaded alcoves you'll find crates of it — stone-inoculant, shipped up the cable in flat trays, being pressed onto the rock by hand, glued on almost, because even the aging here is scheduled. Nothing on this summit has been allowed to happen by accident yet.

Because nothing here happened. Everything was done.

That is the second thing, and it takes longer to understand: the mountain is manufactured, all the way down. The "soil" underfoot is a specified substrate — crushed aggregate, loam, mycelium starter — laid in documented layers over drainage grids. The streams begin at spigots. Follow any charming brook uphill and it terminates at a brass outlet set into the rockface, discharging a designed flow-rate, its banks pre-planted with rushes so the water will learn to look natural as it runs. Even the springs are fittings. And the cliffs weep constantly — bead after bead after bead — because cold condenser coils are threaded through the stone behind the faces, chilling the rock deliberately below the dew point so that the whole summit sweats. The moisture isn't leakage. It's function. This is a wet place because someone decided it would be, and built the wet in.

It is warm, which at this altitude should be impossible. The air sits at a mild, hothouse mildness that has nothing to do with the sun and everything to do with the engine halls buried in the mountain's core. Waste heat is routed outward through the flanks in buried manifolds; steam vents rise among the ferns like natural fumaroles, smelling faintly of warm iron and cut green stems. Your breath joins the general exhalation. Paper droops. Metal is always slick. The atmosphere condenses against everything cooler than itself, which is nearly everything, so that by late afternoon every leaf, rail, and boulder wears a fine skin of water.

And the plants — the plants are glorious. Someone chose them from catalogues and planted them in drifts, and freed from drought and frost and competition, they've gone riotous. Tree ferns unfurl on the terraces with preposterous confidence. Giant rosette plants stand in silver-green colonies on the exposed shoulders, their woolled leaves beaded with manufactured fog. Mosses have already claimed every damp seam and valve housing; shrubs lean over the walkways; young trees rise staked and guyed with steel cables, their trunks still thin enough to show the nursery marks burned into the bark. The planting is dense but not crowded — layered belts of green alternating with open gravel courts, reservoirs lying flat and mirror-calm between them, stone parade grounds where nothing grows on purpose. The density breathes. But everywhere you look, life is stacked three and four stories tall: canopy, understory, floor, root-zone, and beneath all of it, the pipes.

For the machinery is everywhere and nowhere. You rarely see it. Boiler house, pumps, compressors — all buried behind thick walls, damped with felt and muffled under a meter of growing medium, which is why the place is so *quiet*. What reaches the surface is only the mountain's respiration: a slow hiss when a pressure valve eases somewhere below the meadow; the tick of cooling metal; a deep, patient throb underfoot, felt more than heard, like a heartbeat conducted through bedrock. Once an hour a bell sounds — you never see who rings it — and along the rim a ring-manifold opens, and mist pours out in long soft banners and rolls downhill across the gardens, beading every surface, feeding everything at once. Then it closes, and the dripping begins, thousands of small drips counting themselves out in the hush. The whole soundscape of the place is essentially water and breath.

The fauna you can number. There is a hive box near the reservoir — one hive, its bees on pollination duty, logged like equipment. There is a pair of small gray birds nesting in a vent stack, the only song on the summit, and they sing at dawn with such authority that for a minute the whole engineered world seems to belong to them. Moths gather at the caged lamps when the mist-hours run past dusk. Someone swears a frog lives in the spillway pond; it appears in the logbook twice a year, always as rumor. Each animal here is an event. When the gray birds fly overhead, workers look up from their gauges.

Ah — yes, there are workers. You glimpse them rarely: figures moving along the north rim where the growth is happening. Because the mountain is still being *made*, and this is the strangest thing of all, the thing you only understand on your second day. At the northern edge, hydraulic jacks rise through the terrace like mechanical tree trunks, and every few weeks they lift a new course of freshly cast stone into place. The summit is growing — centimeters a week, surveyed, recorded. Cable cars heavier than the passenger line haul aggregate up from somewhere beneath the clouds. Survey stakes dot the eastern slope marking the course of a river that does not exist yet; the maps in the survey hut show contour lines labeled *Year Ten*, *Year Twenty-Five*, *Year Fifty* — the shape this mountain intends to become. The gullies on the south face aren't erosion. They're seed-gullies, machined grooves cut in advance so that when real rain finally finds them, the water will know where to go. Weathering itself has been planned. Somewhere a waterfall has a completion date.

So you walk this small roofed world — bounded by parapet, surrounded by cloud, comprehensible end to end — and everything in it is simultaneously ancient in form and days old in fact. Alpine meadow ecology assembled last spring. Rock with no memory. Rain on a schedule, warmth from a boiler, springs from a manifold, silence from insulation, birdsong from two birds. The strangeness isn't dreamlike; it's the strangeness of *intention* — a landscape with a birth certificate, a maintenance manual, and a growth chart pinned above the desk.

In the late afternoon the light goes amber and slantwise through the steam plumes, throwing moving shadows from vents and fern-fronds across the terraces. The mist-hour bell rings. The fog rolls down the slopes, swallowing the rosette colonies, the staked trees, the weeping cliffs, and for a while there is nothing anywhere but warm white breath and the sound of ten thousand leaves drinking.

Then the manifold closes. The mist thins. And the mountain stands there in the clearing air, sweating gently, growing almost imperceptibly, rehearsing — drip by drip, course by cast stone — the millions of years of weathering it has decided to have.
