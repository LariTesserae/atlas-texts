# The Spring-House

Under the western shoulder of the hill — under the sheep pasture, under the root-plate of the beech that has stood there longer than any fence — there is a small stone room that the water took.

It was built low on purpose. A spring-house, the old people called such places: a vault sunk into the hillside beside the spring, where milk and meat could be kept cold in the marble dark. The spring is ancient; it came up out of the rock before there were hills shaped like this one. But its taking of the room is recent. Within living memory the water rose one winter and did not go back down. It climbed the walls a finger-width a month, unhurried, thorough, and one autumn it kissed the crown of the vault and sealed it, and the room became what it is now: a pocket world, twenty strokes from wall to wall, drowned, dark, and very nearly finished becoming itself.

The water is cold the way wells are cold — a cold that comes up through the stone, not down through the sky. It numbs the wrist slowly, politely. And it is *clear*, black-clear, so that the first thing you understand about the place is that there is almost nothing between things. You can see the whole room at once: every shelf, every jar, every curtain of growth, all suspended in a stillness so complete that the water seems less like a liquid and more like a decision the world made and then forgot to unmake.

Nothing decays here. The cold sees to that, and the stillness, and something else besides. So the drowning is legible everywhere, crisp as the day it happened. Edges remain sharp. The threshold stone still holds the shallow dish worn into it by eighty years of feet — wear from *before*, fossilized now, while the edges cut by the flood itself remain bright and unworn, like a wound that refuses to scab.

---

There is almost no light. What light exists is grown.

The vegetation came in fast after the flood — faster than anything else in this slow place — and now it is everywhere, upholstering everything. Pale thread-moss felts the walls in mats thick as your thumb. Ghost-fine weeds hang from the ceiling seams like spun sugar. Root-ferns with fronds the color of drowned ivory climb down through cracks in the vault, following the beech's own roots, which pierce the roof here and there in ropes as thick as an arm — the tree above drinking from the room below, the garden below climbing the tree. Nothing in the room is bare. Stone, wood, clay, bone of any old thing: all of it furred green-white, layered, packed, so that surfaces have lost their boundaries and the room feels less like a container full of objects than like one solid organism with a room-shaped hollow.

And scattered through the felt are strands that carry their own cold light. Blue-green, dim as starfall, they pulse — not quickly, not brightly, but steadily, in rhythms that seem coordinated without being identical, so that the whole room glimmers faintly, like a sky seen from underwater, like the memory of a sky. After ten minutes in the dark your eyes adjust enough to read shapes by them. After an hour you can read the room's handwriting.

Because the growth does mimic. Here a mat of moss has risen into steps — four of them, perfect, leading up to nothing. There, threads have arranged themselves into rows of marks suspiciously like letters, or like tally strokes, on the underside of a shelf. Nobody planted them. Nothing teaches them. The room had furniture once, and thresholds, and a door, and the garden seems to be *remembering* these categories and rehearsing them, the way a child rehearses words it does not yet understand.

---

The built things remain, scattered and plain and pre-tool-simple: a stone trough. A stool gone mineral-stiff, holding its own shape forever, its legs furred like a cat's. A row of crocks with wax seals — most broken open and empty, their insides converted by the years and the water into clear glassy pebbles, but two still sealed, contents unknown, patient. A ladle hanging on its peg. On a hook by the door, a coat, stiffened into the exact posture of a man shrugging it off, sleeves bent mid-gesture, and the flood came so soon after its owner left that the wool never even finished rotting — the water simply moved in around it and kept it, the way amber keeps.

Beside the doorframe, scratched into the jamb: rows of tally marks, one row per year, counting floods survived. The rows continue for a lifetime's worth and then stop. Mid-stroke.

---

Now the rules of the place, which are not the rules outside it.

Look straight at the water and you see water. Catch it at a slant — in a reflection, off the face of a jar, in the black mirror where wall meets floor — and sometimes you see the room *dry*. Candlelit. Whole. The coat soft on its hook, the crocks full, warmth in the stone. The reflection is not of this moment; it is of other moments, stacked in the water like leaves pressed in a book, and it shows you whichever one it likes. Once, reportedly — once, by whoever counts such things — the reflection showed the room *half-built*, masons' hands moving soundlessly in the glow.

Distance is negotiable. The passage behind the third shelf is three body-lengths long going in and seven coming back, every time, both true. Certain patches of water decline to be touched — the hand meets a resistance like cold glass, holds a heartbeat, then gives way as if embarrassed. And *down* is a rumor here. The deepest dark shifts. Some nights it pools behind the trough; some months, in the doorway. Things in the room orient themselves toward it anyway, without complaint.

Once a year, for less time than a blink, the room is dry. This is stated as fact, not legend. The candles are lit. Then the water returns from all directions at once, having never left.

---

The animals are few, and that is part of their strangeness — each one arrives like an event.

In a handful of gravel live pale shrimp-things no bigger than commas, blind, translucent enough to show their hearts. There are hundreds, and you will see three. A long eel-shaped shadow crosses the doorway perhaps twice a year. It does not disturb the water as it passes — the water parts around it without ripple — and afterward the nearest curtains of weed lean subtly toward the direction it went, for hours. And once, balanced on the rim of a sealed crock, there sat a frog. Brown, ordinary, breathing. In a drowned room under a hill. It was gone on every visit after, and the moss where it sat glowed brighter for a season, as if in thanks.

---

The sound of the place is mostly silence under pressure — a hush with weight, felt in the teeth. Into it come small voices: the creak of growing moss, audible because water carries sound so faithfully and there is so little else to hear. Ticks and clicks of the shrimp, like knuckles. Drips — though some drips fall upward, and everyone pretends not to notice. And beneath everything, the stone itself holds one low tone, below hearing, continuous, the note of a room that is resonating very slowly with the hill around it. Dive down through the colder layers near the floor and the water changes — older bands, denser bands — and sounds leak out of them. A latch lifting. A cough. Rain on a roof, from when there was a roof above this water instead of clay and root.

---

Time here moves the way roots move: invisibly, and all at once, eventually.

The room breathes on a nine-day cycle — the water rising a finger-width, pausing, withdrawing, and when it withdraws a thin lens of air gathers along the highest curve of the vault, and the smell of the old room concentrates there for a day: beeswax, bread, cedar, wool. Then the lens thins and closes, and the breath completes.

A hairline crack runs up through forty feet of clay and root to some pinhole of sky no map admits to, and through it enters a needle of grey daylight, thin as wire, which bends — light should not bend here, but it does — and lays itself across the room. It points at the trough in spring, the tall crock in summer, the tally-jamb at the equinoxes. Where it rests, the moss glows brighter afterward, grazing on it. It is the room's only clock, and it is accurate.

Somewhere below the stool, a bubble released on the first day of the flood is still rising. It has moved the length of a hand in all these years. It will reach the vault-ceiling in a few decades more, touch it gently, and hold there, the last exhalation of the dry world, waiting for the room to finish whatever it is becoming — and the water grows clearer every year, and the felt grows thicker, and the glow steadies, and it becomes possible to say what the room is turning into, though no one above ever will, walking their sheep over the grass, unaware that beneath them a small dark lens is ripening, patiently, learning to see.
