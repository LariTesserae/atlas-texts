# The Sough

You come to it first as a feeling in your bootsoles, a tremor you mistake for your own pulse until you stand still and it keeps going without you. Then the ears catch up, and you understand that the ground has been speaking the whole time, and that everything you took for silence on the walk down was this: the sound of a plain-sized machine, running.

Old surveys would have called it a sough — a drain, a cutting that carries water off low land. The word also means a sigh. Both meanings survive here, fused.

---

There is no soil. That is the first thing to accept. Push a rod into any gap between the structures and it rings against fired clay, against slag, against the buried shoulder of a wall older than the walls above it. Dig for a lifetime and you will not reach a single unworked grain. The whole world underfoot is aggregate — clinker, crushed brick, ceramic worn round as river pebbles — and some of it is the ground-up previous edition of the ground itself. The plain grinds itself down gently and lays itself down again somewhere else. Nothing is lost. Nothing arrives.

The water table stands a hand's width below all of it. You feel it before you see it, in the way every surface sweats. Foundations weep. Retaining walls run with beads that gather and drop and gather again, and the runoff has carved channels in the stone that are themselves polished smooth, so that the water flows in grooves its own ancestors cut. The puddles are permanent, shallow, black mirrors lying between the structures, filmed with olive and rust-red growths that thrive on dissolved iron. Step near the discharge outfalls and the puddles are warm. Step anywhere else and the chill comes up through your boots like the ground is exhaling winter.

Everything is cool. Six degrees, eight, never freezing — the steam sees to that — but never warming either, a clammy maritime cold trapped under a sky that never opens.

---

Age here does not mean ruin. That is the second thing to accept.

Things in the Sough wear *toward* their function. Edges that were laid out square centuries ago are now round as sea glass, and the rounded versions run better than the originals did. The oldest pumps are the smoothest. Valve wheels have worn to ovoids that fit a hand no one has seen. Staircases have eroded into ramps. Girders that once carried load-bearing ornament carry none, having shed it into the clinker fields below, and stand lighter and truer for it. Corrosion progresses to a certain orange-black bloom and then stops, checked by the anoxic water and by grease that is, inexplicably, fresh.

Because here is the strangeness at the center: the place is maintained. A bearing no one visits sits bright with yellow grease. A handrail is smoother this year than last. Either someone tends the Sough, or the Sough has learned to tend itself the way a body heals — and nothing in the landscape settles the question. The causeways stretch away into the dark and return. The lamps change color when they fail, and they are replaced. No one is ever met.

Above the low roofs rise the breather towers — two hundred meters of vent stack, hundreds of them, ranked across the plain into haze. Each exhales a column of steam that flattens against the cloud ceiling and spreads. Because the cloud ceiling, it becomes clear, *is* the towers' breath. There may be weather beyond the Sough, but here the weather is municipal: the sky is exhaust, the rain is distillate. It falls every third hour or so, fine and vertical and slightly warm, tasting of nothing at all, and afterward the whole world steams softly and the weeping starts again. To stand in the rain here is to stand in the machine's exhalation. Nothing could be more intimate, and nothing notices.

---

Follow the water. That is the honest way to understand the place.

It gathers in the settling fields, vast shallow basins floored in black membrane, surfaced with a weed so dark it reads as hole in the world. From there it moves through miles of filtering media — gravel beds, woven screens, chambers of charcoal the size of cathedrals — and comes out the far side clean, and is gathered, and lifted. The great piston halls do the lifting: rows of engines the size of foundry buildings, throbbing at perhaps twenty lifts a minute, slow and tidal, raising water through arteries of riveted pipe that cross the plain at waist height and shoulder height and, on the big mains, overhead, arcing from gallery to gallery on stumps of pier.

And then the water falls. It cools, it condenses, it weeps out of the atmosphere and the stonework, it finds the settling fields again.

This is the whole circuit. Lift so it can fall; fall so it can be lifted. Whatever the Sough drained, or cooled, or supplied, whoever waited at the end of those pipes — that commerce is finished or was never finished or has become unnecessary, and the process remains, perfected, unemployed, running on a schedule no one sets. Some sectors stand stopped for decades at a stretch — the hush fields. Cross one and the silence is a weight, architectural, and you can hear your own blood and your own boots and nothing else, and both sound obscene. Then, on some date known only to the iron, the sector inhales and rejoins the thunder.

---

The sound deserves its own accounting, because it is the dominant feature of the landscape — louder than the towers are tall.

Beneath everything: the pistons, felt in the ribs before the ears claim them, a beat like the plain has a heart of about thirty tons. Above that: the white hiss of steam venting from a thousand relief points, a continuous surf. Above *that*: the tick and drip of condensation — millions of drops striking millions of metal surfaces, arrhythmic, ceaseless, the rain that already fell continuing to fall internally forever. Occasionally some great hall shifts with temperature and emits a groan as long as a landslide. Distantly, a horn sounds four low notes across the water, marking an hour, or a shift, or a mood of the machinery. Conversation outdoors is conducted in gestures. Shouting works for perhaps a week; then visitors go quiet and stay quiet, not because the noise has lessened but because they have reclassified it — it is no longer sound. It is ground. It is air. It is the condition of having ears.

And here is the final fact, the one that takes longest to accept: **there are no animals.**

Not rats in the walls. Not gnats above the settling ponds, though the water is warm and rich. No birds ride the thermals above the towers; no worms turn the clinker; nothing swims the channels. The pond surfaces are never broken except by other water. Whether the Sough's founders excluded life by design, or whether life came here once and declined the terms, cannot be said. But the absence shapes everything. Leaves are never eaten. Nothing scatters the seeds of the pale sedge except gravity and overflow. The only motions in this world that were not made are the motions of water.

For there is life — sparse, stubborn, and strange. Under every work-lamp grows a garden. The lamps hang far apart along the causeways, islands of amber in the dark, and beneath each one stands a rookery of sedge as pale as candle flame, white-green straps that have never photosynthesized toward a sun. They grow in rows. Self-seeded now, certainly — but the rows remember planting. Cushion moss greens the flanges of the cold pipes, mapping warmth instead of daylight, since warmth is the only direction this world offers. An orange-black lichen chews slowly through rust, the sole rival to water as a consumer of the place.

---

Stand back, at the end, on some high cistern lid, and look out.

The plain extends past seeing in every direction. Same ranks of towers breathing their sky into place. Same lamps with their small pale gardens underneath, spaced wide, the darkness between them dense and complete. Same black mirrors, same weeping walls, same buried thunder. Somewhere far off, an arc-lamp flickers blue in an open hall — work, of a kind, continuing.

Whatever the world was, it is mostly this now: a wet dark, a worn machine in its final and most comfortable form, lifting the water so the water can fall, and falling so it can be lifted. The sound of that, going on and on, is the nearest thing the place has to a voice.

It is saying the only thing it knows.
