# Sinterhold, the Weeping Town

You would hear it long before you saw it.

The way in is a dry throat of karst, a crawl behind fluted stone that tightens until your shoulders remember their joints — and then, while the rock still holds you, the sound arrives. Not a roar. Fullness. A thousand small waters conversing at once: drip answering drip, runnels chuckling over lips of stone, and underneath everything, slow as a sleeper turning over, the double-knock-and-swallow of something drinking far below. By the time the crawl opens and lets you go, you already know the chamber's temper. Sinterhold does not greet visitors. It simply continues, loudly, exactly as it was continuing before you existed.

The chamber itself is modest — a nave of limestone two hundred paces long, half that across, its vault rising forty feet into darkness that never fully resolves. Apse-like pockets branch off it, and off those, smaller pockets still, until the place reads less like a cavern than a nautilus cut open: one public hall wrapped in whorls of private ones. Every surface sweats. The ceiling weeps with a patience that feels deliberate, a fine beaded condensation gathering and releasing, gathering and releasing, so that the whole air hangs at the edge of rain. Breathe deeply and it tastes of wet chalk and a thread of iron. The temperature never moves — a cool ten degrees, yearless, the chill of a cellar in all seasons — and the folk who live here wear it the way surface dwellers wear altitude, without complaint and without notice.

There is no green here. Nothing has grown green in this place for longer than its people can conceive. What life exists is film and fuzz: bacterial felts the color of clotted cream lining the weir-boards, harvested with shell scrapers and pressed into chalky curds; and the lampcrust, a pale celadon mold cultivated on lattice racks of fired clay, which exhales a dim grey-green glow wherever it is kept damp. Lighting the town is therefore a form of gardening done backward — the tenders carry watering-shells among the racks, and brightness rises where the moisture goes. Most of Sinterhold lives at dusk-vision regardless. Its people are broad-eared, wide-pupiled, quiet-footed, and they consider a carried lantern an act of mild aggression.

The water is governed. That is the second thing you understand. None of this wetness is wild. Somewhere in the deep past — longer ago than the oldest story, older even than the Quiet Congregation in their alcoves — hands cut runnels into this stone, and the water, given channels, accepted them. Now the channels have gone glassy with their own mineral, self-sealed with calcite, and the weirs stand dished and polished like riverbeds. Slate sluices lift on stone pivots. Spouts are lips carved in faces so ancient the expressions have eroded into serenity. Everything is metered; nothing pours. Each cistern receives exactly what it loses to air and use, each overflow finds its appointed stair, and the whole hydrology has settled into a condition the folk describe with their highest word for a thing: *even*.

They build the way the water builds — by addition only. This is law, liturgy, and habit at once: the water adds; so may we. Nothing in Sinterhold is demolished, nothing removed, because the water cannot give back what is taken away. Change happens by layering. Homes are cells of stone and clay stacked against the chamber walls, terraced up the flanks in dense courses, each house founded on the roof of an older house, each street paved over a former street, until the town's archaeology is vertical and everyone's floor is somebody's ancestry. Neighbors share walls thin enough to hear one another turn in sleep. There are exactly two hundred fourteen hearth-niches, and there have always been — well, *always*, in the working sense — exactly two hundred fourteen. Households swell, divide, recombine, but the frame never grows. The number of fires is a fact of geology, like the number of stalactites.

Their tools suit the permanence of everything else. No forge burns here; there is no fuel to burn and no need to try. Edge-tools are flint and antler, inherited, resharpened on sandstone until the handles have worn to silk. Joinery is pegged and wedged. Cordage is plaited from mycelial thread and sinew. And for anything that must last, there is the Mother Pool: a broad basin of supersaturated water beside the workshop terrace where objects are laid and left, and the water, doing slowly what it does to everything, turns them to stone. A knife-blank goes in as carved bone and comes out weeks later as pale mineral, identical to the eye, eternal to the touch. Braided rings. Dolls. A whistle. On one shelf waits a single copper pin, found in the Dry Time, the only metal anyone has ever held — kept not for use but as a kind of question, displayed the way another people might display a relic of the Flood.

Yes — the Dry Time. The folk know, dimly, that the world above has moods. Sometimes the ceiling weeps harder for a season; sometimes a seep thins to almost nothing; once, in the deepest story they keep, the third cistern went silent for the length of a childhood. They speak of the upper world the way coastal people speak of the open ocean: real, vast, irrelevant. Weather happens elsewhere. Down here there is only the Weather — a single district near the eastern apse where the vault sweats so heavily that a curtain of fine drizzle falls perpetually through the lamplight, and the townspeople walk through it on purpose, tilting their faces up, because being rained on indoors is one of the two luxuries this world affords. The other is the Kindness: one thin vein of tepid water, blood-warm, seeping down a bench of stone near the nurseries, where residents sit in solemn queue, shoulder to shoulder, saying nothing.

Nothing, in Sinterhold, means conversation is over. The town is loud. It is loud the way a forest is loud — total, textured, and somehow equivalent to silence. Hundreds of soda straws hang from the vault, each dripping at an interval set by its own length, and together they make an organ no one tuned and no one could retune, a chord that has been sounding since before the town. The people have simply moved in underneath the music. Children learn pitch by matching a straw. Lovers leave messages at the whisper-nodes — spots where a murmur at one wall blooms, impossibly, from the opposite wall — so that courtship here is conducted in confidences delivered across two hundred feet of dark air. Public announcements work the same way: the Crier stands at the Node and barely speaks at all, and every household hears the news arrive softly in its own corner of the room, like something remembered rather than told. Rudeness, in Sinterhold, has a precise definition: *adding a sound*. The room owns sound. Persons borrow it.

And beneath all the small waters, the clock. In the center of the lowest terrace, behind a grate of petrified timber, the floor breathes — a natural siphon where water gathers in a buried bend, rises, glugs twice, and swallows down with a long reverberant gulp that arrives through boot-soles a heartbeat before it reaches the ears. Two hundred paces away you feel it in your ribs. It is the town's bell, its drum, its metronome; appointments are kept "three swallows past," ceremonies begin "at the swallow." The plaza around the grate is kept clear and dished with use, and it is there, after each gulp's echo dies, that the town conducts whatever business it still bothers to conduct — because business is scarce. Nothing here accumulates toward anything. The calendar notches cut into the Tally Wall heal shut within a generation, closed by fresh calcite like wounds, so that written history is impossible and memory must stay oral, circular, content. Births answer deaths. What one hand raises, mist and time round smooth. The town does not progress. It *sustains* — which its people insist is different from standing still, the way a held note is different from no note at all.

The fauna keep to the margins, as everything marginal does. Pale nips graze the bacterial felts, thumb-sized amphibious clickers that startle easily and taste of chalk. Hushmoths drift at ceiling height, blind, drinking straight from the weeping stone, so silent that the townsfolk describe one passing as *nothing, flying*. And in the well-shafts of the Underside — the drowned gallery where all runoff finally goes, down steps that end in black mirrors — there lives, by universal agreement, the Neighbor: an eel-dark presence seen perhaps once in a lifetime, always at the edge of lamplight, never twice by the same witness. Offerings of chalk-curd are lowered on plaited cords. Whether the Neighbor takes them is undetermined. The cords come back bare, but cords would anyway.

Only one event is feared here, and it is not flood, not collapse, not the Neighbor surfacing. Parents hush children with it at the swallow-grate: the horror of Sinterhold is a stopped drip. A blocked runnel, a failed seep, a straw gone dry — any silence inserted into the chord would mean the even water had tipped, the balance that holds stone and town and people in their long agreement had shifted at last. So the town listens, always, the way other places watch horizons. And the water keeps falling — metered, wept, promised — and the straws keep their pitches, and the siphon gathers itself and knocks twice and swallows, and two hundred fourteen hearths burn their dim grey-green under a vault that has been finishing this same sentence for longer than there has been anyone to hear it.

Not frozen. Not finished. Sustained.
