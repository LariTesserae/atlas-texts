**The Unnamed Shelf**

The first thing to understand is that these mountains are new, and they have not yet learned to lie about it.

They stand very high — four, five thousand meters, a whole province of them, massif beyond massif — and yet almost nothing has worn them down. Most ranges at this altitude are blades and needles, carved by ten million years of ice and floodwater. These are still slabs. The summits are flat. Not rounded-flat, not plateau-flat in the gentle way — flat the way a floor is flat, planar to the horizon, because these surfaces were once the bottom of everything, an ancient plain lifted wholesale into the sky before weather could get a word in. The uplift broke the old plain into enormous tablets and tilted some of them, stepped others in clean fault-scarps that rise like staircase treads a hundred meters tall. The corners are still sharp. You can see, from one tabletop to the next, the exact geometry of the breaking, unblurred by any rubble cone or talus skirt. Rockfall happens — you'll hear it at night, a dry clatter somewhere below, a slab letting go — but the debris fields are years old at most and look like spilled cargo rather than landscape.

Frost is the only sculptor at work so far, and it works slowly, prying grains loose each night, ticking away at the joints. There is no soil to speak of. Plants grow directly on stone, in films of water, and you can date any surface here by the inch of lichen on it the way you'd date a gravestone. Fresh fracture faces are bare and rust-colored — the young rock oxidizing as it breathes, orange blooms creeping across grey quartz — so the whole range carries the blush of a wound a few centuries old.

And the range is not finished rising. It is still going up, measurably, a few millimeters a year, and the water hasn't caught up.

Because water is everywhere. That's the second thing. A mountain this young has no drainage — no valleys cut, no channels decided — so the water that falls on it, condenses on it, and seeps out of it has nowhere committed to go. The rock is shattered by the uplift itself, cracked through in a million places, and it holds water the way a sponge holds water, perched high above its own base. The result is that the entire massif weeps. Every scarp face is striped with seep lines, dark streaks down pale stone, each one fed by groundwater that hasn't found a pipe. Springs ring the base of every fault-step in chains of marsh. On the tabletops, meltwater and rain sheet across those intact planar surfaces in thin silver films, pooling in shallow bedrock hollows, overflowing into the next hollow, linking tarn after tarn into strings of standing water — marshland on the summits, bogs riding on top of the sky. There are no rivers here yet, only rumors of them: a thread of current through the wet that runs one way this month and another way after the next hard rain rearranges the ground. Any map drawn of this place expires within the season. The water is still deciding where it lives.

It is cold — properly cold, hovering just above freezing most of the year, dipping well past it every clear night. Which means the daily rhythm of the whole range is freeze and thaw. Rime builds on everything after dark, fur-white on the sedge, on the rocks, on whatever else stands out there. Then the dim morning warms it a degree or two and the entire landscape begins, softly, to drip. Stand anywhere at midday and you hear it: not a sound so much as a texture, ten thousand drippers keeping slightly different time, the mountain counting.

The light is pewter. The mountain makes its own weather — all that wet surface, all that lift, wrings a permanent cloud deck out of the air — and the sun behind it is less a body than a brightening, a direction without a shape. Shadows are faint and doubled. Colors go muted: grey stone, black wet streaks, the green-black of moss, the orange of the rust blooms, white rime at the edges of the day. Some mornings the basins between massifs fill completely with fog — cold-air lakes of it, deep as the valleys themselves — and the tablet-mountains stand out of the mist like islands, and for an hour the region is an archipelago. Then the fog lifts and you see what the density of this place really is: distance. Whole valleys with nothing in them. A horizon with nothing on it. You can walk half a day between sights.

But the sights, when you reach them, are crowded.

Life came into this range fast and hasn't finished sorting itself out — a young ecosystem in the middle of its first population boom. The base of everything is water and film: diatoms and bacteria painting the seep faces, midges hatching in the tens of billions from the warm shallow tarns, springtails and mites swarming invisibly in every wet lichen mat. Vegetation proper is sparse — the cold and the missing soil see to that — but where it takes hold it takes hold lushly: moss carpets thick as upholstery on the spring lines, cushion plants clenched against the wind, sedges colonizing the boggy tabletops in dark islands. Sparse, and green as a flag.

Everything eats the midges. Pipits and wagtail-things work the pool margins in flocks of hundreds. Swifter birds hawk the hatches in the evening. Along the scarp faces, falcons ride the updrafts where the seeps breathe moist air upward, picking off the careless. The bird spectacle peaks in the short summer, when a goose-analogue arrives by the thousand to molt flightless on the big tarns — for three weeks the summit lakes are raucous with them, honking, paddling, helpless, fat, and the local fox-analogues patrol the shores living very well indeed. Broad-hoofed herd beasts — woolly, low, built for wet turf — cross the empty basins between tabletops in groups of two hundred, appearing as a dark smudge two valleys off and then, an hour later, all around you, breathing steam, churning the bog edges to porridge, gone again. In the shallows there are no fish — the pools are too new, too disconnected — but there are frogs: a heavy, slow species that freezes solid every night among the sedge roots and thaws by day. At midday, when the rock warms the necessary degree, they sing. For an hour the whole range carries a thin metallic chorus, thousands of small throats ringing off the fault-walls, and then the light lowers, the temperature drops a fraction, and it stops as if switched off.

Warm seep-lines thread through all of this — water rising along the young deep faults carrying a few degrees of the earth's new heat. These are the hearths of the range. Herds winter at them. The midges hatch earliest there. In the coldest weeks, the map of life in this region is simply the map of its warm springs.

And the machines.

They were not built here. Nothing here was built — that bears repeating, because the machines are unmistakably industrial, and there is not one structure in the entire province. No road, no pad, no fence, no cairn, no cut step, no wire. Whatever hands or companies sent them intended it that way, and they obey: they construct nothing, they leave nothing, they claim nothing. They only take.

There are perhaps a dozen of them across the whole region — which is why the emptiness stays empty. The workers are six-legged frames the size of houses, patient as barnacles, that lean against a fresh scarp face for months at a time and take it apart: silent coring drills, onboard mills, tanks that reduce crushed rock in the mountain's own copious water. They came for exactly what a young range offers — ore bodies still unweathered, metals never leached, lying exposed in virgin strata that erosion hasn't yet had time to smear across the countryside. They assay a face, mill what pays, and when the seam thins they simply stand up and walk to the next massif. The haulers are larger, slower, wider — low-slung carriers that move between the tablets at night on legs that step over ponds, carrying dense ingots out along the fault-ramps. Where they came from and where the metal goes are not questions this landscape answers. They refit themselves from their own yields. Nobody visits. They check on themselves.

You locate a worker unit before you see it, because it exhales — heat exchangers breathing a private column of steam into the cold air, a little weather that belongs to one machine. Up close it hums at the bottom of hearing; you feel it in your teeth before your ears catch it, and once an hour, briefly, a drill whines through some band of harder rock and the note rolls away down the scarp and fades. That is the entire industrial soundtrack of a region two hundred kilometers wide. Quiet industry. Slow industry. Industry as patient as geology, which is presumably the point.

The animals have folded the machines into their calendars without ceremony. The ground around a working unit stays thawed year-round — waste heat, spilled warmth — and so each machine sits inside a ring of early green, a heat-oasis where midges hatch in February and the frogs chorus off-schedule. Birds roost on the frames. The haulers' footprints fill with water and become new ponds, and new ponds mean new midges, and new midges mean new birds, so that the frog choruses and the pipit flocks have begun to follow the haulers' ancient routes across the basins like satellites. The industry builds nothing, and the ecology builds itself on the industry's shadow. Both parties seem content with the arrangement.

A full day here, end to end: fog seas in the basins before dawn, burning off into that boundless pewter light. Midday thaw — the drip beginning, the frog chorus, the midge hatches boiling up off the tarns, birds pouring out of nowhere to meet them. Afternoon drizzle drifting through on no schedule, the scarp faces darkening stripe by stripe. Evening: the herds moving along the ramp routes, breath steaming, the molt-gatherers settling noisily onto the summit lakes. Night: hard frost. Rime feathering out across everything — the sedge, the rust-orange rock, the spines of the sleeping machines, the land quietly beginning to reclaim even those. And underneath it all the tick and click of frost-shatter, mineral by mineral, the only carving tool this place owns, working at a rate the uplift easily outruns.

Nothing here has a name yet. Names need things that stay put — a bend in a river, a distinctive crag — and nothing stays. The water reroutes yearly. The springs migrate uphill as the massif grows. Even the machines will be gone from any given valley within a decade, leaving nothing behind but rock faces trimmed a little too cleanly, which in a century will pass for landslide scars.

What you are looking at, if you stand on a tabletop at dusk with the rime forming and the last drips slowing, is a first draft. Give this range ten million years and those flat summits will be fins, those undrained tarns will be canyons a mile deep, the weeping will be finished and the water will know its way. None of that has happened. Right now the land is still being born, still wet, still soft-edged, holding all its water on its face like sweat — and the only witnesses are the frogs, the birds, the herds, and a handful of quiet machines that take what they came for and build nothing, promise nothing, stay nowhere at all.
