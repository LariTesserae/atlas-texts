The first thing is the light. It comes from everywhere at once — a high white diffusion with no point of origin, as if the whole sky were a lit bedsheet. There are shadows here, but barely: grey-blue smudges beneath every lip and cornice, soft at the edges, never dark enough to rest in. Squinting is a way of life. The eye waters constantly, which seems appropriate, because everything else is watering too.

You arrive, after a long climb through cloud, at the skirts of a mountain that is not a mountain. It takes a while to understand this. From below, wrapped in mist, it reads as peaks — a massif, white and grey, terraced by what you assume is geology. Then you get close enough to see the arrises. Every edge is crisp. Every course of stone meets the next with a fingernail-tight joint. This is a raised thing: thousands of terraces stacked up a manufactured slope, cistern against stair against wall against channel, packed so densely that there is no ground between presences — nowhere to stand that is not also a structure, no surface that is not also a tool. Even the air is occupied; it holds a fine perpetual beading, the mist of a place that has always just rained.

Because the mountain sweats. That is its engine. The massif stands high in cold air, and whatever warm, wet weather passes below strikes its flanks and is wrung out against the chill stone. The whole structure is a condenser. Its upper faces glisten; gutters that look ornamental are actually harvest; every roof in the upper terraces tilts a degree inward to feed a channel. Nothing here is decoration. Nothing is idle masonry. The water comes from the sky, and the mountain has been shaped, over centuries, into the shape of a cupped and waiting hand.

And it was done by hand. That is the second thing you understand, once you stop staring at the water and start looking at the joints. There is no machine-work anywhere, no cast pipe, no sawn plate. Every block was struck, dressed, and fitted by somebody's arms. The chisel marks are still legible under the thin mineral glaze that coats everything — fans and commas of tool-work, each terrace signed unconsciously by a different wrist. Some hands cut long and shallow; some chop. On a lintel near the third tier lies a hammer that someone set down and did not pick up: stone head, handle of some dark springy ribbon-rock, already furred white with new sinter, like a thing asleep.

That handle deserves attention, because there is no wood here. There is nothing green anywhere — no moss in the seams, no algae scum, no soil in any crevice. Green simply does not occur in the palette. Whatever the builders needed that grows, they could not have, so they took their flexibility from the stone itself: a micaceous schist that splits into pliable ribbons, which they wove into cable, into matting, into baskets and slings and rope. Their baskets are stone. Their ropes are stone. It is a civilization with exactly one material and an infinite patience for it.

The patience is the technology. Follow the channels upward and you find the reason for all this plumbing, and it is the most quietly astonishing thing on the mountain: the builders did not quarry their building material. They grew it. Above the residential tiers lie the mold-fields — grids upon grids of stone boxes, shoulder-high, arranged in ranks across whole terraces, each one fed by a trickle routed from the weeping upper faces. The water here is heavily charged with dissolved carbonate; run it slowly through an empty box for a decade and the box fills with clean cast stone. Wait longer and you can lift free a block smoother than any chisel would leave. The mold-fields are agriculture. It is the closest thing to farming this world permits: crops of rock ripening under thin sheets of falling water, tended by hands that visit and go.

Which raises the question of the hands, and the honest answer is that you never see them. Fresh chisel work appears. Channels that were silty last season run clear. Somewhere, always, someone has just left. The mountain is not abandoned — nothing about it is neglected — but its makers exist only as evidence: a tally scratched into the Ledger Wall, a diagram of flow-arrows chiseled beside a junction, and once, unforgettable, a line of handprints pressed into a sinter skin that was soft two hundred years ago and is now glass-hard. Generations of palms, preserved like fossils under an inch of new stone. Whoever they are, they are not here when you are. Perhaps that is a rule of the place. Perhaps it is simply courtesy.

The water itself behaves beautifully, and slowly. A drop condensed on the highest gutter spends roughly thirty years getting from the Rim to the lowest weir — decades of short journeys, channel to channel, cistern to cistern, sometimes standing nearly still in a settling tank while its minerals decide what to become. Because the water deposits faster than it cuts, this is a landscape where erosion runs backward. Nothing wears away; everything thickens. Basin rims gain lips. Stairs grow toes of translucent stone. The drip-curtains hanging under the aqueducts are knife-edged and young, glittering in the flat white light like frozen spray. The whole massif is geologically newborn and yet nothing on it looks used — every surface looks rinsed, renewed, skinned in a glaze thinner than a fingernail. Age here is measured in layers, not in wear.

It is nearly silent, and the silence is not empty — it is full of very small sounds, each one audible precisely because there is no competition and because the mist eats echoes before they can travel. You can stand on one terrace and identify individual drips on another. Each overflowing basin has a pitch, determined by the depth of its pool, and here is the slow music of the place: as decades pass and the pools silt deeper, every note falls. The city is tuned slightly lower every century. Somewhere below, the largest feature — a spillway the workers call the Throat, where the surplus finally leaps down a cut shaft — makes the only broad sound on the mountain, a wide soft hiss like a crowd heard through a wall. Even the loudest thing here whispers.

Life, such as it is, keeps to the margins of the flow. In the fastest weeps, nothing lives at all — only glassy skins of sinter. In stagnant corners, dark felts spread across the pool floors: threadlike mats, neither plant nor animal nor quite fungus, creeping a finger-width per year, staining the stone the color of wet slate. And in the spray zones grow the closest thing this world has to flowers — rosettes of aragonite needles, white and brittle and radiating outward, blooming on the walls over years. They are pure mineral. They are also unmistakably trying to be flowers, and in a place with no green, no seed, no season, nobody is in a position to tell them otherwise. Nothing hunts. Nothing flies. Nothing breathes audibly. The fauna of this mountain is the water, and it is everywhere, and it is content.

There are rules, and the residents — the stone ones and the unseen ones both — live by them. First: every surface is either a cup or a roof. There is no neutrality; anything unmaintained decides on its own whether to hold water or shed it, and it will hold. Second: gravity owns everything eventually, but on the mountain's schedule, not yours — haste accomplishes nothing in a place where the building material arrives at a trickle. Third: time is not told by the sky. The light never really angles; noon and dusk differ only by a shade of pewter. Instead the great cisterns serve as calendars — each one sized so that its fill-level marks a month, a season, a year — and the whole city functions as one enormous water-clock, patient and legible, keeping time in levels rather than hours.

On the Ledger Wall, beside the tallies and the flow-diagrams, the inscriptions turn occasionally personal. Instructions, mostly: SILT THE THIRD BASIN BEFORE THE FIRST FROST. THE WEST RIBBON-ROPE IS FOUR GENERATIONS OLD — RETIRE IT. And once, smaller, deeper-cut, as if the writer expected it to be the last thing to survive: WE DID NOT RAISE A MOUNTAIN. WE ARE GROWING ONE. DO NOT STOP THE WATER.

Perhaps the deepest impression, though, is of a project mid-sentence. The builders' plan is legible in the plumbing: the terraces march downward, tier by tier, each one's overflow feeding the next, and far below the current works there is a foundation roughed in for one final cistern — vast, unhurried, decades from receiving its first drop. When the water reaches it, the deepest basin on the mountain will begin to fill, and its note — the lowest sound this place will ever make — will begin its own long fall. The builders will likely not hear it. They seem entirely comfortable with that. Up here everything is wet, and bright, and cool, and made, and the only ambition anyone ever chiseled into the stone is to still be handing the water onward when your own hands are long since part of the glaze.
