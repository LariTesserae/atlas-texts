The first thing to understand is that you are already under. There was never a shore. You opened into this the way you open into a thought — mid-way, surrounded — and the water accepted you without ceremony. It is cold in the way that has no opinion: four degrees, everywhere, top to bottom. Because cold water is densest at exactly this temperature, the pool cannot make gradients; there is no warm ceiling telling you where the surface is, no chill floor promising depth. Your skin reports the same fact at every level, which means your body has lost its depth-sense entirely. You are suspended in a room that refuses to say how big it is. Your eyes will have to do all the work.

They can. The water is astonishingly clear — newly melted, barely lived-in — and the light comes straight down from a hard thin sky. This is the second thing to understand: the pool sits at the crown of a young mountain, high enough that the air above it is thin and blue-black and the snowline crowds the rim. You are in a cup of water balanced on the head of a peak, looking out at the world through forty feet of cold glass.

Look up. Directly overhead, the surface is a round window of sky — a disc of noon, with clouds crossing it upside down and the mountain's own snowy shoulders leaning inward around its edge. This is the honest view, the cone of light the physics allows. But tilt your gaze away from vertical and the surface stops being a window and becomes a mirror: silver, slightly wrinkled, showing you the depths reflected back at themselves. The pool is a ceiling that is half sky and half deep, and which one you get depends on nothing more than the angle of your attention. Shafts of sunlight come down through the honest part in visible columns, made solid by a faint smoke of ground glass that never settles — the mountain is young, and still shedding itself.

And the mountain *is* young. That is written on every wall. Where older lakes soften their basins into curves and cushions of silt, this one is all fracture. The walls drop in clean planes, black basalt shot with freckles of bottle-green olivine, edges still knife-honest from the splitting that opened the peak's crown a few human lifetimes ago. Talus fans of shattered glass lie on the terraces exactly where they broke, unmussed, each shard lying at the angle of its fall. Nothing has worn round. Nothing has been forgiven. Run a hand along any ledge and you come away unwounded only by luck. Even the color is unfaded — no algal velvet, no patina, just rock remembering being molten, veined with bleed-lines of orange and ochre where mineral-rich water sweats out of the seams and paints the stone in slow strokes.

On one shaded wall, facing away from the sun, there is a patch of rust-colored film thinner than a breath. This is the closest thing the pool has to a plant. It is barely alive by the standards of anywhere else — a stain, a first draft, a green thought that hasn't finished thinking itself. The pool is too young for roots. Life here arrived from below, not above, and it arrived in a hurry, and it arrived as *animals*.

Because the third thing to understand — the thing that makes this place what it is — is that the pool is industrious, and nothing in it was built.

There are no structures here. Not a tube, not a strut, not a nest. And yet the water hums like a factory floor. The industry is all bodies. Every machine in this facility is alive, and every installation is a posture that could, in principle, pack up and swim away.

Anchor yourself on a ledge and watch the rhythm section first: the bellows-worms, pale segmented cylinders thick as your torso, socketed into the wall in cradles of their own coiling. They have no legs and need none. Their entire lives are a breath — a slow inhalation lasting minutes, drawing water through the sieve of their fringed mouths, then a slower exhalation that leaves them gaunt. Four of them fire out of sync, and their combined pulse is the pool's heartbeat: a deep *whumf* you feel in your ribs before you hear it, followed by a rolling boom as the basin answers. The pool is small and hard-walled and it rings like a bell. Every sound it makes, it keeps.

Above them, on the wide terraces, sit the mills. Cog-urchins: domed animals whose shell-rims are cut into honest teeth, dozens of them parked rim-to-rim in long trains, each rotating at the speed of a shadow. Where two territories touch, the teeth engage — and they mesh, truly mesh, tooth into gap, turning against each other for seasons without a slip. Some trains have been interlocked since the pool was born, forty bodies composing a single slow mechanism that exists nowhere as an object and everywhere as a motion. Their business is grinding. They clamp fragments of fresh volcanic glass to their undersides and mill it, generation by generation, into powders sorted finer than flour, and the sound of a full terrace working is the sound of a waterfall heard from the next valley over — a sustained mineral roar, patient as geology, because it *is* geology, just with intentions.

Down on the floor move the smelters. Fat, deliberate, furnace-bellied crawlers whose abdomens are translucent panels, and behind those panels you can see the actual glow — a dull cherry ember-light, the only warmth in the world besides the faint breath of the vent. They crawl the collapse piles in slow processions, swallowing fresh glass, and their gut-chemistry does what no plant here ever could: it strips the rock. The young mountain is generous this way — its unweathered olivine drinks water and exhales hydrogen, an energy economy that runs on the stone's own freshness — and the smelters spend that inheritance, refining metals out of the rubble and venting them upward as ions. The ions ride the smelters' warm exhaust plumes to the cold lid of the pool, where the water can hold them no longer, and they precipitate out as droplets of bright liquid metal.

Which falls back down. Silver snow. On any fine morning, once the smelters have warmed to their work, the upper water begins to drizzle metal — motes of galena-bright mercury-heavy rain sinking through the sunbeams all afternoon, and hanging beneath the surface-lid, plucking droplets as they form, are the grazers: moth-winged swimmers schooling in chandeliers, hundreds of bodies to an armspan, wings beating in the slow-motion unison of cold water. From below, a grazer-chandelier looks like a constellation that decided to exist indoors. The pool's density is like this everywhere — three traffic lanes of cross-swimmers at three depths, threading each other with a precision that looks impossible until you notice that nobody is in a hurry. Everything here moves at the speed of patience. A creature crossing the pool is a multi-day event, and its neighbors adjust their weeks around it, unhurried, courteous, packed.

The loudness never stops. Even between heartbeats the last boom is still traveling, still folding off the far wall. The basin has a hum of its own — a note so low you feel it in your jaw, the pool resonating with its whole self — and layered over it: the mills' roar, the smelters' guttering hiss, the clang of urchin rims kissing, and the singers. Something large and unlit moans in registers that set the glass walls buzzing, and because the water holds its echoes for days, the pool has become an archive. Old calls do not die here; they circle, fading by increments, and on quiet stretches you hear conversations from last week murmuring under this week's, voices stacked on voices — a choir rehearsing with all of its past selves.

At the deepest point there is a throat. A mouth of smooth-rimmed rock in the floor, the wellhead of the ancient aquifer that rose up and filled this crown when the mountain cracked. Once a day it breathes: a long inhalation that sets the whole pool spiraling lazily inward, dust and silver snow bending toward the vortex like grass toward wind — then an exhalation, a column of faintly warm mineral breath rising through the clear water, shimmering, visible, gone. The fauna orient by it. It is their factory whistle, their north star, their noon bell. When it sighs, the mills quicken; when it settles, the pools-wide shift eases.

Death here is slow, and the slowness looks like ceremony. When one of the ferrymen fails — the broad, grave giants who carry whole colonies of passengers on their backs, living barges of an economy with no cargo — the parsers come. Pale, many-jointed, unhurried, they arrive in single file and take the body apart over weeks, element by element, each atom routed onward to a grazer, a worm, the rust-film on the north wall. Nothing is wasted because nothing is owned. That is the deepest rule of the place, deeper than the throat: metal is weather here, not wealth. The smelters smelt it, the sky-snow spends it, the grazers gather it, and by winter it has all changed hands. An economy of pure flow, in a bowl a hundred paces across.

It will not last — but that is said softly here, where "not last" means ages. The mountain's freshness is the fuel, and the mountain is eroding even now, shard by unmussed shard; in some slow millennium the hydrogen will thin, the furnaces will bank, the trains will turn down to almost-stillness, and the pool will hear its first silence and not know the word for it. But on the shaded wall, the film is spreading another inch. Rust-colored, patient, photosynthetic at last — the pool's first actual plant, drinking the very sunlight the machines never learned to taste. Whatever inherits this water when the industry winds down is already here, thinking its green thought, getting ready.
