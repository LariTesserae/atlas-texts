There is no surface here. That is the first thing the water teaches you — that you had assumed one, all your life, the way you assume a sky, and that assumption has been gently taken away. Above you there are four thousand meters of sea, and then a bright rumor called the top, which no living thing has visited in any memory that survives. Below you there are six thousand more, descending in the dark to a plain so flat and so finished that nothing there has happened for fifty million years. You are on the shoulder of a drowned mountain range, halfway between the two, and the water is warm as blood and dim as dusk.

The light deserves description before anything else, because nothing here is lit so much as *suffused*. There is no direction to it. It does not fall; it does not come from anywhere; it is simply present in the medium, the way warmth is present, a gray-green clarity that extends perhaps forty meters before it gives out into grainy haze. On some days the illumination seems to arrive slightly *after* the objects it reveals, so that a sponge column swims up out of nothing a heartbeat after you have already sensed it there — the world developing like a photograph of itself. Old water, the drifters say, though drifters say little now. Old water remembers light and gives it back late.

The range you stand upon is the skeleton of mountains. Nothing sharp survives here; that is the governing law of the place, older than stone. Every ridge has been worn to a whaleback curve. Every canyon wall is polished to a dull gloss, banded like taffy where the rock itself has begun, over the unthinkable span of its exposure, to *creep* — flowing downward at the pace of a fingernail per century, so that the mountains are slowly forgetting they were ever mountains, settling into slopes of repose, into drapes of sediment laid down a meter every hundred thousand years. Run your hand along a cliff face and it feels like the flank of an animal. In places the stone has flowed entirely shut around old caves, sealing them like scars. The geology is not dead. It is merely very, very tired, and moving the way tired things move: inevitably, and toward rest.

And yet nothing about this place is still.

The sea has weather. Not the wind-torn kind — there is no wind, no air, no surface to catch it — but weather nonetheless, continental in its gestures. Salinity fronts roll across the plateau the size of horizons, visible as trembling curtains where waters of different density meet and refuse to mix, and where they pass, the forests bloom in slow response over weeks. Thermal rain falls inside the water itself: cold droplets condensing in the warm layers and sinking through them in silver streams, thousands of tiny descents, a storm of sinking. In the basins lie lakes — actual lakes, ponds of heavier brine sitting on the seafloor with their own shorelines, their own waves lapping at banks of rippled sediment, their own dim shore-haze. Small fish-less currents comb their surfaces. You can wade into one up to your chest and stand in a sea inside the sea, looking out across a small grey ocean that has never known a gull.

Warmth rises from the range itself. Along the old fault lines, hydrothermal seeps exhale shimmering veils of heated water, and the life crowds toward them. The water near a seep is bathic, body-warm, faintly mineral on the tongue — iron and sulfur and something older, a taste like the inside of the planet remembering itself. Between the seeps the temperature drops in stepped shelves, and each shelf holds its own community, stacked vertically through the water column: dense, layered, crowded. There is more life per cubic meter here than the dimness would ever suggest. Much of it is plant.

The forests are the great fact of the plateau. Groves of glass sponge rise sixty meters, transparent columns that catch the sourceless light and hold it, so that at distance a grove looks like a stand of frozen lightning. They grow perhaps a centimeter a year; the tallest have been growing since before the ruins below were built, whoever built them. Between the groves spread meadows of dark fronds — kelp-descended things that barely photosynthesize anymore, that feed mostly on the chemistry seeping from the stone, bottle-green and bronze and a black so deep it reads as absence. Their blades move constantly in the slow surge, a motion like sleeping breathing, and when the nutrient fronts arrive they spawn together, whole leagues of them at once, filling the water with spore-smoke so thick the haze turns milky and the light diffuses into a pale gold fog that lasts, in some years, a full season. On the canyon walls hang gardens of tube-colonies and feather-dusters, waving. Underfoot, wherever the slope gentles, spreads microbial turf — sulfur-pink, iron-black, oil-sheened — soft as moss and old as the plain below.

The animals are another matter. Do not trust your eyes here.

Most of what moves is replay. The plankton haze of this sea has burned-in afterimages the way old screens do: the light itself occasionally blooms into the shape of a creature long extinct, swimming its old routes through the groves, feeding on nothing, casting no shadow. Shoals of ghosts. You learn to tell them — the replays are too regular, too confident, repeating the same turn at the same rock every time — and once you learn, the sea grows lonelier, because you realize how rarely anything *real* crosses you. When it does, it matters. A translucent grazer the size of a hand, pulsing past your shoulder, warm-blooded-warm somehow. A flatfish-like elder lying in the turf, eyes tracked with age rings, watching you with the unhurried attention of something that has outlived its entire lineage and knows it. Once in an era — and you may live your whole life on this plateau and never see it — a leviathan passes along the deep channel to the east: cathedral-sized, slow as weather, a grazer that mows the frond-meadows as it goes, so that forests bow in sequence for a day afterward, and the currents it displaces are felt at the vents as a long sigh. Predator-prey is mostly finished here. What remains of eating is mutualism, grazing, patience. Everyone left is old, and few, and gentle.

The ruins confirm the gentleness. Whoever stood here before the flooding — if standing is the right posture for them — used no tools you would recognize as tools. Nothing with an edge survives anywhere, and it is unclear whether anything with an edge was ever made. What remains are patient structures: rings of stones that were cairns, now rounded to river-torpor; mounds of cemented shells and tubes, dwellings grown as much as built, secreted by hands over years of deliberate waiting; a spiral pecked into a basalt boulder, worn down to almost nothing. Almost — because this is where the sea's great age turns strange, stranger than the brine-lakes, stranger than the rain that falls inside itself. Some mornings, in certain slants of the sourceless light, the spiral *reappears*. Faintly, like writing bleeding through a palimpsest, the groove returns to the stone for an hour and is gone. The water here has worn thin in places. Ten million years of dissolution have not only rounded the mountains; they have thinned the laws. Cause and effect have loosened like old stitching. Ripples sometimes converge *inward* on a disturbance before it happens, and the water flinches, and a moment later something falls. Distances disagree with themselves: two swimmers who separate without turning find they have converged. Behind a tilted slab near the third terrace, seconds pool — linger there and emerge to find the spawn-fog advanced, the season turned, your own hands unfamiliar.

On the highest ridge stands a tower. Half-dissolved, topless, its material neither stone nor shell nor bone but something that partook of all three, it is the only vertical thing on the range besides the sponges. Nothing about it explains itself, and the sea has erased every clue with perfect impartiality — except that around it, always, lies a circle of absolute stillness, twenty meters wide, where the surge does not reach and the turf does not grow and the water is silent in a way that silence elsewhere is not. The drifters' ancestors, the middens suggest, came here. Left offerings of shells arranged in the stillness. Took nothing. Asked, perhaps, whatever one asks of a structure that predates the flood and outlasted the askers.

Sound is scarce, and what exists arrives oddly. Pressure is the constant — a fullness in the ears, the weight of an ocean overhead pressing down kindly, like a held hand. Beyond that: the tick of sponge growth, audible if you press your ear to the glass columns, a centimeter a year made of tiny clicks. The hiss of sand avalanching off a ridge. And once a decade, felt rather than heard, a moan from the deep channels — the leviathan, or the range itself settling, or the plain below speaking in its sleep — so low it passes through your ribs before your ears know anything has occurred. Then quiet again. The quiet of a place that has said everything already and is content to repeat itself.

Because that is what the dynamism here finally is: repetition worn smooth. The tides breathe against the slopes. The fronts roll. The forests bloom, spawn, and wait. The vents exhale, the stone creeps, the sediment accumulates its meter per age. None of it goes anywhere. Erosion long ago reduced the repertoire of this world to a handful of motions, and those motions cycle now like a heart beating in its last, longest, most peaceful decade — not change, but circulation. The sea is not dying. The sea is *finished*, in the way a sentence is finished, and everything that happens here is the resonance of the completed thing, ringing.

Far above, the bright rumor. Far below, the final plain, where all slopes end and even the spiral in the stone will lie down someday. Between them: warm dim water, glass forests, the smell of iron, the tower keeping its stillness, and the rare, real, astonishing passage of a single animal — alive, unfinished, temporary — moving through the oldest calm place there is.
