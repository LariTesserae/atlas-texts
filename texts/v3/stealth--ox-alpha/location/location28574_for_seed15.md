# The New Water

You could pace it out in forty steps, corner to corner. A rectangular room sunk below the level of the shore, open to the sky, walled in dressed limestone and holding seawater to about the height of a chest at flood tide. From the paved terrace above, you would look down into it and find it glowing — which is the first thing to understand: this is a room that shines from the inside, like a lamp set into the ground.

The glow comes from the water itself, and the water is not clear so much as *young*. It is still full of stone flour, the fine pale dust of the chisels that made everything here, and that suspended milk turns every beam of sun into a solid object — slanted columns of light standing in the water, tilting slowly westward through the afternoon, laying a trembling net of reflections across the walls. The white limestone takes the light and gives it back warmer. Looking down through it, the floor seems nearer than it is; looking up, the surface is a ceiling of shaken silver. There is exactly one darkness in the entire room: the square of the central drain, where the bronze grille has gone sea-green and the conduit below drops away into honest black.

Everything you could touch here was cut by hand. No bedrock shows anywhere; whatever original stone lies beneath the chamber is buried under its own dressing. The blocks fit without a gap, their joints still packed with mortar pale as chalk, unstained by any century. Chisel flutes run across the faces in neat overlapping fans. The arrises — the long edges where two dressed planes meet — are sharp enough to sting a fingertip drawn along them. And on faces turned inward, half-hidden, the masons left their marks: small glyphs pecked into the stone, a circle with a tail, three strokes like bird tracks, a spiral. Signatures on work someone expected either nobody to see, or everybody, forever.

The room was furnished before it was flooded. Steps descend in one corner, each tread dished slightly for grip by the adze. A bench runs the length of the north wall, and beneath it clay pipes emerge from the masonry and stop — aimed toward a furnace room that lies somewhere else, or nowhere. Lamp niches are cut into the wall at intervals, their little cups clean, never smoked. Along another wall a frieze of interlocking waves advances for six feet and stops mid-block, the design trailing off into blank stone at the exact width of a resting chisel. Stacked on the ledge above the waterline sit a dozen more dressed blocks, squared and ready, waiting to become a wall that apparently was never needed — or was never wanted.

The sea gets in through a channel cut low in the seaward wall, lined with stone and closed, at will, by a sliding bronze plate. Left open, it makes the pool breathe. Each swell outside sends a pulse down the channel: the water here rises the width of a finger, hesitates, and draws back with a long hollow sigh. Twice a day the exchange runs deeper, the whole room climbing and sinking through a foot of height as the tide turns. The pool does not keep sun-time or bell-time. It keeps sea-time, and everyone in it must adjust.

Which is why an empty room can be loud. The channel gulps and exhales on its rhythm of seconds. After a larger surge slops over the parapet, drips tick down the inner face, each one returning an echo doubled by the hard geometry of the walls. When wind crosses the open top at the right angle, the room hums one low note, resonant, patient. Whispers come back enlarged; a dropped coin would ring like a struck bowl. And from far above the rim, sometimes, gull cries — the only animal voices in earshot, and they never come down. They belong to the weather, not to this place.

Warmth settles here in layers. By late afternoon the surface skin is bathwater, sun-beaten and still; sink a hand and you find a cool tongue sliding along the floor, the incoming tide keeping its own temperature, so that one pool holds two seasons of water at once, stratified and slow to mix. The stone banks its noon like a stove. Long after the light has gone copper and the beams have stretched and thinned, the lower courses of the wall still radiate the day back outward, warming the water from the sides.

Life has begun, but it is obeying the wrong rules — or the right ones for a built world. Green threads follow the mortar joints the way moss follows the cracks between city flags, tracing the builder's lines rather than spreading as nature spreads. On the sunniest floor flags a golden felt of diatoms has taken hold, thin enough to wipe away with a thumb. In one shaded corner a stain of red blooms quietly where the current never reaches. Seeds blow in over the parapet and sprout in the silt pockets of the steps — and die at the next serious surge, so that the room greens and browns and greens again in small experimental gestures. Nothing here eats anything else. There are no mouths to feed. The only metabolism is the slow green arithmetic of the film on the stones, and it grows strictly along the geometry: along joints, along seams, along the chisel's path. Never yet across an open face.

And the animals do not come. That is the strangest ordinary thing about the place. The lowest courses stand permanently awash, the sea pressing right there beyond the wall, and not a single barnacle has fixed to the stone. The drain grille guards a dark doorway and no crab sits in it. Fish gather at the channel mouth sometimes — visible as shadows at the lip of the light — and turn away. Whatever passes, passes. Spores drift in and settle; larvae arrive and starve or leave; the room stays sterile the way a new house stays silent before the family moves in.

Everywhere, the youth of it. A salt crust is beginning its first inch above the waterline, white and crisp. Veils of quarry dust lie settled on every horizontal surface — the actual footprint of the work, still drifting down. No edge has rounded, no corner softened, nothing has learned to be old. The flood that filled this room is younger than one winter.

As for the people: they left in the middle of a sentence. A wooden mallet sits on the bench, its handle bleached pale. Coils of new rope hang by the steps. The half-carved wave waits for its continuation. Whether the sea came early, uninvited, or was let in on purpose through that bronze-gated channel, the stones decline to say — and the room, which repeats every sound it hears, has somehow kept this one quiet.

At dusk the beams go long and amber, the channel's voice slows to a whisper, and the pool exhales its last warm breath of stored daylight. Under the surface, just visible, the masons' marks sit on their hidden faces like a letter held face-up, waiting for its first reader. Nothing here has ended. Everything here has only started.
