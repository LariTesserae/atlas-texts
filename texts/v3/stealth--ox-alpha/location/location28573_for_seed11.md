You hear it three days before you understand it. Climbing through the last forest belt, there is a sound beneath the sound of your own footsteps — a broad, unbroken hiss, like the sea heard through a hillside. By the second day it has swallowed birdsong. By the third it is the whole world's voice, and when you finally crest the rim of the summit country, you realize the sound was never getting closer. You were climbing *into* it.

This is a mountaintop with the manners of an ocean floor. The summit plain runs farther than the eye insists on believing — two days' walk to the nearest fount, five to the far spring-seeps, and beyond them more plain, hazed to nothing at the edge of the sky. The thin air is so clean that distance stops meaning anything; a dome of rock eight miles off looks close enough to throw a stone upon. And the stone — the stone is the strangest thing here, though it takes a while to see why.

It is *finished.*

Every ridge, every dome and whale-backed swell of the plateau has been worked by wind and time past all further improvement. Whatever grit the gales ever carried, whatever ice ever pried, they took it all ages ago. What remains is what could not be moved: a shield of case-hardened rock, mineral-armored over eons until its skin grew harder than anything beneath it, so the wind can no longer carve. It can only polish. And it polishes endlessly, vainly, a sculptor working a statue that left the studio ten million years ago. The domes gleam like the backs of submerged animals. Run your hand along one and it is glassy, faintly rippled, warm —

— warm, always warm. Blood-warm, even at night, even in wind. The mountain's heart is slow and stubborn, still giving off the heat of its own deep formation, and the whole plateau lies on it like a bed. The people sleep directly on the stone here, palms and cheeks pressed flat, and say it is the only honest warmth left in the world.

Kneel anywhere and look closely: the rock is full of spirals. Shells. Small, perfect, fossilized, at four miles above the sea. And wandering the high ground are dry channels — branching, meandering, unmistakably riverbeds — carved when this summit lay somewhere else entirely, under different skies, perhaps under water. The rivers died so long ago that even their memory has turned to stone. Geologically speaking, nothing here is happening. The place has settled into its last shape the way an old man settles into a chair. All the turbulence left in this world happens above the ground.

Because the water never falls here. Rain gave up on this altitude before the rivers did. What water comes, comes as breath. Each evening the warm stone and the cold starlit sky conspire: every exposed surface cools faster than the air around it, sinks below the dew point, and begins, silently, to drink. By midnight the whole plateau is beaded silver. By dawn it is raining *upward* — droplets swelling out of rock and leaf, gathering weight, running. Every dawn, for an hour, the finished world drips.

That hour is the economy of everything alive up here.

The founts stand miles apart, alone on the gray, and from a distance they look like green fireworks caught mid-burst — or like fountains indeed, plumes of frond spraying outward and arcing down from crowns forty spans wide. They are not trees, quite. Each trunk is a braided colony, dozens of fused stems, buttressed into its own private island of root-stone and trapped soil. The fronds are engineered around a single purpose: catch dew, shed inward. At sunrise every fount rains on *itself*, channels the silver down ribbed leaves into cupped axils, into moss-gardens, into the dark reservoir at its heart where the water sits warm and sweet. A mature funt holds a pond in its chest.

And so each one is a world. In a single crown: mosses, hanging fern-analogues, pollinating things that live and die without once touching ground, a species of small chattering climber found on that one fount and nowhere else on earth — because nowhere else exists, for it. Twelve green plumes on a hundred miles of stone, each with its own Noah's cargo. Walk between them and you pass through hours of absolutely bare rock, wind-scoured, shell-flecked, empty. Lushness and emptiness are not contradictions here. The lushness is just very concentrated, and jealously hoarded.

When a fount dies — and they do, slowly, over decades, a limb at a time, browning from the crown down like a slow explosion in reverse — its seeds do not sprout on the open stone. The wind would shred a seedling in an afternoon. They sprout in only one kind of place, and to explain that place, you have to stand in it.

The Roar is not one sound. It is a chord that never resolves: a broadband hiss of air on stone, a fleet of deeper drones where the gale finds fissures and plays them, sudden booming gusts that arrive like dropped cargo, and overhead, all day, all night, the ragged tearing of the jetstream itself. There are fields of breathholes on the eastern plain — vertical flues in the rock, some wider than a hut — and the wind plays them like a stopped organ, bass notes you feel in your molars half a mile away. The people have been cutting new slits and tuning old ones for two thousand generations. They live inside an instrument and they have opinions about its tuning.

But the gusts come from everywhere, and sometimes they come from opposite directions at once — and where opposing streams meet and cancel, there is a seam. A hush.

You stumble into one by accident the first time: a doorway of stillness in the roaring air, sometimes no wider than your shoulders, sometimes a whole sunken court between domes where the noise simply ends. Inside, your own pulse is embarrassingly loud. Your breath sounds like surf. People come from miles to stand in one — to speak aloud for the first time in a season, to say vows, to name infants, to sit with the dying. The dead are carried to the far hushes and left in the quiet, because everyone agrees that after a lifetime of the Roar, the least one deserves is silence.

And the founts knew it first. Every seedling grove on this plateau grows in a hush. Life here begins in stillness, or not at all.

The people themselves are few — you might walk a week and meet one family's worth of cairns. They are short, wind-squinted, with forearms so expressive they seem to be gesturing even at rest, because outdoors their voices are useless and they know it. Conversation is conducted with fingers, wrists, taps on stone. At night you can eavesdrop on a whole village by pressing your ear to any rock: gossip travels through the warm granite in code, tap-tap, pause, tap. Children learn hand-speech before they learn to risk their throats against the wind.

Their dwellings hug the lees of boulders and fount buttresses: low dry-stone teardrops, thatched with fount-frond stalks, the roofs netted down with stones against the gales. Fire lives in ember-cairns — squat stone lanterns with slit vents that whistle a steady note, part furnace, part windbreak, part music. Nobody owns fire here; households carry coals from cairn to cairn in clay pots, and lighting a new flame from scratch, in that wind, is a feat attempted perhaps once a year, with witnesses.

They farm the dew. Nets of plant fiber on wooden frames, angled to the cold sky; dew-ladders lashed against fount trunks to intercept the overflow the tree doesn't claim; clay tanks sealed with resin, sunk into the warm stone so the water stays drinkable. Around the spring-seeps — places where the mountain's own sweat emerges, warm and mineral — they line shallow ponds with clay and guard them the way lowlanders guard gold. Their tools are quartzite flakes, bone awls, cords twisted from frond-silk. Nothing metal has ever been smelted on the Old Back. Nothing needed to be. The stone was already perfect; they only learned to listen to it.

The animals are few and strange, as animals must be in a world with so little middle ground. Gliders ride the gust-fronts — wide membranes, hollow bones, minds like hinges — spending their whole lives aloft on the chaos the ground-dwellers suffer, landing only to die, whereupon other, larger gliders attend the body. In the fount-crowns, the little climbers drink from the reservoir hearts and have never known thirst or ground. In the rock cracks live fat sealed things, amphibious and patient, that cocoon themselves in mucus against the dry months and wake only for dew-season, blinking, ancient-faced. And once, at dusk, you may see something flat and low flow across the path ahead of you — a belly-scaler, a listener, hunting not by eye or ear but by reading your footfalls through the warm stone itself. The people walk lightly near dusk. Some of them walk in deliberately wrong rhythms, patterns that scramble the reading. It is the closest thing this world has to a monster, and even it is mostly quiet.

The weather is the only drama left, and it spends extravagantly. Fog arrives off the drop-off like a tide of milk and erases the world to ten paces, then tears open into light so clean it stings. Gust-fronts come visible across the plain as darkened sheets of stirred dust and pollen, chasing their own shadows. Cloud-shadow races over the domes at speeds that make the whole plateau flicker like a struck drumhead. A day here can hold four seasons' moods, all of them loud.

At dawn, if you press both palms to a dome and hold very still, you may feel it beneath the roar: a slow pulse, rising from far below, every few minutes, regular as something asleep. The shamans call it the Old Back dreaming. Anyone with different words is welcome to them; the stone keeps its own counsel either way.

Night is the tenderest thing here. Stars hard and close in the thin air, the warm rock at your back slowly giving up the day, dew beginning to bead on your lashes and the cairn-whistle moaning its one note, and the Roar all around you — no longer an enemy by then, just the sound the finished world makes while it waits. Nothing on this summit will ever be young again. No peak will rise, no canyon open, no river run. It is the oldest kind of beauty there is: the kind that endures by having nothing left to lose, singing the whole while — not because it hopes to change, but because, having finished, this is simply what it does instead.
