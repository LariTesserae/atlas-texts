You come up the last slope on hands and knees, and then the ground levels, and you are inside it.

It takes a moment to understand how small it is. The whole place — shelf, pools, rim, sky — would fit inside a large farm. You could walk its length in under an hour, and you can hear all of it at once: water ticking off stone somewhere behind you, a chorus swelling near the pools, a thin metallic pulse that seems to come from everywhere, like a heartbeat heard through a pillow. There is no horizon. Where the land ends, a pale haze begins, close enough that the far rim looks like the edge of a table seen through milk.

The air is the first thing that decides you'll stay a while. Warm — nearly blood-warm — and saturated, the kind of warmth that doesn't evaporate sweat so much as negotiate with it. Every breath tastes faintly mineral, like the air off a spring. Your clothes go damp in minutes and stay that way. Above, there is no sun. The light comes from nowhere in particular: a diffuse amber-grey glow, as if the entire dome of the sky were a lamp behind frosted glass, dialed to late-afternoon and left there.

**The stone is new.**

This is the thing you keep returning to. The rock underfoot has no strata. No fossils, no veins, no history — break a nodule loose and the inside is as bright as cut fruit, unoxidized, almost wet-looking. Edges everywhere are crisp, unweathered, the way fractured stone looks in its first hour. Yet the stone is warm to the palm, warmer than the air, as if recently poured. In places you can find a seam where two pours met, a hairline of glassier material, and you understand that you are standing on something *cured* rather than something ancient.

Nothing here is old. That is the governing fact, and everything else follows from it.

Because the rock is young and no channels have been cut, the water has nowhere decided to go. It sits just beneath the surface of everything. Along the curved escarpment at the north of the shelf, groundwater exits through hundreds of seeps at once — a wall that weeps, hung with bead-strings of water, dark with wet, already streaked with the first mineral stains: ochre, a green so vivid it looks lit from within. Colors here haven't had time to dull.

In the low center, water gathers in wide, level sheets — terraced pools, mirror-flat between rains, because erosion hasn't taught the water to carve yet. At one corner the overflow finds a lip and goes over: the world's first waterfall, knee-high, running down the world's first ravine, which is a hand's depth and about a season old. You can crouch and put your eye level with it. Given a hundred years this groove becomes a gorge; given ten thousand, a valley. You are looking at the first centimeter of all of that.

And the water hesitates. Where two flows meet at a junction the machine redrew last cycle, the stream stalls, pools, trembles — visibly unsure — before committing to the new channel. Water here has a memory of a few days. It keeps trying to go where it went yesterday.

**Look up during the mist.**

When the fog thickens, you start to see it: droplets strung in long lines across the sky at impossible heights and irrational angles, hanging in perfect rows that catch the amber light. The mist is beading on something — hair-fine filaments, a lattice stretched over the whole pocket, invisible until the air gives it away. A loom. The world is stretched on a warp you're not supposed to see, and can only see when it rains.

Small things commute along those lines. Palm-sized, six-legged, soft-bodied — dew-grazers — they move head-down along the filaments, licking droplets off the strands, their bodies pulsing gently like slow hearts. When the lattice is dry they descend and graze the felt-mats instead. They are everywhere. They are, as far as you can tell, patient and content.

**The green is a rumor.**

Vegetation exists, but barely, and shyly. Felts of moss-analog pressed into damp seams, colored olive to bruise-violet. Crusts of lichen-paint stippling the rock in rings and whorls — whoever seeded them used broad strokes. A dozen pioneer trees, perhaps, on the whole shelf, standing absurdly far apart, each alone in its own acre: thin, adolescent things with translucent leaves, still figuring out wood. Their trunks flex in wind that barely exists. After each rain the ferns unfurl within minutes — eager, overshooting — then retract slightly, embarrassed, when the surface dries.

Nothing rots here. That takes getting used to. Fallen leaves don't decay; they cure — crisping over days into thin glass husks that hold the leaf's exact veins, tinkling underfoot like broken ornaments. Eventually the husks dissolve into the soil as silica, and the pioneer trees drink their own ancestors. Around the base of each grey stake driven into the ground — and the stakes are everywhere, spaced wide, humming just below hearing — rings of mushrooms appear overnight, every mushroom in a ring exactly the same age, exactly the same size. The ground is being dosed. The fungi are the dose made visible.

**The noise is the animals.**

Flora is sparse; fauna is not. Whatever ordered this place seeded its creatures generously and its plants stingily, and the result is an ecosystem running hot on very little fuel — generalists, opportunists, things that eat mist and mineral film and each other. Over the pools at the dim hour, columns of midge-analogues rise and twist, thousands-strong, catching whatever the air carries. In the shallows, tadpole-shaped sifters hang translucent in the water, their tiny organs visibly sorting sediment, the whole pool quietly chewing.

A herd of five soil-makers — capybara-sized, slow, armored in dull plates — works the fresher rock at the shelf's edge, grazing the mineral skin, excreting something dark and crumbly that will, in a few thousand generations, be called dirt. They are the plow. They do not know it.

And twice a cycle, the chorus. From the reed-margins of the pools, amphibian voices build in layers — at first scattered, then synchronized, then *dense*, a chord that tightens and rises toward some threshold you can't hear but they can. You learn the pattern by your third day. The chorus swells. Holds. Breaks —

— and the rain arrives.

Not metaphorically. The irrigation listens. Whether the singers are following the schedule or *driving* it, no one here can say anymore, and it stopped mattering some cycles ago. The animals conduct the weather. On the day the chorus misjudges — one singer too eager, a half-tone early — the rain comes early too, stuttering, apologetic, and you watch a filament high overhead flicker like a nerve firing, adjusting, forgiving.

There is one predator, because there has to be. A wading stalker works the sheet-water margins: heron-tall, grey, patient, with a mouth where a beak should be. Everything else keeps one eye on it. The system counts its strikes. Somewhere, presumably, someone decided the equation needed a term like this.

Some of the fliers are mistakes, and everyone tolerates them. Gliders with paper-pale wings — a few asymmetric, first-draft bodies that should have been recalled and weren't. They fly anyway, crookedly, with tremendous commitment. Life here improvises faster than the design anticipated, and the design, apparently, has decided to allow it.

**The one building.**

At the highest point stands the only structure: an open pavilion, slender, four legs and a roof of something that isn't quite glass, holding — suspended in nothing, touching nothing — a slowly turning form like a lens crossed with a bell, black, seamless. It turns once per cycle. When it completes its revolution, the light dims toward the blue interlude the animals treat as night. Its tick is audible everywhere on the shelf; the acoustics here have been *tuned*, and the tick arrives at every point at once, the way a thought does.

Nearby, a half-buried slab of the same black material, mostly sunk in young mud, warm, faintly pulsing, shaped to fit no hand you own. Once, while you stand near it reading the stakes' etched indices — coordinates, a serial number, nothing else — a filament drifts down from the lattice, hovers at the level of your mouth, samples your breath for three seconds, and withdraws politely upward. You are logged. You are, you suspect, welcome.

**The rim.**

Walk to the edge. The rim is rougher, brighter rock, steaming faintly where seepwater meets stone too newly cured to have cooled, and it is *rising*. Measurably. A finger-width a week. Stand there at cycle-change and you'll feel it: a deep shudder underfoot, a breath held and released, as new rock is added from beneath.

Throw a stone over. It arcs out, enters the haze, slows — not falling so much as being declined — and comes back to you on a gentle updraft, landing at your feet with a click, like change returned. The world does not accept deposits yet. Outside the boundary, distance stops behaving; the haze has no resolution; sound sent out that way never comes back, but sound sent *upward* comes back enriched, harmonized, as if the air itself wants to sing in tune with whatever you offer it.

**The cycle**, then, laid out as you eventually come to know it: warmth and bright haze; condensation beading the hidden loom; the chorus tightening; rain — warm, thorough, drumming on ten thousand glass leaf-husks; the little waterfall running hard, sandbars migrating in the pools where you can watch them move; steam lifting off warm stone; then the blue dim hour, brief, when fungus-crusts and pool-margins glow a soft green and the midge-columns shine, and the only sounds are dripping, and the tick, and the stalker's feet in the shallows.

Once — only once in your stay — the system hiccups: rain falls upward for ninety seconds, a soft silver reversal, while somewhere a filament flickers erratically. Then a patch of ground magnetizes for an hour and the pebbles stand on end in little crowds, leaning together like conspirators, until it passes. The dew-grazers don't even pause.

On your last evening you understand the actual rule of the place, the one under all the others. Someone drew this landscape — every seep placed, every stake surveyed, the rain scheduled, the acoustics tuned, a complete and careful blueprint for a world in its first draft. And the animals are already ruining it. Singing ahead of schedule. Grazing the wrong rocks. Going feral in small, irreversible ways. And the machinery, instead of correcting them, keeps quietly rewriting itself around what they do.

The blueprint wanted a garden. What's growing is a world.

Behind you, at the pools, a single singer tries the first note of the chorus, out of order, early, hopeful. The filament lattice flickers. Somewhere overhead the great lens pauses, considers, and — you'd swear — waits for the rest of them to join in.

The rain is coming. You count along.
