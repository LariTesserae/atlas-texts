# The Undermoor

The moor above is thin grass over white rock, wind-scoured, full of holes. Sheep avoid it. The shepherds who cut peat up there know better than to graze the high pasture, because the pasture is a lid — a few arm-lengths of soil and stone, and beneath it a country. On calm nights you can find the vents by feel: upwellings of cool air that smell of wet chalk and mushroom rot, with a sweetness under it like fruit gone soft, and beneath *that* the sharp farmyard tang of ten million animals.

They call the whole system the Undermoor. Nobody made that name; it has simply been agreed on, the way the depth of a well is agreed on. And nobody lives down there. Nothing has ever been built down there. But nothing down there is empty either.

**The way in**

You enter through a collapse throat on the north slope — a slanted hall of rubble where ferns grow in the last usable light and then give up. The rock around the entrance is startlingly fresh: fracture faces like broken porcelain, conchoidal shells glittering, edges you could shave with. Everywhere else in the world, stone rounds itself off with age. Here every scar looks an hour old. Woodcutters on the moor will tell you the Undermoor broke open in a single night, long ago, and that it has been breaking open ever since — the same break, held.

The light follows you down for thirty steps and quits. After that you walk in a gray gloaming that comes from nowhere in particular, and then your eyes adjust, and you understand where it comes from: the ceiling. Stars. Hundreds of pale blue points hanging on threads of silk, each one a glowing larva fishing for moths. The galleries away from the collapse mouths are lit the way a room is lit by starlight through a window — enough to see shapes, not enough to read a face. Under the great vertical shafts — the sky-wells, where the lid failed entirely — daylight falls in silver columns through drifting mist, and for a few paces around each one you could read large print at noon.

**The country**

It is a country down there, not a cave. The passages run for hours. Shepherds who have descended on a dare talk about districts the way they talk about valleys on the surface: the Chimney, the Roost Vaults, the Root Halls, the Note Pools, the Long Empty.

The Chimney is a shaft you could drop a church spire into, rising sheer to a coin of moor-sky. A current of air pours down it in one direction, at one speed, forever — the Undermoor's single held breath. Standing in it is like standing under a cold waterfall made of air; you walk out of it and the cave closes around you again, twelve degrees, humid as a mouth.

The greatest of the vaults could swallow a town square and stack the cathedral twice. Sixty meters up, its warm air meets the cold drainage from a hairline crack in the lid, and a cloud forms — a flat gray ceiling of permanent drizzle, raining gently onto black stone, the same rain in the same season it has rained since the mountain cracked. The floor drinks it as fast as it falls. Puddles never grow. Rivers never cut.

Between the districts lie the Long Empty corridors: kilometers of scalloped passage, sharp-edged, dustless, silent enough that your own blood is loud in your ears. The Undermoor keeps its wealth the way a poor country keeps cities — in a few dense places, with wilderness of pure vacancy between them. You can walk an hour through nothing and then round a bend into a sound that knocks you back a step.

**The roar**

Because the Roost Vaults are full. That is the first fact of the Undermoor, the fact that reorders everything else: the vaults are full, and they are loud. Millions of free-tailed bats hang in the upper dark in curtains and columns, and the mass of them makes a sound like surf heard through a hillside — a broadband roar with shrieks braided through it, and under the shrieks the squealing of pups in nursery creches, rising and settling in slow waves. Near the great clusters the air itself is warm, twenty degrees and rising, thick with the smell of ammonia and hot fur; the bats' metabolism runs a heater the size of a house, and the mist plumes above it like breath in winter.

The wingbeat traffic never stops. Bats pour up the sky-wells toward the night moor and others pour down from it, and somehow — this is part of the lore, one of the three laws the shepherds recite — the ledger always balances. However many leave, that many return. Walk into the Roost at any hour, on any visit, and the census comes out identical. The roar never swells. The roar never fades. It is not silence that is eternal down there; it is noise.
