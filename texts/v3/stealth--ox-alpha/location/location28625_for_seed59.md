# Holdfast

You hear the place a full turn of the path before you see it — a rolling boil of sound lifting off the moor, part birdsong, part water, part something percussive and human underneath. Then the path crests a sedge flat, the marsh opens out gray and glittering to every horizon, and there it is: a single green thunderhead parked on the only hill for miles.

The moor itself is a drowned country — an upland gone soft, holding rain the way a bowl holds it, chain after chain of dark pools linked by black channels, sedge to the edge of seeing. Nothing rises out of it anywhere. Except this. One hill, bare-backed and russet along its flanks, its summit swallowed whole by a dome of foliage. Threads of smoke stand up out of the green. Herons cross between the reeds and the crown in a slow commuter traffic, going and returning, going and returning.

The path becomes a causeway, then a stair of stone and root, and as you climb, the sound resolves into layers you will spend the rest of your visit learning to separate.

Here is the thing the first view cannot tell you: the town on the hill is one tree.

Not metaphorically. A fig — or something figlike, something with the patience and the appetite of a fig — seeded the crown of this hill uncounted generations ago and began to walk. Its roots ran down the slopes like fingers closing around a fistful of something precious, and every root that touched ground thickened into a trunk, and the trunks leaned into each other and fused, and now there are thousands of trunks sharing a single circulatory system, a single intention. What looks from the marsh like a forest is one organism wearing the shape of a crowd. The people — three hundred or so, give or take a season — did not build this town. They found it half-made, the stories agree on that much and nothing else, and they finished making it. Or it finished making them. Ask three elders and receive four answers.

Nothing dead bears weight in Holdfast, because nothing dead lasts — the wet sees to that. A felled beam left in the open is mush by the following autumn, swollen and black and giving way at a touch. So the town is constructed entirely of persuaded living wood. Limbs are tension-trained: pulled down with ropes and slung stones over years until the grain gives up and accepts the curve permanently. Walls are aerial roots let down on purpose from selected branches — a wall planted in spring is shoulder-high by winter, and if you keep its tip clipped it thickens instead of climbing, becoming a pillar, a jamb, a lintel. Joints are grafts. There are no nails because there is no metal, and no need: green wood cuts like cheese under a sharp stone adze, and two trees pressed firmly together for long enough become one tree, which is a stronger joint than any forge ever made.

Floors, where a floor is wanted sooner than a limb will provide one, are platforms of lashed sapling, replaced every generation, creaking pleasantly underfoot. Everything else grows from the ground or the wall: benches that swell out of the baseboards, table-stubs, bed-niches lined with moss mattresses harvested and refreshed monthly from the shaded side-streets. Windows are absences — gaps kept open by someone with shears coming weekly, or the green closes them like flesh over a wound. Doors are root-arcs polished glass-smooth by a century of hands.

Streets are corridors of kept light between trunks, wandering, sometimes roofed, named for how they misbehave rather than where they go. Dripping Stair. Leak Lane. The districts stack in vertical strata like shelves: the Undercroft along the hill's wet skirt, half cellar, half harbor; the middle tiers of dwellings wrapped around their own light-wells; the Rookery in the western trunks; the Nursery Terraces near the crown where seedlings are raised like livestock; and overhead, everywhere, the canopy — the roof of the whole town, so thick that rain takes a full minute to reach the street, arriving as a hundred thousand separate decisions.

The hill sweats. That is the honest description. The moor is soaked to the bedrock and the bedrock here rises, and the water wants through this hill forever, pressing upward, looking for exits. It finds them at mid-slope: a ring of springs, a hundred weeps and seeps and dribbles, each caught in a clay lip and led onward. The largest feeds the Rill — a channel of split gourd and hollowed trunk that runs the crown's entire circumference, spilling downward through smaller rills to cisterns on every level. There is no private water and no need for it. Drinking points stand everywhere, cup-shells hanging on knotted cords, and you drink when you pass, the way you breathe.

Everything drips. Strings of beads gather at every eave and let go one at a time, all day, all night, into moss basins that drink and darken and overflow into the next. Most afternoons a shower comes up warm off the moor and crosses the hill in fifteen minutes, and the town simply steps aside under leaf without breaking conversation, then resumes. In the Undercroft they don't bother stepping aside. Down there you are wet to various depths at all times and have made peace with it.

And the town makes its own weather, which keeps it cool. Thousands of leaves exhaling into moving air pull the temperature down and down; morning mist stands ankle-deep in the streets and pours off the crown's edges in a slow silent waterfall that vanishes into the moor-light. The folk wear felted wool and oiled bark-cloth. Their hands are red with chill most of the year. Breath ghosts in the upper galleries on certain dawns. Nobody minds. The cold here is the clean kind — the cold of moving water — and the alternative, down on the marsh flats, is worse in a different way.

Light, on ordinary days, is silver: the moor's overcast snagging on the crown and fraying into shafts. Coins of sun travel the floors when the clouds bother to organize. Upper tiers are bright, middle tiers green-gold, the Undercroft dim as a church interior. At dusk the pitch-lamps come out — little clay saucers of resin, one to a landing — and from outside the whole town shows as a constellation strung through leaves.

There are more animals than people in Holdfast. There have never not been.

Bats pour out of the roof at dusk in a river that lasts the length of a song and hunt the marsh all night, returning at dawn heavy and chittering; sheet-clay trays beneath their roosts collect the guano that feeds the moss gardens, which clothe the beds and chink the walls. Swiftlets glue their shelf-nests to the walls of the darker galleries, and all night the corridors patter with their echo-clicks, a soft rain of sonar. Herons own the western trunks outright in nesting season — the Rookery, a deafening, pungent, entirely marvelous slum of stick platforms and squabbling chicks — and the folk count it fair rent for the way the hunting herons flush fish into the rills, where the fish are penned and netted at leisure. Frogs crowd the spring-line: small gold-eyed ones in the moss cushions, deep-voiced drummers in the pool-chains below, and at dusk their chorus swells like a tide coming in until the whole hill thrums with it. Eels live in the cisterns by long treaty — they eat the larvae, and once a year, at the Eel-Tide, the treaty is renewed in the other direction. A civetlike climber raids the granaries, is cursed, is secretly fed at the town's edge so that it stays at the town's edge, and is known to every child by name. Bees keep the hollow of the Mother Trunk, and their swarms are caught and given away like daughters.
