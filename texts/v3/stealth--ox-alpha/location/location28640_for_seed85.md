# The Parish of the Held Rain

First, understand the water. It is everywhere, and it does not move.

It is cool — cellar-cool, the same exact coolness at every depth and every distance, without current, without season, without the faintest thermal shiver. It presses gently and identically on all sides, like the interior of a held breath. And it is *still* in a way water should never be. Swim through it and it parts for you precisely where you are and closes behind you without wake, without swirl, without a single eddy, the way a solid relents only grudgingly and only locally. Fast movement is possible but feels obscene, like shouting in a chapel. Everything here happens at a drift.

If you exhale, your bubbles do not rise. They hang beside your cheek, a string of perfect glass spheres marking your passage, and they will hang there until you physically push them aside, hours later, exactly where you left them.

This is the Parish. It is small — you could swim its width in an hour, if swimming quickly weren't a kind of blasphemy — and it is bounded, though the boundary has no wall. It occupies the summit of a seamount so young it embarrasses the geology around it: a volcanic peak that heaved itself up from the abyssal plain recently enough that its basalt is still glassy, still bearing the first cracks of its cooling, unsoftened by any sediment. There is no silt on it. There has never been silt on it. The stone is bare down to the molecule — no film, no fuzz, no mineral bloom, nothing green or nearly-green or formerly-green. Whatever life is, it was never invited here. Only admitted, rarely, on sufferance.

The summit is the highest solid ground for a thousand miles in any direction — and it is drowned anyway, two thousand meters beneath an actual surface no one here has ever seen. Elevation in the Parish is a relative mercy. The city floats above the peak, in altitude if not in air, and the only sky it gets is one that was made for it.

Look up, at the top of the parish's bounds: the rain.

Water enters this place from above as discrete drops, and each drop, the instant it crosses the horizon, *stops*. Billions of them. A ceiling of hanged rain, every bead fixed in place like a jewel in resin, motionless, each one holding a single pinpoint of reflected lamplight from the city below. Looking up is like looking into a false night sky — thousands of tiny inverted stars, and every star is one of the city's lamps, seen once, refracted, kept. It never falls. It never thickens. It is raining up there in the sense that rain is arranged up there, and it will be raining in that arrangement forever.

---

The city itself is a scatter, not a mass. Perhaps sixty structures distributed across the summit plain and up into the water column above it — spires, drums, open arches, slender towers trailing taut-lines (cables of constant length that have never stretched and will never slacken) — each one separated from its neighbors by hundreds of meters of deliberate, zoned emptiness. The nothing-between-things is as carefully designed as the things. Six of the structures are inhabited. The rest are instruments, anchors, archives, or dark — and the dark ones have always been dark; their shadows stand beneath them like additional architecture, razor-edged, permanent, because no lamp moves and no sun crosses the water to shift them.

The buildings look *new*. All of them. Always. Seams sharp enough to draw blood, edges unrounded, colors structural rather than painted and therefore incapable of fading — matte ceramic-white, bone-colored lattice, prismatic filaments in the joints, windows of vacuum that show nothing at all, pure black panes pretending to be apertures onto further darkness. The city looks like it was finished yesterday morning. It has looked like that for longer than anyone can measure, because measurement of duration doesn't work here and no one pretends otherwise. Young and ageless at once: this is the parish's central paradox and its simplest fact, and the fact is this —

*Nothing here changes unless it is changed on purpose.*

No grain settles. No seam dulls. Warmth does not disperse — a swimmer's body sheds a human-shaped ghost of heat that clings and then simply stays where left, and another swimmer crossing that spot an hour later will pass through you like a memory. Clocks run, technically, but they disagree, and no two have ever been reconciled. Even cause flinches here. Strike a hull and the sound dies within arm's reach — a dry click, like knuckle on glass, gone before it can become echo. Speech works at conversational distance and nowhere beyond; words arrive pre-shrunk. Voices here are naturally whispers, partly by necessity and partly because loudness feels like vandalism.

And underneath all that hush, one sound: a single continuous note, far below hearing, felt as a pressure behind the sternum rather than heard. It does not pulse. It does not pause. Whether it has been sounding for a year or for ten thousand years is a question the parish declines to answer. The keepers call it the reading voice.

---

Because that is what maintains this place. Not engines. *Attention.*

The Founders understood something about watching. A thing continuously observed cannot change — and the Founders scaled that truth until it could hold a city. But the observing must be done by something that moves, since the water certainly won't, and so the parish has its readers: seven pale creatures, translucent as peeled paper, radial, eyeless or many-eyed (opinions differ; they do not stay still enough to be counted confidently, and the count never changes, which may prove there are seven or may prove that counting doesn't work here). They range in size from a handspan to a door. They propel themselves on rings of force-cilia that grip the water directly, and they follow fixed circuits among the structures, pausing at nodes to dock and press their bellies briefly to the material.

Where they touch, a hairline of brightness runs along the seam and then ceases, cleanly, like a switch — not a glow fading but a glow *ending*. That touch is the parish's heartbeat. Under a reader's passage, broken things return to their founding condition. Keepers bring cracked tools and chipped dishes to the circuit-paths and wait, sometimes days, for a lector to drift over; the repair is not mending but *restoration*, the object restored to its recorded self.

An object the readers ignore is called *unwritten*, and unwritten things are the only things in the parish that can age, rust, rot, or break. Everyone begs the readers to look at their belongings. People sleep with their tools pointed toward the circuit-paths. The same courtesy extends, in principle, to people: the register holds forty names, and it has held forty names since anyone remembers, because no one has been born here, no one has aged, and no one has died — except those who left, or were never read in at all. A newcomer the lectors decline to notice remains unwritten: hair growing, candles burning, the only weathered face in town, pitied openly and watched warily, because a mortal body is a walking source of entropy and the parish can feel it.

The forty keepers maintain nothing. Maintenance is meaningless here. Their occupation is witness — they sit at their windows in the lamplight, in the enormous silences, attending to the city the way a congregation attends to scripture, adding their gaze to the readers', keeping the count going. There is an archive, naturally: a complete record of the parish's state, written into material as changeless as the parish itself, so that if the reading ever stops — if the last lector fails its round, if the note behind the sternum ever breaks off — the place could, in theory, be begun again. No one has ever needed to read it. That is the point. The archive exists for the day everyone prays never arrives, and it is dustless, and it will wait.

---

At the boundary — and you find it not by sight but by texture, a line where the silence changes flavor, where the water begins, faintly, to *remember* — the rules relax. Cross it and your exhaled bubbles suddenly recall gravity and race upward, the first falling thing you'll have seen in weeks, and it is strangely heartbreaking to watch them go.

But that's outside. Inside, you drift between the hard unblinking lamps, whose light does not scatter or soften — beams pulled taut as wire, darkness between them absolute, since there is no marine snow to catch a ray and forgive it. Above you, the hanged rain holds its fall, billions of beads each cradling one borrowed star. Below you, the young mountain keeps its first cracks. Behind your lips, your next breath gathers — warm, mortal, unwritten.

You are the only process in the parish. The only weather. The one thing here that will not stay.

Exhale slowly. Leave your pearls beside you in the dark, exactly where you were.
