# The Spindle

There is one vertical thing.

That is the first fact of this place, and in a way the only fact; everything else is elaboration. The world is a chamber of warm water — you could learn its full inventory in a lifetime, and some residents surely have — sealed like a jar, walled in dark plate that a strong swimmer could circle in a day. Above, far above, a lid. Below, a floor. And rising between them, floor to lid, without break or joint that anyone has found: the Spindle. A built thing. A mast, a mill, a plumb line dropped by someone who wanted to know the depth of a room and never hauled the string back up.

Seen whole, from the middle distances where most life keeps itself, the Spindle is almost nothing — a hairline, a scratch on the lens of the water, trembling slightly with its own working. Swim closer and it becomes a wall. Cling to it and it becomes a country. This is the paradox the place runs on: the smallest world imaginable, containing the longest distance anything could fall. Up and down are the only directions here. North and south were never invented.

The water is bath-warm in the upper reaches, cooled by veins that thread down from somewhere in the machine's cold side, and everywhere it moves. It never stops moving. That is the second fact: the Spindle works, and the sea is its workload.

---

Every two hundred heartbeats — everyone here counts in heartbeats, though the Spindle counts too, in its own way — the engine takes its stroke. You feel it before you hear it: a tremor in the teeth, a tightening behind the eyes. Then the boom arrives, rolling up out of the hull like a cathedral door closing underwater, and then the current *leans*, the whole sea tipping a degree or two toward the intake grates, and loose debris, sleeping grazers, drifting spores all slide gently in the same direction. Then release. The water sighs back. Somewhere below, turbines sing their descending note.

This is the tide. There is no moon to make one, so the machine makes it instead — drinks and discharges, inhales and exhales — and everything alive has arranged itself around that rhythm the way shore-life arranges itself around the moon's. Grazers feed on the lean. Filterers open on the release. The gardens irrigate on the stroke. It is loud here, ceaselessly loud, in the way a body is loud if you live inside it: vent-hiss and valve-tick, the groan of old metal taking load, echo-trains that chase each other up the shaft and come back seconds later softened into rumble. Silence is not one of the materials this place was built from.

And the Spindle is warm. Heat bleeds from it constantly, rising in visible columns — the water shimmers where the warm discharge meets the cold returns, curtain after curtain of mirage hanging in the open dark like aurora laid sideways. When the warm and cold fronts argue badly, the chamber gets weather: pressure storms that ripple through your bones, salt coming out of solution as slow snow, brine-falls dropping from the middle dark in ropes heavier than the sea around them. Storms pass. Blooms follow. The machine pumps nutrients up from the silt-floor and for days afterward the water goes green-gold and thick with life, and every garden-ring on the hull erupts.

---

Which brings us to what grows here, because things grow here *magnificently*.

The gardens happen in bands. Ring-terraces circle the Spindle at generous intervals — old maintenance galleries, someone's agricultural ambition from an era no one can date — and in those bands the growth is riotous. Ribbon-kelp streams off the railings in twenty-meter fronds. Bladder-fruits bob in their rows, fattening on engine-warmth. Crusted lawns of filter-moss coat every horizontal surface, grazing themselves right up to the edges of things. Flowers here are not metaphor; pale four-petal things open exactly on the stroke-boom and shut on the release, ten thousand of them along a terrace opening at once like a held breath let go.

Then the band ends, and there is nothing. Hull, plain hull, for a thousand meters of ascent — bare plate furred only with mineral rime, crossed occasionally by a shoal passing through like weather, or by nothing at all. The emptinesses are as much a feature as the gardens. This place is mostly room. Presences occur in it the way islands occur in an archipelago: deliberately, with sea between. You can hang alone in the open middle dark, equidistant from everything, hearing the engine boom arrive from below and its echo arrive from above, and feel like the only tenant of an enormous instrument.

But you are never actually alone. The fauna would embarrass a reef.

Shoal-fish in ribbons and helixes, silvering when the lid-light catches them. Slotted-shell grazers trundling the moss-lawns like small armored carts. Cleaner-claws working the valve seams, picking parasites and corrosion-lice off the machinery with the tenderness of surgeons. Long pale migrants — whale-things, taper-shaped, soft-glowing at the snout — that spend the cold season in the deep silt-dark and rise, when the engine runs warm, all the way to the lid to spawn in the brightness, their songs pouring down the shaft for days. Lantern-jawed hunters hanging motionless in the mid-heights, patient as furniture, taking the unwary from the shoals. And stranger tenants, the ones that blur the line between animal and maintenance: star-polyps crusted over weeping seams, sealing hairline leaks with their own bodies, fed fat by engine-warmth; tube-worms colonizing the pipes in neat rows like rivets; a species of deliberate crab that sorts the intake-grate debris, hauling out what jams and piling it in tidy middens, and no one taught it, or someone did so long ago that the teaching has become instinct.

The Spindle does not seem to mind. It is difficult, watching the crabs work, to say where the machine ends and its ecology begins.

---

Now touch the hull, and understand how old this is.

Everything here has finished eroding. That is the eerie gift of the place: it is older than wearing. Every edge has been rounded, over uncountable time, to exactly the radius its current prefers — no more, no less. Bolt-heads stand up smooth as river stones. Railings have worn to the softness of ship's rope. Nameplates survive in a dozen places, and on each, the same story: raised lettering polished to ghosts, two or three strokes legible, the rest a shallow shine where meaning used to be. Paint persists in exactly one spot — a flake of red sheltered in a shadowed notch, kept like a museum piece, the last color of the builder's world.

Nothing degrades anymore. Rust itself has stabilized into rinds thick as bark, and the rinds are habitat: whole civilizations of small things live in the corrosion-crust the way birds live in cliff-face. Even the floor agrees. The silt at the bottom of the chamber is not natural sand — nothing here is natural, there is no natural substrate anywhere, the floor itself is cast plate — it is ground century: hull-dust, shell-fragment, slag-grain, worn fine and blended into a single gray-gold sediment that records nothing because everything in it has already been worn the same. The place has settled into its final form. Whatever the Spindle was made to do, it will go on doing it in exactly this condition, this shape, these surfaces, until the lid fails or forever, whichever comes first.

---

A few more rules, since the place enforces them anyway.

Falling is slow here and rarely fatal; the water forgives, the way is cushioned, and more than one grazer has ridden a long drop to the silt and climbed back up over a season. Sound is architecture — you hear around corners, you hear *through* the hull, and the wise navigate by ear as much as eye. The pulse is the hour; the bloom is the month; the migrant-rise is the year. You do not sleep across an intake. You do not anchor a garden over a discharge vent. And at the very top of the Spindle, just under the lid, there is a door — handholds below it sized for hands, some broad, some slight, a script beside it in strokes no resident can read — and the door opens on its own schedule, which is to say: rarely, quietly, and never for anyone watching.

Whether the builders went up through it, or inward, becoming the crabs and the polyps and the patience of the machinery, is a question the place declines to answer. It is not waiting for anything. It has finished waiting. What remains is work, warmth, and growth.

Evening, now — the lid-light dimming from noon-white to ember, the terrace flowers folding on the stroke, the gardens pulling in like fists. The engine draws its long breath. Down in the middle dark, one of the pale migrants lifts its voice and begins the long note, and the note climbs the shaft, gathering echo, louder than the water, louder than the years, and the Spindle carries it the way it carries everything: steadily, warmly, all the way up.
