Come at dusk, or come at dawn. Those are the honest hours. From the wet meadow above, you would walk past it — the rim of the place is a low ring of fitted stones you could step over if you didn't know to stop, hardly a knee high, sitting in grass that grows strangely bright toward it, as if the ground approved of the arrangement. There is no door. There is no sign. The first indication that the world here has been *arranged* is the sound, which reaches you before the sight does: a soft industry of dripping, pitched — you notice this only after a while, and then you cannot stop noticing — pitched, as if on purpose.

This is the Weirs. Children call it the Frog Cathedral, which is funny because it is small — a hamlet could sit on its footprint, and the chambers inside are room-sized, and most of what it contains is air. It is a hole in the world with a lid of sky, and it is entirely made. That is the first thing to understand, and the hardest to hold onto: not one surface in it is natural. The mud at the bottom was carried down in baskets and packed by hand to seal the pools — puddled clay, the oldest waterproofing there is. The gravel is graded. The walls are dressed stone laid without mortar in the lower courses and with clay mortar above, every block chosen, every face worked. Even the one alder leaning over the northern rim, the only tree in the place, is argued about: some say it seeded itself and no one had the heart to pull it; others swear it was planted, and that its roots drinking from the second-basin channel are part of the design — the tree sipping the flat water all summer so the algae never choke the pool. Either way, it is the closest thing to wild in here, and it is compromised.

Descend by the Stair. Forty-one steps, everyone says, though no one counts the same number twice, and the local etiquette is that you do not count aloud. The treads are broad and shallow, worn into shallow dishes by centuries of feet and rain, so that each step holds a spoonful of standing water, and each spoonful is a small world: a tadpole here, a colony of diving beetles there, a skin of ice in winter with a bubble trapped under it like a held note. Climb down the ladder of ponds. Look closely at the risers and you will see how all of this was made, because the makers had no iron — or almost none; there are two copper staples, green with age, binding a cracked slab on the fourth landing, and they are as rare in this place as teeth in birds. Everything else was done with antler picks and cord saws and sand and water and time. The tool marks remain: adze scallops overlapping like fish scales, drill-core scars in the walls where holes were bored straight and clean, grooves worn into the lintels where ropes once ran, polished into the stone by rope alone. The finest lines were cut with cord and abrasive slurry, a stroke at a time, by people who had patience instead of steel. You feel the patience everywhere. It is the dominant material.

The water explains the rest. Dig a spade's depth anywhere in the surrounding meadow and the hole fills; the water table here lies just under the turf like a mood. The builders did not fight this. They invited it down. The whole complex is plumbed — weep-channels cut behind the walls so the stone sweats rather than leaks, drip-stones coaxed into forming at the exact points the designers wanted, sluice-gates of blackened oak with bone pivots and fired-clay tiles, an overflow runnel that slips out under the eastern rim toward the rushes, the only road in or out that a fish could take. Nothing floods. Nothing dries. The walls weep constantly, a fine cold perspiration, and everything below the drip-line beads with condensation: the rails, the spiderwebs — the webs especially, strung across the mouths of dark channels and hung with droplets, so that the emptiness itself wears pearls.

And the level never changes. This is the second thing to understand. Rain for nine days, drought for ninety — the third weir keeps its line within a knuckle's width. There are scratches on the sluice-post recording flood-years going back further than any memory in the district, and the scratches all sit at the same height. There is presumably a mechanism — a float-valve, a counterweight, some cunning of the old engineers — but it has been found, and examined, and it is just a gate, an ordinary gate. The water behaves as if the place still has a keeper. Which brings us to the listener, later.

Light comes down the Throat: a single shaft, wide as a well, opening off-center through the crown of the complex, plus a scatter of pinholes too small to name. Because the place is sunken and the Throat is narrow, daylight arrives late and leaves early; the Weirs keep their own private dusk, cool and gray-green, an hour offset from the world above. Midges discover the beam and turn it into a column of weather of their own making. For a few weeks around midsummer, on clear mornings, a coin of actual sunlight crosses the lowest floor — the locals call it the sun-hour, and everything in the place knows it: the whitest fish rise to warm themselves in it, the newts haul out onto the lit stone, and the whole dim hall attends, briefly, to a different version of itself.

Because the place is loud. That surprises everyone; a hole in the ground ought to be quiet. But hollow chambers amplify, and these chambers were cut to pitch. Each basin speaks in its own note when a drop falls into it — the shallow stair-pools tick, the second basin answers a low wooden *tok*, the Deep says *donn* like a struck bell under a blanket — and the builders tuned the ceilings, it seems, the way you would tune a drum. Stand still on the second terrace and the drips resolve into a scattered, patient melody, always changing, never random-feeling. Say *here* in the chamber called the Choir and somewhere deeper, after a pause slightly too long to be a simple echo, comes *here*, politely, from a direction the geometry does not permit. Do not whistle below the rim; the local rule is that the Weirs whistle back better than you, and will keep doing it after you stop.

At dawn, the birds. The upper walls are drilled with dozens of neat roost-holes — bored on purpose, it looks like, though for what original tenant nobody knows — and they belong now to the swifts. When they leave, they leave all at once, pouring up the Throat in a spiral, and the shaft becomes a flute: the displaced air sounds one long rising moan, a bottle blown across, and you hear it in your sternum. Some mornings a hobby hangs above the rim like a thought, and the exodus bends around it, a river of wings parting for weather. At dusk the ownership reverses: bats pour down where the swifts poured up, clicking in registers the chambers kindly return to them, and the frogs begin.

The frogs deserve their own paragraph. They are the congregation, the resident population, the reason for the children's name. Clouds of tadpoles shade the stair-pools; adults occupy every weir-edge and wet step, and at dusk they sing into an instrument — that is the only honest way to put it. The basins gather their voices and give them back enlarged, blended, sustained, until the whole sunken complex hums like a struck harp left out in the rain. A night heron fishes the second weir through all of it, standing like a folded umbrella, doing the heron's perfect nothing, and every few minutes the water gives up another frog, and the chorus does not pause for grief. In the Deep swim eels and small blind fish, white as peeled garlic, and sometimes at night you hear the soft slap of an eel taking a moth off the surface, then the polite rings. Snails graze the algae films in clean lanes, writing lines through the green that fade behind them. Dragonfly husks cling at the waterlines like shed gloves.

Two more things, and then you may climb out.

The first: watch the weeping walls. Where the seepage runs, the stone darkens, and in the darkening, figures appear — hands, dozens of small hands, palm-out, pressed as if into plaster, child-sized, arranged in loose rows. They are carved, faintly, under everything; the water merely chooses which ones to show. Different hands on different days, depending on which seam is feeding the wall. Whether the makers meant them as signatures, or prayers, or a record of who was present when the digging finished, no one can say. The hands do not appear dry. They wait.

The second: twice a year, roughly — the elders say near the equinox rains, when the flow through every channel comes into momentary balance — the Weirs play themselves. All the drip-notes align. The basins speak together. What it sounds like, no one manages to describe; the attempts are always embarrassed. The frogs go quiet when it begins. The swifts, if it catches them at the rim at dawn, hold where they are. It lasts perhaps the length of a slow breath, and then the tuning drifts apart again and the ordinary dripping resumes, patient, scattered, forgiving. There is a listener who comes down — not every year, but often enough that the ritual survives — an old woman with an antler tuning-fork, which she strikes against her knee and holds up in the Choir without correcting anything at all. She says the place is nearly in tune again. She leaves a pebble on the top step. Others have begun leaving pebbles too, in imitation, without quite knowing why, so that the top step is becoming a little cairn, which is also, if you think about it, a kind of note.

And the place is slowly turning to stone — properly to stone, I mean, in the geological sense. The same water that rounds the sharp edges off the old cuts also carries lime, and the lime heals what it wears: calcite seams weld crack to crack, flowstone skins the weirs, the clay mortar is being replaced joint by joint by mineral glass. Erosion and masonry have reached their truce. Give it enough millennia and the Weirs will be indistinguishable from a hill that always was — except that the swifts will still hold the drilled holes, and the drilled holes will still be there, because the water rebuilds around its tenants, never over them.

Climb out at night, when you go. The mist lies in the basins in layers by then, each terrace carrying its own low cloud, and the frog chorus has thinned to a few stubborn tenors, and the drips continue their patient retuning in the dark. Before you step over the rim, look back once. Each stair-pool is holding its own few stars — the entire sky inventoried there in spoons — and somewhere below the last terrace, unhurried, in a voice like water finding a channel, the dark says *here*.
