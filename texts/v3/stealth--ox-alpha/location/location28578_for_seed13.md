# The Heldmark

Come up the last ramp onto the plateau and the first thing you notice is the edges. Every edge here is a first edge. The cliffs stand in slabs as clean as quarried blocks, their fracture faces still glossy where the rock cooled fast, their corners unrounded by any century of weather. Talus fields lie beneath them in long aprons of angular stone — and the stones are *leaning*. Whole slopes of them lean at the angle of incipient fall, tilted the way a rock tilts in the moment before it rolls, and they have been in that moment for longer than anyone can say. This is a young country that will never grow old. It was lifted up sharp, settled into itself quickly, and then — this is the thing about the Heldmark, the thing every visitor eventually stops trying to explain away — it stopped.

Not dead. Stopped. The distinction takes a day or two to understand.

The plateau proper is forty miles of high ground, rimmed by scarps and ice-held peaks, and its surface is unsettlingly level — planed flat by whatever force raised it, sloping almost imperceptibly toward a chain of shallow basins. There are no gradients here, in either sense. Nothing steepens, nothing slackens. Water does not carve, because water does not run long enough or hard enough; instead it arrives on a schedule.

The schedule goes like this. The nights are cold enough that by first light every surface on the plateau — every leaf, every wall, every railing and rooftop and blade of grass — has sweated a skin of condensation. At sunrise the whole world drips at once. For perhaps half an hour the city rings softly with it: ten thousand small waters ticking off eaves, running down channels cut into the stone streets long ago, filling cisterns, feeding rills that thread between the buildings and out into the terraced fields. Then the bright high-altitude sun climbs, the air — thin, crystalline, cool enough to see your breath at dawn but never cold — drinks the moisture back, and by midmorning everything is dry again until the next dawn. Once or twice a season a rain front crosses the rim, and it is always the same rain front, the same grey sail of cloud, the same measured hours of falling, the same puddles in the same hollows. The cloud empties exactly what it carried. The ground drinks exactly what fell.

Out of this stingy, faithful damp comes a shocking lushness. The plants have learned to eat the air. Moss lies on the north faces of stones in felt pads an inch thick. Terraced garden beds are paved with dark radiating slabs that pull the cold out of the night sky and comb the dew down little stone funnels into the roots below. Trees stand in perfect full leaf — every leaf unfurled, none overgrown, no branch lankier than another — as if each one had grown for exactly as long as a tree should and then simply continued, complete. Flowers hold mid-bloom for years. Orchards bear fruit that ripens on its old clock and is picked, and eaten, and the ledger balances: what is taken from the trees is returned to the soil, what the soil spends the trees reclaim, and no orchard has grown an inch heavier or lighter in living memory. Everything here moves. Nothing trends.

The city is how you notice the difference between *sparse* and *empty*. By any measure it is a city — thousands of dwellings, granaries, cistern-yards, plazas, a web of causeways and cut stairways linking district to district across miles of high ground — but it is a city poured thin, like metal across a wide mold. The buildings stand one and two stories, dry-stone below and dark timber above, each set apart from its neighbors by gardens, meadow, or bare shelves of rock. The streets are wide and mostly vacant. The plazas are vast, and in the middle of the largest one a seep-fed fountain falls in a veil off a stone lip, and every drop strikes the pool at the same point, and the splash opens in the same small crown, second after second, hour after hour. You can stand there a long time before you realize why it bothers your eye: it isn't repetitive. It's *identical*.

Because nothing wears, nothing needs repair — and the crafts continue anyway. Workshops ring with chisel taps at the same hours they always have. A chair carved this year is indistinguishable from chairs carved a thousand years ago, because style cannot drift when nothing pushes it, and the stock of things stays constant: what a builder raises, another dismantles, the stones going back into the common yards to be raised again elsewhere. Masonry as circulation. Weaving as breathing. The oldest joints in the oldest walls are as tight as the day they were fitted, and the masons walk past them without pride, because pride requires improvement and improvement requires a direction, and the Heldmark has none.

People live here, threaded among the fauna — and the fauna are everywhere, abundant, busy. Herds of small antlered grazers come down to lick the dawn off the stone at the same minute each morning. Flightless rails commute along the tops of the courtyard walls in single file, punctual as water. Raptors climb a thermal that rises off the south scarp at midday and ride it in the same slow spirals. Moths with dew-drinking tongues work the moss pads at dusk. Foxes hunt, and their hunts succeed exactly as often as they fail; the pools hold silver fish, and every year the census-takers count them and arrive at the same number, and this is not disappointment but liturgy. Counting is the holiest act on the plateau. To count and find the sum unchanged is to confirm the covenant: every birth answered by a death somewhere, every kill by a litter, every mouth fed from a store that refills as it drains. The books always balance. The world is a wheel turning at constant speed, and its axle does not move.

The people are quiet the way the land is quiet. Sound here does not accumulate into roar; it stays discrete. You hear a woman sweeping a plaza that collects no dust — she sweeps anyway, the bristles hissing a soft rhythm, and it looks like prayer. You hear a loom, a hammer, a child's shout that carries a mile in the clean thin air and is gone. You hear the bell in the central tower struck once at dusk, a note so familiar to the inhabitants that they no longer hear it, though visitors report dreaming of it for weeks. At night the silence is nearly total, and the stars come out hard and white and unwavering, and if there is a moon it keeps the same face and the same appointments, rising over the east scarp like a functionary.

They have a language suited to the place. Verbs conjugate identically for yesterday and tomorrow — one word serves both — and only *now* gets a form of its own. Their oldest stories describe change as a foreign country, full of wonders and horrors both: things wearing out, things getting better, hills losing their edges. They tell these stories with a shiver. Their myths agree that once the world was still settling, still drifting, and that in those generations the ancestors watched processes slowly converge — rains matching evaporation, births matching deaths, growth matching rot — until one morning the last imbalance closed and the world *set*, like glue going hard, like a note resolving. They call that morning the Still. Nothing has changed since, including their certainty that nothing ever will.

Whether that certainty is peace or a kind of vertigo depends, visitors say, on how long you stay. On the third day you find yourself checking the fountain's splash-crown against your memory of it, and finding no discrepancy, and feeling something turn over quietly in your chest. On the tenth day you stop checking.

And the Heldmark receives your tenth day exactly as it received your first: the dew coming up off the black stone at dawn, the rills singing their half-hour song, the herds descending to drink the morning off the world — the same morning, always the same morning, held out flat and bright under the hard stars like a bowl of water that has never once spilled.
