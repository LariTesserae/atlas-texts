# The Plain at the Bottom of the Old Sea

Begin with the pressure, because everything here begins with the pressure. Four hundred atmospheres, give or take — a weight that has stopped being weight and become simply the shape of the world. The water holds you the way a hand holds a grape: gently, totally, without interest. And it is cold — a degree or two above freezing, a cold with nothing left to prove, a cold that has been cold since before there was anything alive to feel it.

The dark is not empty. That is the first thing to understand. Turn on a light — you must carry your own light here; the sun gave up on this place three vertical miles ago — and the beam does not cut through the water so much as stain it. The sea down here is *full*. Suspended clay, fine as smoke; the perpetually falling drift called marine snow, which is not snow but everything else: dust blown off continents, ash, pollen, the dissolved remains of creatures that lived and died in the bright upper leagues. It falls a millimeter per thousand years. It is falling now. It has always been falling. Snow that takes weeks to land, in a sky that goes up farther than most skies go sideways.

And below that sky: the floor.

## The Flattest End of the World

The plain announces itself as an absence of announcement. No wall rises to meet your light. No slope tilts away. Over a kilometer of travel the land may consent to rise a single meter, and that is topography here. The plain is so old it has forgotten how to be dramatic. Mountains stood in this basin once — every ocean starts loud — but the mountains went to powder ages ago, and the powder fell, and the falling never stopped, and now relief itself has been worn down to a rumor.

Look closely at what the light does reach. The ground is pelagic clay, rust-colored, fine as talcum, cold and slightly greasy to the touch — the slowest sediment on Earth, accumulated from almost nothing, which is why it took a hundred million years to get this deep. And scattered across it, as far as any beam can swing: nodules. Knuckle-sized to potato-sized stones, black as cooled slag, smooth on top, sitting on the clay like fruit fallen from a tree that isn't there. Each one grew a molecule at a time out of the seawater itself — iron, manganese, a skin of metal deposited over epochs — and each one began around a nucleus that was once alive: a shark's tooth, the ear-bone of a whale, a fragment of something that died ten million years before it became a stone. Every rock in this field is a fossil wearing armor it grew for itself. There are fields of them to every horizon. Nothing built them. Nothing placed them. They are simply the oldest stable thing this world knows how to make, and it makes them the way frost makes patterns: patiently, everywhere, all at once.

Here and there a storm has peeled the clay back and left a window: pillow basalt beneath, green-black and rounded, lava that met cold water a hundred million years ago and froze mid-gesture. Even these old wounds wear the same black manganese skin. Anything the plain exposes long enough, it plates in metal. Nothing here stays raw.

## Features, Such As They Are

Travel far enough — and "far enough" here means days of drifting over sameness — and the plain offers up its landmarks, each one modest, each one enormous in implication.

There are sediment waves: ridges of mud hundreds of meters from crest to crest and only a few meters tall, dunes raised grain by grain by currents you will never feel, so gentle in profile that only a sideways light betrays them. There are the ghost channels — the abandoned beds of rivers that ran *here*, underwater rivers of sand called turbidity currents, which pour off the distant continental slope when something shakes loose up there, and walk across the plain for hours at the speed of a car, brown thunderheads in the dark, carving meanders as they go. Most of the channels are dry now, leveed high with their own old spills, fossilized plumbing. Any of them may wake again.

There is a hill — one of the last, a stump of volcanic rock the storms never finished, furred with black corals and basket stars that raise their coiled arms into the flow like antennae. And there, in a shallow dish of the clay: a lake. An actual lake, on the floor of the sea — brine, salt-saturated, heavier than the water around it, lying in its depression with a visible surface, a shoreline, ripples that spread when a nodule rolls in. The water above it shimmers like heat-haze inverted. Nothing that swims into it swims out. Around its rim, mussels in thousands, fed by bacteria that eat the methane seeping up through the salt — a cold oasis, chemically lit, the only "vegetation" for kilometers being gardens of microbes and the pale filament-mats that coat the seeps like frost.

## The Weather

Twice a day, far away on the flanks of the basin, the internal tide breaks — waves in the deep boundaries of the sea, crashing against the underwater slopes the way surf crashes on a beach, except the beach is three miles down and the surf takes days to arrive here, reaching the plain as a slow breath: the murky bottom layer inhales, thins, exhales, thickens.

And sometimes — once a decade, once a century — a storm comes. Not a surface storm; a *benthic* storm, a month of fast, grinding bottom current that strips the clay, blinds every light into pure glare, marches the nodules along the floor with a sound like distant applause, and redraws the ripple-marks on dunes the size of counties. When it passes, the plain looks exactly as it did before. That is the point. That is the whole secret of this place:

The plain is ancient *because* it is violent. The turbulence is not the opposite of the antiquity — it is the custodian of it. Nothing tall survives here, so nothing tall is attempted; the storms level whatever the calms begin to raise. This ocean is dying, in the way oceans die — slowly
