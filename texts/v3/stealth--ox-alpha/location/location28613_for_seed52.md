There is no sun here — only brightness with no address. A ceiling of pearl-grey haze hangs perhaps two hundred meters up, featureless, unbroken, and all light arrives pre-diffused, as if the whole basin lay inside a lamp of frosted glass. Shadows are soft suggestions rather than facts. Color survives only in the middle distances: grey-greens, wet blacks, the pale bone of fresh-cut stone. And yet the air is warm — not sun-warm, which would come down onto your shoulders, but warm from below, the way air feels near a wall that holds afternoon. You understand quickly that in this place the ground, not the sky, is the generous one.

Put your palm flat on any stone. Blood-warm. On the dark basalts, warmer than that. Along the basin's low margins, especially in the first cool hour, the earth exhales a faint steam that rises a meter or two and unravels into the general damp. The warmth is the residue of making. This land is new — not recovering, not ancient and worn smooth, but *recent*, in the way a scar is recent — and it has not yet spent the heat it arrived with. Nights stay mild because the ground gives back what the ceiling refuses to send. People here sleep outdoors without thinking about it, the way you'd sleep against the flank of a sleeping animal.

Look at the shapes of things and you can read their age, because nothing has softened yet. The basin sits low — a shallow dish tens of kilometers across, ringed by hills so young their slopes still stand at the angle they were born at, unslumped, their crests knife-true. Terraces step down to the floor with crisp arrises; silt banks stand vertical where any older land would have sagged into slopes; the gravel in the streambeds is angular, unworn, clicking against itself rather than murmuring. Even the mud cracks sharply, polygons with clean edges, like broken porcelain. On a quiet night the ground ticks — small thermal adjustments, stone settling into its new arrangements, a sound like a house cooling, except the house is a province.

Water comes in episodes. Between them, the land waits without wilting, because nearly everything drinks the air instead.

The rain announces itself by smell, a quarter hour early — that mineral-green exhale of approaching water — and by the spires. The spires deserve description first: hundreds of slender black towers, eighty or a hundred meters tall, scattered not in clusters but in a deliberate constellation across the basin floor, each one a stacked lattice of fine fins. They are the city's oldest infrastructure and its least glamorous. They drink the haze. All day and all night they comb moisture from the air, and at dawn each wears a skirt of mist and begins, audibly if you stand close, to weep — threads of freshwater running down channels into cisterns the size of village ponds. When rain is coming, the spires shiver and shed their gathered drops ahead of schedule, a soft preliminary patter, and then the rain itself arrives: warm, brief, vertical, twenty minutes of full-spectrum hiss, the loudest event in weeks. Afterward the whole basin steams. Every leaf-tip hangs a bead. Dripping becomes the dominant sound of the world for hours — water finding its way down fin-stocks, fern-fronds, terrace lips — a vast slow percussion with no player.

Pools form in the pans and hollows, and the pools are miracles of haste. Within hours they cloud with hatching life — shrimp-things dormant in the dry sediment for months, exploding into existence the moment water touches them, sieving the water into clarity again. Grey wading creatures arrive almost as fast, impossibly long-legged, silent, three-toed, standing in the shallows like folded instruments. They nest nobody knows where. When the pools shrink — always within a day or two, rings of drying mud, then lacework of mineral — the waders walk away over the flats, deep prints the only common signature anything leaves here.

The rivers are stranger than the pools. They braid across the floor the way young rivers do, constantly reconsidering — but their reconsiderations are wrong. Channels keep reopening along lines that were never carved here: a stream will abandon its sensible course and run, stubbornly, up and over a low ridge where no valley is; another cuts through a terrace as if following a map of terrain that isn't this one. The engineers' models fail against it. They have stopped correcting the channels and now build their culverts where the ghosts insist. The local explanation is offered without embarrassment: the land did not form *here*. It was set down here, lifted or carried or made elsewhere and placed, and its waters still remember the shape of wherever it came from. Whether that's true, or a story shaped to fit a mystery, is exactly the kind of question this place declines to answer. Some of the ghost channels run warm, fed from below, and in the cool of the morning they steam gently, like breath.

The green, given so little reliable water, is nonetheless lush — because it drinks fog. Moss lawns deep enough to swallow your footsteps whole, which is why the basin is quieter than it should be. Tree ferns and umbrella ferns with drip-spouted fronds, engineered by evolution or by someone for exactly this climate. Plates of lichen mapping the newest rock — you can date any surface by which lichens have reached it: last decade's flows already grey-green, last year's still black and bare, a slow clock written in colonization. Almost nothing flowers. With so few pollinators, the plants reproduce by runners and fragments, advancing as slow green fire; once every few years, for reasons no one has established, everything blooms at once — a single synchronized week of pale blossom across the whole basin — and then years of pure vegetative ambition. Growth here outruns hunger. Nothing much eats anything else yet; the food web is still a draft. In places the forest runs in unnervingly straight rows, even-aged stands aligned like orchards, the geometry of some original seeding softened by decades of growth but not erased. The old rows and the ghost channels cross each other at angles that make people stop talking mid-sentence.

And threaded through all of it, the city — if a thing this sparse deserves the word, and it does, because the word describes the *system* rather than the crowding.

It spreads across perhaps ten kilometers of the floor: long low halls, shallow domes, cisterns, the black constellations of spires, all joined by roads that are less pavement than *decision* — strips of raw young ground fused into permanence, wide enough for four walkers, empty for most of their length. Between structures lies everything the city chose not to fill: moss flats, orchard rows, untouched terrain, simple deliberate space. Density here was rejected as a design value. The intervals are used — for wind, for water, for the fog's slow traffic, for walking.

The buildings themselves look less constructed than *secreted*. Walls of seamless ceramic, matte, warm to the touch, without visible joints except where the designers wanted lines. Scratches heal overnight; a gouged wall closes like skin. There are no cranes because there is no building, properly speaking — there is *growing*. At the basin's eastern edge a new district is emerging month by month, printed upward by machines that move like patient insects, silent as coral, and the older districts show their strata honestly: the first camp's crude cisterns, the second generation's halls bonded seamlessly over them, the third's spires rooted through both. Five years old, and already archaeological.

The people — and they are people, or close enough — are few relative to all this apparatus. You might walk for an hour and see one figure crossing a plaza carrying nothing, or a window lit amber at night, or two silhouettes in a cistern gallery checking gauges by touch. Water disciplines everything here. The city keeps its own time by collection cycles rather than clocks: dawn-weeping, the shed before rain, the accounting of cisterns. Wealth, if anyone says the word aloud, is measured in hours of reserve.

The fauna you will mostly miss, and that is accurate rather than negligent. Besides the waders and the pool-shrimp: drifting sacs of translucent tissue that hang in the haze among the treetops and spire-fins, barely motile, grazing airborne spores, descending at dusk to drink from leaf-tips; touch one and it deflates around your wrist with a sigh. Crickets under the warm stones, heard as faint ticking on quiet nights, almost never seen — the settlement's children have a tradition of failing to catch them. No fish in the streams; nothing has arrived to live in them yet. No songbirds, no dawn chorus. And once a season or so, after a storm, a single print at the hills' edge far too large for anything accounted for — one track, never two, never followed.

Sound, when you inventory it, is embarrassingly short. Drips. Ticking stone. The sigh of drifters. Thunder that arrives as a feeling in the sternum, directionless, smothered by the ceiling. Footsteps vanishing into moss. Doors closing with felt thuds. The printing machines whisper. Once, standing in the empty interval between two spires, you may realize you can hear your own pulse, and that the place is not silent so much as *listening at the same volume*.

Time passes as a stain of slightly brighter pearl drifting along the ceiling — the sun's ghost, noon by inference. True dark never quite arrives; night is a deeper pearl, the ground's warmth steady under you, certain seams of the city traced in the cold-green shimmer of luminous lichen planted along the irrigation ways, enough light to walk by. At first pale brightening, the spires put on their mist skirts, and the basin begins its day of waiting for weather.

If the place has rules, they seem to be these: heat comes from below, light from above, and the two ignore each other
