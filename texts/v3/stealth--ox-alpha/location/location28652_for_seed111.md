There is no way to speak of this place from below, because below does not resolve into anything. The world under the Bell is a dark that goes down farther than any eye has funds to follow — cloud, then colder cloud, then a hiss of rain that never lands anywhere, only evaporates mid-fall, a sound like the world breathing through its teeth. What rises out of that dark is a single tooth of stone, and the tooth is new.

That is the first thing to understand, and everything else follows from it: the Bell is young. It shouldered up through the crust within living memory — not geological memory, actual memory; there are animals alive on its crown today who remember when this address was empty air. It came up so fast that it overtook the older ranges around it, and it has not yet had time to be softened. No rain has ever touched its uppermost hundred meters. No freeze-thaw has rounded it patiently over ten thousand years. Its faces are the faces rock makes the moment it breaks: conchoidal, glassy, curved like the inside of shells, sharp enough that the wind moving across certain edges produces not a whistle but a note. Frost does all the sculpting here, and frost is a precise worker. Every fracture is crisp. Every arête would cut you before you noticed touching it.

And the crown is small. That surprises everyone who survives the climb. After a vertical mile of nothing, you pull yourself over the final lip expecting a country, and find a courtyard — a platform of dark stone you could pace across in a few hundred steps, walled by spires, floored with fresh talus that clinks underfoot like crockery. You can stand anywhere on it and see all of it, and beyond the rim, in every direction, only the dropping-off. This is a place with boundaries. It is less a mountain than a room at the top of the air.

You hear it before your eyes adjust, and after they adjust, you keep hearing it. The Bell rings. Not dramatically — there was no strike you could point to — but continuously: a low held tone underneath everything, plus slower overtones, plus, if you lie with your ear to a broad slab, fragments. Because the stone here has a property that no one fully understands and everyone uses: vibration leaves it reluctantly. A tap rings for hours. A falling block can be heard decaying until midnight. The result is that the crown is a palimpsest of sound — the day's chimes layered over the morning's, last season's avalanched boulder still finishing its argument somewhere in the deep registers. The inhabitants treat this as ordinary. Some treat it as useful, but that comes later.

It is dark here, genuinely dark — the sun, wherever it is, does not clear whatever shoulder of the world stands east of the Bell, and the light that arrives comes sideways, thin, and low: a dull ember smoldering on one horizon, more suggestion than sunrise. Against this the summit runs its own small economy of glow. Warmth leaks from the rock in two or three places — hairline vents, faintly red, the last exhalations of whatever pushed the tooth up — and around these seams the life gathers, because warmth up here is not comfort. It is territory. It is law.

Picture the ledges ranked accordingly. The slabs nearest a vent hold the densest crowds you will ever see outside a hive: bodies edge to edge, layered three deep in the crevices, a shaggy dark thatch that resolves, when a lamp passes over it, into hundreds of folded things — round-shouldered grazers, plated and patient, who spend their lives licking hoarfrost felt off warm stone. The frost is the water. That is the trick of the whole summit. Rain cannot reach this height; storms pass underneath like ships. Moisture arrives only as rime, laid down silently through the coldest watch when the stone sweats and every surface grows a fur of white fern-crystals, fine as eyelashes. Then the slow tide turns, the grazers wake, and the frost is eaten before it can think about becoming dew. Nothing drips here except in one place — but we'll get to the pools.

Vegetation, properly speaking, barely exists, and what does exists the way money exists in a poor household — counted, guarded, placed exactly. The hoarfrost felt itself straddles the border between plant and weather, and whether it counts depends on whom you ask. On the warm slabs grow true lichens in paint-strokes of black-green, faintly luminous where they're freshest, so that the best-fed ledges carry a soft ghostlight of their own abundance. In the deepest warm pocket, wedged where two glassy faces meet, a single rosette-succulent the size of a dinner plate sits like a green coin — the only flower the summit owns. It blooms perhaps once a decade. When it does, everything on the mountain knows within the hour, and everything that can fly, crawl, or bribe its way there arrives.

Because almost everything here can arrive. This is the paradox the parameters insist upon and the place resolves beautifully: cold, dark, dry, tiny — and drowning in life. The explanation is simple geography. For miles in every direction there is nothing. No stone above the storm-line, no fresh surface, no condensation trap, no warmth. The Bell is the only balcony over the abyss for a day's flight in any direction, so the sky funnels toward it. Overhead, always, wheel the flocks — membrane-wings, thousands strong, riding the updrafts that shear off the spires, rarely landing, their passage a rolling roar that swells and fades on a rhythm slower than weather, more like breathing. They come down to drink at the pools, to chip at salt crusts, and to fail sometimes: the ledge-lurkers see to that, flat things the color of basalt with a single lure-light swaying from the brow, patient as the stone they imitate. Below the lurkers work the beetle-farmers, tapping their territorial codes on the rock. Above everything drift the moth-curtains — columns of pale wings that hang in the windless hours like slow smoke, sifting the air for spores off the talus-fungi, which bloom white and rootless through the scree and hatch their gnats in glowing threads.

And among all of it, walking upright when it suits them, move the climbers.

Six limbs, four of them for gripping, two for holding. Faces that carry their own light — paired cheek-lamps, dim amber, dimmed courteously in company and flashed for emphasis. They are not the strongest thing on the summit nor the most numerous, but they are the only thing on the summit that alters objects without altering places, and that distinction is their entire civilization. The young rock of the Bell fractures glass-clean, and the climbers have learned to help it: they knap flakes from the dark vitrophyre at the north rim, striking carefully, choosing their cores, and the flakes they carry tucked into skin-folds at the wrist are the sharpest edges for a thousand vertical meters. With them they scrape felt off stone too hard for grazing mouths. With them they open the egg-pods of the cliff-roosters, whose shells nothing else on the mountain can breach. With them they cut lure-lights, on occasion, off dead lurkers, and wear them.

What they do not do is build. There is not a cairn on the crown, not a windbreak, not a marked grave, not a pile of flakes left where a pile could be called a store. Everything is carried, used, worn, given away, or dropped back into the talus. Territory is held by sitting on it. Memory is kept by repeating it. Time is kept the same way: there is a notch on the eastern spire through which the horizon-ember shows at its lowest, a keyhole of light, and each year the mountain rises — imperceptibly, a hand's width, the tooth still growing, still being pushed — and the keyhole's light shifts a finger's breadth along the floor of a particular crack. No mark is made. An elder watches the crack each season and remembers where the light stood when she was young, and where her mother said it stood, and the count continues back to the lifting itself. Nothing is written. The stone keeps the sound; the old ones keep the count.

The climbers speak partly in air and partly in rock. A phrase clicked against a broad face will ring there for hours, traveling, fading, and this makes conversation a slow art: messages are spoken into chosen stones the way letters are posted, and answers collected later, and certain cracks in the southern wall function as archives — places where you press your cheek to listen to arguments concluded days ago, courting songs, warnings about a lurker's new position by the big pool. The whole system runs at the speed of the mountain: patient, tidal, impossible to hurry, loud only in the sense that a bell is loud.

Which brings the narrative, at last, to water gathered in one place. Along a seam above the largest warm vein, a thread of melt seeps year-round from a pocket of old ice fused into the rock, and feeds a string of thumb-pools down a shallow channel — five of them, six in wet watches, each one a meeting hall and a flashpoint. Grazers queue. Flocks descend in screaming spirals. Lurkers relocate hopefully. And at the channel's head sits the well: a deeper basin over the warmest point of the vein, water that has never frozen, ringed by the oldest and densest felt-lichen painting on the summit, luminous even at the dark watch. No one built it. Everyone defers to it. And on its rim, immovably attached, lives the oldest resident — a limpet-grey elder, broad as a shield, carried up asleep inside the rock when the tooth rose, older than the summit it sits on. Its shell is banded with every season it has endured. The climbers do not eat it, do not scrape near it, and bring their hardest questions — the ones about before — to the well-stone to ask aloud, slowly, into the ring.

Stand on the crown at the change of the frost-tide and take it in whole. The ember failing on the horizon. The vent-seams breathing their faint red. Ten thousand wings droning overhead in the dark like the mountain dreaming. The talus ticking as it cools. Somewhere in the deep stone, last spring's rockfall still murmuring itself to silence. A climber at the well, cheek to the rim, listening for an answer that may come tomorrow. The whole apparatus — crowded, resonant, ritual-bound — turning on its slow gears atop a spire younger than many of the creatures clinging to it, every edge still sharp, nothing yet worn smooth, everything that will ever happen here still in its first chapter, ringing.
