# The Finished Room

You find it the way you find most hidden things: by falling a little. The hills here are dry grass and grey rock, treeless except for a few twisted hawthorns hunched in the lee of boulders, and in the bottom of a shallow quarry-hollow — old workings, abandoned so long ago that the spoil slopes have softened into something like terrain — there is a slot in the rock face at ankle height. Sheep could pass it. People have to kneel, exhale, and pour themselves through.

The shepherds who know about it don't call it anything grand. They say *down to the singers*, or *the room*, and they go rarely, because afterwards they hum for days without meaning to.

Inside the slot there is a stair — six steps cut through a shell of material that is neither quite stone nor quite ceramic, cool as a cellar wall — and then the chamber opens around you and the first thing you learn is that the two shocks arrive together. The light and the loudness. Both at once, both absolute, neither changing.

The room is small. Ten paces long, six across, domed low enough that you can press your palm flat against the ceiling at the center. Every surface of it is made. There is no bare earth anywhere, no joint where the excavated ground shows through — the shell closes over everything, ivory-colored, faintly nacreous, like the inside of an enormous shell or the palate of some vanished animal. And every inch of that surface is worked. There is no blank space in this room. That is the second thing you understand, after the light and the sound: whoever made this place could not tolerate emptiness. Relief covers the walls floor to dome — vines, waves, rivers, reeds, and then the animals.

The animals are everywhere. Birds in flocks of forty, fish swimming up the walls in carved shoals, deer stepping through carved grass, hares, foxes, herons, a bull, an eel, bees the size of fingernails crowded along a cornice. None of them are alive. Nothing here is alive except a few pale crusts of lichen in the seams — more on that later — but the makers seem to have compensated for the emptiness of the world by filling this room with its portrait. All the creatures are here, rendered in stone-stuff, packed fin to antler, edge to edge, a menagerie at maximum density. And among them, in a frieze that runs the length of one wall, walk people. Processions of them, robed, carrying what might be tools or instruments or offerings. Their faces are gone. Faces project; faces wear first. Centuries of the room's slow settling rubbed them smooth, and now the procession advances featureless, serene, like eggs carried on shoulders.

Because the erosion here is not damage. That takes a while to accept. Nothing in this room is broken or decaying — everything is *finished*. Every edge has been rounded to precisely the radius where the air's passage no longer abrades it. The fine crackle-network in the glaze-like skin of the walls spread once, long ago, and then stopped, its work complete. The dust fell exactly once, sifted into every carved groove, and has not shifted since. The room reached its final shape before anyone now living was born, and it holds that shape the way a held note holds.

Which brings you to the sound.

It is loud. Not uncomfortably, not painfully — but undeniably, physically loud, a chord you feel in the sternum and the molars before you properly hear it. Press your palms over your ears and it barely diminishes, because it is arriving through your bones as much as your ear-drums. It has no source you can point to and no beginning you can remember hearing. It is simply underway, and it was underway before you came down the six steps, and it will be underway when you climb back out.

The shepherds say the walls breathe — that air seeps through the shell from the cold ground around it, the way sap moves, too slow to see, and escapes through a thousand small mouths. And it's true that the carved animals all have open mouths, and that the rims of certain channels and grooves are pierced with flue-holes fine as pinpricks. Whatever the truth of it, the effect is a standing chord: dozens of tones locked in place, layered, each occupying its own region of the room. Walk slowly from wall to wall and the chord changes *geography* — a high silver tone lives near the dome's crown, a dark vowel-note pools at the east end, somewhere a tone like a bird's single sustained call threads through, though you'll argue with yourself about whether you really heard that. The loudness has a map. What it doesn't have is a history. The note at the end of an hour is the note of the first second. There is no crescendo, no drift, no decay. Your sense of time, deprived of anything to measure against, quietly lies down.

The light behaves the same way. It comes from the seams — the joints between the great panels of the shell carry a thin crust of something living, lichen or something older, a green-gold gleam like the underside of a leaf held to the sun — and the ivory walls throw it back and forth until the whole chamber is filled with a bright, even, heatless fullness. There are no shadows. Nothing in the room occludes anything else; the carved fish cast no shade; the dome is as lit as the floor. It is the brightest kind of light and the flattest, and it does not flicker, because the crusts stopped growing a thousand years ago. They reached the exact extent their economy allowed — a few square feet of pale green in a room of sixty, hoarding the room's last bound moisture, giving back light — and there they stopped. Sparse, patient, permanent.

There is no water here. That becomes clear through your lips first: the air is dry as paper, scrubbed of moisture, and within minutes you feel it drawing on you. In the driest corners there is a white fur of salt-crystal bloom, grown once from the last escaping damp and then arrested mid-shimmer, like frost photographed. The carved rivers are dry. The carved rain-clouds rain nothing. Whatever water this world once had, the room has finished with it.

And there are no animals. No insects in the cracks, no swallows nesting in the entry slot, no mice, no spiders. The air circulates too steadily and offers nothing to eat. Everything with a heartbeat that the makers knew is here in relief, mouths open, and none of it sings — or all of it does, depending on how long you stay and what the chord starts to sound like to you, which is the room's one joke, if it has one.

Here is the strangest thing, and it takes some sitting still to notice: you cannot leave a mark. Brush your sleeve along a wall and the mote of dust you dislodged is simply gone within moments, carried off through the flues by that slow circulation. Breathe, and your breath hangs as a brief ghost in the cool air, then unravels and is taken. Warm your hands on a panel and the warmth drains out of it in under a minute, back through the shell into the indifferent cold of the hill. The room is in equilibrium so complete that it rejects addition. You are the only gradient in it — the only moving thing, the only warmth, the only source of shadows, which appear around your body for the first time in centuries and close up again behind you like water.

At hand height on the north wall there is a carved figure apart from the procession — kneeling, arms open — and unlike everything else in the chamber it is polished to a mirror shine. Generations of palms, apparently, all landing in the same place. You will find your own hand rising toward it without your having decided anything. Whether the burnish was counted on by the makers or is just the accumulated agreement of everyone who ever stood here, the figure has no face to tell you.

Why was it built — grown, fired, sung into shape; the shepherds have three theories and no interest in the argument? The likeliest answer is the simplest: someone wanted one thing to be *done*. Not maintained, not defended, not renewed. Finished, the way a bell is finished when it cools. They put the whole living world inside it in portrait, tuned it, sealed it, and walked away, and the room has been keeping their one promise ever since — holding its light, holding its chord, holding its shape, asking nothing of the future.

When you finally pour yourself back out through the slot into the grey daylight, the silence of the hillside will feel like a pressure on your ears, and somewhere behind your sternum the chord will go on standing. It stands in you now. You'll hum it for days.
