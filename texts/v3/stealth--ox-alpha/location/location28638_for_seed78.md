# The Greenwork

There is no ground here that deserves the name.

Rock exists — you know this the way you know the bottom of a lake, by faith and by the stories of divers — but it lies somewhere beneath a floor of cloud that has never once, in anyone's memory, opened. The summits that pierce that cloud are few, dark, and crusted over at the shoulders with descending roots, and between them, spanning every gap, goes the growth: a lattice of living members, branch joined to branch joined to branch, grafted across hundreds of kilometers until the question of where one plant ends and another begins has stopped having an answer. At first you count trunks. Later you understand you have been counting fingers on one hand.

Everything you can stand on was, not long ago, sap.

Stand, then, on one of the great horizontal members — a limb wide as a river, running off into gloom in both directions until it narrows to a vanishing line — and look at what the world is made of. Not stone. Not soil. Bark underfoot, ridged and faintly warm; above you, leaves expanded past their usual ambitions into sails and roofs, membranes the size of fields, translucent enough to show the dim gray wash of sky behind them like light through skin. Hollow internodes big enough to house a village. Bladder-vessels slung in the crotches, taut and pale, swelling and softening on a rhythm you will learn to set your clock by, because out here the clock is not the sun.

The sun, such as it is, does very little. The sky is a low ceiling of the structure's own breath — transpired moisture risen and stalled, a permanent gray overcast — and through the rare rifts you catch only a scatter of faint stars, more felt than seen. Day does not brighten. What passes for day is pressure. Every so many hours the whole continent-sized organism flexes, minutely, as root-pressure drives sap upward from the warm dark below, and that is morning: the membranes tighten, the great drums retune, and at the tips of millions of branch-ends, beads of guttation water squeeze out and hang in strings like pearls. Then comes the slow ebb, and the cold takes back its share, and frost furs every cuticle edge with crystals that tick and crackle as they grow. You hear the freezing. Everyone here hears the freezing.

Because the first thing this place teaches you — after it teaches you about the air below your heels — is that it is loud.

You expect silence. Cold, dark, and empty ought to be silent; the density of things here is so low that a thrown stone might fall past leaf and member and cable for a long time before touching anything. But emptiness carries sound, and this world is an instrument that never stops being played. Wind draws a deep organ note from the mouths of the hollow internodes, a drone that moves along the main limbs like weather of its own. The bladder-vessels drum as the sap-tide shifts, boom and boom, a heartbeat with a continent for a body. Wood grows fast here — young world, hungry world — and growth makes noise: creaks, groans, and every little while a flat cracking report like a distant gunshot where a frozen fiber lets go or a new cell inflates too quickly. The fauna adds its choirs. Whole colonies of whistle-nesting things deliberately lay their eggs in the resonating chambers, breeding generation over generation for louder chambers, so that certain hollows now blast pure fluting tones across the void whenever the wind swings round. Insect-song saws and ticks through the crust-lichen. Somewhere out in the dark, always, something is calling to establish that it exists.

And through it all, threaded under the air-sound, there is the knocking. The people knock.

They are few — this is the bargain of the place, room without crowding, a whole green continent with perhaps a few thousand souls strewn across it like punctuation — and they live scattered, one household pod every few days' walk, lashed into strong crotches, woven from split withies and thatched with overlapping leaf-shields. Their technology is what the world offers, and the world offers no metal and almost no mineral. What it offers instead is amberflint: resin that has pooled, aged, and cured hard as glass in old wound-scar basins, knapped into blades and awls that hold a fine edge. Cordage twisted from inner bark. Spines for needles and dart-points. And voice, carried through wood — a knock-code rapped on a main member travels through the living grain for kilometers, farther and faster than any shout, so that neighbors who will never see each other's faces speak daily in percussion, greeting, warning, news of a birth, news of a fall.

Falls are the one law with no appeal. There are rails of a kind — the people plant their roads, coaxing two limbs toward each other with woven splints until they graft into a bridge, a process of seasons, so that every safe path on the continent was once a gardening decision — but away from the grafted ways the members run single and slick with frost, and below every path there is only air, and below the air, the cloud floor, and below that, whatever the cloud keeps. Nothing dropped is recovered. Nothing fallen returns. Even death here is quick to erase itself: there is no soil to hold a corpse, no litter layer, no decayed accumulation anywhere. Scavengers and pale fungus attend to any dead thing within hours, and the world stays clean the way a young face is clean. Surfaces are either alive or freshly broken, and freshly broken faces gloss over with sap before they can begin to look old. Nothing rounds. Nothing rusts. Erosion has barely been invited.

The damp arrives in forms, not in weather. True rain is an event — when a big bladder vents or a storm-cell manages to gather in the lee of a spire cluster, water comes down in sheets that turn the leaf-roofs into roaring, and every rosette-cup and folded bract becomes a well brimming with cold fresh water, miniature watering holes where the drama of any dry country replays itself in wrist-size: moth and beetle and spider-lurker converging at the rim, hunter waiting among hunters. More often the moisture is intimate. Frost every rest-period, melting in the warm pulse, refreezing. Fog that threads the lattice when the chimney-vents exhale.

The vents are this world's secret hearth. Here and there among the great members, a vertical shaft opens downward into depths you cannot see, and warm mineral air rises steadily through it — smelling faintly of wet iron and struck flint, carrying heat like a gift. It is from these that the lattice drinks its real living, you come to suspect; roots more like gills, the whole organism breathing chemistry out of the earth's warm dark, supplementing the feeble sky-light with something older. Around each vent seam the cold-stiffened life of the neighborhood gathers to thaw: mossbacks with their armor plates cracked white, steaming; pearl-moths in drifting constellations, sipping at strings of fresh guttation beads; and riding the thermal column above it all, the sailhawks, membrane-winged and patient, stooping without warning through the crowd to take a moth or something bigger midair. Hunts happen at the thaw. Prey knows this. The gathering at the vents has a tense, festive quality, like a market held in a borderland.

Once in a span of years, the rhythm breaks and the whole thing flowers at once.

No one predicts it well. Some concurrence of sap-tide and cold and time; and then, on a single long night, across the entire continental greenwork, bloom — billions of blossoms opening together in the dark, and the scent arrives before the news does, a sweetness so dense it is nearly a taste, nearly a touch, rolling over the members like a warm front. The sound changes too: every pollinator in the world airborne at the same hour, and the combined wingbeat comes up out of the void as a sustained surf-roar that does not stop until dawn-equivalent. The people call it nothing grander than the Bloom, and they spend the weeks after it harvesting fruit, rendering wax, and telling the story of where they were standing when they smelled it.

Their ruins are gentler than ruins elsewhere. When a pod is abandoned — a family moved on, a line ended — the lattice does not honor the vacancy for long. Graft creeps over the lashings; the frame is absorbed; within a generation the dwelling has become a burl in the wood, a knuckle of anomalous grain, with here and there a carbonized lens of an old hearth-fire preserved in amber where someone once spilled the lamp. Open flame is the deepest taboo of the culture and the plainest sense: on a continent that is one contiguous flammable body, fire lives only in thumb-sized resin lamps, shielded, carried like a sleeping animal, or burned on dead-timber rafts moored apart from the living wood. Wealth, among the scattered households, is counted in dry fiber, cured amberflint, and well-water rights. Grief is conducted by carrying the dead to a sky-well, or giving them to the cloudfloor, returned to the hush.

Go far enough outward, along the newest members, and you reach the place where the world is still being decided.

They call it the Reach. Out here the growth is pale — no pigments laid down yet, tissue tender and translucent, glossy with unhardened sap — and it moves. Shoot-tips extend visibly, inches in an hour in the warm pulses, feeling ahead of themselves in the total dark, reading the world by touch and by the warmth-gradient of the vents rather than by any light. Where the lattice meets its next gap — the last stretch of void before the crusted shoulder of the next hidden summit — the leading tendrils have gone out over it like fingers testing a table's edge, some failing and hanging, some finding purchase and thickening week by week into members that will one day carry roads. The air here hisses with inflation. Frost forms on the new faces and is pushed off by growth. Everything is taut, unchewed, unnamed.

Behind you, the established continent goes on breathing — drums and drones, the tick of frost, the knock-code traveling member to member through the grain, some household answering some other household across ten kilometers of empty green dark. Above, the gray ceiling. Below, the cloud and its kept secret. And the whole vast structure, cold and damp and almost entirely air, presses slowly outward into the void at its edges, a young thing still inventing its own shape, loud as a drumhead, green all the way down.
