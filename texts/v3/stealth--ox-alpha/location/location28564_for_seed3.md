# Scaffold, on the Yearling

Come up through the fog field and you will see them first as backs — a dozen rounded hills surfacing out of the pearl-grey like whales rising to breathe, and among them, largest and newest, the Yearling. It has no cliffs yet worth the name, no scree, no soil, none of the softening that a mountain earns by being old. Its flanks are sharp as broken porcelain. Rain has fallen on it perhaps four hundred times. Wind has had no time to round even its smallest edges. The whole landmass is raw geology, still warm at the core, still rising — a finger-width a month, more in restless seasons — and on its shoulders sits the city of Scaffold, which climbs with it.

You will notice the light before anything else, or rather the strangeness of what light there is. The sky is a lid of unbroken grey; the sun is a coin behind linen, bright enough to walk by, too dim to cast a hard shadow. But the ground gives its own light. The Yearling's crust is thin, and in a hundred places the heat of the still-forming interior bleeds through in seams of dull ember-orange, glowing cracks that wander slowly across the rockface over years, like veins deciding where to go. And because the light comes from *below*, the shadows here fall upward. Stand near a seam at fogfall and your silhouette climbs the mist above you, head last, a tall ghost standing on nothing. The city has learned to read these upward shadows the way lowland towns read sundials.

The air is cold — not killing, but a working cold, the kind that gets into knuckles — and it is always, always damp. There are no rivers. There is almost no rain. What the Yearling has instead is condensation: this high, the air wrings itself out against every surface colder than it, which at this temperature means every surface. By morning everything wears a skin of water. Cables drip in slow metronomes. The slag-brick walls weep dark tears down their courses. Each district posts its dew-reeves before dawn, who sweep the night's harvest from the condenser-vanes and fog-nets into cisterns, and the whole city runs on that patient skimmed moisture — acre after acre of black mesh strung on frames along the ridgelines, drinking the fog. Water is measured here. Water is piped, owned, inherited. In the oldest quarter there are three "cold ponds," basins of genuinely ancient stone where rainwater pooled once, ages ago, and simply stayed, and children are brought to look at them the way lowlanders visit relics.

When true rain does come — a wet hour, twice or thrice a year — the bells ring it in, and everyone stops. Not celebration exactly. Attention. Rain on the Yearling behaves strangely: it does not splash. It enters the new stone directly, silently, the way a word sinks into wax, leaving the surface dry. There is no runoff because the rock has not yet learned to shed anything. And so the city employs rainwrights, whose whole art is teaching drainage — opening spill-chutes, cutting starter-gullies, guiding each rare wet hour down chosen paths so that, century by century, the mountain begins to acquire its first habits of water. The gullies they are cutting now will be the Yearling's first rivers. Their grandchildren will farm their banks.

Understand why the city is here at all, and you understand everything else. High on the central summit stands a dome of black stone older than anything else for a hundred miles — the Anchor — and old stone is the only thing in this region that is entirely *true*. Everywhere else the world is provisional. Where rock is new, reality has not fully set. Gravity near a fresh extrusion is a rumor: dropped tools hang a heartbeat too long before remembering to fall, and the jack-yards paint slack-rings on the ground where lifting is easier and falling is slower, and loads are roped there accordingly. Sound misbehaves worse. Unweathered stone has not learned to carry an echo honestly — the noon factory whistle's echo sometimes comes home at dusk, fainter, apologetic, and hammers ring in the Seams that match no working crew, which the old-timers say is the sound of tomorrow arriving early. Distances across the saddles disagree with themselves. Clocks drift apart district by district, and no one corrects them, because the only time anyone trusts is the great bronze bell Marrow, hung from a frame rooted in the Anchor itself, whose voice is true because its stone is true. All contracts, marriages, and executions are timed to Marrow.

So: someone found the one old thing on this young mountain, and built outward from certainty into provision. That was long ago. Now Scaffold is five terraced tiers of it, wrapped around three summits and two saddles, connected by switchback ramps and cableways, its districts strung apart with deliberate gaps — wind-lanes left empty so storms have somewhere to go, and the Quiet Quarters left empty for another reason entirely: there the new stone is too soft-minded, and things lean wrong, and no one sleeps well. Between districts the space is real space, open grey air, the fog field visible through the gaps with the rest of the Brood — the other young hills — surfacing and submerging in it. From the upper tier you can count eleven neighboring rises, each with its satellite works, its fog-nets, its thread of cable reaching back toward the city like a hand feeling for its mother.

The technology of Scaffold is the technology of keeping pace. Colossal screw-jacks stand under every platform, taking up the rise a turn at a time; the jack-yards employ a third of the city, and the sound of their long ratchets — *tk. tk. tk.*, all day, all night, patient as chewing — is the city's heartbeat under everything else. Foundries smelt the virgin ore that comes up inside the new stone, ore of impossible purity because no water has ever touched it, and the slag comes out the other side as the grey-green brick that builds the next tier up. Kites cross the saddles hauling mail and light freight, riding the one free gift of the geography: a mountain that is growing displaces the air above it, and so the entire flank of the Yearling exhales gently upward, all day, every day, a permanent updraft. Liftbirds — the huge condor-grey fliers — ride it in spirals without a wingbeat for hours. So do the kite-men, strapped under their frames, ferrying bolts of canvas and sacks of lichen-flour summit to summit while the fog turns slowly below their feet.

And the life here is richer than the bare parameters suggest — richer than it has any right to be. Sparse vegetation, cold, dim: and yet the niches are fat, because warmth and moisture are wealth, and the Yearling pays out both continuously. The greenfields are genuine jungles in miniature — terraces of cultivated moss, deep as mattresses, irrigated by condensate channels, glowing faintly blue-green at night because the brightest strains were bred for lane-lighting and escaped. Lichen lawns cover the old slag heaps in silver continents. Wind-sculpted holdfast shrubs clutch the sheltered saddles, gnarled, patient, leafed in waxy grey. The seam-moths swarm the ember-cracks in drifting constellations, wings powdered with mineral dust; grubs grow fat in the warm stone and are harvester's delicacies; the warmbacks — broad, sluggish, lizard-things — bask in rows along the foundry outflows and in winter are hired out to sleep against pipes and beds alike. Stone-goats lick salts off the newest extrusions, wearing their teeth to stubs in a few years, and are consequently treasured and mourned. Listener finches nest on every bell tower; they sing constantly, horribly, and their silence is the earthquake warning, and when the finches stop, Scaffold stops.

What eats what here is still being negotiated — the ecology is as embryonic as the rock — but abundance is not in question. Warmth plus water plus emptiness fills fast. Even the thin places have their tenants: things seen at the edge of the Quiet Quarters that do not quite commit to being visible, hares that are briefly in two places, a moth whose shadow lands before it does. The city does not study these. It leaves offerings.

Everything here moves at the speed of geology wearing a work apron. Stairs must be re-cut monthly as the risers drift out of true. Doors are hung on adjustable pivots. A surveyor's career is spent watching a single benchmark climb. And over all of it lies the Project — spoken of plainly, worked at without rhetoric: the Yearling is rising, the cloud lid is a fixed height, and someday — centuries, millennia, the jack-yards do not care — the summit will breach it into clean air and actual sunlight, and the city that rode the mountain up will step out of the dimness it was born in. The rainwrights are teaching the rivers now so the valleys will be ready. The greenfield tenders are breeding moss that can handle glare. Children learn arithmetic partly by measuring how much higher their bedroom window sits above the street than it did last year, and every one of them can tell you the number that matters:

How much farther to the light.
