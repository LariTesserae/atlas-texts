# The Terraces Above the Grey

You reach it by climbing out of the dust. That is the first thing to understand: the city has a coastline, and its coast borders no water at all.

Below the lowest causeway, the land dissolves into the Grey — an ocean of powder finer than flour, the pulverized remains of a seabed, stretching past every horizon. It moves in slow tides, swelling up the outer terraces in the warm part of the long day and draining back when the light cools, so that the city's margins are marked by a tide-line of packed dust, crisp as foam. Whole quarters down there have been swallowed for centuries. Streets stand intact under thirty meters of it, doorways filled, salt windows buried and blind. The Grey is patient, and it keeps everything.

The world has almost no water. What remains of it has been taken into custody by the stone. The mountain itself is an exhumed piece of ocean floor — an abyssal plain heaved eleven thousand meters into the cold — and it carried its sea up with it in the only way it could: locked into crystal. The white veins running through the grey ignimbrite are gypsum, and gypsum is stone that remembers being wet; every molecule of it holds water the way amber holds an insect. In the deepest salt cells you can press your ear to a selenite pillar and feel a faint damp coolness, the ocean's last hostages, sealed in mineral, refusing the sky.

## What the city is made of

Grey welded ash, black with desert varnish that has been growing for a million years. White salt, glazed thick on windward walls until it runs like candlewax frozen mid-drip. Panes of clear halite set in window frames — salt-glass, faintly cloudy, warming amber when the low sun finds them. Beams of selenite forty meters long, quarried by no one, spanning halls that have never held a fire.

Because there have never been fires here. There is nothing to burn, and no one to burn it, and no tools anywhere — no cut face, no drill mark, no smelted thing in the entire extent of the Terraces. And yet the city is real, and vast, and organized. It climbs four major summits and the saddles between them; it descends in stepped districts for kilometers; its tallest towers stand at angles that no mason would permit, leaning out over sheer drops, and they do not fall, and they have never fallen.

Walk one and you understand why, eventually, if you're paying attention to your feet. Weight here is negotiable. Mass is a rumor that the rock repeats back imperfectly. Along certain lines — the builders clearly knew them, the way farmers know streams — things simply weigh less. Dust motes drift *upward* in those places, unhurried. A dropped stone takes its time. The towers cluster along these weight-quiet lines like willows along a river, and the great leaning ones lean because they can afford to; they are barely heavy at all. Wherever the thin places are — the spots where the world's rules wear through and a soft pale glow comes off the bare stone — the architecture gathers thickest.

No one knows who laid the first course. Three stories survive, told by nobody, written nowhere. In one, the city grew, secreted over ages by ancestors of the shelled things that graze its walls today, the way coral grows, except upward and deliberate. In another, it fell from the sky already standing, like hail. In the third — the one the stone itself seems to favor — the city is a *memory*. Rock here remembers its earlier shapes; under certain slants of the dim light you can see the mountain's previous selves hanging faintly in it, ghost-strata, old valleys that are no longer there. The third story says the city was never built at all. It says the mountain dreamed a city once, in some earlier age when something lived here that could build one, and the dream never quite ended, and this is what remained standing after the dreamer was forgotten. It is worth noting that the third story requires no builders. Only a rememberer.

## How it is lit

The sun crawls. It never properly rises — it skims the horizon through a permanent haze of high dust, a huge dim red coin, and it slides laterally along the edge of the world in a slow circuit that takes most of a year to close. There is no night, exactly. There is the sunward side of the city and the far side of the city, and the far side is where the cold pools and the frost persists and the true darkness lives, in courts that have not tasted direct light in ten thousand years. Districts are named by light, not street: the Amber Steps, the Long Shadow, the Quarter of Underneath. The air is thin and cold and dry as paper, and it carries sound the way a miser carries coins.

Which is to say: almost never.

## Who lives here

Life exists, but it runs on geology's clock and drinks almost nothing.

On the windward cornices hang the **veils** — grey filtering colonies, rooted, branching, waving in the dust-laden wind like the skeletons of weeping willows. They look like vegetation and are not; they are animals, colonial ones, straining the endless dust for its cargo of ancient kerogen, the organic ghosts of the dead sea, and packing the residue into dense little pellets that drop and roll downhill by the millions. The pellets collect in drifts at wall-base and stair-foot.

Grazing along the walls come the **rasps** — flat, broad, patient things under shells of self-secreted selenite, so that when the low sun passes behind one it lights up like a lantern with a slow animal inside. They scrape the black varnish off the stone and eat the veil-pellets, and their grazing leaves polished clean trails across the dark rock in long wandering lines — roads, of a kind, though no one planned them, and it amuses the stone that scholars of any future age will argue over whether the roads came first.

Something eats the rasps. It is called the **Tenant**, and it is a tower. That is the only accurate way to put it: one tower on any given ridge is warm to the touch in a cold country, and its salt windows are dark in a way that feels attended, and the glints — the tiny bright mote-creatures that swarm and orbit around every thin place, feeding on the upward-drifting dust and whatever loose charge bleeds through — the glints will not orbit the Tenant's tower. Once in a generation, silently, on no schedule anyone has caught, the Tenant is simply on a different summit. The city's silence is not empty. Some of it is held breath.

Underground, fungal threads thread the rock itself, eating sulfides, excreting salt, closing the loop. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is watered. The whole ecology is a slow fire burning the corpse of an ocean, and it has been burning, quietly, since before the mountain finished rising.

## The economy of falling

Here is the rule that governs everything, and once you see it you cannot stop seeing it: **nothing leaves the city.**

Stone falls — yes, constantly, somewhere, always. But the tonnage of the standing equals, at every moment, the tonnage of the fallen, and it always has. Each passage of the sun, some wall lies down and some wall stands up. Collapse and construction are one economy, perfectly balanced, and have been for so long that the city's form is not decaying but *circulating*, the way a flame keeps its shape while eating itself. The erosion finished its argument with the building ages ago. They reached terms. What the wind takes from the western saddles it pays back onto the eastern ones, grain by grain, and the Terraces keep their count.

Other rules run looser. Distance behaves badly in the inner districts: two gates that stand a hundred steps apart on the map may take a morning to walk between, or three steps, depending on something no one has pinned down — the hour, the direction, possibly the walker. Time pools in certain courts; an afternoon will sit in a shaded quadrangle for a whole season, reluctant to drain. Echoes in the approaches to the city's heart arrive before the sounds that cause them, politely, as if the acoustics have heard it all before. Visitors who stay long enough report meeting their own footprints coming from the opposite direction. The city does not explain itself. The city redistributes itself.

## The Wet Hour

Once each circuit of the sun, the cold comes down off the far darkness hard enough to cross a line, and the world performs its single miracle.

Hoarfrost blooms across every north-facing stone — real frost, drawn out of the dry air by forces the rock pretends not to understand. And in the same hour the gypsum *sweats*: the imprisoned ocean weeps bead by bead out of the crystal lattice, silvering the pillars, pooling in shell-cups and salt hollows. For roughly sixty minutes, the waterless world is watered.

Everything converges. The rasps lift their heads for the only drink of their long lives, translucent shells glowing, dozens of them lined along the drip-lines of the great selenite beams. The veils curl inward and glisten. The glints swarm the wet thin places and burn brighter than they ever do otherwise, hundreds of them, a drifting constellation at knee height. Even the air changes character — a faint cold briny smell, the smell of the sea, arriving once a year through solid stone. Then the sun slides on, the warmth returns, the frost sublimates, the beads sink back into their mineral prison, and the Terraces return to their arithmetic of dust and silence.

## An inventory of a day's sounds

Five. The tick of grit against stone when the tide-wind gusts. The click of a rasp's shell, once an hour, like a distant door. The sub-hearing hum of the veils, felt in the sternum rather than the ear. The crackle of the frost at Wet Hour, which is less a sound than a texture. And your own blood, which in the silence becomes embarrassingly audible, a reminder that you are the loudest thing for fifty kilometers and the only thing here with a heartbeat to spare.

When the reality shifts — when a wall lies down or rises, when a distance renegotiates itself — there is no sound at all, only a change of pressure, a popping in the ears, the sensation of the city clearing its throat in a register below sound.
