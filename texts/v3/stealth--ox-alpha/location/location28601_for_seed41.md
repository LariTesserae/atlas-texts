**The Warm Room**

You come down to it by a spiral of footholds cut into the shaft wall — each one worn glossy, each one older than any memory attached to them. Thirty-some steps, a quarter turn each, and then the last hold is a smooth pocket in rock that has clearly been used by many hands and improved by none, and you drop the last arm's length onto stone that is warm through your boots before you've even stood up straight.

The chamber is small. Sixty paces across if you walk the rim, and the ceiling rises maybe ten meters at the dome and comes down low enough to brush at the edges. You could shout across it — though you won't, for reasons that will become obvious. It is a room, not a country. Everything in it is within sight of everything else, which gives the whole place the quality of an held breath, or a terrarium, or the inside of something living.

It is hot. Not dry heat — the air here is thick with moisture, a hammam warmth around forty degrees that wraps you the moment you step off the last foothold. The stone sweats. Every surface is beaded, every wall traced with threads of runoff too fine to be called streams, running down to gather in hollows. There is no rain here and no river; the water arrives entirely as vapor, rising off some warm mineral seam deep below, condensing on the cooler ceiling, and falling back. The chamber drinks its own breath, over and over. Your skin understands this before your mind does: within minutes you are damp everywhere, glistening, part of the local economy.

The light is dim and mostly alive.

There is one opening — a flue, a crack near the top of the dome wide enough to admit a forearm, angling up through thirty meters of rock toward the surface. Through it comes a pencil of grey daylight that lays a coin of pale gold on the floor, moving slowly with the seasons, brightening toward noon. Around that thread of light hangs the only true vegetation that isn't moss: a curtain of roots. Somewhere far overhead, trees are sending their hair down through the crack, dozens of white rootlets dangling into the warm air, each one beaded with condensation, drinking the chamber's humidity the way the chamber drinks its own. They sway very slightly in the thermal updraft. When the indoor rain comes, they are the first to shed.

Everything else glows. In crevices and along the ceiling margins, larvae — grub-pale, each sealed in its own woven tube of silk and debris — burn with a steady blue-green lantern-light, fishing for the midges that mistake them for escape routes upward. On the damper walls grows a film of luminous mold, dim as old clock-dials. Together these give the room a light like late dusk indoors: you can make out every shape, count every animal, but reading is impossible and shadows are negotiable. And there is a strangeness to it worth standing still for: the mold is sensitive to disturbance. Walk close along a wall and a slow wave of faint brightness follows you, flaring where your body heat and movement pass, fading behind. The room watches you go by in the only way it can.

The floor is a garden. Over the warm stone lies a spongy felt of moss and fungus, knee-deep in places, lush well past what the thin light should allow — fed by the mineral seep-lines, by the roots' leavings, by whatever sifts down the flue: seeds, leaf-fragments, the occasional cooked corpse of a surface insect. And the felt is being farmed. Ploughers move through it — pale isopod-things the size of thumbs, thousands upon thousands, tillers working the mat the way earthworms work soil. Stare at the floor long enough and it seems to ripple, to breathe, the whole surface creeping in slow convulsions of small backs. Their trails heal over behind them within minutes. Nothing here stays marked for long, except the things that were made on purpose.

Of which there are a few. Beyond the foothold spiral: a natural ledge at waist height on the east wall, its face rubbed to a concave polish — a seat, or a mortar, or both. Three stones stacked against a wall-corner, flat ones, fitted, unmoved for longer than the moss can tell you. And hanging from a nub of rock near the root-curtain, a cord of braided rootlet strung with pierced shale discs, each one drilled at its center, the whole thing turning almost imperceptibly in the convection current, ticking through its slow orbit without a sound. Whoever the makers were, they brought nothing that survives and left nothing that explains. The snails now graze the ledge. The discs belong to the draft.

The animals, though — the animals are the reason you will keep quiet.

There are plenty of them, densely packed, layered floor to ceiling. Snails with glass-thin shells stud the walls like dew. In the four shallow pools — basins of still, mineral-milky, blood-warm water — hang finger-length fish with skin instead of eyes, motionless for hours, rising in perfect formation to anything that disturbs the surface film. Skimmer-shrimp row those films in lazy circuits, leaving spreading wakes. At the flue, at certain hours, rise the moths: grey, silent, fluttering up the light-column to intercept the seeds and fragments that sift down, snapping them out of the air. In the crevices wait the snare-legs — flat centipede-things coiled beneath bark-fungus shelves, patient as traps, lunging with a speed that is the fastest motion in the entire chamber and still completely noiseless.

And somewhere in the room, most days, stands the Listener.

You may not see it at first. It resembles a fold of pale wax upright on its base — taller than a person, or sometimes hanging bell-down from the ceiling like a shed cocoon — and it does not move. It can stand through an entire day without motion, ear-plates spread wide along its flanks, thin as drumskins, turned outward to the room. It hunts sound. Not movement, not scent — sound alone, which is why everything else in this chamber has renounced it. Nothing here sings, calls, chirps, buzzes, or cries out. The ploughers till in total hush. The moths fly on muffled wingbeats. Even courtship here has gone visual: the glow-film's flare-language, the lamp-larvae's pulsing, the little rippling waves of light that cross the walls at night like slow applause — those are the voices of this place. Everything that wants to be heard in the Warm Room says it in light, and holds its breath otherwise.

Because of all this, the soundscape is almost nothing, and precisely shaped. Most minutes contain a single event: one condensation drop, gathered all morning on the ceiling, releasing at last and falling the ten meters to strike moss or open stone — a tap, clear as a struck cup, followed by the long soft tail of the room absorbing it. During the afternoon this becomes weather. The thermal cycle builds toward its peak, the ceiling load grows too heavy, and for five or six minutes it rains indoors — a fine patter over the whole floor, every animal tilting its head up to drink, the pools stippled with rings — and then it stops, and the counting of individual drops resumes.

Nothing dies loudly here, either, and nothing lasts long as a corpse. The heat sees to that. Anything that falls — moth, fish, plougher, the rare bird blown down the flue and cooked on its way — blooms overnight in white pin-molds and is gone inside two days, hauled apart by the tide of ploughers, folded back into the felt. The room wastes nothing. It is a closed loop with exactly two openings: a thread of fire below feeding it warmth, and a thread of sky above feeding it light and gifts. Everything else — carbon, water, shell, bone — has circulated through these sixty paces so many times that the chamber feels less like a place than like a cell, patiently metabolizing.

Stay still long enough and it accepts you. The glow stops flaring at your passage; the ploughers resume crossing your shadow; a snail may begin the twelve-hour commute over your boot. Stand in one place through the indoor rain, through the noon shiver of hot air and the evening rise of moths, and you become furniture — until you swallow, or shift your weight, or let one pebble click underfoot. Then you will feel it. Not hear it. Feel it: the entire room's attention arriving at once, every ear-plate in the dark rotating toward the point where the silence broke, the light-language going flat and careful, the nearest snare-leg shelf seeming, somehow, deeper than before.

The Warm Room forgives, eventually. But it never forgets a sound, and neither, while you're in it, will you.
