# The Highland Engine

You come up to it the way you come up to everything here: slowly, diagonally, wet. For days the trail—if the trampled seam of moss can be called a trail—switchbacks through fog, and the fog does not thin with altitude the way fog should. Instead it thickens, until somewhere past the third day you realize the clouds below you and the clouds above you have joined, and you are walking inside the joined thing. The air turns cold enough that your breath shows at dawn. Your fingers ache in the early hours. And yet the moment you sit down to rest, the warmth comes up through the soles of your boots—steady, patient, animal—because the stone of this place is warm the way a sleeping body is warm, and it has been warm for longer than there have been backs to lie against it.

That is the first thing to understand about the highland, before anything else: the cold is on top. The heat is underneath. Everything else follows from that arrangement.

Underfoot, the ground gives like a sponge pressed to capacity. Moss lies knee-deep wherever the stone allows, black-green and gleaming, so saturated that your footfalls make no crunch and barely a hiss—the moss drinks the sound along with everything else. Where the moss fails, the rock itself takes over the work of weeping. Whole cliffsides seep; water stands in beads on vertical faces without falling, gathering weight, letting go all at once down some hairline fissure. The stone is banded—white sinter like poured porcelain, rust-orange stain where old water surfaces and meets air, gray-black basalt beneath it all, polished by wet into something that looks less like rock than like cooled metal. Terraces step up the slopes in shallow bowls, rim after rim of mineral dam, each holding a lens of green water. Nobody built those stairs. The water built them itself, one dissolved grain at a time, the way it has built everything here.

Because here is the second thing: the water climbs.

On the broad benches above the terraced slopes stand the groves. They look at a distance like columns of jointed bamboo scaled past reason—ribbed, pale-rinded, two hundred feet tall, three hundred, standing in loose ranks by the thousand, each trunk sweating so heavily that a permanent rain falls in a ring around its base. Put your ear against one and you hear it working: a hydraulic rush, a shudder in the ribs, fluid dragged upward through capillary bundles with an effort you can feel in your teeth. At the crown, each column breaks open in a fountain. The streams pour from the tops, gather, find channels—and *descend*, becoming the headwaters of rivers that will not see the sea for five hundred miles. Every river on this side of the world begins at the top of its course, hoisted there by trees. Walk beside one of the climbing channels and listen: the water going up makes a sound, a faint reversed gurgle, and the gurgles travel upstream past you, toward the sky.

The groves are the pumps. The warm strata far below are the boiler. The whole plateau—a mass of stone wider than most kingdoms, heaved up over some ancient swelling of the deep—is the machine casing, and it has been running, in balance, wearing its own shape into itself, for an inconceivable stretch of time. Nothing here looks young. Every slope rests exactly at its angle of repose; every channel has cut to its exact grade; the rare slump or washout is re-knitted by moss within a season, absorbed back into the finish of the place like a scar fading in old skin. The land is not eroding and not erupting. It is simply *running*.

Then the fog opens—it does this once or twice a day, on a tide you learn to anticipate by the way the drip-chorus changes key—and you see where you are.

The highland rolls to every horizon. There is no far side of the view. Chimney-groves stand away in ranks to the limit of sight, ten thousand plumes of steam standing upright in the slanted noon light, and between them the terraces shine in braided ribbons, and the fog-sea moves over it all with the slow authority of weather being *manufactured*. Because it is. This roof of the world drinks the sky. Mornings, the plateau inhales—a drawn-out land breeze, mist pulled uphill from the lowlands in rivers you can watch, feeding the pumps. Afternoons, it exhales: the spent vapor pours off the eastern shoulders in cloud-levees that march inland to rain on continents. The storms here walk fixed circuits, arriving on schedule to the day. Farmers a thousand leagues downriver set their calendars by weather this place decided.

And it is quiet. That is the third thing, and the hardest to believe from the descriptions: an industrial scale of process, and almost no sound. The moss swallows the small noises. The fog diffuses what's left. The machinery itself is too big to be loud—its strokes take minutes, its cycles take seasons, its exhaust is a sigh. What you hear, in the hush: dripping, everywhere, arrhythmic, like applause spread infinitely thin. The scheduled vent-releases, a soft chuff from some valley over and gone. Your own blood. And beneath everything, felt in the soles more than heard, the Pulse—a slow swell, once every few minutes, the deep recharge wave rolling through the saturated rock as the aquifer refills. Animals here move to it. You will find yourself doing the same before you've been a week on the stone.

The fauna comes dense, because the density here is total: there is no unoccupied surface, no idle niche, nothing waiting. Life is stacked in layers from the warm plumbing up. Down in the terrace shallows graze the dredgers—low, wide-mouthed, patient beasts that spend their days keeping the flow-channels scoured, mouthful by mouthful, the way fish clean a reef. Without them the mineral silt would choke the risers within a generation; they are maintenance, and they are paid in what they eat. On the vent-fields, where old water arrives hot and mineral-rich, pink-skinned skulkers browse the bacterial mats with blind contentment, and smaller quick things pick mineral scale off the spring mouths, unclogging them service by service. Overhead, in the columns of buoyant damp air that stand above each chimney grove, the soarers kettle by the hundred, wings locked, riding exhaust that never stops rising. On the terrace rims bloom the only flowers—pale, wet-proof things—feeding pollinators that in turn feed the small hawks; in the acid hummocks the sundews take their nitrogen where they find it. Even death is infrastructure. The dead sink into the peat, the peat subsides into the warm strata, and what comes back up through the vents is gas and heat and a faint sweetish smell of digestion. The highland composts its own.

Predation here is a strange, slow ballet, because sound—the usual currency of hunting—is nearly worthless in the hush. The hunters have solved it with pressure. They carry, in padded forelimbs and hollow chests, organs that read the Pulse, and they cross the open channels only in the troughs between swells. The prey solve it differently: the dredgers simply stop, mid-stride, and fall into step with the engine, timing their movements to the great refill-wave so their footfalls vanish inside the ground's own motion. Watch it once—a hunter ghosting along a channel-edge, everything frozen, the whole marsh holding one held breath in time with the planet—and you understand that the food chain here is bolted to the machinery. Predator and prey alike are load-bearing.

Which raises the question that every visitor eventually asks, usually at dusk, looking at some arrangement that seems too convenient: did anyone *make* this?

Nothing was ever built here. There is no wall, no tool, no shard, no nail—not a worked flake in a thousand miles of sediment. No creature here builds; even the dredgers, the closest thing to engineers, alter nothing they don't eat. And yet. The spring mouths on the western benches are spaced with a regularity that itches at the eye, row upon row like rivets. Certain channels run ruler-straight for miles before surrendering to the meanders that water prefers. In one dry basin stand twelve chimneys, perfectly equidistant, around perfectly nothing. Selection could explain it—water loves efficiency, and efficiency can look like intention from enough distance—or the explanation could be that there was an intention once, a designer or designers whose every artifact, being made of the same wet stone as the mountain, has long since been drunk back into the terraces and re-precipitated as scenery. There is no way to know. The highland keeps no records it hasn't already eaten. The absence of the built is total, and somehow that absence has a shape, and the shape looks like a blueprint with everything drawn in water.

So you learn its laws, because they are few and absolute. Water climbs before it falls. Heat lives under the cold, not the reverse. Nothing here is young, nothing is wasted, and nothing is finished. Move with the Pulse or be noticed. And when the fog closes again at evening—as it always does, the inhalation reversing, the mist drawing back up the slopes like a tide returning over a flat—you stand in the middle of the largest quiet thing there is: an engine the size of a world's roof, running without a mechanic, without a blueprint, without an owner, breathing in, breathing out, warm under your hand where you rest it on the weeping stone, and beating, faintly, patiently, underfoot, like something with nowhere in particular to be going and all the time there is to get there.
