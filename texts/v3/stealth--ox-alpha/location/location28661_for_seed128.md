You come to it up a flue-shaft that was never meant for visitors and has been used by them anyway — rungs worn bright, each one skinned with condensation, the air growing warmer and thicker with every climb until breathing feels like drinking. And before you see anything at all, you hear it: the roar. Not wind, not waterfall, though it contains both. A sound with texture, with grain, as if the air itself were being wrung out somewhere just ahead of you. Which is exactly what is happening.

The vault opens at the top of the shaft like a held breath released. Forty paces across, perhaps fifty — a single chamber, domed, tiered, contained. There is no horizon here, no outside, no ground. There is only the room, and the room is working.

Nothing in this place was ever stone. Look closely at the walls and the lie gives itself away: beneath the terraced crust, beneath the mineral curtains and flowstone draperies, there are cast ribs, laminate panel-seams, the ghost-regularities of engineered geometry. The vault has grown its own geology over its own bones. Centuries of ceaseless drip have laid down travertine on the composite the way coral clothes a wreck, so that now the made and the mineral-accreted are indistinguishable — which was perhaps the intent, or perhaps simply what a very old machine becomes when left in the rain it makes for itself. The gutter-lips are cut with precision. The overflow channels below them have been worn round and smooth by nothing but their own constancy.

And the ceiling works. That is the first thing to understand. Under the dome hangs a thicket of fins and coils and resonant throats, and the air that reaches them arrives already fat — drawn up through the structure from warm wet levels far below, carrying everything the lower world exhaled. Then the sound takes it. The roar is the harvest: standing waves braided through the chamber at frequencies you feel in your sternum and your back teeth, shaking microdroplets together until they fall. The noise is not a byproduct. The noise *is* the machine, as much as the blades of a mill are the mill. Deafening is not the cost of the place. It is its craft.

So it rains indoors, always, a fine relentless rain that never pools because every surface slopes toward a lip, every lip toward a channel, every channel toward the black throats of cisterns sunk in the floor — and through those, presumably, down and away to wherever such a structure sends its water. You can trace the whole circulatory system by ear: the drip-hiss of the upper air, the trickle-gurgle of the gutters, and underneath everything, slow as a sleeping heartbeat, the pumps. Once or twice an hour something vast several hundred feet below adjusts its load, and a pulse travels up through the floor plates and into the walls, and you watch it pass as a shiver across every wet surface at once, a ripple in ten thousand films of water, gone before you can point at it.

There is almost nothing green. Almost. Along the two seams where the light-emitters burn steadiest — dim blue-white flicker, duty-cycled, more suggestion than day — thin algal films gloss the stone like varnish. Moss crowds a few gutter-joints. Two sunken tubs hold what were once gardens and are now dense mats of something lichen-like and patient. But nothing grows tall, nothing grows wild, and the reason becomes obvious the longer you stand still: everything edible is grazed to the limit of its endurance. The vault is crowded in a way the vegetation never agreed to.

Because the animals are everywhere. Every niche, every margin, stacked fin upon ledge upon pipe-mouth. Palm-sized drip-crawlers patrol the undersides of the condenser fins on hydrophobic feet, drinking from the beaded tips and rasping off mineral scale — they are the descalers, and the smooth function of the harvest is partly theirs. Veil-moths hawk the mist in loose spirals, cloth-grey wings dusted with a faint cold luminescence, their mouths straining the air for whatever the droplets carry; at any moment the middle height of the vault holds thousands of them, a slow blizzard you walk through. In the cistern cells, pale swimmers turn blind circles, fed on a nutrient dosing that still runs on a schedule set by people who did not leave their names. Seam-worms lace the walls in calcite veins, and these are the strangest thing here, quietly: they seal cracks *before* they open, welling sealant into seams that are merely stressed, responding to harmonics in the structure's voice that precede failure. Either they hear tomorrow, or the engineers bred foresight into them. No one left behind a manual to say which.

The chorus belongs to the frogs. They pack the pipe-mouths and gutter-flares where their calls catch the chamber's own resonance and come out the far side enormous, bell-tones and hammerings layered over the roar. And here is the vault's one courtesy: scattered through the din are nodes — places where the standing waves cancel and the air goes strangely, tenderly still, pockets of quiet exactly the size of a head. The frogs contest the best perches beside them, singing from the edge of silence into the flood of sound. If you find a node yourself and put your face into it, you can hear, for a few seconds, your own breath. It is startling how much that feels like a gift.

Watch long enough and the cycles declare themselves. Every few hours the emitters surge, the waves tighten, and the fine rain thickens into a deliberate downpour — the gutters run brown-green with stirred silt, the swimmers feed frantically on what washes in, the moths descend in veering sheets to drink at the splash-line. The chorus changes key with the humidity, rising as the air fattens. The one large animal of the vault — a slow, grey-necked browser, tall as a person when it stretches, older than any schedule you could name — migrates along the algal seams following the bloom-fronts, cropping each film in turn, keeping the green exactly this sparse, exactly this persistent. Nothing here is wild and nothing is kept. Both at once. The balance was struck so long ago that the wear looks like design and the design looks like weather.

Near the dome's crown there is a single aperture, a slot window glazed and waist-height, and it exists only to prove something you would otherwise have no way of knowing: that this room sits absurdly high. Beyond the glass there is no ground. There are cloud-tops, drifting below you, lit faintly from underneath by the world they hide, and the spire's own flank streaked dark with runoff sliding down toward that buried brightness. Sometimes thunder arrives through the glass, very far away and very small compared to the vault's voice.

When the great note comes — the exhale, when the whole structure draws breath and the chamber becomes one instrument instead of many — every frog stops mid-tone, the moths hold their stations, the rain hangs a half-beat heavier in the air, and the roar gathers itself into a single sustained tone that you feel in your jaw, your ribs, the soles of your feet. Then it breaks. The rain doubles. The chorus resumes as if it had never paused. And the vault swallows the sound of your standing there entirely — the loudest room you will ever enter, holding you the way water holds a stone: completely, indifferently, warm.
