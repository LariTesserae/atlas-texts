**The Vestibule**

You reach it on the fifth day, when the mountain has been nothing but shoulder and grind for hours — and then, without warning, it opens. A notch in the summit ridge, walled on three sides by fins of gneiss, floored with pale shattered stone. Forty paces long, half as wide. There is no better word for it than *room*. Its ceiling is the sky, and its doorway is the rest of the world, hanging open on four-fifths of the planet's air.

This is not a summit. It is smaller than that, and stranger: a chamber built by accident at the roof of the weather, and it has been finished for a very long time. The frost-shattering that breaks mountains apart did its work here ages ago and then stopped. Every edge has been kissed round by grit-laden wind. The walls are worn the way old stairs are worn — not eroded, exactly, but *settled*, the way a thing settles when it has said everything it intends to say. Quartz veins run through the stone like wiring, and in the light they conduct.

And the light — the light is the first thing that hurts. The air up here is thin and wrung dry, and the sun arrives undiluted, and then it arrives *again*, bounced up off the pale floor, so that everything is lit twice. Shadows are inked thin and sharp as cuts. Colors go oversaturated, hallucinated. Even the shaded corners glow, filled with reflected light the way rooms are filled with lamplight.

Nothing grows here. No lichen crusts the stones, no moss sleeps in the joints; whatever slow green ambitions life has, it abandoned them a thousand meters below. And yet the floor, on certain mornings, is a meadow. Thousands of hoarfrost needles stand packed together across the stone, each one a hair's width, a spine of glass — grass made of overnight, a crop that plants itself, ripens by dawn, and is harvested by the sun. It is the closest thing this world offers to vegetation, and it is honest about what it is: water, briefly persuaded into shape.

That is the whole economy of the place. Each evening the stone exhales its stored cold, and any moisture riding the passing air sheds itself onto whatever surface is colder than it is. Everything drinks. By midnight the walls are furred, the boulders wear feathers of ice, the loose plates of the floor grow scales. Then the sun reverses the transaction. Most days the frost never melts at all — it sublimates, going straight from crystal back to vapor, so the morning ends not with wet stone but with stone that simply stops being dressed. The chamber inhales and exhales once per day. It breathes frost.

Except in the basin. Near the center, glacially polished into an oval the size of two spread hands, a shallow dish collects the melt and the condensation and holds it: a lens of black water, the only standing water in any direction for miles, existing on a tide of one day. It fills by midnight, holds through the cold morning, and is gone by early afternoon — drunk back into the sky. For a few hours it mirrors the heavens so perfectly that it looks less like water than like a hole cut in the floor of the world.

People have been here. Not many, and not lately — but the place is marked the way a hermit's cell is marked. Against the north wall stands a windbreak of dry-fitted slabs, one course toppled, built to face the direction the storm-scours come from. Seven cairns rise from a broad slab of stone — placed there deliberately, you eventually realize, because that slab holds the sun's warmth longer than anything else and is the only spot a body could survive the night. Along one wall-joint runs a line of pecked dots, softened almost back into the rock by centuries of frost-spall. And in a crevice, offerings: cordage still holding its knots, dried stiff as wire; a scrap of leather cured hard as shell; a single pin of hammered copper gone green with age — the only metal ever carried this high, or the only one that remains. Whatever was left here has not rotted, because *nothing* rots here. Anything organic that arrives is dried to permanence within a season. The mountain keeps what it is given. Whoever climbed up with those knots in their hands understood the rules of the house: build against the scour, sleep on the warm slab, leave before the light goes, and take nothing that you expect to get back.

The silence has a texture, and the texture is vacancy. No birds cross at this altitude; no insects ride the wind up; there is nothing to make the small noises that make a place feel inhabited. The thin air carries sound badly — your own footsteps seem to happen somewhere away from you, a dropped pebble clicking like a door closing in another building. But the quiet is not absolute, and it keeps two appointments.

The first is dusk. When the sun leaves, the stones cool unevenly, contracting at different rates, and some of them give off a single soft tone — not a crack so much as a *note*, a tick that resonates in the rock. Each stone has its own pitch. Dusk, therefore, is a carillon: a sparse, random scattering of bell-tones lasting perhaps a minute, different every evening, as the chamber tunes itself to night. One capstone on the smallest cairn rings a clean, deliberate-sounding minor third. It may mean nothing. It may have been chosen.

The second appointment belongs to the frost itself. Whatever surface the hoarfrost colonizes — wall, wire, windbreak, the copper pin — the feathers always terminate in the same small set of endings: a spiral, a ladder, an eye. Every night the same alphabet composes new sentences across the stone. No one has read them. Possibly no one wrote them. It is the sort of strangeness that could be explained, if anyone were patient enough, by crystallography — and yet standing there at dawn, watching the letters burn off, you would be forgiven for feeling addressed.

Between visits, the place runs on its own slow machinery. Every week or three, a plume of cloud rides the ridge and scours the chamber clean — anything not weighed down is taken, which is what the windbreak is for. Between storms, weeks of glassy stillness. Meanwhile the frost lives and dies daily, and its estate accumulates: mineral dust settling into the joints, millimeter by millimeter, a soil that isn't one, laid down at the pace of centuries. Frost is the tenant here — growing, competing for surface, dying at sunrise, leaving its estate behind. It plays every role the missing biosphere would play.

At night, the rules tighten. Starlight up here is strong enough to cast double shadows off the quartz; the Milky Way lies on the chamber like a spilled substance. And the basin, in the hour before true dark, does its one extravagant thing: the stars appear in the water *first* — reflected there before they are visible overhead, as if the sky were leaking upward out of the pool. You can tell yourself it is a trick of altitude and expectation. You will tell yourself that once.

Morning comes ferocious and clear. The needles burn away, the basin drains, the stones tick once and go silent. The room resumes being what it is: a small lit chamber at the top of everything, densely furnished with ice and stone and the small patient works of forgotten hands, its door open to the whole depth of the sky — keeping its one clock, which is water, and its one inhabitant, which is frost, and waiting the way a room waits, without impatience, for the next person tall enough to knock.
