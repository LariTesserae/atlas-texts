The air reaches you before anything else. It comes up the service stair like a held breath finally released — warm, wet, sweet with something vegetal and mineral at once, geosmin and hot stone and the faint ozone bite of old electrics. Forty meters down, the tunnel ends at a threshold, and the heat crosses it to meet you like a dog that has been waiting a long time.

The room beyond is smaller than the journey suggested. Thirty paces long, ten wide, the ceiling a low five meters — a nave built to a freight car's proportions, with boundaries you can feel: end walls, side walls, the throat of the tunnel behind you. Everything about the rock says *recent*. This chamber was cut, not worn. The blast fractures are still angry, edges crisp, faces planed smooth where the drill passed and ragged everywhere it didn't. No water has had time to round anything. At the ceiling seams, stalactites are only beginning — soda-straw nubs, a few millimeters of ambition. The room is younger than the machines in it, which is the first wrong thing you notice, though it takes a while to understand why it's wrong.

It takes a while because the dark is total at first. Not night-black — night has weather, stars, the memory of sun. This dark is architectural, a material the room is built from. Then your eyes adjust and the ceiling acquires a constellation: dozens of small lights holding their posts on cabinets and junction boxes, amber and green and one stubborn red. Pinpoints, steady, unhurried. They are the only sky this place has, and no one has lain on the floor naming them.

Underfoot, the terrain is bedrock scored with drill spirals, interrupted by grout pads and anchor bolts where equipment was once set. Cable trays run the walls, half-filled now with pale rope-roots that followed the conduits in. The floor drains are clogged with the same roots, so water has negotiated new routes — thin dark tracks across the rock, appearing and vanishing. Every surface wears a film of condensation. Beads gather and swell on the bolt heads, on the flaking enamel of the racks, on the cold water lines especially, where the chill coming in from the surface wrings a false dew out of the hot air. Touch anything and your fingers come away glossed.

The machines are what hold this world up. Against the far wall, a capped bore-head breathes — a geothermal tap, radiating heat you can feel from three steps away, too hot to palm. Its energy goes into a cabinet the size of a refrigerator, and the cabinet, by means you don't inspect too closely, drives everything else: a sump pump that gulps at long intervals, solenoid valves, and two banks of grow-bars suspended over the planting racks like sleeping fluorescent gods. Somewhere inside the wall a turbine spins — you don't hear it so much as feel it, a tremor in the floor that lives in your molars. On the oldest cabinet, a brass plate stamps a manufacture year that predates, by decades, the company whose stencil advertises the drilling of this bay. Either the plate lies, the sign lies, or the room is older than its own blueprints. The plants do not appear to care which.

And the plants are the point of everything. They grow in islands — lush past reason, each colony dense as a thicket, with bare swept rock between, aisles of emptiness wide enough for a cart that will never come again. The largest colonies occupy the old growing racks, though several racks have rusted through and been swallowed, metal ribs protruding from vegetable flesh like the bones of eaten furniture. Nothing here is green. Chlorophyll is a sun-culture technology, and no sun has ever reached this room. Instead: paddles of oxblood, ropes of ivory, felt-textured fronds in bruised violet, fungus shelves stacked in sulfur yellow. Waxy skins, thick cuticles, everything built to hoard water against the long dry hours between mists.

Every stem in the room leans. Not randomly — toward the machines. Toward the grow-bars, toward the transformers, toward the warm cabinet sides. Heliotropism retrained: the entire flora holds a permanent posture of attention, a congregation facing an altar that rarely speaks. Where a root meets insulated cable it turns and follows, hugging the conduit for meters as if current were a nutrient — and perhaps, at some leaking splice, it is. Stranger still: in the deep dark, after ten minutes of adjustment, you can see faint celadon lines threading along the wire runs, bioluminescent fungal filament tracing the electrical routes exactly, a nervous system the room grew for itself. And the aisles are clean. Not abandoned-clean. Swept. Root tips patrol the walkways between cycles, drinking the condensation film, dragging dust home to their stations. The garden has inherited housekeeping.

Twice a day — or whatever the cabinet believes a day is — a solenoid knocks twice inside the wall, and it rains. There is no cloud above it; the misters simply open, hissing a fine warm rain over the racks for ninety seconds. Gutters chuckle. The shallow pools widen, and every droplet hanging from every root-tip ignites briefly with reflected machine-light, a galaxy lasting less than a minute. The pools are rimmed with white — travertine terraces already building, mineral laid down in weeks that elsewhere would take centuries, the young geology working hot and fast. Where spray never reaches, alkali effloresces from the rock in whiskered crusts. Then the valves close and the heat begins reclaiming its own, a haze lifting off the stones, the room exhaling until the next appointment.

Between events, the clock of the room is a single drip. One seam above the north rack lets go a drop every eleven seconds, and the pool receives it with a sound too large for its size — wood-block clear, a struck note — and the interval is exact. Around that metronome: the transformer's hum at the edge of hearing, a relay's occasional tick, the sump's distant swallow. Otherwise nothing. No gnats orbit the lamps. No moths scatter when the light changes. No wings, no skittering, no breath but yours, and your own body becomes the loudest ecology present — blood in the ears, cloth rasping, joints ticking like the relays.

Every seventh hour, the grow-bars wake. Ninety seconds of amber-white, a dawn on a timer, and in that window you learn what color has been doing in the dark all along: the paddles flare oxblood, the ropes blaze ivory, the pools turn copper, shadows fall hard and specific for the first time in hours. Then the bars die, and the dark closes like water over a stone, and the colors fold themselves away again. Nothing feeds directly on these dawns — the flora long ago shifted its appetite to heat and trickle-current — but the schedule holds regardless. The room performs its duties for an audience of leaves.

Fallen fronds do not rot here. Rot requires eaters, and no eaters were ever hired. Instead the dead leaves cure — heat and dry hours leathering them into amber plaques that stack like shingles beneath the racks, a sediment of former growth lying intact, patient, slowly mineralizing into the floor. Death down here is not a process so much as an archive.

You stand a long time in the warm dark, and eventually, if you're still enough, you hear the last sound the room owns: a faint fibrous creak, distributed everywhere and nowhere, the sound of stems swelling millimeter by millimeter. Growth, audible. Change here is real but unhurried — the pace of condensation, of crusts, of racks being digested over decades. Two clocks run at once and disagree: the rock, which is weeks old in its bones; the machinery, which is generations old in its serial numbers; and threaded through both, a third time entirely, the plants', which keeps its own count in rings nobody will ever core.

On top of the control cabinet, fused to the paint by mineral sweat, a coffee mug stands filled with hardened crystal — a small geode of somebody's morning. Whoever it belonged to left mid-shift, apparently, and the station simply continued: misting, pumping, counting, dawning.
