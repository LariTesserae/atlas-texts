**The Roof**

Come up at noon, if you must come at all, because noon is when the plateau admits what it is. The trail ends at the rim and the rim is a lip of dark basalt, and beyond it the land opens outward and downward in steps — terrace under terrace, each one a shoreline from some lake that died before anything had a name — until the far side of the plain is a blue-gray suggestion, days away, trembling in its own heat. You are standing near the top of the sky. The air is thin enough that sunlight arrives unfiltered, unbargained-with, and it lands on the black stone and comes back up again as a second sun, so that you walk inside heat the way a fish walks inside water. The sky is a blue harder than the eye was built for. On clear evenings you can see the curve of the world.

This is not a young landscape. Nothing here is mid-argument. The terraces were cut when the water was generous, and the water left, and the wind finished its work, and then the wind too moved on to younger country. Yardangs stand in herds on the northern quarter, worn smooth as sleeping animals, holding their poses because there is no longer enough grit in the air to change them. Dunes lie pinned under their own salt crust. Slumps of talus sit exactly where they slid, mid-gesture, ages ago. The plateau is in balance — the particular, exhausted balance of a place that has already said everything it intends to say.

And yet it is crowded. That is the second thing noon tells you, once your eyes stop watering. Crowded the way a city is crowded: not spread evenly, but congested along corridors, saturated in seams, every available niche claimed. Between the corridors runs bare stone, and the bare stone is honest emptiness. But follow any line of darker ground — a crack, a leak, a shadow — and the crowding begins at once, pressed shoulder to shoulder, no room between presences.

The corridors are made of water. There is almost no other water here. Rain comes perhaps twice a year, and when it does it is an event of violence and joy — gullies run brown for a few hours, the air smells of wet dust and iodine, and from nowhere, from cracks that have been sealed for years, come fat spadefoot frogs that mate and lay eggs in puddles destined to die. The tadpoles hatch in four days. Some make it. The rest become bird-food, and the birds know the calendar better than anyone.

But the standing water — the water the plateau actually runs on — arrives as fog. Every dawn, without fail, the fog climbs the eastern ramps of the mountain, a gray tide moving uphill against all sense, thick as wool and slow as wool. It rises from the warm lowlands at night, condenses in the cold of altitude, and comes home in the morning. It climbs to a precise elevation and stops there, along a contour so consistent that the line it draws has a name among the people who once worked here: the woolline. Above the woolline, drought. Below it, morning.

Someone understood this a long time ago, and built accordingly.

The machines are scattered across the plain like the bones of something that starved slowly — but they are not bones. They run. Along the woolline, in fields extending past sight, stand the combs: thousands of tall panels of fine mesh, angled east, and when the fog hits them it surrenders. Beads form on the wires, gather weight, and fall into troughs beneath. At dawn the combs drip in unison, a rainfall of exactly one width, and the sound — if air carried sound here, which it does not — would be the sound of the whole plateau quietly drinking.

Smaller structures dot the routes between the comb fields: pumphouses with rounded, wind-polished shoulders; cisterns sunk to their lids in the ground; valve stations no bigger than coffins; a single foundry built over a vent field where the earth itself is warm, its mirror array tracking the sun and boiling brine drawn up from the salt basins, sweating out minerals that go down-mountain in sacks alongside the water. And down-mountain is where nearly everything goes. Through a notch in the western rim runs the Throat — the great main conduit, wide as a wagon, descending in switchbacks toward the green countries below. It ticks as it fills and releases. It ticks day and night. The plateau sends its water away forever and does not seem to mind. It has arranged itself around scarcity the way a tree arranges itself around a wall.

Who keeps it running is a question the plateau declines to answer. The machinery is of several eras bolted together — hand-forged flanges beside machined alloy, a brass gauge older than the glass in it — and yet the pumps turn, the bearings sing their one low note, and in the lowest pumphouse, most mornings, there is a lamp lit behind grime-yellowed glass, and a line of washing strung between two stacks, stiff with salt by noon. Bootprints approach the door. None have ever been seen leaving.

Here is the rule of sound on the Roof, and you learn it in your first hour: the air will not carry your voice. Speak, and your words die less than a meter from your lips, swallowed by the thinness. Shout, and you produce nothing but embarrassment and a faint pressure on your own eardrums. The silence up here is not absence — it is structural, load-bearing. But stone carries. Put your palm against the rail of any pipe and you will feel the combs dripping three valleys away, a rain-of-one-width transmitted through iron. Press your cheek to the Throat and feel its tick arrive in your teeth, in your sternum, punctual as conscience. The old workers, it is said, held entire conversations by tapping the water mains. Whether this is true cannot be verified, because no one up here can hear anyone ask.

Into this silence the life pours — abundant, insistent, layered ten deep wherever the water is. The fog-beetles come first each morning, in numbers that stop the eye: ridged black carapaces head-standing on the comb mesh, mist condensing on their backs, rolling forward into their own mouths. By midmorning the combs are carpets of them, and by late morning the birds have taken their tax. Kettleflies swarm above every warm cistern in columns you can see from a kilometer off, feeding swallows and salt-fly wasps and the small quick lizards that live inside the valve housings, which are always warm. Rock-conies colonize the transformer pads, sunning on the hum. In the Kettles — the spring-fed basins where ancient water still surfaces, hot and mineral and blue-green — live fish found nowhere else on earth, tough as coins, thriving in water other fish would refuse like scalding tea.

The long-legged grazers walk the terrace roads between seeps in family queues, single file, unhurried, each herd holding its place in a schedule older than the herds. They drink in order. The pale ridge-cats know the order. The cats lie flat in the thin shadow-lines of the pipelines — the only shadows worth having at noon — and they are patient the way stone is patient, and when the queue breaks, the condors are already stacked in the thermals above, riding the heat-blast off the black rock higher than anything has any business flying, waiting with professional calm for the outcome. Nothing is wasted on the Roof. Even the deaths are scheduled.

Plants exist here the way rumors exist — sparse, local, believable only at close range. Gray-green saltbush. Cushion-plants clenched like fists. Lichens splashed on north-facing stone in colors paint companies would charge extra for. And along every seep, every bad joint in every pipe, hanging gardens stitch the wet line — mosses, small orchids, ferns no larger than hands — so that the plumbing of the plateau is drawn on the land in green. Where the machines fail, paradise breaks out. The engineers, whoever they were, may not have intended this, but the gardeners of the place — the wind, the fog, the seed-rain from who knows where — have never once consulted them.

At dusk, the heat lets go of the stone reluctantly and the mirages begin. This is the hour of the other strangeness: the air over the rim bends light until the lowlands appear, hanging upside down above the western notch — lakes, river-smoke, the green blur of orchards, a rumor of ordinary world floating in the sky like a threat or a promise. It wobbles. It never quite resolves. Then the stars come out, ferocious at this altitude, and the salt pans glow faintly under them like pages left open, and the temperature falls — not to cold, never quite to cold, the rock has stored too much day for that — but past the dew point, and the whole plateau begins, softly, invisibly, to sweat.

If you sleep out, you sleep badly and deeply at once. You dream to the rhythm of the Throat, felt through the ground. Before dawn the woolline brightens, the fog arrives on its ramps, right on time, obedient as livestock, and ten thousand combs begin to drip in the dark, and every beetle on the Roof turns its back to the east.

Below, in the lowest pumphouse, the lamp goes out. Someone, somewhere, has been awake all night.
