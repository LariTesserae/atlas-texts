# The Ascending Flood

The drowning was the last step of construction.

For a long time this room stood finished and dry at the crown of the tower — tiled, furnished, wired, swept. Then, one day without ceremony, the builders opened valves far below, and warm water rose through the building's spine and filled the hall to the ceiling. This was not damage. This was the final act of making. A room is not complete until it is full, and they had chosen what would fill it: warm, still, luminous seawater, and everything that would grow there after.

The hall itself is modest — a long parlor, a small chapel, twenty paces end to end if you swam them slowly, which is the only permitted speed. Arched ribs of enameled steel cross the ceiling. The walls are glazed tile the color of old cream, laid in courses so true that the grout lines still throw faint straight shadows. A stone stair — no, a cast-ceramic stair, nothing here is stone — spirals up along one wall to a hatch in the roof, and the treads are clean, unsilted, polished smooth by maintenance arms that pass over them weekly. A door at the bottom of the stair stands shut. Its handle turns freely. It has not been opened since the flooding, and the water leans on it patiently, the way a cat leans on a door it has decided will eventually open.

Light comes from two sources and meets in the middle. Above, a lantern ring set into the roof lets the actual sky pour down — columns of pale gold that stand in the water like slow pillars and lean, over the course of hours, from one wall toward the other. Along the walls, electric globes in brass cages burn a steady warm white, spaced so evenly that the room has no dark corners worth the name. Where sun-columns cross lamplight, caustic patterns crawl across the floor tiles at the pace of growing things. You could watch one pattern travel a meter in an afternoon.

The warmth is the oldest resident here, even though nothing here is old. One entire wall is the engine: copper coil after copper coil, gauge dials behind glass, a flywheel turning one full revolution per minute with a whisper you feel in your teeth rather than hear. The machinery idles. Its waste heat has nowhere to go except into the water, and so the water is blood-warm everywhere — no thermoclines, no cold seams, no gradient at all. Stirred too gently for layers, warmed too evenly for weather. The whole room holds one temperature the way a body does.

And the plants have taken the invitation completely.

Moss grows in velvet lawns on every horizontal surface — the tables, the chairs, the housings of the pumps, the broad flat tops of the railings. Ungrazed by any creature, it grows lush past all sense of restraint, rising in little tufted forests, miniature hillsides on a desktop. From planters bolted to the ceiling, ribbon-weed hangs down in curtains ten feet long, swaying on currents too slow to see, so that they seem less moved than remembering movement. Vines follow the electrical conduits exactly, leaf to junction box to junction box, and it is impossible to say whether the wiring was strung as a trellis or the vines were trained as insulation — the question stopped mattering seasons ago, if seasons are the right unit here. Weeks, probably. The flooding was recent. Everything is first-generation.

In the center of the room, where a parlor would keep a table and a chapel would keep an altar, grows the tree. Someone planted it in a ceramic planter and grafted its roots directly onto the heating pipes, so that it drinks warmth the way other trees drink rain. Its trunk is still slim, its bark unweathered, and once every hour or so it flexes — a single wooden creak, like a ship settling, the loudest event the room regularly offers. Around it, waxy pale flowers open on cycles measured in weeks. They are pollinated by the water itself, or by the scheduled stirring of the tender-arms, or by nothing; the builders did not bring insects, fish, snails, worms, birds. Nothing here hunts. Nothing hides. Nothing grazes, which is why the moss has gone mad. The only animal-adjacent life is a faint golden haze of motes — pollen, bacteria, shed plant cells — that hangs in the lamplight without ever settling, marine snow that has forgotten which way down is.

Because down is negotiable. That is one of the room's quiet doctrines. With buoyancy canceling weight, the ceiling gardens and the floor gardens are equals; some flowers point up, some point down, and neither direction is treated as ambition. At the top of the hall, pressed flat against the ceiling, the water's surface is so still it functions as a second floor — a mirror-perfect sheet in which the whole room repeats itself, and for a disoriented moment you read the reflection as a dry twin of the hall standing above you, furnished, sunlit, waiting. In the corners, a few sealed pockets of air survive under glass bells and inside overturned jars — kept deliberately dry, holding the few things that must never drown: paper, matches, a small brass birdcage containing nothing and labeled, in careful script, *for later*.

Nothing rots where it falls. That is the second doctrine. Dead leaves are collected by the tender-arms on their slow circuits and carried to heated drawers in the engine wall, where decomposition is performed on schedule, and the minerals return to the water as dosed nutrients. Death here is administered, not suffered. The room's ecology is a closed loop with human hands on two of its valves: carbon dioxide from the machinery feeding the leaves, oxygen from the leaves venting down into the systems below the floor. The hall breathes with the tower in exchanges that take hours.

Which points at the strangest fact of all, the one that reorganizes everything else: the flood is ascending. Below this floor — down the stair, through the shut door, down and down — lie other flooded halls, older ones, their tiles dulled, their mosses matured into turf, their waters a degree cooler and their light a shade greener. Each floor was drowned after the one above it was finished. The builders are working upward, one level per era, converting the tower from the inside into a column of warm gardens, and this room — brightest, youngest, nearest the sky, its weld seams still bright and its paint unblistered and its copper innocent of the first spot of rust — is the leading edge. Somewhere below, the plans show the next ceiling waiting to become a floor of water. Erosion will find this place eventually. It always does. But it has not arrived yet, and the room wears its newness openly: edges sharp, glass clear, a maintenance logbook sealed in a case with its latest entry's ink still confident, a chair drawn slightly from a desk as if someone had just risen, in the water, unhurried, and would be back on a schedule measured in moons.

Three courtesies govern the place, though no sign announces them. Move slowly. Touch nothing sharply. And do not stir what the room has spent weeks calming — because everything here is patient, the flywheel and the flowers and the flood itself, all keeping time together at the tempo of geology compressed into botany, an instrument fully built and fully tuned, holding its note in warm green silence, waiting without any hurry at all for whatever it was built to begin.
