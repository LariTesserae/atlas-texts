## The Foundling

In the valley countries below, they call it the Foundling, because no elder alive remembers it standing there, and yet its bones are old rock — pale granite-porphyry, the color of unbleached linen — thrust up through the older mountains within living generations. It is not large. From the low pastures it looks like a knuckle, a single closed fist of stone rising above the tree line, half a kilometer across at the crown, wearing a small private cloud like a borrowed hat. Hawks cross below its summit. That alone tells you how high it is, and how strange: a hot place at an altitude that should be cold.

The climb takes a morning. The stair was cut into the southern face by hands unknown — steps still sharp-cornered, unworn, as if finished yesterday — and halfway up, the joints between the stone blocks begin to click. Softly. Twice a day, at dawn and dusk, the whole mountain rises about the width of a finger and then settles back, and the old masonry announces it. The Foundling breathes. Surveyors in the valleys have confirmed it with strings and plumb lines; the instrument-cairns on the summit confirm it better, having shifted a hair-width overnight, every night, for as long as anyone has thought to check. Whether the mountain is inflating from some deep slow artery or merely flexing, nobody knows. Nobody has been able to ask whoever built the stair.

At the rim, the air changes character. You pass through the cloud collar — a ring of vapor the dome manufactures out of its own warmth, condensing on the filament lattice — and step into the bowl.

Inside, the first impression is *light*. Thin, fierce, high-altitude daylight falling on pale stone, so that the whole caldera-floor glows like the inside of a kiln. The second impression is wet. Every rock weeps. Not dramatically — no gushing, no rivers — but persistently, the way a clay jug sweats in summer: beads of warm water standing on vertical faces, trickles threading down rills so freshly cut they look chiseled, seeps surfacing in the middle of bare slabs with nowhere obvious to come from. The ground underfoot is warm through your boots. Press a palm to almost any stone and it feels like a cheek that has been lying in the sun, even in shade. Especially in shade — and here is the Foundling's central paradox, the thing visitors remember longest: the sunlight is scalding, oven-hot at noon, while the shadows are *cold*. Cold enough for frost. On the north sides of boulders, in the lee of spires, hoarfrost blooms in white feathers an arm's length from moss cooking in full sun. Lizards commute between these climates all day, shuttling from scorch to chill and back, running their bodies like thermostats.

The floor of the bowl is mostly emptiness — wide aprons of bare pale rock, cracked into polygons, breathing steam at the seams. The presences are islands in it, spaced far apart, each one a small dense world.

There are the seeps, perhaps thirty of them, and around each one a lawn. Moss grows here in strict concentric bands, ring inside ring, each species claiming its exact temperature: gray-green at the outer cool edge, then brighter greens, then a black-green velvet right at the vent where the water comes out nearly too hot to touch. The lawns are living thermometers. Where a new seep broke through two seasons ago, the rings are still narrow, still establishing — the whole history of the spring written in color. Ferns crowd the outflow channels, drip-tipped, permanently beaded, and in one sheltered northern notch stands a grove of nine slim birchlike trees, the only trees on the summit, fed by a warm rill, their leaves never once drying. They shed a constant fine rain of condensate. Standing under them is like standing in the softest shower.

And there is travertine. Below the largest spring, the overflow has built terraces — white, wet-lipped basins stepping down the slope — and they are growing now, visibly, mineral precipitating at the lips faster than anywhere else on Earth should allow. Count the benches and you count years: eleven. The Foundling is eleven terraces old. Each spring the dome adds one more shelf to itself, the way a tree adds a ring.

The animals can be counted on one hand. Steam-moths, gray and small, winged with patterns like water stains, hatching from the warm mud and living one feverish week. Glassworms in the terrace pools — translucent larvae that, when they die, do not decay but crystallize, leaving little transparent crescents along the basin rims that catch the light like chips of moon. And the swifts: three dark birds, never more, that arrive every midday from somewhere beyond the cloud collar and bathe in the hottest pool, the one that would parboil anything else, dipping and shivering in water that steams around them. They leave by early afternoon. No one knows where they nest. Nothing else lands here. The dome is too small, too strange, too hot-and-cold for ordinary colonists; it supports specialists only, a handful of lives arranged like jewels in a mostly empty case.

The artifacts are the reason most climbers come, though few speak of them plainly.

There is the stair, already mentioned. There are the cairns — nineteen low stacks of fitted stone, distributed across the bowl in a pattern that resolves only from directly overhead, each one hollow, each one containing a suspended weight that has moved, minutely, since it was set. They are instruments. What they measure, besides the mountain's breathing, no one has determined. There is the lattice: wherever the fresh rills have undercut the turf, you find it — filaments finer than spider silk strung in catenary curves between buried anchor-stones, thousands of them, silvered, humming faintly when the thermal gradient peaks. They condense water out of the moving cloud and feed the seeps. They draw power from the difference between hot stone and cold sky. The whole dome is irrigated by a machine with no moving parts, no fuel, no owner — a closed water-loop the size of a chapel, running itself in silence. Metallurgists who have taken fallen strands away report that the material conducts heat *against* its gradient, which is either an error in their instruments or an error in physics.

And there is the pavilion, and the door.

The pavilion stands near the highest point: a single chamber of black material that has not weathered in eleven years of steam and frost, seamless, warm to the touch. Once an hour — precisely, patiently — it emits a tone. Low, brief, more felt in the sternum than heard, it rolls across the bowl and dies against the inner walls, which swallow sound the way the stone swallows heat. Nothing enters it; there is no seam that yields. Fifty meters away, unattached to any wall, any ruin, any logic, stands a door. A door-shaped slab, upright, freestanding, its frame engraved with lines that remain invisible until steam drifts across them — then they wake, glowing a dull amber in sequences that repeat but never resolve. Travelers have put their ears against it. Some say it is warm and silent. A shepherd swears he heard, for three seconds, rain falling on leaves — a sound that does not exist anywhere on this summit.

The rules of the place, gathered by those who stay long enough, run something like this: iron lasts weeks here and then crumbles to rust-dust, which is why the builders used none; glass and stone endure. Growing things reach for heat before light — seedlings lean toward warm stones the way seedlings elsewhere lean toward windows. The water tastes of depth, of pressures and strata far below this young crown, as if every drop remembers being ancient. Reflections in the central pool lag. Watch the cloud collar mirrored there and you will notice the reflection's clouds arriving a few seconds behind the real ones — the pool showing you a sky that has not quite happened yet. Whether that is the water, or the light, or the mountain thinking, is the kind of question the Foundling exists to refuse.

By late afternoon the dome has brewed its evening weather. The collar thickens, darkens, and rain falls — warm rain, straight down, only on the summit — then keeps falling past the rim into cold air and turns to virga, vanishing midair, so that from the valleys it looks as though the little mountain is dissolving upward instead of raining down. Night brings hard frost to every shadow, steam ghosts rising off the black-green lawns, the hourly tone tolling in the dark. And an hour before dawn, the whole mass shivers, clicks along its old stair-joints, and rises a finger-width higher — eleven years of that, and counting, a young world still deciding how tall to become, still warm from its own arrival, sweating quietly in the thin bright air, keeping its small engines running, waiting, with the patience of stone, for whoever comes back to read the gauges.
