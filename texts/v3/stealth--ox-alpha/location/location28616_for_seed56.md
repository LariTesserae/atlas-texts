# Seedroom

Somewhere overhead — eleven feet up, through gravel and hardpan and permafrost laid down over four thousand winters — there is a muskeg frozen iron-hard. Black spruce stand in it like nails driven into the world. Wind combs snow off the hummocks and the wind is the loudest thing for a hundred miles.

None of that reaches here.

The room knows nothing of weather. It is a pocket of unfrozen ground inside the permafrost — a talik, the geologists would say, if geologists knew it existed — and it is not unfrozen despite the cold. It is unfrozen *because* of the cold, or rather because of what wants the cold. The chamber holds its water one degree above ice and has done so for millennia, because it was asked to, and the asking continues. Touch a bead of it hanging from a ceiling root and you understand immediately that its liquidity is not a temperature. It is a decision.

The chamber is small. Thirty paces end to end, with alcoves and side-pockets, the ceiling low enough in places that a tall person would stoop — though no person comes, and nothing here was made for persons, and nothing here was made at all. That distinction matters more than anything else about the place: nothing was *made*. The rock was not cut away. It was persuaded. Around the margins you can find seams where the native stone is still dissolving, still settling into the shapes it was requested to take, raw geology barely finished agreeing to exist. The organism did not build its room. It grew it, the way a seed splits its husk.

And the organism is everything else.

---

Stand in the middle and look down first, because the floor is the oldest argument in the room. There is no soil. There is no ground. There is a felt of living tissue knee-deep or deeper — moss-analogues and liverwort-things and lichen-leather pressed into strata, generation upon generation, and here is the first strangeness worth naming: none of it is dead. Dig anywhere and every layer is metabolizing. The deepest layers, ten thousand years of ancestors compressed to a dense green-black cake, are being slowly reabsorbed even now, the floor digesting its own history and lending it back out as new growth. It is peat that reads itself. When you stand on it, you stand on the organism's memory, and the memory yields slightly, warm-less and damp, like walking on the tongue of something patient.

The rasp you hear underfoot — faint, continuous, like distant surf heard through a wall — is the tillers. Palm-sized segmented worms, translucent gray-green, numberless, they turn the felt the way gardeners turn compost. They are everywhere. Put your hand into the substrate and six of them will pour over your fingers, unbothered, going about their work of tilling and nitrogen-fixing and casting the fertile dust that seeds the next inch of floor. They are one reason the census of this small room would embarrass a coral reef. The other reasons hang above you.

Look up. The ceiling is curtained with roots — fine, bone-white, hundreds of them, hanging into air so humid that the roots drink it directly, no soil required. Moisture condenses out of the atmosphere onto them in visible beads. Your own breath, if you exhale slowly, fogs, drifts, and beads onto the nearest curtain within the minute. Your breath joins the organism. Everything breathed here joins the organism. The air is less an atmosphere than a circulatory system that everyone shares.

---

Now the light, because you will have been wondering when your eyes would adjust, and the answer is: gradually, and only partly.

There is no sun down here and no lamp. The illumination is an accounting of small things. Patches of the tissue glow — a foxfire pallor, jade-pale, in islands the size of spread hands. Beneath the felt of the floor, brighter fronts of luminescence move like weather seen from orbit: slow sheets of brightness crossing a district, gathering at a junction, pausing, dispersing. Watch for an hour and you will notice the fronts are not random. Watch for a week and you will suspect they are sentences. You are watching it think, at the resolution of weeks.

And there are the lenses. This is the detail that undoes people who study such places, if any ever do: scattered through the tissue are lenses of perfectly clear ice, lentil-shaped, grown deliberately and positioned — over years — to catch the foxfire glow and fold it, focus it, deliver it to wherever the work is. The organism freezes water into instruments. It manages its own dim light the way a jeweler manages lamplight, one degree above melting, and if a lens drifts out of alignment it may take a decade of growth to nudge it back. Nothing hurries. Nothing needs to.

Most of the room remains murk regardless. Gray-green dark, bone-white roots adrift in it, the wet gleam of weeping stone. Color survives here in small budgets: a bruised violet where two glows overlap, the rust-faint stain of minerals bleeding through new rock, the pale gold of sugar-dew beading on root tips. The rest is shadow with texture.

---

The sound is louder than the light, and it never stops.

Beneath everything is the hum — low, felt in the sternum and the molars more than heard, a tone the chamber itself sustains, the whole room acting as the body of an instrument. Twice a year the hum changes key. Nobody knows what that means. Possibly it is the sound of a page turning.

Above the hum: drips, spaced minutes apart from a half-dozen ceiling roots, each fall landing in the felt with a sound like a syllable. The intervals are not random either, but that is a door you do not have to open today.

Above the drips: the chorus. The film-swimmers — half-inch crustacean-forms commuting through the water film that coats every vertical surface, carrying gametes between districts, clicking constantly, a high shimmering static that is the soundiest fact about the upper air. And threaded through it, the voicers: thumb-sized, blind, moth-soft creatures perched in the root curtains, producing structured tones — two notes, three, a held fifth — phrases that repeat and vary. Here is the mechanism, strange but legible: the tissue *grows toward* certain harmonics. Song precedes form. A district that needs to thicken is sung to, for months, until it thickens. The voicers are paid in sugar-dew. They are, functionally, architects who draw with their throats, and the building is the listener.

Press your ear to the floor felt, past the tillers, and you will hear the growth itself: fiber tensioning, a long creak like ship's rigging played at a thousandth of its proper speed, the sound of a structure becoming itself. Occasionally — once a season, perhaps — a structural strand settles with a groan that rolls around the chamber for a full minute. The room resounds like a struck bell that declines to stop.

---

One more animal, and it deserves its own paragraph.

Sometimes a warm shape crosses the murk. Dog-sized, slow, shaggy with insulating filaments, a browser that moves through the chamber on a circuit of weeks, pruning congested growth, opening galleries, dropping inoculant dung that starts new microbial districts. There are perhaps five of them in the whole room. They are the only warm-blooded things here, and because everything else is one degree from ice, their body heat makes them visible — not to the eye, exactly, but to the room. Where a browser sleeps, the tissue *leans*. Over years, over decades, the surrounding growth orients itself subtly toward that resting warmth, the way a houseplant leans at a window. The organism cannot help it. Warmth is attention here. Warmth is the only sun this place remembers, and it loves — clumsily, geometrically, at the pace of decades — toward whatever kindness of heat passes through.

If you stood still long enough, it would lean toward you too.
