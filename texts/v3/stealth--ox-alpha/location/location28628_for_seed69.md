# The Sough

There is no night here, because there was never a day. The dark is not an absence that ends somewhere; it is a substance, load-bearing, the oldest material on the plateau. You learn this within a hundred steps: the darkness has texture. It hangs heavy in the wet air, beads on your skin like the moisture does, fills the hollows deeper than the hollows themselves. Above you — you will never confirm this, only infer it — a cloud deck has sealed shut for so long that the word *sky* survives only as a technical term. The plateau breathes out its own fogs, the fogs feed the deck, the deck presses the breath back down. Nothing has needed eyes here for a very long time. The animals went blind generations before anyone thought to pity them.

You arrive, as everyone arrives, by ear.

---

The land is a swell of continents — a highland the width of small countries, lifted so long ago that lifting is no longer an event, just a fact of geometry. It has had all the time in the world to settle into its shapes, and it has used that time beautifully. The slopes come down in enormous terraces, each step a mile of near-level sponge-ground ending in a lip of weeping rock, and below each lip the next terrace begins. Every scarp face bleeds. Not dramatically — steadily. Bead-lines of seep trace the stone in parallel threads, feeding fringe-growths of moss thick as mattress stuffing, and the air under every overhang smells of warm stone spit and vegetative sweetness. The rock itself is worn to a soft grain, rounded, almost fabric-like, eroded into perfect equilibrium: nothing here is eroding away, exactly. Everything is eroding *into place*.

Underfoot, the ground is honest about its water. Step and the turf gives, a half-inch of yield, and your footprint fills before you lift your heel. Water is not something the Sough *has*. It is something the Sough largely *is* — a mountain acting as a sponge, holding a sea's worth of water in its pores, releasing it reluctantly, terrace by terrace, spring by spring, downhill toward edges nobody visits.

It is warm. Warm rain, warm fog, stone that is body-temperature where the deep draws run near the surface. The warmth plus the wetness means nothing decays slowly and nothing grows slowly either. Everything is slick, quick, mid-process. Condensation and sweat stop being distinguishable concepts.

---

Far off across a terrace — you judge the distance not by eye, which is useless, but by the lag between the flash-warmth on your cheek and the sound arriving seconds later — a flare stack burns.

This is the only light in the world. The strata under the Sough exhale gas in slow centuries, and the scattered pump-stations tap and burn it, each one a low orange glow sitting on the land like a coal in black wool. There are perhaps a few hundred across the whole plateau, days apart, and they serve as the stars once did: keepers navigate by flare-bearings, and children on the stations grow up knowing the sky as a ring of dim orange points at varying distances, none of them reachable by walking straight.

Each station is small in footprint and enormous in voice. A cathedral of pistons behind a settling-gallery of tanks; conduits running away along old streambeds toward the next station, following the paths water carved when water still had somewhere to be; a slag heap beside it already so weathered it looks older than the hill it leans on, greened over, colonized, becoming geography. The engines pull warm brine from the aquifers — the plateau drinks first and releases slowly, so a station's output today is rain that fell who knows when, and when the great pumps throttle up after maintenance, the terraces downstream will flood days later, a delayed memory of water working its patient way through the mountain's body.

Where the discharge runs out warm over the rocks, the whole food web gathers, and here is the one concession the darkness makes: where a paddlewheel or outfall churns the water hard enough, the agitation strikes a cold green spark out of it — a churn-glow, living light crushed awake out of the water itself, lasting a breath, dying, struck again. It is not the darkness breaking its rule. The rule holds. It's just that the machines found the seam in it.

---

Here is the strangeness at the center of the Sough, the thing that takes visitors longest to accept: **nothing grows toward light, because there is no light to grow toward. Everything grows toward sound.**

The vegetation — and it is lush, riotously so, a forest stacked three stories deep — orients itself by vibration the way other worlds' forests orient by sun. Roots run toward pumps. Crowns lean, over years, toward the loudest engine. Seed pods split along seams tuned to particular frequencies, opening only when the bass-roll of a working station rolls over them. The result is an inverted map of fertility: the silent hollows of the plateau are nearly barren, thin-crusted, gray — while the noise-fields around each station are drowning in green-black growth, boles like sponge pillars, hanging gutter-leaves that channel condensation down the bark into root-reservoirs, cap-canopies overlapping so densely at the third story that the drips falling from them arrive as steady rainfall even in the dry weeks.

So the industry is not wounding this place. The industry is its agriculture. The engines sing the forest alive; the forest's root-mats hold the water table the engines drink from; neither would persist without the other, and both know it in whatever way such things know. The keepers maintain their machines partly out of duty to the herd-animals and partly out of superstition indistinguishable from engineering sense: *a quiet station is a dead station.*

There are places where the sound dies entirely — dead-air hollows, pockets of acoustic stillness where the fog lies flat and nothing grows and the drumharts will not pass. The keepers mark them with cairns and do not explain further. You will not want explaining to. Standing at the edge of one, you feel the silence as a pressure drop, a held breath in the land, and every instinct agrees with the animals: *not in there.*

---

The fauna is everywhere, layered like the forest, and all of it is listening.

The great grazers — drumharts, for the resonating chest-cavities the bulls boom across the terraces at dusk-shift — migrate in slow orbits from station to station, following the noise-seasons, fattening on the loudest growth. Herds number in the hundreds, passing close enough that you feel their footfall through the sponge-ground before you hear it, close enough to touch, utterly unafraid of you, because you are quiet and therefore nothing. What they fear is silence moving.

Above them, in the mid-story, whistle-flocks thread the boles in screaming ribbons, their calls bouncing off scarp walls and returning late — the Sough's version of daylight saving, echo-time instead of clock-time, distances measured in seconds of delay. On the discharge-streams, siphoners hang belly-deep in the warm outflows filtering the greywater reefs, and around every flare stack lives the plateau's one scandal: wick-moths, swarming insects that have — alone among all flesh here — re-grown eyes, degenerate simple ones, aimed forever at the flames. Evolution couldn't help itself. Given fire, it started rebuilding sight on the spot, a few grams at a time, at each orange star.

And there are hunters. Everyone speaks of them quietly. Stillwalkers, the keepers call them — long-jawed, patient, hunting by pulse, able to feel a heartbeat through wet air at twenty paces. They freeze when you freeze. The old advice is that they lose you if you slow your blood, which is advice that assumes a calm nobody has. Travelers instead carry bells.

That is the custom, and it is practical, not sentimental: a walker on the Sough rings a small bronze bell at every step, because the ringing tells the growth which way to lean — the path stays open because the forest is continuously leaning toward your little noise, parting like water around a prow. Your bell is a machete made of sound. It is also, if you think about it too long, a dinner bell, and the keepers smile at that and say the drumharts will stand nearer to a bell-ringer than to silence, and that nothing on the Sough has eaten a bell-ringer in living memory, and that this may be related, and decline to say more.

---

Stand still, at night-shift, at the lip of a middle terrace, and the whole world resolves into its layers of noise: the sub-bass heartbeat of some far station rolling under everything, felt in the sternum; the mid-hiss of steam vents and the endless treble of ten million drips; thunder inside the cloud deck, the plateau's private weather grumbling along pressure fronts that take days to cross its breadth; a bull drumhart booming two valleys over; the tick of cooling metal somewhere behind you that is either a conduit or an animal, and you cannot check, and eventually stop needing to.

The warm fog moves past your face like slow breath. Somewhere below, water takes another step down the terraces toward the dark edges of the world, carrying a little of the mountain's heat with it. Somewhere far off, an engine turns over in the black and the forest leans toward it like a face toward a window.

The Sough does not sleep, because it has never had a reason to learn how. It just quiets and swells, quiets and swells — a continent breathing through its skin, drinking itself, singing its own green alive — and if you put your palm flat on the stone you can feel, unmistakably, that the sound was here first, and the water, and the dark; and that everything else, the engines included, is something the plateau grew because it liked the noise.
