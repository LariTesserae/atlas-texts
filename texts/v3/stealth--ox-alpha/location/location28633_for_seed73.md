# The Weeping Stair

There is no cold at the top of this world.

That is the first thing to understand, because everything else follows from it. The massif stands taller than anything should — its highest shoulders push up through a cloud sea that stretches past every horizon, and by every law the lowlands remember, its summit ought to be a crown of ice. It is not. It is warm. Warm the way a body is warm. You can lay your palm against a cliff face four thousand strides above the wool and feel a mildness there, steady as a sleeping animal, and the stone will be sweating against your hand while you do it.

The mountain weeps. That is not metaphor. Every wall of it, every terrace lip and gully and overhang, beads with water that arrives out of the air itself. The atmosphere here is saturated from top to bottom; the heat will not let the moisture let go, and so the water goes nowhere except into everything. It films the leaves. It swells the moss until the moss drips when a bird lands. It gathers in the fine seams of the rock and runs out again in threads no thicker than string, thousands of them, so that a cliff face viewed at dusk looks like it is growing hair made of light. The locals — the few keepers who live up here, in twos and threes, at stations their great-grandmothers' great-grandmothers founded — call the whole region the Weeping Stair, because the mountain descends toward the wool in a series of enormous terraces, each one a few hundred meters of old stream-cut bench, ringed at its edge with spring-lines where the ground simply gives up its water, generously, continuously, without any apparent effort.

The water runs warm. Blood-warm, always, summer and what passes for winter. It crosses each terrace in braided sheets a finger deep, slows through meadows of sponge-turf that swallow your footsteps to the ankle, and then pours quietly over the terrace lip in a hundred small falls, or dives into swallow-holes and is gone. Drink it and it tastes of stone and faintly sweet, like minerals remembering sugar.

## The light

Because the cloud sea lies below and the broken sky lies above, everything here is lit twice. White-gold light comes up off the wool, diffused and sourceless, filling shadows from underneath. Paler light comes down through ragged tears in the high haze. The result is a world with almost no shadows at all — a flat, luminous, aquarium brightness in which distance stops meaning much, since a summit island five kilometers out looks nearly as crisp as the fern at your boot. Only when the gaps align does a sunshaft fall through, a hard-edged column of gold that lands on one hillside and crawls across it over the course of an afternoon, so slowly you could outrun its shadow. Things caught in a shaft steam. The whole region steams, gently, everywhere, all the time, and the smell of it is warm wet granite and green rot and crushed fern and something faintly metallic underneath, like a coin held in the mouth.

Noon is not bright here so much as *thick*. The air holds you. Sweat does not evaporate so much as relocate.

## The green

Lush is an undersell. The fog-forest on the terraces grows in layers the way a city builds in floors, and every floor is full. The canopy belongs to the fog-trees — great grey-trunked things that are less tree than root-system-in-search-of-a-tree, their crowns a lacery of aerial rootlets that drink directly from the passing air. Beneath them, orchid curtains hang thirty feet long, so densely packed along branch-shadow lines that from a distance the mid-story looks upholstered. Moss fills the roles that grass holds elsewhere: moss on the trunks, moss on the moss, moss banks deep enough to have their own internal water tables and their own small fauna of things that live inside a sponge and have never once seen sky. Two leaf-strategies coexist everywhere you look: leaves shaped to shed a film of water in seconds, glassy and quick-draining, and leaves shaped like cups and throats, built to catch and hold and drink. Neither strategy wins. Both carpet acres.

And nothing here has ever burned. That bears saying plainly, because nowhere else can say it. Lightning comes down out of the round grey ceiling and grounds harmlessly into the wet lattice of the world; there is no tinder, no dry season, no word in the keepers' old songs for wildfire. Fire is a traveler's rumor. Seeds, freed from any need to survive burning, have gone elsewhere with their inventions — here they mostly simply *drift*, riding the fat slow thermals off the warm stone, and so the forests' maps are drawn by wind rather than by animals, species arriving on terraces like letters delivered by weather.

## The animals

Life stacks vertically here, and loudly.

Along every seep-line, in numbers that stop meaning anything, live the chime-frogs: thumb-sized, translucent-skinned, singing a continuous shimmering tone that merges across kilometers into one sustained glassy note underlying everything. On the meadow floors graze the dew-cattle — broad-footed, patient, barrel-sided beasts that stand for hours tilted into the breeze with their horn-sails spread, condensing their own drinking water out of the air. They are slow in the way of things that have never needed to be fast; a herd crosses a terrace the way a weather front crosses a country. At night, seep-eels make their travels, going overland from pool to pool along the wet stone, breathing easy as long as their skins stay slick, so that on dark evenings the terrace paths ripple quietly with migrating silver.

Above the wool — out where the summit islands rise through the cloudfield like dark whales surfacing — live the drifters. Balloon-bodied, sail-finned, some of them the size of river barges, they hang at the cloud margin filtering lifted pollen and aerosol out of the updrafts, and when they call to one another the sound comes *through* the cloud sea rather than over it, arriving everywhere at once as a soft mournful moan with no direction to it. Raptors whistle among the islands, taking insects flushed up by thermals. And inside the wool itself, the keepers insist, there is a whole pale ecology that no one has properly seen: things heard, tracked by ear, never landed.

## The old warmth

Now — the strangeness under all of it.

The warmth is not geological. The keepers' oldest records, scratched in a script three languages dead, already refer to it as *old*, and geology does not stay this politely constant. The truth, as near as anyone has worked out, is that the mountain's bones are threaded — through every shoulder and terrace-wall, in veins finer than hair and in conduits wider than a road — with a lattice of some manufactured substance still performing its function after a span no one can estimate. It pulls heat from somewhere and spreads it with perfect evenness. It lifts water, too: certain polished channels run *uphill*, steadily, silently, carrying spring-water up to high cisterns by capillaries working on principles nobody living can name. The keepers stopped wondering generations ago. They set their cups in the ascending streams and give thanks in whatever words they've inherited.

This is also why the mountain never changes shape. It has been eroding forever — every terrace is a wound of immense age, every gully centuries of patient water — and yet the Stair's profile today matches the profile in the oldest carvings, because the lattice knits microfractures closed as fast as the warm rain opens them. The mountain wears away the way skin wears away: continuously, and never. It is not young and it is not dying. It is *settled*, in a balance maintained by machinery indistinguishable from grace.

## What hands have left

People have been here, thinly, for a very long time.

A stair — the Long Stair — switchbacks up one full wall of the massif, its steps cut so precisely and finished with such subtle texture that though they have been climbed for millennia they have never once gone slippery. Way-stations stand at intervals along the terraces: single domes, half-swallowed now by fig-moss and orchid, their interiors dry, their cistern mouths worn smooth-lipped by ten thousand generations of ladles. On one high shoulder stands the Chimeyard: several hundred thin rods planted in a grid, each one singing softly when the Round Storm passes — a slow cyclonic haze that circles the massif endlessly, taking the better part of a week per lap, its thunder rolling around inside the cloud basin for hours so that storms here arrive from every direction at once. The rods sing ahead of it. They are antennas, clearly, still reporting something, still tuned to something, to no one who could read them.

And in one cliff face there is a door. Seamless, warm to the touch, exactly body temperature. It has never opened for anyone.

## How time moves here

Slowly, and mostly underground. There are no real seasons; the year is legible only in the wool, which breathes — rising and falling tens of meters over weeks, drowning and revealing the lowest terrace paths, so that travel up and down the Stair is scheduled to the tide of cloud. Change happens in the register of generations: spring-lines migrate centimeters per lifetime; the fog-trees' range shifts a valley per century.

But once — roughly once in a keeper's lifetime — the whole massif adjusts. Overnight, imperceptibly, everything warms by a single degree. Every spring on every terrace tastes faintly of iron for a month afterward. And when it happens, the keepers climb to the head cistern and scratch a new tally-mark into the wall beside all the older ones.

The column of marks is very long. What it counts, no one alive remembers. The mountain keeps its own books, and it is in no hurry to finish them.
