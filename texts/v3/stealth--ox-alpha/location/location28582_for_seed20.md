# The Mouth of the Unfinished Mountain

You hear it with your skeleton before your ears. Three switchbacks below the basin, climbing through cloud that has snagged on the young peak's shoulders, the roar arrives up through your boots, settles into your molars, and stays there like a stone swallowed whole. By the time you crest the last slope your ears have caught up, and then there is no catching up at all — there is only the sound, total, everywhere, a waterfall heard through a mountain's skull.

The basin itself is small. That is the first thing you understand, standing at the rim with your jaw clenched against the noise: such a little mouth for such a voice. The whole calderalet is a bite taken from the mountain's shoulder, a few hundred paces across, walled in rock so new it has not learned weather. Nothing here is rounded. The ridgelines are knife-work. Black glass sand slides underfoot in sheets, and fist-sized volcanic bombs lie scattered across the benches like words dropped mid-sentence. There is no soil to speak of. There is no name for the peak either — naming takes weathering, and this rock is younger than the oldest person who might have named it. The mountain is still becoming. Everyone who climbs here understands this viscerally, because everyone who climbs here can hear it happening.

At the center of the basin, thirty paces across, roughly round, sits the pool.

It does not look violent. That is the second thing you understand, and it is worse than the first. The surface trembles, no more — a skin of steam drifting off it in the cold thin air, rings expanding from the center with no visible cause, convection columns rising through the water like heat-shimmer stood upright. Near the margins the water runs tea-dark over felted mats; above two submerged vents it glows opal-green; and in two places, deep down, a thread of pale gold light rises vertically, slow lightning kept on a leash. You kneel and hover a bare palm over the surface. The warmth coming off it is blood-warm, bath-warm, the breath of something alive. The mountain's heart is still cooling, and this pool is where it exhales.

The sound has anatomy. There is a fundamental you feel in the sternum, a pressure-note below hearing that makes the steam shiver in ranks. Above it, overtones wheel and cross, swelling in long slow breaths — louder through the night, the old carvings suggest, though the carvings' makers are generations gone. And sometimes, rarely, the roar articulates. A pressure shaped exactly like a word moves through you and is gone before you can parse it. Listeners have disagreed forever about what the voice says. Some hear counting. Some hear a name — their own, always their own. Some hear weeping, or a list being read aloud, or a language spoken very carefully by someone learning it. The old ones, whoever they were, left a stone near the western rim incised with tally marks beside wave-signs: transcription attempts. Page after page of failed dictation. On the last slab there is nothing but a hole drilled dead center, as if to let the word enter the stone directly and spare everyone further trouble.

Walk the rim — carefully, on rock that has never settled — and you will find the quiet nodes. Knee-wide pockets where the roar abruptly cannot reach, and you stand in one hearing only your own blood, your own swallow, your heartbeat enormous and embarrassing. The pool has not stopped. You have merely stepped between its words. The smoothest of these listening seats are worn concave by sitters long vanished; one holds a neat pile of seashells, carried up from some ocean weeks and weeks away, offered here for reasons the shells decline to explain.

Nothing lives in the water. That is a rule the pool enforces on itself: it will not hold tenants while it is still revising. Life crowds the edges instead, sparse and stubborn and strangely timed. Ochre and rust bacterial felts steam faintly at the seeps. A single rank of rushes grows at the southern margin, every stem leaned toward the center — not blown, inclined, the way plants lean toward light. Lichen rings spread across the bomb-fields, wide and old-looking, though the rock beneath them is younger than the lichen's own patience should allow; the roar's constant shaking vibrates the rim-years double-speed, aging everything at the water's edge in fast-forward. Gray finches nest in the warm stones and have rebuilt their songs entirely out of the roar's pauses, singing in the gaps, whole phrases threaded through rests. Small moths with combed antennae ride the vibration itself, breeding in the felts, their wings tuned to the fundamental. Once, overhead, you watch an eagle hanging motionless on the thermal column that rises off the warm water, climbing higher than any honest updraft should carry it, and you decide not to think too hard about that.

And once — only once — you see the pool try something on.

It is late afternoon, the light pewter-dim, the sun long gone behind the western wall. Out near the center the water gathers itself into a shape: an arch of standing water, holding, transparent, showing the opposite shore through itself upside down. It holds for the better part of a minute. No drop spills. Then it folds down without splashing and the surface is just a surface again, and you cannot say where the water went. Another day, a shoal of silver comma-fish pours through the shallows for an entire afternoon — quick, real, scaled — and by morning there is no such fish, and no trace, and no one else has ever reported them. The water drafts. The water discards. This is a place where the world is being composed, and composition is mostly deletion.

The old ones understood that better than anyone, because they left proof of it at the offering shelves: flat slabs wedged into cracks along the rim, holding the leavings of decades of trade. Leave something on the shelf, and the water may accept it. What returns is never what you left. A pine sprig surfaces across the pool an hour later, dry, younger, its needles unfurled a bright green it never had in life. A carved wooden bead comes back as amber, older by epochs. Bone returns as white coral stone. A knot of grass comes back tied differently — tied by someone. Sometimes the item is simply improved, cleaned, corrected, as though the water had found a flaw in your sentence and fixed it. Sometimes it keeps the gift and you understand you said something wrong.

There was a ladder once. Notched logs descend into the pool from a shelf of rock on the north side, rotted off at the fourth rung below the waterline. But on windless evenings, staring down through the tea-dark, you can make out a greenish suggestion of rungs continuing far beyond any plausible preservation — rungs going down past the point where light should surrender, past the point where a cord should end. Hair-ropes, the carvings show; braided human hair, sacred and strong. Forty arm-lengths paid out without finding bottom. One rope came back up on its own, knotted in a configuration no living person could tie, smelling of meadow.

The reflections are the least trustworthy thing of all. In ordinary light the pool mirrors the sky badly — and not inaccurately, but *differently*: stars at noon, constellations no navigator claims, once, according to a glyph, the pool reflected from below, looking up at a swimmer no one else could see. Only in the twenty minutes when the sun crosses the notch in the eastern wall and lays a blade of true gold across the water does the pool reflect this world honestly. Honesty, apparently, requires direct light. Everything else is paraphrase.

And then there are the silences.

They come every few years, if the carvings can be believed — minutes only, never warning. The roar stops. All of it, fundamental to overtone, and the stopping is absolute in a way sound never is: the basin's silence has weight, texture, a pressure of its own. In that silence the colors drain. Edges go soft. Your own outline begins to feel negotiable, provisional, a pencil sketch of a person. The birds freeze mid-gap. And the pool becomes — this is the horror of it — merely a pond. Warm, pretty, forgettable. Without the voice, it is nothing special. Neither, briefly, are you. The old relief-carving of the silence shows figures standing with mouths open and arms flung out among outlines that are fading like breath off glass.

Then comes the inhale. The whole basin draws toward the center by a finger-width — sand, pebbles, the hem of your coat — and the roar resumes, and reality snaps back into focus with ringing, oversaturated vividness that lasts an hour. The old ones did not worship the voice. Read the glyphs closely and you'll see: they took dictation. They seem to have believed the mountain is pronouncing itself — that geology here is recitation, that the roar is a young peak saying its own true name over decades, warming its mouth, trying a phrase again where it stumbled. When the recitation is complete, the mountain will finally, fully exist. And existence, once achieved, may begin quietly to be forgotten. Erosion is just forgetting with weather.

You leave at dusk. Descending, the roar departs you in stages — from the ears first, then the teeth, then the sternum, and last of all from the soles of your feet, as though the mountain releases you reluctantly, clause by clause. For days afterward you will hear it in still rooms, under floors, inside shells held carelessly to the ear. You will catch your hands moving in the old carved gestures, answering.

Behind you the cloud closes over the basin.
