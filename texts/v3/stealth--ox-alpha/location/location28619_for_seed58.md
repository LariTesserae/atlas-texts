# The Still

First there is the plain — salt, white to every horizon, cracked into plates the size of doors, so hot at noon that the horizon shivers and swims. One structure breaks the flat: a timber headframe, tar-black, trailing guy-wires, with a bore sunk beneath it no wider than a cart. The winchman takes your fare in water-script, which is the only currency worth anything here, and lowers you into the dark.

The shaft is hot within thirty fathoms — hotter than the surface ever was — and then it opens.

You arrive not at a floor but at a railing, and beyond the railing is the chamber they call the Still: an egg of hollowed salt perhaps three hundred paces across and better than a hundred tall, every surface raw and glittering, every edge knife-crisp. This is a young place. Twenty-four years old, by their reckoning — everything here dates from the First Cut, and the elders who remember sky can count on one hand the things that have had time to grow old. Tool marks still ridge the walls in crisp chevrons. Blast scars star the rock in neat, deliberate rosettes. Nothing has softened. Nothing has had time.

Into this fresh emptiness the town has been bolted. Tin and timber terraces cling to the walls in stacked tiers, tenements and workshops riveted to the living salt, connected by stairs, ladders, rope-walks, and pipes — always pipes, copper and iron, sagging in catenaries from tier to tier, sweating beads of brine. It is a hamlet's population pretending to be a city by refusing to spread out. Eight hundred souls, stacked like drawer-bunks. Every horizontal surface carries something: a rack, a coil, a sleeping man, a row of bread loaves baking on the flue-sills of the furnace duct. Laundry hangs *low* here, never high — anything above shoulder height scalds or drips — so the middle air of the chamber is strung with shirts and sheets like flags of a small damp nation.

The light is amber and comes from everywhere and nowhere. Oil-lamps in polished brass reflectors hang on chains at intervals, throwing pools rather than illumination; the vault above them is lost in brown haze. But the salt itself helps: hold a lamp near a wall and the wall *glows*, faintly, honey-deep, light sinking a hand-span into the halite and stopping. Buildings appear lit from within their own stones. At the bottom of everything, far below, something enormous exhales — a double thud, felt in the floor and in the sternum, once every six seconds. That is the Engine. You will stop noticing it in an hour and never quite forgive yourself.

## The vertical country

The Still has weather, and the weather is organized by altitude.

The floor is the furnace country. Deep heat rises through the Tap — the shaft at the chamber's heart where the Engine drinks from the hot rock — so the lowest terraces sit in a dry, pressing scald. People there wear pads on knees and palms, work in short bursts timed to the shift-whistle, and pay children a half-share of water-script to run errands across the hot plates. Cooking fuel is nearly unknown; you carry a brick of hearth-stone up from the flue and it will boil a kettle on the way.

The crown is the opposite country: cool, dim, and *wet*. Warm air climbs, meets the vault skin, and dies there into mist. Canvas awnings and slate trays are racked across the ceiling in fields — the Weep, they call it — and climbers work the rows with lamps bound to their brows, squeegeeing condensation into channels, harvesting the town's entire water supply one pass at a time. Brass "weep-trees," branching condensers hung in the coldest pockets, mark the wealth of their owners the way granaries once did. The law is simple and absolute: *every drop belongs to the gutter beneath it.* Gutters here have names like streets. There is a Gutterway, a Longweep, a Widow's Run. Disputes over a diverted drip are heard by the same magistrate who hears assaults.

And twice a day, the Rain Hour. When the Engine's exhaust cycle reverses and the crown-air crosses its dew point all at once, mist blooms across the whole vault — the lamps halo, the upper town vanishes into a low glowing cloud — and then the first fat drops ring on tin, dozens, hundreds, thousands, a drumroll resolving into drizzle. Pans slide out from under beds. Children are loosed into the squares to stand open-mouthed under it while it lasts, because it lasts twenty minutes and no longer. Afterward the whole chamber steams gently, and for a quarter hour the Still smells of petrichor — wet stone — which nowhere else in the world at that hour smells of anything but salt.

## The Engine, the gardens, the edge

The Engine itself is less a machine than an organ. A single piston the size of a tree breathes in its housing at the bottom of the Tap, driving fans, winches, pumps, and the conveyor that serves the Digging. Its exhaust, vented through long damped runs, makes a sound like wind in reeds — this is why the town is quiet. Loud work is confined to Hammer Hour at shift-change, when the hammers ring out together in a scheduled chorus and the young rock returns every stroke bright and immediate, echoes so crisp they arrive sounding rehearsed.

Around the Engine's warmth, life that isn't human goes on quietly. Fungus terraces step down the southern wall — oyster mushrooms, a pink shelf-fungus prized for broth — fed on pulped stalk-waste and gutter overflow. An algae film paints the wet gutter-walls green. On the eastern side, a lightwell climbs sixty feet to within arm's reach of the surface, and down its throat sifts a faint grey daylight, the only natural light in the town, falling on a stair where someone has coaxed seven stunted ferns and a carpet of moss out of the drip-line. A keeper tends them. Weddings are held on that stair. It is the closest thing the Still has to a chapel, and its light, weak as tea, makes visitors gasp.

And at the far rim, the Digging: the frontier. Chisels and scheduled blasts advance the chamber's wall a hand's breadth a week; the rubble goes up the conveyor, sorted by size, nothing wasted. The freshly cut faces are dark and wet-looking, like cooled wax sliced by a razor. Grandmothers watch the blasts from a bench kept for the purpose. Everyone in town remembers, personally or by inheritance, that the world used to be bigger than one chamber.

## The room nobody cut

Except one.

At the oldest end of the town stands the First Chamber, and no one claims it. Its surfaces are too regular. Its corners are exact. There are no tool marks, no blast stars — it was *found* this way, pristine, the day the shaft broke into it, and the founders pitched their tents on its flawless floor. Today it holds the cistern: a square black pool of perfect stillness in a perfect room, gathering the town's reserve drop by drop, each impact opening a slow ring that travels to the walls and dies. The locals will tell you, if you ask why there are no rats in the granaries, no weevils in the flour, no moths in the wool, not even flies at the fungus racks — they brought cats down in the third year, and the cats would not eat, and were sent back up — they will tell you, shrugging: *the Still keeps itself clean.*

They say it comfortably. Mostly they are comfortable saying it.

## Night

When the Engine slows for the night cycle the whole vault settles into its loose canon of drips — the Weep counting time, the gutters answering, each drop distinct in the hard acoustic of the young rock. Mist thickens at the crown. Somewhere up in that low cloud a climber moves lamp-first along the Longweep, a floating ember dragging its halo, harvesting tomorrow's drinking water from tonight's breath. The salt walls glow faint amber wherever lamplight leans on them. The Engine thuds. The count of drops continues.
