# The Fogshed

Come down into the bowl at dawn and you will walk through the fog before you see the town inside it. The surrounding country is dry hills, tawny and thin-skinned, but every night the cold air pours off those slopes carrying its load of mist, and the mist pools in the basin the way water pools in a dish. By morning the whole depression is a lake of cloud. The town sits on the floor of it, four hundred paces across, and you can walk from one side to the other in twenty minutes. Nobody has ever wanted it bigger. The town is exactly the size of its fog.

Underfoot, at first, the ground seems bare: pale raked clay between raised beds of dark root-mat. But look closer and the clay is only a path dressed over living ground, the way a rug lies over a floor. Everything here grows out of everything else. The path is a seam. The emptiness between buildings is a material, maintained like any other, raked and hoed and defended, and later you understand that it is the most important material the town has, but hold that thought.

Because the first thing you notice, once the fog thins to a pearly daylight with the sun a white coin behind it, is that the buildings are breathing.

## The architecture

There is no carpentry in Osier in the sense you mean it. There is only pruning. Every structure in town was germinated in the nursery rows at the eastern edge, staked, watered by hand, trained over bamboo-and-leather forms for thirty or forty years, and finally occupied. A house is a cultivated organism: a willow-oak hybrid with a habit like a fist, grown dense and room-shaped. The walls are living cambium under a finishing layer of dead outer bark, sanded smooth by generations of palms. Doors are healed-over seams, cut and grafted shut in summer so they inosculate into a living hinge; you can see the scar-tissue swell where a hundred thousand openings have thickened the wood around them. Windows are frames stretched with cellulose film, waxy and gold in the low light, or, in the council hall and the wellwardens' lodge, panes of amber, orange-lit from within like slow honey.

Nothing is nailed. Beams are joined by grafting, unions wrapped in oiled cord until they take. Chairs are burls encouraged at sitting height. Stair treads are layers of callus, each one hollowed by feet, so that the oldest staircase in town, in the hall grown by the founding generation, descends through shallow bowls of polished wood worn a hand-span below their original surface. The town reads its own history in grain. Narrow rings mean the drought years. There is a smoky brown band in the meeting-house posts where the kiln jumped its trench eighty years ago, and every schoolchild is brought to put a finger on it.

The gaps between the houses are wide. Wide as a threshing floor, some of them, empty clay with a raked pattern like a zen garden, and this is deliberate twice over. First, fire. In an all-green town, fire is the only god worth fearing, and the bare lanes are firebreaks; they radiate on a plan, and every adult takes a rotation walking them at night with a nose lifted for scorch. Second, room. Each building needs light and air and root-run, and the next generation of houses stands waiting in the nursery rows anyway, hip-high, tagged in bark-script with the family names that will tend them. Selling a house means selling an obligation: whoever takes it inherits the feeding, the pruning, the eventual death and replacement. Houses outlive their people. In a real sense the houses *are* the family line, since every one is a graft descended from the original mother-stock, genetically immortal. When someone dies, their coffin-less body goes quietly, respectfully, to the shredders with all the other deadwood, and what remains of them is standing at the address they lived at, alive, and will outlive their grandchildren.

## Water

The town drinks three ways. There are wells, sunk into the shallow aquifer beneath the old lakebed, and the water comes up cold enough to ache, near freezing, tasting of stone and faintly of leaf. There are the dew-fences: tall screens of ceramic fins lining the northern quarter, with the wellwater circulating through them; the cold metal-and-clay wrings the night fog out of the air, and all morning the fences weep, each fin trailing a bead that fattens and drops into the gutter-channel below. Gutters everywhere are living troughs, fused stems trained into piping, running clear water with a sound like a whispered conversation. And there is the ninth day.

Every ninth morning the overcast lowers, goes from pearl to slate, and rains for most of the day. Everyone turns out. Children run the high gutters clearing leaf-drains; adults swing the rain-shutters out from the walls, broad leaves bred stiff as boards; cisterns gulp. It is half festival, half labor, entirely liturgy. On the tenth morning the sky clears briefly, actual sunlight falls, and the whole town steams gently, every surface exhaling, until the overcast heals over again.

## The industry

At the center stands the Hearttree, and you will hear it before you understand it: a deep, patient knocking, like a knuckle on a door underwater.

It is the tallest thing for miles, a monstrous composite organism, many species grafted onto one root-system over two centuries, with a crown so dense and so high that it transpires through thousands of square yards of leaf every daylight hour. All that pull, all that lift, draws water up through engineered vascular mains in the trunk, and the pressure at the base is enormous. The wellwardens tap it. Ceramic-and-rubber pipes carry hydraulic mains from the Hearttree's roots to risers all over town, and at each workshop a small head of that pressure spins a wheel, drives a spindle, works a bellows, a lathe, a loom. The industry of Osier runs on thirst. The town's factories are, strictly speaking, one enormous plant drinking.

So the day has a tide. Pressure builds through the morning as the sun climbs behind the cloud, peaks at noon, and the heavy work happens then: the mulch-mill out on the clay flats chewing last winter's deadwood into pulp for the nursery beds, the big screw-press bending new pipe, the pulping vats. By evening the mains slacken, the wheels slow, and the town winds down with them. At night only the dew-fences hiss and drip, and somewhere a valve in the Hearttree's root-crown seats itself with that soft recurring knock, once every few seconds, all night long.

The machines themselves are mostly grown or plant-made. Rubber from the latex groves for belts and valve faces; resin from the pine-lines boiled for pitch and burned, carefully, in the lamps; vegetable oil for lubrication; cord and rope of flax and silk; ceramic for every pipe that carries heat. Steel exists, a little: edge-tools, saws, the mill's shredding teeth, traded once a year from the caravans that cross the dry hills and call this place, in their own tongue, the green pocket. ## The animals

There are almost none, and the town likes it that way, since rot and gnawing are the two dangers after fire. But three species are kept the way other places keep cattle.

The graft-moths come first: grey-brown, palm-sized, kept in wax-paper cages in the moth-loft and released at dusk through the flowering weeks to pollinate the buildings, since unvisited blossom sets no fruit and fruit-set is how the wardens know a house took its grafts properly. Their cocoons are reeled for silk thread, and silk is what the grafters stitch deep unions with, so the moths are pollinators and tailors both, and the mothkeeper's office is hereditary and much envied. Second, the menders: glossy black bark-beetles housed in little log-hives at each quarter, turned loose onto cankers they can clean out faster than any knife, tunneling out the sick tissue and leaving the sound wood. Third, the earthworms, unglamorously, in the root-gardens, where the mulch ends its journey.

And there are the three cats. Descendants of mousers bought off a caravan three generations ago, they have never in their lives seen a mouse; there are no mice. They are fat, adored, fed cream by children, and they sleep in the warm ash-edges of the kilns. Once a year, in autumn, a flock of something passes high over the fogshed, going south, never landing. Work stops. Two hundred faces tilt upward. Then the sky is empty again and everyone goes back inside the green.

## Rules

Two customs govern daily life, and both are practical.

The first is quiet. Loud sound, and especially sudden sound, stresses a growing tree: the grain pinches, twists, scars. Shout in the nursery rows and the chair-legs of forty years hence will come out of the form warped. So the town speaks softly, walks softly, teaches its children to drop things from low heights, and signals alarms not with bells but with rapid mallet-work on the hollow resonance-trunks, a triple knock passed from quarter to quarter. The quiet is not piety. It is quality control. It is also, after a few days, the thing visitors find they cannot leave behind; the silence gets into your breathing.

The second is that nothing is buried. Deadwood, spent forms, failed grafts, the dead themselves: all of it goes to the mill, and the mill feeds the beds, and the beds raise the next generation of houses and, eventually, of people. The town is a closed loop drawn in green, centuries old, settled into its own state the way water settles level. Nothing rushes. Nothing is wasted. The erosion here is not decay but polish: every surface touched, every tool handle molded to a lineage of hands, every lane raked to the same pattern for so long that the rake-strokes seem geological.

Stand at the northern firebreak at dusk, on one of the ninth days, with the rain just ending and the lamps of resin-oil coming up amber behind cellulose windows. The gutters talk. The dew-fences begin their long nightly weeping. Under everything, felt more than heard, the Hearttree knocks, counting out the pressure for tomorrow's work, and overhead the fog starts down from the hills again, right on schedule, filling the bowl to the exact brim the town was built to hold.
