You come at it from above, because there is no other way. The track ends where the pines give up, on the shoulder of a valley walled with old snow, and there the land opens into a bowl, and the bowl holds a lake the size of a held breath — under a mile across, round as a coin, so high that the light arriving in it has already traveled farther than most travelers do. From the rim you see water, reed-fringe, a scatter of tree crowns standing out of the eastern cove like the backs of grazing animals. You do not see the village. That is the first thing to understand about Holtmere: it is a town the size of a grove, hidden inside a wood the size of a room, and both of them are underwater.

Wade in off the shelf and the lake takes you in layers. First a hand's depth of warm skin, sun-held, gone in a stroke. Then the cool — temperature 1.3, remember; this is snowmelt country, patient and cold — and the light goes amber, because the cedars give tannin to the water the way hearths give smoke to a house. The water is clear the way old things are clear: every grain of silt that was ever stirred here settled out centuries ago, and now the only motion is light. It falls in shafts through gaps the villagers deliberately prune in the canopy — they call these *windows*, and they are exactly that, openings cut in a roof of leaves so that the sun can reach the garden beds below. Nets of caustic light slide over everything, forever rearranging.

Underfoot is the Weave.

This is the part that takes longest to believe. The floor of the cove is not soil. It is one continuous mat of living root — cedar root, willow root, the rhizomes of a thousand generations of pondweed, knit and interknit until the whole basin floor is a single animal of vegetable matter, ankle-deep, faintly resilient, holding its silt the way a fleece holds lanolin. Mussels stud it in constellations. Crayfish tunnel it and aerate it. When you walk on the Weave you feel it think — nothing dramatic, just a millimeter of give, a slow flex, ground that is also creature. And the paths wander. Every route through the village is marked with white quartz pebbles and little shell-chimes, and every year the routes are somewhere slightly else, because the ground migrates toward traffic the way a river migrates toward its own weight. The villagers don't fight this. The paths are voted into existence one footprint at a time, and the elders read the year's movement like weather.

The trees are amber-cedars, ten men tall, trunks rising the full height of the water column and crowning six or eight feet into the air. Their bark is burnt umber; their wood, when a branch must finally be taken, smells of resin and honey. Around each trunk at the waterline stands a family of woody knees, and the knees vent — the whole grove breathes through them, marsh-gas and root-exhale ticking up in fine sheets of bubbles all day long. Put your ear to a lily stem at noon and you can hear the meadow drinking light. The grove is loudest exactly when it is brightest.

And here is the second thing to understand: the grove is tuned.
