# The Weeping Hall

You hear it before you see it. The stair that brings you down — ninety-one steps, cut into the hillside, each one dished like a spoon by feet that stopped coming centuries ago — begins to transmit the sound through your boot soles before your ears have properly taken it in. By the last turning, it is a pressure. By the door at the bottom, an atmosphere. And then the door (iron once, now a lime-crusted aperture with the ghost of hinges) opens, and the sound stops being something you hear and becomes something you stand inside.

It is a single room.

That is the first fact, and the strangest. After the descent, after the dark of the stair, you expect a complex — galleries, branches, more stairs going further down. Instead: one hall, one vaulted chamber, and from the keepers' causeway at its edge you can see all of it. Every object the room possesses could be counted before lunch. The room itself would take much longer to cross. It is perhaps eighty paces end to end and half again as wide, and it is nine parts air. A row of massive piers marches down the center, holding the vault, spaced so far apart that each one stands in its own private acre of shallow water and emptiness. Three ragged mouths open in the ceiling — collapsed bays, or windows worn into openings, no way to tell — and through them fall three columns of ordinary daylight, slanting, dusty-green, absolutely steady. Otherwise the hall belongs to the diffused brightness that bounces off wet stone. There is no glow here, no luminescence, nothing clever about the light. The only unusual thing about it is its patience.

The water is everywhere and it is going nowhere.

That bears saying carefully, because the water is also, in another sense, extremely busy. The walls weep — a fine film of groundwater squeezes perpetually through the masonry, so that every surface shines and every joint carries a thread of silver down to the floor. The floor itself lies under a lake, shin-deep over most of it, deeper in the scoured basins where machinery used to sit, and so clear that you can watch the tiled bed slide past beneath you, drains in straight lines, silt-free, scrubbed clean by a century of flow that is itself indistinguishable from any other century of flow. Four channels — races — cut the floor from wall to wall, carrying water fast enough that it ceases to look like moving liquid at all. It looks like poured glass that has learned to travel. And around every solid thing in the hall, at exactly the height of your hip, runs a single dark band: a watermark. A tide-line drawn once and never redrawn, because the tide it records has never varied by the width of a hair. The whole room wears this ring, pier after pier after pier, a horizon that stands still.

Here is what the place is: it is a machine, still running, for moving water.

The aquifer in the hill above feeds it without pause. Header troughs run along the upper walls, crusted over until they resemble ribs, and from them the water drops through vertical slots — penstocks — in ropes of falling white that hiss like sand. The races carry it across the floor and under the lake lie the pump floors, felt rather than seen: through iron gratings the hall breathes a warm, damp, mineral breath, and under everything, slower than a heartbeat and just as exact, the floor beats. A great double-throb, metronomic, patient. Somewhere down there, wheels turn. You catch the top arc of one through the shallows — a black segment rising and sinking, rising and sinking, geared through sealed works to the pumps, driven by the water it serves. Gravity feeds it; it repays gravity; the loop closes on itself and needs nothing from the world but rain, which the hill handles. Whatever the water left behind when it exits — under the far wall, through a culvert too dark to follow — is no longer the hall's business. The hall is the middle of a journey of which only its own half survives.

And the reason anyone built it? Dissolved. Not metaphorically — chemically. There is a brass plate beside the causeway, and if you press your thumb into it you find the valleys of letters, worn to rumor, their meaning gone at the same rate as the mortar, which is to say completely, over exactly as long. The hall still does what it was made to do. What it was *for* is beyond recovery. It is a sentence whose grammar remains perfect and whose noun has evaporated.
