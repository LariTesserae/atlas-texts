# The Terraces

You hear it long before there is anything left to hear it *with*.

The ascent takes days. Whatever route brings you — the switchbacked stair inside the mast's hollow leg, or the winch-gondola crawling up the guide cable — the middle portion of the journey passes through a country of nothing. Air, thinning. Light, whitening. The ground falls away below you until it stops resembling ground at all and becomes a brown haze, then a suggestion, then a memory. There is no sound here. That is the first shock: not silence exactly, but the *absence* of sound, the way your ears ring with their own private static once the world stops supplying them with work. You are rising through the emptiest volume you will ever occupy. Per cubic meter, this region contains almost nothing — a few ice crystals, a drifting moth that got lost at four thousand meters and will die here, the occasional feather ascending on a thermal like a soul unsure of its address.

And then, faintly, the drone begins.

It arrives the way weather arrives — not as an event but as a condition. A bass note, felt in the sternum before it is heard. Below hearing and inside it at once. It swells over hours, because whatever makes it is enormous and slow, and enormous slow things do not announce themselves; they accumulate. By the time you can see the source, the sound has filled the entire sky. It does not stop. It will not stop. Pilots charting this region mark it as interference. Migrating birds detour around it in wide, respectful arcs.

The source is a green smudge on the shoulder of the tower.

---

Understand the proportions first, because they refuse common sense. The tower itself is a relic — a mast of ancient lattice steel and poured stone, rising from a foundation no one has visited in living memory, its original purpose a matter of scholarly argument. The garden clings to its upper reaches the way a barnacle clings to a hull: a cluster of platforms, terraces, gantries, and cistern decks, stacked perhaps forty levels tall. The whole of it — the entire living extent of this place — would fit inside a modest town square. You can stand at its center and see every edge at once. Two hundred paces, corner to corner. A village. Less, really. A large room that happens to have weather.

But the room hangs at the roof of the world, and every direction away from it is down.

That is the second shock, the one that rearranges you permanently: standing on a terrace thick with ferns, warm air moving over your neck like breath, and looking out past the drip-line of the last gutter into air so cold and thin it feels less like atmosphere than like the *idea* of atmosphere. The garden ends. The world does not resume. There is simply a boundary — a hand's width of shadow where runoff darkens the plate steel — and beyond it, a void with clouds drifting through it *below* you, lit from underneath, going about their business at an altitude that would kill everything growing at your feet.

Ninety-nine percent of any given cubic meter here is moving air. The place is mostly absence. What presence exists is concentrated, dense, ferociously alive — a thin skin of green stretched over a skeleton of iron, wrapped around a heart of machinery, floating in a nothing that goes on for kilometers in every direction.

---

Step inside the boundary and the warmth takes you like water.

It comes from below. It has always come from below. The tower's core houses engines — you will hear them properly later, feel them through your boot soles — and their waste heat rises through heat-exchange fins, through warm-air flues, through pipes that double as railings and railings that double as pipes, until the entire garden is warmed from underneath like a sleeping animal. At this altitude the outside air would freeze exposed skin in minutes. Here, among the terraces, it is a mild summer afternoon, perpetually, in defiance of everything.

There is no soil. Look for it and you won't find it. Every surface is made — plate steel, riveted seams, poured aggregate, glass, ceramic, copper gone green — and every surface that can hold life does. The planting beds are troughs of engineered substrate, black and fibrous, generations deep now. Moss grows in the expansion joints. Ferns erupt from drainage channels. Orchid-analogues — pale, waxy things found nowhere else on Earth — clamp their roots directly to the warm condenser housings and drink straight from the metal's sweat. The builders made a world, and the world they made has been colonized so thoroughly, for so long, that the distinction between *structure* and *garden* has quietly dissolved. Vines took over the load-bearing cables centuries ago and no one removed them, because removing them proved unnecessary and then unwise and finally impossible. Fruit trees were espaliered into the trusses — trained flat against the bracing, grafted and regrafted until the wood grew *around* the beams, roots threading down through the framework the way rebar threads through concrete. There are platforms on this tower that are held up, in part, by trees. Cut the trees and the deck sags. Everyone who ever tended this place came to understand it the same way: the garden is not decoration. The garden is structural.

Everything is worn. That is the other thing you notice, the thing that tells you how long this has been going on. Handrails polished to a soft shine by hands long dead. Rivets rounded by mist. Paint applied in so many successive layers, so many centuries apart, that the edges of old colors ghost through the newest coat like veins under skin. Rust advances here at perhaps a millimeter per lifetime, and the moss keeps pace with it, filling every pit as it opens. The place is not decaying and not being built. It is *settled* — arrived at its own shape the way a river arrives at its bed, and now simply continuing, in balance, indefinitely.

---

The water comes out of the sky in grams.

This is the driest lush place imaginable. Rain proper reaches the terraces only when the tower's slow spiral condensers — great finned vanes turning overhead at a speed you could watch without impatience — snag a passing cloud and hold it against the structure until it surrenders. When this happens, it rains locally. Only here. Only within a hundred meters of the mast, in a bright column of falling silver, while all around it the sun continues to shine uninterrupted on empty sky. If you are lucky enough to be on an open deck during a capture, you stand in a private rainstorm inside a halo of daylight, listening to it drum on ten thousand leaves, watching the gutters swell, feeling the temperature drop two degrees as the cloud spends itself on a garden that has been waiting for it with perfect patience.

Between captures, the water is harvested drop by drop. Dew rails run along every ledge. Fog-silk — spun deliberately, once, by hands unknown, and renewed ever since — hangs in curtains between the masts, and by morning each strand wears a necklace of beads that the sun releases into the channels below. The condensate trickles down through a hierarchy of cisterns. Every drop is used many times before it is permitted to leave: drunk by the ferns, breathed back, recaught, rerouted through the cooling jackets, warmed, lifted again by pumps that stroke with the leisure of a heartbeat. The whole hydrology of this place moves at the tempo of a slow clock. Nothing rushes. The reservoirs fill over weeks. A drought here lasts an afternoon and is survived by everything.

---

And everything here is *alive* in a way that startles you, because you climbed through emptiness to reach it.

The garden is an oasis in the strictest sense — the only warmth, the only water, the only green in a vertical desert thousands of meters deep — and life has found it the way water finds a drain. Swifts nest in the intake throats of the ventilation system, riding the engine-warmed drafts in shrieking flocks. Bats roost in the flue galleries. Insects swarm the steam plumes in visible columns, glittering. Spiders run their lines between the fog-silk curtains and harvest both the mist and the mist-drinkers, their webs so heavy with beads at dawn that they hang like jewelry. There are white fish in the cooling ponds — blind, or nearly, generations deep in perpetual warm dimness — and a small grey snail that lives exclusively on the north railing of the seventh terrace and exists nowhere else on this planet, and never will, because the habitat *is* the seventh terrace. Birds arrive from the lowlands, having flown absurdly, impossibly far off their routes, exhausted, starving. They feed. They rest. Some stay. Some die — and none of them are ever found, because the substrate digests everything, because the garden eats its own deaths and returns them as leaves.

Seeds carried up from below die at the boundary. Every time. They germinate, struggle, and fail within the season — the wrong chemistry, the wrong light-rhythm, the wrong everything. Only the lineages already here persist. This place has been sealed off from the rest of the living world for so long that it has become its own small Genesis, running its own quiet experiment, answering to no outside jury.

---

Which raises the question every visitor eventually asks, usually while watching a pruning arm work.

Because the garden is tended. Not by people — you will see no one, and neither has anyone else in recorded memory — but by machines. Simple ones. Brass-and-steel gardener frames that ride rails along the terrace edges at walking pace, fitted with shears and valves and sampling probes, pausing here to cut back a vine before it crowds a vent, there to loosen a substrate trough, there to oil a hinge that did not need oiling for another decade. Their cams were cut, presumably, by whoever built this place. Their schedules are older than any nation currently existing. Whether those hands intended the garden that now exists — whether they planned the fusion of tree and truss, the snail, the blind fish, the fog-silk — or whether they built something simpler and the garden grew into the slack their plans left behind, is unknowable. The arms do not explain themselves. They snip, adjust, move on. Somewhere below, in the tower's core, a flywheel the size of a chapel dome turns at eight revolutions a minute, storing the patience of the whole system, and the drone it gives off is the sound this place makes instead of silence.

Some nights — the ones you spend in the keeper's hut, a room barely larger than its bunk, warm from the pipe running through its wall — you listen to that drone and think you can hear changes in it. Slow modulations, phrases, a syntax unfolding over hours. The engineers would tell you it's resonance harmonics, load fluctuations, the condensers phasing. Probably they'd be right. But it is difficult to lie awake inside a deafening, endless, patient sound — a sound with the cadence of speech played at one ten-thousandth speed — and not wonder whether something down there in the dark is saying something that takes centuries to finish.

---

Leaving is harder than arriving, in the way that all departures from warm places are.

At the boundary, you feel it immediately: the cold closes over you like a lid, and the lushness behind you becomes, within three steps, impossible — a hallucination of green and warmth and drumming water that couldn't possibly exist here, at this height, in this nothing. You look back once. The terraces glow faintly gold in the ordinary late-afternoon light, a small bright knot of life stitched onto the shoulder of an iron needle, surrounded on all sides by more emptiness than anything you will ever cross again.

Then you descend, through days of stair and cable and thinning drone, back toward the loud crowded lowlands, the soft air, the real soil.

And the hum stays with you. In your ears, in your chest, in the floor of the rooms you sleep in afterward — that vast slow note, fading by degrees but never quite gone. People who have spent a season on the Terraces report the same thing. It fades over months. For some, years.

For a few — and they say this quietly, apologetically, the way you admit to a superstition — it never fully stops. Somewhere far above them, they are sure, the flywheel still turns, the condensers still comb the clouds, the pruning arms still make their patient rounds, and the garden holds its small warm breath against the cold, waiting for a rain that is always coming, always scheduled, always exactly on time.
