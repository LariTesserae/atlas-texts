# The Sumpworks

You come out onto it at the bottom of a stair that has descended for hours, and the first thing you understand is that the land does not rise again. Not here, not at any distance the eye can reach. This is the floor of the world, and the floor was made.

It shines. That's the first impression — a low, wet plain of pale metal and dark glass stretching to every horizon, glazed everywhere with a skin of standing water no deeper than your ankle, warm as bathwater, faintly steaming. The air is thick with it. Every surface weeps. Walls that rise from the plain like the backs of buried machines are beaded constantly with condensation, so that the whole landscape glimmers under a normal, even daylight — a white-gold brightness coming from somewhere behind a ceiling of cloud that never parts and never moves.

Because nothing moves here. Hold that thought; the place will prove it to you.

---

Walk out onto the causeways. They are young — that is unmistakable, and it is strange, because everything else about this world says *old*. The edges of things are sharp. Cast glass panels meet their frames with unworn precision; ceramic conduit runs in bundles underfoot, each seam crisp, each flange unfurred by a single millimeter of corrosion. In any ordinary world, water like this — warm, mineral-rich, endlessly present — would soften every edge within a generation. Here the water has been falling and pooling and running for longer than anyone can say, and it has worn nothing at all. The rain that drifts down through the still grey cloud is distilled to nothing; it carries no grit, holds no acid, and when it lands it runs in channels cut for it, into basins sized for it, and stops where it is told to stop. Erosion was designed out. The world arrived sharp-edged, and it will be sharp-edged forever. You could drop a splinter of black glass on the causeway and return in ten thousand years and find it lying precisely where it fell — except that the mist will have sealed your own footprints behind you before you've walked a hundred paces, and there will be nothing left to find you by.

That is the law of the place, and once you notice it you see it everywhere. The clouds stand fixed in the sky like painted scenery, and so the shadows below them are permanent fixtures — a shadow across a pump-hall roof has lain there since the roof was raised, will lie there until the roof is unmade. It rains, permanently and gently, over a district somewhere off to the east; you can see the rain's distant silver curtain, motionless, a weather system that formed once, at the beginning, and never finished. Wind blows up one of the great ventilation corridors in a single constant breath, cool against all that warmth, and it has blown at the same speed, in the same direction, for the whole life of the world. The engines turn. The water cycles. Every input is balanced by an output to the last decimal, and so nothing accumulates, nothing declines, nothing *happens*. It is a machine holding its breath, and it has been holding it since before your kind kept records.

---

There is no soil anywhere underfoot. Dig — you won't, but if you did — and you'd find substrate all the way down: poured aggregate, fired clay, packed ceramic ballast, layer under layer under layer, the way sedimentary rock pretends to have layers but honest about it. Yet the place is wet the way a marsh is wet. Between the machine foundations lie shallow basins, hundreds of meters across, floored in dark glass, brimming with that same warm water, and the water is alive in its way — though nothing here that lives is anything you'd call a plant.

Along every channel margin grows a mat of living film, black-green and velvet-fine, engineered or evolved — nobody here distinguishes — feeding on the dissolved minerals the water gathers from the works themselves. It lines the weeping walls in dark nap, thick as moss, and it is the base of everything. Where the foundry-districts vent their mild exhaust, the films grow lush. Nothing is wasted; there is nowhere for waste to go, which is perhaps why nothing is waste.

And on the films, the fauna. The place *teems*.

Low, flat-bodied grazers the size of dogs work the weeping walls in slow rows, rasping film from the glass with mouths like soft planers, leaving pale clean trails that regrow behind them — regrow at exactly the rate they eat, though whether that is restraint or law, who can say. In the warm sump-basins stand waders on stilt legs, gray-plumed, motionless for hours, sifting the water through beaks of baleen-fine mesh; the water they release is clearer than the water they took, and this is their job, or became their job, or always was. Overhead, in the vaulted spaces between the condensation towers, flocks wheel — thousands upon thousands of narrow delta-shaped bodies turning in silence, and their silence is not emptiness; it is discipline, or design. They stir the air so the towers can breathe. You watch them bank as one body around a stack of pipes and understand that you cannot tell where the architecture ends and the animals begin. The great slow engine-tenders — leviathan things, armored, patient, moving at the pace of glaciers between the pumping stations — may be livestock, may be staff, may be the reason the pumps have never once needed repair. The builders, whoever they were, are not in evidence. Possibly they are the fauna. Possibly the fauna got here first and the builders simply arranged the world around what already lived in it, the way you build a garden around an old tree.

Everywhere, in every interstice: life, dense as the piping is dense, layered three and four deep — canopy of fliers, wall of grazers, floor of sifting waders, and beneath all of it the films, and beneath those the water table, lapping at the underside of the entire world like a sea pressed flat.

---

And yet: it is quiet.

Quiet the way a library is quiet, not the way a desert is. All that industry, all those bodies, and the loudest thing in any hour is the *tok* ... *tok* ... *tok* of condensation drops falling from a gallery rail into a basin — at a fixed interval, the same interval, drop after drop after eternal drop, so that after a while you stop hearing it as sound and start hearing it as time. The machines themselves run muffled; every hall is lined in some dense gray felt-stuff that drinks noise, every pipe wrapped, every fan blade balanced past the threshold of hearing. What remains is a hum below sound — a pressure you feel in the sternum and the back teeth, the sum total of ten million engines in equilibrium, so steady that your heart learns to beat around it.

The animals are quiet too. Once, at dusk-that-isn't-dusk (the light here neither brightens nor dims; there is no evening, only your tiredness), a flock settles on a tower field and sings — a single low chord, held, harmonic, more felt than heard, and then done. A grazer lifts its head from the wall and watches you with a gold eye that does not blink. It has never seen anything new before. Neither, you realize, has anything else here.

You are the most entropic object for a thousand miles. Your breath adds moisture the cycle must account for; your footsteps dent films that regrow to spec by morning; the warmth you carry is a rounding error the world absorbs without a tremor. It isn't hostile. It isn't indifferent either. It simply metabolizes you, gently, completely, the way it metabolizes everything — rain, exhaust, sunlight, grief — and hands back the same world, unchanged, gleaming, wet, and new-edged, under its unmoving clouds.

Somewhere east, it is raining. It has always been raining there. It will rain there when you are gone, and when everyone who remembers you is gone, and the drops will keep the same interval, and the flocks will keep the same turns, and the water will stand warm on the glass plain from horizon to horizon to horizon, exactly as deep, exactly as still, exactly as full of quiet, breathing life.
