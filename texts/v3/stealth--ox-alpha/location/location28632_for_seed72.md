# The Sough

You reach it by stair — four hundred steps wound down the inside of the intake shaft, and by the last hundred the air has started coming up to meet you. It arrives warm and thick, smelling of wet stone and far-off sulfur, and it breathes against you in a slow tidal way, because the whole chamber below inhales and exhales on a schedule set by an engineer at a valve wheel two districts away. The handrail under your palm is brass. Iron lasted nine years; brass lasts thirty. Down here, corrosion is a kind of weather, and the town builds accordingly.

At the bottom, the stair lets out onto a gallery and the chamber opens — not vast, as such places go. You could shout across it, though shouting is discouraged; the acoustics carry everything and embarrass everyone. Five minutes' walk end to end along the main boardwalk, its boundaries known to the step. It is a room that became a valley that became a town. Overhead, the ceiling disappears into a brown murk where, if you look long enough, ten thousand small bodies can be seen stirring, and the murk itself seems to have texture, the way smoke does.

Everything here is young. That is the first thing the rock tells you, if you know how to listen. The collapse blocks along the north wall still fit together like dropped crockery, their edges crisp, unrounded, waiting for centuries they haven't had. Flowstone drapes thin and waxy over the ledges, glossy-wet, looking poured last week. There is none of the dry chalky whiteness of old caves; every surface is either weeping or about to. The chamber was a sealed pocket of pressure and hot water until a mining survey drove a drainage adit into it a hundred and forty years ago and found, instead of ore, this — a warm hollow room full of drip and dark and life, worth more than any vein. The surveyors stayed. Their descendants are still here, and the cave is still, geologically speaking, being born.

It rains indoors. Faintly, always, somewhere. The vault sweats along its seams; beads gather and let go in no particular order, so that walking the chamber means walking through scattered private weather — a thread of drips to your left, a hissing sprinkle against a leaf far to your right. Glazed-tile gutters follow the main galleries and catch what they can, feeding the cistern quarter, where drinking-water tanks wear permanent beards of condensation. Around every persistent drip point, a white stalagmite is rising, ankle-high and ringed like a candle, and the town reads those rings the way other places read tree stumps. Children are taught to count them. A birth in the family gets chalked onto the pillar nearest the house, beside the year.

The heat is the constant everything else negotiates with. Near-blood-temperature air, warmer at the springs and much warmer at the Boiler Reach, where the furnaces breathe. Sweat does almost nothing here — the air is already full — so the town moves at half pace, always has, and regards hurry as a kind of sickness. Work happens in the cool hours, which is to say the hours after the lamps are trimmed low.

Because the town makes its own daylight, and rations it. Strings of shaded amber bulbs hang low, hooded to throw their light down and keep it from scattering — bright light pulls the moths off their routes and into everything, costs fuel the furnaces want, and anyway the dark belongs to the animals, who outnumber everyone. Twice a cycle the dynamo is throttled back and a long artificial dusk slides through the chamber over the course of hours, a slow violet-brown fading, and this is the town's evening. The bats wait for it. Everyone waits for it.

The settlement itself is scattered rather than dense — knots of construction joined by boardwalks and pipe-bridges, with long reaches of dark floor and black standing water between them, where nobody builds because the ground there is still deciding what it is. Structures stand on stilts and stone footings above the wet. Zinc roofs gone dull with oxide. Ceramic drain tiles. Creosoted timber, rope stiffened with mineral salt. Nothing ferrous survives a decade, so there is brass everywhere, and the whole town glimmers dully at knee height wherever a fitting catches lamplight. Districts, loosely: the Boiler Reach, with its two big vertical engines — one pumping water, one driving the great ventilation fans — plus the dynamo, the mill, a smithy whose hammer-strokes arrive flattened and late through the saturated air. The Guano Works at the west wall, directly beneath the largest bat curtains: scrapers, drying racks, a press stamping out fuel bricks that go straight back into the furnaces, a closed and slightly comic circle the residents are proud of. The Pens and Terraces. The Cistern Quarter. Housing lofts stacked over workshops on the one dryish knoll. Narrow-gauge hand-cart rails stitch it all together, carrying brickets and sacks and crates.

But the overwhelming fact of the Sough is not the town. It is the animals.

They occupy every layer of the space. The ceiling is theirs absolutely: curtains of bats in the high dark, so many that their combined body heat measurably warms the upper air, and their ammonia-sweet smell is the town's second climate. Guano falls beneath the roosts like the world's least pleasant snow. The Works harvest on rotation, section by section, ringing a bell first to clear the area. The middle height belongs to moths — swarms thick enough that each lamp shines inside a cone of wings, a soft gray static that never quite resolves — and to the cave swallows stitching back and forth, their bracket nests glued in ranks along the flowstone shelves. The nests are harvested twice a year and go down the stairs in lacquered boxes; they are the town's cash crop, its one export the outside world wants.

The water is stocked like a pantry. Blind white catfish cruise the warm pond, tame and fat on kitchen scrap. Shrimp cloud the spring-run in shifting pink masses and get seined with cloth. Pale crabs work every wet stone surface, industrious and completely dishonest — anything set down is being dismantled within the hour, so the town's first law of childhood is *nothing on the ground overnight*. And the frogs. Several kinds, including one true giant, slate-colored and the size of a hatbox, which sleeps through the lit hours wedged in crevices and then, through the long artificial dusk, erupts into chorus. For an hour the sound stops being a sound and becomes a pressure, something felt in the sternum, and conversations happen by leaning close.

Domestic stock fills the gaps: banded snails grazing wet terraced ramps in rows neat as typeset letters; cavies in tiered hutches along the boiler wall, fed on algae cake; beetle larvae in warming drawers; eels barrel-trapped from the outflow. The whole economy is a loop with the ceiling at the top of it — guano feeds the gardens and the algae channels and the fly-vats, larvae feed fish and cavies, fish and snails and nests feed people, and the bats answer to no one. The ceiling is the sovereign power here. Everyone lives in its weather.

Green exists, but barely, and it is fenced. Garden terraces cling to the wettest south wall under dedicated lamps: taro in black muck beds, watercress choking the gutter outflows, moss lawns, and two stunted fig trees under their own private lamps, both of which have names and one of which has a caretaker who argues with visitors about it. A salad is an event. Green is budgeted like metal.

The sound of the place, when you stop trying to separate it, is one fabric: the fans' baseline hum, the boilers' hiss and tick, the exhaust shaft's occasional low organ-note when conditions are right, the thousand-pitch arrhythmia of drips, moth static, frog pressure, the papery applause of bat wings, cart wheels, a far hammer. Human voices run quiet underneath it all, trained flat, because everything echoes here and the animals startle.

There are rules, some written and some simply absorbed. Walk under the gutters; wear an oiled hat elsewhere. Trim your lantern near the roosts. The light schedule is charter law — disputes about brightness go to council, and people have held grudges for years over a bulb. New ground is claimed by lottery when the surveyors open it, which happens rarely, because the cave grows at centimeters per decade. At the chamber's far north end, past the last boardwalk, a red string line marks a fissure that exhales steadily — the mouth of a room still forming, decades from open. The town talks about it the way coastal towns talk about tide land. Grandchildren will farm it.

And the dead go up the stairs. Four hundred steps, carried in a woven litter, out to the sunlit country the living chose to leave. The dead get the sun; the living keep the warmth.

In the long dusk, with the lamps sunk to ember, the bats pour off the ceiling in a river that has no top, the frogs reach full voice, the fans hold their note, and steam ghosts off the cistern lids into the brown air. Somewhere a woman is trimming wicks on the terrace lamps. The founding charter, it's said, ends on a single sentence, and everyone in the Sough can recite it: *nothing here is finished.* Above the town, indifferent and generous, the ceiling begins again its slow indoor rain.
