# The Night Gardens of the Summits

You reach it near the top of the world's oldest mountains — peaks worn so long ago into their final shapes that the stone has forgotten it was ever sharp. There is no climbing drama left here. The ridgelines roll like the backs of sleeping animals, rounded by rain before your species had names, and the summit underfoot is smooth as an old knuckle. The air outside is thin and cool and smells of nothing but distance.

Then you find the notch. A slot in the rock, waist-wide, exhaling.

The breath of the mountain is warm. That is the first wrongness, or the first wonder — warm air, humid as a mouth, pushing gently out of the stone in a place where the wind outside carries frost. You turn sideways and go in. The passage bends once. The gray behind you thins. It bends again, and then even the memory of gray is gone, and you are inside the peak, inside the dark, inside the warmth.

## What Darkness Does Here

This is not the dark of a closed closet or a starless field. This is darkness as a climate — total, settled, and apparently maintained. No phosphorescence anywhere. That is the quiet astonishment of the place: in a world this full of life, nothing glows. Every cave creature you have ever heard of eventually invents its own lantern. Here, nothing has. The dark is treated as a fact, an inheritance, something the life has organized itself around for so long that light would be a catastrophe, an invasion.

Your eyes give up within minutes. They were never going to be useful. What replaces them comes online slowly, sense by sense, like instruments warming: the map-making ears, the reading skin, the warmth-aware face.

And the first thing those senses tell you is the shape of the room. It is not vast. The echoes come back quickly, politely, from walls perhaps sixty paces away. You could walk its perimeter in ten minutes. It is a grove-sized pocket hidden in the crown of a mountain — a single chamber, intimate as a chapel, with a ceiling you cannot see but can, with a thrown pebble and a count, estimate at forty meters. All of it hangs a short climb below the summit. You are standing in one of the highest rooms in the world, and there has never been a ray of sun in it.

## The Floor Is Lush; the Volume Is Empty

Here is the essential architecture of the place, the paradox it resolves: everything alive happens within arm's reach of the ground, and above that living layer rises an ocean of pure vacant dark.

The floor is extravagantly fertile. Pale forests of fungus stand in loose groves — trunks soft as skin, waxy, translucent at the edges, colored nothing at all, because color is a negotiation with light and no light ever came. Shelf-fungi spread wide as dinner plates from the weeping walls. Moss — bone-white, thread-fine — carpets the low ground in lawns that drink directly from the stone. From the unseen ceiling hang veils of root-thread, kilometers of them by feel, each strand beaded with condensation, sipping the air. Some of the taller fungal forms lean together overhead like the boughs of a orchard, and their caps collect dripwater and shed it in scheduled ticks.

But it is all low. Two meters is a tall organism here. The lushness is a rug, a tide-line of life hugging the floor, and the chamber above it — the overwhelming majority of the room's actual volume — is emptiness. Not empty the way a desert is empty, with its waiting and its glare. Empty the way the inside of a bell is empty: shaped, resonant, held. You feel it press down overhead like deep water inverted. When something moves up there — and things do move up there — you hear it the way you'd hear a fish pass beneath a boat.

## Water, Everywhere, Going Nowhere Fast

The walls weep. That is the engine of the whole place. Somewhere far above, rain falls on the summit meadows and vanishes into the old stone, percolating down through cracks polished round by a million years of the same journey, arriving here as a fine perpetual sweating of the rockface. Threads of water trace channels in the walls so worn and smooth they look poured rather than carved. The drips fall in thousands of places, each one a tiny struck bell, and the aggregate sound is less like rain than like slow applause — a sound with texture, direction, geography. Your ears learn the room through it faster than any surveyor could.

Shallow basins dot the floor, many of them stone-cut, all of them full. Their surfaces lie absolutely still between disturbances, black on black, and then a blind fish rises and the ring of its arrival expands and fades. The water is warm. Everything here is warm — the air holds a steady, body-friendly temperature, the gift of whatever residual heart the mountain still keeps, or of something else that will explain itself later.

Because later, when you have learned to read the floor, you find the machines.

## The Scattered Works

They are easy to miss until you are almost touching them: slender dark spires, needle-thin at the top, driven upright among the fungal groves — perhaps thirty of them through the whole chamber, spaced wide apart, so that walking the floor you meet one every few minutes and the rest of the world stays wilderness. They are warm. They hum, below hearing, a hum you feel in your molars. And along their fluted sides, beads of water gather out of the air itself, grow, merge, and slide down channels into carved stone basins at their feet, which overflow in a gentle constant spill that feeds the nearest lawn of white moss.

Atmospheric condensers. Still running. Their seams are too true, their surfaces unscratched after an age you cannot guess at, their tolerances the kind modern workshops discuss as theory. Whoever raised them understood matter the way a musician understands an instrument — and then left. There is no other sign of them. A few steps cut into the wall near the entrance passage, fitted to a stride. One joint in the northern wall where two kinds of stone meet in a line straighter than anything erosion makes. A single disc of some glassy substance resting half-sunk in moss near the chamber's center, cold to the touch, producing no reflection at all — you pass your hand before it and see nothing change, though you see nothing anyway, and you are left with the unprovable suspicion that it is drinking.

That is all. Scattered works, patient machines, and no maker in evidence. The garden grows around their legs.

## The Animals

The fauna finds you before you find it. Warmth arrives out of the dark — a moving patch of body-heat the size of a sheep, unhurried — and stops just outside the range of your breath. It is grazing the fungal lawn. You hear the soft tearing of it, the wet-lipped working of its mouth, and when it finally shifts closer you feel the displacement of air and catch its smell: mushroomy, milky, mineral. Its hide, when your fingertips graze it, is smooth and cool on top and furnace-warm beneath. It has no eyes. Nothing here has eyes. It knows you by the stamp of your heat and the rhythm of your breathing and it files you, apparently, under harmless.

Smaller lives are everywhere in the living layer. Clicking things work the walls like typists. Soft-winged flyers cross the chamber in long glides, their wingbeats a sound like pages turning, navigating by echo and by the humidity gradients that map this world more truly than any light could. In the basins, blind pale fish hang motionless and then dart at falling droplets, striking the water exactly where the drop will land — they have learned to read the drip-sound and compute the future. At night — meaning always — the chamber carries conversations: long bell-tones from something large and unseen in the western dark, answered after thoughtful pauses by a chorus of chirrs. Sound here is social, territorial, navigational. It is also, simply, beautiful, and nothing in the dark is listening for beauty, which may be why it is allowed to accumulate so freely.

## The Pace of the Place

Almost nothing here is in a hurry, and the things that are hurried are small. A droplet falls; a fish strikes; a moth crosses; and meanwhile the true clocks of the chamber tick in units of centuries. The stalagmite beside the third condenser has risen perhaps a hand-span since the machines began to hum. The fungal groves migrate toward whichever wall is weeping most generously, a procession visible only as geology. The basins overflow at a rate measurable in finger-widths per generation. The whole chamber is settled — this is what deep age looks like from inside: not ruin, but completion. Erosion finished its argument with this mountain ages ago and the mountain conceded everything, and now the winner sits quietly at the top of the world keeping a garden in the dark.

Water enters as seep and leaves as flesh, as spore-drift, as the slow mineral breath of stone. Nothing wastes. Nothing rushes. The dark holds it all the way a held breath holds a note.

You stand a while longer among the pale groves, dripping-on, warmed-through, mapped by ears and whiskered attentions you cannot see. Far above your head, past forty meters of velvet emptiness, past the ceiling and a few strides of living rock, lies the summit — and above that, the whole lit roaring sky. None of it gets in. Down here the world's oldest lightless garden drinks its mountain drop by drop, and the machines hum, and the blind fish listen for the future in the falling water, and the dark, which seemed at first like an absence, reveals itself slowly as the opposite:

a fullness. Old, wet, warm, and paying attention.
