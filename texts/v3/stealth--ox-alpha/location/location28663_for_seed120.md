# The Younghouse

You come to it out of cloud, because anything this high has to be approached through cloud. There is no approach otherwise. Somewhere below the cloud there is presumably a mountain, and below the mountain a world, but nobody who works here speaks of them with any confidence, and the cloud keeps its opinions to itself. What you find is a doorway in a curved dark wall — a wall that curves the wrong way, concave, as if you were standing inside a cup — and a door of woven fiber hanging across the gap, and heat coming through the weave like breath through cupped hands.

Inside: the first thing is the wet. Not rain, not yet. An atmosphere that has given up on remaining vapor. It beads on your eyelashes in the time it takes to blink. The second thing is the sound, arriving underneath the wet like a heartbeat under skin: a vast slow pulse from below, the Under exhaling, and scattered over it thousands of small percussive taps — knock, knock-knock, pause, knock — as if the entire structure were being tested for hollowness by an army of patient doctors.

Which, it turns out, is exactly what's happening.

You are inside a tower. You will have to take that on faith for a while, because you cannot see its shape from within — only that it has an axis, and that the axis goes up, and that going up takes far longer than the space you're standing in should allow. Stand in the entry gallery and press a palm against each wall: they're four strides apart. Climb those same walls' stairways and you will climb for days. The Younghouse is taller inside than out. Nobody pretends otherwise. The keepers shrug at the arithmetic and say the sky up here is thin and stretches easy, and after your third day of ascending you stop arguing with it.

Everything you touch is handmade, and everything you touch is new.

That is the law of the place, and it takes a visitor a long time to understand that it is meant *literally*. Look closely at the stone underfoot: no lichen, no polish of feet, no rounding at the tread-edges — every arris knife-crisp, every tool mark as legible as writing. The stair-risers carry the swirls of the trowel that shaped them. The mortar between blocks holds, in perfect relief, the finger-drag of whoever smoothed it — and there are hundreds of such drags, thousands, going back further than memory, none of them faded, none of them worn. Nothing here has ever worn. Rain does not erode it. Water sheets off it and leaves the surface exactly as it was the moment it set. The Younghouse does not age. It was finished yesterday. It will be finished tomorrow. Time deposits nothing here, and so the place accumulates instead an archive: every handprint, every dropped-tool dent, every hoofprint (more on those later), held crisp forever in surfaces that refuse to forget.

Because the stone here is born soft.

This is the central fact, the engine of everything. The stuff the keepers build with comes out of the ground below in a warm, pliable, half-committed state — callable stone the way a larva is callable a moth — and for a span of some hours, sometimes a day, it can be pushed, pinched, carved, dragged into shape with nothing more than antler picks and wooden floats and wet hands. Then it cures. And once cured, it is harder and more final than anything geology ever managed, immune to water, wind, frost, centuries. The keepers' whole calendar hangs on this. Their gongs mark not hours but cure-windows: a signal rung, and forty figures drop their weaving and sprint along the galleries to wherever a delivery of young stone has come up through the floor-hatches, because the stone will only be persuadable once.

So you hear the knocking. Every keeper walks the ways tapping as they go — knuckle, knuckle, against walls, columns, stair-noses — listening for the dull note of stone still young, the ring of stone gone permanent. The calls travel. Knock-codes descend the spiral ahead of you: *soft stock on level nine, bring floats.* The whole tower is an instrument being played by its own maintenance. At night — insofar as night exists in this ember-lit gloom — you lie in a hammock alcove and hear the building ticking as great members set: a slow irregular clock of thuds and pings, the sound of a world committing to itself.

And because nothing weathers, nothing *finens* either — and this is the labor that fills the loudest galleries. Sand does not happen here. Grit does not happen. Time will not grind the mountains for you, so the keepers grind their own. In the milling halls, rows of them swing stone pestles onto blocks caught young, pounding boulders into gravel, gravel into sand, sand into flour-fine silt for mortars and potting beds — and the sand thus made, every angular grain, is permanent. Frozen mid-birth. Grains that in another world would spend ten thousand years rounding against each other in a riverbed sit here forever at their first sharp moment. Run your fingers through a keeper's hopper of paving-grit and you are touching an entire erased epoch of weathering, performed by arm and shoulder in an afternoon.

The heat comes from the same place the stone does. Beneath the lowest floor — below the sump galleries, below the flooded chambers where the tower keeps its marshes — the Under is still unfinished. It glows. Through the floor-grates near the base you can look down into a dull red murk where the ground has not yet decided to be ground, where shapes move slowly in the melt like animals under ice, and the heat pours up, and with it a sound: a tide-draw, a long inhale-exhale lasting minutes, as if the proto-earth were practicing breathing. The base galleries are unbearable — wet heat, kiln heat, air you wear — and this is where the marsh lives, deliberately, because the keepers farm it. Rice-paddy terraces? Call them that. Stepped pools spiral up the inner wall, each a hand-dammed pond of young silt and rotted fiber and kiln-ash, and the water falls from terrace to terrace in a chain of small bright cataracts, one long braided waterfall descending through fields all the way from the crown.

For the rain is indoors. The tower makes its own weather. Heat rises through the central shaft, hauling vapor; far up where the air thins and cools against the crown, the atmosphere wrings itself out, and warm rain falls through the atrium darkness in slow grey curtains, drumming on ten thousand leaves, ringing on ceramic funnel-basins, hissing into the cisterns. Near the crown the rain sometimes brings flickerless lightning with it — dry thunder, sheet-glow inside fog, no true strike, as if the storm were rehearsing. The keepers schedule their sowing by it.

Ah, the gardens. Lush barely covers it. Every horizontal surface is a bed; every vertical surface is claimed by something climbing. Ferns the size of oars. Vines that you can hear growing — genuinely, audibly, a creak-and-sigh chorus on humid nights, stems lengthening with a sound like rope under strain. Moss is agriculture here, not nuisance: strains cultivated for glow, painted onto stair-risers and lintels in slurry, so the ways are lined with faint green foxfire and you ascend through a dim corridor of cold little lights. Oil of crushed resin-seeds burns in gourd-lamps. The overall light never rises above brown-green dusk, ember-red from the grates below, phosphor-green at ankle height, wet gleam everywhere else. Your eyes learn to work at the edge of their range. Colors come to you like shy animals.

The terraces climb with you. Grain in the low wet paddies; fiber-plants for cordage above them; sap-plants whose latex mortars the stonework; a bitter leaf brewed against the heat; fungus in the dark galleries stacked like shelved loaves. Nothing is allowed to die of old age — nothing *can*, but the keepers don't permit the attempt anyway. Every leaf is harvested, cut, replanted, reworked before it can yellow. Death here happens only by harvest. The whole place runs on the principle that nothing should ever get the chance to decline. Even the compost is young.

The water is the oldest citizen. The keepers say so plainly: water arrived already finished. It never needed curing, never needed making — it is the one substance here that predates craftsmanship, and they trust it absolutely, speak of it with something like deference. Fired-clay vessels only; unfired clay surrendering back to the water too easily, a humiliation the potters take personally. Kilns squat in vented side-chambers, small and hot, their glow the brightest thing in the tower, and firing-day smells scorch through the green.

Gravity, near the crown, leans. This is not discussed much because it is simply used. In the upper thousand turns of the stair the pull of the world tilts inward and slightly upward, and the keepers exploit it the way farmers exploit a slope: channels run uphill there, quietly defying their educations, carrying crown-water to the highest gardens with no pump, no wheel, no effort — just bad geometry, honestly farmed. Spills fall sideways. Your balance learns a second dialect.

The keepers themselves you see only in pieces, at first: a silhouette crossing a lamplit junction with antler tools slung on a bandolier; four hands receiving a hatch-load of soft stone; singers whose work-song loops in no language you know, a rising phrase answered from three levels down. Their skin is pale as the cisterns' edge and corrugated with permanent prune-wrinkle; they were raised in this humidity and it has taken them as material. They measure time in cure-windows and rain-shifts. They sleep in felt-lined alcoves mid-spiral where the temperature forgives. They are courteous to visitors in the way of people who expect no visitors, and if you ask what all this is *for* — the tower, the terraces, the endless finishing — they look at you with real surprise, and one of them gestures upward with her chin, toward the last stair, the topmost landing, the frontier.

Go up there, if your legs and lungs consent. Past the highest finished tread the construction simply stops — not into emptiness, but into the uncommitted: a warm luminous fog where geometry hasn't signed yet, full of almost-shapes, suggestions of arch and ridge and valley drifting like fish under silt. The keepers work its edge. They cast cords up into it and haul back whatever conspires to return — a timber, a seam of soft stone, once (they don't elaborate) something with a joint in it. Here the tower is being added, turn by turn, year by year, at the roof of the sky.

And the rumor the younger keepers tell, shyly, when the rain is loud: that the Younghouse is a cradle. That they are raising a landscape the way one raises anything delicate — warm, wet, fed, handled constantly, kept newborn — and that when it is cured all the way through, finished to its last unbudging grain, it will be lowered through the cloud to some emptied place below and set down, brand-new and immortal, to begin.

Whether or not that's true, know this about the floors you walked: every print you left in the soft patches has already set. The tower will hold your footprints, crisp and exact, for as long as it holds anything at all.

It has never once let a print fade.
