# The Settling Grounds

You come into the valley the way everyone does, down the river gorge, and the first thing you notice is that the sky is wrong — or rather, that there isn't really a sky, only a ceiling. A lid of cloud hangs perhaps four hundred meters up, flat-bottomed, unmoving, and you understand gradually that the valley made it itself. Everything below exhales. Ten thousand vents and seeps and sweating stones breathe warm vapor into dead-still air, and the vapor rises until it can't, and flattens, and stays. The sun behind it is a pale coin, a rumor of noon. It could be six in the morning or six in the evening at any hour of the day. The lamps along the causeway burn always, small orange points receding toward a horizon that is less a line than a gradual whitening.

The heat arrives next. Not desert heat — nothing here is dry — but the heat of a room with the door shut. Wet heat. It sits on your shoulders and beads immediately on your forearms, and you realize the whole valley is doing what your skin is doing: condensing. Every railing drips. Every wire strung between poles wears a string of hanging drops. The rocks themselves weep; the terrace walls glisten, and the water that runs off them is warm enough that touching it is a small decision.

The floor of the valley is a staircase worn by nobody. Silica terraces step down in broad shelves from the western rim toward the central marshes, each shelf rimmed with a lip of stone deposited drop by drop over centuries — the erosion here is not violence but patience, a landscape that has been finishing itself for a very long time and is now, more or less, done. It has settled into its own state the way an old man settles into a chair. Where the ground slopes, water sheets across it in thin warm braids. Where it pools, the pools are milky with dissolved stone. Anywhere you care to dig — and the locals will tell you not to bother — you'd strike scalding water within arm's length. So nothing here is dug. Everything is piled, stilted, floated, or simply accepted.

There is no grass. There is nothing that could be called a plant anywhere in the valley, and the absence takes a while to register because the ground is not bare. It is *clothed*. The terraces wear bacterial mats the way hills elsewhere wear turf: zones of color laid down strictly by temperature, so precise you could read a thermometer from a ridge. Blue-black leather near the boiling sources. Rings of outrageous orange. Long rivers of olive-green where the outflows run warm, rippling faintly, breathing — literally breathing, strings of oxygen bubbles rising through a gelatinous skin that flexes if you press it, like the rind of something immense asleep under the valley. In the drying margins the mats cure into cream-colored crusts that ring faintly underfoot. The only other living green is human-made: the algae runs, long concrete channels of cultivated scum, thick as pea soup, harvested with scraper barges. That's the extent of botany. One attempt at a shade garden exists behind the assay office — three stunted, chlorotic shrubs under misters — and locals point to it the way you'd point at a shipwreck.

The town is not a town in the sense of a knot. It is a *web*. Thirty-odd kilometers of basin floor strung with structure, and almost none of it dense. The population could fit in one block of any other city, but the works themselves — the reason anyone came — sprawl. Overhead, on rack after rack marching to the horizon, run the trunk pipes, insulated and cloth-wrapped, bleeding white scale at every seam, each span humming its own faint note when the wind crosses it. The Long Gallery is the main artery: a covered corridor of pipe and walkway running eleven kilometers from the turbine pits to the White Fields, lit at intervals by sodium lamps, and you can walk it for forty minutes meeting no one, hearing only your boots and the drip.

Because that's the second thing about this place, after the heat. It is *quiet* — astonishingly, given what's here. The explanation is partly engineering: the turbine halls are sunk into pits so their vibration goes into bedrock instead of air; the pumps sit submerged, muffled by the water they move; valves are wrapped in cloth like kneeling figures in bandages. Partly physics: the saturated air eats sound, gives back nothing, so even a shout travels oddly, flattened. And partly habit. People here speak at the volume of a sickroom. After a week you do too.

But listen longer and the quiet turns out to be occupied. The mats feed a biomass that would embarrass a savanna. Drift-lines of snails lie along the flow channels like tidewrack, thousands to the meter, grazing the bacterial turf, and where the current freshens they stack on each other's shells three and four high to reach it. The channels themselves hold shoals of brine shrimp so dense the water turns the color of milk cut with blood; in the cooler runs quick silver pupfish thread through them. And above it all, on still days, rise the swarm pillars — mating columns of flies, each column millions strong, standing in the motionless air like heat-shimmer given a spine. From a distance they look like smoke that decided to behave. The sound of all this life exists, technically: a grain, a grit, a soft frying of millions of small mouths, audible only as the faintest texture under the hiss of the vents. Close up, individual ticks and clicks. At any distance, nothing. Snowfall makes more noise.

The predators are correspondingly unhurried and almost entirely mute. Pale stilt-birds sweep the shallows in lines, bills open, taking shrimp by the ounce, calling perhaps twice a day — single flat notes that carry the whole length of a terrace because nothing else competes. Rails sprint the hot margins on absurd feet. And the bats hunt at all hours, since for them every hour here is dusk; they work the swarm pillars in soft flapping passes, and the clap of their wings is one of the loudest recurring sounds in the valley.

The town has made its peace with the ground, and the ground with the town. Buildings stand on concrete piles, and the piles are slowly turning to stone — sinter grows up around them year by year, so a house's ground floor calcifies over a generation and the family simply moves up, sealing the old doors forever. Whole districts have migrated upward this way; you pass windows bricked in by mineral deposit, staircases that end at solid rock, a crane arm from some defunct works standing entombed to the elbow in white terracing, left where it died because no one could move it and no one minded. The town drinks its own air: condenser fins hang from every roofline, wringing fresh water from the vapor, dripping ceaselessly into cisterns — the weeping of the rocks reproduced in aluminum. The White Fields south of the Gallery are the industry's showpiece: hundreds of shallow evaporation ponds in strict geometry, steaming, changing color as the brine concentrates, scraped by slow dredges on schedules set by the ponds themselves. Bad-air hollows — carbon dioxide and worse pooling in the lows — are flagged with red cloth, and the men who work them still carry flame-lamps, because a flame gutters before a person does.

Time is kept by the vents. The Deacon, the great steady column near the center, plays on a schedule known to the minute, and its eruption is the shift whistle — when it stands up white against the lid of cloud, the day crew walks home along the causeways through drifting steam-shadow. Midday brings the Lift, when the ceiling thins slightly and the light strengthens from dim to merely muted; evening brings the Settle, when the lid descends and the dew falls so heavily it rains upward-off the wires. A few times a season the ceiling saturates completely and the valley gets true rain — warm, mineral, faintly bitter, laying fresh orange streaks down every wall.

At Settle, the lamps come on along the Gallery like a second horizon. The birds go wherever pale birds go; the bats take the pillars apart; the swarm columns dissolve into the general haze. The hush, already small, gets smaller. And then, right on schedule, the Deacon clears its throat — a rising rush, a white roar lasting ninety seconds, the loudest thing this valley ever says — and afterward, for a while, the only sound anywhere is the drip.
