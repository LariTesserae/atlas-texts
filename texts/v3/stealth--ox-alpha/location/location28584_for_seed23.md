# The Terraces

Come up the last pitch of switchback and the mountain sets you down gently, the way a bowl sets down water. The climb stops being a climb. You stand on a shelf — a terrace of pale stone no more than half a morning's walk across, hung on the mountain's shoulder like a tray — and above you the cliffs go on up into glacier country, and below you, at your feet, the world simply ends: a drop into a sea of fog that boils softly against the rock, rises and retreats, rises and retreats, breathing. Between ice above and cloud below lies all there is. The whole place fits in the eye at once. That is the first thing you understand about it: it is a room.

The second thing takes longer, because you have to stop talking to notice it.

---

**The stone and the water.** The terrace floor is travertine — spring-stone — laid down over ages by water that seeps from the cliff base in a hundred places and spreads across the shelf in shallow steps, each step rimmed with fresh white mineral. The stone is old beyond its size; it has the worn, settled look of a river bottom that was somehow lifted into the sky. Rounded basalt shoulders break through the chalk here and there, black and slick. Iron seeps stain rust-red fans. Where a vent comes up faintly warm, the runnels wear mats of green so dark they look like cut velvet. Vegetation, such as it is, lives only in these ribbons — mosses threaded along the warm channels, dwarf willow flattened to a mat by centuries of wind, orange lichen crusting the dry hummocks. Green here is not a ground cover. It is a vein-work, a wiring diagram of where the water goes.

And the water goes everywhere. The ground is sodden past argument. Pools sit in every depression, clear as glass and cold as knives; runnels stitch the whole terrace together; the air itself is saturated, so that by late afternoon every stone sweats and the beads run and gather and join the general circulation. Nothing ever dries here. Rain is unnecessary; the fog does the raining, condensing on anything colder than itself, which is everything.

Some of the springs are not quite cold. Only a few — five, six vents where the mountain's plumbing brings up water with a faint sulfur sweetness — but they matter out of all proportion to their warmth. Around these open pools the entire ecology crowds. The rest of the terrace freezes nightly; the vents never close; therefore every living thing arranges itself, in concentric desperation, around those few dark steaming eyes. Density is not abundance here. Density is compression. All the life of a wide country squeezed onto a shelf because the shelf is where the liquid is.

**The light.** At this height the air is thin and scrupulously clean, and the sun is less a warmth than a fact. It comes down fierce off the white stone and the ice fields above, doubled and redoubled by reflection, so that noon is nearly unbearable to look at and the shadows are the darkest things on the terrace — blue-black slots of absolute shade where frost survives the day. The place runs on the border between that sun and that frost. Walk a line of shadow's edge at midday and you can put one hand on stone warm as skin and dip the other, an arm's length away, into slush. As the clouds cross, the line moves. Channels that ran all morning stiffen and seal; ice skins snap across pools in seconds; twenty minutes later the sun releases everything and the terrace begins dripping, ticking, exhaling. The freeze-thaw is not an event here. It is the respiration of the place, hundreds of times a day, in miniature.

**The silence.** Now the second thing. The ground drinks sound.

No one knows why — the folk say the water table takes it, that the whole terrace is a membrane stretched over the mountain's throat and every vibration poured onto it pours through — but the effect is absolute. A voice carries an arm's length, perhaps two. Beyond that, words arrive as gesture alone. Clap your hands ten paces off and it is a picture of a clap. You will stand and watch an avalanche calve off the upper cliff — a mass of blue ice the size of a house letting go, falling, detonating into the fog — and hear nothing whatsoever. A thousand wings lift off the nesting ledges and the air fills with a silence so total it has texture.

Only nearby things have sound, and only small ones: the tick of freezing water beside your ear, your own pulse, the squelch and crunch of your feet, the crack of ice forming close enough to touch. All sound here is intimate sound. The world is enormous to the eye and the size of your two hands to the ear.

The people who live here have built everything around this law. Outside, among the terraces and channels, no one speaks — there is no point — and so the town converses in hand-talk, fluent and rapid, and in ripple: a flick of the fingers on the surface of a shared pool sends a coded pattern to the far side. Indoors, in thick dry-stone rooms, voices work again. Voice is a household possession. Song happens behind walls. A traveler arriving sees a town in constant animated discussion and hears — nothing. An empty-looking room full of festival. The name of the town cannot be told to you aloud at all; it is a shape of the hands, a cupping and a folding, and it exists in no other form.

**The town.** And it is a town — a true settlement, several hundred souls, though you'd sooner call it an organism than a village. Low round houses of drystone and marl plaster crowd the driest spine of the terrace, packed nearly touching, roofed with turf and stone slab and shored against the annual violence of ice-heave, which buckles walls every winter and is repaired every spring in the same way, with the same stones, as it has been for — no one counts. The doorsills rise a finger's width each generation, because the ground itself is growing: the springs lay down fresh white stone over everything that holds still, and the town sits on its own accumulating past. Rooms become cellars become foundations become simply stone. Here and there in quarry cuts you see the strata of it — the ghostly outline of an old wall entombed inside newer stone, a buried doorway, the shadow of a beam.

Wood is scarcer than metal, which is to say there is no metal. Antler wedges, bone awls, stone hammers; cordage of sinew and braided hair; felt pressed from bird-down. Every plank and lintel was carried up from the forests below and has been inherited, patched, and re-inherited beyond memory — wood here is heirloom, passed with names. Windows are not glass but ice, cut each morning from the clear spring-frozen pools and set into the walls, glowing blue-green all day, melting free at dusk. Every day the town builds its windows out of the night.

The work never stops, and it is all maintenance: cutting the window-ice, reopening the channels that froze shut, scraping new travertine off the sluice-boards before it seals them (the scrapings become plaster, whitewash, mortar — the town whitewashes itself with its own ground), probing the walking-paths with long bone rods because the wet ground hides voids where a crust may be standing over nothing. Watch how they walk: rod forward, tap, read the tremble through the wood and bone by palm — since air refuses to carry sound but solid things tell the truth of it. They listen through sticks. Everyone here walks listening.

**The animals.** The fauna crowds in the same compression as everything else. Over the warm vents rise the glintflies — midge-swarms in towering bright columns, glittering motes in the hard sun, and they are the base of everything: the fish in the channels rise to them, the birds take them, the people render their fat into tallow for the little lamps that warm the smoky interiors. Shoals of pale spring-fish turn in the clear pools like one thought passing through many bodies. White birds nest in the cliff crevices in shrieking — visually shrieking — thousands; up close they make a small dry clicking, and at any distance they are mute as snowfall.

In the warm mud around the vents wallow the mire-oxen: broad, slow, mud-caked beasts that widen springs with their rooting and are, themselves, becoming stone. The travertine grows on them as on everything — a calf's coat picks up white scale by its second year, an elder wears armor plates of living rock — and when a mire-ox dies it tends to die standing, and stays, and the springs finish the work. Along the terrace's quieter edges stand the ancestors: ox-shaped monuments of white stone, decades thick, grazed smooth. Herds pass them and rub against them. No one quarries them.

And in the pools live the gossips — palm-sized water-shrews that converse entirely in surface ripples, dialect differing pool to pool. Children keep them in wooden troughs. An old gossip on a doorstep will answer a child's fingertip, after a fashion, and both parties seem satisfied.

**The laws.** Four things are true here, and the folk gesture them as proverbs:

*What stands still becomes stone.* Tools left out are sheathed by spring. A dropped button is a fossil by autumn. The terraces keep everything pressed into them — and here is the soft strangeness under all the rest: fresh marl takes an impression and holds it as the stone hardens. Handprints, bootprints, a pressed leaf, the outline of a lost knife, the spread fingers of someone bracing a load two hundred years ago — the ground is an archive you walk on. Sometimes, deliberately, a person kneels at the terrace edge and presses their hand into tonight's fresh rim, joining the record.

*Sound belongs to the ground.* Spend it indoors, or spend it on the water, or keep it.

*Distance is mute, but the fog borrows.* Rarely — on still evenings — voices rise out of the cloud-sea below. Muffled, directionless, fragments of lowland speech or sheep-call or cart-wheel, delivered upward to the terrace like muttering from under the world. The folk do not explain it. They pause, listen with their faces, and resume.

*Nothing is finished.* The mountain melts and freezes hourly; the town rebuilds daily; the stone grows; the fog breathes. Yet nothing changes shape. This is the deepest law and the hardest to say: the terrace is not calm despite its churn, nor churn despite its calm. The turbulence is the settled state. The riverbed does not move; the river never rests.

**Evening.** Come dusk the fog-tide rises, swallowing the lower steps, and the cold wins its daily argument. Ice seals the channels with a last ticking chorus — the loudest hour of the day, all of it within reach of your hand. The town goes indoors, and then, and only then, you begin to hear it: low voices, a sung phrase passed between houses like a coal, a laugh behind a foot of stone. Through the day's new ice-windows the tallow lamps glow, each pane a slightly different blue. Above the roofs the stars come out hard and close. Somewhere an old ox stands turning slowly to chalk under them, still chewing. And below it all, faint, borrowed, rising out of the breathing white: someone else's evening, someone else's dogs, the lowlands murmuring up through the floor of the sky — while the whole bright room holds still around its work, and listens with its hands.
