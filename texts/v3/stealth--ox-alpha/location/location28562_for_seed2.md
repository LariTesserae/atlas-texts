# The Cistern

*Basin One, Primary Ocean Volume*

## The medium

The first thing to understand is that there is no surface. Anywhere.

Whatever instincts you carry from other seas fail here, because those seas are defined by their skin — the bright trembling line where water gives up and becomes air. This world has no such line. It is water all the way up and all the way down, a lens seven kilometers thick pressed between the machinery floor below and the lamp-decks above. When you look up — really look, tipping your head back until your hood fills — you see what the crews call the sky, and the sky is the far ceiling: six kilometers of flooded distance, and behind that distance, the Array, thousands of great lamps laid out in continents of light, diffused by the water into one soft, sourceless glare. It reads exactly like a bright overcast noon. There are no shadows worth having. Light arrives from everywhere at once, gently, like weather.

Visibility is sixty meters on a good day. Beyond that, things assemble themselves out of the haze — first a dimming of the grey-blue, then a shape, then detail, then finally sound, which got there minutes ago and waited for you.

## The scale of the emptiness

The basin runs four thousand kilometers from wall to wall. You could ride a tender at full burn for eleven days and count the things you passed on your fingers. This is not neglect; it is design. The builders computed their volumes and then deliberately left more than nine-tenths of it vacant — open water as structural material, ballast against the pressures of circulation and heat, room reserved for a future no one has specified yet. The emptiness here is not the absence of the built world. It is part of the built world, poured as carefully as the concrete.

So the landscape is mostly *interval*. A superstructure appears, hosts its ecology and its crew, drops away astern, and then there is nothing again for a hundred kilometers — nothing but the long swell of the Loop current, the tick of distant transformers, and your own exhaust beads climbing toward a sky that never gets closer.

## Sharp

Everything here is young, and everything shows it.

This sea was finished thirty-one years ago — pumped full, seeded, commissioned — and the water has not yet had time to argue with what was built into it. There is no rust lace. No silt burial, no softening of edges, no crust of settlement turning girders into geology. The concrete still bears the crisp mirror-image of its formwork boards. Weld beads stand proud and unbitten. Stencil codes remain perfectly legible on pipe runs that have already carried a billion liters: THERMAL EXCHANGER 44-B. DEPTH −2,140 M. FRAME SECTOR 9 — NO MOORING.

The oldest corrosion in the entire basin is a scatter of orange freckles on a valve housing in Sector 3, and it is tracked in a maintenance ledger like a medical record. When the first barnacles colonized a girder off Forestry Station Two, the crews photographed them the way other worlds photograph endangered birds. A scratch in a hull plate is a reportable event. You can cut your glove on the century, here. The edges have not been lived in yet.

## Warmth runs uphill

The bulk water runs about six degrees Celsius — a cold that ignores your suit's ratings and goes looking for wrists, for the thin skin at the throat. But the engine districts exhale. Waste heat from the pump galleries and the hull's thermal economy bleeds into the sea in fat warm plumes, thirteen degrees and rising, visible from a distance as shimmering refraction columns — walls where the light bends and the view wobbles like a mirage.

Because warm water climbs, the climate of this world runs inverted: the tropics are at the sky. The plumes rise kilometer upon kilometer and spread out in broad warm sheets beneath the lamp-decks, and that is where the life wants to be — cruising the warm ceiling-country, then diving back down through the thermocline into the cold to feed, and up again, and down, in slow vertical migrations that the charts mark as faithfully as shipping lanes.

Once a year the operators run the Stirring. Every impeller on the floor takes full load for six weeks, the Loop doubles its speed, construction silt smears the visibility down to meters, and the whole sea goes turbid and roaring and brown-green — the only storm season this world has, scheduled, charted, and announced in advance by bulletin.

## The sound

You never hear silence here. The quietest coordinate in four thousand kilometers still holds the hum: the deep pedal-note of the great impellers, felt in the sternum before the ears, swelling on a slow twelve-second breath as the pump halls phase in and out of balance.

Layered onto that: the tick of the Array's transformers, arriving faint and delayed from six kilometers up. The groan of the hull itself, flexing through each dusk-cycle as the lamps power down and the shell contracts — the whole world takes one long breath at every mechanical evening, and you feel it pass through your chest like a train through a station. Sonar pings exchanged with elaborate courtesy, short and polite, answered promptly, because everyone here learned etiquette from the same manual. The crackle of arc-welders from whatever construction front is active this decade, flickering blue in the distance like sheet lightning and tapping across the water in ghostly delayed bursts. And underneath everything, crossing four hundred kilometers of quiet cold water to arrive as pure vibration in your ribs: the songs.

## Architecture is reef

There is no natural substrate anywhere in this world — no rock, no seabed, no coast. Every solid thing was cast, welded, or assembled. Which means that for anything that needs a foothold, the architecture *is* the ecosystem. Life concentrates ferociously at built things: anemones crowding a valve bonnet, cod-analogues schooling in the lee of a cooling fin, octopuses denning in junction housings with an eye to the maintenance schedule, kelp — sparse, deliberate, planted in designated forestry frames only twenty-two years old and still patchy as spring ice — rattling softly in the current like rigging.

Between the structures, the open water belongs to the drifters. Moon-jellies by the million, pale and barely present, occupying the emptiness the way dust occupies a cathedral — proof that the vacancy is inhabited after all. Shoals pouring along the bloom roads downstream of the upwelling stations, where the intake galleries pull cold nutrient water off the floor and the food web gathers in shifting crowds, appearing and dissolving on the currents' timetable.
