**The Conservatory at the Seam**

You reach it through a crack in the roof of the world.

The mountain is one of those summits that stands above weather — above cloud, above almost above air — its upper faces scoured to a matte gray smoothness by winds that gave up changing millennia ago. Near the top, where the rock folds into a shoulder, there is a fissure. It looks like nothing. It looks like the mountain shrugged once, badly, and left the wrinkle. But the fissure goes back, and down, and then up, and after two hundred meters of squeezing through stone polished slick by ten thousand years of the same exhalations, the passage opens into warmth of a kind — dry, amber, humming — and you are inside the garden.

Understand first what is *not* here. There is no water. Not vapor, not dew, not one molecule of it anywhere in this chamber. The air carries no humidity; nothing here has ever rusted from the inside of a raindrop, and thirst, if you brought yours in, would go unanswered. The cold is the clean dry cold of a vacuum-adjacent place, a cold with nothing in it to freeze. And yet —

And yet the garden is lush. Lush the way a jewelry box is lush. Lush past bearing, in fact: every surface of the chamber is grown over, hung with, upholstered in vegetation that never knew water and never wanted it. These are mineral things. Trees whose trunks are braided basalt weeping beads of true amber — the beads hang in strings along the undersides of limbs like lanterns, each one hardened centuries ago and each one exactly as shiny now as the day it set. Fronds of actual glass unfurl from the trunks in fern-curves, transparent, faintly green-tinted at the nodes, ringing softly against one another whenever the ventilation draft passes — which it does, always, on schedule. Shrubs of layered mica open rosettes the size of dinner plates, their petals thin enough to read firelight through. Lawns of copper-colored moss — filaments finer than hair, metallic, faintly warm to the touch — carpet the terraces between the beds and creep up the chamber walls in velvet continents. From the ceiling hangs an entire inverted forest, roots gripping stone, glass fronds dangling down like the pipes of a broken organ.

The chamber itself is not large. That is the second shock, after the lushness: this riot of growth is contained in a pocket, a vaulted room perhaps forty paces long, wedged into the mountain's heart near its crown. Everything is packed. Density here is not abundance; it is *pressure*. The flora grows against itself, into itself, leaf pressed to leaf pressed to trunk, until there is no space that is not either plant or machine. A single iron catwalk crosses the room on stanchions driven into the terrace walls — the only path, the only gap in the green — and walking it means brushing both walls of foliage at once, glass chiming at your elbows, mica rasping your sleeves, the amber beads swinging in your wake and then going still, instantly, because stillness is the default here.

The light is dim and it comes from three places. There is the furnace-grate: a low amber eye glowing behind slats in the engine-house at the chamber's far end, throwing long warm bars across the nearest terraces and leaving the rest in brown shadow. There are the amber beads themselves, which catch and pass along whatever light exists until the whole garden seems lit from within by thousands of tiny preserved sunsets. And there is one window — a mere arrow-slit cut through three meters of living rock — through which enters a pale, thin, high-altitude light, almost violet with thinness. Look through that slit and the meaning of the place's situation arrives all at once: the clouds are *below* you. Whole ranges stand beneath your eyeline, diminished, hazy, ordinary. Whatever the builders wanted to protect, they protected it by putting it where nothing climbs.

The machinery occupies the near end and threads through everything else, and it is the machinery that explains the paradox at the center of this place: a garden that is lush, and finished, and never changes.

There is an engine — modest, brick-and-brass, about the size of a chapel — and its flywheel turns. It has always been turning. Brass governor-balls spin above it in a fixed halo, holding the tempo, and the tempo never drifts, because there is nothing left in this system to drift. Belts run from the flywheel to overhead shafts, and the shafts drive fans, and the fans push the great slow tidal draft that keeps the glass fronds chiming. Gantry rails cross the terraces, and on the rails ride shear-frames with rows of blades, and here is the heart of it: the shears trim the garden. Constantly. Every frond that extends is met by an edge; every reaching bough is cut at precisely the length it reached yesterday and will reach tomorrow. The clippings fall to conveyor pans, the conveyors carry them to the digester — the furnace behind the grate, which melts spoil and trimmings alike into mineral fume — and the fume rises through ducts into the beds, where the roots drink it. That is the whole ecology. No rain falls because none is needed. Nothing is born and nothing dies; the machines have taken over the office that death normally holds in an ecosystem — breakdown, return, redistribution — and they perform it tirelessly and identically, so the garden grows and is eaten and grows and is eaten at exactly the same rate, forever, like a flame burning in a perfectly balanced draft.

Along the far wall, coils of pipe wear a white fur — ammonia frost, dry-rimed, sublimating and redepositing in the same crystals in the same places, cooling the chamber by bleeding its warmth outward through the mountain into the thin killing cold of the sky. The garden likes it cool. It has always liked it cool.

The sound is enormous once you stop resisting it. Flywheel-whir, piston-chuff, belt-slap, the fans' long inhale and exhale, the digester's gut-murmur, the shear-gantries' traveling *snick*, and over it all the glass canopy ringing like a harbor of bells — and every element locked to every other, so that the total noise is not a noise but a *composition*, several minutes long, which repeats. Exactly. You can learn it. You can wait for your favorite bar. Nothing interrupts it, because nothing in this room has happened in a very long time except this room.

Even the stone agrees. The chamber walls have eroded — of course they have, given eons — but the erosion is *finished*. Those same tidal drafts have fluted the rock into grooves aligned with the airflow, worn the edges round, polished the floors of the old channels, and then stopped, because there is no longer any irregularity left for the wind to work on. The mountain inside this room has reached its final shape. It is a sculpture now, not a process.

There is no animal life and there never was any here — no insects in the ducts, no birds in the slit-window, no rats, no worms to turn the soil, because the soil is turned by augers. Whatever small lives might have ridden in on a visitor's boot would find nothing to eat and nothing to breathe but mineral fume, and, the locals say, would be incorporated anyway: this is the last rule of the place, and the strangest. The equilibrium is *greedy*. Anything that enters and stays becomes part of the loop. Your warmth registers on the coils and is compensated; your footprints in the copper moss hold their shape because nothing collapses and nothing grows over them at the wrong rate. You would be preserved not like a fossil — fossils change — but like a feature. Permanent the way the amber beads are permanent.

Which makes the scattered human traces, at the room's margins, almost unbearable. They are few, and small, and off in the shadows where the garden thins against bare rock: a pegboard with the painted silhouettes of tools that were removed decades or centuries ago and never came back. A ladder bolted to the wall ascending to a hatch that opens on solid stone. Initials scratched into a beam and filled with wax. And on a shelf beside the engine, a clay mug, tipped on its side, that an amber bead dripped onto sometime in the deep past and slowly, slowly swallowed — so that the mug is still there, half-emerged from a golden lump, exactly as it was on the afternoon someone set it down and walked into the loop and did not come out of it.

Perhaps they meant it as an argument: that a finished thing need not end. That a garden could be brought to completeness and then simply *kept*, the way a chord is kept by a hand on the keys. If so, the argument is still being made. It will go on being made, at the same volume, in the same key, while the ranges beneath the slit-window rise and fall and the clouds underneath them spend themselves and renew. The flywheel turns. The shears trim. The glass answers the fans in bells. Nothing changes, and so nothing is ever lost.
