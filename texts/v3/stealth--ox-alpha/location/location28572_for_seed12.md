There is no shore.

That is the first thing to understand about the Spires, and the last thing anyone believes until they see it. The water does not lap against anything. It has no edge to lap. It simply *is* — a sea sitting at the top of the world, held in the cup of a young mountain ring, and the only dry land in the entire region is nine summits, a scatter of ridgebacks, and whatever rooftops the people have raised above the line. You arrive by ferry across open glass. The boat leaves a wake like a scar on silk and the silk heals behind it, because the Slow does not keep wounds. Nothing here does.

**The water itself**

Cup it in your hands and drink: that's what the ferrymen do, idly, leaning on their oars. The sea is fresh. Warm — blood-warm in the shallows, never colder than a cellar even fathoms down — and faintly sweet, tasting of stone and green things. It is the mountain's own water. The range beneath is so young it has not finished being born; its rock still exhales, springs threading everywhere through the raw stone, and the sea is the mountain's breath, gathered and held. The people know this the way farmers know soil. Heat comes up out of the ground. Light comes down out of the sky. Neither is strong, and everything that lives here lives in the middle, between the two gifts.

Because light *is* weak. Above the sea hangs a lid of pearl-grey cloud, low and close, so low that from the summit towns it seems you could reach up and pole it like a raft. It never breaks. Day under the lid is a long soft dusk, shadowless, directionless; the horizon is not a line but a gradual dimming where the sea's grey meets the lid's grey. Once or twice a season the lid thins in a single spot and parts — and the stars come out shockingly hard and close, altitude-stars, cold and enormous — and then the seam knits shut again as if embarrassed. The folk treat these partings as omens of an ordinary kind, noting them on the tally beside the rise of the water.

And the water rises. That is the second thing.

**The Rising**

It comes up about the width of a hand each year. Not a flood — nothing so dramatic has ever happened here. No waves have ever been recorded on the Slow; no storm crosses the lid; the finest rain falls from it in curtains so slow the drops take the better part of an hour to descend, arriving without wrinkling the surface. The sea only climbs. Patiently. The way a sleeper's breath climbs a blanket.

Every hundred years or so, it rests. The Gauge-keepers — the order who maintain the Decade Stairs on the tallest spire, a staircase cut straight down the rockface into the water, one step per decade, each step notched with the year's exact line — have records of rests lasting a single season and rests lasting most of a human life. During a rest the waterline holds just below some threshold, sometimes just below a doorway, a shrine step, the lip of a cistern, and waits there like a guest deciding whether to enter. These are the celebrated years. Children born during a rest are said to be "stood-upon-by-the-sea" and get an extra name.

But the water always resumes. Everyone builds accordingly.

**Down**

Put your face in the water anywhere and you see the country that was.

This is the third thing, the one that unsettles visitors most: the drowning left everything *intact*. Because the sea moves so slowly and touches so gently — no current, no surf, no sediment, no abrasion — the valleys it has swallowed over the past eight centuries still look like valleys. Hedgerows run along the bay floors in dark green lines. Field boundaries persist; you can swim the length of an old lane between its fences. Orchard trees stand in rows down the slopes, their fruit ripening underwater, pale and sweet and heavier than water, and the Root-tenders harvest them on the long dive with nets. Doorways stand open. The oldest town of all lies forty fathoms down in the deep basin between the Twins — stone footings, stone hearths, stone lintels — and it is as crisp as the day it was abandoned. The water took nothing. The water only ever adds.

Light falls away through it in soft steps. The upper fathoms are a green gloaming where everything can be seen clearly for a long way — the dimness here belongs to the sky, not to murk. Below that the color drains through olive into a brown-violet hush, and below *that* is the deep country, the first valleys drowned, where the only lights are the few the divers carry: clay lamps burning pressed fungus-oil, or cages of glowmoss, which cast a pool of amber around a swimmer like a private room moving through a dark house.

Voices carry down here. That is the sound-rule of the Spires, and it reverses everything a visitor expects: in the air, hush — sound dies within arm's reach, swallowed by the lid and the stillness — but underwater a voice travels astonishingly far, sliding along the warm strata. Divers converse across whole bays. The towns speak to each other by knock-code on hulls and on the great submerged trunks, and at dawn, if you lie with your ear in the water, you can hear the whole sea murmuring: greetings, tallies, the thermal vents singing their one long low note.

**The race**

Everything alive here is running the same race against the same opponent, and the opponent is patient.

The trees are winning, so far. The great climb-trees that crown the spires and the ridges began as ordinary forest eight hundred years ago; now each one is a tower whose lower two-thirds is submarine, its drowned trunk transformed by the warm water into something like whale-hide over sea-cork — timber left in the Slow slowly turns corky and mineral, neither rotting nor petrifying, just *changing* — and only the topmost crown still properly lives, a burst of huge dim-adapted leaves spread flat to catch what little the lid allows. The trees grew to keep pace with the water, generation by generation, a race run in centuries. On very quiet nights, with the air-hush absolute, you can hear them doing it: a faint fibrous creak from the crowns, the sound of wood adding itself to the world.

Between the trunks hang root-curtains and vine-bellropes, descending from the floating gardens on the surface film down into the dark — the density of the place is vertical, layer upon layer: surface rafts of reed and flowering water-herb; the mid-water orchards and meadowlands of ribbon-grass that still follow the outlines of the old hayfields; the deep lanes and cellars; and threaded through it all, the towns.

**The towns**

The folk build the way the water rises: slowly, additively, upward. A house here is not a building but a *stack*. The ground floor of any dwelling was somebody's ground floor two hundred years ago, and somebody's before that.
