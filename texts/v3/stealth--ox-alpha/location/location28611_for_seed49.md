## First Fact

However you arrive, the fact arrives with you: **there is no outside.** The built plain runs to every horizon without a gap — no frontier of forest or desert to measure it against, because there is nothing here to be *not-city*. The sprawl crosses what would be seas on other worlds, fills valleys that were never carved, covers low archipelagos so completely that the islands read only as slight thickenings in the pattern. It is continental the way weather is continental. Somewhere far off a storm is happening, and the storm is happening *to the city*, and the city does not mind.

And the city is low. Almost nothing stands tall. The mass of it hovers a few meters above the warm shallow water that threads it — not beside the water, not above it, but *through* it. Streets are canals at high tide and glistening flats at low. Boardwalks stitch between rooftops that serve as plazas. Sluice-gates stand open at the corners of blocks, breathing with the tide. Put your hand flat on any wall and you will find it damp, faintly warm, and very slightly alive.

The air is the air of a mouth. Blood-warm, thick, tasting of iodine, hot stone, and something faintly sweet — the smell of growing film. Humidity is total; sweat stops working here; you wear the climate. And everything drips. Locals call it the Weep, if there are locals: the ceaseless condensation running down ten million ledges, ticking off stone, pooling in the joints, feeding the roots of the world.

## Stone

There is no green anywhere. Look for it and you will find its substitutes instead.

The city is built in strata, like a reef or a tree. The deepest exposed courses are black and vitrified, seamless, older than the oldest song sung about them. Above those, ages of brick and poured ceramic streaked with oxides — rust red, verdigris, manganese black — because in a world without leaves, color belongs to chemistry. The newest work is pale composite that heals: scratch it and the wound closes overnight with a seam like scar tissue.

Between the stones, the mortar is a crop. It was seeded, someone believes, with mineral-fixing microbes unimaginably long ago, and it has never stopped working. Walls grow a millimeter a decade. Buildings gain weight across the centuries. Edges everywhere are worn round and polished — drip-fluted under every leaking cornice, hand-smooth by nothing that ever had a hand — and yet nothing collapses, because decay and repair run neck and neck and always have. That balance is the local style. Ruin, here, is a phase, not an outcome.

Every surface is occupied. Every hollow is tenanted. Every damp face carries its fur of life. Emptiness is the only resource this place does not produce.

## The Film Prairies

The grass of this world is a skin.

Where sunlight reaches the shallows and the damp faces of walls, microbial mats grow in velvet nap: turquoise where the water carries copper, rust where it carries iron, violet in the shade lines, gold at the waterline where the film dries to a crisp lacquer each afternoon. It grows fast and is grazed faster. Stand still in a quiet cul-de-sac and you can hear it happen — thousands of small rasping mouths, a sound like frying, like far-off rain on foliage in some other world that has foliage.

Snail-things and limpet-things tile the walls in shifting mosaics, each leaving a clean stripe through the color. Insects without number hatch from the standing pools — at dusk they lift off in fog-columns that look like smoke deciding to be alive. The water itself is crowded: larvae, grazers, hunters, the whole packed economy of a warm sea with no room left and no need for any.

And threading through all of it, the machines.

## Tenants

Follow any conduit seam — the humming joints where the city's buried power leaks a little into the air — and you will find the lamp-moths: pale, wide-winged things that hover at the seams and drink the field directly, their abdomens glowing faintly after a meal, like lamps warming up. They cannot digest the film, and the film cannot spread without them; their bodies carry spores and mineral microbes from wall to wall. They are this world's pollinators. Its flowers are power lines.

Above them work the flocks: swift-analogues in their tens of thousands, nesting in niches and statuary and the mouths of ventilation flues, riding the updraft off the warm machinery stacks. At dusk the sky over the towers braids itself into murmurations, and the flocks pour down into the flues to roost in the warm dark, filling the city's lungs with feathers.

On the ground, the sanitation. Tile-crabs in numbers you stop counting at four figures patrol the gutters and sluices, clearing debris, dredging blockages, sealing their doorways with their own bodies before rain. Anything that dies is disassembled within hours — beetles first, then crabs, then the pale bone-worms that mine skeletons for mineral and leave the calcium where the mortar can reach it. Nothing is wasted, because wasting is structurally impossible here. Every output is somebody's input. Leave an object in the street and within three days it will be dismantled, inhabited, or grown through.

Out in the flooded avenues, the big browsers wade: herd beasts with backs crusted over generations into genuine reefs, carrying whole ecosystems on their shoulders as they mow the mats off the canal beds. In the cisterns, eels long enough to be geography. In the shade, where sun never dries the stone, forests of fungus glow softly all night — the closest thing this world has to leaves, and it kept them luminous instead of green.
