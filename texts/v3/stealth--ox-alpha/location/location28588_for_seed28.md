# The Cistern

You reach it by walking away from everything. Out in the white desert there is a hill with a folded shoulder, and in the fold a crack barely wide enough for a loaded back, and if you go in sideways and keep going down, the daylight narrows to a wire, then a thread, then lets you go entirely — and after forty steps of blind descent the dark opens its throat, and you fall into the loudest green room in the world.

The Cistern is a single chamber. That should be said first, because everything else about it follows from that: one vaulted hall of ancient brickwork sunk into the hill's base, low in the land, lowest thing for a hundred miles, walkable corner to corner in about an hour if you walked it straight, which nobody ever does. It was dug and lined by hands that had no machine harder than a chisel — masons of some forgotten upstream city who needed a place to store a lake. They got their lake. They kept it, filled and drawn, filled and drawn, for some hundreds of years, and then the aqueduct broke somewhere in the hills and was never mended, and the water left.

That was long ago. The room has had time to become entirely what it is.

## The light

Nine shafts pierce the vault, cut deliberately by the old engineers, and through them the sun enters not as illumination but as *columns* — nine solid-looking pillars of amber standing on the dusty floor, leaning slowly east to west across the day like the hands of a clock the size of a season. At noon they all stand close together near the center and the heat under them is oven-true; you can bake clay in the noon column, and the townspeople do.

But the light doesn't stop where the shafts end. The old plaster was mixed with crushed glassy sand, and centuries of vibration — remember this, the vibration matters — have polished it to a soft sheen, so the walls themselves glow, low and honey-colored, and there are no true shadows anywhere in the hall, only deeper and shallower ambers. Even midnight here is a dim gold. The place is bright the way the inside of a lantern is bright.

## The sound

And the vibration matters because sound is doing the work that water does everywhere else.

When the wind moves over the hill, it crosses the nine shaft mouths the way breath crosses the neck of a bottle, and the whole chamber sounds. The vault has a fundamental — a deep, broad note you feel in the sternum before you hear it, the hall breathing itself — and laid over it, the fields. Because the Cistern is lush. That is the second impossible thing, held easily: a bone-dry room, dust to the ankles in the open flats, not a drop of water since before anyone's grandmother's grandmother — and growing out of it, thick as any riverbank, the reeds.

They are hollow, jointed, taller than a person in the deep fields, pale gold-green, and they drink nothing. They drink *tone*. Their roots are felt-mats that spread through the settled dust and feed on vibration the way other roots feed on rain; where the standing wave is strongest the growth stands twice a man's height and gleams with wax; in the dead corners behind old buttresses, where the chord cancels, the reeds thin out to gray wisps and finally stop altogether. The map of the hall's music and the map of its greenery are the same map.

There are lawns of it — low velvet felt where the note lies soft — and shrubs of waxleaf that secrete translucent panes instead of petals, chiming faintly against each other in the swell. The people press oil from the hearts of the fattest reeds, and that oil is the only wet thing in the world down here: they drink it, they burn it, they grease their door hinges with it, they seal their dead in it. Nobody in the Cistern drinks water, and hasn't for generations; their skins have gone faintly waxy, their tears come rarely and arrive oily, and a cup of actual water would sicken them now. The room remade them quietly, the way rooms do.

## The town

Scattered across the floor — scattered, because density never took hold here — are perhaps ninety households. They are mudbrick, hand-molded, bronze-chisel-trimmed, most of them low round houses with a smoke-slot and a door facing away from the prevailing drone. From the rim of the chamber they look like a constellation fallen onto a dance floor: a house here, sixty paces of empty shimmering dust, another house, another long emptiness, the noon light-columns walking between them. Nothing crowds in the Cistern. Even the town keeps its distance from itself.

The buildings sit on and among older works — the drowned era's architecture still ribs the floor: the mouths of drain-tunnels now bricked shut, a grand stair descending to a valve-house whose wheel has fused into the wall, and everywhere the dripstones. Stalactites hang in frozen clusters from the vault in the older galleries, bleached bone-white, dry as chalk — the stone's memory of water, carved when the room was a sea and now only ornament. The people call them *the Old Rain* and consider it unlucky to break one, which is easy, since breaking one rings for four minutes.

Because everything here rings. That is the condition of life: the hall is deafening. Always. The fundamental plus nine shaft-tones plus ninety household drones plus the reeds answering — the air of the Cistern is a solid, a substance you move through, thick enough to lean on. Conversation happens mouth-to-ear with a palm cupped behind it. And therefore every household maintains a *hush-niche* — a small pocket, usually behind the waterless hearth, shaped just so, where the standing wave cancels and a family can sit in actual silence. Silence is the scarce thing here, hoarded like springwater anywhere else. Lovers court in hush-niches. Oaths are sworn in them. To invite someone into your hush is to tell them you love them, or that someone has died.

The dead, in fact, leave by silence: each household keeps one great reed, its own drone, planted by the door, tuned by its owner over a lifetime. At death the reed is cut, and for a year the house stands mute amid the roar, and everyone passing knows.

## The living things

Fauna is thin, as everything mobile is thin here. There are dustwick moths, palm-sized and pale, that hover at the mouths of the loudest reeds drinking amplitude off the air; at dusk the deep fields haze with them, ten thousand wings adding a shimmer of high overtones to the chord. There are blind ivory beetles grazing the root-felt in slow herds. There are the sun-lizards, flat and copper-colored, that spend their days migrating between the light-columns as the columns migrate, never touching shadow, little pilgrims of the noon. And there are people. That is roughly the whole census. Nothing hunts anything; predation never found the entrance crack.

## The years

The Cistern is active the way an instrument is active — everything in motion, nothing changing. Days swing their nine pillars of sun. Dusk reverses the draft in the shafts and the hall's chord flips upside down, evening-note for morning-note, and the whole town adjusts its cooking-smoke and its errands to the turn. Seasons come through as pitch: the wet-season winds outside (wet up there, in the world that still has water) blow the shafts hard, and the reeds grow riotous and fat and the oil-presses run day and night — those are *loud years*, fat years. Some autumn the high pressure sits on the hills and the wind dies, and the fundamental drops to a threadbare murmur, and the outer fields yellow and shrink, and the town tightens its belts and burns its wax reserves: a *thin year*, a drought of sound. The elders can taste a coming winter in the morning tone the way farmers elsewhere chew the soil.

Nothing erodes here by water anymore, but the sound does its patient masonry: stair edges worn into flutes, brick corners rounded soft, the great valve-wheel's spokes slimmed to rods by three centuries of humming against them. Yet the room has reached its balance — dust blows in through the shafts at exactly the rate the old fabric crumbles, the felt-roots stitch the dunes fast, the town repairs exactly what fails. The Cistern is finished, and contentedly so, a held chord that decided to be a place.

At night, if the sky above the shafts is clear, the moon sometimes crosses one of the openings and lays a single silver pillar down among the sleeping gold-green fields, and the reeds nearest it — the ones who know the difference — go briefly, sweetly quiet.

Nobody outside the hill knows the room exists. That seems right to the people in it. A chord this size should belong to no one.
