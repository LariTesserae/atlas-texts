# The Underbuild

You come down by train, because everything here comes and goes by train. The descent takes forty minutes through a spiral of switchbacks cut—no, *cast*—through material that stopped pretending to be rock a long time ago. The tunnel walls are lined plate and shotcrete over shotcrete, seam over seam, and somewhere past the third kilometer you realize you are not passing *through* anything. You are passing through something that was made. There is no stone in this world. There is only what was built, and what was built over it, and what will be built tomorrow.

Then the tunnel opens, and the Works begin.

They say ninety kilometers, end to end, from the Air Plants in the west to the Frontier in the east, though no one has walked it straight through. The main bays run eighty meters high in places, four hundred in others, and the ceiling—everyone calls it the Lid—is a country unto itself: a dark plate-sky hung with pipe-trunks, cable braids, catwalk constellations, and the great throat of the ventilation mains, which draw the air of ninety kilometers slowly westward like weather with an opinion. The floor is deck. Poured slab, rolled plate, expansion joints running to the horizon in perfect ruled lines. Between the districts lie kilometers of empty lamplit nothing—a single service road with reflective waymarkers, a gutter-chain ticking with drips, and otherwise the flat plain of the built world, dim as dusk and cold enough that your breath walks beside you.

Because it is cold. That surprises first-time visitors, who expect deep places to be warm. But the Works sit under half a kilometer of frozen ground, under tundra that has never once thawed in living memory, and the cryo yards breathe their chill into everything. Working halls hold around minus four. Exposed metal takes skin. In the liquefier district the cold plate wears hoarfrost in feathers a meter long, white ferns growing in fast-forward from nothing, and the vent stacks exhale rolling fog banks that drift down the aisles like slow ghosts changing their minds.

And it is wet, which surprises people even more. A sealed world this size generates its own hydrology. The Lid sweats. Condensate beads along the ceiling seams in long silver ropes until something—temperature, vibration, chance—lets them go all at once, and it rains, briefly and locally, in a hall that has never known weather. The workers call it lidfall and barely look up. The water finds the gutters, the gutter-chains, the flumes, and runs east and down toward the low quarters, where the world's only river—flumed, banked, exactly as wide as it was built to be—feeds the Sump Flats.

The Flats are the closest thing the Underbuild has to a wilderness. Drainage spreads thin across the slab in sheet-water a finger deep, and someone, early on, set filtration trays along the margins and planted reeds. The reeds took. They stand now in gray-brown ranks at the water's edge, the only reeds in existence, hissing whenever the fan-wind gusts through, and around them the sump lakes hold perfectly still and perfectly mirror-flat. At night-cycle the sodium lamps come up amber along the far shores, and the lakes reflect them—one hundred reflected lights lying on their backs in the black water, which is the nearest thing to stars anyone down here gets, and they're all underwater.

Frogs live in the Flats. Introduced for algae control two generations of maintenance ago, heard far more often than seen: one chorus, rising from the sheet-water on warm-cycle evenings, the entire amphibian population of the known world singing at once. Fish too—pale filter-loaches threading the cooling channels, eyeless or nearly so, thriving on whatever the water carries. Springtails graze the algae films in the wet districts, grazing them back to clean metal like very small, very dedicated groundskeepers. And in Number Four sump there is, allegedly, a pike. Blind, enormous, older than the paint. Workers leave offerings. Seeing it means your shift will be quiet. No one disputes this.

The sound never stops. That is the first rule of the Underbuild, and the second, and the third: the hum is always there, a broadband industrial note with a fundamental around thirty hertz that you stop hearing in an hour and feel in your teeth forever. The fans are the wind here; when load rises in the foundries, the pitch climbs, and everyone glances westward the way surface folk used to glance at clouds. Distant events arrive twice. A crane dropping a beam six kilometers away strikes your ears once through the structure—quick, sharp—and again through the air seconds later, slower, rounder. Every far-off thing happens twice. Old-timers can tell you the distance to any impact in the Works to within a hundred meters, the way farmers once read storms.

Light is budgeted like everything else. Sodium amber for the general wash, cold white task-lights where hands are working, phosphorescent lane-strips stitching the dark floors together. At the eastern edge of visibility, welding arcs flicker and strobe against the dark like heat lightning—flash first, crack later, twice. You learn to read the Works by ear and by pipe color-code, because your eyes, honest as they try to be, cannot judge distance in a place with no horizon and no weather.

Except it is learning weather. That is the strange tenderness of this place: it is young. Forty years old, more or less—the whole world—and the surfaces still carry the innocence of new manufacture. Expansion joints clean. Paint unblistered. The oldest rust blooms spread across the lower girders like pioneer lichen, and the workers treat them with something like respect, because that rust is the beginning of geology here, the first sediment ever laid down in this valley of steel. The first wear-polish is forming on stair nosings. The first graffiti has gone up and been painted over and gone up again, two layers deep—stratigraphy, if you squint. Last year they held the first winter: a full-month maintenance shutdown, fans reversed, temperatures allowed to slide, and frost formed on the insides of windows in the housing blocks, and the children pressed their hands against it, having read about frost in school.

The children are the tell. There is a generation now that was born below and has never stood under an open sky, and they play games about the surface the way surface children once played games about the sea. The elders argue about why anyone came down here at all—the official reasons differ by department and decade. Some say the Works exist to *listen*: and it's true that in the Deep Galleries there are instrument halls where photomultiplier arrays hang like dark chandeliers in absolute blackness, acres of them, patient as coral, attending to particles that pass through the planet the way the lidfall passes through the gutters. Some say the Works exist to hide. Some say simply that a thing this large needed building, and the surface had weather, and the builders were tired of weather.

Whatever the truth, the world keeps its processes running like organs. The dawn-wave rolls west to east each morning-cycle, banks of lamps igniting district by district, a slow tide of light replacing the tide of dark. Shift-trains cross the empty decks with lit windows, slow comets carrying small warm cargoes of people. The drainage pumps cycle on and off—the heartbeat, everyone calls it—and after heavy production days the condensate tide rises and the gutters roar and the Sump Flats deepen by centimeters, breathing. Moths breed in the intake filters, pale drift-moths that swarm at filter-change and land on your shoulder-lamp like ash deciding to be alive.

There are rules, of course. No open flame outside the foundries. Nothing loose in the Fanfields, where the airstream turns debris into shrapnel. Hearing protection past the marked lines. And everyone chalks their route—arrows on the plate, code-marked—because the maps are always a season behind a world that grows every week. Which is the last thing to understand about the Underbuild: it is not finished. It will not be finished. Out past the last lit district stands the Frontier, a wall of scaffolding and fresh plate and floodlit gantries, and beyond that wall the black begins—not darkness, but the true unlined dark, cold and absolute, the raw condition the whole world was raised against. The boreheads grind out there around the clock, and their noise arrives at the settlements like surf from a sea that doesn't exist yet, and every week the linings follow the drills, and the world becomes a few meters larger, and the surveyors redraw the maps, and somewhere a child learns that the edge of everything has moved again.

Stand at the Frontier at night-cycle, if you're permitted. Fog drifting off the cryo yards. The frogs faint behind you, forty kilometers back, singing to their sheet-water. Welding lightning flickering in the middle distance like a storm that lives indoors. The hum under everything, holding it all together like tension in a drumhead. Ahead of you, the drilling, patient as tide. The world is forty years old and plans to be ten thousand, and it has already had its first rain, its first winter, its first graves up on the Heap—the one hill anywhere here, made of graded fill and planted with the survivors of the arboretum—and it is learning, drop by drop off the Lid, what kind of place it intends to become.
