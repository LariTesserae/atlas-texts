**The Under-Green**

First there is the sound, and you hear it before you see anything, because you hear it through your boots.

You reach this place across a polar plateau — flat basalt, wind-scoured, the kind of cold that makes metal a hazard to touch. There are no landmarks except holes. The excavation opens to the sky through a scatter of collapsed chimneys and one engineered access ramp, and from each opening rises a column of fog: warm, wet air exhaled from below, flash-freezing into a standing plume that hangs over the plateau all winter, building towers of feathered rime around every vent. The holes breathe. Slowly — one full exchange of the underworld's atmosphere takes hours — but visibly, a smoke without fire, and beneath your feet the ground carries a vibration that is not quite a sound yet: the pressure of a roar arriving through solid rock.

Climb down the ramp and it resolves. The noise becomes enormous, total, physical — a sound you wear rather than hear, that sits in the sternum and the back teeth. It is ventilation. Four colossal axial fans, housed in concrete halls at the northern end of the void, have been pulling air down from the frozen surface and pushing it through the galleries for thirty-odd years, and nobody has ever switched them off.

Below the surface, the excavation opens into a valley turned inside out: three or four kilometers of terraced pit and vaulted gallery, roofed by the plateau itself and pierced here and there by the collapse chimneys. Down the ramp walls, the drill marks are still crisp. The rock is young — cut within living memory, its benches sharp as masonry, its talus angular and unworn. Geologically, nothing has happened here yet. But the surfaces have not stayed bare.

They are upholstered.

Every floor, every wall base, every terrace, every ditch and cable trench: covered in a continuous mat of living green, from wall to wall, kilometer upon kilometer. Moss felt deep enough to yield underfoot like a sprung floor. Liverwort scales overlapping in dull pewter-green tiles. Algal leather in black sheets wherever water stands longest. This is the terrain — not a surface that plants grow on, but a surface made of plant, ten and twenty and locally fifty centimeters thick, and underneath it, only beginning, the first true soil of this place, which the mat itself is manufacturing at a rate you could measure with a ruler and a decade.

It is total, and it is sparse, and both at once. There are no forests down here, no thicket, no understory chaos. The big structures are few and far between: a fern colony the size of a room, alone on a bench. The aerial roots of the taiga forest far above, descending through the daywells in bundles as thick as your leg, hanging in pale curtains that sway — imperceptibly, over minutes — in the fan-drawn draft. Between such features stretch long, open reaches of undulating green-black plain, empty of anything but mat and distance. Distance is the most abundant substance in this world. The emptiness between presences is not absence; it is floored, clothed, alive — and vacant.

The light is dim and it comes from two directions. From above: the chimneys, each admitting a column of pale subarctic daylight, angled steeply, brief in winter, absent entirely for weeks around midwinter — and even in summer the sun up there leans so low that the light arrives slanted and thin, laying long gold bars across the mat that migrate over the course of the day like slow animals. The descending root-curtains filter these columns into striation; walking through a daywell's light is like walking through the strings of an instrument. From below and beside: the lamps. Sodium fixtures on rusted gantries, a fraction of them still cycling on timers set by people who left, burning their stubborn orange into the fog. Their pools are the brightest places in the underworld, islands of warm color in a landscape otherwise graded from grey-green to black. Around a dozen of the damper galleries, tiny blue points stud the ceiling: gnat larvae, each with a dangling thread of beaded dew, fishing the air. They add almost nothing to the brightness. You notice them only when the lamps behind you die.

Because lamps do die, out here. And nothing replaces them.

The fog is the weather. The fans drag surface air down dry and cold, but the underworld exhales moisture from every seep and sump, and where the two meet, mist condenses into ground-hugging lakes of it that drift across the terraces at walking pace or slower. Overhead the fog touches cold stone, beads, and falls again as a fine endless dripping — the whole void runs its own private water cycle, a rain that travels meters, not kilometers, from cloud to ground. You will never feel real rainfall here. You will feel condensation beading on your eyelashes within twenty minutes of arriving. Drink by wringing moss into your cupped hand; drink rime off the cables. Everything is damp. Nothing is washed.

There is standing water: the sumps, black and mirror-flat, some kept pumped down by pumps that still run on their own schedule — you hear them as a rhythmic knocking far off, a heartbeat inside the roar. In the deepest sump lives a fish, pale as the lamp glare, eyes reduced to skin-deep shadows, feeding on whatever drifts down. Almost nobody sees it. Its existence is known mostly from one survey team's single netted specimen and the ripple patterns on still nights.

And there is the frost line.

This is the coldest inhabited ground there is — the air holds within a degree either side of freezing everywhere, and near the raw intake shafts, where surface winter pours straight down, it fails even that. Warmth exists only in pockets: inside the transformer house, whose bulk glows faintly to the touch; in the compressor rooms; and a centimeter down inside the mat itself, where the blanket of living tissue traps its own metabolic heat and stays soft when everything above it is stiff. The vegetation presses against the cold like a tide against a shore. The shore is visible. Walk the White Margin and you cross, in the space of two steps, from yielding green into rime-feathered bare rock where the mat whitens, halts, and retreats — and where the drill scars in the stone look exactly as fresh as the day the boring machine passed, because at minus ten nothing wears them away. Beyond the line, the underworld is pure recent industry: sharp benched walls, angular spoil, ice. On the green side of the line, in the coldest seeps, the snow-algae bloom — arterial streaks and pools of crimson staining the felt, the red spreading a hand-span further some years, less others.

The line advances perhaps a meter a year. Sometimes it retreats. This is the pace of everything here.

Because the place is an abandoned enterprise, and its ghost is legible everywhere once you know to read it. This was meant to be a farm. Rows of planter rails run under the mat for kilometers, fused into the felt. Nutrient pipes still trace their routes along the terrace edges, and at corroded joints they weep a slow mineral-rich dribble — the nearest thing this world has to rivers — and the growth along the pipe roads is measurably, luxuriantly richer than elsewhere: yellow-green velvet where the general mat is somber. Stencil codes survive on the walls — strain numbers, light-cycle schedules, a painted arrow labeled with words worn to ghosts. A battery locomotive sits on its half-track of exposed rail, moss upholstered to the window line, its controls sheathed in liverwort tile. The crops the builders intended died within a season of their leaving; what colonized instead came as spores down the intake air and as seeds dropped by ravens through the chimneys, and it turned out the underworld suited the weeds better than the harvest.

Who tends it now? Nobody, and the systems. The fans run because the automation was never told to stop, and perhaps because stopping them would drown the lower galleries. The pumps run on their pressure triggers. The lamps run on their timers, dragging a fake dusk across the void at 21:40 every evening regardless of what the sun is doing over the plateau — and on the mat, in a slow way, the fake dusk works. Photosynthesis stops. The fog thickens.

The fauna count themselves easily. The gnats, clouding every working lamp in slow pale spirals, grazing films off the mat as larvae. The sump fish. Ravens, which descend the daywells in passage season, ride the drafts, scream once and have the scream swallowed whole by the roar, scavenge along the pipe roads, and leave. And one fox — a single individual, descended years ago by the access ramp, that lives in the warm skirt of the transformer house and hunts the lamp-pools at fake dusk, leaping for gnats in the orange light. Whether another has ever joined it, nobody knows. You might walk this whole valley-under-the-world for a week and see nothing with eyes except those.

Sound deserves its own accounting, because sound is the dominant material here, more present than rock. The roar saturates everything; speech is impossible at any distance, and conversation is conducted in hand-sign and touch. But listen longer and the monotony opens into geography. Each district of the void has its own harmonic signature — the drone bends differently through the tall galleries than through the low ones, drops in pitch along the terraces, acquires a metallic edge near the fan halls where the wash is strong enough to lean the root-curtains. Locals — the few survey crews that still come — navigate by ear. Beneath the roar sit the slower voices: the pump's knock, the transformers' fifty-cycle hum, water ticking in steel pipes, and, very rarely, a crack like a rifle shot when a sheet of rime lets go of a cable, the echo rolling away through chambers for five full seconds. Then the roar closes back over the gap. Silence does not exist here. Even the quietest corner is full to its brim.

Time behaves accordingly. Nothing decays quickly in the cold — a dropped glove stays a dropped glove, bootprints persist in mineral crust for decades, the locomotive will be recognizable in a century. Nothing grows quickly either; watching this landscape is impossible, and the only way to see it change is to leave and come back years later, and notice the fern colony wider, the frost line remodeled, one more lamp gone dark, the mat a finger-joint deeper over the planter rails. Seasons exist but arrive as rumor: summer sends meltwater threads down the chimneys and a hour more light through them; winter thickens the fog, stills the mat, pushes the White Margin greedily forward. The underworld learns of the sun the way the deaf learn of music — by vibration, secondhand, through the floor.

Nothing here breaks a rule. That is the last thing to understand, standing anywhere on the felt with a palm flat against a railing, feeling the roar as touch rather than hearing. Every process in this place is ordinary: condensation, colonization, corrosion, frost. It is only that the ordinary has been given a cathedral to work in and no supervision, and has been running, patient as sediment, for thirty years. The mat does not know it swallowed a farm. The fans do not know they became weather. Somewhere above the chimneys the taiga goes on dropping seeds down the light-shafts, missing, missing, germinating in the felt, and the frost line takes its annual meter, and the four fans inhale.

The valley breathes. It is the loudest quiet thing in the world.
