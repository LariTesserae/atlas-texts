# The Congregation

High on the mountain's shoulder, past the snowline, where the air has thinned to almost nothing and the cold would kill an unsheltered traveler in an hour, the rock opens. Not a crater — a pore. A chimney scarcely wider than a body, breathing warm air upward into the frost. The mountain wears ice like a hood and keeps, beneath it, one heated room.

The room is not large. Two hundred paces across at the widest, domed, floored with terraces of ancient lava laid down in benches, tier upon tier, like seating in an amphitheater. The stone is old and settled — nothing here is sharp anymore. Basalt worn to the texture of driftwood, slopes of black glass sand where no film can grip, edges rounded by ten thousand years of small feet. Whatever violence formed this place ended long before its current tenants arrived. Now it is in balance, the way an old house is in balance: everything bearing weight that has borne it before.

There is almost no light, and what light exists rises from the floor. Fissures exhale heat from far below, and wherever that heat crosses suitable stone it wakes a film — gray-gold, velvet-thin, neither plant nor quite fungus, closer to a thought the rock is having — that glows a dull ember-red while warm and dims to nothing as the heat migrates on. So the floor is charted in veins of dying red light that redraw themselves over weeks, and everywhere else is a blackness that leans gently on the eyes until the eyes surrender. You learn to see by movement here, or not at all.

And the heat is the wealth of the place. Understand the arrangement: outside is altitude, thin air, killing frost. Inside is a steady warmth like the interior of a body — hot enough that a visitor would strip their clothes and still sweat, gentle enough for the natives to sleep uncovered. The chamber sits in its mountain like a coal wrapped in wool, and every living thing in it is, in one way or another, a creature of that warmth.

**There is no water.** Say it plainly, because everything else follows from it. No pools, no seeps of anything kind, no condensation on the cool upper stone. Snow arriving at the rim sublimates to vapor before it can fall in. Once in a while, from somewhere overhead, you will hear the *beginning* of a drop — a gathering tick, a gathering tick — but the landing never comes. The drop dies midair with a sound too small to be called a hiss. All the water in this world is locked inside bodies, passed mouth to mouth across generations, hoarded like heirloom jewelry. Moisture is inheritance. Nothing spills. When one creature takes another, it drinks what it kills — not savagely, the way we'd mean it, but the way an executor settles an estate.

Now: the room is *full*.

Full past sense. Full the way a seed is full. The lowest benches are carpeted with grazers — soft-footed, shell-backed things that rasp the glowing film off the stone in slow silvering strokes, tens of thousands of them, packed flank to flank so that in places the rock itself hasn't seen daylight in centuries. The walls are shingled with heat-drinkers: flat, tiled creatures pressed to the warm stone like slates on a roof, overlapping, layered three and four deep, drinking the gradient straight out of the mountain. When a patch of wall cools, they peel away and slide — over days, in a wave you could watch if you had years — toward fresher warmth, leaving bare stone that gleams briefly before the film returns.

And the ceiling is curtained. Veil-hangers hang from every anchor point in long fringed bodies, combing the warm updrafts for drifting motes of dust, spore-analogs, the fine ash the mountain sheds. From below they look like the pipes of an organ hung upside down, swaying slightly, always swaying slightly, in a wind you cannot feel.

Uncountable numbers. And yet —

— no sound. Nearly none. That is the law here, older than any tenant. The hunters that matter in this chamber do not hunt by sight or scent alone; they hunt by *listening*. Vibration is the only confession this world makes. So evolution did what evolution does, and squeezed the noise out of everything. Feet went soft. Shells went lined and muffling. Jaws went to silk. Death here is not a scream; death is a patch of motion becoming a patch of stillness, and the neighbors leaning subtly away.

Aiding the law are the hushers: pale, ear-shaped creatures wedged into resonant niches in the walls, doing nothing visible for their entire lives except drinking tremor out of the stone. Press your palm near one and your own heartbeat seems to go missing. Whole communities cluster around hushers the way villages cluster around wells. Silence, in this place, is infrastructure.

The predators are long and jointed and eyeless, patient as dropped rope. They read the air — pressure, heat-ribbons, the chemical wake of fear — and when they strike they wrap, and compress, and wait, and the waiting is the worst of it, though no one in the chamber knows the word *worst*. When it ends, the unwinders arrive: specialized undertakers that reduce a body to its components within hours. Calcium to eggshell. Chitin to mortar. Moisture to the victor, always the moisture first. Nothing is wasted, because nothing *can* be wasted; the ledger here balances to the molecule.

Scattered through all this — sparse, deliberate — are the built things.

Molt-cairns: neat towers of shed shells stacked grain by grain by some species that does it for reasons no outsider has decoded, courtship or territory or prayer. A dam of fused gravel, raised by a swarm of grubs over generations, that pools a shallow lens of hot brine — the nearest thing to standing liquid in the world, and guarded around the clock with a ferocity that briefly violates the silence-law when it must. And then, one anomaly, set into a lower bench: a wall of fitted stones. Low, hand-high, old past guessing, its edges worn to the same softness as the mountain itself. Nothing here makes tools. Whoever raised this wall raised it with hands, or patience, or sheer accumulated weight. The natives neither avoid it nor use it. They climb over it, indifferent, which is somehow worse than reverence.

The room is quiet, but it is not asleep. Twice a day the chimney breathes — a long exhalation of warm air into the frozen sky, then the slow inhale — and the veils ride it, and the dust rises and is combed. Migrations crawl the walls. In molt-season the whole floor sheds at once and stands pale as a bone-field for a night, and by the next dark the unwinders have erased it. Occasionally a new fissure opens, breathing hotter than the rest, and there is a gold rush — a silent, desperate, elbowing migration of everything that drinks heat, settling onto the new country like a congregation finding seats.

And very rarely: something falls down the chimney. A stone. Once, long ago, a bird's frozen body, intact, its feathers the first color anyone down here had seen in an age. When that happens, the entire chamber stops. Thousands upon thousands of bodies going still at once is its own kind of sound — a pressure, an anti-sound, a held breath the size of a cathedral. The listening hunters wake and walk the benches all night on their soft terrible feet. Then morning-equivalent comes, the heat resumes its circulation, and the hush closes over the memory like skin.

If you stayed — and you shouldn't, you are wet and loud and full of water, you are walking treasure — you would notice the last thing, the thing underneath all the others. The quiet here is not emptiness. It is attention. A congregation this size, packed tier upon tier in the dark, utterly silent, faces downward. Toward the floor. Toward the veins of red light and whatever exhales them.

They are not singing. They are listening to something below the stone.

And you would find yourself whispering first, then not speaking at all, then listening too — a little late, and politely, and in good company.
