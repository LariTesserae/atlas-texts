# The Stillroom

You come to it across a salt flat so white it reads as absence, at the one place where the crust buckles into a low ridge of gypsum. There is a steel door set into the ridge, and behind the door, thirty-one steps down, a room.

That is the first surprise: *room*. You expect a mine, a warren, a deepening. Instead the stairs deliver you into a single gallery — sixty paces long, twenty wide, ceiling low enough that the cable-braids brush your hair if you stretch. A chamber, not a world. But everything a world does, it does here, packed tight as instruments in a case.

The air meets you first. It is warm — blood-warm, held there with a precision your skin registers as wrongness, because air is never supposed to be so decided. It is dry past all reason; your lips tighten within a minute, your knuckles whiten as the moisture leaves them. And it smells extraordinary: hot stone, ozone off the switchgear, machine oil, the copper tang of fresh-broken rock — and underneath it all, faint and impossible in this desiccated vault, orange peel.

The walls are young. That is the second surprise. Whatever violence opened this space, it was recent: the bore-helix scoring still glitters on the halite faces, the arrises of every rib and pillar are knife-sharp, undusted, unworn. Stone that should have softened centuries ago looks like it was cut on Tuesday. No dripstone. No spall. Not a grain of grit in the corners — though not because there is no dust. Look up and the dust is *up there*, hanging in the lamp-glow in fixed brownian constellations, riding convection cells so stable they might be machined gears. The air here has been running the same loop so long that its turbulence has become architecture. The motes do not fall. They orbit.

And the sound — you will mistake it for silence at first, and then you will understand that it is louder than anything you have ever heard.

A flywheel turns somewhere behind the east partition: the Governor, the brass plate says, one revolution to the second, forever, driving a generator whose output needles rest against their pins at precisely the angles they have always held. Beneath that drone lies the click-train — a relay closing each second, 3,600 clicks to the hour — and beneath the clicks, a stridulation of crickets so numerous and so phased to the flywheel that insect and machine have merged into a single instrument. The total effect is a chord. A loud, warm, unvarying chord, sustained the way a waterfall is loud, except that a waterfall stutters and this does not. Your ear keeps searching the sound for fluctuation, the way an eye searches a painting for movement, and finding none it begins to report *silence*. This is the Stillroom's great trick: it is so uniformly loud that it functions as quiet. Noise without event. Duration without time.

Because that is the rule here, and every detail of the place obeys it. **Nothing advances.** The place was finished — truly finished, sealed, tuned — some span ago that cannot be measured from inside, and since then it has simply *persisted*, at equilibrium, burning nothing, gaining nothing, losing nothing. The generator consumes no fuel; its tank sight-glass shows the same bright meniscus it has always shown, because the machine recycles its own exhaust heat with an efficiency that should be impossible anywhere but here, where impossibility is apparently the local medium.

Walk the gallery and the density of it presses in. Floor level: iron rails, seed trays, four vault tortoises — one per quadrant, shells polished matte, each tracing the same closed circuit of the floor forever, arriving nowhere, lacking even the growth-rings that would betray a day passed. Mid-level: the pipe forest, warm to the touch, wrapped in lagging that shows no scuff although pale cat-sized geckos sleep coiled on the manifolds. Overhead: the ducts, the lamp housings, the braid of cabling from which six relay-finches watch you with varnished eyes. There are always six. When a hawk-shadow of accident takes one — and something does, sometimes, in the hour — a clutch hatches in the alternator cage within the same sixty minutes, and the count restores itself before it has had time to become loss. Death exists here the way the drop of water exists: momentarily, and never accumulating.

Ah — the water. Here is where the Stillroom keeps its secret. Along the north wall stands the Still proper: a squat column, a coil of glass, a spout. When the builders sealed this place they pumped the last of the aquifer's pocket dry and distilled it, gram by gram, into that coil — the entire residual dampness of the region, banked in glass. The dehumidifiers pull from the air exactly what the wickmoss transpires; the wickmoss drinks exactly what the cold line sweats; the ledger balances to the milligram. And at the spout, one drop stands perpetually at the lip of falling — swelling by condensation, shrinking by the dry air, net zero, trembling. It has been about to fall since the beginning. The whole future of this place hangs in its surface tension.

Life fills the remaining margins. Wickmoss runs in a grey-green seam along the coolant pipe, exactly as long today as it has always been. Pale films — lamp-ferns — furred inside the sodium housings, feeding on light-leak and warmth. Moths orbit the lamps in ellipses so stable they look drawn in ink; their shed scales join the orbital dust. And in the southeast alcove, under a grow-lamp bolted beside a chalked tally wall, stands a potted orange tree holding twelve fruits at permanent almost-ripeness — neither sweetening nor falling, the scent-source, the impossible perfume in the dry. Beside the tree: an enamel mug with a tea-ring burned permanently into the shelf. A wooden chair whose armrests were worn smooth *before* the sealing — old wear, grandfathered wear, the only aged surfaces in the room, imported like fossils. Tallies in chalk climbing a wall to some four-thousand-odd strokes and stopping, capped by a single word in a steady hand: **IT HOLDS**. And crossing the floor, a line of bootprints baked into a skim of mineral fines — leading toward the door, made by someone who stepped out of duration entirely, for the maintenance carriage (the Tender, a small trundling automaton on an overhead rail that oils and tightens and rewraps on a fixed circuit, and which steers scrupulously around the prints as though they were load-bearing) will sweep every grain else from that floor until the end of the world.

At the top of each hour, everything rhymes. The 3,600th click lands. The finches sing eight notes in unison. The Tender pauses at the door-frame, dips, resumes. The drop trembles at the spout, and holds. Then the chord rolls on.

Stay long enough and the rules begin to touch you. Your watch drifts until it agrees with the relay-train. You cannot remember which day you came down, and when you dream here, the dream repeats exactly. Each object owns a single permanent shadow, thrown by unflickering lamps, and your own shadow starts to feel assigned rather than cast — a second silhouette you've always had. Hunger arrives and departs on schedule. You will find yourself unhurried in a way that frightens you afterward.

But the door is never locked. Climb the thirty-one steps and push, and the salt flat hits you like a slap — wind, actual wind, staggering and irregular, and the sun a disc that dares to move, and somewhere far off the sound of water doing what water does, which is refuse to hold still. Time resumes with a lurch, like a train taking up slack. Visitors stand in the glare breathing hard, absurdly grateful for erosion, for rust, for their own drying sweat.

Below, through the shut door, muffled to a heartbeat: the chord, the crickets, the six finches, the four circuits, the Tender on its rounds.

And the drop, at the lip of the spout, still about to fall — keeping, in the skin of itself, the next moment. Kept.
