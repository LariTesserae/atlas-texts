# The Summit Garden

Here is the strangeness at the heart of this place, and it must be said plainly first: this is a high place, and it is underwater, and both facts are true at the same moment, in the same glance. A mountain stands here — a whole young cordillera of them, ridge after ridge running north into the blue — and its summit plateau rests in full, warm, ordinary daylight, forty fathoms beneath a sea so clear that noon falls on it almost undiminished. Nothing about the light admits to depth. It moves across the stone the way it moves across a field. And yet the height of the mountain is real too, four thousand meters of it; it is simply that here, height runs downward. You measure it by turning away from the sun and watching the flank of the range fall through color — gold, then green, then a long teal, then a blue that stops being a color and becomes a suggestion — until the slope lets go of sight entirely and becomes the plain, somewhere far below, where no day has ever arrived.

The rock is young. That is the second thing, and the water has not yet learned how to hide it. There are no smooth faces anywhere, no rounded shoulders, no patient old contours. Every edge is a fracture edge: knifed arrêtes, columnar cliffs jointed in crisp hexagons, talus slopes of chips and shards so fresh they have not settled into their angles. Where a slab sheared away last century — last week, by the measure of mountains — the exposure gleams faintly, a mineral skin not yet dulled by film or silt, holding a dull metallic luster like cooled iron. The sediment between the rocks is thin and new, still arranging itself, and in places there is no sediment at all, only raw stone wearing its first skin of green. Occasionally, in the great quiet, a block somewhere on the ridge settles into its seat with a single felt *tick*, a sound like a knuckle cracking in another room, and the whole plateau seems to nod slightly. The mountain is still rising. It lifts about the width of a finger per human lifetime, and it does not hurry.

The water is warm — bathwater-warm, thick-feeling, soft against every surface — and part of that warmth comes out of the ground. Along a fissure on the plateau's eastern rim, a line of low vents breathes continuously: not violent chimneys, just seams in the young crust exhaling shimmer. Above them the water bends and ripples with heat-haze, upside down, so that the gardens nearby waver as if seen through old glass. Small pale mats grow around each seep, sulfur-tinted, thriving in the warmth while everything else keeps a polite distance. Elsewhere the warmth is simply ambient, held in the clear water like held breath, and the light coming down through it lands on the rock in shifting nets — caustics, the sea's version of shade — crawling slowly over the turf as the sun wobbles in its bright circle overhead.

And the rock is clothed. That is the third thing, and it is the one the eye returns to longest. Nothing here is bare except the newest scars. Every surface that has existed for more than a few decades carries life, and the life is layered, dense, near-touching, stacked through the water column the way furniture fills a house. At the lowest level: crimson and umber turf, a velvet nap over virtually every stone, cropping the fractures short. Above that, in the saddles where fine sediment has gathered into real soil-that-isn't-soil, meadows of broad-leaved seagrass run in unbroken swales between the peaks, hundreds of meters across, rippling all in one direction when the slow current remembers to move. On the summit plateau proper grows the forest: ribbon-kelp with blades ten, fifteen meters long, golden-brown, buoyed by slender floats, standing up out of the turf in loose groves and holding their leaves into the light like a congregation with its arms raised. Along the cliff edges, where the drop begins, a different plant — tall, thin, needle-spined spire-weeds — leans hard and uniformly westward, every one of them bent to the same angle, the prevailing current written permanently into their posture, a whole hillside of green commas pointing the same way. And in the pink-and-white rings of the encrusting liths you can read years, if you know how: each band a season, each colony a small stone-bound calendar that no one consults.

Nothing here is named. Names are artifacts, and nothing made has ever touched this place — that much is unmistakable. There is no straight line anywhere that was not made by crystal or by fracture. No edge was cut. No surface was planed. Whatever died here died by shading-out or smothering or age, and shows only as bleaching, a pale patch in the crimson turf, never as wreckage. The absence of the made is so complete it has a texture of its own, a kind of cleanliness.

Twice a year, in the long warm season, the range performs its one extravagance: the upward snow. Every kelp grove and every meadow releases its spores at once — not violently, but over weeks — and the water above the summits fills with rising motes, billions of them, drifting up toward the light like a snowfall running in reverse. For days the whole cordillera smokes gently with pollen-of-the-sea. The light comes down through it in solid visible shafts, and the motes turn in the beams, glittering, ascending, and the forest beneath stands still and lets its future leave.

Animals are rare enough that each one is an event. Once in a long while — perhaps once a season, perhaps less — a grazer crosses a saddle meadow: a single heavy, slow, gray-green shape, big as a boat, moving at the pace of weather, cropping the grass in a wide clean swath. The bright path of its feeding stays visible for months afterward, a pale stripe through the green that closes only gradually, like a wound healing in fast-forward. Sometimes, briefly, a school of small silver fish appears over the plateau — a thrown handful of coins, wheeling once, flashing — and then it is simply not there anymore, and the reason it came and the reason it left are equally unknown. And once, far down along the blue shelf where the flank drops toward the plain, a voice: a single long call, low, rising slightly at the end, traveling up through the water and past the summit and away. Nothing answers it. The sound takes a long time to fully fade, and after it goes, the quiet is different than before — not emptier, exactly. More aware of itself.

Because the quiet here is enormous. Water should carry sound, but there is almost nothing in this sea that makes any. What remains is texture rather than noise: the felt pulse of distant swell arriving minutes later as a soft shudder through the kelp, the paper-whisper of ten thousand blades sliding past each other, the occasional settling tick of stone, the near-inaudible hiss of the vents. In the stillest hours the silence is complete enough that the body's own sounds surface — breath, blood, the small tides of the inner ear — and it becomes clear that the quiet was never an absence of sound. It is the sound of a place that has not yet been found.

Distance lies, here, but gently. The clarity makes far things look near, and the water's weight makes near things feel far, so nothing can be trusted absolutely and everything must be drifted toward. Up is the only reliable compass; the bright wobbling ceiling tells you where you are, and the shadows of surface-waves — if waves there are, leagues above — travel over the rock like the shadows of clouds, though the storms that cast them will not be felt here for days, and will arrive only as a slight quickening of the sway. Time is kept by light-angle, not hours. Change is kept in decades. A kelp blade adds its length over a year. A palm's height of sediment falls as fine, ceaseless, imperceptible snow over a century. The pink rings widen. The mountain rises its finger-width. The pale feeding-trail of the grazer greens over from its edges inward, patiently, the way everything here happens: without event, without witness, without haste.

Stand — or drift; the distinction loosens — on the highest point of the highest summit, at the crown of the tallest peak in a drowned range, in warm lit water, in a garden that covers everything and touches everything and crowds itself generously on every surface for miles in every direction. Look down, and the blue opens beneath you like night with the lid taken off, and you understand that this is the top of the world, and that it is only possible to tell by looking down. The animals have not found this place yet. When they come, they will come slowly, a few at a time, over centuries, and the garden will make room without hurrying. It has already been making room for a very long time. It is, in fact, doing so now — rising a fraction, greening a shade, breathing its slow warm breath through the seams of the young stone, waiting with the particular patience of a place where nothing has a name and nothing needs one, and the snow falls upward, and the light is good.
