## Verdigris

You come to it from the levee, because you can only come to it from the levee — a long earthen spine, grass-grown and cart-tracked, running straight as a laid rule across the wet country until it ends at a ring of trees that is, you will eventually understand, a wall, a roof, a factory district, and a neighborhood all at once.

The basin itself is not large. You could walk its diameter in half an hour if the walkways were clear, and they are not, quite. Six hundred people live here, give or take, inside a saucer of land that sits perhaps two meters above its own water table. That is the first thing to accept about Verdigris: it does not sit *on* the water so much as float on it, held up by roots and brick and habit. Dig anywhere more than arm's length and you strike seep. The founders knew it. Everyone since has known it. So nothing is dug that doesn't have to be, and everything that exists does so by permission of the water — raised, drained, channeled, or politely tolerated.

The trees resolve as you get closer. What looked from the levee like ordinary woodland is in fact load-bearing. The houses of Verdigris are grown: pollarded planes and willows, trained over generations along wattle armatures, grafted and folded until the living wood closes around the frame and becomes wall, lintel, stair-stringer, roof-beam. Windows are gaps the growers left and glazed with panes set into wooden rebates that were carved *around* the glass as the cambium crept. Doorways swell and shrink with the seasons; every household keeps a record book, and once a year the joiners go door to door planing jambs back to tolerance. A door in Verdigris is not an object. It is a maintenance schedule.

Between the houses the streets are causeway — brick and cordwood and packed ash laid over open drains called rhyne, each one running with a shallow permanent current. The water is everywhere and always moving, but slowly. That's the character of the place: nothing rushes. The rhyne turn brown-green wheels at intersections, little overshot mills no bigger than barrelheads, each driving a shaft that disappears into the nearest workshop. Rain is frequent and warm and rarely dramatic; the rocks weep here, the old mine spoil at the rim of the basin sweats mineral stains down its faces in long teal streaks, and every dawn a fog lifts off the warm water and hangs in the canopy until mid-morning.

That water, incidentally, is why there are almost no animals. The streams come down from abandoned copper workings upstream, and three hundred years of runoff have seasoned the whole watershed with just enough dissolved metal to poison small things. Not enough to trouble the plants — many of them came to love it, stacking the metal in their tissues in glittering concentrations — but enough that the insect life is sparse, the amphibians gone, the fish reduced to a few thick-shelled snails, some black leeches, and a population of stunted sticklebacks clinging on in the one channel the town keeps flushed with boiled condensate. There are no birds. There are no birdsong mornings here. There is a heron, singular, which arrives some autumns and stands in the settling ponds for a week like a visiting dignitary, and when it comes, it is the only thing anyone talks about.

Pollination, therefore, is a chore. On flowering days, children move through the orchard tiers with soft wands tipped in lamb's-wool substitute — moss, actually, grown for exactly this — dusting blossom row by row while the adults shout instructions about which variety goes to which. The whole reproductive life of the higher plants in Verdigris passes through human hands, or did until the wind-seeded crops took over the outer tiers. It makes the town oddly tender about flowers. Nobody picks them casually.

What replaced the fauna, ecologically speaking, is fungus and machine. The fungal load is enormous — bracket shelves the size of dinner plates on every dead limb, the managed rot of the compost tiers steaming gently in the cool of the evening, mushroom houses dug into the levee's shaded flank where the temperature holds steady year-round. And the machines fill the audible space the animals would have.

Ah — the sound. You hear the Engine before you see it, and you never stop hearing it afterward. It stands at the center of the basin in a house of grown timber and salvaged brick: a Cornish-type beam engine, cast somewhere else two hundred years ago, hauled here in pieces, and running ever since. The town calls it Grandmother. She is not fast. She turns at a pace you could match walking, four strokes to the minute, each stroke a deep wooden-and-iron *knock* followed by a long exhalation of steam from the condensing gear, and each stroke travels through the ground and the root-network and the floorboards of every house in the basin. Grandmother is the clock. People date events by strokes. *It happened around the forty-stroke mark.* Her beam rocks overhead in the engine house like the slow arm of someone stirring an enormous pot, and the line shafts off her flywheel run out on woven-bark belts to drive the lathe shop, the pulping mill, the press house, the rope-walk winch.

The boilers burn dried brash and pressed peat-brickette, banked low, because Verdigris does not believe in hurry. Pressure rises over hours. Once a week there is a blowdown — a full-voice blast of steam that rolls around the basin like weather — and the whole town schedules around it, hanging washing, closing shutters, putting in earplugs if they have them. Otherwise the noise is a fabric: Grandmother's heartbeat underneath, the tick and rush of water over a hundred sluice gates, the creak of the chain pumps on the main drain, the hiss of the condenser towers where the air itself is wrung for drinking water, and over it all the big leaves of the upper canopy slapping together in any breeze at all, flat and percussive, like distant applause that never quite finishes.

Because yes — the town drinks the sky. Along the highest walkways hang the dew-fences: tall screens of dark mesh, and every morning before full light a shift of workers walks the rows with scrapers and pails, taking the harvest of condensation off the wire. Between that, the rain, and the shallow wells at sanctioned points (private wells are forbidden; the water table is communal property and the drainage tax is the oldest law on the books), nobody wants. But nobody wastes, either. Every roof sheds to a gutter, every gutter to a cistern, every overflow to the rhyne, every rhyne to Grandmother's pump-well, and she lifts it all again, forever, patient as geology.

The industry is botanical. This is the part that takes longest to sink in: the factories are fields and the fields are factories. The upper story of standards supplies structural timber — cut on a strict rotation, each felling matched to a planting made decades ago by someone now dead. The middle tiers give bark fiber for the rope-walk (a quarter-mile of twisting walkway along the inner levee, where cables for other towns' cranes and dredges are spun), and pulp for the paper mill, whose product is famous downriver for its faint green-tinged strength. And the metal-loving plants — the ones that hoarded the copper out of the poisoned water — are harvested, burned, and their ash leached in the dye yard, yielding the pigment that gave the place its name. Verdigris green goes out by barge, a few crates a month, along with rope, paper, preserved mushrooms, pressed seed-oil for lamps, and greenmeal — the algae grown in long shallow raceways warmed by boiler exhaust, stirred by paddle-wheels, and eaten daily by everyone. It is not delicious. It is complete. There are no livestock here; there was never pasture; the protein question was answered a hundred and fifty years ago by a pond, and the answer stuck.

The light is ordinary — that surprises visitors. Given everything else, you expect gloom, but the basin opens over the central canals into broad white daylight, warm and humid, the sun finding the water and bouncing up under the canopy edges so that even the shaded walks glow softly from below. Only deep in the residential tiers does the green filter take over, rooms lit in underwater tones, lamps lit at odd hours because the householders lost track. Evenings smell of lamp-oil and sap and coal-smoke and the sweetish breath of active compost, with a thin metallic top-note you stop noticing on the third day and dream about after you leave.

There are rules, and they are mostly about time. Every household owes canopy-hours — pruning, training, grafting — logged in the Book of Grafts. Cutting live wood without license is the closest thing Verdigris has to a serious crime. The dead are returned to the memorial grove at the rim, as mulch around young standards, and those trees are never harvested; the founders' grandchildren are still standing there, growing, adding a ring a year, holding the skyline at the collar-drain. Because the town is bounded — the outer ditch, called the Collar, circles everything, and beyond it is wet scrub that floods in the wet months and no one has ever wanted to build on. The whole settlement fits inside its ring like a boat inside its own wake. Sediment dredged from the canals goes up onto the raised beds; nothing washes away anymore, nothing silts in. Three hundred years of patient engineering have brought the basin to grade. Nothing erodes here. Everything composts.

And the town is still growing, at its own speed. Each decade, on the same week, a new room is begun somewhere — an armature erected, a sapling bent to it, a family assigned — and everyone alive knows they will not sleep under its finished roof. The youngest apprentices sign the beam of the newest engine-house extension with chisels, knowing the building will close over their marks. Grandmother knocks. Four strokes to the minute, sixty minutes to the hour, the dew-fences filling in the dark, the rings going down unseen under bark, the heron arriving or not arriving in October. The loudest thing for fifty miles is also the slowest. In Verdigris those were always the same thing.
