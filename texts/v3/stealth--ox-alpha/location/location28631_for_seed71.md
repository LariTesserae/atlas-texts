# The Old Level

You climb for eleven days through the skirt-country to reach it, and then the land does something no land should do at this height: it stops rising. Not levels off — *stops*. The slope ends the way a sentence ends, and ahead of you opens a plain so wide that its far edge is a theory. This is the Old Level, and it is high not because anything pushed it up lately but because it was born highest and everything lower has had the patience to fall. The mountains that once crowded it have spent their whole inheritance becoming valleys elsewhere. Here alone the original surface stays, worn smooth as an old coin, holding its elevation the way an elder holds a chair at a table where all the guests have died.

The first thing to say about the Level is that the light is honest. Whatever else fails up here — and things fail — the sun keeps its appointments. It rises where it should, warms the skin while the air steals the warmth back, throws shadows that are exactly as long as they have any right to be. After a while you understand why this matters. In a place where distance drifts and time pools in the hollows, the truthfulness of light is the one courtesy the world still extends. Navigators trust it absolutely. Everything else, they check twice.

The air is thin and cold and smells of stone and rime and, faintly, of green — because for all its dryness the Level is lush. That is the second contradiction you must accept. Water hardly ever falls here. What water does is *visit*.

Twice a day, the sea of cloud that lies below the rim of the plain swells and presses inward, and the fog comes across the Level in a slow silver tide. The locals — by whom I mean the builders, long ago; nobody local remains — timed their whole civilization to this pulse. They called it nothing grander than the drinking-hour. Along the old causeways you pass their machinery still working: fields of slate plates pitched at the angle that makes fog-bead run instead of cling, channelled by hair-thin grooves into troughs, from the troughs into stepped cisterns the size of village squares. The grooves were cut by hand, with knapped chert. You can put your thumb in them.

Where the fog lies deepest, the dry riverbeds run — for one hour, sometimes two. The water arrives without rain, condensing along cold stone in threads, and by afternoon the rivers are beds again, pale with frost-dust, looking as if they had never once been persuaded to flow. The builders' settlements cluster at these hourly confluences. Green does the same.

For green on the Level is not a condition. It is an event. Between events, the plain is grey — frost-grey, lichen-grey, the grey of slate under an honest sky — and then you crest some shallow rise and a grove opens around you, dense, dripping, almost tropical in its insistence, entirely dependent on the two fog-tides a day. The combfirs stand thirty feet with needles like finework combs, stripping moisture from air that barely holds any, shedding it in a constant whisper-rain onto moss a foot deep. Under them, dew-lilies open only during the thaw-hour, when the frost lets go of the ground in one long exhaled creak and the whole ecology takes a single bite of the day. Nothing here grows continuously. Everything grows in pulses. The freeze-thaw cycle is the heartbeat, and the plants have all synchronized to it, so that in the thaw-hour the groves seem to inhale — blossoms snapping open, moss lifting, sap audibly ticking up the stems — and then exhale back into stillness as the cold reasserts itself.

There are no animals. None. No birds cross the sky, no insects dust the blossoms, no worms turn the soil. The silence this produces is not emptiness; it is a full silence, a silence with contents. Stand in a combgrove at thaw-hour and listen to what fills it: frost-tick, stem-creak, the drip-count of harvested fog, and underneath everything a tone so low and steady you hear it in your teeth rather than your ears — the sound, the old surveyors guessed, of the Level settling another fraction into its final shape. Life has redistributed the animals' work. Fog pollinates the lilies. Wind and meltwater carry seed. A species of moss migrates in herds — genuinely, in herds, advancing a finger-width per generation across the flats, so that what looks like a grey patch of ground is actually a slow stampede. Death here is not rot. Things die by drying, by sublimation, fading to husk and dust, and the fungal nets beneath the dew-yards — one organism, the mycologists said, older than the city above it — fold the departed back into the common purse.

Ah, yes. The city.

It is easier to call the whole Level a city than to find the city in it. There is no downtown. There are quarters — clusters of dry-stone buildings, turf-roofed, wall-eyed with tiny windows — strung along causeways of fitted slab, and between quarters run engineered wildernesses: hours of empty causeway past cisterns and watch-cairns and dew-fields, all of it built, all of it maintained once, none of it casual. The builders had no metal. Every joint is gravity and patience; every lintel is a chosen stone; every rope on the sealed granaries is twisted grass, and the knots are still holding. The tool marks in the quarried faces are crisp — rows of little percussion scars, fresh as last week's work — and this is the third thing to accept about the Level: those marks are tens of thousands of years old and look new because time, up here, does not flow evenly. It has eroded too, in its way, and it pools. Step into certain basins and your dropped staff takes a beat too long to land; frost melts in minutes that should take hours; a ruin stands half in fast time and half in slow, one wall crumbling, the wall beside it keeping the mason's fingerprints. Near the rim, the surveyors swore, the marks were *re-sharpening*.

Because the fourth thing about the Level, the thing the light's honesty is protecting you from, is that it has worn through. Erosion here went all the way down and came out the other side into earlier drafts of the world. In the hollow places you can look into the ground and see the valley that stood on this spot before the valley — a ghost-stratum of former weathers, laid out like pressed ferns. Travelers describe standing at the lip of one, cold coming up out of it like breath from a cellar, watching an older version of the same plain lie down there under the current one, slightly greener, slightly wrong, its own fog moving in its own patient tide. Some hollows hold deeper layers. Nobody has worked out how many. Nobody has wanted to.

Other failures are smaller and stranger. From the south, a certain tower in the Ash Quarter stands complete; walk around it and you arrive at foundations. Both are true. The city has not decided whether it fell, and revisits the question by angle. Lightning occurs on the Level that climbs from ground to cloud and makes no sound at all — or makes its sound the following dusk, when a low flat rumble crosses the plain and everyone old enough to know checks the sky out of habit and finds it clear. Near the rim, walking straight can return you to your starting point from the opposite direction, gently, as if the Level were turning you like a plate. And the horizon is slightly too high. Not curved — *elevated*, a hand's-width above where geometry permits, the way a very old face is still recognizably a face.

What holds here, reliably, is this: light tells the truth. Cold keeps its schedule. Water honors its two visits a day. Stone forgets more slowly than flesh but it does forget — names wear off things, the surveyors wrote, and then they wrote it again lower on the same stele, thinner, as if practicing for the loss. Hunger is extinct; nothing eats anything quickly, and so nothing has needed to learn fear, and so nothing runs, and so the quiet has never once been broken by urgency. Whatever was going to happen on the Old Level, for the most part happened long ago. What continues is momentum: the fog-tides, the thaw-pulse, the moss-herds, the storms that circle the rim like moths around a lamp, feeding on weather the Level generates out of its own sheer extent — for the plain is large enough to make its own climate, and does, and has, longer than most worlds last.

If anything still tends it, no one has proven it. Once a generation, someone reports a figure standing at the edge of a dew-yard at drinking-hour, upright, patient, gone when looked at directly — and the report is always filed next to the standing stones in the same quarter, which resemble such a figure from the right distance, in the right fog. The Level does not confirm. The Level, as far as anyone can tell, stopped confirming things when it finished eroding.

Stand on it at dusk. The sun sets punctually, honestly, and the frost comes down on schedule, ticking into existence across ten thousand square miles of ancient stone, and somewhere below the rim the cloud-sea begins its rise. The causeways run grey into the distance toward quarters that will drink tonight whether or not anyone is there to drink. Your footprints will keep until noon tomorrow. Behind you, quietly, without malice or welcome, the Old Level resumes the only activity it has left: keeping its small appointments, in the enormous room of itself, waiting with the patience of finished things to be worn through.
