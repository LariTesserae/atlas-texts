First there is the warmth. Before any shape resolves, before your eyes surrender to the dark and begin negotiating with it, you feel it on your face: an even, sourceless warmth, like standing inside a folded coat, like the palm of a closed hand. The air carries no current at all. Nothing moves it. The whole hollow — rock, dust, the pale shapes ahead — sits at one single temperature, top to bottom, wall to wall, so perfectly equalized that the heat has nowhere to rise to and no reason to stir. This is a place without gradients. It is the reason for everything else about it.

Your eyes adjust, and adjust again, and find there is always one more layer of dimness beneath the layer they just mastered. The light here is real but rationed: a dull amber-green radiance that pools around certain stones and fades to a brown shadow — never quite black — at the edges of vision. Trace it back to its sources and you find them clinging to the rocks like skins: a luminous crust, thin as paint, growing over the stone the way lichen grows over a gravestone, except that this glows. It burns nothing. It flickers never. It simply shines, thriftily, spending its light the way misers spend gold, and it has been shining, unbroken, longer than any nation has had a name.

You are underground, though it takes a moment to understand that. The ceiling is low and domed, closer to a chapel vault than a sky. This is a hollow beneath a dry country — a pocket in the rock, perhaps the size of a tithe barn, perhaps smaller; distances are hard to trust in light like this. Long ago, water moved through here. The proof stands all around, dead: calcite formations that grew drop by drop in the wet era, curtain-folded and candle-dripped, each stalactite the fossil of a drop that will never fall. They are finished sculptures now. Whatever carved them packed up its tools and left, and the formations have stood unchanged since, dry as bone, keeping their final shape the way a bell keeps the shape of its last ring.

The floor is the most remarkable surface, and it is the plainest: a fine powder of crushed limestone and salt, packed by sheer millennia of stillness into something with the give of felt. It is soft enough to take a print.

It has never taken one.

Nothing has walked here. No animal lives in this hollow or ever has — no beetle, no bat, no worm in the dark soil of it. There are no burrows, no nests, no bones, no droppings, no track of any kind. The dust lies blank as an unwritten page, and it has lain blank since before your species learned fire. If you step onto it, you will be the first weight it has ever recorded.

The life that does exist here is sparse, pale, and unhurried past all human patience. Scattered across the floor at wide intervals — you could walk twenty paces between encounters — stand the pillar-plants: knee-high, waxen, ribbed like altar candles, grown up out of fissures in the rock. They have no leaves worth the name, only translucent scales, and they do not eat light the way green things do; they sip the rock's own breath, trace minerals and the steady warmth, and pay for their place in the world with almost nothing — a new rib per lifetime, a millimeter of height per generation of your family. Between them run thread-vines, hair-thin filaments strung pebble to pebble and beaded with nodes of the same dim glow, so that looking across the hollow is like looking at a constellation laid out on the ground.

And here is the strangest courtesy the place pays you, the thing you'd notice on your third or fourth circuit: the threads are *rigged*. They are knotted around their anchor stones. They arch where they cross gaps, propped with gravel that someone gathered — that *something* gathered — grain by grain. Belowground, unseen, the roots wedge cracks open with selected pebbles and lever flakes of stone loose, point their growing tips like awls. No mind directs any of it. But the technique is there, in embryo: the beginnings of craft, practiced by organisms, at the pace of geology. Tool-use without a tool-user. It is as if technology itself were a seedling down here, just cracking its shell, ten thousand years from its first thought.

Nothing dies here in the way you're used to. Too dry for rot — decay requires water, and the water left this place before your continent had its current coastline. Instead, when a pillar-plant reaches its end, it dries, mineralizes, and simply remains: a perfect effigy of itself, standing in the row where it stood in life. The hollow holds its ancestors among its living. You cannot tell the congregation from the departed, except that the departed no longer shine.

As for sound: the silence is not empty but upholstered. The dust on every surface kills echo before it is born; a sound here dies the instant it's made, absorbed into powder and rock, so that even your own breathing arrives at your ears muffled and apologetic. Events are measured in centuries. Somewhere a salt whisker on the wall, grown one atomic layer past its limit over nine hundred years of patient accretion, finally shears under its own weight — a *tick*, tiny and clean, the whole news of the epoch. Once in a great while a spore lets go of a vine-node and drifts down through the still air, straight as a plumb line, the only falling thing in the world, and lands without a sound at all.

Understand what the rules of this place are, because it obeys them absolutely. Nothing here is eaten; everything is accrued. Nothing rots; everything turns to mineral. Nothing hurries; the swiftest visible process is dust settling, and dust here settles at the speed of centuries. Nothing is wasted: the hollow balances its ledger to the molecule, trading a whisper of air with the outside world across some hairline fracture, taking in exactly what it loses, holding its warmth, its light-budget, its inventory of salt and calcium in perfect equilibrium — a sealed economy that has run without deficit or surplus since the sea withdrew. Time has no handles here. No days, no seasons, no weather. An hour spent in this room and a thousand years spent in this room would leave identical records. The glow-crust writes its eras in faint rings of brightness, tree-rings made of light, and that is the closest thing to a calendar the dark owns.

And you — you are the one variable. Walk in, and you become the only event. Your breath fogs briefly in front of your face: the first cloud this skyless world has ever seen, a small local weather system, born and dissolved in a second as the parched air and hungry salt drink the moisture out of it. The cave takes your minerals and keeps them. Your footprints will remain in the felt-smooth dust, crisp and permanent, unweathered, until the rock itself is recycled — the first writing the page has ever held, and it will hold it forever, because there is no wind to smudge it, no rain to blur it, nothing that moves.

Then leave, and the dark closes behind you — not like water, for there is no water, but like a door settling into a frame, back into its exact previous shape. The warmth resumes its even hold. The constellation of thread-vines goes on shining at nothing. And somewhere in the black, a salt needle that has been leaning for nine hundred years finally, quietly, lets go.
