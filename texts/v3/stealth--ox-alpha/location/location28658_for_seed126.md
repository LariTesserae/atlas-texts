# The Floor

Come out onto the plain at whatever hour you arrive — the hour hardly matters — and the first thing you will understand is that the light comes from nowhere and everywhere at once. There is no sun. There is the Shell: a luminous ceiling so high that its panel seams vanish into a soft white-gold haze, radiating evenly across four hundred thousand kilometers of engineered ground. The light is warm and thorough. It finds the underside of your chin. Shadows here are faint, multiple, and short, cast by a dozen angled sources at once, so that everything stands in a small crowd of soft dark copies of itself. Twice a day the panel banks shift their angles for redistribution, and for about eleven minutes everything on the plain wears double shadows — two of you, slightly offset, walking slightly out of phase — and then the banks settle and you are one person again.

They call this place the Floor. Not a poetic name. A taxonomic one. Because that is the entire truth of the world: it is floor, all the way to every horizon, and — this is the part visitors take longest to believe — all the way *down*.

The surface itself is nearly featureless in the way that a still lake is featureless: absolutely, and full of implication. Great hexagonal tiles of pale ceramic composite, each hundreds of meters across, fitted with hairline seams of darker material, extend in every direction with a precision no natural process would tolerate and no natural process could undo. Low elevation, and lower still in feeling: the land never rises into anything you'd call a hill. There are shallow basins, gentle ramparts, ramps the size of counties. The air is so clean and so dry that distance stops meaning anything — you can see a station tower eighty kilometers off and it looks like you could reach it before lunch. You cannot. But it takes the eye a long time to accept that, and meanwhile the world keeps looking nearer and more possible than it is.

Now kneel down and put your hand in a seam, and understand where the density lives.

Beneath the tile there is another tile. Beneath that, a service layer: conduits, ducts, dormant rails, rooms. Beneath *that*, another former surface — an older floor, superseded, sealed, preserved intact like sediment. And below that one, another. The world was not built in a day; it was built downward, stratum by stratum, era after era, each generation installing its new ground beneath the last until, some unimaginable span ago, the project met whatever limit projects meet, and stopped. The Floor is saturated. Packed. Every cubic meter of it accounted for, catalogued, occupied by something — structure, archive, machinery, silence. Dig anywhere and you will find floor under floor under floor, going down further than any expedition has returned from. The inhabitants treat the deep levels the way we might treat the ocean: known in principle, visited rarely, dreamed about.

And yet almost nothing moves down there. That is the strangest arithmetic of this place: a world of enormous internal density and almost no apparent life. Vegetation clings to the margins of things. In the seams grows the seam-black — a lichen, though the word is a courtesy, a vitreous black mat a few millimeters thick that drinks the Shell-light directly and pays it back as a faint trickle of charge into the tile grid. It grows maybe a handspan a year. Where two seams cross, you sometimes find the silver shrubs: waist-high constellations of fine filament, leafless, trembling slightly, harvesting stray potential from the ground. They do not seed or flower as far as anyone has recorded. They simply accumulate, slowly, the way dust does, and occasionally one is gone, harvested or collapsed, and a century later another stands nearby.

Fauna is rarer still, which is why every sighting is an event worth writing down. Pale motes ride the thermal columns where waste heat breathes up through the vents — flat, translucent grazers the size of your palm that drift in slow flocks of six or nine, scrubbing biofilm off the tiles, utterly silent. Deeper in the seam networks live the siphon-worms, segmented and patient, farming the lichen gardens they will never leave. And once — if you are lucky, once in a season of walking — you will see a Curator: a large, pale, four-legged shape moving along some ancient circuit at a fixed unhurried pace, tending, adjusting, reading the ground. Nobody knows if the Curators are animals, machines, or the descended middle of some spectrum we no longer have words for. They do not acknowledge you. They were here before the concept of acknowledging, probably, and will be here after.

Because here is the thing about age on the Floor. Erosion did not break this world. Erosion *finished* it.

There is no rain, no frost, no river — the word *water* survives only as a mythological term, something children learn alongside ghosts. What would have been rivers are the Channels: broad, mirror-smooth troughs cut into the plain, running straight for hundreds of kilometers, holding nothing at all. You can walk down into one and feel the temperature drop by two degrees, walk along a channel-bed as dry and smooth as the inside of a bell. Nothing flows in them. What erosion means here is polish. Ten million years of contact, of the Small Work — the maintenance swarms, insectile glints perpetually moving a handwidth above the surface — passing over every edge, every corner, every inscription. The result is that the whole world has arrived somewhere. Edges are beveled into soft gloss. The makers' original markings survive only where someone thought to lay protective glass over them, and those protected patches now look impossibly sharp, violently new, like wounds. Everywhere else, intention has been worn round. This is what a thing looks like when it has completely happened.

Do not mistake finished for dead. The Floor is profoundly busy; it is simply busy *quietly*. Watch the horizon at the right moment and you may catch a Leveling: a correction front, kilometers wide, where the plain re-trues itself — a barely visible line of subtle brightness sweeping toward you and past, the tiles beneath your feet adjusting by microns, imperceptible except as a faint warmth through your boot soles. At dusk — that is, at the Dimming, when the Shell steps its output down through amber into a deep administrative blue, because night here is a policy and not an astronomical fact — stand still, and listen as hard as you can.

You will hear, if the air is right, ticks. Thousands of them per second across the continent, each one the tiny thermal adjustment of an expansion joint, each one audible for only a meter or two around itself. Standing alone on the darkening plain, you hear a sparse, random patter of ticks all around you, close and intimate, coming from everywhere and touching only you.

It is the nearest thing this world has to rain. The station folk say their ancestors stood like this on real nights, under real clouds, and listened to water fall, and that the feeling is the same. Whether or not that's true, they stand anyway, every Dimming, in the warm — the ground gives its stored heat back all night through the vents, a slow geological exhalation, so that the Floor is never cold, has perhaps never once been cold — and they listen to the tick-rain, and watch the Shell's blue settle, and look out at a country so old it has come all the way around to stillness.

Somewhere out there, a Curator is walking its circuit. Somewhere below you, a hundred forgotten floors hold their breath of stored data. The lichen advances a fraction of a millimeter. A mote flock turns above the Channel, all together, like a thought changing direction.

Nothing ends here. That was the whole design. It only continues, at its own pace, in perfect silence, finished — and tending itself, carefully, forever.
