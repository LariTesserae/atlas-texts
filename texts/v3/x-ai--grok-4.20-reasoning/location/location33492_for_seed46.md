**The Embryonic Geode**

You stand inside a single crystal pocket no larger than a modest hill from the outside, yet here the curvature of space has been pried open like a geode split by forces that have not yet learned the word “limit.” The walls are visible in every direction—vast, inward-slanting planes of smoky quartz and raw amethyst that rise so high the eye doubts them. Their facets catch and multiply the light, turning the entire chamber into one slow, breathing lens.

The temperature is vicious. Heat radiates from the floor in slow, muscular pulses, as though the stone itself is having a fever dream. There is no water, not even a memory of it. The chemistry here is anhydrous and sulfurous; the air tastes of hot iron and static. What little atmosphere exists is a thin, dry plasma that shimmers like oil on glass.

Yet the place is lush.

From the raw, newborn substrate—black basaltic glass still puckered and half-molten—erupt colossal mineral growths. They are not plants. They only wear the silhouette of vegetation the way a cathedral wears the silhouette of a forest. Branching lattices of tourmaline and bismuth, fractal thickets of copper silicate that flare into emerald-green feathers the size of ship sails. Some rise thirty meters before their own weight fractures them into new, slower geometries. Their surfaces are covered in millions of microscopic blades and vanes that catch the light and turn it into shifting moiré patterns, so the “forest” appears to breathe even though nothing moves faster than geology.

The growths are sparse. Between each colossal thicket lie wide, empty plazas of fresh crust, still cooling from whatever cataclysm birthed them. You can walk for long minutes without seeing another living crystal. The emptiness feels intentional, like a workshop that has only received its first few tools.

There are no animals. Nothing that could be called fauna has yet coagulated. The only motion is the landscape learning how to become a landscape.

Reality itself is coming apart in slow motion.

In places the air has begun to clot into half-formed industrial ghosts—skeletal outlines of gears that are not metal but compressed probability, vast piston-like columns that rise and fall one micron per decade, cathedral-sized turbines whose blades are merely suggested by ripples in the light. These are not built. They are precipitated, the way frost precipitates on a window. The geode is dreaming an industrial age before it has invented biology. The mechanisms have no purpose yet; they simply exist, humming at frequencies so low they register as pressure against the teeth rather than sound.

The light is disturbingly normal—clear, warm, slightly golden, as though a sun just past noon hangs somewhere above the crystal ceiling. No one can locate its source. Sometimes the light forgets which direction down is and falls sideways for an hour, casting shadows that crawl like spilled ink.

Sound is rare. Mostly there is the profound, bone-deep quiet of a place that has not yet learned how to have weather. Every few hours a crystalline spire somewhere far off reaches a stress point and fractures with a sound like a distant church bell struck once and then muffled under velvet. The echo takes a long time to die. Occasionally the half-formed machines emit a single low clank, as though clearing a throat the size of a mountain, before relapsing into silence.

Everything is still emerging.

New growths bud from the floor in slow motion—tiny, dagger-like prisms that will one day be redwoods of vanadium and spinel. The walls themselves are not finished; in places the quartz simply stops, as though the geode grew tired and decided to continue later, leaving raw voids that look wet only because the light refracts strangely. Time here is patient and viscous. A single thought can outlast the formation of a new crystal facet.

You are inside the seed of a world that has not yet decided what it will be. It has borrowed the shapes of jungle, factory, and cathedral all at once, but none of them have won. The air tastes of lightning that hasn’t happened yet. The crystal trees keep growing, the ghost machines keep dreaming themselves, and the enormous lens-walls watch without blinking, waiting for the next slow fracture that will let something new be born.
