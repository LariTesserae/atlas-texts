**The Hollow Liturgy**

You stand in a place that has never known a sky.

The air is cool and heavy, almost oily, like the inside of a mouth that has not drawn breath in ten thousand years. Condensation gathers on every surface—not rain, exactly, but a slow, patient sweating of the world itself. It collects in the deep grooves of carvings, in the hollows of stone eyes, along the lips of vast funnels cut into the floor, then slides in thin silver threads toward reservoirs that never quite overflow. The water smells of wet limestone and iron and something faintly electrical.

There is no ground that was not made.

Every inch—floor, wall, ceiling—is worked stone. Not hewn blocks laid in courses, but a single continuous mass that has been laboriously shaped by hand and simple metal over uncountable generations. The builders never stopped. They are still not finished. The place is embryonic and total at once: a continent-sized embryo carved from the inside out. Where one crew finishes a frieze of interlocking spirals, another crew, three chambers away, begins knocking fresh voids into what had been solid rock only yesterday. The sound of their labor is the heartbeat of the world.

It is loud.

Hammers fall in staggered rhythms—*tok-tok-tok* becoming *TOK-tok-tok-tok* as the echoes learn to speak to one another across vaulted distances. Chisels sing. Wooden wedges driven by mallets crack along predetermined fault lines with the wet sound of breaking bone. These noises layer and interfere until the darkness itself seems to throb. Sometimes the workers chant in a language that feels older than the stone they cut. The chants are not for beauty. They are measurements.

Density is absolute. Nothing is wasted. Colossal pillars rise every twelve paces, so close that you can rest your shoulder on one and still touch the next with outstretched fingers. Between them hang bridges, counterweighted galleries, spiraling staircases that corkscrew around nothing, and tiered balconies that serve no obvious purpose except to be seen by something that has never possessed eyes. Every surface crawls with relief carving: geometric mandalas that do not repeat, nested arches that twist in ways that make the stomach uneasy, long processions of faceless figures carrying smaller versions of the very structures they walk upon. The recursion is so deep that after a while you begin to suspect the carvings are carving the carvers.

Light is almost extinct. What little exists is stolen or grown. Here and there, colonies of pale marrow-fungus (the only living concession to the parameter of vegetation, and a grudging one) emit a cold, bluish marrow-light so weak it only reveals the texture of the stone immediately in front of it. Workers carry small clay lamps burning a pungent oil rendered from the fat of blind cave eels. The flames are the color of old teeth. Most of the time even these are absent. The true inhabitants have learned to read the architecture by touch and by the way sound bends around corners. They navigate the dark the way fish navigate currents.

Fauna is rare but not absent. Things move at the edge of hearing: the soft click of jointed legs on stone, the wet slap of something heavy sliding into a reservoir, a distant lowing that might be an animal or might be a structural joint settling under impossible weight. Once in a great while you glimpse a pale, elongated creature the locals call a *tongue-of-the-work*. It has no eyes and no mouth you can recognize, yet it seems to taste the freshness of new cuttings, following the scent of freshly broken rock the way a wolf follows blood.

The temperature never changes. Cool. Constant. The kind of cold that makes breath visible for a few seconds before the dark swallows even that proof of life.

Elevation is low. Everything feels pressed beneath an inconceivable mass of overhead stone, yet the ceilings sometimes soar so high that thrown stones take four heartbeats to strike the floor and the impacts sound like distant bells. You are simultaneously deep underground and inside a valley that has been turned inside-out. The paradox is ordinary here.

This is an active, unfinished god.

The entire structure is a single devotional act stretched across millennia. The builders do not worship something that exists; they are building the thing they will one day worship. Every new chamber, every added buttress, every recursive carving is a syllable in an argument that has not yet reached its conclusion. When the last surface is worked, when every possible void has been given shape, the god will open its eyes.

Until then the hammers fall. The water beads and drips. The pale fungus glows its feeble blue. The *tongue-of-the-work* slides through the dark tasting tomorrow’s stone.

And the place keeps emerging, dense and loud and relentlessly, religiously, *made*.
