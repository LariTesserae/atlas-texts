**The Nidus**

It is smaller than a grave and older than breath.

You find it only by accident, following a hairline fissure that opens like a reluctant eyelid in the wall of a young basalt trench. The fissure is no wider than a wrist. You slide through and the world behind you forgets you ever existed. There is no surface above, no floor below—only this pocket, this sealed alveolus no larger than a modest room, entirely filled with water the color of quenched iron. The water is so dense it feels like cold blood still remembering it was once alive.

The walls are newborn stone, sharp enough to cut thought. They have not had time to forget the violence of their birth. Fractures stand out like fresh knife wounds, edges glassy and black, meeting at angles that refuse Euclidean comfort. One corner simply *stops* existing for a moment if you stare too long; the geometry heals itself with a lazy, guilty slowness. This is a place where reality has not yet finished knitting. The rock still dreams.

Every available millimeter is occupied. There is no water without life and no life without water. A single slow current—the only current—moves in a spiral so vast and patient it completes one revolution every few decades. In its wake it leaves filaments, fronds, lattices, veils. The vegetation (if the word still applies) is not planted. It is *expressed*. Pale, almost translucent structures that look like the love-child of glass sponge, nerve tissue, and hoarfrost. They grow in impossible density, braided so tightly that the water itself is forced into microscopic capillaries between them. Some of these structures are soft as lung. Others have edges that sing against the skin like obsidian. All of them move, but only just—breathing on geological timescales, exhaling faint chemical ghosts that taste of iron and forgotten rain.

There is almost no light. What little exists is not light at all but a kind of reluctance to be completely dark. A faint silvering at the edge of perception, perhaps the memory of photons that drowned here when the pocket first formed. In that non-light the vegetation reveals itself as faintly luminous in colors that have no business underwater: corpse-moth white, bruise-lavender, the particular green that appears in dreams just before waking. These colors do not illuminate. They confess.

Fauna is rare and aristocratic. Once every few years a blind translucent isopod the length of a fingernail drifts through the thicket like a thought reconsidering itself. Occasionally a ribbon of something that might once have been an eel folds itself through the lattice, moving with the resigned patience of a saint. They do not hunt. There is nothing to hunt. They simply *are*, and their passage is registered by the vegetation as a kind of slow, courteous applause that takes days to finish.

Scattered among all this are the Built things.

Not many. Never many. A perfect calcium hexagon no larger than a coin, embedded in the wall like a tooth. A spiral of silica that suggests—without quite becoming—a tiny staircase. A hollow sphere of something like pearl that contains another, smaller hollow sphere, and inside that a third that is somehow *older* than the first two. These are not artifacts. The pocket made them the way a fever makes visions. They are reality’s baby teeth.

The sound is almost entirely the sound of listening. A sub-audible creak as the young rock settles another hair’s width. The microscopic click of chemical bonds deciding to become something new. Once, over the course of a month, there was a low note like a glass harmonica played underwater—beautiful, unbearable, and then gone. The silence afterward felt embarrassed to have been caught making noise.

The rules here are still being written, and the ink is cold.

Gravity is optional in certain corners. A loose filament may hang motionless for a generation, then suddenly remember it should fall—upward. Cause and effect sometimes lose track of one another and have to be reintroduced. If something dies (rarely), its shape remains for a long time, outlined in negative space by the vegetation that refuses to grow there, as though the pocket is reluctant to admit the loss. Entropy has been bribed. Complexity increases. The longer you remain, the more the distinction between “you” and “the Nidus” begins to feel like a negotiable suggestion rather than a law.

This is not an ecosystem. It is a thought that has not yet decided whether it wants to become a world.

And it is still young.

The sharp walls may one day blunt. The packed life may one day thin. The pocket may one day rupture and spill its half-formed dreams into larger seas. But not yet. For now it floats inside the crust like a secret, cool and dark and saturated, a place where reality is still soft enough to take the shape of whatever brushes against it.

Stay long enough and it will begin to take your shape.

Best not.
