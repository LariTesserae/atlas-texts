**The Hollow Bloom**

You stand on the lip of a living shelf that did not exist an hour ago.

The air is bright enough to hurt, yet it carries the taste of iron and cold stone. Every surface glows with its own milky, merciless light—veins of luminous sap threaded through translucent leaves the size of ship sails, clusters of fist-sized photophores pulsing in slow, tidal rhythm. The light is cold and shadowless. It reveals everything and forgives nothing.

This is not a cave. It is a wound.

Some planetary catastrophe—recent, violent, still echoing in the flex of the crust—ripped open an interior cavity so vast it breeds its own weather. The ceiling (if such a word still applies) vanishes behind drifting banks of condensation cloud thirty kilometers overhead. From those clouds fall thin, intermittent rains that flash into sleet before they reach you, hissing as they strike the living floor. Moisture condenses on every cold surface; it drips, beads, freezes into knife-edged hoarfrost along the margins of every leaf and root. The world is damp without being wet, perpetually on the edge of freezing.

And the plants have taken everything.

They do not grow *on* the stone. They *are* the substrate. Colossal rhizomes thicker than cathedrals snake through the emptiness like suspension cables for a bridge that was never finished. Between them hang curtains of pale, waxen foliage that ripple slowly though there is almost no wind. In places the vegetation has folded itself into natural vaults and spiraling staircases, into balconies and buttresses and half-formed halls that look deliberately architectural yet are clearly accidental—mere expressions of vegetative hunger. Where two rhizomes cross, they graft together into knots the size of villages, their surfaces patterned with breathing pores that open and close like slow lungs.

The density is deceptive. Step twenty paces from the shelf and the plant mass falls away into a gulf so enormous your eyes refuse it. The opposite “shore” is a faint green wall on the edge of visibility, fourteen kilometers distant across nothing but glowing air. Between these rare living continents the space is near-void. A single spore drifting on an updraft can travel for days before it finds purchase. Sound itself falls into the emptiness and is swallowed; the world is mostly quiet even while it seethes.

Because the vegetation is turbulent.

It grows at speeds the eye can track. You can watch a new tendril uncoil from a parent stalk, thicken, split, and begin budding secondary leaves in the time it takes to draw three careful breaths. Elsewhere entire platforms die in sympathetic waves—leaves blackening, collapsing into fragrant dust that is immediately reabsorbed by fresher growth pushing up from beneath. The floor you stand on is only a few days old. In another week it may have become a soaring vertical thicket or a collapsing bridge into the abyss. The entire biosphere is in a state of permanent Solaris-like frenzy: creation and destruction braided so tightly they cannot be told apart. Yet the violence is hushed. The loudest sounds are the soft *pop* of frost fracturing along a new leaf-edge, the wet sigh of a collapsing gallery far away, the occasional glassy chime of ice-rimed fruit dropping into darkness.

Fauna is vanishingly rare. Once in many hours you might see a pale, membranous thing the size of a handkerchief glide on thermals between two distant rhizomes, trailing feathery feeding tendrils. Or a clutch of translucent beetles no larger than rice grains, moving in perfect synchrony across a glowing petal. They feel less like animals and more like mobile organs of the greater vegetable mind.

There are people here, but barely.

Scattered across the more stable platforms—those rare places where the plant-mass has achieved temporary equilibrium—you find their traces. A bone needle. A rope bridge spun from braided root-fiber, already half-rotted and swaying over an abyss. A low shelter woven from still-living leaves that have been patiently trained into walls. Their technology is paleolithic at best: sharpened silicate flakes pressed into root handles, sinew, carved fungal lamps, the occasional precious flake of meteoric iron beaten cold. They do not build so much as negotiate with the vegetation, coaxing it into useful shapes before it changes its mind and eats their work. Their markings are faint—shallow spirals cut into luminous bark, already healing over.

The strangeness is not loud. It accumulates.

Sometimes the growth pauses. Every leaf on a platform will turn in unison toward a single point in the void, as though listening. At other times the luminous veins pulse in complex standing waves that resolve, for a few heartbeats, into what look like schematic diagrams or the shadowed suggestion of enormous faces. The cold itself feels watchful. Condensation gathers on your skin in perfect droplets that refuse to fall. And every so often the entire cavern experiences a slow, organ-like contraction—kilometers of plant mass tightening at once—shifting the clouds, squeezing out a brief, silent flurry of luminous pollen that drifts like snow across the abyss before the world relaxes again.

This is a young place. The wound is still closing. The plants are the scar tissue—ferocious, luminous, unstable—trying to knit a planet back together from the inside. They have not yet decided what shape the scar will take, or whether anything else will be allowed to live inside it.

You are standing on the edge of a question the world is still asking itself.

And the question is growing while you watch.
