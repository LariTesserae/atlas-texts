**The Lattice of the Slow Engines**

You stand inside something that is no longer a place but a body. The scale is that of a cathedral nave folded in on itself, yet every cubic meter is saturated. There is almost no air that does not belong to a leaf, a root, a pipe, or a piston. The boundaries are soft and treacherous: walls of living wood and dead iron press so close that to move is to negotiate, never to stride.

The ground is not ground. It is a mattress of rhizomes, some as thick as a man’s thigh, braided through collapsed catwalks and rusted grating. Where metal still shows, it is the color of dried blood and lichen. Water does not flow here so much as sweat. The atmosphere itself condenses; warm, mineral-scented mist beads on every surface and runs in slow, deliberate tears down stalks, along the seams of boilers, and from the undersides of vast, leathery leaves that serve as ceilings. The sound of dripping is constant and loud—hollow, metallic, choral—echoing through the tight chambers until it feels like the entire structure is clearing its throat.

The vegetation has won so completely that it has begun imitating what it consumed. Great strangler figs have swallowed entire steam engines; their aerial roots now act as drive belts, flexing with glacial patience around flywheels that still turn, one revolution every four hours. Pipes have become vascular tissue. Valves open and close on their own, triggered by turgor pressure rather than steam. Where once there were factory floors, there are now layered mats of liverworts and bromeliads so dense they form living floors that sigh and rebound underfoot. Everything is green, rust, and the wet black of peat.

Light arrives filtered and normal in its color, yet strange in its behavior. Shafts of warm daylight pierce the upper canopy in rare places, but most illumination is second-hand: it diffuses through translucent membranes of giant leaves and through clouds of condensing vapor, arriving as a thick, honeyed gold that makes every droplet glitter. Shadows are fat and slow.

The machines are not dead. They are molting. Old turbines, half-digested by fungal enzymes, still hum with a deep, sub-audible throb—power drawn from gradients of heat between decaying matter and living tissue. Every few hours a distant pressure valve, overgrown with orchids, releases a jet of warm mist with a sound like a factory whistle played backward through a throat made of moss. The noise is loud enough to make the leaves tremble, yet the motion is so gradual that you can watch a single vine complete its contraction over the course of a morning.

Fauna is almost absent. A few pale millipedes the length of your forearm move like priests, tracing the same devotional circuits they have followed for decades. Tiny translucent frogs no larger than thumbnails live inside the water-filled cups of bromeliads that grow from the eye sockets of abandoned welding masks. That is all. The ecosystem has no need for animals; the plants trade sugars and electrochemical signals directly along fungal highways that run through the metal like nerves.

There is a strangeness deeper than the fusion of industry and jungle. The entire Lattice appears to be a single clonal organism that learned factory logic the way a child learns a song. It remembers production quotas in the slow swelling of certain seed pods. It maintains “shifts”—periods of accelerated sap flow that last weeks, followed by long dormancies. If you remain still long enough, you can feel the whole chamber breathing: a soft, patient expansion and contraction that takes twenty-six minutes per cycle.

Rules of the world emerge only by staying:

- Nothing here is quick. Even violence is geological. A breaking branch may take three days to finish its fall.
- The air is warm, heavy, and sweetly metallic. Breathing too deeply leaves a taste of iron and chlorophyll on the tongue.
- The Lattice is in balance. It does not expand outward; it densifies inward, tightening its own guts, adding new layers of root and pipe in perfect equilibrium with decay.
- Artifacts remain: a foreman’s brass whistle fused into a flower that only opens when the pressure valves sing; rows of riveted iron pews now used as trellises; a control room whose gauges have become fleshy dials that pulse with sap pressure.
- Sound is exaggerated by the tight, wet spaces. Every drip, every creak of wood expanding in the warmth, every distant metallic sigh is amplified until the Lattice feels like a constant, slow-motion argument between its two natures.

You understand, gradually, that this is not ruin overtaken by nature. It is a single, centuries-long act of digestion in which the factory was never allowed to die. It was simply taught a new, vegetable way of being industrial—patient, packed, weeping, loud, and alive beyond any reasonable definition of the word.

The slow engines turn. The leaves sweat. The Lattice dreams in revolutions per decade.
