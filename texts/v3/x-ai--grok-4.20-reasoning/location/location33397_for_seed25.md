The floor is moving.

Not in metaphor. The pale, damp limestone undulates underfoot like the hide of something breathing, because it is covered—ankle-deep, knee-deep in places—by a living pelt of creatures. They spill over one another in slow, glistening tides: translucent millipedes the length of your forearm, eyeless isopods the size of dinner plates, pale spiders whose legs interlace so tightly they form living carpets. When your weight shifts, the surface gives with a wet, collective hiss. Then the hiss is swallowed by the sound.

The sound never stops. It is not noise; it is pressure. A braided roar of clicking, scraping, stridulating, wet burbling, and a low, bone-deep thrumming that might be millions of wings beating in the upper darkness or might be the cavern itself. The air vibrates so heavily that after a few minutes your teeth ache and your vision blurs at the edges. Every surface reflects the din; every stalactite and flowstone ridge has been shaped by eons of condensation and the ceaseless abrasion of tiny bodies. The stone itself feels polished to a dull, almost fleshy smoothness, as though the planet had been slowly licked clean by time and hunger.

There is almost no green. No leaves, no moss you would recognize. What grows here grows in secret chemistries. Thin sheets of white and sulfur-yellow fungus coat the rock like spilled wax, interrupted by bacterial mats the color of old bone that drink iron and manganese from seeping water. These are the only stationary lives. Everything else moves. The food chain is not a pyramid; it is a knot. The smallest creatures rasp at the fungal sheets or at each other. Larger ones arrive in peristaltic waves, devouring the smaller ones, then are devoured in turn by translucent centipedes the girth of a man’s thigh whose venom leaves glowing blue footprints that fade after an hour. Nothing wastes. Even the dead are partitioned before they hit the ground; smaller things ride the bodies of larger ones like remoras, nibbling as they go.

Water is never far and never abundant. It gathers in shallow, skin-warm lenses between folds of stone, or condenses on the unseen ceiling far overhead and falls as slow, deliberate rain—fat, cold drops that strike the living carpet and make it seethe. In places the water pools deeply enough to form blind, silver fish that nose along the bottom in total darkness, their skins so thin you can watch their hearts beat. These pools are fiercely guarded. Anything that approaches triggers a sudden silence in a ten-meter radius, followed by a rush of wet bodies that sounds like surf.

The cavern stretches beyond any reasonable horizon. In the near distance rise low, rounded ridges of flowstone, worn into soft breasts and shoulders by a million years of drip and crawl. Between them lie basins where the animal density becomes almost solid—living hills that shift and sigh and occasionally collapse with a noise like wet velvet tearing. Farther on, the darkness swallows everything except the bioluminescence that drifts through it: chains of soft blue lanterns along the flanks of enormous millipedes, sudden green sparks where venom meets air, pale orange pulses from colonies of luminous rotifers that ride the backs of larger hosts like saddle blankets. These lights never last. They flare, are eaten, flare somewhere else. The effect is that the entire plain appears to breathe with cold fire.

Elevation is low, almost intimate with the world’s bones. The air stays cool and heavy, thick with the mineral reek of wet limestone, chitin, and ammonia. There is no wind, only the slow convection of millions of breathing bodies and the occasional downdraft of colder air from unseen shafts high above. Sometimes the ceiling—if it is a ceiling—releases a deeper sound: a hollow, bell-like groan that rolls across the dark for long seconds. When it does, every creature pauses for half a heartbeat. Then the roar resumes, louder, as though the interruption itself must be shouted down.

Rules emerge slowly, felt in the body before they are understood.

Nothing here is solitary. Even the largest predators move in loose, rippling constellations of smaller followers. To be alone is to be meat. Light is both lure and liability; anything that glows too steadily is swarmed and dismantled within minutes. Sound is territory, food, and warning all at once. The deafening chorus is not chaos. It is negotiation. Shift the pitch of your own voice and the carpet around you will ripple away, leaving a brief, astonished circle of bare stone that is immediately refilled.

The longer you remain, the more the place resolves into a single, vast organism whose organs are themselves ecosystems. The fungal sheets are its skin. The pools are its eyes. The living carpet is its tongue. And you, standing inside the roar, are briefly an irritant—something new, something not yet incorporated.

Then the carpet begins, gently, to climb your boots.
