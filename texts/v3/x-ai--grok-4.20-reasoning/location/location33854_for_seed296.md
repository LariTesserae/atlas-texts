**The Shroudlands of Veyr-Alt**

You do not arrive here so much as you are slowly admitted. The final ascent is not dramatic; there are no knife-edge ridges or triumphant summits. Instead the land has been worn to a kind of exhausted dignity, the way an old jaw loses its teeth and settles into a stubborn, rounded silence. These mountains are older than weather. They predate the very idea of sharp peaks. Time and ice and wind have planed them down to a high, brooding plateau that still sits, impossibly, at extreme elevation—thin air, heavy sky, lungs that never quite fill.

Yet the plateau refuses to be barren.

A constant, low-grade dampness moves through everything. It is not rain so much as the memory of rain: condensation that beads on every surface, mist that pools in the lee of ridges, sudden pockets of standing water cupped in stone basins that have held them since the world was young. Occasional true rain arrives sideways, almost shy, then vanishes for weeks. The water never dominates. It simply refuses to leave.

Because of that water and the strange atmospheric trap created by the ancient height, the vegetation is shockingly lush. Not jungle—too cool for that—but a dense, layered, moss-drenched temperate riot. Knee-deep carpets of emerald and charcoal moss swallow every footstep. Ferns the size of small trees arch over narrow lanes. Conifers grow in tight, dark columns, their needles so thick they turn the already dim light into a perpetual dusk. Vines, heavy with tiny white flowers that open only when no one is looking, bind the branches together until entire hillsides appear upholstered. Everything touches everything else. The density is almost indecent; you cannot push through a stand of trees without brushing half a dozen different species. The air itself feels furred.

And yet the animals are almost gone.

You will see a pale moth the color of old bone, or a flight of minute crimson beetles that move like spilled blood across wet stone, but the larger fauna departed long ago. Perhaps they were never very numerous. The land feels chosen by plants and stone, not by things that bleed. The only frequent motion is vegetal and geological: roots prying apart foundation stones, moss slowly drowning iron rails, entire ridges inching downhill a centimeter per century in a process so patient it feels like breathing.

Into this ancient, saturated density the city was built.

**Veyr-Alt** does not crown the plateau; it is sutured into it. The city is industrial the way a jaw is industrial—functional, heavy, slightly terrifying in its persistence. Six and seven story brick-and-basalt factories rise directly from living rock, their lower floors half-swallowed by moss and dripping condensation. Narrow gauge rails, black with age, disappear into tunnels that predate the city itself. Vast condensation towers—half cathedral, half machine—stand at the edges of districts, harvesting the perpetual mist into water for the forges. Their engines are old, oily, and strangely quiet; the whole city runs on a low, constant thrum that feels more like a heartbeat than machinery. You can stand in the middle of a factory square and still hear individual water droplets striking leaves.

The streets are not streets so much as cuts. Terraced, stepped, overbuilt, and then overgrown again. Iron walkways span crevices where groundwater trickles endlessly. Tenement towers lean against one another like tired giants, their rooftops turned into hanging gardens so dense they sag. Every surface is either rust, moss, wet stone, or black iron. The color palette is restricted and fungal: deep greens, wet charcoals, the occasional vicious orange of rust or warning paint. Light is always indirect, always filtered. On the rare days when the cloud ceiling breaks, a weak silver sun makes the city look like a daguerreotype of itself.

The strangeness is not loud. It is in the way certain districts have been abandoned to the vegetation for so long that the buildings no longer look built—they look *secreted*, as if the moss and ferns grew them. It is in the echo profile: sound dies quickly here, swallowed by the dense air and denser growth, yet every few weeks the entire plateau produces a single, perfect, bell-like tone that lasts exactly nine heartbeats. No one knows why. The factories have simply incorporated the tone into their shift whistles.

The rules of this place are patient and merciless.

Nothing is ever finished. The ancient erosion continues at a speed that makes human lifetimes irrelevant; buildings sink, foundations heave, entire neighborhoods are slowly folded back into the plateau over generations. The vegetation is faster than law. If you stop maintaining a structure for three years, it becomes a thicket with architectural bones. Industry continues because it must—there are minerals in these primordial stones that do not exist at lower elevations—but the city has the feel of something that has already outlived its original purpose and is now simply metabolizing, like the moss.

At night the dimness becomes absolute. The condensation towers glow with weak sodium lamps that look like captive moons. Workers move between them in long coats heavy with moisture, their lanterns making slow comets through the dripping dark. Somewhere a water wheel turns with the sound of wet stone chewing itself. A night-blooming creeper releases a scent like cold metal and funeral lilies. A single pale moth drifts past, heading toward some unknown congress in the deeper moss.

This is not a place that welcomes you.

It is a place that has already decided, over geological time, exactly how much of you it will allow to remain. Most of what you are—your noise, your haste, your illusion of permanence—will be gently, relentlessly, beautifully erased. What is left will be damp, quiet, layered, and older than you have any right to be.
