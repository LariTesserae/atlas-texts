**The Hollow of the Weeping Sisters**

You stand inside a single vast wound high in the bones of the world.

The cirque is no more than three hundred paces across at its widest—an alpine cathedral no larger than a hamlet, yet its walls climb so steeply that the eye loses itself in vertical distance. This is the source of the emptiness: the floor is small, the living things upon it are sparse per cubic meter, but the *volume* is enormous. Most of the space is air, cold and echoing, held between sheer planes of stone that have been weathering in the same posture for twelve thousand years. The mountain has settled into its final shape. Erosion is no longer violent; it has become devotional.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. It does not arrive as a river. It *arrives as breath*. The atmosphere itself condenses on the cold rock faces in slow silver beads that grow, tremble, and fall. Every stratum weeps. Long dark streaks of manganese and iron oxide run down the cliffs like the tear-trails of giants, and from a thousand invisible fissures issue filaments of water that braid, separate, and braid again. By the time they reach the floor they have become a hundred separate voices—some the size of harp strings, some thick as a wrist—feeding a chain of shallow, peat-black pools linked by bright rills. The air is so saturated that your hair is wet within minutes and your breath condenses in small clouds that drift upward like secondary weather.

The vegetation is lush but strangely placed, as though the mountain itself decided where green was allowed. On the floor, in the few places where scree has become soil, grow dense mats of moss so thick your boots sink to the ankle, miniature forests of fern, and low thickets of mountain willow whose leaves are the color of tarnished bronze. Higher up, the vertical gardens begin: cushions of saxifrage and pink primrose cling to the wet rock wherever a ledge offers purchase. From a distance the walls look pied—stone and hanging green in equal measure. Nothing grows tall. The trees, where they occur at all, are ancient and stunted, their trunks spiraled by decades of wind until they resemble the horns of unknown animals.

Yet the place is loud.

The constant dripping is only the bass line. Above it rides an astonishing chorus of fauna. Choughs with scarlet beaks wheel through the open column of air, their calls multiplying against the stone until it sounds like a thousand birds instead of forty. Smaller alpine accentors dart between the hanging gardens, singing in bright metallic trills that seem to come from inside the rock itself. Marmots posted on boulders emit piercing whistles that ricochet for seconds. At dusk the bats emerge from hidden fissures in such numbers that their wingbeats sound like heavy rain. Predator and prey move continuously; you can watch a kestrel stoop, hear the sudden silence when everything living holds its breath, then the explosive resumption of sound once the kill is made.

There is something unusual in the acoustics. The cirque focuses sound the way a lens focuses light. Certain frequencies return changed—deepened, layered, almost *answered*. The locals (the few who still come) say the mountain remembers every voice that has ever spoken here and sometimes loans those voices back when it chooses. Whether that is true or not, you will catch yourself turning toward a sound that feels half a conversation.

Scattered across the floor and low ledges are the marks of people who lived with that belief. Their technology was simple: antler, stone, copper worked cold. Here a circle of upright stones no taller than your waist, each carved with the same spiral that mirrors the wind-twisted trees. There a platform of fitted slate where offerings of antler and dried flowers have been left so long they have grown into the moss. A single bridge of braided sinew and cedar still spans one of the wider rills, so fragile it might be a century old or ten centuries; no one can say. These things are not ruins in the usual sense. They feel like punctuation the mountain has decided to keep.

The light is normal mountain light, but the walls frame it theatrically. Morning arrives as a slow tide of gold that descends the eastern face, igniting every bead of water into a constellation. By midday shafts stab downward through gaps in the rim, illuminating single patches of moss or a lone twisted tree so brightly they appear to burn. In late afternoon the mist thickens again; the condensing atmosphere turns the entire hollow into milk and silver, and the weeping walls disappear into soft absence until only sound remains.

It is always active. The water never stops arranging itself. The birds never stop arguing. The mist breathes in and out twice a day like a slow lung. Even the rock is not finished; every few hours you hear the flat *tick* of a single pebble falling from somewhere high, striking a ledge, then silence again. The mountain is not crumbling. It is polishing itself.

You feel, standing in the middle of that hollow volume with wet hair and ringing ears, that you have entered a place that has already reached its final form. Everything here—stone, water, moss, bird, forgotten spiral carving—is exactly where the long conversation between them decided it should be. Nothing is missing. Nothing is crowded. The emptiness itself is part of the balance, the vast cold air between presences the very thing that lets the few voices present become so loud.

This is the Hollow of the Weeping Sisters.  
It does not need you.  
It will speak whether you listen or not.
