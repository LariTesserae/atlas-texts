**The Weeping Lattice**

It has no proper name, only designations that fail. The locals—if any could survive here—might call it the *Nascent Floor*, or the *Underforging*. It is low, almost impossibly low, a single continental basin sunk so deep that its own curvature becomes the horizon. Everything is built. There was never a natural substrate; even the idea of “bedrock” is a later invention imposed by minds that needed comfort. The ground is courses of cyclopean brick and rough-hewn basalt blocks, each the size of a village hall, fitted with primitive iron pins thicker than a man’s torso. Tool-marks are everywhere—chisel scars, wedge-holes, the spiraling grooves left by hand-cranked drills the size of redwoods. The labor that placed them must have been titanic, stupid, and patient beyond reckoning.

Yet the work is not finished. That is the first thing the place tells you: it is still *emerging*. Whole districts remain embryonic—scaffolds of lashed cedar trunks (somehow transported here across emptiness) rise in skeletal lattices that climb thousands of feet before vanishing into the dim. Rope bridges thick as galleon hawsers sway between incomplete piers. In places the brick courses simply stop, raw edges exposed like broken teeth. Elsewhere the structure has begun to dismantle itself again, as though the blueprint keeps revising.

The heat is ferocious, 2.6 in the old scale, a dry, mineral roar that makes the air feel like opening an oven. Yet the atmosphere is wet. Condensation is constant and perverse. Every surface weeps. Moisture beads on the hot stone in fat, trembling globes the size of fists, then runs in perfect braids down vertical faces before evaporating halfway, only to re-form lower down. The air itself seems to sweat; walk fifty paces and your lungs feel soaked. Shallow pans and troughs—clearly deliberate features of the design—hold steaming alkaline water that never quite boils and never quite cools. These are the only “lakes,” and they are rare. Between them the terrain is sparse, almost lonely. Kilometers of bare constructed plain lie between one half-built ziggurat and the next. The emptiness is not absence; it is negative mass, a pressure that makes every finished object feel like an event.

There is no vegetation. Not even the fungal crusts or chemical gardens that sometimes colonize dead worlds. What grows instead are *patterns*. In certain seams between blocks, fractal accretions of silica and iron oxide bloom in perfect spirals that mimic leaf veins or fungal mycelium, yet they are pure geometry—cold, repeating, non-living. Touch them and they ring like glass. Leave them alone and, over days, they migrate a hand-span, re-rooting elsewhere as the underlying stress patterns shift.

No animals either. Nothing that breathes. The only motion is architectural.

The Lattice is turbulent. Sections rise and sink on a scale so slow it feels geological, yet it is clearly deliberate. You can stand in one place for an hour and feel the bricks beneath your boots lift a fraction, then settle with a sound like a mountain clearing its throat. Distant towers visibly tilt, correct themselves, then tilt again. Sometimes entire galleries fold upward like paper, reconfiguring into staircases that lead nowhere, before collapsing back into rubble with a noise too deep to be called sound—more a pressure on the inner ear. The world is building and unbuilding itself in the same breath, a Solaris of masonry.

Light is dim, sourceless. A uniform umber-red glow seeps from within the stones themselves, as though the bricks contain trapped sunset. There are no shadows exactly, only regions of deeper saturation. The sky—if it can be called a sky—is a constructed vault so high that perspective collapses. It appears as hammered sheets of corroded copper riveted to an unseen frame. Condensation drips from its seams in perpetual slow rain that evaporates before it ever reaches the Floor. The droplets fall for miles, shrinking, until they become mist that drifts sideways in thermals of its own making.

Sound is mostly quiet. The turbulence is too vast for ordinary noise. What you hear is intimate: the constant *tick-tick-tick* of water beads racing down stone, the soft hiss where they flash into steam, your own pulse in your ears. Only rarely does the deeper architecture speak—low, grinding moans when two continental plates of brickwork shear past one another, or a single enormous pin snapping somewhere beyond the haze. These noises arrive hours after the event that caused them, as if sound itself must walk the same impossible distances as everything else.

And then there is the reality breakdown.

It is worst at the edges of vision. Walk toward what looks like a finished colonnade and the columns will drift apart, increasing in number, until you are moving through an infinite regress of identical pillars. Look down and the mortar lines sometimes spell words in languages that have not been invented yet. Stare too long at any repeating pattern and it begins to *watch back*. On certain days—though there are no days—the geometry simply forgets itself. A staircase you climbed an hour earlier now descends in a spiral that violates every rule of handedness; your left foot becomes your right foot by the time you reach the bottom. Whole districts phase, becoming translucent, revealing other versions of the Lattice stacked beneath or beside this one, each at a slightly different stage of construction or decay. Sometimes you see figures moving in those phantom layers—humanoid, perhaps, laboring with primitive tools—but they are only pressure shadows cast by the stress between realities. They cannot see you. You hope.

This is a made place that was never completed, and now the making has become a kind of endless, mindless metabolism. It generates its own weather: banks of superheated fog that roll for hundreds of kilometers, sudden upwellings of steam that birth temporary lakes, thermal columns so strong they bend the copper sky. It is hot, wet, empty, and ceaselessly revising itself with the patient stupidity of a god who only ever learned one skill—*to build*—and has been left alone with that skill for geological ages.

Stand still long enough and you can feel it deciding what to do with the patch of pavement beneath your feet. The bricks are warm. They are sweating. They are listening.

There is nowhere to go that is not still under construction. There is no final form. Only the Weeping Lattice, turbulent and quiet, vast and low and dim, forever emerging from its own ruins, building its own grave and then refusing to lie down in it.
