**The Hollow Lattice**

You are far beneath any memory of sky. The crust above you is not a ceiling but a thickness—kilometers of it—yet the space it encloses is so immense that it generates its own sluggish convection of warm, bone-dry air. This is not a cave system. It is a world that happens to be inside another.

The rock is young. Everything still wears the sharp signature of its making. Vast planes of obsidian-black feldspar rise in knife-edged fins that intersect like the struts of a cathedral built by geometry itself. Between them bristle dense thickets of acicular crystal—millions of translucent spears no thicker than a finger, packed so tightly that in places they form walls you cannot push a hand through without drawing blood that will never quite heal the same way again. The angles are merciless. Every edge catches what little radiance exists and kills it.

There is no water. None has ever existed here. The chemistry is anhydrous and ionic; minerals migrate through solid lattices by thermal diffusion and quantum tunneling rather than dissolution. The air itself is a heavy, inert soup of argon, nitrogen, and trace sulfur hexafluoride that carries sound poorly and heat too well. It tastes of hot iron and old lightning.

Light is an event, not a condition. Most of the volume is absolute dark. Only scattered colonies of chemoluminescent salts—deep violet and corpse-green—stain the crystal tips, so faint that after an hour your eyes begin to doubt them. The glow moves, but only when you are not looking directly at it.

And yet the place swarms with life.

They are not animals. The word fails. The Liths, if one were forced to name them, are living mineral lattices that have achieved locomotion and something like purpose. Some are small—hand-sized many-legged prisms that click faintly as they rearrange their internal bonds, forever adjusting their positions by fractions of a millimeter per hour. Others are enormous. You will round a crystal buttress the size of a cliff and suddenly understand that the cliff itself is watching you with a million microscopic fractures that function as eyes. They do not hunt. They do not breed. They simply *are*, in numbers that feel impossible, layered in three-dimensional herds so dense that in certain chambers the air itself seems crowded.

The silence is not empty. It is saturated. Every few minutes you hear a single crystalline *tink*—the sound of two lattice-creatures brushing one against the other and politely realigning. Nothing more. No wind. No dripping. No breath except your own, which begins to feel like an obscenity after a time.

Scattered across this endless interior are the Works.

They appear without pattern. A single low dome built of fitted hexagonal crystal plates, no larger than a shepherd’s hut, its interior containing nothing but a perfectly smooth bowl carved by hand tools of native copper and flint. A ring of seven obelisks, each incised with the same spiral groove that never quite completes its turn. A bridge that spans nothing, leading from one razor ridge to another, its parapets worked in primitive but patient reliefs of angular figures that might be the Liths themselves. The craftsmanship is early-metal at best—cold-hammered, fireless, reverent. Whoever made them is either gone or has become indistinguishable from the living stone. You never see them building. The Works are simply *there*, as permanent as the equilibrium that governs everything.

And that equilibrium is the deepest law.

Nothing changes.

The temperature remains 41 °C forever. The faint lights never brighten or dim. A Lith that is standing in a certain attitude will stand in that same attitude until the end of time; if you topple it, others will slowly, over weeks, migrate into new positions that restore the original statistical distribution. Energy does not flow. It balances. The entire volume exists in a strange, warm stasis that feels like the moment just after creation and just before death have cancelled each other out.

This is not peace. It is saturation.

The longer you remain, the more the Lattice offers its strangest gift: the slow realization that your own atoms are not fundamentally different from the crystals around you. Your heartbeat begins to feel like an imposition. Your thoughts start to echo with the subsonic groan that sometimes travels through the stone—an ancient, patient frequency that may be the voice of the world itself, counting something too large to name.

There is no decay here.  
There is no succession.  
There is only the dense, warm, razor-sharp, never-changing abundance of what already is.

Welcome to the Hollow Lattice.  
Try not to disturb the equilibrium.  
It remembers.
