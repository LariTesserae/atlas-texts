**The Condensate**

You stand at the bottom of the world after everything else has worn away.

The land is low and open to the point of vertigo—flattened by inconceivable age into a single, horizon-wide plane that still somehow feels cramped. Every square meter is occupied. The surface is a dense mosaic of primordial stone forms: rounded, liver-smooth lobes of metamorphic rock, interlocking like the knuckles of a single fossilized hand. They have been eroded for so long that they have settled into their final, almost architectural arrangement—tightly packed, puzzle-locked, with no wasted space. Between them run millions of shallow, interconnecting channels no deeper than a finger, yet they form a single continuous water system that covers the entire visible world. This is not a marsh. This is a mineral plain that has learned how to weep.

The air itself is wet. Cold, dim, supersaturated. Moisture condenses on every surface the moment it appears, including your own eyelashes. Rocks sweat. The mist beads, gathers, runs, drips, collects in basins, evaporates back into the fog, and begins again in an endless, loud, mechanical cycle. There are no plants. Not a blade of grass, not a lichen, not even the usual black crusts of extremophile life. The only growths are crystalline—fractal lattices of quartz and amphibole that bloom in slow motion from fissures, looking less like vegetation and more like the exposed circuitry of the stone itself.

Yet the place swarms with fauna.

They are everywhere, packed into the same saturated geometry as the rocks. Most are the color of wet slate and frost. Some are small, scuttling things that move like spanners and ratchets, clicking across the stone with precise, industrial rhythm. Others are house-sized and move with the ponderous inevitability of continental plates. Their bodies are not flesh in any familiar sense; they appear to be living mineral accretions, animated by whatever leaks through the seams where reality has worn thin. You see a herd of them now—thirty or forty—flowing between the rounded lobes in eerie synchrony. One suddenly folds inside out without breaking stride, becoming a hollow silhouette that continues walking. Another gives birth by fission, splitting into two perfect copies that immediately begin arguing in a language of subsonic grinding.

The sound is constant and deafening.

It is not mere noise. It is industrial without machines. The land itself produces a deep, rhythmic thump, as though titanic engines were buried kilometers down and operating at the pace of geology. Every drip is amplified by the fog into a wet slap. Stone plates occasionally lock or unlock with a sound like freight cars coupling. The creatures add their own layer—metallic scraping, wet bellows, harmonic shrieks that linger too long and then duplicate, spawning secondary sounds that hunt the originals. The cumulative roar is so thick it feels like a second atmosphere. You can lean against it.

Light is thin, sour, directionless. It arrives already tired, filtered through perpetual banks of condensing cloud that glow a bruised, metallic gray. Shadows behave wrongly. Some trail behind their owners like oil. Others race ahead and arrive before the thing that cast them. At irregular intervals the reality breakdown becomes locally acute: a patch of ground will stutter, repeating the last three seconds of motion like a jammed cog. A creature will freeze mid-stride, its body vibrating at impossible frequency until it either shatters into glass butterflies or simply forgets it was ever solid and pours itself into the nearest channel as living slurry.

This is the oldest stable state of a world that was never young in the way we understand. Erosion has won so completely that the land has achieved a kind of exhausted perfection—perfectly flat, perfectly packed, perfectly ancient. And yet it remains ferociously active. The water cycle, the mineral metabolism, the endless migration of the not-quite-biological herds, the periodic fracturing and re-knitting of local physics—all of it runs like a factory that has outlived its operators by geological epochs. There are no artifacts. There never were any. The industrial character is emergent. The world itself learned to manufacture motion, sound, and repetition long after anything that might have built it had dissolved into the same weeping stone.

If you stay long enough, the rules will begin to apply to you.

Your memories will smooth and round at the edges, losing detail until they fit the final form. Your pulse will slowly synchronize with the deep tectonic rhythm. Water will bead on your skin and refuse to fall. You may look down one day and realize your hands have taken on the same liver-smooth curvature as the surrounding rock, as though you too are being lovingly, relentlessly, eroded into the last stable shape this reality can hold.

The Condensate does not end.  
It has simply reached the point where ending is no longer necessary.
