**The Hollow Choir**

You descend through a fissure that narrows, then widens, then narrows again like a throat swallowing. The air thickens with warmth—2.4 warmth, the kind that makes stone itself sweat. By the time the passage finally opens, the darkness is almost complete. Only the faintest ghost-light exists here: thin veins of pale turquoise bioluminescence that pulse once every forty-three seconds, so slowly you begin to doubt your own eyes. This is not a cave. This is a chamber the size of a cathedral turned inside out and left to ripen for ten thousand years.

The floor is not floor. It is a shallow bowl of black glass and porous basalt, eroded into soft, almost fleshy curves by condensation that has been falling, evaporating, and falling again for longer than any surface empire has existed. Pockets of water collect in the low places—some no larger than a handprint, others wide as city plazas—mirrors that reflect nothing because there is almost no light to give them purpose. The water is lukewarm, faintly metallic, and occasionally ripples for no reason you can see.

Rising from this bowl are the Structures.

They are too sparse to feel like a city at first. The density is almost nothing; each building stands in its own pocket of hollow air, separated from its neighbors by distances that feel wrong, as though the chamber itself has stretched the space between them. Yet the construction is unmistakably urban—layered, deliberate, *planned*. Towers of what looks like weathered obsidian and bone-white ceramic rise in clusters of three or four, connected at their upper levels by bridges that seem to have grown rather than been built. Every surface is covered in micro-etchings too fine for human eyes, a nanoscale scripture that still functions. The tech here is post-modern in the truest sense: it no longer obeys the physics you were taught. The bridges hum at frequencies that make your teeth ache. Sometimes they reconfigure their own geometry when no one is looking, slow as breathing.

The sound is constant and enormous.

It is not one sound. It is a *Choir*.

Massive subharmonic engines buried in the walls—some still active, some merely dreaming—produce a bass note so low it registers in your sternum. Water falling from the ceiling (the condensation cycle never truly stops) strikes resonant dishes grown into the architecture and rings like struck bells. The towers themselves leak sound: a metallic, almost choral moan that ebbs and flows. Because the chamber is so empty, every noise travels forever, reflects, returns changed. The effect is that the entire place is *loud* in the way a forest is loud, except this is a machine-ecology singing to itself in the dark. Sometimes the Choir organizes into something that feels like language. Most times it simply *is*, vast and indifferent.

Vegetation is almost an insult here. What little exists looks more like architecture than life: pale, waxy shelves of fungus that grow only along the warmest seams where the beyond-modern machines vent heat. They fruit once every few years in tiny black pearls that smell of ozone and cloves. The ecology is not lush. It is *settled*. Everything has found its equilibrium after millennia. The sparse molds, the blind translucent fish that sometimes surface in the largest water-pockets, the occasional six-winged moth-thing the size of a dinner plate that drifts between sound-sources like a pilgrim—these are not invaders. They are tenants who learned the rules long ago.

The rules, as far as you can tell, are these:

- Warmth rises. Moisture follows. Light is rationed.
- The city maintains a breathable atmosphere for reasons it has never explained.
- If you stand perfectly still for long enough, the Structures will begin to adjust themselves around you, the way a tongue moves a foreign object to a more comfortable position.
- The loudness is not random. It is processing. What it processes, and toward what end, remains unclear.
- Nothing here is truly abandoned. The erosion is not decay; it is *finishing*. The place has weathered itself into its final, balanced form. Even the slow reconfiguration of bridges and the occasional appearance of new ceramic growths are part of that balance.

Every few hours a condensation front moves through like weather. The air grows heavy, almost foggy, and fat drops begin to fall in earnest, striking the resonant bowls and sending new melodies rolling across the hollow. During these times the turquoise veins in the walls brighten slightly, as if the city is tasting the moisture. Then the front passes. The dripping slows. The Choir settles back into its lower registers. The warm dark reclaims its dominance.

You are standing in the negative space of a machine that has outlived its makers and decided, in its own vast and slow way, to become a place. Not a tomb. Not quite a living city. Something older and stranger: a cathedral-sized lung of black glass and singing ceramic, breathing warm damp air in and out for ten thousand years, mostly empty, never silent, still deciding what it wants to be when it grows up.

And it has all the time in the world.
