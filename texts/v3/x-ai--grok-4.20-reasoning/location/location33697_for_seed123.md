**The Condensery**

You step through a fissure no wider than a doorway, and the world outside ceases to exist. The crack seals behind you with the finality of a closing jaw. This place is no larger than a generous warehouse, yet it feels boundless in its inwardness—like a single breath held for a thousand years. A pocket that should not be, embedded in the bone of young stone.

The walls are savage. Fresh black basalt and fractured quartz rise in vicious angles, edges still sharp enough to cut memory. They have not had time to grow kind. Water does not flow here so much as *remember* flowing: it condenses in slow silver beads along every razor ridge, gathers, trembles, and falls in fat, deliberate drops. The air is warm, almost mammalian, 1.7 warmth wrapped in damp velvet. Each breath tastes of iron, ozone, and something faintly sweet, like overripe battery acid.

The floor is almost nonexistent. Every centimeter is claimed. Industrial ruins have been crushed together with living matter into a single dense knot. Rusted pipelines braid themselves around one another like mating snakes; corroded turbines sit half-buried in black moss; a single derelict pump house leans in the exact center like a crooked tooth. Nothing stands alone. A fallen catwalk has become a bridge for translucent millipedes the length of your forearm. Valve wheels are encrusted with pearl-like fungal growths that open and close in time with some internal rhythm. The few surviving gauges still twitch behind cracked glass, needles shivering at numbers that no longer mean anything.

Vegetation is sparse but maliciously efficient. There are no soft leaves here. Instead, black-glass grasses grow in hairline fractures, their edges metallic. Colonies of liverwort the color of fresh bruises cling to anything warm. And then there are the *threads*—long, vein-like filaments, neither root nor wire, that pulse faintly and connect machine to stone to creature in one continuous circulatory system. Where they touch metal, the metal heals. Where they touch flesh, the flesh learns to rust beautifully.

The fauna is everywhere. You cannot move without disturbing something. Clouds of thumbnail-sized moths with wings like etched copper foil rise in synchronized pulses. Fat, eyeless salamanders the color of motor oil lounge inside the warm throats of abandoned boilers, their gills fluttering in time with the faint electrical hum that never quite dies. Above them, things that might once have been bats hang in dense curtains, their bodies segmented like articulated armor. When they shift, you hear the soft click of microscopic gears.

Everything hunts and is hunted in miniature. A centipede whose every segment ends in a tiny sparking contact will stalk the copper moths. The moths, in turn, lay eggs inside the salamanders. The salamanders are eaten by the bat-things, who shit nutrients back into the threads. The threads feed the moss, which insulates the machines, which stay warm enough to keep condensing the water that falls as the only rain this world will ever know. A perfect, horrible, intimate circle.

The sound is mostly quiet, but never silent. It is the sound of a sleeping engine dreaming. Constant soft dripping—like a thousand tiny clocks. The occasional sigh of steam escaping a cracked fitting. A low electric purr that seems to come from inside the rock itself. Sometimes the entire Condensery shudders with a deep, biological clunk as some ancient pump down in the foundations kicks on for three seconds, moves a few liters of fluid, and falls back asleep. The creatures have learned to time their lives to these pulses.

Light is normal, even gentle—diffused daylight falling through a single jagged wound in the ceiling far above, crossed by slowly turning ventilation fans that have not moved in decades yet still creak forward one degree every few hours. The light catches on every wet surface and fractures into rainbows thin as razor wire.

This place is young. It tore itself open only recently, perhaps days ago by the sharpness of the stone, perhaps centuries ago by the slowness of the things that live here. The rules are simple and absolute:

- Nothing leaves.
- Everything is used.
- The machines are not dying. They are molting.
- If you stay long enough, you will begin to condense too.

Already you can feel the first beads of moisture forming on the small hairs of your arms. They do not feel like sweat.

They feel like the Condensery learning the shape of you, deciding where you fit in the knot.
