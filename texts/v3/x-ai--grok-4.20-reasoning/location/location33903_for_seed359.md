**The Chamber of Unfinished Becoming**

You stand inside a space no larger than a cathedral nave crossed with a walled grove—perhaps eighty paces across at its widest, though the precise measure slips if you try to hold it. The ceiling arches low enough that its weight feels personal. Everything here is *made*. There is no natural substrate, no untouched earth or native stone; every surface, every particle, was shaped by hand-tools and simple metal chisels in an age so deep it has forgotten its own beginning. Yet nothing is old. The edges remain knife-sharp, the reliefs crisp, the corners still bright with the memory of the mallet. The world is young in the way a wound is young—freshly opened, still deciding what it will scar into.

There is no water. Not a drop, not a vapor. The air itself is dry as baked bone, warm (almost feverishly so), and carries a faint mineral dust that tastes of iron and distant myrrh when it touches the tongue. Life here abandoned aqueous chemistry long ago. What grows instead is lithovore and geometric: dense, near-touching, layered fronds of crystalline matter that push from the constructed walls like slow coral. Their “leaves” are translucent planes of carnelian and pale sulfur-yellow, edged with fractal teeth that catch the light and split it into colors that have no business existing. Some of these growths mimic ferns, others resemble antlers or folded prayer scrolls, but all are mineral through and through. They increase by infinitesimal accretion—atom by atom—over decades. The process is so patient it registers less as growth and more as *remembering*. Where two fronds nearly touch, faint sparks of violet static dance in the narrow gap, a sign that local reality is straining.

The builders (if they were ever separate from what they built) worked with primitive persistence. Every surface is carved: not merely decorated, but *composed*. Walls, floor, and vaulted ceiling are one continuous relief of interlocking spirals, nested mandalas, and half-finished faces whose eyes have not yet decided which direction to look. The density is almost claustrophobic. You cannot take three steps without your shoulders brushing living stonework. The air itself feels crowded, saturated with presence.

Yet the place is mostly quiet. Sound dies quickly here, swallowed by the porous mineral lattice. Your own footsteps arrive muffled, as though the floor is politely lowering its voice. The only regular noises are soft, infrequent clicks—like a fingernail tapping once against porcelain—when a crystal frond finally shifts position after years of pressure. Sometimes a deeper, almost sub-audible thrum rises, the sound of the Chamber slowly inhaling. It breathes on a scale of days.

Fauna is abundant but behaves with the same deliberate slowness. Small creatures the locals (if any still existed) might call *chisel-mice* move across the reliefs. They are not animals in any fleshly sense; each is a mobile knot of animated carving, no larger than a fist, whose limbs are repeated ornamental scrolls. They browse the lithovore growths, rasping away microscopic layers with diamond-hard tongues, polishing the fronds into new and more intricate patterns. Larger beings drift between the dense thickets—slow, mantis-like things of smoky quartz and tendon-stone whose every movement takes minutes. Their compound eyes are perfectly cut rubies. When they pause, they become indistinguishable from the ornamentation, as though the Chamber itself is thinking through them.

Light arrives at normal intensity but from no single source. It simply *is*, a warm, even radiance that feels like late-afternoon sun trapped inside an amber bead. The light has weight. It presses gently against the skin.

Reality here is badly frayed. The weirdness is not spectacle; it is a low, constant nausea of wrongness. Angles do not add to 360 degrees if measured twice. A pillar that appears to support the ceiling from one side reveals, when you circle it, that it is actually descending from above like a stalactite. Walk toward what looks like an open aisle between two colossal carved trunks and you find the aisle has become a wall behind you. The Chamber is still deciding its own floor plan. Sometimes entire sections of relief slide upward like slow elevator cars, carrying their clinging chisel-mice and crystal fronds with them, only to settle again hours later in a new configuration. The changes are so gradual they feel courteous.

There is a rule, though it is never spoken: nothing may finish. Every face on the walls lacks at least one feature. Every frond ends in a ragged crystal edge that clearly intends to become something more elaborate. The chisel-mice are eternally polishing and never completing. The Chamber was built as an act of endless devotion by a culture that understood perfection would be death. So it keeps itself forever young, forever sharp, forever becoming. To remain is to participate in that becoming; the longer you stand here, the more you feel certain small details of your own body— the whorl of an ear, the line of a knuckle—have grown slightly more intricate, slightly more *carved*.

The air is warm. The silence is warm. The slow, dense, impossible garden of stone and living geometry is warm.

And it is watching you with the patient affection of something that has all the time in the world to decide what you will become part of.
