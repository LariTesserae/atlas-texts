**The Hollow Nave**

You step through a living sphincter of braided roots and the sound hits you like a physical blow. It is not one sound but a thousand braided into a single, ceaseless roar: the wet clicking of beetle legions, the metallic screaming of unseen cicadas, the low brass bellowing of something throat-like hidden inside the vaults, the endless dripping of water magnified by hollow wood until it sounds like hammer strikes on cathedral bells. The noise is so total that after a few heartbeats your own pulse begins to feel like an intruder.

The space beyond is cathedral-scaled yet intimate, no larger than a modest town square but so vertically vast that the mind keeps rewriting its sense of proportion. A single living structure fills every boundary. There is no soil, no stone, no dead matter; every surface is plant, grown so ancient and so interlocked that it has achieved architecture without intention. Massive columns—once separate trees—have fused over centuries into fluted pillars thick as grain silos, their bark carved by time and symbiotic fungi into repeating fractal reliefs that refuse to resolve. Between the pillars, buttresses of woven vine and aerial root form arches that rise, curve, and then deliberately contradict themselves, looping back into knots that should not bear weight yet do.

The floor is a slowly breathing lattice of exposed roots, each as wide as a cart. Between them lie black mirrors of still water—condensation basins, not rivers—where moisture exhaled by the upper leaves has gathered and refused to sink further. These pools tremble constantly with the vibration of the roar above. Occasional fat drops fall from the distant canopy-ceiling, each one detonating like a struck gong when it hits the root-floor.

Light arrives in thick, dusty shafts. Normal sunlight, yet altered. It passes through overlapping membranes of living leaf that function as stained glass: translucent emerald, blood-red vein, and a violet so deep it feels ultraviolet. Where the beams strike the root-floor they ignite tiny galaxies of mites and midges that glitter like metallic dust. The light moves; the membranes above open and close in slow, tidal rhythm, so the entire nave breathes between brilliance and gloom every few minutes.

This is the contradiction the place demands you accept: it is overwhelmingly vegetal—vegetation at 2.6—yet the volume itself is almost empty. The center is a hollow nave of astonishing air, a near-void where only sound and flying creatures move. All the density is pushed to the edges and overhead, where the plant mass has compacted into city-like strata. There are “streets” of fused trunk and liana forming layered galleries along the walls. There are “balconies” of bracket fungus large enough to stable horses. There are “spires” that are actually upward-growing root cones, their tips exploding into flower chandeliers that drip nectar in steady golden strings. The longer you look, the more the architecture reveals itself as deliberate, as though the vegetation spent three thousand years imitating a civilization that never existed.

And it is moving.

Vines thick as thighs creep visibly, millimeters per breath, knitting new bridges between columns. Orchids the color of raw meat bloom, scream with perfume, then rot and collapse into black slime within the same hour, feeding the next generation already swelling beneath them. In the root-floor, trap-bladders the size of barrels gulp air and small creatures with wet smacking sounds. Predator-prey cycles are not hidden; they are theatrical. A flock of translucent moths suddenly ignites into blue flame as hunting bats descend in a spiral; the flame is not fire but a chemical defense that makes the bats’ echolocation scream in audible pain. The roar of the place momentarily spikes into something that hurts the teeth, then settles back into its usual oceanic violence.

The animals are not visitors. They are organs. Bright scarlet millipedes the length of your arm run root-roads in perfect formation, their legs ticking like water clocks. Tree-frogs with skin like polished jade perch inside resonant cavities and add tuned percussion to the wall of noise. Something like a sloth, but segmented and many-headed, hangs from the ceiling vaults and slowly rotates, drinking condensation from its own fur. None of them fear you. You are simply another vibration moving through the body of the place.

Reality here is thin, worn through by sheer biomass and time. Look too long at any repeating pattern in the bark and the pattern begins to echo behind your eyes when you blink. Walk ten paces and the quality of sound changes as though you crossed an invisible membrane; one moment the roar is outside you, the next it is inside your bones and you are outside it, listening to your own skeleton conduct the symphony. Small eddies of air move independently of the larger currents, carrying scents that evoke memories you have never lived. At the edges of vision, the columns appear to breathe—expanding and contracting by fractions—though when you stare directly they freeze into perfect stillness, pretending to be architecture again.

There is no weather as such, only the internal climate of a single vast organism. Warm mist condenses on the high leaves, runs along veins, gathers into the black pools, evaporates under the next shaft of light, and rises again in an endless private hydrological cycle. Rain happens in localized drips, never everywhere at once. The temperature never deviates from the slow, mammalian warmth of living tissue.

This is an established place. It has found balance. Erosion and growth have negotiated a treaty measured in centuries. The root-floor is so densely interwoven that nothing can truly fall through; the ceiling membranes are so thoroughly reinforced by secondary trunks that nothing can truly collapse. The system has become its own monument.

And still it hungers for more shape. Every few hours a new arch extrudes itself somewhere overhead with a sound like wet canvas tearing. Every few days a pillar splits, revealing a fresh interior gallery lined with white flowers that open only for the deaf.

You are standing inside a living city that was never built, only grown. A cathedral that prayed to itself until its prayers took physical form. A grove that swallowed its own boundaries and kept expanding inward.

The roar never stops. The light keeps shifting. The roots keep breathing beneath your feet.

And the longer you remain, the more you feel the place considering you—not with mind, exactly, but with the slow, enormous curiosity of something that has had ten thousand years to perfect the art of being both empty and completely, suffocatingly full.
