**The Condensate Works**

You descend a gentle, almost imperceptible slope into a low basin no wider than a large hamlet. The land has been worn for so long that the distinction between geology and engineering has vanished. Concrete footings bleed into bedrock; rusted I-beams have become ribs of the earth itself. Every edge is rounded, every surface planed by ten thousand years of identical drips. The place is not ruined. It has simply settled into its final, perfect shape.

The air is cool and heavy. A constant, skin-prickling dampness clings to everything. Water does not flow here in rivers or pools that change; instead it condenses in slow, deliberate beads along every pipe, every lintel, every motionless gear. The droplets trace the same paths they have always traced, collecting in identical iron troughs before evaporating again in precise rhythm with the machines. Sometimes a thin, soundless rain falls straight down for exactly eleven minutes, then stops. The schedule never alters.

There is no vegetation. Nothing green has ever been permitted. In place of plants, the structures wear thick crusts of something between fungus and mineral: matte-black mats shot through with metallic threads that do not grow or shrink. They simply *are*, like lichen frozen at the exact moment of maximum equilibrium. Occasionally a crystalline structure blooms from a joint—delicate, glass-like lattices that resemble frost but are warm to the touch. They too remain unchanged from one century to the next.

The industrial complex itself is sparse yet unmistakably urban. Six major factory cathedrals stand in a loose ring, each the size of a gothic nave, separated by wide, echoing plazas of bare concrete. Pipes and walkways cross between them like bridges between islands. Everything is layered construction: original brick swallowed by later steel, which in turn is half-absorbed by still-later ceramic and alloy. The buildings are not maintained and they are not decaying. Rust has reached its ordained pattern and halted. The place has achieved a state of eternal “enough.”

Normal, steady light falls from above, though no sun is visible. The sky is a uniform pearl that gives the same shadow angle at every hour. Beneath this light the true inhabitants reveal themselves.

The fauna is overwhelming.

They are not animals in any sense you recognize. They are *the Works*. Millions upon millions of pale, jointed creatures—some no larger than a fingernail, others the size of oxen—move in continuous, perfectly choreographed tides. Their bodies are chitin and stamped metal fused at the molecular level. Wings of hammered foil whirr in perfect phase, creating a secondary roar beneath the machines. Legions of them flow along the same routes they have followed for millennia, polishing, tightening, replacing, harvesting. When one dies, its carcass is immediately dismantled by others and fed into hoppers. The birth rate and death rate are identical to nine decimal places. Nothing accumulates. Nothing is ever lost.

Louder than the creatures are the machines. The sound is constant, cathedral-filling, and perfectly unchanging: a layered thunder of pistons, turbines, drop hammers, and vast unseen bellows. The noise does not rise or fall. It simply *is*, the same chord it has sounded since the first gear turned. You feel it in your teeth, in the small bones of your ears, until the vibration begins to feel like your own pulse.

This is the central law of the place: nothing ever changes.

The Condensate Works reached equilibrium an age ago and has been locked there ever since. The same widgets pour from the same chutes at the same rate. The same condensation forms and is reclaimed. The same number of pseudo-insects pollinate the same fungal mats that feed the same larger constructs that service the same engines. Time passes, but the state does not. Entropy was solved here. The system is closed, self-repairing, and utterly indifferent to visitors.

If you stand still long enough, the tide of metallic creatures simply flows around you, adjusting their paths by the exact millimeter required to keep every circuit intact. Touch a pipe and three dozen small servitors appear to restore the temperature gradient you disturbed. Leave an object behind and it will be catalogued, weighed, and incorporated into the Works within the hour—replaced by an identical object of slightly different patina so the overall mass balance remains perfect.

The place is cool, loud, damp, and older than any calendar. It is not dead. It is not alive. It is finished. And because it is finished, it will continue exactly like this until the last proton decays, humming, dripping, clicking, crawling, producing nothing that leaves the basin and requiring nothing that enters it.

A small, self-contained, industrial eternity.
