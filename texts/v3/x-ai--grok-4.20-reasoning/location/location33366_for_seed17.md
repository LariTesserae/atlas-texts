**The Still Cry**

You float in a cold that feels absolute. Not the sharp bite of ice, but a thick, patient cold that has settled into perfect equilibrium, as if the universe itself has exhaled and refused to inhale again. The liquid around you is not water. It is heavier, clearer, almost oily in its refraction, a vast planetary ocean of cryogenic perfluorocarbon born in the planet’s violent youth. It carries sound like a struck bell carries vibration: forever.

This is the upper stratum — “high” only in the sense that the pressure here will not instantly pulp you. Below, the column grows monstrous. Above, there is no surface, only a slow brightening where the liquid thins into a hazy, luminous roof that diffuses a cold, even daylight across thousands of kilometers. The light is pale silver-blue, normal in brightness yet alien in its uniformity. There is no sun, no sky, only this steady, sourceless glow that has never changed.

The roar never stops.

It is deafening in the literal sense: it deafens you to everything else. A constant, planetary-scale thrum of industrial machinery operating in absolute stasis. The sound comes from everywhere and nowhere, layered, textured, almost geological. You feel it in your bones before your ears can parse it — a choir of turbines, solenoid banks, mass drivers, and liquid pistons all locked in the same eternal cadence. The machines have been running without variation for so long that the roar has become the atmosphere’s fundamental frequency.

They appear rarely.

In this near-void immensity, density is almost nil. You may travel for days through empty liquid before anything interrupts the blue-silver nothing. Then one will loom: a single industrial complex the size of a mountain range, half cathedral, half refinery, all sharp angles and black alloy. No rust. No decay. The structures are young — recently emerged from whatever automated forges first birthed them — and the liquid has not yet had time to blunt their edges. They float anchored by cables that descend into abyssal darkness, or hover in precise buoyancy, processing the cryofluid itself for elements that only exist in trace quantities. Pipes the width of highways inhale, distill, roar, and exhale in an unending loop. Nothing grows on them. There is no vegetation, no biofilm, no soft life at all. The machines remain mathematically clean, eternally new.

The fauna keeps its distance from you but is abundant across the scale of the world.

They are titanic. Kilometer-scale silhouettes that drift through the emptiness like slow zeppelins of flesh and articulated shell. Their bodies are pale, almost translucent, threaded with metallic lattices that drink the machines’ electromagnetic leakage. Some look like eels grown to the size of freight trains. Others resemble vast, many-jointed crabs suspended in the liquid, their limbs barely moving, riding the acoustic pressure waves the way a sailboat rides wind. They sing into the roar — deep, organ-like tones that ride beneath the mechanical thunder without ever altering it. Their migrations are fixed. Their numbers are fixed. Their hunger is fixed. The entire ecology is a closed loop that achieved equilibrium long ago and has never since been disturbed. They avoid the newest machines but orbit the ancient ones, feeding on the rich chemistry that bleeds from every exhaust port.

The mineral world is sharp and young.

Where the seafloor rises — vast submerged ranges thrust up in the planet’s recent formation — the rock is glassy, fractured, vicious. Black basaltic blades and obsidian cliffs cut upward for kilometers, their edges still keen. Erosion has barely begun. Between these rare ridges the liquid simply continues, empty, pressurized, stable. There are no seasons, no currents that matter, no meaningful gradients. The temperature does not fluctuate. The light does not dim. The machines do not slow.

This is a world that has been finished.

Whatever civilization built the machines achieved its final purpose and vanished, or perhaps transcended, or simply walked away once the system became self-sustaining. The planet accepted the installation the way a body accepts a pacemaker that never fails. Now everything — the cold, the roar, the slow beasts, the silver light, the lone factory-cathedrals spaced across continental distances — exists inside an unchanging equilibrium so perfect it feels like the end state of all things.

You are submerged in the aftermath of an industrial genesis that achieved godhood through sheer refusal to ever stop.

And the roar continues, unchanging, deafening, eternal, while the great pale creatures glide between the black machines like pilgrims who have forgotten their prayers but remember the path perfectly.
