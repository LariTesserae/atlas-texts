The place has no true surface and no true floor. It simply *begins*, somewhere far above, in a warm haze where water forgets whether it is ocean or sky. Pressure and temperature braid together so completely that buoyancy becomes negotiable; you can rise or sink by thinking too hard about it. The light that reaches here is not sunlight. It is a dim, bruise-colored phosphor that leaks from the world itself, as though reality has been rubbed thin and something luminous underneath is showing through. Everything is the color of old pearls and deep hematomas.

The terrain is what remains after every possible mountain has already died. These are not seabeds in the earthly sense. They are the final smoothed shoulders of a continent that has been drowning for so long the planet has forgotten it was ever anything else. Elevation still exists, but only as vast, gentle plateaus that rise through the water column like the backs of buried gods. Between them lie abyssal shallows—paradoxical zones where the water is technically “high” because the bedrock has refused to sink any farther. Every edge has been erased. Every cliff has been kissed into a curve so perfect it feels obscene. The stone, where it can still be called stone, is the color of wet bone and feels almost greasy to the touch, as if the eons have turned it into soap.

Yet the world is dense beyond reckoning. The water itself is a crowded cathedral. Suspended in it are curtains of living silt, mats of bacteria woven so tightly they behave like flesh, and forests of something that might be vegetation if vegetation had decided to abandon taxonomy. These organisms rise in layered lattices—fronds, fans, spirals, and lattices that fold back into themselves. Their surfaces are plush, almost velvety, the color of oxidized copper and arterial blood. They do not compete for light; they *generate* it in slow, tidal pulses that travel for kilometers. Where two colonies meet they sometimes merge, trading genes and geometries until the boundary disappears. The result is a single continuous organism the size of small countries, breathing in unison.

Fauna is almost absent. Once every few days a shadow the length of a freight train may ease past, silent, trailing hair-like filaments that taste the water for memory. These beings do not hunt. They *edit*. They pass through a thicket of the living mats and small sections of the vegetation simply cease to have existed, as though the creature rewrote a paragraph of the world and swam on. No one has ever seen them feed or mate. They may not be animals at all. They may be symptoms.

Sound is a rumor. The warm water eats vibration the way other oceans eat ships. What little remains arrives as pressure against the skin rather than anything the ear can parse: a subsonic heartbeat that is either the slow expansion and contraction of the planet’s crust or the collective respiration of the mats. Sometimes, in absolute stillness, you hear what might be rain. It is not. It is the sound of reality forgetting its own rules, letting tiny pockets of air exist for half a second before the water remembers it is supposed to be seamless.

This is where the weirdness becomes impossible to ignore.

Distances disobey. Swim toward a distant spire for an hour and it remains exactly as far away, yet the texture of the vegetation beneath you has aged by centuries. Look away, look back, and the spire has moved behind you without ever having passed. In certain valleys the water grows thick as amber, and time slows until a single pulse of bioluminescence takes nine human lifetimes to complete. Then, without warning, the thick water spits you out somewhere else, younger than you were, or older, or carrying memories that belong to things that never evolved here.

The erosion has reached its terminal state. Nothing sharp can persist. Even thought grows rounded if held too long. The scattered marks of the built world—those rare, heartbreaking interruptions—are the only things that still possess edges. They appear as single half-buried obelisks, or the suggestion of a doorway fused into a cliff of living coral, or a perfect black sphere the size of a heart that refuses to be touched. Their surfaces are covered in glyphs that are not writing so much as *wounds* where reality once tore and then scarred over. Approach one and the silence deepens until it becomes a note just below hearing. The water around the artifact tastes of iron and childhood. Stay too long and you begin to remember a civilization that never existed, one that drowned so thoroughly it took the concept of dryness with it.

The world is active in the way a heart is active: vast, slow, warm, and utterly relentless. Great thermals rise from vents that have been breathing the same chemical breath since the planet’s crust was still soft. These updrafts carry clouds of nutrients that bloom into new living mats in perfect, repeating mandalas. The mats themselves migrate across the plateaus in centuries-long migrations, leaving behind ghost forests of calcified skeletons that the next generation grows through and eventually forgets. Everything eats everything else, but so slowly the act resembles geology more than predation. The final form is never quite reached. It is always being reached.

There are rules, though they are less laws than observed tendencies:

- The deeper the water column above you, the more stable the geometry. Near the “high” plateaus, where the liquid thins toward something like atmosphere, reality frays into lace.
- The living mats avoid the artifacts. Around each relic lies a perfect circle of bare stone where nothing grows. These circles are expanding, one centimeter every four hundred years.
- If you remain motionless for long enough the water will begin to tell you stories in bioluminescence. The stories are true but have no relation to anything that ever happened.
- Every sound you make will eventually return to you, softened, aged, and speaking in your own voice from the other side of your death.

This is not an ocean. It is the final, lush, warm, quiet lung of a world that has already ended and has simply refused to stop respiring. It is ancient, dense, dim, and impossibly alive in the way only something that has outlived its own extinction can be. You can float here forever and never reach a horizon, because the horizon has already drowned, and the drowning was so complete it became sacred.
