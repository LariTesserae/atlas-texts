**The Shatterveil**

You stand on the roof of the world that has already died once and forgotten it.

The plateau is so old it has rounded itself into a kind of planetary indifference. Elevation 2.6 has worn the stone down over epochs until the horizon itself feels flattened, yet you are still so high the sky has thinned to a brittle porcelain blue. Normal light (1.6) falls mercilessly, unfiltered, casting no mercy and no shadow longer than a man. What shadow exists is devoured by the wind.

And the wind never stops.

It is not mere weather. It is the sound of the place (2.7), a continuous oceanic roar crossed with the shriek of stone flutes. The ancient erosion (2.8) has left behind millions of pitted pillars, blades, and perforated combs of rock—remnants of mountains that died so long ago their names are older than oxygen. The wind plays them. It plays them deafeningly, chaotically, joyfully. Sometimes the note drops into a subsonic heartbeat that you feel in your teeth; sometimes it climbs into a metallic screaming that makes thought impossible. After three hours here, newcomers begin to mistake the wind for their own pulse.

Water (1.2) does not flow. It appears.

It beads on the leeward sides of the singing stones as heavy, trembling condensation. It gathers in shallow, mile-wide pans where the bedrock has cupped its hand for half a billion years. It falls in sudden, localized silver rods of rain that last seven minutes and then vanish, leaving the ground steaming. These ephemeral gifts collect in the basins and give birth, almost violently, to the vegetation.

The lushness (2.3) is therefore obscene in its isolation. Across the vast empty density (0.5) of the plateau—where one can walk an entire day and see only stone, wind, and sky—there appear, without warning, emerald craters. From above they look like emerald bullet wounds in the gray hide of the world. Inside them the growth is delirious: trees with leaves like wet leather fans, vines thick as thighs that pulse with sap you can hear, pale orchids that open only when the wind hits a certain screaming note. Their roots drink the intermittent water and the constant mist, then grow so fast you can watch the stems lengthen between heartbeats. The moment any plant reaches the lip of its basin, the wind seizes it, strips its leaves, and flings them into the dry nothing beyond. The vegetation lives in a state of permanent exuberant panic.

This is the turbulence (2.9).

The plateau is not stable. It only looks stable because its changes are either too slow (the grinding of bedrock) or too fast (the wind, the sudden green explosions, the flash rains, the die-offs). It behaves like a half-dreaming organism that keeps forgetting it is stone. One basin will be riotously alive while the neighboring one, only four miles away, is already bleaching back to bone. Tomorrow the dead one may erupt again. The wind dictates the rhythm and the wind has no memory.

Fauna is rare (1.3) because most things cannot endure the noise or the unpredictability. What does live here is either enormous or almost not there at all. Vast, slow, lichen-colored megatheres the size of small hills migrate between basins on feet like overturned boats, their backs encrusted with miniature ecosystems. High above, long-winged skimmers ride the roaring thermals without ever flapping, their cries lost inside the greater roar. In the quietest moments—when the wind drops for perhaps ninety seconds—you can sometimes hear the soft clicking of translucent insects that live only inside the hollow stone pillars, tapping out messages in code no one has ever deciphered.

The built remnants (0.6) are almost insulting in their smallness. A single ring of basalt menhirs no taller than your shoulder, placed with obsessive precision in the exact center of an empty plain. A ruined stair carved into a cliff that now leads nowhere, its bottom third erased by time and its top third swallowed by cloud. A lone watchtower of dry-stacked stone, primitive (1.1) in the extreme, containing nothing but a circle of ash and a single obsidian blade. These marks are so far apart that finding two in a single lifetime would be considered a miracle or a curse. The people who made them—whether they still exist or not—left no other trace. Perhaps they learned that any louder statement would be torn apart by the wind.

There is strangeness (1.5), but it is quiet strangeness.

Sometimes, after the loudest storms, the basins fill with a low, internal violet light that has no visible source. The megatheres stop moving and turn their massive heads toward it like supplicants. Condensation sometimes gathers into perfect spheres that float an inch above the rock, spinning, before the wind finally shatters them. And every few days the wind will arrange itself into something that sounds almost like language—huge, polyphonic, and grief-stricken—before tearing itself apart again.

This is not a place that invites you.

It is a place that has outlived every reason for its own existence and has therefore become pure expression: wind, stone, sudden green, sudden silence, the roar that never quite becomes music and never quite becomes silence.

You can stay.

Most things don’t.

But the plateau itself remains—ancient, deafening, warm enough to make you sweat inside your coat, high enough to make the stars look close enough to cut yourself on—endlessly tearing itself down and clothing itself again in violent, emerald bursts.

It is the oldest argument on the planet, and it is still shouting.
