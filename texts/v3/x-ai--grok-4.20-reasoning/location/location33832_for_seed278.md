**The Shroudlands**

You stand on a surface that does not feel like ground so much as the skin of something breathing on geological time. The plateau is so high and so old that the sky has thinned to a permanent iron-grey dimness, as though the sun itself has grown tired and moved farther away. Light arrives muted, indirect, the color of tarnished pewter. There are no sharp shadows. Only gradients.

The cold is not dramatic. It is simply *present*, a constant low-grade subtraction that has been subtracting for so long it has reached equilibrium with the rock. Nothing fights it anymore. Water appears only as an occasional concession: thin films of condensation that bead on northward faces of stone, fragile ribbons of melt that last a few kilometers before vanishing into porous ground, shallow frost-lakes that form in the lee of great upthrusts and evaporate or freeze according to cycles no human calendar could name. Dampness without rivers. Moisture without abundance. The land is neither wet nor dry; it is *remembering* wetness.

Erosion has won so completely that the word “erosion” no longer feels appropriate. The plateau has settled into its final form the way a dying fire settles into coals—gentle, vast, and beyond further argument. Over distances that mock the eye, the stone rolls in immensely subtle undulations, as if a frozen ocean had been pressed flat by the weight of time itself. Here and there the bedrock has been planed into long, almost architectural ribs and swells that suggest the contours of enormous engines half-buried in the crust, though nothing was ever built. The planet simply grew these shapes through patient tectonics and wore them smooth across fifty million quiet winters. The scale is continental in the way a lung is continental to a microbe: the land generates its own slow weather. You can watch lenticular clouds form above one distant ridge and dissipate before they ever reach you, hours later.

Vegetation is almost an insult to the place. A few black, leathery crusts—more fungus than plant—cling to the dampest hollows like scabs. Sparse does not begin to describe the emptiness between them. Kilometers pass with nothing living that photosynthesizes. The emptiness itself feels balanced, intentional, as though the land long ago negotiated a treaty with life and kept most of the continent for itself.

Yet the fauna is overwhelming.

They are not *many* in the way a flock or herd is many. They are overwhelming in the way that realizing the horizon itself is moving becomes overwhelming. The creatures—there is no adequate name for them—resemble titanic, slow-motion isopods crossed with cathedral pipe organs. Their segmented carapaces, the color of oxidized iron and old bone, rise and fall with breaths that take weeks to complete. Some are the size of freight trains. A few, far off, appear the size of small mountain ranges. They move at the speed of glaciers, grinding microscopic lichen and airborne minerals through internal chambers that function exactly like foundries, refineries, electrochemical batteries. Inside their bodies, ores are leached, compounds are forged, electricity is generated in slow blue arcs that never quite become lightning. They are living industrial processes that evolved here when the plateau was young and have been refining the world ever since. No one built them. They *are* the industry.

Their density is sparse. You may walk for three days and see none. Then you crest a shallow rise and discover four of them spaced across the next hundred kilometers like ships at anchor, each one alone in its enormous plot of emptiness, and the mind simply balks at the numbers it knows must exist across the full continental reach. The quiet is absolute. Their internal engines produce only the faintest subsonic thrum, more felt in the bones than heard. Occasionally a carapace segment settles with a sound like a single distant cathedral bell struck once and then muffled under kilometers of felt. That is all.

The ecology is therefore almost entirely internal to the beasts. They seed the few damp pockets with spores and minerals from their own bodies. They exhale faint plumes of warmer air that condense into the only reliable mist the plateau receives. When one eventually dies—after what must be centuries—their colossal corpse becomes a temporary oasis of concentrated chemistry before it is slowly, lovingly, incorporated back into the living stone by the next generation. The cycle is so slow it feels like the land itself is thinking.

There is a rule here, though it is never spoken: nothing is allowed to hurry. Any creature that tries to live quickly starves. Any process that tries to accelerate collapses into frost and silence. The Shroudlands have reached a state of exquisite, industrial-grade patience. The giant fauna move like continents. The frost grows like coral. The very light seems to migrate across the stone over decades.

You can walk for weeks and the only change is that the dim grey light has rotated thirty degrees around the compass. Sometimes you find a place where the rock has been worn into shapes that look exactly like abandoned engine blocks the size of apartment buildings, except no hand shaped them and no hand will ever touch them. The plateau made them because it could. Then it forgot them, the way a mind forgets a dream that lasted ten million years.

This is not a place that invites you. It simply *continues*, vast and sparse and cold and quietly, monstrously alive, whether you are here to witness it or not. The silence agrees.
