**The Hollow Engine**

You step from the descending shaft into a single sealed vault no larger than a cathedral nave crossed with the floor of a medium factory. The space feels both compressed and impossibly full, as though the walls themselves are holding their breath. There is no water. The air is warm, dry as powdered bone, and carries a metallic sharpness that coats the tongue. Every surface—rock, metal, creature—looks fresh, untouched by time, the edges still sharp enough to cut memory.

The cavern was born yesterday and will remain yesterday forever.

Its walls are young volcanic glass and newly fractured anhydrite, jagged plates and fractal blades of pale yellow and rust-red crystal that have not had time to blunt. They jut inward like frozen explosions. Between them, industrial machinery has been packed so densely that almost nothing is horizontal. Boilers the size of locomotives stand shoulder-to-shoulder with gear trains, piston banks, and latticeworks of blackened pipe so intricate they appear woven. Valves kiss valves. Conduits braid like nerves. The density is claustrophobic; you cannot stretch an arm in any direction without brushing hot metal or living chitin.

Yet nothing corrodes. Nothing loosens. The entire apparatus runs at perfect, deafening equilibrium.

The sound is a physical force. A layered, relentless thunder of synchronized strokes: vast trip-hammers falling in 4/4, steamless gas vents shrieking on the off-beat, chains rattling in perfect canon, the constant dry clicking of millions of tiny mechanisms. The noise never rises or falls. It is a single chord held for ten thousand years. After a few minutes your bones begin to hum in tune whether you wish it or not.

In the dimness—provided by stuttering amber arc-lamps and the dull cherry glow of ceramic heating elements that have never needed refueling—you see the fauna.

They are legion.

Swarms of mirror-bright beetles no larger than thumbnails move in glittering sheets across every surface, their legs ending in tiny rotary files. They polish, tighten, scrape carbon, and excrete microscopic gears. Where two sheets meet they simply flow through each other like oil and mercury. Larger creatures—something between a mantis and a steam valve—crouch in the thermal gradients, bodies segmented into black iron and chitin so perfectly matched you cannot tell where the exoskeleton ends and the armor plating begins. Their eyes are pressure gauges; when they blink, tiny needles twitch.

There are things that are mostly sound. You glimpse them as distortions in the air, translucent sacs filled with harmonic turbulence. They drift between the machines like jellyfish made of noise, amplifying certain frequencies, silencing others, keeping the chord pure. The beetles ride them like rafts.

Vegetation, if it can be called that, is almost insulting in its sparsity. Here and there a silica “tree” grows straight from a boiler casing—translucent, geometric, its branches following impossible recursive rules. The creatures tend them with the same indifferent reverence they give every other component. The trees flower once every thousand cycles with a single glass petal that is immediately harvested and fed into a hopper. Nothing is wasted. Nothing accumulates.

Scattered among the machinery are the Built remnants: a single operator’s booth whose glass has grown into the surrounding crystal; a row of hard hats hanging on a pipe, each one welded in place by mineral accretion that occurred the instant they were hung; a control desk where the same yellowed logbook has lain open to the same page since the moment the chamber achieved equilibrium. The entries are all identical.

The world is warm (never hot, never cool), loud (never louder, never quieter), and strange in the way only something that has solved death can be strange. The creatures do not reproduce. They do not age. When one is damaged, the beetles disassemble it on the spot and recast its parts into the next generation of identical creature before the sound of its fall can disturb the chord. The machines, in turn, are kept alive by the constant attentions of the swarm. Each needs the other exactly as it is. The system is young—every edge still bears the tool marks of its sudden creation—yet it has always been this way and will always be this way.

There is a rule here, felt more than spoken: change is the only sin. Any introduced object, any living thing not native to the Hollow Engine, is either incorporated or erased with surgical politeness. Drop a coin and within minutes it will be ground to dust and pressed into a new bearing. Speak too loudly and the sound-beasts will fold your voice into the chord so perfectly that even you will forget you ever made it.

The place does not hate you. It simply refuses to notice that you are an anomaly.

Stay long enough and you may begin to feel the pull—the almost erotic comfort of a system that has achieved absolute stability. The warm air, the roaring lullaby, the glittering motion that never alters. The beetles will polish the sweat from your brow with the same tenderness they give every other moving part.

Most choose to leave before the offer becomes attractive.

The ones who remain are still here, somewhere, perfectly maintained, perfectly quiet, another component among the dense, never-changing, ever-thundering dark.
