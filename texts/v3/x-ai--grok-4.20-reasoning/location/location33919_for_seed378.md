**The Weeping Plateau of Veyr-Keth**

You stand on a high shelf of the world where the air itself seems to sweat. The elevation is not mountain-sharp but broad and imperial, a regional expanse of tilted stone perhaps eighty kilometers across, ringed by farther cliffs that vanish into heat-shimmer. Everything is hot—2.9 hot—yet drenched. The water does not fall as rain so much as it *arrives*, condensing out of a bright, metallic sky in slow, fat beads that cling to every surface before sliding away. The rocks weep. Every ledge, every fracture, every upthrust tooth of basalt or rhyolite is fringed with perpetual rivulets of scalding water that hiss where they meet cooler stone.

There is no vegetation. Not a blade, not a leaf, not a patch of moss. What looks at first like pale undergrowth resolves, on closer inspection, into forests of inorganic bloom: colossal clusters of amethyst and sulfur crystals growing in slow, branching lattices that mimic stunted trees. Their edges are razor-sharp. Between them stretch mats of thermophilic extremophiles so thin they are almost paint, staining the stone in violent oranges, poison greens, and corpse purples. These are not plants. They are closer to living rust or geologic infection.

The industrial city that claims the plateau is deliberately sparse. Its districts sit kilometers apart, each a self-contained knot of blackened iron, ceramic pipe, and sweating concrete. Between them lie wide, echoing barrens where the only movement is steam and the occasional glide of silent fauna. The factories themselves are old—erosion has rounded their corners, fused their foundations with the bedrock, turned once-sharp gantries into organic-looking ribs. They have been here long enough to belong. Conduits as thick as subway trains snake across the stone, carrying superheated mineral slurry from geothermal taps to distant separation halls. The engines are strangely hushed; the entire industrial web runs on low, sub-audible frequencies that you feel in your teeth rather than hear.

Light is merciless. Even when the sky fills with condensing vapor and the plateau vanishes behind curtains of steam, the brightness remains. Every wet surface becomes a mirror. The crystal “trees” fracture sunlight into blades of violet and gold that move across the ground like searchlights. You squint constantly. Shadows are sharp enough to cut.

The fauna is abundant and deeply strange. Vast herds of knuckle-high silicate beetles drift across the hot stone in glittering waves, their carapaces clicking so faintly it might be imagination. Tall, mantis-like grazers the color of wet slate move between the crystal groves on jointed stilts, sipping mineral-rich water from crevices without ever making a sound. High above, membranous things—half jellyfish, half manta—ride the thermals in total silence, their bodies pulsing soft bioluminescence that is still visible in the glare. They never cry out. Nothing does. The plateau’s quiet is absolute, as though every living thing long ago agreed that noise was wasteful. Only the water speaks: constant dripping, the sigh of steam escaping fissures, the distant hollow clang of a pipe contracting as it cools.

The place feels active yet settled. Hot springs surge and subside on cycles no one has fully mapped. Every few hours a new fracture opens in the stone with a muffled groan, releasing a fresh gout of scalding water that immediately begins carving its own miniature canyon. The factories adjust without fanfare—valves sigh open, pumps shift tone by a single Hertz, and the great mineral engines drink whatever the plateau offers. The ecosystem and the industry have achieved a wary equilibrium. The silicate herds migrate along pipelines warmed by process heat. The mantid grazers lick algae from the outsides of cooling towers. The flying membranes cluster above the tallest exhaust vents where the updrafts are richest. No one seems to be in charge of the arrangement; it simply is.

There is a rule here, felt more than spoken: nothing is allowed to remain dry for long. Anything new brought to the plateau—boot, machine casing, visiting dignitary—acquires a glistening coat of moisture within minutes. The water finds every seam, every joint, every secret. Over decades it has infiltrated the oldest factories so completely that some sections no longer distinguish between architecture and mineral spring. Entire wings have become sacred grottoes of dripping iron and living stone where workers move carefully, speaking in the barest whispers.

Stand still long enough and the plateau reveals its final strangeness: the light and steam together sometimes conjure phantom structures—half-seen towers or bridges that are not there when you look directly. They flicker at the edge of vision, industrial echoes of buildings that may not yet exist or may have been worn away centuries ago. The silent creatures seem to navigate by them.

This is Veyr-Keth. A hot, wet, mineral silence stretched across high stone. A city that learned not to fight the weeping of the world, but to pipe it, refine it, and let it run through every heart and gear until the difference between factory and geology became academic.

The plateau endures, bright and dripping and alive with things that make no sound at all.
