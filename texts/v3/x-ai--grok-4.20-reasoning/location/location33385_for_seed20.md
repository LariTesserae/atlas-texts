**The Keening Font**

It is not a place that should exist. It occupies a volume no larger than a cathedral, yet the mind keeps trying to stretch it larger because the violence inside it feels planetary. You stand—float—hang—inside a warm, deafening womb of water that has forgotten which way down is.

The sound comes first and never leaves. It is not one roar but every roar braided together: the bellow of a continent splitting, the shriek of steam exploding through fresh lava, the wet impact of a million tons of water hammering itself from every direction at once. The noise is so complete it becomes texture. Your teeth vibrate. Thoughts drown. After a few moments you stop trying to speak because the sound has already taken your name.

The water itself is thick, fever-warm, almost mammalian. It moves with the restless fury of something that has just been born and already wants to die. Currents collide, spin off, slam upward, then whip sideways as though gravity keeps changing its mind. Walls of white foam erupt from nowhere and vanish again, leaving behind perfect spheres of air that drift, intersect, and sometimes *pop* into smaller spheres that scream like glass breaking. Sometimes the water simply forgets to be liquid for a heartbeat; it turns mirror-bright and rigid, reflecting broken fragments of other worlds, before shattering back into chaos.

The architecture of the Font is young, viciously young. Black basaltic stone—freshly fractured, glassy, razor-edged—thrusts upward in cruel spires and shattered buttresses that have not had time to dull. Some of these formations look almost deliberate: a half-arch here, a spiraling colonnade there, the ghost of a vaulted ceiling that melts into foam before it can complete itself. These are not ruins in the usual sense. They are *suggestions* the world keeps making and immediately regretting. Between them float scattered, impossible artifacts—half a stone bench hovering in a pocket of still water, a single carved step that leads nowhere, the broken face of a statue whose features keep rearranging themselves when the currents shift. Nothing technological. Only the mineral memory of something that might once have been a monastery, or a temple, or a mind.

Vegetation is almost absent. Only the occasional translucent ribbon of something like kelp appears, ghost-pale and veined with slow bioluminescence. These strands do not grow; they *condense* out of the water itself when the pressure drops, then shred apart again minutes later. The few creatures that exist here are rarer still—silvery, multi-bodied things that seem to have too many timelines. A school of them will phase into view, swim through a stone column as though it were smoke, then unravel into threads of light that the currents swallow. They never linger. Nothing lingers.

The light is the color of old bruises and dying embers. It comes from no single source. Sometimes it leaks from the fractures—jagged, lightning-like rips in reality that open and close with wet sucking sounds. Through these wounds you glimpse fragments of other places: a red desert under three suns, a library burning underwater, a street where everyone is walking backward. The glimpses last less than a second before the wound seals with a sound like a mountain slamming shut.

Elevation and submersion have fused here into something that hurts to perceive. You are simultaneously thousands of meters above sea level and crushed beneath kilometers of impossible water. The sharp stone peaks rise toward a surface that is sometimes above you, sometimes below, sometimes wrapped around you like a sphere. When the turbulence momentarily calms, the water forms a perfect curving lens overhead and you can see, far above the chaos, the thin blue edge of an atmosphere that has no business being there. Then the lens explodes and the roar redoubles.

This is a young wound in the world. A reality abscess. A place that was torn open yesterday or perhaps a thousand years from now—time is one of the many things being chewed apart by the currents. The Font is building and unbuilding itself in the same breath. New stone cathedrals surge upward only to be planed away by walls of water moving faster than thought. The scattered remnants of whatever once stood here are being simultaneously preserved and erased, like a memory the universe cannot decide whether to keep.

And beneath the deafening roar, if you press your hands to your skull and listen with the body instead of the ears, you can almost hear something else: a low, vast, wet heartbeat that is not yours, not the water’s, not anything that should have a heart at all.

It is learning.

It is hungry.

It is singing with a mouth made of every direction at once.
