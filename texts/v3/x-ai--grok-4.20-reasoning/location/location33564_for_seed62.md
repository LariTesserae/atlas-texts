**The Shroudlands**

You stand on a knife-edge ridge of black basalt still so fresh it glitters like obsidian under the merciless white light. The rock feels newborn, as though the world only shrugged it up from the mantle a few thousand years ago and has not yet had time to round its edges. Every fracture is sharp enough to cut memory. Between these young ribs lie deep, narrow valleys still deciding what they want to become.

The air is cool—perpetually ten degrees below what your body insists is comfortable—yet saturated. Water does not fall here so much as *arrive*, condensing out of the bright emptiness in slow, deliberate beads. It gathers on every leaf, every rusted girder, every hair on your arm, until the whole world drips. Occasional thin rains come like afterthoughts, barely loud enough to be heard. The moisture is patient; it pools in stone cups, slides along translucent root systems, and vanishes into thick mats of moss so dense they have their own topography—miniature ranges and valleys you could map with a fingertip.

Vegetation has answered the light and water with obscene enthusiasm. The density is almost violent. Ferns the height of houses jostle shoulder to shoulder with trees whose trunks are sheathed in living green velvet. Vines thick as wrists braid themselves through every available gap, their leaves a strange metallic green that throws the sunlight back harsher than the stone does. Beneath them lies a saturated understory where smaller plants grow so tightly they form single continuous organisms; step on what looks like ground and your boot sinks six inches into a sponge of clubmoss and liverwort that exhales cold mist when compressed. Nothing is sparse. Even the air feels furred with pollen and spores.

Yet animals are almost absent. You might see a single translucent moth the color of fog once in an afternoon. Nothing larger. The ecology appears to have settled on pure vegetal and mineral intensity, as though fauna simply never received an invitation.

Scattered through this riot are the Machines.

They are not ruins in the usual sense. They are too intact, too *busy*, even though their activity registers only across decades. A three-story refining hall stands half-buried in a slope, its roof collapsed under the weight of a single ancient tree whose roots have replaced half the support columns. Inside, conveyor belts still creep—literally creep—moving a few centimeters per year, carrying chunks of the young black rock toward silent crushers whose gears are now half root, half iron. Rust has become a geological layer here; it flakes into long red strata that the moss drinks like blood. In certain hollows you can find places where the distinction between architecture and organism has quietly dissolved: pipes that have thickened into woody stems, valve handles that have grown bark, turbines whose blades have sprouted pale lichen fans that turn with the faintest breath of wind, generating micro-currents no meter could measure.

The soundscape is almost perfect silence. The vegetation drinks noise the way it drinks water. A distant rockfall is reduced to a soft thud. The slow grind of a half-living gearbox becomes a vibration you feel in your teeth rather than hear. Only the water makes any real sound—single drops falling from leaf to leaf with the patience of monks.

The light is overwhelming. At this elevation the sky feels too close and too thin; the sun (or suns—perspective sometimes fractures strangely here) pours down without apology. Every wet surface becomes a mirror. The metallic leaves, the wet basalt, the sweating metal skin of the factories—all of it throws glare until the world looks over-exposed, edges too crisp, colors almost painful. Shadows are rare and coveted. You find yourself migrating toward the few places where the canopy has grown so thick it creates pockets of genuine shade, only to discover that the shade itself is full of slowly pulsing bioluminescent veins running through the roots of what used to be transformers.

There is a rule here, though it reveals itself slowly:

The land is still deciding whether it is waking up or going to sleep. The young geology keeps pushing upward in infinitesimal annual spasms. The vegetation answers by throwing itself into every new crack. The Machines—left by whatever civilization once thought this plateau worth industrializing—have become part of the argument. They no longer fight the green. Instead they metabolize it, and it metabolizes them. Every few centuries a new kind of fruit appears on the iron-vines: small silver globes that taste faintly of ozone and iron. No one knows what they are for. The local ecology has simply incorporated them into its slow, patient calculus.

Stand still long enough and you can feel the gradient. A single valley over, a new ridge is rising; you can watch its black spine pushing through the moss at a rate that would bore a glacier. In the opposite direction, an entire factory complex is being swallowed so gently it appears to be sinking into green water. Everything moves. Nothing hurries.

This is the Shroudlands. Bright, cold, dripping, overgrown, half-devoured by its own inheritance. A place where industry and wilderness have met, argued for a few hundred years, and then quietly agreed to become the same slow organism. The only question still unresolved is which of them is dreaming the other.
