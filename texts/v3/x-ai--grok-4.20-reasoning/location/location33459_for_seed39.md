**The Shuddering Threshold**

You stand on a knife-edge ridgeline that has no business existing yet. The stone under your boots is so young it still carries the memory of the pressure that birthed it; blackish-gray phyllite and serpentinite fractured into geometric plates, edges sharp enough to cut the wind. Nothing has had time to round them. The range rose only yesterday, geologically speaking—perhaps three thousand years ago, perhaps thirty—and the planet has not finished adjusting. Every few hours the ground gives a low, sub-audible *clunk*, like a locomotive coupler settling into place far underground. The sound arrives through your bones before your ears.

The air is thin and wet at the same time. Elevation has sucked the oxygen thin, yet a cold mist condenses on every surface the moment it forms. Water does not fall here so much as *arrive*, beading out of the rock itself. Every cliff and fracture weeps slow, clear tears that freeze into translucent whiskers before they can run far. Where the tears meet, they pool in perfectly circular iron-stained basins no larger than dinner plates, then overflow in braided threads that disappear into cracks again. The whole mountain is a vast, half-built plumbing system still testing its valves.

Vegetation is almost an insult to the place. A few crusts of black lichen cling to north faces like rust. In the lee of boulders, tufts of pale moss glow faintly against the stone, but they are so widely spaced you can walk for twenty minutes between one living thing and the next. Nothing else survives. The sparsity is absolute.

Yet the emptiness is full of motion.

Fauna is shockingly, almost violently present once you learn how to see it. The animals here are all large, widely spaced, and perfectly engineered for the industrial hush. Great shambling beasts the color of wet slate—something between musk ox and giant ground sloth—drift alone across the high cirques, cropping lichen with metronomic patience. Their breath explodes in white clouds that hang in the still air for a long time before the cold collapses them. Farther out, packs of long-limbed canid-like predators follow at a distance of several kilometers, never closing, never resting, as if the hunt itself is a slow geological process. In the air, vast flocks of white-winged raptors ride the updraughts between peaks, wings locked, never flapping, turning the sky into a moving lattice.

They are all listening.

The soundscape is mostly quiet, but the quiet has texture. There is the soft hiss of water freezing and unfreezing inside rock. The occasional *crack* of a stone plate shifting. And beneath it all, a low, rhythmic mechanical throb that seems to come from the mountain’s own circulatory system—hot geothermal fluids forced upward through fractures, meeting permafrost, flashing to steam, driving natural rock-pistons that rise and fall a centimeter at a time. The range is, in its own way, an engine still being run in. You can smell hot iron and sulfur on the wind. Where steam vents breathe, the rock around them is stained the violent oranges and acid greens of cooling slag. Nothing was built here. The industry is native.

Light is permanent late-afternoon dim, even at noon. Thick lenticular clouds crouch on the highest summits like resting machinery, diffusing everything into a cold pewter glow. When the sun does appear, it is only as a white coin behind veils of ice crystals.

The rules of this place are simple and merciless:

- Nothing is finished. Every slope is provisional. A ridge that looks solid may calve away tomorrow with a sound like a dropped girder.
- Life must stay in motion. To stop is to freeze or starve. The great beasts walk without cease; the predators follow without cease; the mountain itself grinds and sighs without cease.
- Distance is not measured in kilometers but in time. A herd you see on the opposite wall of the valley is three days’ hard travel away. You will lose sight of it long before you reach it.
- The water remembers heat. Even when it emerges ice-cold from the weeping rock, it carries a faint mineral aftertaste of the deep engine below.

Stand still long enough and you begin to feel like an uninstalled part. The landscape is not hostile. It is preoccupied—busy assembling itself, testing tolerances, running its first cold cycles. You are simply standing inside the factory floor while the line is still being laid.

And somewhere far below, in the black root of the new range, something massive and slow takes another deliberate, metallic breath.
