**The High Lattice**

You stand on a fractured plateau that has not yet learned how to be old.

The stone is newborn—raw, blackish-green diabase shot through with seams of pale feldspar and metallic sulfides. Every edge is violent: knife-blade ridges, cubic jointing, hexagonal columns still wearing the clean geometry of their cooling. Erosion has barely touched it. The world here is still remembering the violence that lifted it, nine kilometers above whatever passes for a sea on this dry world. There is no water. Not a single molecule of liquid anywhere. The chemistry is anhydrous, oxidizing, electric. Dew is an alien concept.

Yet the plateau is not barren.

Across its immensity—hundreds of kilometers in every direction—stand isolated thickets of something that can only be called vegetation by courtesy. They are spaced so far apart that from any one cluster the next is a faint metallic glint on the horizon, like a ship at sea. Density is almost nothing; the land between them is perfect emptiness, a silence of stone.

Each thicket is enormous.

The largest rise four hundred meters, branching and re-branching in fractal cascades that look like frozen explosions of glass and copper wire. Their “trunks” are bundles of hollow silicate tubes; their “foliage” consists of razor-thin metallic lattices that catch the steady, ordinary daylight and splinter it into painful rainbows. Up close the color is not green but the colors of industry: gunmetal, cuprite red, tarnished silver, acid yellow, the blue of copper sulfate that has never seen sulfate. They are lush the way a coral reef is lush—every square meter crowded with detail, every surface furred with secondary crystals, every branch tip budding new lattices in perfect self-similarity. Nothing moves. Nothing eats. Nothing breathes. They simply *are*, in ferocious geometric excess.

This is the industrial signature.

The lattices are not decorative. Their atomic structure functions as vast natural catalytic arrays. Slow thermoclines inside the thickets—warm stone below, cooler upper air—drive ionic currents through the crystal veins. You can feel it if you press your palm to a trunk: a faint, steady vibration like a transformer humming on a distant hillside. Nodules of pure elements accrete at certain junctions—mercury that has never known liquid, filaments of native iron, beads of elemental silicon. The thickets are refineries. They have been refining the same few cubic kilometers of crust for geological ages, locked in perfect stoichiometric equilibrium. Growth and dissolution exactly cancel. The industrial process is real, but it has achieved a plateau of its own; nothing measurable changes in a thousand years.

The silence is surgical.

Wind exists but is thin at this altitude and has nothing to stir. It passes through the lattices with a sound like a single distant bell struck once and then forgotten. Mostly there is no sound at all. Your own pulse becomes audible. Footsteps on the raw diabase click and scrape with embarrassing volume. Occasionally a crystal face will ping as daytime expansion opens a microscopic fissure, a sound like a distant rifle bolt. That is all.

No animals. Not even microbes in the conventional sense. The chemistry here never invented the wet bag of salts that animals require. The lattices themselves perform the only “metabolism” the world possesses.

The light is Earth-normal, almost insultingly familiar. A white sun hangs in a deep indigo sky. Because there is no water vapor and almost no aerosol, shadows are violently sharp. Every crystal edge cuts the light like a scalpel. At noon the thickets blaze so brightly they hurt to look at directly; by late afternoon they cool into a million shades of ember and steel.

This is a world in frozen emergence.

It is still being born, yet it has already reached its final form. The geology rose, the lattices seeded themselves along fracture zones where mineral gradients were steepest, and then everything—stone, crystal, chemistry, heat—settled into a single, self-reinforcing equilibrium. Time still passes, but it passes through a place that has nothing left to become. The landscape is embryonic and ancient at once.

There are rules here, though they are not written.

1. Everything necessary is already present. Nothing is added, nothing is wasted.
2. Distance is sacred. The lattices seem to repel one another; no two thickets will ever merge.
3. Change is permitted only if it cancels itself out. A crystal may grow one atom thicker; another must dissolve by the same atom elsewhere. The books always balance.
4. Sound is an intruder. The world prefers the pure tone of its own internal currents.

Stand long enough between two distant thickets and you may begin to feel the tension that stretches between them—like the air before lightning, or the silence between two notes that have not yet been played. The industrial vegetation is not communicating, exactly. It is simply aware, in its slow, catalytic way, that the other lattices exist. That awareness is enough. It has sustained this place for longer than mountains last on wetter worlds.

This is the High Lattice.

A desert without sand.  
A forest without leaves.  
A factory without workers.  
A newborn geology that has already achieved eternity.
