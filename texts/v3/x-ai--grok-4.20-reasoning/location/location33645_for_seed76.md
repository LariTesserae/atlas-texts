**The Shroudlands**

The plateau has no name and needs none. It is simply *the high place*, a single landform so vast it possesses its own curvature, its own weather, its own slow heartbeat. You can walk for weeks and the horizon still refuses to yield an edge; the sky itself seems to bend to accommodate the scale.

There is no water. Not a trace. No memory of it remains on the surface. The ground is an ancient, bone-dry mineral matrix—feldspar, mica, and strange anhydrous silicates powdered by time into a fine, floury dust the color of pale rust and ivory. Where bedrock shows, it has been planed by wind over hundreds of millions of years into gentle, almost sensual undulations, as though the planet itself had relaxed into its final shape. Erosion has finished its work. The land is *established*: weathered past drama, past youth, past even the idea of change. It simply *is*.

Yet the plateau is lush.

Life here abandoned water long ago. The dominant organisms—called *serein* by the few xenologists who have gazed upon them from orbit—are not plants in any terrestrial sense. They are vast, slow photochemical lattices, half crystal, half hyper-specialized aerophyte. Each individual stands kilometers apart, respecting the sparse mandate of the world. Between them lie empty leagues of mineral dust where nothing larger than a fingernail grows. The emptiness is not barren; it is balanced, deliberate, like the negative space in a calligraphy scroll.

The serein themselves are enormous. A modest specimen rises four hundred meters, spreading a canopy wide enough to cast shadow over a small city. Their “trunks” are not wood but recursive lattices of translucent silicate branches that look like frozen smoke. From these hang curtains of filamentous growth—millions of delicate, blade-like structures that stir even when the air is almost still. In the brutal light they shimmer between platinum, ash-violet, and a cold, metallic teal. They are lush the way an iceberg is lush: complex beyond comprehension, layered, fractal, impossibly *full* of surface and edge and quiet industry.

The light is merciless. A blue-white sun hammers the plateau without mercy or filter; at this elevation the atmosphere is thin enough that the sky is nearly black at zenith and only the brightest stars are washed out. Every crystal edge of the serein catches the glare and splits it into razored rainbows that drift across the dust like wandering auroras. Shadows are sharp enough to cut.

The temperature never rises above cool. It is the cool of deep autumn that has forgotten how to end—steady, dry, preservative. Metabolic rates here are measured in decades. A serein may add a single new major branch every seventy years. The entire ecology moves at the pace of geology wearing silk gloves.

And yet it is unmistakably *active*.

Wind systems the size of small continents crawl across the plateau in stately procession, carrying microscopic spores and charged dust in slow cyclones that last for months. The serein breathe in the thin air, exhale complex hydrocarbons, and slowly alter the very chemistry of the dust around their roots. Every few centuries a particular alignment of light, wind, and mineral saturation triggers a simultaneous “bloom” event: millions of the filamentous blades across an entire region release iridescent pollen that hangs in the air for weeks like living mist. The silence during these events is absolute.

Sound itself has been winnowed down to almost nothing. You hear your own heartbeat. You hear the faint crystalline ticking as a serein grows another few millimeters of lattice. Sometimes, when the wind finds the right angle through a particular grove, it produces a single, low note so deep it is felt in the sternum rather than heard. Then the note fades and the silence returns, deeper than before.

Fauna is vanishingly rare. What little exists drifts rather than walks: translucent membrane-things like living kites, paper-thin and almost weightless, riding the thermal columns between the serein. They feed on the pollen mist and the electrostatic charge that builds along the crystal lattices. Their population is so low that witnessing two interact feels like stumbling upon a secret meeting of immortals. There are no herds, no flocks, no visible predator-prey chases. The drama happens at chemical speed, invisible, patient.

There is a quiet strangeness to the place, a 1.4-weirdness that never quite resolves. Look too long at any single serein and you begin to suspect the entire plain is *one* organism—that the empty kilometers between individuals are not separation but connective tissue made of time and dust. Sometimes, at certain hours when the light strikes the lattices just so, you can see faint standing waves of color moving across distant canopies in perfect synchrony, as though the whole world were thinking.

Nothing is built here. Nothing has ever been built. No artifact, no ruin, no footprint but your own. The Shroudlands were already ancient when the first microbes stirred in Earth’s oceans. They have reached their own equilibrium and now simply persist, vast, bright, cool, crystalline, and perfectly indifferent to whether anything ever witnesses them again.

Stand still long enough among the titans and you begin to feel the deep, slow pulse beneath the silence: the mineral lattice growing by microns, the wind moving its cargo of spores, the light being drunk and transmuted, the centuries stacking quietly atop one another like flawless sheets of mica.

This is not a dead world.

It is a world that has learned how to be eternal without hurry.
