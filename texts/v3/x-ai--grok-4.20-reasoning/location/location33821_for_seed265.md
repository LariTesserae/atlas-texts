**The Sump**

It is never night here because night implies a sun that has set. There is only the dark, warm and wet as a mouth.

You stand in a low basin that feels recently born. The ground itself is young—raw plates of black basalt shoved upward only decades or centuries ago, their edges still sharp enough to cut the air. Between them lie shallow, skin-warm sheets of water that never quite drain. The water does not arrive as rain; it sweats out of the rock. Every stone weeps. Every concrete slab left by the old industries is fringed with slow, clear beads that gather, tremble, and fall upward as often as down. The atmosphere itself condenses on your skin, in your lungs, on the inside of your thoughts.

Vegetation is sparse but strangely dense in its placement: rubbery black reeds grow in tight, perfect rings around anything metal, as though the rust itself is seeding them. Their leaves are thick as tongues and make no sound when the warm wind—barely a breath—moves across them. Between the rings the ground is naked, glossy, almost greased. Nothing wastes energy on green.

The industrial remnants are scattered the way a giant might drop tools and then forget the names of the tools. A half-collapsed cracking tower leans like a broken tooth twenty meters to your left, its guy-wires dripping steady chains of water that never reach the ground; they simply vanish midway, swallowed by a reality that has grown tired of gravity. Further off, perhaps two kilometers, a refinery complex sits half-sunk in its own mirror: the black water has risen exactly to the level of the old pipewalks so that the rusted catwalks continue seamlessly beneath the surface, descending into a second, submerged factory that was never built by human hands. Both versions hum.

The hum is the loudest sound. Everything else is mostly quiet.

Engines that should have died lifetimes ago still turn, but so slowly you can watch a single piston complete its stroke over the course of an hour. Conveyor belts creep like glaciers, carrying nothing. The abundant life here has learned the same patience. Pale, blind things drift between the reeds—creatures that are partly eel, partly machine, partly something that has forgotten which category it belongs in. Their bodies phase in and out of solidity; you can put your hand through one and feel only a mild electric sorrow. Larger fauna—things the size of horses but built like wet paper—move in extreme slow motion across the open flats, their many legs sinking centimeters per minute into the weeping clay. They make no noise except the softest wet kissing when their feet pull free. Sometimes they simply stop existing for a span of days, then reappear a little further on, still walking.

Reality here is not broken so much as soft. It has the consistency of warm tallow. Distances forget themselves. A single rusted valve chest can be seen from seven different angles at once, each at a different stage of decay, as though the landscape is trying every possible version of its own past. If you stand still long enough, your own footprints begin to walk away without you. Light itself is forbidden; any flame or bulb you attempt to strike down here gutters immediately, not from lack of oxygen but from embarrassment. Only the creatures carry faint bioluminescent bruises—sickly violet and stomach-bile green—that appear on their bodies in symbols resembling forgotten maintenance runes.

The scale is open in the way a wound is open. You can see for many kilometers if “seeing” is the word for what happens when your eyes adjust to almost nothing. Low ridges rise and fall in gentle, organic waves, each one studded with the skeletal remains of petrochemical plants, pumpjacks frozen mid-prayer, and the occasional lone switch-house standing like a confessional. Everything is near-touching. The air is so moisture-saturated that sound travels strangely; a drip behind you can sound like a factory collapsing on the horizon. Yet the overall impression is of profound, geological silence. The place is in no hurry. It is still deciding what it wants to be.

Rules have accreted here like the condensation:

- Nothing may move quickly. Even thought grows sluggish after the first hour.
- Every machine must be allowed to finish its original task, no matter how many centuries that takes.
- If you speak your own name aloud, the dark will try it on for size. It may answer back in your voice, but older.
- Water is the only honest thing. It weeps because it remembers the light that used to exist before the basin was born.

This is not ruin. It is gestation. The Sump is a place that has swallowed an entire industrial civilization and is now, with glacial, dripping slowness, digesting it into something that has never been named.

Stay long enough and you will feel your own edges beginning to weep too. Small clear beads of yourself gathering on the fine hairs of your arms. They do not fall. They wait, perfectly patient, for the next version of the world to decide what it wants to do with them.
