The place has no name, nor any need for one. It simply *is*, stretching so far that its own curvature becomes a kind of weather.

You stand on a plane that feels both newborn and ancient. The ground is not soil, not rock in any ordinary sense, but a single continuous substance: a dark, iron-rich sinter that looks as though it were poured in one unimaginable session and then left to cure for ten thousand years. Every square meter of it is *made*. There is no substrate beneath, no hidden geology that might once have been wild. The material itself is the world, extruded, fitted, and still adjusting.

It emerges in polygons.

Most are hexagonal, though some pentagons and heptagons appear where the pattern must accommodate curvature or stress. The tiles are not uniform. Some have only just broken the surface—raw, glassy-edged nubs no higher than your ankle—while others stand half a meter tall, their sides still warm to the touch as if the forming heat has not yet bled away. Between them run hairline fissures no wider than a fingernail, yet these are the only places where anything else can exist.

Water (never abundant, never absent) lives in those fissures. At dawn, condensation beads along the cooler upper edges of the polygons and slides downward in perfect silence, gathering in shallow lenses no deeper than a finger joint. By mid-morning most of it has vanished, sucked upward again by the heat or wicked into the stone itself. Every few days a localized storm—born entirely above this plain because the plain is large enough to make its own sky—drops a brief, violent rain. The water flashes along the cracks like nervous silver wiring, pooling for an hour in wider basins where several polygons meet, then evaporates in spirals of steam that smell faintly of hot iron and wet chalk.

The heat is enormous, but not dry. It is a damp, bodily heat. The air feels pre-chewed. Your skin is never quite dry; instead it stays slick with a mixture of sweat and the faint mineral condensation that refuses to leave. At noon the polygons shimmer like black mirrors. Heat devils rise in slow, deliberate columns that sometimes twist into dust-devils, though there is almost no loose dust—only the finest grit shaken out by the slow, ongoing adjustment of the tiles.

Vegetation is not absent, merely humiliated by scale and scarcity. Every few hundred meters a black, leathery thing that might be a lichen or might be something older clings to a crack. Its leaves (if they are leaves) are thick as shoe-leather and edged with microscopic hooks that snag the morning dew. In places where several cracks converge, these growths form small, sullen colonies no larger than a dinner plate. They do not spread. They simply endure, respiring so slowly that their yearly growth is measured in microns. Nothing eats them. Nothing pollinates them. They exist in a state of perfect, solitary patience.

There are no animals. Not a lizard, not an ant, not a microbe detectable by eye or ear. The silence is therefore total enough that it becomes a sound of its own. You hear your own blood. You hear the minute ticking and clicking as the polygons expand in the day and contract at night—thermal contraction joints performing their endless, microscopic labor. Sometimes, far off, a deeper *thunk* travels through the stone like a distant hammer blow. It is not thunder. It is the land settling, or perhaps rising; the difference is academic here.

The horizon is a lie. Because the plain generates its own convection, banks of cloud often form at the edge of vision, creating the false impression of mountains or tree lines that dissolve when you walk toward them. There are no mountains. There is only this single, low, planetary floor still in the act of being stamped into final shape. In some directions the polygons grow larger—kilometer-wide plates whose edges rise in gentle escarpments you could walk up in ten minutes. In other places they shrink to a fine mosaic that looks, from a distance, like dark leather pulled tight over the curve of the world.

Everything is still under construction. The tiles keep coming. You can see fresh ones emerging even now if you are still enough: a faint trembling in a crack, then a glassy lip pushing upward with the patience of centuries. The process is neither geological nor technological in any sense our languages are built to hold. It is closer to crystallization on a continental scale—slow, inevitable, and somehow *intending* the pattern. The rules of the world are written in geometry and thermodynamics. Water is allowed only in the seams. Life is tolerated only in the seams. Everything else must be this dark, patient, self-tiling stone.

At night the heat radiates upward so fiercely that the sky above you looks bruised. The polygons lose their daytime shimmer and become matte black shoulders hunched against the stars. Condensation returns, heavier now, and the cracks sing with tiny, almost sub-audible trickling. You can walk for days and the sound profile barely changes: your footsteps, the click of cooling stone, the occasional distant report of the land adjusting its own bones.

This is not a ruin. It is not abandoned. It is a thing that has not yet finished announcing itself. The atmosphere is one of pregnant stillness—like standing inside a kiln that has already fired but whose glaze is still deciding what colors it will wear for the next ten million years.

And still the polygons keep emerging, one quiet millimeter at a time, across a plain so vast it makes its own rain, its own silence, and its own slow, inexorable mind.
