**The Pocket**

It is not much larger than a cathedral, yet it feels like it contains an entire world that has not yet finished deciding what it is.

You stand inside a cupped fold of fresh stone, a hidden basin no more than two hundred paces across, entirely enclosed by raw black basalt that rose only recently from some slow wound in the earth. The walls are still sharp, glassy in places, fractured along clean angles that have not had time to blunt. They rise steeply and then overhang, almost meeting above you like half-formed ribs, leaving only a narrow, irregular seam through which a muted, pearl-grey light filters. That is all the sky you get. The rest is dimness, warm dimness, the temperature of breath against skin.

Water is not a river here, nor a lake. It is a mood. Moisture gathers on the stone in slow silver beads, slides, collects in shallow thumb-print basins no deeper than your palm. Every few hours a droplet falls with a sound like two fingers snapped inside a closed mouth. Sometimes a brief, warm mist condenses out of nothing and drifts between the pillars before vanishing. Once a day, perhaps, a soft rain the width of the pocket itself passes through the seam above, hisses on the warm rock, and is gone. The stone drinks it instantly, but not completely; small dark mirrors remain in the low places, reflecting the ribs of the ceiling like tiny uncertain skies.

Life has arrived anyway.

In the thin beds of grit that have begun to collect along fractures and in the lee of stone ribs, vegetation explodes in defiant islands. Not a forest. Islands. Dense, almost embarrassingly lush clusters of broad-leafed things with leaves the color of wet jade, ferns whose unfurling fists are larger than your head, creeping vines whose stems are thick as wrists and whose flowers open only in the brief periods when the mist turns gold. Between these green pockets lie wide stretches of bare stone, so the eye can travel. The contrast is startling: one moment you are pushing through wet leaves that slap softly against your shoulders; the next you stand in an open nave of rock where nothing grows and the silence feels architectural.

The stone itself has begun to suggest city.

Not metaphorically. The way the basalt cooled and contracted created columnar joints that stand in imperfect rows like half-built colonnades. Layered shelves of differing hardness have weathered at different speeds even in this short time, producing what look like foundations, lintels, the suggestion of doorways. A tall, flat-topped block the size of a small house leans against the western wall exactly where a gatehouse might have stood if anyone had planned it. Nothing is finished. Everything is still being born. You can hear the land thinking: occasional deep ticks and settling groans travel through the rock as it continues to cool and adjust. The sound is so low it is felt in the teeth more than the ears.

There are no tools. No blades, no wheels, no firepits. The only technology is whatever the mosses and liverworts are doing to turn bare stone into soil one cell at a time.

Yet the place is urban in its bones. The eye keeps completing the architecture. You find yourself walking along what feel like streets—long, straight runs of smooth stone between the natural pillars—then stepping into what feel like plazas where the sparse green islands stand like deliberate gardens. The proportions are wrong in a way that feels intentional, as though the world were practicing being a city before it had inhabitants.

The animals understand this better than you do.

Fauna is abundant but never crowded. Small dark lizards with throats like living opals move across the warm stone in stop-motion bursts. Tiny frogs the color of new leaves wait at the edge of every water pocket, throats pulsing in perfect silence. Somewhere above, in the half-roof of overhang, bats shift and rearrange themselves with the softest paper sounds. A single pair of pale owls—almost white against the black rock—hunt the abundant blind crickets that live in the deeper fissures; you never hear the kill, only the faint snap of wings opening and the knowledge that something has ended. Every niche is occupied, yet the pocket never feels full. There is always stone visible. The emptiness is part of the design.

Sound is disciplined here. The parameter is low. What you hear most is the patient dripping, the occasional creak of the rock adjusting its shoulders, the papery rustle of broad leaves rubbing when a warm updraft moves through. Even the insects are quiet. Their wings beat with a low, almost sub-audible thrum that you feel in your sternum rather than hear. The place seems to want to remain embryonic. Noise would be a claim, and nothing here is ready to make claims.

The ecology is in rapid, visible motion. You can watch a vine lengthen over the course of an afternoon, its tip blindly searching for purchase on the next stone rib. A fallen leaf becomes soil by the following morning. A lizard that was born yesterday is already hunting. Predator and prey move through the pocket in slow, overlapping circuits, visible from one side to the other because of the generous emptiness between green islands. Everything is happening at once, yet nothing feels hurried. The warmth keeps chemical reactions eager. The damp keeps everything supple. The dimness keeps ambition modest.

There is a rule here, though it is never spoken.

Nothing that arrives from outside may remain unchanged. Metal rusts to powder in days. Cloth frays into green threads that the mosses adopt. Even memory seems to blunt at the edges. The longer you stay, the more the pocket convinces you that it has always been here and that you are the thing still forming.

It is not a ruin.  
It is not yet a city.  
It is the moment between the two, still warm from creation, still damp with possibility, still deciding which shape it will keep.

Stay long enough and you begin to feel the stone measuring you—deciding whether you are another kind of moisture, another kind of seed, or simply another quiet animal moving through its slow, green, dripping dream of becoming.
