**The Undershrine of Vesh-Ka**

You pass through a final lintel of fitted basalt, so low you must stoop, and the space beyond unfolds like a lung. Everything here is made. There is no living rock, no raw earth; every surface was cut, dragged, and levered into place by hands that worked with stone chisels, wooden wedges, and ropes of braided fiber. The builders have been dust for so long that their tool-marks have been worn into soft, almost fleshy curves by condensation and the endless rub of wings, scales, and feet.

The chamber is cathedral-tall yet hamlet-small: a single bounded volume no larger than a village square, but dense with intention. A thicket of columns—more than seventy—rises from floor to vault, many so close their capitals almost kiss. Between them hang dense curtains of ropework and wooden lattice the builders left behind, now black with age and furred with mineral crust. The air is warm, thick, mammalian. Geothermal warmth trapped for centuries mixes with the humid breath of countless bodies, producing a damp heat that sits on the skin like a second hide.

Moisture never falls as proper rain. Instead the vaulted ceiling sweats. Beads of condensation gather along the worn grooves the builders cut as gutters, swell, and drop with deliberate, metronomic plops into stone basins placed centuries ago to catch them. Some basins overflow in thin sheets that slide down walls carved with spiraling glyphs now softened into indecipherable braids. The water smells of iron and bat urine and something faintly sweet, like overripe fruit left in the dark. Small reflective pools tremble on the floor where the flags have settled and cupped, each one a mirror to the writhing life above.

Life is everywhere, layered, loud, and close enough to touch.

The ceiling is upholstered with bats—thousands upon thousands—hanging in a single shifting mat so dense their folded wings overlap like scales. Their constant low-level chittering and the papery rustle of readjusting bodies creates a sound like rain on dry leaves, only louder, wetter, alive. Every few moments a ripple moves through the colony and dozens launch at once; the sudden clap of wings is sharp enough to make the air slap your ears. They are not alone. Pale cave-swiftlets rocket between the columns in tight squadrons, their clicks ricocheting until the whole chamber feels like it is shouting at itself. On the columns themselves, geckos with translucent skin patrol vertical territories, snapping at the clouds of midges and stoneflies that drift in slow, humming galaxies. The floor is a moving carpet: black millipedes the length of a finger, iridescent beetles, tiny blind scorpions, and small russet rats that flow like liquid between the columns, pursued by sleek, half-blind weasels whose ancestors clearly slipped in centuries ago and never left.

Vegetation is almost an insult here. A few resilient mosses stain the wettest stones emerald, and long threads of pale fungus hang from the undersides of archways like wet hair, but nothing green truly thrives. The animals eat each other, the fungus, the mineral-rich water, and one another again. The food web is audible: the wet crunch of beetle under jaw, the shriek of a rat taken mid-scurry, the endless insect hum beneath it all like a nervous system firing.

The architecture itself has reached a state of balanced exhaustion. Erosion has not destroyed the Undershrine; it has finished it. Edges meant to be sharp are now rounded like river pebbles. Reliefs of what might have been gods or ancestors have been worn into smooth, eyeless shapes that the swiftlets now use as nesting ledges. The builders’ original geometry—strange heptagonal vaults and corridors that never quite meet at right angles—has become stranger with time. In the diffuse, normal light that falls through seven angled light-shafts bored through the ceiling, the angles seem to shift when you are not looking directly at them. Not impossible, not sorcerous. Simply *unusual*, as though the place has been thinking to itself for three thousand years and has not yet reached a conclusion.

Sound never stops. The dripping, the wings, the chittering, the clicking, the scrabbling, the wet chewing—it layers into a single roaring hush that feels like being inside a living throat. Yet the noise is not chaotic. After long minutes you begin to notice rhythms: the bats’ mass launches come every seventeen minutes or so; the swiftlets change shift in perfect synchrony; even the dripping seems to fall into slow canons across the different basins. The Undershrine is loud, but it is conducting itself.

This is not a ruin. It is a finished thing. The primitive makers built a stone womb and then vanished, and the world’s smaller lives moved in and perfected it. Predator and prey, water and stone, sound and echo, heat and moisture have all settled into a single, dense, warm, dripping equilibrium. Nothing here is natural. Nothing here is dead. Everything is made, and everything is eating or being eaten or waiting to be eaten while the ceiling gently weeps onto carved basins older than any language still spoken above ground.

You stand among the columns and feel the place noticing you the way a stomach notices a new piece of food: curious, warm, already calculating where you might fit.
