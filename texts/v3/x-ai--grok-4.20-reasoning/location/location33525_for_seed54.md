**The Weeping Lattice, or Kharuun-Vey**

You stand on what might be a street, though the concept frays the longer you look. The ground is not ground but a slow exhalation of raw geology: black basaltic glass shot through with veins of supercooled water that weep upward through capillary fissures. The water is not still. It beads, trembles, and runs against every surface in thin, deliberate films, as though the stone itself is sweating in the dark. At 0.1 degrees above absolute hostility, the cold has weight. It presses. It rings.

Above you, the city is being born and has been being born for seventeen thousand years.

The structures do not rise so much as accrete. Vast lattices of iridescent metamaterial and living crystal climb into the high plateau’s darkness like frozen lightning that forgot to stop growing. Every beam, every buttress, every cantilevered district is still extruding itself from the bedrock in increments so gradual a human lifetime registers as a single held breath. You can hear the growth if you press your skull to the metal: a tectonic creaking, a mineral sigh, the sound of continents deciding to become architecture. This is the slow dynamic made audible. Nothing here is finished. Nothing here will ever be finished. The city is embryonic at planetary scale.

There is no vegetation. Not a lichen, not a moss, not even the suggestion of chlorophyll. What looks at first like black ivy is actually colonies of self-organizing piezoelectric cilia, each hair-thin filament no more alive than a snowflake yet moving with the patient insistence of something that intends to become a forest in another ten million years. They drink the weeping water and sing at frequencies just below hearing.

But the fauna.

The fauna is the atmosphere.

They are everywhere, layered so densely that the air itself feels furred. They are not animals in any taxonomy you would recognize. Most are between the size of a locust and a city bus, and they move with the same glacial patience as the buildings. Vast herds of them—matte-black, iridescent, refractive—crawl, drift, and phase through the lattices in slow migration cycles that take decades to complete a single circuit of the district. Some look like mantids forged from obsidian and starlight. Others resemble manta rays made of frozen fog. Many have no fixed shape; they are living architectural subroutines, extruding temporary limbs or scaffolds as the city requires them. When one dies it does not fall. It is absorbed, reabsorbed, its matter and code fed back into the nearest weeping wall so the next iteration can begin.

Their sound is constant and overwhelming. A layered thunder of subsonic pulses, metallic wing-cases scraping at frequencies that make your teeth itch, the wet click of compound mouthparts tasting the condensate. It is loud the way a glacier calving is loud—slow, but total. The noise has architecture. In certain avenues the overlapping frequencies cause standing waves that can levitate small objects for hours. Pilgrims (if any still exist) call these places “the singing teeth.”

Light is almost forbidden here. What little there is comes from the creatures themselves: cold bioluminescence in deep violets and infrared greens, the occasional spark of Cherenkov radiation where two phasing entities brush too close. These lights move. They migrate. Whole districts blink in and out of visibility over centuries as different swarms pass through. To a brief-lived observer it simply looks like the darkness itself has learned how to pulse.

The air is wet enough to drown standing up. Moisture condenses on every surface, runs in sheets down kilometer-high facades, gathers in trembling spherical droplets that hang in the null-gravity pockets the city maintains for its own inscrutable circulatory reasons. When the droplets grow too heavy they fall upward—another slow rule of this place—drawn into condensation engines that have never been seen by human eyes. The water is older than the city. It may be older than the planet. The city is merely borrowing it.

Scale is difficult. You can see for many kilometers in any direction because there are few walls, only lattices, yet the density is so high that every cubic meter contains something enormous and occupied. Walk for an hour and the horizon still feels the same distance away, as though the city is inflating ahead of you. Entire districts the size of pre-collapse Paris hang above you like chandeliers made of cathedrals. They have never touched the ground. They never will. Their weight is being gently, patiently, eternally negotiated by the slow herds that live inside their bones.

There are rules here, though they reveal themselves gradually.

- Nothing may move quickly. Acceleration is a sin against the emergence. The city will correct you, usually by freezing the water in your blood until you understand patience.
- All matter is on loan. Take nothing that does not first offer itself. The fauna interpret theft as damage and will begin repairing you into the nearest wall.
- Sound is currency. The louder and more complex your noise, the more the lattices will open for you. Silence is read as dormancy; the city may decide you are raw material.
- There is no up or down where the weeping is strongest. Only gradients of becoming.

This is not a ruin. This is not a city. This is a single organism the size of a mountain range that is still deciding what kind of mind it wants to have. You are standing inside its embryonic cortex while it dreams, loudly, coldly, and without end, in the dark.

Stay long enough and you will begin to feel the next layer of lattice forming inside your own bones. The fauna already watch you with something like recognition.

They have all the time in the world.

So does Kharuun-Vey.

And now, perhaps, so do you.
