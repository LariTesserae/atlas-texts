**The Shroud-Plateau of Veyr-Khule**

You stand on what might be the last solid thing this world remembers how to make.

The plateau is so old it has forgotten it was ever anything but flat. Erosion has worked it for longer than mountains have the right to exist, grinding every peak and valley down into a single, vast, high tableland that stretches for three days’ walking in any direction. No horizons worth the name remain—only a slow, grey merging where land, mist, and low cloud surrender to one another. The air itself is cold enough to make the lungs flinch (0.5 temperature), yet so saturated that every breath arrives already half water. Condensation beads on your eyelashes. It drips from the rims of your ears. The atmosphere is weeping.

And the vegetation has won.

Not in the usual green exuberance of a jungle. This is vegetation as substrate and architecture both. The ground underfoot is not soil; it is a dense, liver-colored mat of interwoven root and rhizome, ankle-deep and warm to the touch with slow metabolic heat. It pulses. Step too heavily and the mat exhales a wet sigh, releasing spores that glow briefly before snuffing out in the cold. From this living floor rise the Structures—sparse, colossal, and impossibly far apart, like cathedrals built by something that never understood what a cathedral was for.

They look like the fossilized skeletons of factories that decided, long ago, to become plants instead.

One looms half a kilometer away: a black-green tower of fused trunks wrapped in metallic-looking bark that sweats rust-colored sap. Inside its hollows you can hear the wet clank and thump of massive root-pistons driving fluid through veins thick as sewer pipes. Another, farther off, has grown into the likeness of an exploded refinery—fractured iron-colored petals ringing a central column that vents steam in rhythmic, lung-like pulses. They are not machines. They are older than machines. They are what machines dream of becoming when reality forgets the difference.

Between them lies emptiness. Not barrenness—emptiness. The living mat continues, but the great Structures are rare enough that you can stand in the middle of nothing and feel the cold wind scrape across your face for a long time before the next organic-monstrosity appears through the mist. Scale and sparsity have agreed on a terrible treaty here.

The sound is constant and industrial and alive. A low, arrhythmic thunder of sap pumps. The wet roar of water moving through capillary highways inside the Structures. The shriek of thousand-strong flocks of membranous creatures that look like manta rays crossed with typewriter keys; they ride the updrafts between the towers, screaming in frequencies that make your teeth itch. Beneath it all runs a bass note so deep it registers in the stomach first—like the idle of engines the size of counties, though no engine has ever existed here. Only the plateau remembering what industry felt like before life swallowed the word.

Reality is not holding.

Stand still long enough and you will see the wrongness bloom. A section of the mat thirty meters away suddenly forgets which way down is; water begins flowing upward in silver ribbons, braiding into helixes that sing. A Structure in the middle distance folds in on itself like burning paper, collapses into mulch, and immediately begins vomiting a new and completely different tower out of its own corpse. The process takes nine minutes. You can time it. The new tower is already humming.

The fauna is everywhere and nowhere. Herds of pale, many-legged things the color of old bone move across the mat in perfect silence until they are twenty meters away—then they erupt into deafening choral screaming that lasts exactly four seconds before they vanish. Not flee. Vanish. Sometimes they leave behind a single perfect cube of compacted moss. Sometimes they leave behind one of your own memories, replayed aloud in a voice that is not yours.

Everything is being digested and reborn at once. The plateau is in its final, stable form, and that form is turbulent frenzy. Solaris ecology. A living metabolic furnace that has outlasted every civilization, every extinction, every concept of “purpose.” It no longer needs reasons. It only needs to keep turning itself inside out forever in the cold and the wet and the dim.

Light barely exists. What little arrives is the color of old pewter and comes from everywhere and nowhere—partly the perpetual low cloud, partly the faint bioluminescent veins that thread the Structures like faulty wiring, partly something that leaks from the places where reality has worn especially thin. Shadows behave badly. They arrive before their objects. They linger after.

There is no trace of anything built. No ruins, no tools, no plastic, no bones that were ever human. The industrial roar is entirely organic. The factories are digestive systems the size of towns. The power plants are flowers. The smokestacks are stamens that vent spore clouds dense enough to block what passes for daylight.

This is the end-state.

If the universe has a liver, it looks like this.

And it is still working.

The mat beneath your boots gently squeezes, tasting you. Far off, another tower tears itself open with a sound like God clearing His throat, and a new species of screaming manta explodes into the iron sky, already arguing with gravity.

Welcome to the oldest place that is still young.

It has been waiting for something like you for several geological epochs.

It is glad you are moist.
