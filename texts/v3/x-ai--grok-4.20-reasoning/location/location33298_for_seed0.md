**The Churn**

You descend into it along a fresh fracture plane no more than a few centuries old. The rock still wears the raw angles of its birth: black basalt daggers tilted at forty degrees, glassy obsidian that rings like struck iron when anything heavier than rain strikes it. Between these knives lies a low basin eight days’ walk across, cupped by newer scarps that steam in the warm dark. Nothing has had time to soften. Every slope is still deciding what shape it will keep.

The air itself is wet without being flooded. Condensation beads on every surface and falls in slow, fat drops that patter for hours between the true rains. Those come as sudden, warm deluges that last only minutes but arrive without warning, slamming through the canopy like thrown stones. Pockets of black water collect in the jagged troughs, never deep enough to be called lakes, never still enough to be called pools. They seethe with larval things that surface, thrash, and vanish again.

Light is a memory. The sky is hidden behind a triple canopy so dense that even at noon the basin receives only a bruised violet twilight. Most illumination comes from within: pale nodules on fungal trunks, drifting clouds of luminous midges, the occasional flare of a lantern-jawed beetle the size of a human hand. These lights are brief and nervous. They reveal rather than dispel the dark, outlining the wet gloss of leaves the color of old bruises and the glistening chitin that moves between them.

The vegetation is not patient. Nothing here waits its turn. Colossal pale ferns uncoil in hours, their fronds thick as ship’s rope. Vines thick as thighs loop and re-loop, knotting themselves into living cables that twitch when touched. Between them rise soft, spongy pillars that are neither tree nor mushroom but something that arrived earlier—perhaps the first successful colonists—whose skins split open to release fresh clouds of spores the moment they sense a gap in the press. Every surface is already occupied; every new growth must shove, strangle, or overtop what came seconds before it. The smell is iron, sap, and wet bread left too long in the oven.

Sound is not background. It is weather.

A continuous roaring, clicking, sawing, screaming that presses against the ears until thought becomes difficult. Cicadas the length of a forearm drill the air with metallic shrieks. Tree-toads the color of raw meat inflate translucent throat sacs and hammer out bass pulses that vibrate the sternum. Something high in the canopy produces a sound like tearing metal slowed down and played backward. Beneath it all runs a wet, constant rustle of millions of legs, wings, mandibles, and soft bodies sliding over one another. When the sudden rains come, the noise redoubles—every creature shouting at once as though the water itself were an enemy.

The fauna is not merely abundant. It is the landscape wearing flesh.

On the ground, millipedes as long as oars braid themselves into living bridges across crevices. Above them, translucent frogs with luminous organs pulsing in their bellies hunt in vertical packs, leaping from vine to vine in perfect silence until they strike. Higher still, bat-things with wingspans wider than a man’s reach flare their nostrils and echolocate in piercing triplet bursts that cut through the general din like knives. Every level is eating and being eaten in the same breath. Nothing finishes a meal undisturbed. A fallen fruit is covered in insects before it finishes rolling; the insects are covered in smaller insects before they finish feeding; a predator arrives to eat them all and is immediately beset by parasites that ride its eyelids.

The entire basin exists in a state of permanent turbulent overshoot. It is geologically newborn and biologically hysterical. Species appear, flourish, collapse, and are replaced within single human lifetimes. You can watch a colony of glowing shelf-fungi claim an entire fallen pillar in a day, only for a species of acid-spraying grubs to arrive the next dawn and dissolve it back into black sludge by dusk. New reproductive strategies erupt faster than names can be given to them. Some creatures are born already pregnant. Others bud directly from the corpses of their parents. A few seem to borrow shapes from whatever is watching them.

There are no trails. There are only temporary gaps that close again behind you. No tool-marks, no carved signs, no firepits. Whatever primitive hands exist here—certain clever amphibians that use sharpened beetle mandibles as hooks, a species of small furred climbers that lash sticks into temporary shelters—leave nothing permanent. The churn erases craft within weeks. Even bones dissolve quickly in the warm acidic damp.

The rule of the place is simple and merciless: *keep moving or be incorporated.*

Stand still longer than a few heartbeats and you will feel the first exploratory tendrils. A filament of moss will taste your sweat. A cloud of luminous gnats will orbit your eyes. Something with too many knees will climb your calf to see if you are substrate. The basin does not hate you, but it also does not recognize any difference between you and the next fallen log. Everything here is either food, shelter, or temporary interruption.

Yet for all its violence there is a terrible, humming vitality. In the rare moments when the canopy above tears open and a single shaft of real sunlight strikes the floor, the response is immediate and orgasmic: every plant within twenty paces lunges toward the light with audible creaks and pops. Flowers that were not there a minute earlier unfurl, release pollen in visible clouds, and begin to rot again before the beam has moved on. The sound momentarily spikes into something almost like cheering.

This is not a jungle. Jungles are old. This is a wound in the world that life has not yet learned to scab over. It is still bleeding abundance. It is still shouting its newness in every frequency at once.

And it is still growing.
