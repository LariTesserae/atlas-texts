**The Weeping Lattice**

It is not a forest. It is not a city. It is a single, continent-spanning organism that has been persuaded, over forty thousand years of patient hand-work, to grow in the shape of architecture.

You stand on a root-road thirty paces wide and so densely woven that its surface feels like warm bone polished by skin. The “ground” is alive; you can see the slow peristalsis of sap under translucent bark the color of old ivory. Every few hundred meters the road widens into a node—a plaza grown as a perfect shallow bowl where water gathers and then, instead of pooling, is drunk upward again by capillary mouths along the rim. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is wild.

The air is warm, almost blood-warm, and so humid that breath condenses on your eyelashes. A perpetual drizzle falls upward in places—fine threads of mist detaching from the undersides of colossal leaves and drifting toward the pale green sky as though gravity has grown confused. The sky itself is not blue but a drowned jade, light diffused through uncountable overlapping canopies far above. True sunlight has not touched this elevation in living memory. The Lattice makes its own weather and its own dim, cathedral twilight.

Distance defeats the eye. Between the living megastructures lie miles of open, humming emptiness. One tower may be visible for three days’ walk; the next appears only as a darker bruise against the mist until you are almost upon it. The space between is not void but a slow, breathing medium. Flocks of pale, silent moths the size of dinner plates drift through it like snow. They do not flap; they ride the exhalations of the Lattice itself. Their wings are veined like leaves and carry no pigment—only the faint bioluminescence of symbiotic bacteria.

The towers themselves are beyond scale. A single buttress root can be wider than a cathedral nave; its surface is carved—literally carved—by generations of tool-bearers using blades of obsidian and deer antler into friezes that depict the slow conversation between human hands and vegetable will. The plants have learned the language of geometry. Arches grow in perfect catenary curves that should collapse under their own weight yet do not. Spirals tighten in logarithmic perfection, then reverse direction when unobserved, as if the organism is dreaming in non-Euclidean space.

This is where reality begins to bruise.

Stand still long enough inside one of the great vaults and you will feel the floor inhale. The pulse is deep, measured, centuries per beat. Certain corridors rotate—slowly, over weeks—realigning themselves according to a logic no one has ever successfully transcribed. Water weeps from the walls not as droplets but as perfect spheres that hover, trembling, until they decide whether to fall or rise. In the uppermost galleries, time loosens; memories older than the species that shaped them leak into the minds of those who sleep there. The builders long ago stopped pretending they were imposing form. They became co-authors in a dialogue that began before their language existed.

Fauna is abundant yet almost never seen in motion. The Lattice has absorbed the animal kingdom into its own rhythms. Great six-legged browsers the color of wet moss move so slowly they appear as topographic features until you notice an eye the size of a shield regarding you with vegetal patience. Colonies of glass-winged lizards live inside the vascular system, sipping sap and singing in frequencies too low for human ears; you feel their chorus as a pressure behind the sternum. Predation exists, but it is architectural: certain corridors are allowed to grow carnivorous barbs that bloom only when a particular species of migratory fruit-bat passes overhead every seventh year. The system is in balance so ancient it has become ceremony.

Sound is reverent. The dominant noise is the soft percussion of water—drip, bead, release—amplified inside hollowed xylem chambers the size of opera houses. Every few hours a column of air moves through the Lattice and the entire structure sighs, a sound like distant velvet tearing. Footsteps are absorbed. Voices feel indecent. Most communication between the few remaining caretakers (a skeletal culture of gardeners, pollinators, and memory-keepers) is done by touch and the placement of small colored seeds.

There are rules, though they are felt rather than spoken.

1. Nothing may grow faster than the Lattice permits. Acceleration is heresy.
2. Every cut must be answered with a gift—usually blood, spit, or song.
3. If a bridge begins to move while you are crossing it, you must stop, kneel, and wait for permission. Most who ignore this are never seen again; some are found years later, perfectly mummified inside a new growth of heartwood, their bodies incorporated into an arch or buttress as though the Lattice found them beautiful.

The deeper you travel, the more the distinction between “made” and “grown” disappears. Walls bear the chisel marks of tools that have not existed for six hundred years, yet the grain of the wood has grown around those marks in deliberate spirals, as if the plant decided the scars were lovely and chose to ornament them. In the oldest chambers—places where the root-road descends into the living mass like a vein—you can press your palm to the wall and feel it press back, gently, with something like recognition.

This is a post-rain world that has never known a dry hour. A high, warm, hollow cathedral that is also a single body. A place built entirely by hand and entirely by photosynthesis. A continent of green bone and slow thought where reality has grown tired of straight lines and is quietly, persistently, breaking.

And still the water weeps upward.  
And still the towers breathe.  
And still the gardeners walk their impossible roads, pruning eternity one deliberate cut at a time.
