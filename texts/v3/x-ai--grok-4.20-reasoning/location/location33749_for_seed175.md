**The Weeping Nave**

You stand inside a single, contained hollow high in a nameless range—less a valley than a cathedral-sized chamber carved by time itself. The space is perhaps two hundred paces across at its widest, bounded on all sides by walls of pale, butter-colored stone that rise in smooth, bulbous folds. These are not cliffs; they are old. Erosion has spent so many centuries here that sharpness has been entirely forgotten. Every edge has become a curve, every face a gentle belly or drooping shoulder. The rock looks half-melted, half-grown, as though stone and flesh had argued for a hundred thousand years and finally reached a compromise.

Water is everywhere, yet it never rushes. It *weeps*.

A constant film slides down the walls in transparent sheets so thin the stone appears to sweat. In places the moisture gathers into heavy, trembling beads that hang from overhangs like amber. Some of these drops take hours to fall. When one finally lets go, the *ploc* it makes upon striking water or moss is startling—almost violent—because every other sound has been retired. The air itself is warm, moist, and still. It condenses on your skin, on the undersides of leaves, on the lenses of your eyes. You are breathing water that has already breathed the stone.

The vegetation is lush but deliberately sparse, as though the place has rationed life. Between wide aprons of bare, glistening rock lie scattered “stands”—islands of green no larger than a cottage. Within each stand the growth is almost indecently vigorous: ferns with fronds thick as a man’s wrist, their edges so perfectly crenellated they look machined; mosses so dense they form emerald pillows a foot deep; ropy vines that hang motionless, heavy with stored water, their leaves broad and waxy and the color of oxidized copper. Nothing climbs or strangles. Each stand keeps to its own territory, leaving generous lawns of wet stone between them. The emptiness feels intentional, like the silence between notes in very old music.

There are no flowers as you know them. Instead, certain plants produce slow, luminous nodules—soft, vein-marbled globes that swell over decades. Some have split open along mathematical seams, revealing interiors of pale, fleshy lace that never quite dries. Others remain closed, pregnant with some future that will arrive long after you are gone.

The strangest feature is the way stone and plant refuse to stay separate.

Many of the rock formations wear what look like slow tumors of green. In other places the green wears armor of stone. You cannot tell whether the pale nodules on the ground are mineral concretions giving birth to moss or ancient moss that has slowly petrified. Veins of quartz run through the largest fern stands like nerves. In several spots the rock has formed hollow, rib-like arches that mirror the curve of the giant fronds above them so exactly that the eye cannot decide which came first. The longer you look, the more the entire nave feels like a single organism that has chosen to express itself sometimes as stone, sometimes as leaf.

There are no animals. Not one. No midges above the pools, no spiders in the moss, no birdsong above the rim. The silence is so complete it develops weight. After a time you begin to hear your own blood. The only motion is patient: the sliding glaze of water, the almost invisible lengthening of a frond over weeks, the gradual rounding of a stone that will not finish becoming itself for another ten thousand years.

Light arrives from above in the ordinary way, yet the condensing mist softens it until every surface glows with its own meek inner lamp. Shadows are few and gentle. Colors feel saturated past realism—deep malachite, wet ivory, bruised jade—yet never garish. The warmth is steady, geothermal perhaps, rising through the stone so that the pools never grow cold. Their surfaces are so still they act as mirrors turned sideways to the sky.

If there are rules here, they are these:

- Nothing is allowed to hurry.
- Nothing is allowed to finish.
- The boundary between living and unliving is considered impolite to enforce.

To remain inside the Weeping Nave is to feel time unspool. An hour spent here feels like a long, slow exhalation. A day feels like a held breath. A century would be barely enough for the landscape to notice your passing; it would simply incorporate your bones the way it incorporates everything else—gradually, wetly, without ceremony—until you too became another weeping rib of stone draped in living velvet.

The place does not want you.  
It also does not mind you.  
It has been practicing perfect, humid indifference for so long that indifference has become a form of reverence.
