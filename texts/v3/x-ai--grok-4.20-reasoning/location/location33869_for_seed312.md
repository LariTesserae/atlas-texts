**The Shattered Roof**

The land begins as a wound that never healed.

It is a high continent lifted too quickly, still raw from the buckling that raised it. Ridges rise in knife-edges and fractured plates; cliffs stand in clean, unweathered faces thousands of feet high, their fractures so recent that shards the size of houses still lie where they calved yesterday—or last year, or a hundred years ago; the difference is meaningless here. Nothing has rounded. Every skyline is serrated. The scale is continental: from the crest of any ridge the eye travels for days in every direction across the same cold geology, until the curvature of the world itself blurs the farthest ranges into pale teeth against a normal, indifferent sky.

The air is thin and permanently cold. Even at noon the breath hangs in small white ghosts that drift only a few yards before they are swallowed by the next wind. There is no true summer, only a long, grudging spring that lasts decades.

Moisture arrives like a rumor. Most days the land is dry as bone, yet it is never truly arid. Clouds scrape across the highest ridges and leave behind a slow, cold sweat: beads of condensation that crawl down black rock, tiny seeps that stain stone with rust-colored lichen, pockets of water cupped in every fracture and hollow. Once or twice in a long human life a real rain may come—short, violent, over in an afternoon—after which every crevice runs for a few hours and then falls silent again. Snow arrives as dry powder that blows like sand, but in sheltered north-facing cirques it sometimes packs into permanent white ledges that feed the seeps all year. The water is never abundant. It is only persistent, and the entire ecology orbits those few reliable pockets the way iron filings orbit a magnet.

Vegetation is almost an afterthought. A gray-green lichen army coats every surface that does not face the wind directly. In the lee of ridges, tight fists of grass grow so slowly that a single clump may be older than the oldest living builder. Here and there a stand of knee-high, iron-hard shrubs huddles around a seep, leaves blackened by frost even in the gentlest months. That is all. The land is vast, sharp, and almost naked.

Yet it is dense with life.

Fauna moves in layered, overlapping tides. Great herds of shaggy, six-legged grazers—broad as oxen, silent as monks—drift along thousand-mile circuits that follow the seeps. Their migration is so slow that a single herd may take twelve years to complete its loop; the youngest calves will be grandmothers before they see their birthplace again. Smaller creatures—fox-sized rock-burrowers, flocks of slate-colored birds with serrated wings, clouds of midges that appear only when a seep has stood for three days—fill every niche. Predators follow: long-bodied cats with pale fur and yellow eyes, condor-like raptors whose wingspan equals a man’s height, and something that looks like a bear but moves with reptilian patience. All of them are quiet. Sound itself seems to have been worn thin by the altitude. What little there is travels forever: the click of a hoof on stone, the distant clack of a stone-mason’s mallet, the low, almost sub-audible rumble a herd makes when ten thousand throats breathe at once.

Upon this raw young skeleton a human civilization has grown like coral—slow, dense, and patient.

The cities are not cities in the lowland sense. They are accretions. Wherever a plateau offers even marginal shelter from the killing wind, stone buildings have been fitted, stacked, and tunneled into the living rock over centuries. The technology is primitive: copper and bronze tools, wooden wedges, ropes of braided sinew, human muscle, and generational time. Yet the workmanship is exquisite. Walls are built of the same sharp-angled rock the cliffs are made of, so that from a distance a settlement looks like a landslide that simply decided to become geometric. Streets are narrow, often roofed over, rising in switchback terraces or descending into the cliff itself. Levels pile atop levels; a single “house” may be twelve vertical chambers drilled through a thousand-year-old masonry shell. The density is astonishing. In the largest settlements, a man can walk for half a day without once seeing open sky or open land—only stone, smoke holes, ladders, and the small blue squares of daylight at the ends of tunnels.

Every sound is muffled and deliberate. The ring of chisel on stone is the loudest thing most people ever hear, and even that is soft, almost respectful. Children are taught to speak quietly. The great herds, when they pass below the cliffs, sound like distant surf.

The ecology is governed by one iron law: nothing hurried survives. A tree—there are perhaps three true trees on the whole continent—takes four human lifetimes to reach waist height. A building is expected to stand for twenty generations. Even the seasonal round is measured in decades; the great herds are tended by hereditary stewards who inherit their routes the way others inherit crowns. When a seep finally dries, entire districts of a city may be quietly abandoned over the course of a century, the stone simply allowed to fall back into the mountain until the water returns.

There is no waste. Every scrap of bone, every dried pat of dung, every handful of lichen is accounted for. The cities and the herds exist in a single slow metabolism, breathing the same thin, cold, damp air, dreaming the same long, patient dream.

At night the normal light of the stars is almost violent in its clarity. The darkness between them feels newly made, as sharp and young as the cliffs below. Sometimes, when the mists rise and catch the starlight, the whole high continent seems to float between two darknesses—rock below, sky above—while the small persistent sounds of hammer and hoof and breathing move patiently across it, like the heartbeat of something that has only just begun to live and already expects to live forever.
