**The place is called the Stillborn Sea.**

It does not lie at the bottom of any ordinary ocean. Instead the water has claimed a high, new-born plateau where the world has only recently torn itself open. The peaks here thrust upward less than a geological eye-blink ago—raw, black, glass-edged basalt and fractured schist still sharp enough to cut the water itself. Their angles refuse to round. Every ridge and blade leans at the arrogant slant of something that has not yet learned compromise with wind, rain, or time.

Yet the water is older than the stone. It arrived as a slow, warm deluge from below and above at once: upwelling from newly opened fissures in the plateau floor while perpetual banks of saturated cloud pressed down from the thin air above. The result is a single warm, continuous medium. You cannot say with confidence whether you stand on land that has been drowned or drift inside a sky that has thickened into sea. The water is not deep everywhere—only in the trench-like wounds between the young ranges—but its saturation is absolute. Every breath tastes of iron and chlorophyll. Every movement pushes against a tangible, living density.

The vegetation has answered the water with ferocious, patient exuberance. It does not grow so much as *accumulate*. Vast colonies of pale, jointed stalks—neither kelp nor bamboo nor anything that has a right to exist at this altitude—rise in tight, overlapping ranks, their bases rooted in crevices of the fresh stone, their crowns disappearing into the luminous ceiling of the surface layer. Between them hang curtains of filamentous moss so fine they move like breath. Where the stalks meet they form layered galleries: a submerged forest with its own submerged understory, and beneath that another, and another, until the light fails in soft stages of green and gold. The density is almost uncomfortable. In places the living mass is so complete that the water itself seems only a lubricant between one plant and the next.

The light, strangely, remains ordinary. Sunlight reaches these depths as though the water were only a few meters deep instead of hundreds. It falls in wide, slanted shafts that the dense vegetation turns into cathedral beams, each one alive with slow-spinning motes of silt and spore. Rainbows are not occasional here; they are a permanent, faint seasoning of the air, appearing and vanishing as drifting curtains of micro-bubbles pass through the light.

There is almost no sound. The water swallows everything into a velvet hush. Only rarely does a deep, sub-audible creak travel through the stone as the plateau continues its patient upward shrug. When it does, the forests answer with a faint, coordinated shiver that lasts for many minutes. That is the loudest event most days.

Fauna is present but spectral. You might pass an hour without seeing anything larger than a translucent shrimp the color of moonstone. Then, far off in a shaft of light, a ribbon of something sinuous and black will uncurl, regard the world with no visible eyes, and dissolve back into the green. The ecosystem feels like it has not yet decided what animals are *for*. The plants have claimed the majority of the energy; the creatures that do exist seem to be waiting for the next slow chapter.

The strangeness of the place is quiet, structural, and growing. The young rock sometimes bleeds thin threads of metallic vapor that rise like reverse rain, forming temporary lenses in the water that magnify or distort whatever drifts behind them. Certain groves of the jointed stalks appear to have grown in perfect geometric spirals that match no known current or nutrient gradient, as though the water itself were remembering a shape from somewhere else. At the deepest points, where the new rifts plunge farthest, the temperature remains warm but the pressure feels wrong—too light, as if the planet has not finished deciding how much gravity this altitude should possess.

Nothing is finished here. The mountains are still being born. The flood is still deciding how high it intends to rise. The vegetation is still inventing new ways to knit itself into a single continuous organism. The silence is the sound of immense, patient negotiation between stone, water, and green life.

You can drift for an entire day in one direction and the character of the place barely changes—always the black knives of fresh stone, always the dense, swaying cathedrals of pale stalks, always the warm, ordinary light falling through water that should not be here at this elevation. The Stillborn Sea is not a landscape that has settled. It is a landscape that has just opened its eyes underwater, and it is in no hurry to close them again.
