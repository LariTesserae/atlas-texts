**The Weeping Cradle**

You step down into it and the world narrows at once. The depression is no larger than a modest cathedral, yet so densely crowded with form that it feels far smaller, almost intimate, like a room that has learned how to breathe. Low walls of smoothed limestone enclose it on every side, their surfaces rounded by ages of patient water until they resemble the flanks of enormous sleeping animals. Nothing here is sharp. Everything has been worried into curves.

The air itself is wet. Not misty in the ordinary way, but *condensing*: a warm, heavy breath that beads on your skin the moment you enter and refuses to leave. Temperature lingers in the low eighties, the kind of warmth that makes breath and atmosphere feel like the same substance. Water is everywhere and nowhere. It does not roar or flood. It weeps. From every overhang, from the undersides of ledges, from the bellies of bulbous flowstone bosses, slow perfect drops fall at intervals so regular they feel devotional. Each drop lands in its own shallow basin with a single, clear *tok* that travels through the saturated air like a struck bell. The sound is sparse, almost sacred. Between the drops there is only the softest hiss of water moving in films so thin they barely deserve the name *sheet*.

There is no vegetation. Not a blade, not a lichen, not even the usual crust of moss that likes damp stone. The chemistry here is too rich, too perfectly balanced. The water carries heavy loads of calcium carbonate, magnesium, and stranger dissolved salts; where it flows, it deposits. Where it sits, it builds. The entire floor is a single continuous sculpture of travertine and flowstone: thousands of overlapping rims, tiny dams, miniature terraces, and pale, fleshy-looking mounds that rise knee- to chest-high. They press against one another with no space between, a mineral crowd. Narrow channels and rills snake between them, never more than a handspan wide, carrying water the color of very weak tea. In places the stone has grown into translucent curtains no thicker than parchment; in others it forms smooth, cream-colored benches and thrones that look almost intentional.

Everything is the color of old bone, butter, and faint rust. Occasional bacterial mats stain the stone in muted halos of peach, mint, and iron red, but they are not life as we usually mean it. They are simply part of the mineral grammar.

The Cradle has been in equilibrium so long that the concept of *change* feels like an insult. Erosion and deposition have reached perfect parity. The same drops have been falling in the same places for so many centuries that delicate soda-straw formations hang from certain overhangs like frozen eyelashes, some of them grown into the very stone basins they feed. If you were to return in ten thousand years the pattern would be identical. The place is not dead. It is finished.

Scattered among the mineral bodies are the faint marks of hands. Primitive, almost shy. A low wall of dry-stacked stone no higher than your waist, fitted without mortar, now half-engulfed by new travertine. A cairn of carefully chosen pebbles balanced on a flat boss; the lowest stones have begun to grow into the living rock. In one sheltered alcove sits a single hand-carved basin, its rim worn smooth by generations of palms. Inside it, water has collected and precipitated a tiny, perfect stalagmite that has grown exactly to the level of the rim and stopped, as though obeying some agreement. Here and there you find a stone tool — a blunt hand-axe, a pestle — lying exactly where it was set down centuries ago, now lightly coated in fresh pearl.

Fauna is almost absent. Once in a great while something pale and blind moves in the deepest pools — a translucent shrimp no longer than a fingernail, or a salamander the color of skimmed milk that regards you with lidless eyes before sinking back into its crevice. They do not seem to belong to time. They belong to the equilibrium.

The light is dim and sourceless, a perpetual pearl-grey that arrives already tired. It filters down through the perpetual condensation above, never quite reaching full day. Shadows pool thickly between the crowded stone bodies. The overall impression is of a place that has been left in a half-dream for longer than your species has existed, gently sweating, gently dripping, gently growing one molecule at a time into its final and perfect shape.

Nothing here wants anything from you.  
It only asks that you move carefully, breathe its warm wet air, and listen to the slow, patient music of water learning how to never change.
