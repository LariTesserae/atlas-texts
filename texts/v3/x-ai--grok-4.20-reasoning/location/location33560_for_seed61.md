The place is called the Hollow Seam.

You reach it only by crawling. A fracture no wider than a shoulder, hidden in the root of a fault line three hundred meters below the surface, opens like a reluctant mouth. The rock there is neither limestone nor granite but something intermediate—pale, almost fatty, veined with quartz that catches what little light exists and turns it into slow, greenish milk. Once inside, the passage widens by degrees until you can stand, and then the Seam itself reveals its contradiction: it is no larger than a modest cathedral, yet it feels stuffed to bursting. Every cubic meter is claimed.

The air is cool and permanently damp, 0.6 on the tongue, the temperature locked so precisely that dew condenses on every surface at the exact rate it drips away. The ceiling—never more than twelve meters high—weeps without pause. Hundreds of thousands of individual drops fall at once, each perfectly timed to the rhythm of the last ten thousand years. The sound is enormous: not the romantic patter of caves, but a roaring, layered clatter, as though the mountain itself is grinding its teeth. The acoustics of the Seam have been shaped by generations of careful chiseling so that the dripping becomes a single, continuous chord—low, hoarse, and loud enough to make the teeth ache. Newcomers often press their hands over their ears; residents have forgotten there was ever silence.

There is no vegetation. Not a moss, not a filament. What looks like soft black carpet from a distance is instead a living mineral skin—chemosynthetic mats no thicker than a fingernail that drink dissolved iron and sulfur from the weeping walls. They glow a sullen amber where the light touches them, the only native illumination. Everything else is stone and craft.

The city is built directly into and out of the living rock, so densely that the distinction between architecture and geology has been lost. Structures do not stand apart; they bulge, interlock, and fuse. Narrow lanes twist between bulbous towers that were once stalagmites, now hollowed by hand into spiraling staircases and sleeping niches. Bridges no wider than a palm arc overhead, carved so thin they sing when the heavy drops strike them. Every surface is worked—incised with spiral grooves, stippled with thumbprint-sized alcoves, inscribed with the angular script of a people who have never seen the sun. The labor is still ongoing. Small figures move everywhere, tapping, scraping, polishing. Their tools are stone and bone and hardened chitin pried from the rare blind centipedes that sometimes wander in from deeper cracks. The constant chipping adds a metallic, ringing counterpoint to the dripping, so the whole Seam sings with two permanent voices.

The inhabitants themselves are squat, pale, almost translucent beings the locals call the Seamwards. Their skin is damp and slightly adhesive, the color of wet chalk. They do not speak; they click and drum on the stone with specialized ridges on their forearms, adding yet another percussive layer to the roar. Their city is perfectly vertical—homes stacked like honeycomb cells from floor to ceiling, connected by a lattice of hand- and foot-holds so intricate that the entire chamber functions as a single three-dimensional street. Density is extreme. From any vantage the eye is never more than two meters from carved stone or another body. Yet nothing collides. The Seamwards move with the unthinking choreography of ants, never hurried, never still.

Fauna is rare and strange. A few species only: translucent eels the length of a finger that swim in the narrow gutters, feeding on the mineral mats; fist-sized cave moths with wings like damp parchment that beat once every seven heartbeats; and the centipedes already mentioned, armored in segments of living silica. Nothing else. The food chain is absurdly short and perfectly balanced. What the Seamwards do not eat of the mineral skin, the eels consume. What the eels excrete, the moths lick from the walls. Nothing accumulates. Nothing diminishes. The same number of mouths, the same quantity of flesh, the same volume of water, have persisted without measurable change for longer than any record reaches.

Light is dim and sour. It comes from the mineral skin itself and from fist-sized crystal lanterns the Seamwards grow by patiently pressing certain salts into fissures until they fruit into slow luminescence. The color is the yellow of old bone. Shadows are thick and velvety; they seem to pool in the carved grooves like ink.

The weirdness is subtle but absolute. The Seam is in frozen equilibrium so complete that time itself feels negotiated. Erosion and construction have reached perfect parity: every gram of stone removed by tools is exactly replaced by the slow deposition of new mineral from the weeping ceiling. The Seamwards understand this. Their oldest law is not to alter the balance. When a new child is born, an elder voluntarily walks into the deepest fissure and allows the rock to close over them, body and all, adding their minerals back to the system. The population never increases, never decreases. The dripping never quickens or slows. The temperature never shifts by a tenth of a degree.

There is a central cistern—perfectly round, perfectly still—where all the weeping ultimately gathers before vanishing through an invisible throat in the floor. Around its rim the Seamwards have carved a single continuous spiral groove that records every generation. The groove has no beginning and, so far, no end. It simply turns.

If you stand very still in the middle of the roaring, dripping, clicking dark, you can feel the place resisting any story that ends. It has become its own rule: a pocket universe of stone and moisture and deliberate, endless maintenance. A city that is also a single room. A civilization that is also a stalemate with geology.

Nothing here was ever young. Nothing here will ever be allowed to die. The Hollow Seam simply *is*, loud and damp and perfectly full, forever.
