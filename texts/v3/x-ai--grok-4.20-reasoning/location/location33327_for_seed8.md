**The Weeping Plain**

It is not a swamp, nor a lake, nor even a proper marsh in the usual sense. It is what remains after every mountain, every hill, every youthful jut of stone has been erased. The land has reached its terminal form: a single, low, warm sheet of saturated ground that stretches for sixty or seventy miles in every direction before the haze swallows it. Elevation is so minimal that a rise of three feet feels like a ridge. The horizon is not dramatic; it simply fades, as though the world grew tired of defining itself and chose instead to dissolve into vapor.

Water is not a feature here. It is the medium. It lies in trembling sheets no deeper than a finger’s width across whole leagues of ground. It beads on every surface. It sweats from the ancient bedrock that surfaces in smooth, rounded bosses like the skulls of buried giants. These stones do not drip so much as *weep*—a slow, perpetual condensation that gathers in the creases of stone that have not changed shape in twenty million years. The air itself is wet; you breathe water that has already passed through the bodies of frogs and dragonflies and mist. Even when no rain has fallen for weeks, the ground feels post-rain, post-deluge, post-everything.

Vegetation is sparse and aristocratic. A clump of waist-high reeds here. A single ironwood tree there—gnarled, black, impossibly old—standing in its own wide clearing as if it has outlived every rival and now refuses company. Between these scattered presences lie wide, open stretches of wet earth covered only in a velvet of microscopic algae and liverworts so low they look like green breath on the ground. The emptiness between living things is deliberate, balanced. Each island of green feels like a deliberate pause in an otherwise flattened composition.

Yet the plain throngs with life.

The sound is enormous, constant, and ancient. A roaring, pulsing, clicking, trilling wall of noise that never rises to frenzy and never falls to silence. Millions of frogs—some thumb-sized and screaming like torn metal, others deep and bronchial—maintain a chorus that has likely continued without significant variation since the Eocene. Dragonflies the length of a finger rattle past in squadrons, their wings producing a dry, electric buzz that cuts through the amphibian roar. Wading birds—great blue herons, jabiru storks, limpkins—call at intervals with voices like creaking gates and breaking pottery. Hidden in the shallow sheets of water, schools of small fish flash silver when a cloud passes. Turtles with mossy shells haul themselves onto the weeping rocks and remain motionless for days. Everything is loud, but nothing is hurried.

The light is ordinary tropical sun filtered through perpetual low humidity, giving every color a slightly over-ripe, golden cast. Shadows are soft. Distances are hard to judge. A tree that looks an hour’s walk away may take four. The scale is regional rather than continental—you could cross it in a week if you were merciless—but the eye registers it as boundless because there is no hierarchy of feature. One weeping rock is much like another. One slow, ankle-deep channel braids into the next without preference. The land has achieved a kind of geological nirvana: it has been eroded until drama became impossible.

Nothing is young here. Even the insects seem to belong to ancient lineages. The few mammals—small deer no larger than dogs, otter-like creatures with webbed feet—move with the unhurried patience of animals that have never needed to fear sudden change. A tree that falls will lie in its shallow pool for decades before the water and microbes finish their patient disassembly. New channels form on century scales. The plain is not dying. It is finished. It has settled into its final, stable configuration and now simply persists, warm, loud, and wet, while the rest of the world continues its adolescent thrashing elsewhere.

There are no trails except those worn by generations of soft-footed creatures. No ruins. No marks. Only the slow exhalation of a planet that has already done its growing and is now content to breathe through frogs and mist and the endless weeping of primordial stone.
