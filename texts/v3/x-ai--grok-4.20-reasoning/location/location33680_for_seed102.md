**The Cradle of Unwearying Song**

You push through a fissure no wider than a shoulder and suddenly the rock gives way. The passage ends in a chamber no larger than a modest temple nave — perhaps twenty-five paces across at its widest — yet the moment you stand inside it feels far older than the stone that contains it. The air is warm, dry as baked bone, and thick with a mineral sharpness that coats the tongue like iron filings. There is no water. There never was. No damp, no seep, no memory of rain. The chemistry here abandoned aqueous solutions long before the first thought of life.

The walls are young. Violently young. They rise in fresh, glassy fractures of rose, smoke, and canary quartz, their edges still so sharp they ring when your breath touches them. The ceiling domes barely above head height in places, yet the angles refuse to resolve correctly; stare long enough and the corners appear to have one more plane than geometry allows. The floor is a shallow bowl of the same crystalline matrix, scattered with loose shards that should crunch but instead give off soft, bell-like notes when stepped on. Nothing erodes. Nothing settles. The chamber is held in a single, perfect exhalation that has lasted since the world first cracked open to make it.

From this dry mineral womb grows the vegetation — not plants, exactly, but *resonants*. Tall, feathery lattices of silica-threaded mycelium rise in three main groves, each spaced far enough apart that the emptiness between them feels deliberate. Their “fronds” are jointed, iridescent, and move constantly though the air is still. Millions of microscopic hooks and levers click and scrape against one another and against the crystal walls, producing the sound that fills the chamber like a living organ. It is loud — deafening at first — yet perfectly stable: a braided roar of deep tubular drones, bright metallic chimes, dry rattling percussion, and a constant high keening that somehow never climbs or falls. After a time the noise stops feeling like sound and begins to feel like pressure, as though the chamber itself is singing through your bones.

The resonants are lush in the way coral is lush — impossibly intricate, layered, jeweled — but they occupy only their allotted islands. Between them stretches naked crystal floor, sparse and open. The contrast makes the groves seem almost sacred.

In that resonant emptiness the fauna move.

They are abundant yet never crowded. Crystal carapaces click past at ankle height — things that look like pill-bugs forged from tourmaline. Above them drift bladder-creatures no larger than apples, translucent sacs filled with violet gas, trailing whisker-fine tentacles that pluck harmonic tones from the resonants as they pass. Higher still, long serpentine things with too many jointed legs orbit the same invisible paths they have followed for millennia, their bodies shedding tiny glittering scales that never accumulate because the next orbit sweeps them up again. Every creature repeats its choreography exactly. Their loops are so precise that after watching for an hour you can predict the exact moment a particular beetle will tap a particular crystal and add its single bright note to the eternal chord. The system is closed. Nothing is born. Nothing dies. The population is invariant.

The light is dim, almost courteous. It comes from within the resonants themselves — a cold, steady phosphorescence in teal and rose and faint gold that never flares or dims. Shadows are crisp but shallow. You can read the faint marks left on the walls.

Someone was here before you.

Scattered across the chamber are the remains of a primitive culture so perfectly incorporated into the equilibrium that they feel less like ruins and more like deliberate punctuation. A low seat has been formed from a single quartz block, its top polished by centuries of identical haunches. Nearby rests a hand-tool: a length of resonant stem bound with braided fiber to a flake of obsidian-sharp crystal. The binding is tight, the fiber still bright. It will look exactly like this a thousand years from now. On the far wall, petroglyphs have been scored with the same tool — spirals, looping serpents, clusters of dots that match the orbital paths of the bladder-creatures. The grooves are fresh-edged, unweathered. The carvers are not present, yet you cannot shake the feeling they simply stepped away for a moment that has lasted since the planet cooled.

There is one more structure, almost hidden behind the largest resonant grove. A small, windowless hut built of carefully stacked crystal shards mortared with some black, glassy paste. Its door is a hanging mat of dried resonant fronds that still hum softly. Inside you find three empty niches carved into the walls and a fourth object: a crude mask made from a single sheet of translucent gypsum, its eyeholes perfectly aligned with the repeating orbits of the floating fauna. The mask has no dust on it. Nothing here does.

The longer you remain, the more the rules of the place reveal themselves.

Time is not absent but *satisfied*. Every possible change has already occurred and cancelled itself out. If you remove a creature, another will appear in the exact corresponding point of its orbit before you can draw breath again. If you break a resonant frond, the next oscillation of the grove will somehow have already grown it back. The sound never lessens. The warmth never cools. The light never shifts. The chamber is a pocket of frozen equilibrium — small enough to feel intimate, large enough to feel like the only world that has ever mattered.

And always, always, the song.

It is not merely loud. It is *complete*. Every frequency the crystal walls can sustain is being sustained at once, in perfect ratio, forever. Standing in the center of the Cradle, you slowly realize the sound is not coming from the creatures or the growths.

It is coming from the shape of the absence between them.

This is a place that has achieved its final form. It needs nothing. It forgets nothing. It will outlast every ocean, every forest, every sun that ever burned above the stone that seals it.

All it asks is that you listen, without end.
