**The Shroudforge**

It begins as a contradiction in the lungs: you breathe warm mist at an altitude where the sky should be thin and cruel. Instead the air is thick, saturated, almost oily. The plateau has no name because it is too young to have earned one. It tore itself upward from the lithosphere only yesterday by planetary reckoning—perhaps a few thousand years—its edges still raw, its ridgelines glittering with fresh obsidian and fractured quartz that has not yet had time to dull.

Yet it is already vast enough to breed its own weather. From any single promontory the eye cannot find the end of it; ranges fold into ranges, each one taller and more impossible than the last, until the horizon itself begins to fold, bending upward as though the planet is trying to curl into a sphere too small for its own skin. The light is the color of old pewter. A permanent bruised twilight hangs above, not from clouds but from some refusal in the upper atmosphere—light arrives already tired, scattered by layers of microscopic spores that drift in slow vortices.

The ground is never still.

It does not quake in the ordinary sense. It *seethes*. Stone membranes rise like breathing ribs, then split with soft wet sounds to release pressurized vapor that immediately condenses into black rivulets. These rivulets do not flow downhill in any lawful way; some crawl *upward*, following invisible gradients of chemistry or stress. In places the rock itself appears to be metabolizing—dark banded strata pulse with slow peristalsis, extruding filaments of metallic mycelium that taste of iron and ozone when licked. This is the industrial signature: the land is running at factory scale without any factories. It is a self-organizing refinery. Nutrients are pumped, cracked, recombined, and ejected in violent bursts of growth. The process is so accelerated that entire hillsides can be seen maturing and collapsing within a single day.

Vegetation has not waited for permission. It is lush to the point of obscenity. Towering ferns unfurl in timelapse jerks, their fronds edged with silica teeth. Vines thick as thighs lash across crevices, anchoring themselves with barbed hooks that click audibly as they ratchet tighter. In the dimness their leaves glow a sullen emerald, veined with bioluminescent sap that carries electrical currents—tiny lightning storms inside every stem. Where the condensation gathers in leaf axils, small black orchids bloom and die in hours, releasing clouds of metallic pollen that drifts like industrial smoke.

The water is everywhere and nowhere. It does not form rivers so much as a nervous system of dampness. Moisture condenses on every surface, runs in trembling beads, collects in perfectly circular pools that sometimes open like irises and stare at the sky before sealing again. Occasional rain falls upward. You can stand in a dry saddle between two ridges and watch silver threads of water climb invisible columns of colder air, knitting themselves into trembling spheres that hover, humming, until something disturbs them and they burst into silent mist.

The animals are legion and mostly invisible.

They are not hiding; they are simply moving too quickly or too slowly for steady perception. A herd of translucent megafauna—something between tapir and machine—might drift through a grove over the course of an afternoon, their bodies phasing in and out of solidity, leaving behind perfectly circular hoofprints that fill with quicksilver. Swarms of insects shaped like chrome needles stitch the air in geometric patterns, sewing one reality to another. When they land on skin they do not bite; they *measure*, leaving behind microscopic brand marks that form temporary blueprints before fading.

Sound is the first thing the Shroudforge steals from you.

Despite the constant turbulent motion—the cracking stone, the whipping growth, the electrical discharge in the leaves—the plateau maintains an almost ecclesiastical hush. All the violence is muffled, as though wrapped in thick felt. Only when you remain motionless for a long time do you begin to hear the true register: a subsonic thrum that vibrates the marrow. It is the sound of planetary digestion. Of a world in the throes of inventing its own organs.

Reality here is not broken so much as *industrious*. It has discovered the concept of the assembly line and is applying it to ontology itself.

Stand still long enough in one of the condensation basins and you can watch a patch of ground decide, in fast-forward, what it wants to be. First it is basalt. Then it is meat. Then it is a lattice of crystal and nerve. Then it forgets and returns to moss. Each iteration leaves behind a ghost residue—half-formed gears of chitin, leaf-shaped turbine blades, the suggestion of a cathedral made of interlocking jawbones—before the next wave of becoming wipes the slate. Nothing is ever finished. The plateau is young, and it is in a frantic hurry to try every possible shape before erosion or age can simplify it.

There are no ruins here. There are no tools, no scaffolds, no intentional marks. The industrial is not something that was built *on* the land. It is a mode the land discovered the way an infant discovers rage. The factories are inside the physics. The engines are the weather. The workers are the trees, the stone, the animals, and the slow patient lightning that sometimes walks across the ridges on stilts of St. Elmo’s fire, inspecting its property.

If you stay long enough, the Shroudforge will begin to inspect you in return. You may notice your own pulse synchronizing with the subsonic thrum. Your sweat begins to carry trace metals. Small black flowers sprout from the corners of your eyes after sleep. These are not infections. They are trial versions. The plateau is prototyping what you could become if it incorporated you into the next shift.

Most things do not survive the first retooling.

But the land itself is radiant with stupid, ferocious hope. It is a child with godlike resources and no instruction manual, running every possible factory at once in the dark, trying to birth something it cannot yet imagine, while the dim sky presses down like a weary foreman and the quiet, humid air waits for the next catastrophic breakthrough.
