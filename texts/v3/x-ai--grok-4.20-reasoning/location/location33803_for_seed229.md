**The Resonant Basin**

You descend into it through a narrow notch between two weathered ridges, the air warm but so thin it makes your pulse loud in your ears. The surrounding slopes are nothing but sterile scree and wind-scoured feldspar; then the notch opens and the Basin simply *is*, cupped inside the high peaks like a secret room the size of a small hamlet. Nothing prepares you for the noise.

It is loud enough to feel in the teeth. A constant, layered roar of chimes, cracks, glassy scrapes, and deep organ-like drones. The sound never settles. It surges, collapses, reconfigures.

There is no water. Not a drop, not even memory of it. The chemistry here long ago abandoned aqueous solutions for something slower and sharper: mineral vapors that rise on geothermal breath from fissures in the basin floor. These vapors condense directly into crystal, and the crystal grows. That is the vegetation.

The growths are lush in the way a coral reef is lush, except every polyp is a translucent, razor-edged lattice two to four times the height of a person. They rise sparsely, thirty or forty paces between each major colony, so the eye can travel across open mineral pavement and still feel the space as airy, almost cathedral-like. Each colony is a riot of self-similar branching—fractal antlers, feathery sprays, dripping chandelier cascades—all of it singing. When the warm updrafts stroke them, the thinner blades hum at different pitches; the whole basin becomes one enormous aeolian harp played by invisible hands.

The color is wrong. Not the greens of living things, but the deep bottled greens, violent magentas, and electric cobalts that only pure crystal can manufacture when light barely reaches it. The light itself is dim, filtered by the high surrounding walls and by perpetual veils of suspended mineral dust that turn the air into pale amber glass. Shadows are sharp. Every edge throws secondary rainbows that crawl like living things.

Nothing here is still for long.

A pillar thirty paces away suddenly sprouts a new tier with a sound like a cathedral bell struck by a hammer. Elsewhere a whole thicket overbalances, collapses in languid slow motion, and detonates across the pavement in a glittering avalanche. The shards do not lie where they fall. Within minutes opportunistic feeder-crystals—tiny, needle-like growths—begin to migrate across the debris like a slow silver mold, drinking the vapor released by the shattered structures. Destruction and birth are the same gesture. The basin has been performing this same turbulent ballet for so many thousands of years that the overall pattern has settled into an established, almost serene balance. It is old, and it is never the same twice.

The fauna is everywhere, loud and strange.

Swarms of glass-winged pseudoinsects rise in glittering clouds when a new growth surge begins, their wings producing a high metallic keening that drills straight into the inner ear. Herds of low, millipede-like grazers—each segment a living nodule of carapace and crystal—move in clattering waves, rasping edible lattices with diamond mandibles. Higher up, long-legged “herons” stalk between the major colonies on stilts of fused quartz. Their bodies are mostly hollow, resonant chambers; when they call, the sound is a deep wooden groan that makes the larger crystals shiver in sympathy and throw off fresh shoots.

Everything here is partly mineral, partly animal. The distinction is academic.

Among the largest colonies stand the only traces of the built. A handful of primitive shelters—little more than lean-tos and windbreaks—have been wedged between buttresses of living crystal. Their builders are small, chitinous humanoids whose own exoskeletons have begun to resemble the lattice-work around them. They use nothing more advanced than hand tools of sharpened crystal and braided fiber harvested from the feeder-crystals. You see one now: a lone figure standing on a natural shelf thirty feet up a pillar, patiently tapping a new growth with a stone mallet, harvesting a pure tone. The sound he makes is immediately answered by half the basin, a rolling choral boom that seems to approve.

Their marks are faint and ancient. Carved spiral glyphs on fallen monoliths. A single standing stone, no taller than a child, etched so many times that the groove is nearly a tunnel. Occasional tools left behind as offerings or because their owners simply walked away mid-task. The crystal eats everything eventually; even memory is slowly being overgrown.

There is no sky, only the dim amber lid of dust and the black knives of the surrounding peaks. Sometimes the vapor rises in a sudden thick pillar and the entire basin goes silent for three heartbeats. Then every crystal within sight detonates into simultaneous new growth, a roaring, chiming, shattering wall of sound and light so violent it feels like the world is being born again.

When the spasm passes, the new landscape is already different. A path that existed an hour ago is now blocked by a fresh chandelier thicket. A shelter has been gently lifted three feet higher on a newly sprouted column. The herons have moved. The glyphs have acquired new layers of crystalline skin.

This is not a place that tolerates visitors. It tolerates inhabitants only if they learn to change as quickly as the crystal does. Most do not. Their bones become beautiful white lattices that the feeder-crystals wear like jewelry for another few centuries before they, too, are incorporated.

The basin does not care.

It is warm, loud, sparse, overcrowded with life, and utterly empty of anything that remembers water. It has found a better way to be lush.
