**The Glasswomb Ascent**

You stand on a fractured shelf of newly born obsidian at an elevation so extreme the sky has weight. It does not press down; it presses *in*, a dense, warm darkness that feels like black wool soaked in heated oil. There is no sun, no moon, no stars—only this sealed, starless vault above, perhaps a permanent accretion disc of planetary dust that never quite condensed. The air itself is hot (not furnace-hot, but the steady, fevered warmth of something that has never known night’s cooling). It tastes of iron and heated flint.

The land is still deciding what it will be.

Beneath your feet the crust is thin and restless. You can feel the slow, enormous breathing of the world: a deep, almost sub-audible throb that makes the fine glass needles on the shelf tremble and sing at pitches just below hearing. Every few hours the shelf itself will crack with a sound like a cathedral bell struck once and then smothered. When it does, new black blades shoulder upward through the fissure—some no taller than a man, others already climbing like dark cathedral spires hundreds of meters high. They do not erupt. They *grow*, slow and deliberate, as though the planet were trying to remember the concept of “mountain” and kept changing its mind.

There is no water. The chemistry here is anhydrous and violent. Silicate melts and exotic sulfur-metal compounds migrate through cracks under pressure, flash-cooling into iridescent volcanic glass the color of congealed midnight. Some surfaces are so smooth they throw back your own dim reflection as a smear of deeper black. Others are feathered with fractal barbs that look almost like feathers or fur but are only mineral habit following mathematical restlessness.

Vegetation is meaningless here. What grows is geometry. Certain crystal lattices seed themselves in repeating self-similar patterns that, from a distance, can look like ruined colonnades or the half-finished scaffolding of cathedrals that were never meant for hands. These are the only “structures.” They are not built. They are *permitted* by the turbulent mathematics unfolding below. One such formation—a lone, perfectly hexagonal tower of obsidian three stories high—stands alone on the next shelf over, its surfaces still faintly warm from its recent birth. You will find others scattered across the regional scale of the plateau: a single arch, a half-buried cube, a spiral stair that leads nowhere and ends in a razor point. They appear, persist for a few centuries, then fracture and are swallowed again as the land reconfigures.

The turbulence is constant but mostly silent.

Occasionally you hear the glassy avalanche—thousands of black needles sliding down a newborn slope with a sound like distant chandeliers destroying themselves. More often there is only the low organ-note of stressed crystal under pressure, and the soft hiss of metallic vapor venting from fresh fissures. The air shimmers above these vents in veils of superheated nothing. Sometimes piezoelectric lightning—silent, branching blue-white—leaps between two freshly separated faces of glass, illuminating the darkness for half a second before the world drinks the light again.

Nothing lives here. Not even the simplest extremophiles have found purchase. The only motion belongs to the land itself. Great plates tilt like waking beasts. New ridgelines burst upward in slow motion, shoving older formations aside so they topple in dreamlike silence across kilometers of empty black glass plain. The scale is too large for any single view to contain. You can walk for days between major features and still feel you have not left the same embryonic thought the planet is having.

The darkness is not absolute. It is textured.

Faint crimson lymph glows in the deepest crevasses where mantle material still breathes against the newborn crust. Certain crystal faces have trapped pockets of exotic gas that phosphoresce a cold, liver-colored light when stressed. These are the only colors that truly exist here: black, deeper black, blood-red, and the occasional poisonous violet where two crystal lattices grind together and release stored energy.

The rule of this place is simple and merciless:

It is still being born.  
It does not want you here.  
It does not hate you.  
It simply has not yet decided whether “you” is a category it needs to accommodate, and while it decides, the ground will keep rearranging itself beneath your feet with the absent-minded violence of a child kicking through sandcastles it has not finished building.

Stay in one place long enough and you will see a new mountain rise where none existed an hour ago—raw, flawless, already taller than any peak on worlds that have had billions of years to cool. The old one beside it will sigh, crack along perfect geometric faults, and collapse into a glittering avalanche that sounds, for just a moment, like applause.

Then the silence returns, warm and absolute, and the Glasswomb keeps dreaming its dark, mathematical dreams beneath the starless pressure of its own sky.
