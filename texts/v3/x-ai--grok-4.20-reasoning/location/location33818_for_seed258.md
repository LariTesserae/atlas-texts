**The Weeping Lattice**

You stand on a high plateau that should not feel like a plateau. The ground itself is alive — a dense, resilient mat of interwoven root and rhizome, pale ochre shot through with veins of viridian, giving slightly underfoot like living cartilage. It is not soil. There is no mineral substrate anywhere. Every surface, every contour, every cliff and colonnade is plant.

The air is warm and heavy, 1.8 on the scale of temperature but thickened by constant condensation. Water does not fall from the sky here; it is *exhaled*. Every leaf, every fibrous pillar, every horizontal shelf sweats. Droplets form, swell, and detach in slow motion, creating a perpetual vertical drizzle that reverses the usual direction of rain: the sky is dry, the structures weep. The sound of it is everywhere — a thousand thousand soft impacts, a metallic ringing where drops strike hollow stems, a low wet percussion that never ceases. This is the loudness the place demands. The plateau sings with its own dripping.

Yet the structures are sparse.

Across the wide, open basin — many miles across, regional in scale — colossal living forms rise at irregular intervals. They look like architecture that has not yet decided whether it is temple, tree, or geological event. Enormous buttressed columns of braided wood and leaf rise two hundred meters, their surfaces still raw, as though growth halted mid-emergence. You can see individual fibers the thickness of ship masts still twisting around one another in slow, frozen helices. Some columns flare at the top into impossible cantilevered roofs of overlapping fronds, edges ragged and embryonic. Others form half-finished arches that should collapse but do not; the living tension in their fibers holds them in perfect equilibrium. They have looked exactly like this for longer than time has meaning here.

Nothing erodes. Nothing matures. The landscape is permanently *emerging* while never actually changing.

Between these titanic plant-structures lie wide, almost empty stretches of the root-mat, punctuated only by scattered knee-high polypores and waist-high thickets of translucent, veinless grass that chimes when the constant wind moves through it. The vegetation is total — there is no bare earth, no stone, no dead matter — yet the *density of incident* is low. Your eye travels long distances between wonders. The space feels both overcrowded with life and majestically lonely.

The light is normal, almost cruelly so. A bright, even daylight falls through a high, diffuse canopy of linked leaves far overhead, rendering every droplet a tiny prism. Rainbows no larger than your thumb appear and vanish on every surface. The colors of the plants themselves are wrong in a way that prickles the mind: not green alone but bruised plums, oxidized copper, the blue-white of bone marrow, and a particular shade of gold that only appears on new growth that is, here, also ancient.

There are almost no animals. Once in a long while something like a pale, many-winged seed drifts past on the wind, but it is not clear if it is fauna or reproductive material. You have the persistent feeling of being watched by the vegetation itself. The lattice *knows* you are here. It simply does not care. Its equilibrium is perfect and includes you as a temporary moisture gradient, nothing more.

The rules of this world are simple and absolute:

- All matter is alive and continuous. Cut something and it bleeds sap, then seals, then grows back exactly as it was.
- Decay and growth are locked in perfect balance. Nothing rots. Nothing truly dies.
- Sound is mandatory. The constant dripping, the wind moving through hollowed trunks tuned like organ pipes, the low creak of fibers under eternal tension — these are not side effects. They are the metabolism of the place.
- Time has been retired. The light never shifts. The temperature never drops. The half-finished columns will remain half-finished forever, caught at the exact moment of becoming.

You walk toward the nearest colossus. Its base is a kilometer wide, a fused mass of root and trunk that rises in buttresses like the roots of an impossible banyan. Water sheens down its sides in continuous sheets. Where it meets the ground-mat, the impact creates a constant low roar that you feel in your teeth. Up close you can see that the “architecture” is still growing, but so slowly that the motion is invisible. A single new fiber may take a century to twist into place. The structure is being born at the speed of geology while wearing the face of a cathedral that was never built by hands.

This is not a forest.  
This is not a ruin.  
This is a single organism expressing itself as landscape, frozen at the moment of its most beautiful incompleteness, loudly, wetly, eternally emerging.
