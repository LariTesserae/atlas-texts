**The Hollow Lattice**

You stand inside a single, self-contained breathing chamber no larger than a modest cathedral or a walled hamlet. Its boundaries are living and absolute: a continuous mass of pale, cool-green vegetation that has become both ground and roof, substrate and architecture. There is no soil, only layered mats of moss so thick and interlaced that your boots sink a finger’s depth before meeting the resilient, rubbery weave beneath. From this living floor rise colossal stems—some straight as factory chimneys, others spiraling like slow augers—each thick enough that three people linking arms could not encircle one. These stems fork and re-fork into a vaulted canopy that meets overhead in a lattice of interlocking branches no wider than a handspan. The gaps between them are deliberate, almost architectural, letting in normal, silvery daylight that arrives already cooled and filtered.

The air is damp but not wet. Moisture condenses on every leaf and metal surface in minute, trembling beads that gather, tremble, and fall in irregular ticking rhythms. There are no rivers, only hundreds of thumb-sized pockets where water collects in leaf-bowls, in the cupped elbows of roots, in the rusting troughs of old machinery. Every few hours a brief, localized rain falls from the canopy above—never more than a fine, cold mist—then stops as though the structure itself has exhaled. The temperature sits at the edge of a shiver; your breath sometimes ghosts in front of you.

Scattered sparsely through this green volume are the industrial works. They do not feel added to the plants; they feel secreted by them. A brick boiler house has been half-digested, its walls now bark-skinned, its windows replaced by translucent membranes of living tissue that glow faintly when the hidden engines inside cycle. Great iron pipes, green with patina, disappear into hollowed stems and re-emerge elsewhere, sap and condensate running alongside whatever blackish lubricant still moves through them. Conveyor belts of hardened, flexible fiber—grown, not woven—creep in slow, peristaltic waves, carrying fist-sized nodules of resin from one processing sac to the next. The machines are old, heavy, riveted, and mostly quiet. Only a low, almost sub-audible thrum vibrates through the root-mass, like a heartbeat felt through the ribs of the floor.

This place has been in balance for centuries. The erosion parameter has settled into mutual architecture: metal and cellulose have learned each other’s contours so completely that it is impossible to say where the original factory ended and the plant began. Roots have lifted flagstones and re-set them at gentle angles; vines have become guy-wires; old smokestacks now serve as chimneys for transpiration, releasing faint plumes of mist that drift upward and are caught again by the canopy.

Fauna is almost absent. A few translucent insects the color of new leaves move with mechanical patience along prescribed grooves in the stems. Once in a long while something beetle-like, plated in chitin that mimics rusted iron, clicks across an open patch of moss and vanishes into a seam. Nothing larger. The ecosystem has simplified to plant, moisture, and machine.

The weirdness is quiet but persistent. The lattice repeats at multiple scales: the branching pattern of a single stem is identical to the branching pattern of the entire canopy above, as though the whole chamber is a single organism expressing itself fractally. Every few days entire sections of the living architecture shift—slowly, over hours—with soft wooden groans and the trickle of displaced water. The plants are following some internal logic that seems to match the industrial rhythms: when the resin presses reach peak pressure, certain stems widen their internal channels; when the boilers rest, whole quadrants of the canopy tighten, conserving moisture. It is impossible to tell whether the vegetation is exploiting the industry or the industry has been domesticated by the vegetation. Both appear content with the arrangement.

Sound is mostly the architecture itself: the patient ticking of condensation, the soft creak of fiber under tension, the occasional sigh of vapor escaping a pressure valve. Human voices would feel intrusive here. The few workers who still tend the process speak in low murmurs or not at all. They wear oilskin coats beaded with perpetual moisture and move between the scattered industrial nodes like monks between chapels.

This is not ruin. It is a finished state. A cool, damp, breathing industrial grove that has reached equilibrium and now simply continues—processing, transpiring, condensing, growing, releasing—inside its own small, green-walled universe. If you stay long enough you begin to feel the pulse in the floor syncing with your own blood, as though the Lattice is deciding, politely, whether to incorporate you into its next slow, architectural adjustment.
