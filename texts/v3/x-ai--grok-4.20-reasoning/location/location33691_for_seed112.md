**The High Lattice**

The world has no water and has not had any for geological ages. What moisture once existed long ago bound itself into the first living films and never escaped again. Instead the air tastes of ozone, hot iron, and something faintly like crushed fennel. It is cool—perpetually 4 °C at these elevations—so the dry wind feels like a blade drawn across the cheek. Yet the light is merciless. A white-blue star hangs in a sky the color of new steel, and every surface has evolved a refractive sheen that turns the plateau into a single blinding mirror.

From orbit the land would look like a continent-sized sheet of beaten gold-brown foil, but down here the foil reveals itself as a living integument. The vegetation is total. A fibrous, leathery mat—call it the *pelt*—covers every centimeter of rock. It is only finger-thick in the open, yet its mycelial under-weave reaches kilometers deep through fissures and pores, stitching the crust into one planetary organism. Where the pelt is exposed it looks like rust-colored corduroy shot through with violet veins that slowly open and close, breathing gases no earthly plant would recognize.

The pelt is not soft. Walk on it and it feels like walking on the hide of an old elephant: warm, corrugated, faintly elastic. Every few hundred meters it blooms into glassy bristles that chime when the wind crosses them. These are the only “plants” visible in the ordinary sense. Everything else is architecture grown at industrial scale and spaced with the generosity of stars.

Across the high plain the *towers* rise. They stand anywhere from thirty to four hundred kilometers apart—sparse on a planetary hide, yet so enormous that from the base of one the next is still a faint bruise on the horizon. Each tower is a single vegetative individual that has been growing, collapsing, and regrowing for perhaps eighty million years. Their trunks are not solid; they are lattices—kilometer-wide bundles of hollow woody tubes, mineral-reinforced tendons, and pressurized bladders that function exactly like piston chambers. Inside, peristaltic muscles the thickness of subway trains pump supercritical CO₂ and volatile aromatics in place of blood. The sound of that pumping is a continuous subsonic thud that you feel in the teeth before you hear it.

The towers roar.

That is not metaphor. The sound parameter of this world sits at 2.8 because the towers never stop working. Their upper galleries are rigged with sail-leaves the size of city blocks. Wind—shearing at two hundred kilometers per hour up here—slams into them and sets them vibrating like iron drums. Lower down, root mills grind silicate bedrock into talc and release it in glittering plumes that catch the light and turn the air into a permanent haze of ground diamonds. Every few hours a limb the size of a skyscraper fatigues, cracks, and falls in a slow-motion avalanche that sounds like the end of the world. Before the dust has settled, the pelt has already surged over the wreckage, digesting, recycling, rebuilding. The cycle is turbulent, almost frantic, yet the overall forms have reached an established balance. The broken silhouettes against the bright sky have the same inevitability as sea stacks or ancient canyons; they look *meant*.

Between the towers the land is not empty. The pelt supports herds of things that move like gazelles crossed with porcelain beetles. Their legs are jointed glass; their bodies are matte white shells that reflect the brutal light so they appear as flickering absences against the glare. They graze the pelt in vast, silent squadrons, clipping the violet veins and leaving neat parallel tracks that heal within hours. Above them drift the *lungs*—balloon-bodied fauna the size of small whales, translucent bladders veined with black capillaries. They rise on thermals generated by the towers’ waste heat, pulsing slowly, feeding on the aromatic mists the root mills exhale. Their songs are the only sounds that can cut through the mechanical thunder: long, mournful brass notes pitched so low they feel like pressure changes rather than music.

Nothing is built. There are no artifacts, no ruins, no handprints. The industrial roar is entirely biogenic. The towers are simultaneously forest, refinery, power plant, and cathedral, grown from the same genetic libraries that coded the pelt. Their internal chemistry is non-aqueous; they run on organometallic fluids and gas-phase reactions that would be impossible in the presence of water. The bright light feeds them directly through photosynthetic crystals that line every exposed surface, turning the towers into pillars of cold fire.

Stand in the lee of one at midday and the heat rising off its lattice makes the air waver. The wind still cuts like a scalpel, but now it carries a heavy perfume of machine oil and myrrh. The ground trembles with the rhythm of hidden pumps. Every few minutes a new branch erupts from the trunk with a wet cannon-crack, unfurling in seconds, already fully rigged with secondary mills and spore chimneys. Elsewhere a section of the lattice is sloughing away in controlled avalanche, its material already being funneled downward through translucent pipes to be remade elsewhere. Creation and destruction are simultaneous, continuous, deafening.

The rules of this world are simple and merciless:

- All matter is either pelt, tower, or in transit between them.  
- Energy arrives as brutal light and ceaseless wind; nothing else is required.  
- Change is mandatory, yet after eighty million years the pattern has settled into something that looks, from a distance, like peace.  
- Nothing is allowed to remain still. Even the porcelain herds must keep moving or the pelt will gently digest them where they stand.

At dusk—which arrives suddenly at this elevation, the blue-white sun dropping behind the curve like a coin—the towers begin to glow. Bioluminescent bacteria cultured in their outer skins ignite in slow waves, turning each lattice into a vertical constellation. The sound does not diminish; if anything the night wind makes the great leaves sing higher, sharper. The porcelain herds settle into fractal spirals on the pelt, their shells reflecting the new light so the ground itself seems to glitter with fallen stars. The lungs drift higher, becoming invisible except for the low brass notes that now carry for hundreds of kilometers.

And still the root mills grind. Still the bladders pump. Still the planet breathes and sings and breaks and rebuilds itself in the cold, bright, waterless dark.

This is not a landscape. It is a single organism wearing a landscape like a cloak, and the cloak is the size of continents, and it has been awake for longer than most worlds have had life at all.
