**The Basin of the Final Carving**

You stand in a place that has already been finished. Not settled, not grown—*finished*, the way a statue is finished when the sculptor steps back for the last time. The heat is a living thing, thick and wet enough that the air itself seems to sweat. It presses against your skin like a second atmosphere, condensing in tiny beads along any edge it can find: the rim of a leaf, the lip of a stone, the bridge of your nose. There is no wind. There never was.

The ground underfoot is not ground. It is pavement—immense blocks of pale, rose-veined limestone fitted with a precision that makes the joints almost invisible. Every surface still carries the sharp bite of the chisel. You can see the individual tool marks, crisp as yesterday, even though the stones stretch away for miles in every direction. Erosion has not touched this place and never will. Time has been asked, politely but firmly, to stop.

Yet vegetation explodes from it anyway.

Lush does not begin to describe the green. Between the paving stones, in hairline fractures no wider than a fingernail, grow ferns with fronds the size of wagon wheels, their veins glowing an almost painful emerald. Vines thick as a man’s thigh climb the sides of distant monuments in perfect spirals, each leaf placed as though by the same hand that shaped the stones. Orchids the color of fresh blood and sulfur hang in motionless clusters. Nothing drips. Nothing grows. The jungle is here because the design requires it, and the design is complete.

The monuments themselves are scattered across the low basin with deliberate sparsity. A half-mile away rises a stepped ziggurat of black basalt, its edges so new they look wet. Another, smaller, sits three miles off to the northwest, connected by a causeway that crosses a perfectly still mirror of water. Between them lie wide, empty plazas where the only movement is the shimmer of heat above the stone. The scale is human enough to feel made *for* you, yet regional enough that the horizon itself feels like part of the architecture—low constructed ridges ringing the entire basin like the rim of an enormous bowl.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. It weeps from the eyes of colossal carved faces set into the sides of the monuments—great impassive countenances ten stories tall, their tears running in unbroken sheets down grooved channels cut for exactly this purpose. The water is warm, almost hot, and it never splashes. It simply flows, then vanishes into slots in the pavement to reappear somewhere else, in eternal recirculation. Condensation gathers on every vertical surface and slides downward in slow, deliberate pearls. The air itself is a kind of water.

And the animals.

They are abundant beyond counting. Sapphire scarabs the size of your thumb cling to fern stems in their hundreds. Parrots of impossible color perch along the carved balustrades. Tree monitors as long as coffins lounge on sun-warmed stones. A herd of small deer-like creatures—too symmetrical, too perfect—stands frozen mid-stride in the middle of a plaza a mile away. None of them move. Not a tail flick. Not a breath. Their eyes are open, bright, and utterly still, as though the moment of their creation was so flawless that motion became unnecessary afterward. The silence is total. Even your own pulse feels like an intruder.

This is where reality begins to fray.

Look too long at any single thing and the edges go soft. The chisel marks on a nearby obelisk rearrange themselves when you blink—never while you watch, only in the instant of darkness. A flower you were certain was crimson is now veined with gold. The ziggurat that stood due north now stands northeast by a degree or two, as if the entire basin performed an infinitesimal rotation while your attention was elsewhere. The sky is too bright, a white-blue that hurts the teeth. There are no clouds, only the sense that the light is being *poured* from somewhere just above the constructed horizon.

The rules here are simple and absolute:

- Everything that exists was placed by hand.
- Nothing may change once placed.
- The carving is finished.
- Therefore the world is finished.

There is no “nature” beneath the artifice. The substrate itself was quarried, shaped, and laid down in a single inconceivable act of labor using nothing more advanced than levers, copper chisels, and obsessive devotion. Even the microbes that should be breaking down organic matter were apparently carved into existence exactly where they were needed and then told to remain exactly as they are.

You can walk for days and the light will never change. The heat will never lessen. The tears will never stop flowing from the stone faces. The parrots will never fly. The world has achieved its final form, and it is lush, wet, glaring, silent, and quietly, perfectly insane.

Somewhere far off, a carved frog on a carved lily pad reflects the light with such intensity that it becomes difficult to tell where sculpture ends and blasphemy begins. You understand, with the gentle certainty of heatstroke or revelation, that if you were to carve your own name into one of these stones, the stone would simply heal the wound by morning—returning to the perfection demanded by the design.

This place was not built to be inhabited.

It was built to be *complete*.
