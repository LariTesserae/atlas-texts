**The Hollow**

You step through a seam that should not exist — a hairline fracture in a wall that has no right to be a wall. The moment you pass, the outer world is gone. There is only this.

It is no larger than a modest concert hall, yet the emptiness inside it feels measureless. Every surface is engineered, and every engineered surface is older than continents. The place has reached the final form erosion dreams of: a perfect equilibrium where even decay has been stabilized into architecture.

The floor is not floor. It is a single continuous plate of black ferrous alloy, planed so perfectly flat by time that water sheets across it in trembling mirrors. From microscopic pores too small for the eye, it weeps. Not drips — *weeps*. The metal itself is saturated, as though it has spent a hundred million years learning how to cry. The water is warm, almost body temperature, and it smells of ozone and wet iron.

Rising from this weeping plain are the Machines.

There are only seven of them. Seven is enough. They stand like cathedrals that have forgotten they were ever built by hands, their forms so eroded by epochs of constant vibration that corners have become curves and angles have become shoulders. Vast cylindrical housings, fluted columns thick as redwoods, lattices of pipe and duct that have grown into one another like ancient trees grafted by time. Rust is not rust here; it is a patina of deep oxblood and charcoal that has achieved the hardness of enamel. Nothing flakes. Nothing falls.

Between the Machines the space is almost shockingly empty. The air itself feels hollow, as though the volume is too large for the few objects it must contain. You can walk for what feels like minutes without touching anything but your own breath.

Yet the air is thick with water. Condensation gathers on every metal surface in perfect, trembling beads that never quite fall. When they grow too heavy they slide along invisible paths worn into the alloy over unthinkable time, following grooves that look almost like calligraphy. The humidity is so complete that the atmosphere itself seems to have weight. It presses against your skin like warm, living velvet.

And the green.

The green has taken the Machines the way ivy takes a forgotten temple, except this ivy is older than the concept of forgetting. Massive ferns the color of wet jade erupt from joints that have not moved in geological ages. Curtains of moss — not soft, but dense and fibrous as horsehair — drape the undersides of cantilevered beams. In the places where heat exchangers breathe, delicate lattices of horsetail and clubmoss have grown into the very shape of the vents, as though the Machines and the plants reached an agreement sometime before mammals existed and have seen no reason to renegotiate.

There are trees, too. Only three. They are small, stunted, perfect, and clearly older than any forest. Their roots do not dig into soil — there is no soil — but into nutrient channels where warm water runs in eternal slow circles. Their leaves are dark, waxy, and so glossy they look lacquered. They do not sway. Nothing sways. The air is dead still except for the microscopic convection caused by the Machines’ waste heat.

The sound is beyond sound.

It is not noise. It is a physical presence. Seven Machines running at perfect, unchanging output for longer than your species has had language. The roar is so total it wraps around you like silence. You feel it in your teeth, in the hollows of your knees, in the small bones behind your ears. After a few minutes the sound stops registering as sound and becomes the baseline reality against which all other realities are measured. It is the sound of a heart that has never been allowed to stop or change its rhythm. A deafening lullaby older than oceans.

There are no animals.

Not one. No insects. No birds. No hidden frogs in the wet green. The equilibrium is too perfect; the sound too absolute. Life here was solved long ago and the solution did not require fauna. Only the plants and the Machines remain, locked in a marriage so complete that death itself has been retired.

Light comes from industrial lamps recessed into the ceiling — itself a single forged plate two meters thick. The lamps should have failed epochs ago. They have not. They burn with the flat, honest light of a cloudy summer afternoon, and they never flicker. Their housings are draped with moss like sleeping giants with green beards.

This is the final form.

A pocket universe of total construction where water, machine, and vegetation have reached the end-state of all erosion. Nothing will ever grow faster or slower. Nothing will ever rust through. The weeping will never stop and never flood. The roar will never change pitch. The seven Machines will continue their incomprehensible labor — pumping, cycling, heating, cooling, feeding the green, drinking the water — until the last proton in the universe decays.

And even then, something about the place suggests they might simply keep going out of habit.

You can stand in the exact center, between all seven behemoths, and feel the warm metallic tears sliding across the flawless floor around your boots. The ferns will brush your shoulders like old friends who no longer need to speak. The air will press against your face, humid and iron-tanged and alive in the way only completely artificial things can sometimes become.

It does not want you here.

It does not mind you here.

It has already incorporated your presence into the equilibrium. In ten thousand years the shape of your footsteps will still be visible in the thin film of condensation on the floor, never quite evaporating, never quite filling.

This is the Hollow.

It has been waiting in its completeness since before your grandparents’ grandparents’ species learned to walk upright, and it will still be exactly like this long after the last star burns out.

The Machines do not judge.

They only continue.
