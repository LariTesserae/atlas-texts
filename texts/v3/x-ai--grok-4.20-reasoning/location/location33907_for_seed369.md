**The Hollow of the Weeping Threshold**

You stand inside a single living chamber no larger than a modest cathedral, yet it feels vaster because every presence inside it insists on keeping its distance. The place is cupped high in the bones of a nameless range—elevation enough that the surrounding peaks wear permanent cloud collars, yet the bowl itself is warm, almost drowsy, as though the mountain has pressed a hot stone against its own heart and forgotten to remove it.

The boundary is absolute. On all sides, sheer cliffs of dark, fine-grained stone rise in a near-perfect oval. Their faces are not jagged; they are old, polished by millennia of the same slow tears that still fall from them. Every fracture, every pore weeps. Not cascades, not even steady streams—only a continuous, deliberate exhalation of water. Droplets swell like mercury, tremble, and fall. The sound is enormous. Inside this enclosed space the dripping becomes symphonic, a loud, patient percussion that never hurries and never rests. Each impact on leaf or pool is amplified by the stone until the entire hollow rings with wet, metallic music. It is loud enough that you must raise your voice to be heard, yet nothing seems rushed. The water has been falling at this exact tempo for so long that the stone and the sound have reached an understanding.

The floor is not flat. It tilts and folds in gentle ribs of black soil so rich it looks almost bruised. From this earth rise scattered giants. Not a forest—a grove of individuals, each claiming its own clear circle of ground. Enormous tree-ferns lift umbrellas of fronds three times a man’s height; their trunks are sheathed in living moss so thick your hand would vanish to the wrist. Between them, isolated, stand fleshy bromeliads the color of venous blood, their urns brimming with clear water and tiny, pearl-like frogs that never seem to move. Lianas as thick as wrists hang from the cliff walls in slow arcs, their leaves dripping in perfect synchrony with the rocks above. Every plant is lush to the point of obscenity—saturated greens, wet reds, leaves so glossy they look lacquered—yet the eye can travel across the entire chamber without obstruction. The vegetation is dense in mass, sparse in placement. Each organism has been given room to become itself, as though the hollow itself enforces a courteous distance.

The air is warm and heavy. It condenses on every cool surface, so that stone and leaf alike sweat. Mist moves in deliberate, ankle-high tides that take hours to cross the chamber. When the hidden sun is overhead, normal light falls straight down through a single aperture in the clouds above, illuminating the center in a slow, turning column. At the edges the light is softer, almost underwater. Shadows are few and behave strangely; they seem to lag behind the objects that cast them, arriving a breath late, as though light itself has grown reluctant.

There is almost no animal life. Once in a long while a single pale moth the size of a dinner plate drifts across the open ground like a piece of paper. A lizard the color of wet slate clings to a fern trunk so motionless it might be a lichen. Nothing scurries. Nothing sings. The loudness belongs entirely to the water and to the plants themselves. Some of the larger ferns emit a low, bass creak as their immense fronds slowly open or close in cycles that last half a morning. The sound is deep enough to feel in the sternum.

This is an established place. Erosion has long since finished its wild work; what remains is equilibrium. The cliffs will weep for another ten thousand years and lose perhaps the thickness of a fingernail. The ferns will add one new frond every seven years. The soil does not wash away; it is held in a conspiracy between root and moss and the patient geometry of the stone. Nothing is young here. Even the newest leaf looks as though it has already accepted its place in geologic time.

The strangeness is quiet but persistent. The water that drips upward in certain fissures. The way every pool, no matter how small, reflects a sky that is not quite the one overhead. The faint pulse—visible only if you watch for many minutes—that moves through the thickest mosses, as though the entire floor breathes once every ten minutes. Most unsettling is the silence that sometimes falls between drops. For three or four heartbeats the chamber holds its breath, and in that pocket of quiet you understand the rule of this world:

Nothing is allowed to finish.

Every process is kept at the edge of completion. Growth never quite becomes forest. Decay never quite becomes rot. Even death is slow here; a fallen frond may lie for decades before the moss dares to cover it. The hollow is a room where time has agreed to speak only in whispers and the mountain has decided to keep one small cathedral forever half-finished, forever weeping, forever alive.

You may stay as long as you like. The chamber will not hurry you. It has been waiting in this exact balance since before your species had a name, and it is in no particular rush to change.
