**The Hollow Garden**

You are standing inside the final form of a mountain range that died so long ago the planet has forgotten it was ever stone. What remains is this high, flattened world—2.8 on the scale of elevation, 2.8 on the scale of erosion—pressed against the edge of breathable air yet strangely warm, as if the living mass beneath you exhales its own mild summer.

The ground is not ground. It is a single continuous skin of vegetation, 2.6 total, so complete that every surface has been replaced by plant tissue. Yet the density is only 0.5. The contradiction is resolved by scale: the organisms here do not grow in thickets or carpets. They grow as continent-scale architectures spaced so far apart that you can walk for an hour between one living column and the next and never be in shadow. Between them lies a low, springy mat of moss and liverwort no deeper than your ankle, pale green, almost luminous, humming with microscopic life. Step on it and you feel the faint, slow pulse of sap moving through miles of rhizomes below, like the heartbeat of something that has outlived every animal that ever walked the original rock.

The columns themselves are monstrous. Some are wider than cathedrals, others rise like buttresses leaning against nothing, their surfaces wrinkled and rivered with age. They are not trees. No one has ever seen a leaf on the mega-structures; instead they are clothed in overlapping scales of bark that have thickened over geological time into something closer to horn or ivory. Condensation (the 1.2 water) gathers in the deep folds and runs in silver threads down their flanks, collecting in bowl-like hollows the size of small lakes before spilling over in delicate, intermittent waterfalls that hiss rather than roar. The rain, when it comes, is brief and soft, almost apologetic, as though the sky itself has grown tired after so many eons.

Light is normal—2.2—but it falls through an upper story you can barely perceive: a hazy canopy formed where the tallest columns branch and fuse together hundreds of meters above, so distant it looks like pale green cloud. Shafts of sunlight drop between them like pillars of solid gold, catching swirling clouds of midges and tiny iridescent beetles that glitter like metallic snow.

And the sound.

The sound is 2.7—deafening. It never stops. Every column, every crevice, every lake of collected water is occupied by fauna at 2.9 overwhelming. Not dense in any one place (the emptiness prevents that), but collectively absolute. The air rings with layered screams, drone, whistles, and subsonic booms that you feel in your teeth. Flocks of membranous creatures like living kites wheel between the columns in slow, predatory spirals. On the ground, herds of low, many-legged grazers move in near silence across the moss mat, their footfalls absorbed by the living floor, while high above them, in balconies and crevices of the columns, packs of something that might be primates or oversized sloths shriek in complex antiphonal chorus. Every predator call is answered instantly by a thousand smaller voices. The ecosystem is 1.8 dynamic: you can watch entire food chains complete themselves in the time it takes to walk from one shaft of light to the next. A kite-creature dives; a cloud of beetles explodes outward; the kite is mobbed by smaller screaming things; a larger predator unfolds from a crevice and takes the kite in one silent snap. The cycle repeats before the echo dies.

Nothing here is new. The columns have reached their terminal morphology; the moss mat has achieved a stable thickness measured in centimeters across ten million years. Even the animals feel ancient—same body plans repeating in different sizes, as though evolution ran out of ideas and simply began copying itself at different scales. This is the end-of-life state, the oldest-stable-state, where erosion has been defeated by vegetation and the only remaining change is the patient, deafening churn of predator and prey.

Scattered across the moss are the marks of something that once used tools. 1.4 built, 1.3 tech. They are never close together. You find a low dome of woven fiber and dried scale-bark half-swallowed by the moss, its roof collapsed centuries ago. A mile onward, a ring of deliberately placed stones (each no larger than a fist) arranged around a condensation basin. Further still, a single standing monolith carved from a fallen flake of column-bark, covered in shallow spirals that might be maps or simply the doodles of an idle hand. No metal. No writing you can read. Whatever made these things is either gone or has become so perfectly integrated with the Garden that you cannot tell the difference between their shelters and the natural folds of the living columns.

There is something unusual (1.0 weirdness) in the way the place refuses climax. No matter how deafening the fauna becomes, no matter how total the vegetation, the overwhelming impression is still emptiness. You can stand in the exact center between four distant columns and feel as though you are the only moving thing in a green cathedral the size of a province. Then a single beetle lands on your wrist, and you realize every cubic meter of air is claimed, every inch of living surface is speaking, and the Garden is politely pretending to give you room to breathe.

It does not need you. It has outlived stone itself. It will still be humming, dripping, screaming, and slowly pulsing long after your species has been worn as flat as the mountains it replaced.
