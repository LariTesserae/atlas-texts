**The Throat of Veyr**

You are not on ground. You are inside something that finished becoming itself several geological ages ago.

The plateau stretches for hundreds of kilometers in every direction, yet feels claustrophobically full. Elevation and density have fused here: a high, flattened shelf of primordial crust entirely overwritten by a single continuous biomass-construct. There is no soil, no stone, no substrate that was not first printed, grown, extruded, or secreted by the lattice itself. This is the final stable state of an industrial ecology that has outlived its makers by so long that the distinction between factory and organism dissolved into myth.

Everything is hot. The air itself is a dry, metallic shimmer at 68–74 °C, thick enough to taste like heated iron and old lightning. There is no water anywhere. Chemistry here runs on supercritical sulfur dioxide, organosilicon fluids, and molten-salt cycles that the vegetation metabolizes the way Earth plants once used water. The “plants” are black, vitreous, and densely crystalline, their surfaces etched with fractal heat-radiating fins that look like both leaves and turbine blades.

Light is almost absent. A few faint sources persist: dull orange filaments glowing inside translucent stalks like captive lava, occasional arcs of static electricity crawling across metalized bark, and rare bioluminescent nodules that open for seconds at a time before sealing again. These pulses are too weak and too widely spaced to illuminate. They only reveal that the dark has texture.

And the texture is total.

From any vantage the eye is assaulted by saturation. Monstrous braided trunks—some thirty meters thick—interlock like musculature, forming walls, floors, ceilings, and bridges without pause. Between them grow smaller lattices: black filigree pipes, spiraling conduits, bulbous reaction chambers shaped like seed pods the size of houses. Every surface is covered by smaller growths that are simultaneously roots, circuit boards, and heat exchangers. There is no gap larger than a hand could pass through. The entire regional plateau is one continuous, densely packed organ of living industry. You walk on it, through it, between its ribs.

It is ancient past the point of ruin. Erosion has worn every edge into soft, almost fleshy curves. Angles that were once sharp have been blunted by countless millennia of micro-abrasion and self-repair until the whole structure resembles an enormous fossilized nervous system petrified at the moment of orgasm. Yet it is not dead. The final form is not stillness; it is a slow, turbulent metabolism that never quite repeats.

The dynamic is incessant but hushed. A low, almost sub-audible thrum moves through the mass—less sound than a pressure on the bones. Every few hours a distant creaking groan travels across the lattice as some vast pressurized chamber deep below adjusts its chemistry. Sudden, silent eruptions occur: glossy black tendrils burst from a wall, grow meters in minutes, then harden into new conduit. Elsewhere, sections of “overgrowth” are quietly dismantled by specialized nodules that function like slow-motion harvesters, grinding excess tissue into raw slurry that is pumped elsewhere. The system is perpetually editing itself. Solaris-like instabilities bloom and are corrected in silence. The only audible sounds are rare: the soft click of a pressure valve opening, the glassy tinkle of mineral spicules fracturing, the sigh of superheated gas moving through hollow stems. Mostly there is the thick, living quiet of a thing that has no need to speak.

No animals. Not even microbes in the old sense. The ecology is pure vegetal-mechanic. Every function—locomotion of fluids, computation, replication, waste management—is performed by differentiated tissues of the same continuous organism. The lattice thinks, slowly, with electrochemical gradients moving through kilometers of conductive xylem. What it thinks about is unclear. Perhaps it is only maintaining the conditions under which it can continue to maintain the conditions under which it can continue.

The strangeness accumulates the longer you remain.

Some of the larger trunk-columns have developed what look like faces—concavities and ridges that perfectly mimic screaming or serenely sleeping human features, though no human ever lived here. Certain chambers bloom with mirrored interiors that reflect not what is in front of them but what was there a thousand years earlier. In a few places the lattice has grown perfect replicas of extinct machines—locomotives, drill rigs, entire catwalks—out of its own tissue, as if remembering a time before it absorbed its creators. These replicas do not move, but they are warm to the touch and occasionally vent small puffs of aromatic gas that smells like distant rain that never existed.

The rules of the world are simple and absolute:

- Everything belongs to the lattice.  
- Everything is the lattice.  
- Nothing is wasted; nothing is new.  
- Heat is currency. The hotter a region becomes, the faster it grows and the faster it is culled.  
- Light is memory. The few photons that exist are treated as sacred data and routed along fiber-optic vines toward unknown archives deep below.

You can walk for days and never reach an edge. The plateau simply continues, gently rising and falling in ancient waves frozen mid-breath, every centimeter saturated with the same black, hot, half-mechanical vegetation that has become its own horizon, its own sky, its own history, and its own ending.

This is not a ruin.  
This is the oldest living thing that was ever deliberately built, still arguing with itself in the dark, still industrious, still hungry, still perfectly, terrifyingly stable.
