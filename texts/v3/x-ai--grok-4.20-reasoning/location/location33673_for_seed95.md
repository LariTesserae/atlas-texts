**The Shroudlands**

You stand on what feels like the roof of an ended world.

The plateau has no beginning and no end you can see. Erosion has worked here so long that the word “mountain” no longer applies; whatever peaks once existed were ground down to a single, continuous bone of stone that now lies at high elevation like a table left out since the first morning of the planet. The rock is not dramatic. It is patient. It has settled into its final posture—broad, shallow undulations, wide basins worn to the smoothness of an old tooth, and long, low ribs of darker strata that rise only a few meters before flattening again. The color is the color of deep time: iron reds bled pale, granite bleached to the shade of dried bone, seams of quartz that catch the light like old scars.

The air is warm, almost unnaturally so for this altitude. It moves in slow, thick currents that never quite become wind. Sometimes a breath of cooler mist drifts up from hidden crevices, carrying the mineral smell of wet stone and the faint, sweet-rot odor of chitin. Moisture here is not generous. It arrives as condensation that beads on every surface at dawn, as brief, hissing rains that last less than an hour, as pockets of water cupped in the ancient basins like dark, unblinking eyes. These pools rarely overflow. Most simply breathe, shrink, and reappear in the next cycle. Their edges are fringed with a thin living crust—microbial mats of deep ochre and bruised violet that look like rust given breath.

Vegetation is almost an afterthought. Here and there a black, leafless shrub the locals (if any still existed) might once have called *stone-bone* thrusts crooked fingers from cracks. Its bark is indistinguishable from the surrounding rock until you touch it and feel the faint, stubborn pulse of sap. Sparse clumps of silica-rich grass grow in the lee of low ridges, so dry they whistle faintly when the slow air passes through them. That is nearly all. The land has moved beyond the need for green.

Yet it is not empty.

The density is overwhelming in the most silent way possible. Every square meter is claimed. The ground is not soil but a compressed mat of wing-cases, carapaces, abandoned molts, and fine dust ground from a billion exoskeletons over epochs. Walk softly and you still cause minute avalanches of empty shells that click like distant chimes. From this living pavement rise layers of life that have learned how to share the same thin volume without haste:

- Clouds of midges so fine they look like heat haze until they resolve into glittering motes that coat your skin in a living dust.  
- Slow, armored beetles the color of dried blood that move like monks in procession, each following the exact trail of the one before it.  
- Long-legged, translucent spiders that drift on thermals with silk kites no thicker than breath.  
- Flightless moths whose wings have hardened into porcelain-like shields; they crawl in slow, overlapping tides across the open rock.  
- Silent, leathery things the size of ravens that are neither bird nor bat nor reptile, but something older—perhaps all three at once—watching you with eyes like polished jet.

They do not swarm in panic or frenzy. Their movements obey a deep, geological rhythm. A column of beetles may take three days to cross a single basin. A mating dance between two of the porcelain moths may last an entire season. The overwhelming fauna has reached its final, stable form: a single, patient super-organism that breathes, feeds, dies, and is reborn without ever greatly changing its numbers. The quiet is not absence of life. It is the sound of life that no longer needs to shout.

Every few kilometers something built interrupts the natural order. Not often. Never loudly. A half-buried plinth of darker stone, its carvings worn to the point that they could be fossil ripples or the signatures of a language that died before lungs existed. A ring of upright slabs that might once have been a temple, now so eroded they resemble a circle of broken teeth. One leaning monolith still carries the ghost of a spiral carving that catches the light at dusk and seems, for a moment, to rotate. These are not ruins you explore. They are wounds the land has almost healed. The creatures avoid them, or perhaps tend them; you sometimes find perfect circles of empty shells arranged around the bases, as if in offering or quarantine.

The sky is normal light—clear, high, mercilessly blue—but the plateau is so old it has its own atmosphere. A faint haze of insect wings and spore-dust hangs perpetual a few meters above the ground, softening distances. Sounds are rare and precious: the occasional papery click of mandibles, the softest hiss of rain on carapace, the low sub-audible thrum that seems to come from the rock itself when the sun strikes it at the correct angle. Most of the time there is only the slow wind and the sound of your own blood in your ears.

This is not a dying place. It is a place that has finished dying and kept living anyway. The rules here are simple and final:

- Nothing is allowed to hurry.  
- Everything is allowed to persist.  
- The boundary between animal, mineral, and ruin has been gently erased over so many epochs that the land no longer remembers there was ever a difference.

Stay long enough and you begin to feel the slow pulse beneath your feet—the ancient heartbeat measured in centuries per beat. The Shroudlands do not welcome you. They simply tolerate your brief, noisy presence the way stone tolerates moss: without malice, without hurry, and with the calm certainty that you, too, will one day add your calcium to the living pavement.
