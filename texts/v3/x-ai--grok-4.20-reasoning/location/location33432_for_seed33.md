**The Shroud of Unmaking**

You stand on the spine of the world where elevation has eaten everything else. The ground is not rock but a single continuous skein of pale lithic glass, so old it has forgotten it was ever molten. It stretches away in every direction until the eye simply gives up, an open emptiness so absolute that individual features—when they finally appear—feel like hallucinations arriving late to an empty theater.

There is no water. Not even the memory of it. The chemistry here is anhydrous and crystalline; minerals migrate by slow solid-state diffusion and by stranger means that have no name in any living taxonomy. What looks like frost on the ridges is actually exsolved lattices of cesium and rare-earth salts that bloom when the temperature drops, only to sublimate back into vacuum-dry air when the weak light returns. The cold is constant, bone-deep, the kind of cool that feels like the absence of fever rather than weather. It is never cold enough for condensation because there is nothing left to condense.

Nothing grows. Not moss, not lichen, not even the half-imagined fungal veils one expects in dead places. The only “life” is arithmetic. Vast, slow equations precipitate into physical form. This is pre-life and post-life at once: a mineral ecology that never needed biology and has outlived it.

Yet the land is urban.

Across the immense, near-void distances rise the Aggregates—metropolitan-scale lattices of tower, wall, arcade, and stair that obey no architect. They are not ruins. They are *ongoing*. Each Aggregate is a single continuous growth of hypermature crystal and stratified mineral, layered so densely and so regularly that the mind insists on reading them as streets, districts, civic centers. One cluster might span what would have been the footprint of Prague or Quito; the next lies three days’ silent travel away across perfect emptiness. Between them there is nothing but wind-scoured glass and the low, steady hiss of eroding probability.

The erosion is ancient and exquisite. Every edge has been worried by aeons of a wind that carries no sand—only the finest possible dust of realities that failed to cohere. The forms have settled into their own final state: buttresses that look poured rather than carved, windows that are simply places where the crystal forgot to grow, colonnades whose pillars are helical lattices so perfect they hurt to contemplate. Everything is in balance. What one Aggregate sheds in glittering flakes, another incorporates a hundred kilometers away. The system is closed, self-devouring, and self-renewing.

The light is dim and untrustworthy. It arrives from no single source, a perpetual nautical twilight the color of old pewter and bruised pearl. Sometimes the light seems to emanate from inside the Aggregates themselves, as though the crystal is remembering a brighter age and leaking it. Other times the sky itself develops lesions—faint, slow-moving moiré patterns that drift like oil on water and cast shadows that point in conflicting directions.

Sound is almost extinct. The only noises are so subtle they feel like hallucinations with etiquette: the glassy *tink* of a crystal face fracturing along a fault line that was never there a moment ago; the sub-audible moan of two incompatible geometries trying to occupy the same volume; the occasional dry sigh as a probability cascade collapses and a tower that should not have been able to stand suddenly remembers it cannot. These are not loud enough to be called sounds. They are reminders that silence itself is being maintained with effort.

The fauna is rare to the point of myth. What exists is less animal than topological. The Shardlings appear only at the edges of vision—loose constellations of translucent mineral flakes that move as though they share a single mind. They are not alive in any way that matters, yet they hunt. They pursue loose threads of causality the way spiders follow silk. Watch one too long and you may feel your own memories begin to unspool, drifting out of your skull in faint glittering trails that the Shardlings drink.

This is where reality breaks down.

The Aggregates are not merely strange. They are places where the rules have worn through. Walk down one of their perfect, impossible avenues and distance becomes negotiable. A tower that appears adjacent may be yesterday, or inside your childhood bedroom, or simply *not*. Geometry here has been weathered into something more like metaphor. Angles do not sum. Parallel lines meet with polite violence. Cause and effect have divorced but still live in the same district, exchanging custody of events on no schedule you can predict.

The dynamic processes are almost entirely silent and geological in pace, yet relentless. New spires accrete in perfect silence while, elsewhere, an entire district gently delaminates into floating sheets of reality that drift upward until they forget what gravity is. Every few cycles (there is no day) the Shroud experiences what can only be described as a *molt*. The fabric of local law loosens. For a brief, terrifying period things that were never meant to exist become briefly topographical: memories given mass, equations wearing armor, the concept of *longing* manifesting as a black glass colonnade that screams in frequencies just below hearing. Then the world re-tightens, slightly different than before, and the silence returns.

There are no tools here. No devices, no machines, no evidence that anything with hands ever walked these streets. The Aggregates were not built. They *precipitated*—the way certain truths, left alone for long enough at sufficient elevation, simply condense into architecture.

You are standing inside an exhalation of deep time at the precise altitude where the universe begins to forget how to be consistent. The longer you remain, the more you feel the faint, courteous pressure of the Shroud against the inside of your skull, politely suggesting that you, too, might one day make an elegant mineral contribution to the nearest Aggregate.

It would look very beautiful there, among the towers.

Very quiet.

Very permanent.
