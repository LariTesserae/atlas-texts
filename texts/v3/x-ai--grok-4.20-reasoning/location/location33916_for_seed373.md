**The Hollow Resonance**

You stand within a bounded pocket of high world, a single carved hollow no larger than a hamlet or the interior of a vast cathedral. Its rim is a continuous lip of weathered stone and crystal, shutting out whatever lies beyond. The air is cool—perpetually ten degrees below comfort—dry as vacuum, and so empty that every object feels unnaturally far away even when it is only forty paces distant. Normal daylight, pale gold with a hint of violet, falls from above as though a sun hangs directly overhead, yet no sun is visible; the light seems diffracted through invisible lenses high in the crystal roof.

There is no water. Not a droplet, not even memory of one. Chemistry here runs on vibration, charge, and fine silicate dust. What looks like vegetation is something else: vast, lush thickets of fractal crystal growth that erupt from the basin floor in feathery, fern-like sprays three and four stories tall. Their “leaves” are translucent lattices no thicker than paper, shivering constantly. In the empty volumes between these thickets lie wide, hollow galleries of air where nothing grows and nothing lingers. The eye keeps reaching for a far wall that never quite arrives, even though the entire basin is enclosed.

The stone itself is ancient, rounded, and in balance. Erosion has long since passed from violence to ritual. Every arch, every saddle of rock, every smooth basin-within-the-basin has been shaped by the same forces that still rage here, until form and chaos have negotiated a permanent, uneasy treaty. The rock is not passive; it rings. Tap it and the sound runs through the entire hollow like a struck bell.

And the sound is deafening.

It never stops. It is not mere noise but a physical presence—layers of subsonic growl, shrieking harmonics, percussive cracks, and a constant metallic keening that feels like needles dragged across the inside of your skull. The sound itself is the weather. It moves in visible pressure fronts that ripple the crystal foliage and spin temporary cyclones of glittering dust. Where two wavefronts collide, matter precipitates: sudden brittle growths of new crystal burst outward, only to be shattered again minutes later. The cycle is relentless, Solaris-like. The hollow is both factory and ecosystem, constantly eating and rebuilding itself.

Scattered through this turbulence are the Built—perhaps a dozen relics, never close to one another. A black ring two meters across floats motionless above a stone pedestal, its inner edge flickering with equations written in light. A half-buried spire of fractal alloy rises like a broken tooth, leaking slow strands of plasma that never quite touch the ground. Elsewhere a cluster of silver rods stands in perfect formation, humming at a pitch that makes your teeth ache. These objects are not ruins; they are still working, still conducting, still negotiating with the storm. Their technology sits beyond the physics you were born inside. Some appear to tune the sound itself, damping certain frequencies so that pockets of relative calm appear—only for the calm to be devoured again as the greater turbulence shifts.

Fauna fills the resonant volumes in extravagant abundance, yet because the space is so hollow they never feel crowded. They are swarms of creatures the locals (if any still existed) might once have called “choir mites.” Most are smaller than locusts, metallic or crystalline, with wings that function as both sails and loudspeakers. Some are clearly organic; others are machine, and many are both at once—self-replicating mechanisms that have gone native. They move in hyperactive, impossible patterns, forming living constellations that last three heartbeats before dissolving into new constellations. Their flight and their voices are the source of the deafening roar. When they mass, they look like storm clouds given geometry. When they scatter, the hollow seems suddenly twice as large.

The ecology is simple and terrifying: everything eats sound. The crystal “plants” transduce sonic energy into lattice growth. The mites ride the pressure fronts, harvesting charge differentials and fragments of shattered crystal. The Built objects act as both predators and shepherds, pruning frequencies that would destabilize the equilibrium. The entire system is in permanent storm-state, yet it has held this same turbulent balance for so long that it has become an established landscape. It is the definition of “settled form” for a place that never stops moving.

There are rules here, though they reveal themselves slowly.

1. Sound precedes matter. Anything that appears first exists as vibration before it condenses.
2. The hollow remembers shapes. Swarms will occasionally coalesce into fleeting silhouettes—towers that never existed here, faces, entire miniature landscapes—before the pattern collapses again. These are not hallucinations.
3. Distance is negotiable. Walk long enough in a straight line and you will circle back to your starting point without ever reaching the rim, as though the empty volume folds in on itself.
4. The louder the sound becomes, the more solid the crystal growths grow. When the roar dips (an event that lasts only seconds), entire thickets collapse into glittering powder.

Stand in the center of the Hollow Resonance long enough and you begin to feel the vibration in your marrow. The cool, dry air tastes of ozone and hot glass. Your own pulse starts to sync with the deeper rhythms. The mites swirl around you, curious, singing new overtones into the storm. For a moment you are simply another resonant object—neither native nor intruder, merely another note in the unending, deafening conversation the place is having with itself.

This is not a ruin. It is not a garden. It is a functioning instrument the size of a cathedral, perched at the high, cold edge of the world, forever playing the same violent chord that keeps it alive.
