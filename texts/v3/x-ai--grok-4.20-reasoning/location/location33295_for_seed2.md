**The Hollow Forge**

You descend through a lock the size of a cathedral and the world becomes water. Not the sea. Not any sea that ever touched a natural shore. This is engineered water, held in a volume so vast it breeds its own weather.

The temperature is a steady 4 °C, cold enough that every exhalation (if you still breathed) would feather out in glittering micro-bubbles. The water itself is clear, almost painfully so, because nothing is allowed to cloud it for long. Every particle is harvested, every speck of organic drift is combed out by trillion-liter filtration towers that rise like iron redwoods, their bases lost in the indigo below, their crowns vanishing into the haze of distance.

There is no floor. There is only structure.

Everything beneath you is made. The planet’s original crust was cut away long ago and replaced, layer by layer, with a lattice of alloys, composites, and pressure-resistant ceramics. Vast hexagonal plates, each the size of a city district, interlock with geometric brutality. Their edges are still sharp; the entire place is geologically adolescent. You can see the tool-marks—millimeter-perfect grooves left by machines that have not yet had time to be worn soft by current or chemistry. Between the plates run canyons of empty water forty kilometers wide and unguessably deep. The density is so low that a single maintenance rig drifting past can feel like an event.

Light comes from above, but “above” is a ceiling so distant it has its own atmosphere. Suspended beneath that artificial sky are rows of fusion daylight emitters—each a small artificial sun—hanging in precise formation. Their radiance is harsh and honest, the color of a slightly overcast winter afternoon. Beams stab down through the water in pillars the color of pale steel. Between the pillars float motes of white: colossal schools of silver-sided fish, each the length of your arm, moving in synchronized sheets that ripple like breath across a mirror. They are abundant and purposeful. Their genome was written here; they exist to scour the superstructure for microbial films and to carry ultrasonic tags that report structural micro-fractures back to listening stations thousands of kilometers away. Predator and prey have been replaced by maintainer and monitored. Even their hunting looks industrial.

The sound never stops. It is not noise in the ordinary sense; it is the voice of a machine that has outgrown any single purpose. 

Deep bass pulses roll through the water every eleven seconds—tidal pumps the size of mountains drawing cold brine from the abyssal intakes and shoving it through continent-spanning heat exchangers. Between the pulses you hear the metallic moan of expansion joints breathing, the insectile chatter of drone fleets moving in tight formation along the lattice, the sudden hollow bang of pressure doors opening and closing somewhere far below. Sound travels forever here. A single dropped wrench on a service catwalk six hundred meters above you would still be heard, faint as a bell, half an hour later on the opposite side of the chamber.

Currents are the weather. There are no waves, only slow, planetary rivers moving between the hex plates. Some are warm, some cold; where they meet they birth braided turbulence that can spin a submarine like a leaf. These “weather fronts” are mapped the way meteorologists once mapped storms. Entire weather stations—tall, skeletal rigs—stand in the open water, blinking, listening, predicting the next shear zone the way farmers once read clouds.

The ecology is narrow but deep. Besides the silver maintenance schools there are the black eels, long as freight trains, that live inside the primary coolant pipes and emerge only to mate in the open water once every four years. Their passage is announced by a change in pressure and a sudden silence—every other creature clears the volume when the eels move. There are also the translucent shrimp clouds that follow the heat exchangers, feeding on the thermophilic bacteria that bloom wherever coolant meets cold. Everything eats waste or is waste. The food web is a closed loop soldered to the machines.

Rules are simple and absolute:

- Nothing natural is allowed to remain. Any unplanned growth is flash-incinerated by drone-mounted lasers within ninety seconds of detection.
- Every cubic kilometer is monitored. Every creature is tagged. Every current is scheduled.
- The structure is still growing. Far to the north—though “north” is a courtesy—new plates are being lowered into place by orbital cranes that descend through shafts twenty kilometers wide. The clank of fresh alloy meeting ancient alloy travels across the entire ocean like the heartbeat of something waking up.

You hover in the middle distance, half a kilometer above the nearest plate, surrounded by nothing but cold, clear, man-made water. The nearest visible structure is a vertical support column so large it has its own weather: a thin mist of microbubbles clinging to its flank where dissolved gases come out of solution. A school of silver fish drifts past you in perfect formation, their sides flashing like stamped tin. Far below, something enormous and slow turns in the dark between two plates, and the sound that reaches you is only a low, patient groan—like a continent rolling over in its sleep.

This place is not underwater.

It is an engine that happens to be flooded.
