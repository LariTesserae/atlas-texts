**The Deep Flatten**

There is no surface here. There has not been one for longer than names or memory could hold. The water is total: cold, black, and so dense with dissolved salts and ancient particulates that it feels almost oily against the skin. At 0.5 on the temperature scale it sits just above the point where brine itself begins to freeze; the cold is not merely absence of heat but a slow, crushing presence that makes every movement laborious and every sound reluctant to travel. Pressure is a constant fist. Depths exceed four kilometers of water column, and the liquid itself has weight, a thick, silent heaviness that muffles and flattens.

The floor, when it is finally reached, is not what one expects of an ocean bottom. Erosion has had its way for eons. This is the terminal plain, the oldest-stable-state, where every mountain that ever rose has been ground down, every canyon filled, every irregularity planed away until the relief is almost nothing. Yet the plain is not empty. It is packed. Saturated. There is no patch of bare sediment larger than a handspan. The entire visible world is a continuous, shoulder-to-shoulder pavement of nodules, fossil fragments, manganese spheres, smoothed volcanic bombs, and compacted clay concretions. They lie so tightly together that many have fused over time into a single undulating crust, like scales on the hide of something that died before light itself existed. Between them run only the thinnest, winding grooves—narrow as fingers—where the rare currents manage to keep tiny channels open.

Nothing was built here. Nothing ever will be. There are no ruins, no tools, no deliberate shapes. Even the primitive creatures that drift or crawl—blind amphipods the length of a fingernail, ghostly sea spiders with legs like threads, occasional eel-like hagfish—use nothing but their own bodies and the occasional borrowed shard of shell. Their technology is pre-metal, pre-intent: simple mandibles, chemical sensing, the slow patience of scavengers who have never known abundance.

Vegetation is almost absent. Here and there a thin bacterial mat the color of bone clings to the lee side of a nodule, or a single stalk of glass sponge rises like a solitary candle, but these are exceptions. The ecology is that of the end-state: everything feeds on what has already died. Marine snow falls from the invisible world far above—fragments of long-dead plankton, fecal pellets, the dust of continents that no longer exist—settling so slowly in the cold that it can take decades to reach the plain. When it arrives it is immediately trapped in the dense pavement, becoming part of the crust itself.

The light is almost purely absent. What little exists comes from within: the cold green spark of a lone shrimp’s photophores, the faint phosphorescence of disturbed bacterial films, the occasional burst of foxfire-blue from a dying creature. Most of the time the darkness is total. One navigates by pressure, by temperature micro-differences, by the faint electric fields some of the rarer animals emit.

And yet the place is not still.

The dynamic parameter is high. The plain is turbulent in its own slow, crushing way. Every few hours or days—time is hard to judge—a density current arrives. It does not roar; it whispers. A cold, particle-heavy underflow slides across the pavement like a low storm front, no more than two meters thick, moving at the speed of a walking human. In its passage it lifts the lightest flakes and dust, swirls them into brief milky clouds, and redistributes the uppermost layer of nodules with a sound like distant sand falling on glass. Then it passes. The clouds settle again. The pavement closes ranks. The ancient order is restored, only subtly changed. These events are the only real weather. They are chaotic, unpredictable, and brief. Between them the silence is so complete that the soft clicking of a single amphipod leg sounds like a drum.

The plain stretches regionally—hundreds of kilometers in every direction—before the seafloor tilts into deeper hadal trenches or rises into equally ancient, equally flattened rises. There are no horizons. Only the packed crust, the black water, the cold, and the knowledge that this is the final form. Everything that has ever lived on or above this world eventually arrives here, is ground fine, and is pressed into the living pavement. The place has settled into itself so completely that it feels less like geography and more like the skin of the planet after all its fevers have passed.

This is not a dead world. It is a completed one. The few creatures that still move across its surface do so with the unhurried certainty of organisms that understand they are only the latest thin film on an edifice built from the compressed bodies of their ancestors. The turbulence ensures nothing stays buried forever. The cold and pressure ensure nothing decays quickly. The density ensures there is nowhere to hide, and no need to.

It simply is.

And it has been this way, with only the slow turning of its sediment storms, since before the first heartbeat.
