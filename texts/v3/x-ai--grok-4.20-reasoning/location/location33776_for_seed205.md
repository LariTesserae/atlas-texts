**The Thrum**

You emerge from the haze onto the Skin.

It is not ground. It is a single, fibrous mat of vegetation, perhaps a meter thick, stretched across a high plateau so immense it generates its own weather. The surface is warm, almost feverish, the color of oxidized bronze and living moss. Step on it and it gives by a finger’s width, then pushes back with a slow, deliberate resilience, as though the planet itself were inhaling. This is the vegetation parameter at 2.8: total. There is no soil visible anywhere. Every centimeter is plant, root woven into stem woven into leaf woven into fungus woven into more root. The Skin is one continuous organism, ancient, established, in balance with the wind that has worn its ridges into soft, almost architectural undulations over uncountable centuries.

Between the great presences the land is sparse. You can walk for half a day and see only the same living carpet, scattered with occasional knee-high lattices of translucent reeds that click gently when the wind passes. The openness feels deliberate, like the breathing room a cathedral leaves between its pillars. And pillars there are.

They rise at intervals so large the eye refuses them at first. Thirty, sometimes sixty kilometers apart, each is a continent of vegetable matter: a living tower three to five kilometers high and nearly as wide at the base. Their surfaces are not chaotic. Erosion and growth have negotiated a truce so complete that the forms look intentional—fluted columns, spiraling buttresses, cantilevered shelves wide enough to hold forests of their own. Water condenses in the upper vaults and falls in long, silvery curtains that arrive at the Skin as localized, perfumed rain. These are not trees. They are cathedrals that photosynthesize. Their bark is grooved like old whale skin, deep russet shot through with veins of electric green. From a distance they appear to breathe; closer, you see the breathing is literal—slow contractions that move nutrients up their kilometer-long vascular highways.

The air is hot (2.8). Not jungle wet, but high-plateau hot—thin, fierce, and saturated. The altitude keeps the heat from becoming purely tropical steam; instead it becomes a shimmering, metallic pressure that makes every sound sharper. Normal light falls from a pale, almost white sky, yet the haze of pollen and spore turns the world into saturated amber and viridian. Shadows have weight.

And the sound.

It is deafening (2.6) from the first moment. The Skin itself thrums at a frequency just below human speech, a constant sub-bass generated by billions of larval insects chewing in perfect synchrony. Above that rides the screaming of the cloud flocks—millions of bright, bat-winged pseudo-birds that wheel in tight vortices between the living towers. Each tower has its own voice: a colony of resonating hollows and bone-like seed pods that moan when the wind finds them. When two towers sing at once the sound beats in your sternum like a second heart. Predator and prey add their parts without pause—bellows, shrieks, the wet clicking of mandibles, the sudden thunder of a migration herd passing three kilometers away. The noise never stops. It only changes register. After three days you realize you have stopped noticing it the way one stops noticing the sea.

The fauna is overwhelming. Every niche is filled three times over and then filled again by symbiosis. Six-legged grazers the size of bison drift across the Skin in living rivers, their backs encrusted with flowering epiphytes that feed on the animals’ exhaled moisture. In their wake come the Hookjaws—sinuous, vine-mimicking predators that run on bladed forelimbs and drag prey into crevices in the Skin that close behind them like mouths. In the towers themselves live the Choir, pale translucent primates with resonating throat sacs who sing back to the towers in harmonic thirds. Everything is in motion (dynamic 1.6). The great herds migrate in predictable but not clockwork patterns, following the towers’ own slow chemical calendar. When a tower prepares to drop its yearly seed-mountains, the herds converge. When the Skin blooms with narcotic red cups after a condensation rain, the flocks descend in a roaring storm of wings. The ecosystem does not simply turn; it pulses.

There is weirdness here, but it is quiet weirdness (2.1). The towers are not separate individuals. Every three to seven years the Skin transmits a single pulse—an electrochemical shiver that travels beneath your feet for weeks. When it reaches the farthest tower, every megastructure on the plateau detonates into simultaneous flower. The sky turns gold. The air becomes narcotic. Animals that normally tear one another apart stand shoulder to shoulder, swaying. Then the bloom passes, the towers retract their flowers, and the world returns to its deafening, balanced savagery. No one knows why. The Skin simply requires it.

Scattered across this immensity are the works of people.

They are primitive (tech 1.4) and few (built 0.8). You might walk for days without seeing one. Then, tucked against the buttress-root of a tower, you find a village of thirty souls who have grown their homes directly out of the Skin. They use hooks of sharpened bone and fiber ropes to prune and train the living walls, creating water cisterns, sleeping platforms, and delicate rope bridges that sway in the thermals. Their only tools are stone, tooth, and fiber. They speak little. When they do, it is in pitches that match the towers’ moan. Some of them have begun to photosynthesize across their shoulders and scalps—pale green stippling that appears after a decade of eating almost nothing but the tower’s fruit. They are not colonists. They are livestock the Skin tolerates.

At dusk the light turns the color of old brass. The towers cast shadows so long they reach the next tower. The Skin cools by a few degrees and the thrum drops half an octave. The great herds low in the distance. A localized rain begins to fall from the underside of a cantilevered shelf three kilometers above you, arriving as a soft, warm mist that smells of copper and chlorophyll.

You can walk for weeks and never reach the edge. There is no edge. Only more Skin, more towers, more sound, more life devouring life devouring life in one vast, hot, deafening equilibrium that has lasted longer than mountains on other worlds.

This is the place. It does not welcome you. It simply continues, immense and alive, whether you are here to witness it or not.
