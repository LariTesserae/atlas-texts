**The Shroud**

It begins with the sound.

Not a sound *in* the landscape. The landscape *is* the sound. A single, unbroken roar at roughly 145 decibels that has never begun and will never end. It is not wind, not water, not thunder. It is the world’s own resonant frequency, the note struck when reality was stretched too thin across these heights and simply kept vibrating. After the first hour your bones no longer feel it as noise; they feel it as architecture. The roar *holds the place up*.

You are standing on what might be a street.

The ground is warm stone the color of oxidized blood, but the stone is sweating. Every surface weeps. Clear beads form faster than they can fall, so water travels in slow, permanent sheets down vertical faces, across lintels, along the undersides of leaves the size of ship sails. The air itself is condensing; breathe in and you drink. The temperature sits at a murderous 48 °C with near 100 % humidity, yet nothing steams, nothing evaporates. The heat and the wet have reached equilibrium and locked the world in a single, eternal instant of fever.

Above you, the vegetation has achieved urban density without a single tool or mind. Colossal fig-like trunks rise in regular intervals that mimic city blocks, their surfaces carved by centuries of erosion into fluted columns and buttresses. Between them, lattices of aerial roots form bridges, balconies, and tiered galleries so precise they look planned. Vines as thick as ancient sewer pipes thread the structures, flowering with scarlet trumpets that never open or close further than they are right now. Every layer is saturated: mosses packed so tight they look like velvet poured from a vat, ferns pressed flat against ferns, orchids growing out of orchids. The green is so vivid it feels loud even beside the roar.

This is high country. The elevation is murderous. You are above 5,000 meters and yet the air is thick, not thin, because the atmosphere itself has folded here. Look up and the sky is wrong. There is a normal sun (you can see it as a white coin behind layers of mist), but the light bends around invisible angles. Shadows fall in two directions at once. Some towers appear to recede into the distance while simultaneously leaning over your shoulder. The geometry has accepted its breakdown and stabilized into new, impossible constants. A staircase of living wood that begins at your feet can be seen ending at the top of a distant mesa that is somehow also beneath you. The eye accepts it the way the ear accepts the roar: after a while it simply *is* the rule.

There are no animals.

Not one. No insects in the rot, no birds in the canopy, no worms in the soil. The biomass is 100 % botanical and fungal. Even the fungi have grown into civic forms: bracket mushrooms the size of market stalls line what feel like avenues, their gills forming repeating fractal patterns that hurt to count. Everything is alive and everything is perfectly still. Growth has not stopped; it has finished. The place reached its final shape ten thousand years ago and has been holding its breath ever since.

You walk.

Every “street” is a shallow river of warm water flowing at exactly the same depth and speed it always has. The current never varies; the surface is rippled in exactly the same places, forever. Where two flows meet they form permanent interference patterns that look like marble. Your feet disturb nothing. The water simply closes around the intrusion and returns to its previous waveform.

The buildings—if they can be called that—are accretions of living matter that have achieved infrastructure density. A spiraling tower two hundred meters tall is one continuous banyan whose every branch has fused into a helical lattice. Its interior is hollow and filled with hanging gardens that never drop a single petal. You can walk inside it the way one walks into a cathedral that grew rather than being built. Light falls through gaps in the living walls in shafts that never move as the hours pass, because the hours do not pass.

Reality has torn in places and then scarred over.

In the central plaza (a rare open space roughly the size of a European town square), the air simply *stops* being air for a vertical column twenty meters wide. Inside that column float leaves and droplets that have been suspended in the same positions for millennia. They do not fall. They do not spin. They have become part of the fixed ornamentation of the universe. Around the edges of this column the roar warps into a physical pressure that can knock you sideways if you step too close. The place has learned to keep its wound.

Further on, entire districts have been claimed by a single species of black mirror-moss. It grows in perfect rectilinear sheets that look like onyx cladding on skyscrapers. When the normal light hits it, the reflection shows you the city from above, from below, from yesterday, from geometries that have no camera. Then the reflection forgets you were ever there.

Everything is in balance. Erosion has rounded every edge, water has polished every surface, vegetation has colonized every crevice, and then the system froze mid-apotheosis. The roar is the sound of that equilibrium being maintained. It is the sound of a scream that has decided to become architecture.

You can walk for days and the temperature never drops. The water never ceases its slow, hot weeping. The towers never stop pretending to be buildings. The sky never quite closes the holes it tore in itself. Nothing decays. Nothing advances. Nothing is born and nothing dies.

Only the roar continues, vast, wet, deafening, eternal—holding the burning, overgrown, impossible city aloft on a note that reality can no longer un-sing.
