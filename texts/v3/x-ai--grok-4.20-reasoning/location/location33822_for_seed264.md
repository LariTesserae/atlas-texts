**The Hollowworks**

You stand in a low, open basin that feels like the palm of an ancient hand. The land has worn itself into a state of perfect middle age: neither young nor ruined, only settled. Low ridges roll outward for kilometers in every direction, so gradual they are almost invisible until you have walked them. Between the ridges lie wide, shallow pans of dark stone, each one holding its own single mirror of water. The sky is a steady, sourceless gray-gold, the light neither morning nor afternoon, simply *present*, warm enough that the stone exhales a constant low heat against your face.

There is no vegetation. Not a lichen, not a filament of moss. The only living color belongs to thin iridescent films that sometimes sheen across the still pools—metallic greens and oil-slick violets that never spread or thicken. Everything else is mineral: hematite black, rust red, fractured quartz the color of old bone. The ground itself has a machined look without ever having been machined. Long, sinuous grooves run through the stone like dried riverbeds or like cooling channels left by some titanic casting that happened before time began. Their edges are velvet-soft from erosion that finished its work epochs ago and then simply stopped, satisfied.

Yet the place is loud.

A deep, industrial heartbeat lives inside the rock: a layered thrum of bass frequencies, steam hiss, and slow metallic percussion. The sounds do not rise or fall. They do not cycle through phases. They have reached equilibrium and remain there, as constant as the temperature. Stand still long enough and the noise begins to feel like weather. Every few minutes a hollow *clang* travels across the basin from no identifiable source, followed by a sigh of vapor from a vent you had mistaken for an ordinary crack. The vent never widens, never crusts over. It simply breathes, forever at the same pressure, the same temperature, the same rhythm.

Water is shy here. It arrives as condensation that pearls on every surface in the false dawn that is not dawn, then slides along the preordained grooves into the waiting pans. The pans themselves are perfectly circular, as though scoured by an unseen tool, and their level never changes. Occasional brief rains fall—never enough to flood, never absent long enough to dry the stone. The air always carries the faintest metallic dampness, like the inside of an old boiler that has never quite cooled.

Scattered across this openness, kilometers apart, stand the Hollowworks themselves: monumental formations of the same dark stone, shaped by erosion into suggestions of industrial ruin. One looks like the half-buried shell of a blast furnace, its rim worn into soft scallops. Another resembles a collapsed cooling tower lying on its side, fluted interior exposed to the unchanging light. A third is simply a forest of thick, pipe-like columns, each one ringing with a slightly different tone when the perpetual underground engines push gas through them. None of it was built. The stone grew, or was deposited, or was folded this way over inconceivable time, and then the wearing-down reached its final form and froze.

Fauna is almost theoretical. Once every few hours you may notice a slow, flat creature the color of brushed steel sliding across the stone on a self-generated film of moisture. It follows the exact same elliptical path it has followed for ten thousand years, scraping invisible chemical meals from the rock. Its shell bears faint geometric patterns that look eerily like rivets. When it passes over one of the stone grooves it makes a small, bright *tick* that somehow fits perfectly inside the larger industrial chorus, then continues on its eternal route.

Nothing evolves. Nothing degrades. The same mist condenses, the same clangs sound, the same silica beetle traces its loop, the same warm wind that is not wind moves across the same open kilometers. The Hollowworks are not dead. They are not alive. They are a completed process—an industrial ecology that reached balance so perfectly that time itself lost interest and moved on.

If there is a rule here, it is this: everything that can reach equilibrium has done so. The landscape no longer possesses the capacity for surprise. It will outlast astonishment. It will outlast you. It will continue its loud, damp, warm, unchanging labor long after the memory of visitors has been worn as smooth as the grooves that guide each perfect bead of condensation down into the waiting, mirror-black pans.
