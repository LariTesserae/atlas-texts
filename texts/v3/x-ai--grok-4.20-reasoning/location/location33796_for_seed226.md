**The Weeping Nave**

You stand inside a single vast chamber of stone, no wider than a hamlet but tall enough to feel like a cathedral cut from living mountain. The walls rise sheer and unfinished, raw faces of pale granite and fractured basalt still shrugging upward from whatever slow geological birth is happening beneath them. There is almost no soil—only thin black mats of moss and the occasional lip of new rock where minerals have begun to plate out like pale scabs. The land is embryonic, still deciding what it will be.

Yet water is everywhere. The air itself sweats. A cold, mineral mist condenses on every surface and beads into fat drops that fall in slow, endless percussion. From the half-dome of cliff overhead hang thousands of weeping threads—some fine as harp strings, others thick as wrists—each one chiming as it strikes stone or pool or moss below. The sound is loud, ceaseless, and strangely melodic; the entire basin rings like the inside of a bell. Individual drips become a roaring congregation. You can barely hear your own thoughts.

In this cool, high crucible the vegetation is almost violently lush wherever it can grip. Great emerald ferns unfurl like sails. Cushions of moss grow so thick they sag under their own weight and drip their own secondary rain. Pale birches and dwarf rowans cluster in tight, almost conspiratorial groves no larger than a cottage, their leaves trembling with every impact of falling water. Between these green islands lie wide, echoing floors of naked wet stone—empty, resonant, austere. The contrast is absolute: life explodes where it can, then yields to vaulting vacancy.

Scattered across this hollow are the works of people. A small, sparse settlement—perhaps eighty souls—has rooted here in deliberate emptiness. Their buildings stand far apart, each one an island in the stone sea. Most are low, hand-built things of rough basalt blocks levered from the living walls, mortared with the same mineral accretion that the mist itself deposits. Some structures are not built at all but *released*: the inhabitants have simply carved rooms and doorways into the slow-growing rock lips, letting the mountain finish the roof in its own time. Thatched caps of tough mountain grass and woven moss keep the constant drip from flooding the interiors. Narrow stone channels, meticulously maintained with flint and antler tools, guide the louder streams away from living spaces and into a central cistern carved like a font.

There is no metal louder than cold-worked copper. Tools are stone, wood, bone, and patience. The people move slowly, as though imitating the land’s own tempo. They speak little; when they do their voices are pitched to cut through the unending drip-roar and are answered instantly by layered echoes that make every conversation feel like a choir.

The few animals are rare and solemn. A small grey bird with a translucent beak perches on a dripping ledge, singing a single sustained note that blends so perfectly with the water-music you cannot tell where one ends and the other begins. Occasionally a pale salamander the length of your arm drifts across the wet stone like a living shadow. Nothing else. The place feels half-made, still waiting for its fauna to arrive.

There is something wrong—beautifully wrong—with the geometry. Certain pillars of accreted mineral have begun to form natural helices, as if the stone itself is remembering how to grow in spirals. The inhabitants treat these as sacred and will not cut them. In the right light (and the light is normal, almost holy, a shaft of cold silver that drops through a single high breach in the cliffs) the mist turns into drifting sheets of brightness between the dark pillars. The whole chamber seems to breathe.

Nothing happens quickly. A child born here will not see the shape of a particular pillar change in their lifetime, yet every elder insists the stone is still rising, still being born. The moss thickens by millimeters per decade. New rooms are only claimed when the mountain offers them. The people have made their small urban foothold inside a place that has not yet finished becoming itself, and they have done so with the reverent slowness such a sanctuary demands.

You are standing in the nave of a half-built world. The roof is still dripping. The walls are still singing. The city is waiting for the mountain to finish its first sentence.
