**The Plateau of Unfinished Breathing**

You hang in the cold, dim water like a mote suspended in time. The liquid here is not quite water anymore — it is heavier, slower, almost glutinous with dissolved minerals leached from the made-world over four billion years. Pressure presses evenly from all directions. There is no surface above you and no true floor below; only a high, ancient shelf that has been submerged since before multicellular life first learned to crawl. This is an elevated abyss: a drowned plateau lifted so far above the deeper basins that the liquid above you is only two kilometers deep instead of twenty. In this place that counts as “high.”

Everything is constructed.

There is no natural stone, no sediment that was ever born of volcanism or compression. Every surface, every grain, every curve was placed by something that is no longer here. The builders left no tools, no language, no bones — only this total fabric. After eons the material has aged into a uniform bone-colored ceramic that feels both artificial and inevitable. Its edges have been rounded until they seem grown rather than carved. Geometric ghosts still haunt it: faint hexagonal lattices visible when light catches them at the right angle, corridors that end in mathematically perfect spirals, vast planes that tilt by exactly three degrees for hundreds of kilometers, as though obeying an instruction no longer legible.

The erosion has reached its final stable state, yet the plateau refuses to be still.

Currents — chaotic, contradictory, born of temperature differentials and the slow gravitational breathing of the plateau itself — scour and rebuild in the same motion. One moment a smooth wall is exhaling a cloud of fine white dust as an invisible shear rips along a hidden fault. The next moment the same current deposits that dust elsewhere in delicate, self-organizing ridges that look almost like writing before they collapse again. Solaris-like instability rules here: the made-world dreams in slow motion, extruding temporary buttresses or dissolving entire colonnades only to reform them elsewhere hours later. Nothing is destroyed forever. Nothing is allowed to remain the same.

Vegetation never existed here. The ecology is purely faunal and mineral. Vast colonies of chemosynthetic tube-worms the color of old ivory cling to the artificial contours, their feathery crowns filtering the constant mineral rain. They are not plants; they are closer to living sieves. Between them crawl pale, blind isopod-things the size of horses, their jointed legs clicking faintly against the ceramic. Higher in the water column drift translucent leviathans — ribbon-like creatures two hundred meters long that move with the lazy, terrible grace of living banners. Their bodies are so clear you can watch their internal organs pulse like lanterns inside them. When two meet they sometimes intertwine for days, exchanging fluids and fragments of the plateau’s own substance before separating again. There are thousands of them, yet the water remains sparsely populated; the scale is too vast for any gathering to feel crowded.

The light is the color of old pewter. It arrives from above as a bruised, filtered glow that never quite becomes day. Most illumination actually comes from the creatures themselves — soft, cold pulses of blue and violet that appear and vanish like thoughts. When a ribbon-beast passes overhead it momentarily turns the entire world into a moving cathedral window, staining the ceramic structures in shifting pastel geometries before the current carries it away.

Sound is mostly a deep, submarine hush. The turbulent currents are felt more than heard — a constant sub-audible pressure on the ears and chest. Every few hours a distant, bone-deep *thump* travels through the plateau as some colossal artificial buttress finally yields and folds into the deep. The sound arrives muted, almost polite. The fauna speak in clicks and subsonic moans so low they are closer to vibration than music. The plateau answers them with its own slow tectonic singing, a sound like a continent clearing its throat over decades.

There are rules here, though they are not written.

- The currents always move opposite to memory. If you try to return to a place exactly as you left it, the water will have rearranged the path.
- The ceramic never cuts. It only erodes or accretes. Even the sharpest fracture heals within weeks, smoothed by the same forces that broke it.
- The great ribbon-beasts will not cross the perfect spiral depressions. They treat them as sacred or fatal; no one has remained long enough to learn which.
- Light that lingers too long in one place begins to solidify. Bioluminescent clouds left undisturbed for days slowly develop skins and drift away as new, short-lived organisms.

You are not on a seabed. You are inside an ancient decision that has been arguing with gravity, water, and time for longer than your species has existed. The plateau has reached its final form and yet remains furiously alive with change. It is the oldest argument in the world, spoken entirely in cold, manufactured stone and patient, blind animals who have never seen the sun.

Stay here long enough and the liquid begins to feel like breath. The ceramic begins to feel like bone. The turbulence begins to feel like a heartbeat that has never once been calm, yet has never failed to continue.

This is not a ruin.

It is the final, restless state of something that was never allowed to finish being built.
