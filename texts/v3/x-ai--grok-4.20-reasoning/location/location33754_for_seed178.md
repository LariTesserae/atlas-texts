**The Weeping Basin**

You step into a place that is still deciding what it is.

The sound comes first, before shape or distance can resolve: a deafening, layered roar so complete it feels like pressure on the eyes. Water, wind, insects, vertebrates, collapsing vegetation, and something else — a deep, bone-deep thrum that might be machinery or might be the rock itself vibrating — all braided into one continuous avalanche of noise. It never drops below the level of a jet engine. After ten minutes your teeth ache.

The air is cold (just above freezing) and so wet it seems to be breathing on you. Every surface sweats. Mist condenses on mist; water beads on water. The atmosphere itself appears to be slowly precipitating, a perpetual second rain falling upward from saturated air. Rocks weep in steady sheets. Leaves the size of blankets droop under their own gathered weight and then fling the water off in sudden glittering cascades.

This is lowland country, a broad young rift-valley perhaps forty miles across, still raw from whatever convulsion birthed it. The surrounding ridges are sharp as shattered flint, almost painfully new. Their edges have not had time to blunt. Where they meet the basin floor they fracture into cliffs and talus slopes that pour cold groundwater like open wounds. The valley floor itself is a single saturated sponge: peat, silt, living root-mat, and shallow black water all arguing over the same space. Nothing is more than a meter or two above anything else. The entire basin is a marsh-edge that never ends.

Yet the vegetation is almost violent in its lushness. It does not grow so much as surge. Cushions of moss pile into one another until they form emerald cliffs ten meters high; then the weight becomes too much and whole moss-walls calve into the water with booms that punch through the general roar. Giant ferns taller than houses unroll new fronds so quickly the motion registers at the edge of vision. Cold-tolerant willows and dwarf conifers are locked in frantic competition, trunks braided like rope, roots fighting for every inch of unstable ground. Vines thicker than a wrist crawl visibly across the surface of slower plants, stitching the chaos together. Decay is just as fast: fallen trunks liquefy into black soup within days, feeding the next explosive generation. The air itself smells green, mineral, and faintly electric.

Fauna is not “present.” It is saturating. Clouds of midges and thumb-sized moths swirl so densely they look like smoke. Amphibians — thousands of species, it seems — bellow from every puddle and hollow; their combined chorus is a physical wall. Shaggy, deer-like grazers move in nervous, bellowing herds that crash through the undergrowth like living landslides. Above them, vast skeins of migratory birds wheel and scream, while bat-like creatures hunt the insect clouds in ultrasonic shrieks that occasionally Doppler into human hearing as metallic scrapes. Predators are loud too; nothing here hunts quietly. The entire food web seems to be shouting.

And the ground will not stay still.

The basin is in permanent turbulent flux. A ridge of peat twenty meters away domes upward as gas builds beneath it, then ruptures with a wet cough, releasing a bubble of methane that ignites on contact with one of the faint phosphorescent blooms drifting above the water. A stand of giant ferns collapses sideways as its root-mat dissolves, sending a wave across a shallow lake that drenches a herd of grazers. Before the ripples have died, new red shoots are already punching up through the floating mat of debris. Sinkholes open. New springs burst forth. Sections of the valley floor rise and fall like the chest of something feverish. The young geology and the hyper-accelerated biology are feeding on each other in frantic feedback.

Light is scarce. What little there is feels stolen. Most of the illumination comes from bioluminescence: cold blue fungal veils draped across the undersides of fallen logs, chains of green lantern-bugs pulsing in the moss, occasional bursts of violet from something floating in the water. These pinpricks only make the surrounding darkness thicker. The sky is a low, bruised lid of perpetual cloud and condensing mist. You can see perhaps four hundred meters before everything dissolves into charcoal and faint glowing specks. Distance is guessed at by volume of sound and the faint red heartbeat of distant geysers.

Yet every few kilometers something unnatural interrupts the frenzy.

Half-buried in a collapsing bank of moss sits a single obsidian-colored monolith no taller than a man. Its surface is not solid; it behaves like a hole into somewhere with different physics. Light bends around its edges. Plants growing against it grow faster, brighter, almost feverishly, before burning out and being replaced by the next eager cohort. Further on, a delicate lattice like frozen smoke spans a ravine — a bridge or a filter or both — its nanoscale struts glittering with moving constellations of light. It makes no sound of its own, yet the deafening roar around it seems to organize for a few meters, as though the structure were conducting the noise like an orchestra.

These artifacts are rare. Scattered. Some are clearly failing: one lies toppled and half-sunk in a pool, still trying to emit a perfect sphere of warmth that makes the water around it steam and the vegetation grow in concentric rings of escalating lushness until the sheer biomass collapses the ground and drowns the effect. Others appear to be exactly where they intend to be, calmly performing whatever incomprehensible function they were designed for centuries or millennia ago. They feel less like ruins and more like immune-system components left inside a body that has since gone gloriously, deafeningly feral.

This is the Weeping Basin. A young world trying to become an old one at maximum speed. A cold, dark, waterlogged engine of creation and destruction running at full throttle, screaming in every frequency at once. The advanced technology did not tame it. It only taught the wilderness how to go faster.
