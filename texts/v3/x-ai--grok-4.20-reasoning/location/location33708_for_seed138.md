**The Condensery**

You stand inside a low bowl of freshly fractured basalt, no more than a few hundred meters across. The rock walls rise sharply on all sides, their edges still glassy and raw as if the ground split open only yesterday. There is no wind. The air itself feels recently poured—thick, mineral, and permanently saturated. Every surface weeps.

Hot water moves just beneath the skin of the world. It beads on the black rock, trickles down in slow silver threads, gathers in shallow pans between the buildings, and rises again as steam. The steam never quite leaves. It condenses against the undersides of catwalks and pipe runs, then falls back as a warm, perpetual drizzle that smells of sulfur and machine oil. The water table is so high that the ground itself seems to sweat. Nothing dries. Nothing erodes. The sharp volcanic glass and the newer concrete both refuse to blunt.

Scattered across the bowl like iron parish churches are nine major plants and perhaps thirty outbuildings. They are not crowded; each structure claims its own clear ground, so the eye travels easily between them through curtains of steam. A hammer-forge hall the size of a small cathedral. A electrolysis battery works with its forest of porcelain insulators. A refinery whose distillation columns rise like organ pipes. All of them are running at once, yet the entire complex feels no larger than a hamlet. You can walk its full circumference in ten minutes if you don’t mind the heat.

The heat is absolute and unchanging: 2.8 in every direction. It presses against the skin like a second, fevered body. Pipes sweat oil and condensate in equal measure. Steam plumes from pressure valves rise exactly three meters before they are pulled back down by the heavier air, forming a permanent low roof of white vapor that turns daylight into a dim, even glow. There are no shadows worth the name—only zones of slightly deeper brass or pewter.

Sound is everywhere and perfectly stable. The plants do not pulse or cycle; they have reached a single, loud chord that never varies. The bass is the deep, glassy ringing of the hammer-forge. Over that rides the continuous shriek of steam through relief valves, the wet clicking of conveyor chains, the electrical hum of transformers the size of cottages. All of it locked in exact equilibrium. If you stand still for an hour, the sound does not change by a single decibel. The Condensery has found its note and will hold it until the world ends.

Vegetation is almost absent. A thin black lichen survives on the north faces of the buildings, looking like spilled ink. In the warm sump ponds, mats of thermophilic algae glow dull red under the steam. That is all. The place has traded greenery for something else.

Fauna has moved in to fill the vacancy. The Condensery is loud with life. Clouds of white moths orbit every sodium lamp in perfect, unchanging orbits, as though on rails. Lizards the color of wet concrete bask on every hot pipe, throats pulsing in exact synchrony with the rhythm of the nearest engine. Frogs—small, glossy, black—sit half-submerged in the condensate pans and sing a single, endless chord that somehow slots between the mechanical sounds without ever clashing. Feral cats, sleek with moisture, patrol the catwalks in slow, repetitive circuits. Their paw prints never accumulate; the constant drizzle washes the metal clean the instant a cat moves on. Everything is abundant, everything is in balance. The moths feed the frogs, the frogs feed the lizards, the cats feed on nothing visible and yet remain plump. A closed loop.

There is no visible decay. The youngest concrete still bears the imprint of its formwork; the oldest looks freshly poured. Rust is forbidden here. Even the concept of “old” feels alien. The Condensery emerged in a single geological gasp and has simply refused to continue its own history. Every pump, every bearing, every living thing has found the exact equilibrium point where input equals output, wear equals renewal. Time has not stopped, but its teeth have been pulled. The same rain falls, the same steam rises, the same hammers fall, forever.

You can feel the rule of the place in your lungs: nothing is allowed to accumulate, nothing is allowed to diminish. The water condenses, falls, sinks, rises. The machines consume, produce, consume again. The animals are born, live, and die in numbers that never shift. The Condensery is a small, hot, loud cathedral of perfect industrial homeostasis, carved into young rock and left to run until the heat death of the universe, dripping, ringing, and perfectly content.
