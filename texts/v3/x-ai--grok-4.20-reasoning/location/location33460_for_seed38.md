**The Weeping Plateau of Veyr**

You do not arrive here so much as you are slowly absorbed into it.

The horizon is not a line; it is a gradient where land and sky forget which is which. At this altitude the air is warm, thick, and slightly sweet, as though the entire continent has been breathing the same breath for four hundred million years. Nothing sharp remains. Every slope has been gentled by time until the ground itself feels like the back of an ancient beast that long ago stopped bothering to rise.

There is no stone you can touch that is not also plant, and no plant that is not also water.

The substrate underfoot is a living mat several stories deep. What looks like moss is actually a dense interwoven pelt of root-hairs, fungal threads, and microscopic ferns pressed so tightly they have become a single continuous tissue. Step on it and the surface yields with a soft, damp give, then slowly pushes back, as if tasting the weight of your foot before deciding whether to remember it. In places the mat has humped upward into low, buttressed domes the size of cathedrals; these are not hills. They are the collapsed crowns of primordial trees that died so long ago their deaths have become topography. Their trunks have flattened into gentle ridges that run for dozens of kilometers, now covered by new generations growing directly out of their ancestors’ rot. Life here does not build on ruin. It *is* the ruin, perfected.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. It does not flow so much as it *perspires*. Every surface sweats. From the undersides of vast horizontal boughs, from the lips of root-canyons, from the porous “stone” that is actually lignified heartwood older than most mountain ranges, clear beads of water form, tremble, and fall. The sound is constant but never loud: a slow, planetary dripping, like the world itself is crying without urgency. Mists condense out of the warm air and drift between the layers of vegetation in deliberate, half-conscious herds. Sometimes the mist pools in vast shallow bowls of living green until the bowl itself drinks it again. Rivers exist, but they are sluggish, braided things that move between the roots like thoughts moving between neurons.

The canopy is not above you. You are inside it. Layer upon layer of foliage have intergrown until verticality itself feels like a forgotten habit. In some places the leaves are so densely overlapped that light arriving at the lower strata is the color of old jade and diluted blood. Bioluminescent lichens—pale violet, corpse-blue—provide the only consistent illumination, glowing with the gentle rhythm of something that has never needed to hurry. Their light is dim enough that after an hour your eyes begin to invent colors that are not there.

The animals are abundant and almost reverently quiet.

Great velvet slugs the length of barges glide along the root-ridges, their backs forested with miniature epiphytes that bloom only once every few human lifetimes. Colonies of arboreal sloths—each individual permanently draped in its own miniature ecosystem of moss and bromeliads—move so slowly that generations of birds nest in their fur. When one of the sloths finally reaches a new feeding branch, the motion is so gradual it feels like continental drift wearing a face. Somewhere far off, a herd of lowing megafauna that resemble nothing so much as ambulatory peat bogs makes its way across a living bridge formed by two fused mega-trees. Their calls are subsonic, felt in the chest rather than heard.

Everything is near-touching. Vines as thick as thighs braid with thinner ones, which braid with rootlets, which braid with the hair-like tendrils of yet smaller plants, until the distinction between individual organisms becomes academic. The entire Plateau is one continuous body in slow, patient conversation with itself.

Scattered through this living mass are the Built.

They are not ruins in any ordinary sense. They are single, solitary structures—half a tower, the curve of a forgotten gate, a broken obelisk—now almost completely ingested. A vine as old as history has wrapped one basalt pillar so many times that the stone has been squeezed into a helical shape, like toothpaste being slowly extruded by the weight of centuries. In another place a dome of unknown purpose has sunk halfway into the mat; its roof is now a meadow where dwarf trees grow in perfect circles, as if the building is dreaming them. These artifacts feel less like the remnants of a fallen civilization and more like thoughts the Plateau had long ago and then forgot to finish.

The strangeness is not loud. It is structural.

Over enough time the plants here have learned architecture. Certain canopy layers form repeating vaulted chambers that resonate at frequencies too low for human ears but which cause specific spores to release in rhythmic pulses. Other regions grow in precise fractal repetitions that suggest memory, or at least habit. On very rare occasions—perhaps once every few thousand years—a grove will rearrange itself overnight by some combination of explosive fruiting, deliberate root pressure, and the slow toppling of nurse logs. The new configuration is always more beautiful, and always slightly wrong to a human sense of proportion, as if the Plateau has developed its own aesthetic sense and is still experimenting.

The rules of the world are simple and absolute:

Nothing here is in a hurry.  
Nothing here is ever finished.  
Nothing here is alone.

Even the quiet has weight. The few sounds that exist—the drip, the soft creak of a limb adjusting itself over decades, the almost sub-audible exhalation of a continent-sized lung—feel like the Plateau clearing its throat before it speaks a sentence it has been composing since the first bacteria learned to photosynthesize.

You may stay as long as you like. The mat will grow to accommodate your footprints. The mist will learn the shape of your breath. In a few hundred years, if you stand perfectly still, something beautiful may decide to grow out of the place where your heart used to be.

It has all the time in the world.
