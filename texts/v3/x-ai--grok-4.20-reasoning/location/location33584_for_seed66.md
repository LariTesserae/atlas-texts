**The Weeping Conurbation**

You stand on a balcony of blackened iron that has not been painted in four thousand years. The rail under your hands is smooth as river stone, every edge eased by the long patience of water and wind. Warm air, almost blood-warm, presses against your face. It is heavy with moisture that never quite becomes rain; instead it condenses on every surface in slow, fat beads that slide, hesitate, and fall again. The entire city weeps.

There is no soil. There never was, not in living memory. The ground is construction layered upon construction until the stratigraphy of the city is a geology in its own right: concrete fused with brick, steel annealed to slag, rebar grown through with crystalline exudates the color of dried blood. Where water has worked longest the surfaces have taken on the soft, organ-like curves of eroded limestone, except these curves are girders and lintels and the fossilized silhouettes of long-dead scaffolding. Vegetation is almost absent. Here and there a black crust of fungus the thickness of a fingernail clings to the dampest shade, but even that seems more mineral than living. The world is built, and it has outlived every green thing.

Yet it is not dead.

From this height the Conurbation stretches beyond sight in every direction, a single continental scab of industry raised onto a high plateau that has its own curvature, its own weather. Towers the width of mountains stand beside canyons that are actually streets. Smoke rises so slowly it appears motionless, like chalk marks against a pale sky. Normal daylight falls across it all, but the moisture diffracts the light into a constant hazy gold, as though the sun itself has rusted.

The sound is constant and enormous. It is not the sharp clamor of a living factory district; it is the deep, slow throb of an organism that has been digesting the same meal for millennia. Great wheels turn in the bellies of half-buried engine cathedrals, one revolution every forty minutes. Steam vents sigh like whales. Chains the thickness of redwoods clank through their guides with the patience of glaciers. Beneath all this runs the animal sound: a roar composed of uncountable wings.

The fauna is overwhelming.

Every cornice, every crack, every rusted lattice holds nests. Iron-colored corvids with wingspans longer than a man’s arm move in slow, deliberate spirals that take hours to complete. Clouds of midges and flesh-colored moths so dense they look like smoke drift between the towers in living rivers. Rats the size of small dogs flow along gutters in braided currents, their passage marked by the soft clicking of claws on metal. Bats cling to the undersides of bridges in black curtains that ripple like kelp. Nothing is in hurry. Their migrations last weeks. Their breeding cycles span years. They feed on the endless condensate, on fungal crusts, on one another, on the thin organic film that condenses inside every exhaust duct. The city has become their ecology, and they have become its weather.

The Conurbation is in balance. Erosion and construction have reached equilibrium. New layers are added so slowly that the difference is imperceptible within a human lifetime; old layers subside into the mineral record with equal grace. Pipes burst and are simply overgrown by new construction. A collapsed hab-stack becomes, over centuries, a hill that new streets politely curve around. The machines themselves seem half-geological: vast turbines whose bearings have worn into perfect spheres of polished steel, engines whose exhaust has glazed their own chimneys into volcanic glass.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. It beads on every strut and cable. It drips from the lips of gargoyles worn featureless by time. It gathers in vast shallow reservoirs on the roofs of megafactories, reflecting the gold sky like molten bronze. Sometimes the condensate is so heavy that entire districts vanish inside slow-moving banks of fog that crawl between the towers like living things. The air itself feels damp in the lungs.

There is an unusual quality to the place, though it never crosses into the grotesque. It is simply that everything has been here so long that the distinction between architecture and organism has dissolved. A flue might be mistaken for a trachea. A ventilation shaft might pulse, very slightly, with the breathing of some deep internal fan. The rats have learned to ride the moving walkways. The moths time their greatest swarms to coincide with the release of warm vapor from the condensation engines. All of it moves according to rhythms longer than any single life.

This is not a ruin. The forges still glow. The elevators still rise, creaking, through kilometers of lattice. The city makes something, though after so many ages even its inhabitants no longer remember what. The product is less important than the making. The making is the weather. The weather is the city. The city is the mountain. The mountain has always been weeping, and will continue to weep, warm and loud and impossibly alive with wings, long after every visitor has gone to rust.
