**The Basin of Even Drips**

It is a single, self-contained hollow no larger than a cathedral’s footprint, sunk perhaps thirty feet below the surrounding lowlands. You can stand at its center and see every boundary at once, yet the space still feels larger than it measures because nothing crowds it. The air is warm—skin-temperature, almost blood-warm—and never changes. There is no breeze, no thermal lift, no breath of morning or evening. The atmosphere is simply always this same damp, motionless medium.

A low, perpetual mist hangs at chest height, fed by slow condensation rather than rain. Water beads on every surface, gathers into trembling drops, and falls at long, irregular intervals. Each drop lands in one of the stone basins that pock the floor—shallow, perfectly smoothed depressions worn by ten thousand years of identical drips. The sound is the only one the place permits: a single, clear *ploc* every few minutes, never hurried, never silent for long. The pools never overflow and never shrink. The equilibrium is absolute.

The stone itself is the color of old bone and rust. It has been eroded so thoroughly that edges have become curves, cracks have become grooves, and every surface feels deliberate, almost sculpted by time into a state of final rest. Nothing here is still becoming; everything has already arrived at its final form. Lichen grows in thin, map-like films—ochre, sage, charcoal—yet so slowly that a century would not noticeably thicken them. Here and there a single clump of wiry grass or a low, leathery shrub stands alone, each separated from the next by ten or fifteen paces of open stone. Their leaves are small, dusty, and perfectly still. No insect has ever been seen among them.

The light is dim and directionless, a uniform ivory-gray that arrives from everywhere and casts no true shadows. It feels like the inside of a pearl. Because the mist never lifts and the sun is never allowed to focus, the hour of day cannot be read. There is only this unchanging luminance, warm enough to keep the air comfortable, faint enough that colors seem half-remembered.

Scattered at random across the basin are the faintest traces of something once built. A single upright stone, twice the height of a man, stands near the western edge; its surface carries markings so worn they could be natural pitting or the last ghosts of symbols. Twenty paces away lies a collapsed ring of smaller stones, now barely distinguishable from a natural outcrop except that the geometry still hints at intention. On the northern slope, half-sunk into the earth, are the remains of one modest dry-stone shelter—no larger than a confessional—its roof long gone, its walls reduced to waist height and furred with the same lichen that covers everything else. These objects have been here so long they have achieved the same settled dignity as the boulders. The place has absorbed them. They no longer feel like intrusions.

There is no animal life. Not a bird, not a lizard, not a spider. Even the memory of sound beyond the dripping feels inappropriate. The silence is not empty; it is full, like a held breath that has learned how to last forever.

The basin is ringed by a gentle lip of stone that rises just high enough to cut off any view of whatever lies beyond. From inside, the world ends at that soft ridgeline. Nothing has ever been observed to cross it. Nothing grows, nothing dies, nothing arrives or departs. The same drops fall into the same pools in the same warm, dim air, and the stones keep their shapes exactly as they have for centuries.

This is not a ruin. It is a finished place. A landscape that reached its final sentence long ago and has simply refused to turn the page.
