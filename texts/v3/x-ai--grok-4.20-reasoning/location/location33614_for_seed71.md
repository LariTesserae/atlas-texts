**The Shroudlands**

You stand on what feels like the roof of the world after the world has already died and been forgotten.  

The plateau stretches so far that its own curvature is visible—three thousand kilometers of near-flatness at eternal high elevation, worn down by erosion so ancient it has passed beyond geology into ontology. The stone itself looks tired. It has been planed by wind, ice, and time until it lies in broad, shallow pans of granite and dark basaltic glass, separated by long, slow undulations no higher than a man’s hip. The final form of a continent that once had mountains. Now it is almost level with the upper troposphere; the sky feels close enough to scrape your scalp.

The air is viciously cold (0.3), yet damp (1.4). Moisture does not arrive as honest rain but as a perpetual, low-grade conspiracy: fog that clots in the hollows at “morning,” beads of condensation that appear on every surface even when the air is visibly dry, and sudden, localized drizzles that fall upward from the ground before remembering the rules and reversing. Pockets of liquid water sit in perfectly circular stone basins that should have frozen solid eons ago. They never quite freeze and never quite boil; they simply wait, reflecting two skies.

The vegetation is lush in the way rot can be lush. It does not carpet the land—density is too low for that—but appears in isolated, extravagant archipelagoes across the emptiness. Black mosses with metallic sheen grow in fractal spirals that repeat at every scale from fingernail to hillock. Glass-like reeds, jointed like bamboo but brittle as ice, ring softly when the wind moves them, though the wind is almost always too weak to move them. In the wetter depressions rise forests of what might be trees if trees had given up on chlorophyll and taken up geometry instead: tall, pale, branching structures of translucent cellulose and mineral deposit, their “leaves” actually thin sheets of frozen vapor that never melt. They grow in deliberate avenues and circles, as though obeying a plan no longer legible to anything living. There are no animals to eat them, so they grow and die and rot and regrow in long, patient cycles that constitute the only biology left. The ecosystem is entirely floral, fungal, and lithic. It moves, slowly, the way continents move. That is enough.

There is almost no sound (0.6). The quiet is so complete that your own pulse becomes offensive. When sound does arrive it feels imported: a low sub-audible thrum that arrives in the bones first, then later resolves into the distant creak of two stone plates of reality sliding past each other. Occasionally a single reed will snap in the cold and the crack will echo for nine full seconds, folding over itself as though the air has corners.

The cities are the worst part.

They appear at intervals that feel statistically improbable—never closer than four days’ walk, never farther than twelve. Each is a layered, primitive metropolis of cyclopean basalt, fitted without mortar, built by hands that understood levers and simple bronze but not the wheel. Streets spiral inward according to geometries that refuse Euclidean consistency; walk one and you will exit at a right angle to your entrance. The buildings have been here so long they have begun to merge with the vegetation. Mosses spell out half-forgotten street plans. Crystalline trees grow through temple roofs in perfect alignment with old altars, as though the trees remember the religion.

These are not ruins in the ordinary sense. They are still being built, or unbuilt, depending on the day. Stones detach from one structure at night and migrate—slowly, over weeks—to another, slotting themselves in with soft grinding sounds that are the loudest noises the Shroudlands permit. No living hands guide them. The process is simply happening, one of the active dynamics (2.3) that persist in a place that should have finished its story long ago.

And then there is the reality breakdown (2.9).

It is not dramatic. That is the horror. The violations are quiet, patient, almost courteous. The horizon occasionally folds upward like the corner of a page, revealing a second, identical plateau stacked above the first. You can see your own figure walking there, days ahead of you or years behind. Colors that have no business existing pool in the lee of certain stones—colors that make the teeth hum. In certain districts of the dead cities, time has worn thin; stand in the right doorway and the temperature will rise to something almost temperate for exactly eleven heartbeats before the cold reasserts itself. Mirrors of ice form on windless days, reflecting not what is in front of them but what was there ten thousand years earlier, or what will be there when the plateau finally erodes into dust.

Gravity loosens its grip in long, wandering corridors that drift across the land. Walk through one and you rise gently until your feet leave the ground, then descend again minutes later, nauseous and reverent. Birds are not present to be trapped in these zones, so instead the crystalline reeds float upright in slow, vertical schools, their roots still anchored in the stone below.

There are no animals because animals require stable physics and reliable causality. The last ones either left or were edited out of being when the world began to forget the rules that once supported them. What remains is a slow conversation between cold stone, damp air, patient vegetation, and the exhausted idea of “city.” The place is not dying. It is past dying. It has settled into the final form that comes after decay—serene, vast, internally inconsistent, and still faintly, childishly busy with processes no mind remains to understand.

At night the normal light (1.7) fades into a deep, bruised indigo that still somehow lets you see the curvature of the plateau all the way to the false horizon where reality has begun to fray into pale threads. The threads wave like kelp in a current that does not exist.

If you stay long enough, the land will begin to edit you too. First your shadow will walk a few paces ahead of you. Then your footprints will appear behind you before you make them. Eventually you may look up to see yourself standing on a distant rise, perfectly still, waiting for the version of you that is still walking to arrive.

The Shroudlands are not empty.

They are finished.

And the finishing is taking forever.
