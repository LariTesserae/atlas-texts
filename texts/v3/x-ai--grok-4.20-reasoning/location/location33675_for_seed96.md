**The Weeping Lattice**

You stand on a high shelf of the world, where the air itself feels constructed—cool, bright, and edged with a metallic clarity that makes every breath taste faintly of wet stone. The elevation is not the clean thrust of a natural mountain but a stacked, accreted mass: tier upon tier of precisely cut and fitted blocks rising in a single unbroken body that stretches for many days’ walk in every direction. There is no soil, no bedrock, no unworked surface anywhere. The entire landscape is *made*, every cubic inch of it placed by patient hands using nothing more advanced than bronze levers, wooden cradles, and stone chisels. Yet the labor was so total, so obsessive, that the distinction between “building” and “geology” has dissolved.

The geometry is young—sharp arrises, knife-edged cornices, hexagonal sockets that have not yet rounded. Every angle still bites. Because the density is almost total, these angles crowd against one another without pause: colonnades abut ziggurats, staircases invert and become buttresses, bridges tunnel *through* other bridges. From any vantage the eye is refused a single resting place; the structure saturates the visual field the way coral saturates a reef, except there is no softness, only an infinity of deliberate edges. The scale is open enough that you can see the pattern continue for leagues—rising, folding, descending again—yet so packed that you could not stretch your arms fully in most places without touching two different worked surfaces.

Water is the only living movement. It does not fall from a sky that ever truly opens; instead it *condenses* directly out of the cool, bright air, beading on every plane as though the stone itself exhales. Every horizontal surface bears a shallow film. Every vertical face is streaked with slow tears that follow grooves cut centuries ago for exactly this purpose. The water collects in thousands of small basins, lips over edges in thin sheets, drips in perfectly spaced threads from the undersides of cantilevered blocks. The soundscape is therefore mostly quiet, yet never silent: a continuous, delicate percussion of individual drops striking other drops, or stone, or the surface of tiny artificial pools. The echoes are strange—flattened, overlapping, as if the architecture were designed to hold sound inside itself the way a sponge holds water.

There is no vegetation. Not a single lichen, not a filament of moss. The materials reject it. The stone is a pale, almost luminous travertine-like mineral that was quarried, cut, and reassembled from some older layer of the same world; its chemistry seems subtly hostile to the carbon-based. Nor are there animals. The only motion besides water is the occasional, almost geological shift of a single block settling a fraction of a millimeter after decades of pressure. These micro-movements produce a low, dry click that travels surprising distances through the lattice before vanishing.

The light is relentless. Whether from a close, cold sun or from some property of the atmosphere itself, illumination arrives from every angle at once. The pale stone throws it back amplified, so that shadows are thin and blue-edged, and every wet surface becomes a mirror full of blinding sky. In certain sunken courtyards the reflected brightness is almost hallucinatory; you find yourself squinting at your own hands.

The weirdness is quiet but cumulative. The patterns never quite repeat, yet they feel governed by a logic just beyond recognition—fractal in suggestion but executed with obvious hand tools, as though an entire civilization spent ten thousand years trying to approximate an equation using only mallets and straight-edges. Some corridors narrow until they become slits no wider than a forearm, yet still carry their full complement of carved glyphs and water channels. Other passages open suddenly into internal chambers the size of cathedrals, their roofs supported by impossible lattices of interlocking beams that seem to grow *out* of the walls rather than hold them up. You sense that the entire edifice is, in some way, a single object—less a city than an instrument built for a purpose so slow it can only be measured in epochs. The water, perhaps, is both lubricant and signal, carrying infinitesimal stresses from one district to another across the centuries.

Nothing is hurried. The drops fall. The stones sigh against one another every few lifetimes. Light burns. Water beads, runs, gathers, falls again. And the lattice—patient, total, wet, and impossibly bright—simply continues to exist, growing older without ever growing soft.
