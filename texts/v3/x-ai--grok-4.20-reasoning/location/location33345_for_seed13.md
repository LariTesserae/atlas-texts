**The Vale of Unworn Edges**

You stand in a high, open basin cupped between mountains that have no right to be so young. Their ridges rise like fresh fractures in the world’s crust—black, angular, almost glassy in places—cutting the sky with edges that should have been blunted by a hundred million years but have not. The peaks refuse to erode. Rain falls, yet the stone stays sharp. This is the first rule the place teaches you: nothing here is allowed to finish becoming.

The air is cool, the temperature locked at the edge of what feels like perpetual late-spring chill. A thin wind moves through the basin at the same low, steady pressure no matter the hour. It carries the damp of 1.4: not enough for moss to drown the rock, but enough for beads of condensation to pearl on every surface and slide in slow, identical paths down the same gullies they have always followed. Sometimes a soft rain arrives exactly when it is supposed to, lasts precisely as long as it must, and departs. The clouds reform in the same formation afterward. The cycle is not seasonal. It is ritual.

Vegetation is lush where it dares appear, but the density is only 1.0. Between the green pockets lie wide, empty stretches of pale stone and sparse gravel. In the pockets themselves the growth is almost obscene: ferns with fronds too perfectly symmetrical, thick mats of grass whose blades never yellow, clusters of small trees whose leaves are the color of oxidized copper and whose bark smells faintly of iron and rain. Their roots find purchase in cracks that never widen. The plants do not compete. They have reached equilibrium so complete that “growth” has become a kind of memory they no longer need.

Across the middle of the basin spreads the city—Calla Morren, the only settlement the world has ever allowed. It is urban in organization and scale, yet built entirely with primitive means: massive dressed stone, cedar beams dragged by rope and lever, lime mortar mixed by hand. The architecture is layered in three distinct rings. The outermost is a wide terrace of low, square dwellings whose roofs collect the intermittent water into stone channels that feed the middle ring. There the structures grow taller, still windowless on the lower levels, their walls incised with repeating geometric patterns that the inhabitants re-carve every time the rain threatens to soften a line. At the center rises the Keel, a stepped pyramidal complex of black basalt dragged from the young cliffs. No metal tools were used in its making; only fire, water, and patient human muscle across generations that somehow never age.

The people themselves are quiet. Their language is mostly gesture and breath. They move through the same daily circuits they have always walked, performing the same small maintenances that keep the equilibrium from tipping. A woman sweeps the same threshold with the same broom of copper-colored twigs. A man repairs the same hairline crack in the same wall using the same mixture of clay and crushed fern. They are not unhappy. They are part of the stasis.

Fauna is abundant and strangely courteous. Flocks of slate-gray birds that never sing wheel in exact ellipses above the Keel at the same hour every “day.” Herds of small antelope-like creatures with liquid black eyes and spiraling horns graze the lush pockets, always leaving exactly as much grass as will regrow before they return. In the deeper gullies, six-legged lizards the color of wet slate watch you with calm, ancient faces; they do not blink in sequence but in perfect canon. None of them fear the people. None of them truly hunt. Death is not forbidden here, but it is… impolite. When an animal does die, another of precisely the same age and condition appears elsewhere in the valley within hours, as though the basin itself has simply rebalanced the books.

Sound is scarce. The wind is a constant low note that never rises or falls. The drip of condensation from the rooflines is the loudest thing most days. Occasionally a single bell of fired clay is struck inside the Keel—once, always at the same interval—and the tone lingers far longer than physics should allow, as if the air itself is reluctant to let it go.

There is weirdness, but it is quiet weirdness. The young mountains occasionally emit a low harmonic that makes your teeth ache, though no rock ever falls. At certain angles the terraces of Calla Morren appear to have more levels than they did a moment before, then resolve back to three. The trees in the largest copse are all exact multiples of the same fractal branch pattern; stand among them long enough and you begin to feel your own veins trying to copy the geometry. Most unsettling of all is the light. It is normal daylight—warm, clear, slightly angled as though the sun sits at three in the afternoon—but the sun never moves. There is no sunset, no true night. The stars are fixed points that do not wheel. The world is not dark; it has simply decided that this particular quality of afternoon is the correct and eternal one.

This is the final and deepest rule: the Vale of Unworn Edges is finished. It reached equilibrium an unknowable time ago and has been allowed to remain so. The city maintains it. The animals perform it. The rain rehearses it. The mountains refuse to be worn down by it.

If you stay long enough, you may notice your own footprints refusing to deepen. The hem of your cloak stops collecting dew after the first hour. Your heartbeat begins, very gently, to sync with the single unchanging wind.

Some travelers leave before that happens.

Some do not.
