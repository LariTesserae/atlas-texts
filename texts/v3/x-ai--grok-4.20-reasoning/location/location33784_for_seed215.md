**The Shroudlands**

The land is not yet finished.

You feel it in the bones of your feet before your eyes can parse it: a low, almost tectonic humming, less sound than pressure, as though the crust itself is still deciding what it wants to become. This is high country—1.6 on the scale, though the scale itself feels like a joke here. The horizon bends with the planet’s own curvature, and the sky is a pale, iron-cold blue that normal light has to fight to warm. Clouds form and unform in minutes, not because of any ordinary meteorology but because the air is so cold and so full of microscopic water that it condenses the moment anything disturbs it. Every exhalation becomes a small ghost. Every rock face weeps.

The water here is not generous, but it is constant. It does not fall so much as arrive—seeping upward through fractures that are still opening, condensing out of the thin air in silvery beads, sliding down black columnar basalt like tears on obsidian cheeks. In low places it pools into mirror-still tarns edged with rimes of ice that never quite melt and never quite freeze. The ground is spongy with permafrost and new gravel; step in the wrong place and your boot sinks to the ankle in a slurry of stone that was molten only centuries ago. The erosion parameter is almost zero because erosion has not yet been given permission to begin. The land is still being lifted.

Vegetation is a rumor. Here and there a crust of silver-green lichen grips the rock like a secret. In the lees of ridges you find tight fists of cushion moss and the occasional wire-thin sedge that rattles when the wind remembers it exists. That is all. The eye starves on green.

Yet the fauna is ridiculous.

Great herds of shaggy, six-legged grazers the color of wet slate move in near-silence across the middle distance, their breath steaming in slow plumes. They crop the lichen with delicate, almost surgical mouths and leave behind faint geometric trails where their footfall compacts the gravel into something that will one day be pavement. Above them, vast flocks of white-winged raptors ride thermals so wide they qualify as weather systems of their own. Smaller things—burrowers, gliders, delicate many-legged filter-feeders that sip the condensing mist from the air—fill every spare cubic meter. Life has arrived before soil. It is impatient.

The quiet is enormous. Sound falls into the spaces between things and dies there. You can hear your own heartbeat. A distant herd lowing two kilometers away arrives at your ear as a minor chord played inside a bell. Only rarely does something louder assert itself: the glassy crack of a rock face splitting as new pressure from below shoulders it aside, or the subsonic thrum that rolls through the ground when one of the Engines wakes.

The Engines are the reason the land is still emerging.

They are not visible. Not exactly. What is visible are their effects and their architecture. Across the continental plate—too large for any single weather to ignore, large enough to breed its own pressure cells—stand the Cities That Grow. They are not built upon the land; they are being coaxed out of it. Spires of black glass and living metal rise in slow, deliberate pulses from raw bedrock, following mineral veins the Engines have sweetened for them. Streets extrude themselves a few meters per day, self-leveling, self-repairing, drinking the weeping water and exhaling warm, breathable air that creates localized microclimates where hardier plants immediately take hold. The architecture is half geology, half symphony. Buttresses form like crystals. Bridges knit across new canyons before the canyons have finished widening. Everything is in motion, yet nothing feels rushed. The cities are patient the way mountains are patient.

Between the cities lie hundreds of kilometers of deliberate emptiness. The sparse density is intentional. The Shroudlands were designed—by minds that no longer require names—to give every process room to breathe. Predator and prey move across these distances in long, stately arcs that can last weeks. A pack of mirror-furred stalkers will shadow a lichen herd for nine days before the geometry of the hunt suddenly becomes inevitable. When the kill happens it is almost soundless, over in a sigh of exhaled frost.

At night the temperature drops further and the condensing atmosphere becomes theatrical. Fog pours upward out of every fracture, glowing faintly where it passes through the invisible lattices of planetary-scale climate machinery. The stars are hard and close. Sometimes a new ridge simply appears between dusk and dawn, shoved up by forces that still answer to the Engines far below. The locals—those few humans, post-humans, and synthetic cultures who have chosen to live here—call these events “birthquakes.” They are treated with the same calm one might give a particularly vigorous spring tide.

The rule of this world is simple and merciless: nothing is allowed to finish.

The land must keep becoming. The cities must keep emerging alongside it. The herds must keep moving. The mist must keep condensing. If any part of the system were ever to declare itself complete, the Engines would read that as failure and begin to unmake it. So everything remains larval, exquisite, raw.

Stand on the new ridge long enough and you begin to feel the next fracture preparing beneath you—an almost erotic tension in the stone. The air thickens. Water beads on your eyelashes. Far away, one of the living spires catches the normal light and answers it with a slow, deliberate pulse of its own, as though acknowledging a shared heartbeat.

This is not a landscape.

It is a conversation that has not yet decided how many speakers it needs.
