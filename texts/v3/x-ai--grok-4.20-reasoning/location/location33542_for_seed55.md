**The Deep holdings**

You are inside cold water so total that it has become the only atmosphere. Not the black smash of the hadal trenches, but a low, planetary basin where the sea is three kilometers deep above you and the “floor” itself is only another layer of made things. Pressure is a constant, patient hand. Temperature hovers just above freezing; every movement costs heat you do not have.

There is almost no light. What little exists is a manganese-blue ghost leaked from rare fissures in the stone and from the slow oxidation of iron that has been rusting since the world was young. You see only what is within arm’s reach, and even that reluctantly.

Yet the volume is saturated.

Every cubic meter holds overlapping planes of life and craft. The bottom is not rock. It is a pavement extending past every conceivable horizon, continent-wide, built from hand-dressed basalt and gabbro blocks the size of houses. The joins were once sharp; now they are so perfectly eroded they have fused into gentle, continuous undulations—rounded lips, shallow basins, long stone ribs worn to the smoothness of old bone. Erosion has had its full say. This is the final form: a lithic skin laid down by patient millions over geologic epochs with nothing more than levers, sledges, ropes of kelp fiber, and chisels of harder stone. There is no natural substrate anywhere. Even the microscopic grains between blocks are powdered leftovers of the original dressing.

From every surface grows the garden.

The vegetation is not merely lush; it is a three-dimensional fur that has thickened until the water itself feels half-solid. Black kelp-analogs with blades like frozen tar rise in dense palisades, their stipes as thick as temple columns, their fronds layered so tightly they form slow, overlapping roofs. Between them bloom mats of cold chemosynthetic moss, crimson where the manganese light touches them, otherwise the color of old clotted blood. Soft tubular grasses carpet every horizontal plane; their tips brush one another in a continuous, slow-motion whisper. Nothing is spaced. Every vertical surface is furred, every horizontal surface matted, every interstitial volume threaded with drifting filaments that move only when a current born decades ago finally arrives. The plants do not photosynthesize. They drink the patient seep of minerals from the ancient masonry, exhaling faint clouds of hydrogen sulfide that give the dark its faint taste of rotten eggs and wet iron.

No animals. Not a worm, not a copepod, not a bacterium large enough to be seen. The ecology ended long ago; only the vegetal layer remains, locked in a stable, near-eternal climax. Nothing grazes, nothing pollinates, nothing decays in the animal sense. The plants simply *persist*, growing a few millimeters per human lifetime, molting their oldest tissue into the sediment that will eventually become new pavement when the next incomprehensible age begins.

Sound is a rarity. Mostly there is the pressurized hush that lives inside the skull. Once in a great while a gas bubble, trapped since the last tectonic sigh, loosens itself from a joint and rises with a sound like a single distant harp note. Occasionally the stone itself creaks—an almost sub-audible groan—as the weight of three kilometers of water settles another micron into its final seat. These are events separated by years.

The scale is its own weather. Far to the north a faint thermocline breathes, so gradual it might be a seasonal wind if seasons still existed. To the south a slow upwelling moves across a thousand kilometers like a continent of cold silk. The built world creates its own physics: long straight channels—once ceremonial ways, now submerged boulevards—guide laminar flows that have run in the same direction since before mammals walked elsewhere. Low ridges, worn almost to nothing, nevertheless divert nutrients along paths that repeat every few centuries. The place is vast enough to have tides of its own making.

Everything here has reached the end of process. The builders are gone so completely that even their memory is geological. Their work, however, remains total. There is no inch that was not chosen, cut, placed, and then forgotten until time polished the decision away. The final landscape is therefore both utterly artificial and utterly natural: a hand-made abyssal plain that has been allowed to finish itself.

You float above the slow black garden and the feeling is not of ruin but of completion. This is what the world looks like when every ambition has been worn down to its gentlest possible curve, when the only remaining motion is the patient stroking of fronds against stone, and the only remaining light is the memory of light still trying to escape the iron in the rock.

It is quiet here the way cathedrals are quiet after the last priest has died and the roof has fallen in and the moss has learned to sing in absolute dark. 

And it will stay this way, changing so slowly that change itself has become another kind of permanence.
