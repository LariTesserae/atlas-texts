**The Shroudworks**

You stand on a grated catwalk that was never meant for feet. The metal is warm—blood-warm—because something deep inside the lattice still exhales residual reactor heat from a process that ended so long ago the records no longer exist. Everything here is made. There is no soil, no stone, no original crust. The entire elevation is a single continuous artifact: a stacked industrial range built in epochs when the world was younger and more arrogant. It rises in colossal, weathered tiers to 8,000 meters and beyond, far above the weather most days, yet still damp.

Moisture arrives as condensation. The air is thin but supersaturated along every cold pipe and heat-exchange fin; droplets the size of coins form, hang, then fall in slow, patient chains. They trace the same paths they have traced for centuries, etching black-green patinas into the steel. Occasionally a real rain finds its way through the upper vents—brief, warm, almost oily—and the whole structure answers with a sound like distant applause moving across a thousand acres of ductwork and scaffolding.

The vegetation is obscene in its vigor. Lianas thick as thighs wrap I-beams that were once load-bearing for continent-scale atmospheric processors. Broad leaves the color of oxidized copper thrust from every joint and valve. Moss grows in perfect geometric ribs along expansion seams, following the original engineering diagrams as though the blueprints themselves had become photosynthetic. Ferns erupt from ruptured transformer housings; their roots have drunk enough leaked coolant over the millennia that the fronds now fluoresce faintly at dusk. It is not wilderness colonizing industry. It is industry that has become the substrate, the lattice, the very geology. The jungle did not win. The jungle was invited and then never left.

Fauna is everywhere, loud and layered. Iron-colored macaques with oil-slick fur leap between busbars. Parrots the color of safety orange scream directional commands no one has obeyed in four hundred years. Beetles with wing cases like brushed aluminum clatter through vents. In the deeper shafts you can hear the wet slap of something large moving through flooded coolant reservoirs—things that were never supposed to evolve here but did, slowly, while the world above kept turning. Their calls are industrial: metallic clangs, low harmonic drones, the occasional pneumatic hiss that might be breathing.

The light is merciless. Above the cloud deck the sun hammers every reflective surface—polished titanium still bright after millennia, white ceramic cladding, banks of solar film half-shredded by wind and root. The glare is so constant that many animals have developed mirrored scales or leaves that swivel to shed excess photons. At night the Works still glows: ancient sulfur lamps behind wire cages, emergency strips fed by micro-generators turned by trickling water, bioluminescent lichen that has learned to colonize the old filament arrays.

Sound is perpetual but never frantic. The Shroudworks operates on geological tempo. Deep inside the stack, slow pumps—some still functional—move warm water through capillary pipes the size of highways. The vibration is so low it is felt in the teeth more than heard. Every few hours a thermal joint sighs as it expands or contracts by half a millimeter. Waterfalls formed inside disused flue chimneys roar like jet engines but never change volume; they have reached equilibrium. The macaques scream, the parrots curse, the wind finds new holes in the lattice and plays the whole structure like a pipe organ the size of a mountain range. It is loud the way a cathedral is loud—constant, architectural, almost devotional.

The rules here are simple and ancient:

- Nothing is allowed to rot completely. The original builders engineered against entropy so thoroughly that even collapse is stylized. When a girder fails, it fails into a position that becomes new habitat.
- Water is moved, never owned. Every drop is accounted for by machines that no longer remember why they count.
- Change is permitted, but only across centuries. A vine may take seventy years to bridge two cooling towers. A new species may require three hundred. The Works waits with industrial patience.
- There is no “outside.” Every horizon is more Works. Distant ridges are simply later construction phases, their outlines softened by millennia of leaf and rust. Valleys are negative space between megastructures. The scale is open in the way an engine block the size of a county is open: you can walk for days and never leave the same machine.

This is not ruin. This is climax ecology for a civilization that built its own planet and then, with typical industrial humility, forgot to turn the lights off. The Shroudworks breathes, drips, grows, and sings to itself in frequencies older than language, and it will continue doing so long after anything that might have called it beautiful or monstrous has turned to new layers of patina on its endless, patient skin.
