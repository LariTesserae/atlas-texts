**The Shroudlands**

You stand inside a breathing cathedral that forgot it was ever anything but a mountain.

The air is warm and heavy, like breath against the back of your neck. Condensation beads on every surface and falls in slow, fat drops that take minutes to reach the ground, delayed by a thousand leaves. Real rain is rare; instead the sky sweats. Water gathers in cupped leaves the size of shields, in crevices between fused root-walls, in black mirror-pools that never quite overflow. Everything is damp, but nothing is drowned. The moisture moves in patient cycles, seeping downward through living strata only to be drawn up again by capillary thirst.

There is almost no light. The canopy is not above you—it is a ceiling miles thick, built in a hundred stacked layers of leaf, vine, epiphyte, and the bodies of animals that died mid-climb. What little illumination reaches the understory is the color of old brass and feels borrowed. You move through a perpetual twilight that has lasted for ten thousand years.

Yet the vegetation is obscene in its lushness. Trees do not merely grow here; they colonize, fuse, and reinforce one another until a single “tree” is actually a continent of fused trunks wrapped in moss so thick it has its own topography. Lianas thick as a man’s thigh loop through the dark like suspension cables. Every vertical surface is furred with ferns, orchids, and bromeliads. There is no bare ground. There has not been bare ground since before language existed.

Because there *is* no ground.

The entire highland—every ridge, valley, cliff, and plateau—is built. Not constructed by tools, but by life itself. This is what “total built” looks like when biology has had geological time. The substrate beneath your feet is compacted leaf-litter, chitin, guano, fungal mats, abandoned paper nests, the calcium carbonate of trillions of crushed shells, and the slowly mineralizing bodies of creatures whose generations overlap so completely that death and architecture are the same process. The “mountains” are biogenic. The escarpments are the exposed faces of ancient hive-ridges. The caves are the negative space left by colossal root systems that rotted away centuries ago and were immediately refilled by new growth. Erosion has had eons to gentle every edge; the landscape is old, weathered, and perfectly in balance with itself. It feels like nature because it *is* nature. It simply has no memory of a time when it was not also a city built by its own inhabitants.

And the inhabitants are overwhelming.

The sound is not something you hear. It is something that presses on your skin. A continuous, layered roar made of insect stridulation so dense it becomes texture, frog choruses like rolling thunder, bird calls stacked in a hundred frequencies, the wet clicking of mandibles, the low organ-note of larger beasts breathing inside hollow trees. It is deafening in the truest sense: after twenty minutes you stop trying to speak. After an hour you stop noticing it is loud. The noise becomes the silence of this place.

Every cubic meter contains dozens of creatures. Beetles the color of wet hematite crawl over one another in living armor plates. Clouds of midges move like slow smoke between the trunks. Arboreal frogs the size of thumbnails sing from the inside of pitcher plants. Vast colonies of social insects maintain galleries that run for kilometers through the living mass, their tunnels becoming the veins of the plateau itself. Monkeys with luminous eyes watch you from the middle layers, their bodies half-hidden among leaves that are also nests. Everything touches everything else. There is no empty space. Life is shoulder-to-shoulder, generation stacked upon generation, patient and ravenous and slow.

The Shroudlands extend for hundreds of kilometers across the high plateau—ridge after ridge of the same dark, saturated, deafening biomass. From any rise (if you can climb high enough to reach a place where a little gray light leaks through) you see only an undulating carpet of canopy stretching to every horizon, broken by occasional taller spires that are themselves ancient colony structures now colonized by trees.

Nothing happens quickly. A fallen giant tree is not an event; it is a century-long realignment of a thousand species. A cliff face collapses at the pace of moss. Even the rivers move with deliberation, sliding through channels they have been polishing for millennia, their banks soft with living felt.

This is not a jungle that grew on a mountain.

This is a mountain that grew as a jungle, until the distinction became meaningless.

And still it grows, slowly, deafeningly, in the warm dark, one living brick at a time.
