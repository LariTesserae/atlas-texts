**The Underforge of Veyr-Khâd**

You descend through a final fissure where the air itself begins to sweat. The transition is not sudden but inevitable: the stone grows warmer beneath your hands until it is almost feverish, and then the tunnel opens.

The chamber is cathedral-scaled yet feels intimate, bounded. Its roof arches perhaps forty meters above the floor at the highest point, but the space is only wide enough that a shout reaches the opposite wall in under two seconds. Everything here is still being born. The rock is not finished with itself.

Hot mineral water weeps continuously from every surface. It does not drip so much as bead, condense, and slide in slow silver sheets down black basalt and newly-forming travertine. Where it meets cooler air it steams, so the entire volume is filled with a low, sweet-smelling mist that condenses again on every pipe, every girder, every human brow. The water is just below boiling in the deepest channels; it cools to a tolerable heat only in the raised cisterns where the settlement draws its supply. The air is so humid that iron rusts in hours if not painted with thick black tar. Rust and tar are therefore the dominant colors—flaking red, wet black, and the sickly amber of sodium lamps spaced too far apart.

The town itself is sparse within its own walls. A few thousand souls live here in layered iron barracks and brick ziggurats that rise only three or four stories before they meet the living rock. Between the structures lie long, echoing aisles of emptiness where dripping water has carved shallow runnels that never quite become rivers. Factories squat like iron toads along the eastern wall: three geothermal foundries, a carbide works, a battery plant whose acid vats are cooled by the same mineral springs that threaten to dissolve them. Their engines turn slowly, deliberately. Nothing here is in a hurry. The great flywheels revolve with the patience of geology.

The sound is constant and layered. You hear it before you can separate its parts: a deep, sub-audible throb of turbines driven by flashing hot water, the metallic clack of expansion joints breathing in the heat, the hiss of steam finding new cracks in the rock. Over this rides the fauna.

The insects are overwhelming.

They are not monstrous. They are simply numberless. Pale cave crickets the length of a finger migrate across the walls in slow, rippling sheets. Cockroaches the color of old bone scuttle between the rails of the narrow-gauge ore carts. Thick-bodied moths with damp velvet wings cling to the undersides of every lamp housing, their wingbeats making a soft, perpetual flutter that sounds like rain on a tin roof. They feed on the thermophilic mats of bacteria that bloom orange and purple wherever hot water trickles over stone, and on the constant spillage of industrial organics. When the shift whistle blows, the insects rise in a single slow exhalation that momentarily blots out the nearest lamp. Then they settle again, patient as the stone.

There is almost no vegetation. A few ropes of pale fungus hang like drowned hair from the ceiling where steam condenses and drips in one steady place. That is all.

The geology itself is embryonic. This is not an ancient cavern system. It is a fresh wound in the crust where superheated water, driven upward along a fault, is still carving and depositing at the same time. New travertine lips grow across old machinery month by month; workers must chisel them away or incorporate them. In the northern transept a half-formed column of mineral has swallowed an entire dormitory staircase. The residents simply built a new stair around the growing pillar. No one expects the architecture to remain unchanged for more than a decade. The city is a negotiation with a landscape that has not yet decided what it wants to be.

The people move slowly in the heat. Their clothes are dark with perpetual damp. They speak little. When they do, their voices are swallowed by the engines and the soft, endless clicking of insect legs. At the center of the largest chamber stands the Brass Mother, a civic pump the height of three men that draws scalding water from below and cools it just enough to be usable. Around its base the insects are thickest, forming a living carpet that parts and closes again as workers approach.

There are no horizons. There is only the next lamp, the next dripping wall, the next slow turning of a wheel that will still be turning long after every person now alive here has been carried out through the fissure and given to the cooler world above. The place is loud, hot, wet, and unfinished. It is being born, and birth is noisy work.

Yet it holds a kind of terrible patience. The rock steams. The machines turn. The insects flow. Everything waits for the next slow fold of the crust, the next inch of travertine, the next century. 

This is Veyr-Khâd. It is not yet what it will become, and it already is exactly what it must be.
