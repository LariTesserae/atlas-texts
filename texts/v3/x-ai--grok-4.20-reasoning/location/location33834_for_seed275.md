**The Hollow Lattice**

You step out from the access shaft into a coolness that feels unnaturally complete, as though the rock itself has forgotten how to hold heat. The air is so dry it husks the nostrils; there is no moisture, no damp, no seep. Nothing here has ever known liquid water. The chemistry that sustains the place runs on volatile hydrocarbons trapped in micro-cavities within the stone and on sulfur aerosols that drift in slow, invisible currents. Where Earth life would desiccate in hours, this ecology breathes a different oxygen.

The first impression is of an unfinished cathedral the size of a mountain range turned on its side. The ceiling vanishes into haze two kilometers above. The floor is not floor but a slowly undulating plain of living substrate that has only recently decided to become ground. Great pale ribs and lattices rise from it—some slender as cables, others thicker than grain silos—branching, rejoining, and branching again in geometries that feel both crystalline and deliberate. This is the vegetation: not forest, not garden, but a single continental organism whose above-ground expression is architecture. It is still embryonic. Fresh fracture planes glitter on the cavern walls where the Lattice has only begun to pry the rock apart with slow enzymatic pressure and microscopic pressure-wedging. Raw geology bleeds into biology; the border is weeks, perhaps months old.

Yet already a city has taken root inside the organism.

The settlement is not carved *from* the Lattice so much as *grown alongside* it, like ironwork trained through living bone. Factory complexes stand in the wider clearings between colossal trunks, each complex separated from the next by half a kilometer or more of open, echoing space. Density is low; the city feels like a handful of towns dropped across the floor of an inland sea that dried before life began. Between the industrial nodes lie wide lawns of the organism itself—flat, pale, velvety expanses that give slightly underfoot, as if you are walking on the skin of an enormous creature that has chosen, for now, not to flinch.

The plants do not use chlorophyll. Their color is the color of old bone, parchment, and galvanized steel. Surfaces are matte, almost dusty, covered in a fine efflorescence that is both spore and sensor. Where the Lattice has been guided by industrial shaping, it forms perfect cylindrical columns, helical ramps, and vaulted ceilings that double as conduits. Pipes disappear into the living tissue and re-emerge kilometers away carrying not water but a dense, warm mist of synthesized alcohols and metallic salts. Refineries squat on wide platforms grown expressly for them; their smokestacks exhale faint plumes of golden dust that catch the light like pollen before the filtration towers inhale it again. Everything is in motion, yet the motion is so large and so slow that the place feels mostly quiet.

Sound behaves strangely here. The Lattice is an acoustic sponge. Footsteps die after thirty meters. The rumble of foundries and the rhythmic heartbeat of atmospheric processors arrive as a low, constant organ note that you feel in the sternum more than hear. Every few minutes a soft creak travels through the vaults overhead—like an old sailing ship adjusting to the wind—followed by a faint rain of pale powder as the organism grows another few millimeters. That is the loudest event most days. The quiet is not emptiness; it is absorption. The Lattice listens.

Light is normal, almost aggressively so. Vast banks of industrial arc lamps hang from cables grown directly from the ceiling ribs, bathing whole districts in a cold, even daylight the color of winter sun. Between the lit zones the shadows are absolute. The contrast creates an odd theatricality: one moment you stand in a factory square bright enough to read by, the next you cross into a natural nave where the only illumination is the faint phosphorescence that sometimes leaks from the Lattice’s own veins when it is digesting particularly rich ore.

Fauna is abundant but rarely near. The creatures belong to the Lattice as much as the factories do. Most are small—sheathed beetles the color of rusted iron that move in slow, clicking caravans across the pale plains, sipping hydrocarbons from glandular pores. Larger forms drift in the upper air: translucent, gas-filled sacs trailing feathery feeding nets, half animal, half aerial plankton. Occasionally something bigger passes in the middle distance: a long, jointed thing like a pale millipede crossed with a cathedral pipe organ, sliding along a rib with the unhurried certainty of a creature that has never known a horizon. They are not numerous in any one place, yet you sense there are millions. The sparse density of the city is mirrored by the fauna; everything is spread thin across an ecosystem still inventing its own carrying capacity.

The strangeness reveals itself gradually.

The Lattice is one organism. Not metaphorically—literally. Every pillar, every filament, every factory-grown buttress shares the same slow chemical language. The industrial civilization that lives here did not conquer the cave; it was invited. The earliest probes found the organism already chewing through the rock in perfect geometric lattices, as though it had been waiting for company. The factories now serve a dual purpose: they refine the metallic byproducts the Lattice excretes, and they prune and stimulate its growth in directions useful to both parties. In return the organism provides structure, atmosphere regulation, and a kind of distributed nervous system that the engineers have learned to tap. Certain control rooms do not use wires. Technicians simply press their palms to the living wall and wait for the slow electrochemical answer.

This is why the place feels both raw and ancient at once. It is embryonic and post-mature in the same breath. The geology is still being born; the biology has already achieved planetary scale. The city is both parasite and symbiote, both wound and medicine. Sometimes the Lattice decides a particular district has become unbalanced. When that happens the pale floor simply rises during the night, gently lifting buildings and pipes until the geometry is corrected. The inhabitants have learned not to argue.

Rules of the world:

- Nothing here rusts, because nothing here remembers water.
- Growth is sacred and taxed. Every new cubic meter of Lattice must be reported.
- Sound is currency. The quiet is cultivated; loud noise is pruned the way a gardener removes suckers.
- Light is temporary. The arc lamps are on loan. If the Lattice ever decides it no longer needs them, they will be absorbed in weeks.
- The predator-prey dynamic exists, but it is glacial. A kill may take three months to complete. Patience is the only evolutionary pressure that matters.

Stand long enough in one of the wide, empty plains between factory clusters and you begin to feel the faint, seismic pulse beneath your boots: the heartbeat of something that has never seen the sky and is in no hurry to. It is cool, dry, immense, and quietly, industriously alive. The Lattice is still deciding what it wants to become, and the city—half machine, half prayer—is helping it choose.
