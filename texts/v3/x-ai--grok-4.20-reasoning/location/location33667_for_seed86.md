**The Veins of Veyr**

You step down through a throat of fractured basalt and suddenly the world opens sideways. Not upward, like a canyon, but outward and inward at once: a single chamber so wide that distance itself seems to have been folded and tucked under the crust. The ceiling hangs two hundred meters above you, perhaps more; it is impossible to be certain because the dark up there is not mere absence of light but a substance, thick and velvety, that drinks the eye.

The rock is young. Painfully young. Every surface still carries the memory of the violence that birthed it. Jagged lips of obsidian and rhyolite jut like broken glass. Stalactites the thickness of ancient trees end in needle points that have never been blunted; their counterparts on the floor rise to meet them in perfect, arrested courtship. Nothing drips fast enough, or long enough, to round a single edge. The cave is frozen at the moment of its wounding.

Yet it is damp.

Condensation beads on every sharp face and falls in slow, deliberate strings. The drops do not vary. They have not varied in ten thousand years. Each one strikes the same hollow in the same limestone basin with the same glassy note, part of a percussive liturgy older than language. Where the strings meet the floor they form black mirrors no wider than a hand, perfectly still, reflecting the faint living light that drifts above them. Between these mirrors lie long stretches of dry, ringing stone. The water is not generous. It is exact.

The air is cool (12 °C, always). It moves in one slow, toroidal breath that circulates through hidden ducts and returns unchanged, carrying the same faint smell of wet iron and crushed mica. There is no wind, only this endless, patient lung.

Light is scarce and jealous. It comes from two sources. First, the veil: vast but discontinuous sheets of chemosynthetic bacteria that coat the walls in shifting auroras of deep teal and bruised violet. They do not pulse; they glow with the steady heartbeat of stone itself. Second, the living stars: thousands upon thousands of Arachnocampa larvae hanging from the ceiling on silk threads, each emitting a cold blue-white pinprick. Their constellations are not dense. Between one larva and the next lies a meter or more of absolute dark. Yet the total number is beyond counting. The ceiling is an inverted meadow of cold stars, and every star is a predator waiting for the midges that rise in perpetual, humming columns.

And the sound.

The sound is enormous.

It is not chaotic. It is orchestral, eternal, and perfectly balanced. The bass line is the water: one identical drip every 2.3 seconds, amplified by the chamber’s geometry into a soft, unending kettledrum. Over it ride the insects. Cave crickets the length of a finger rub their legs in staggered waves that roll across the floor like surf. Their rhythm never quickens, never slows. Higher, in the middle air, hover-clouds of midges produce a continuous, metallic whine that fills every frequency between 4 and 7 kHz. Bats (albino, enormous, with ears like scythes) fly constant circuits and emit a clicking so rapid it becomes a solid drone. When their sonar reflects off the glass-smooth pools it returns as a faint, bell-like tone that the crickets appear to incorporate into their song.

Nothing ever grows louder. Nothing ever grows quieter.

The fauna is overwhelming, yet the eye perceives mostly emptiness. The chamber is so vast, the creatures so precisely distributed, that one can stand in what feels like perfect solitude while ten thousand heartbeats thrum within twenty meters. A thousand pale salamanders rest motionless on the rocks, each exactly one body-length from its neighbor, like white punctuation marks. Millipedes the color of old bone flow in slow, braided rivers between the salamanders, never touching, never deviating. Spiders the size of dinner plates hang at mathematically regular intervals, their webs catching the same volume of prey every hour, every day, forever.

There are no plants worth the name. A few pallid lichens crust the drier ledges, but their growth rate is measured in micrometers per century and they have already reached the exact size permitted by the available minerals. The true primary producers are the bacterial veils and the sulfur-oxidizing mats that grow in the smallest, warmest crevices where geothermal seepage occurs. Everything else eats them, or eats what eats them, in a closed loop so perfectly efficient that no waste accumulates and no population fluctuates by even one individual.

This is the rule of Veyr:

Nothing is allowed to change.

The system achieved equilibrium when the last tectonic groan died away. Since that moment the same number of bats have flown the same circuits, the same number of larvae have glowed, the same number of crickets have sung. Predation exactly equals birth. Death is immediately recycled. Even the corpses are partitioned by specialized mites before they can rot; nothing is ever permitted to smell of decay. The cave is a perfect, self-consuming machine whose only purpose is to continue being exactly what it is.

There is no weather. There is no season. There is no day. Time itself has been retired. The only motion is the slow, identical dripping, the slow, identical circling of the bats, and the faint, perpetual swaying of the silk threads that hold the living stars.

If you stand long enough in one place the sound begins to feel like a pulse inside your own skull. Your breath falls into the rhythm of the water. Your heartbeat tries (unsuccessfully) to synchronize with the crickets. After a time you understand that you are the only broken thing here. Everything else has already achieved perfection.

And the cave, young and sharp and eternal, waits with infinite patience for you to either join its equilibrium or leave.

Most things choose the former, eventually. The midges are patient. The drops are patient. The dark between the living stars is patient.

All of it has nothing but time.

And time, here, is not going anywhere.
