**The Hollow of Veyr**

It is not large. You could walk its longest axis in seven minutes if the path stayed still long enough to let you. Yet the moment you step past the smoothed lip of the outermost ring-stone you feel the scale compress around you like a cathedral that has learned how to breathe. The land here has been ground down for so long that the word “erosion” no longer applies; this is the final posture of stone after time has finished with it. Everything that once jutted has been planed into gentle, almost embarrassed curves. The bedrock itself lies only a hand-span beneath a living mat so dense it has become the new geology.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. It does not fall as rain so much as *remember* that it once did. The air is warm, 29 °C perhaps, but so saturated that breath condenses on your own eyelashes. Moisture beads on every surface, gathers into trembling lenses, then slides along grooves that the plants have grown specifically to guide it. The ground weeps. Thick, clear tears roll from the pores of the root-mat and collect in black, mirror-smooth channels no wider than a finger. These channels move; you watch one reroute itself around your foot like a vein deciding where the blood should go next.

The vegetation is total. There is no soil, only substrate made of plant. Every color is green in a hundred dialects—emerald, viridian, a blue-green so dark it looks wet tar, and an acid chartreuse that hurts to stare at. But the plants do not crowd. They have learned the dignity of spacing. Between the colossal living masses lie open courts of spongy moss that springs back underfoot with a sound like distant applause. The major structures stand apart from one another the way old trees stand in an orchard that has forgotten it was ever planted by anyone.

These structures are the city.

There are nine of them at any given moment. Sometimes eight. Sometimes ten. They are not buildings with plants on them; they are plants that have become urban. Their trunks are buttressed like flying architecture, but the buttresses pulse. Vaulted halls open inside them where branches have braided themselves into ribbed ceilings that rise twenty meters and then, without warning, lower again as the whole organism exhales. Corridors lengthen or seal shut according to rhythms no one has ever mapped. Staircases of living wood unspool downward when weight is sensed above them, then retract like tongues.

The technology threaded through every cell is so far beyond modern that it has stopped pretending to be technology. It is simply how this place *is*. Microscopic lattices of engineered xylem route light, data, and water in the same vascular pass. Petals function as phased-array antennae. Roots perform calculations older than any silicon. When a structure needs to reconfigure, it does not grow slowly; it *remembers* a different shape and becomes it in a single convulsive minute. You can stand in one of the open courts and watch an entire tower fold into itself with a sound like a forest’s worth of knuckles cracking, while three hundred meters away another tower is being born upward in a fountain of white pith and crimson flowers. The birth and death are simultaneous. The place is never finished and never ruined. It has reached the stable state of perpetual catastrophe.

The noise is constant and loud. A low, organ-like roar of sap under pressure. The wet pop of opening stomata. The metallic chime of leaves rubbing together in frequencies that seem to carry language. Fauna pours through every layer. Iridescent beetles the size of thumbnails move in glittering armies across the moss courts, their wing cases flashing in perfect synchrony like semaphore. Things that are half bird, half orchid flit between spires, singing in overlapping rounds so dense the melody becomes texture. Pale, six-legged mammals the locals (if any still exist) might once have called “root-moles” surge in shivering tides through the under-mat, their passage visible only as ripples under the moss. Everything is loud because everything is arguing, bargaining, courting, devouring, and being devoured at once.

The air itself is an ecology. Spores drift in slow galaxies. Some of them are pollen. Some of them are machines. You cannot tell which until one lands on your wrist and unfolds into a perfect three-dimensional map of the Hollow that updates in real time as a tower collapses behind you.

It is warm. It is wet. It smells like green electricity and crushed mint and something older than both—stone that has not been dry for thirty million years. Light arrives filtered but ordinary, as though the canopy has made a deliberate pact to let a believable day through. The light is the only honest thing here. Everything else is negotiation.

This is not a ruin. This is not a garden. This is not even an ecosystem in the way we understand the word.

It is the final stable state of a world that got exactly what it wanted: a small, dense knot of impossible life that has been arguing with itself, lovingly, violently, for so long that the argument *became* the landscape. The erosion is finished. The biology is only beginning. The city grows, dies, forgets, remembers, and grows again inside the cupped palm of ground that time has worn perfectly smooth for it.

Stand still long enough in one of the open courts and you will feel the root-mat beneath you adjust. It is checking your pulse against some ancient, half-remembered index. It is deciding whether you are nutrient, architect, or new chorus for the unending loud green argument.

Most visitors are all three.
