**The Sinking Lattice**

You are fully submerged.

The water is warm, almost blood-warm, yet so dense with dissolved salts and microscopic life that it feels viscous, like moving through amber that has not quite hardened. Pressure sits on your shoulders and chest with the weight of long acquaintance. There is no surface above you — only an unbroken column of darkness reaching up for what might be a thousand feet before it becomes something else. This is not the open ocean; this is a low, broad basin that has been drowned for so long that the word “flood” no longer applies. The world simply begins here, three hundred meters down, and has never seen the sky.

Light is almost extinct. What little exists is stolen: faint blue-green pinpricks, the occasional crimson pulse of a lantern-jelly drifting high overhead like a dying star, the brief silver flash of a school of glass eels turning in perfect unison. Most of the time you see nothing at all until something living decides to announce itself.

And something always does.

The fauna is overwhelming in its density. The water itself is half creature — a soup of copepods, arrow-worms, larval shrimp, and things that have no names in any tongue that has known sunlight. They brush your skin in constant, tactile rain. Larger shapes move through them: sinuous, blind eels with luminous barbels; colonies of translucent shrimp that lock together into single undulating ribbons twenty meters long; things that look like abandoned coats made of black feathers until they suddenly flex and become a predator. Every cubic meter is claimed. Every breath you take (if you could breathe) would be filtered first through gills that are not yours.

Yet there is almost no vegetation. No kelp, no sea-grass, no fleshy algae. Only thin bacterial veils that cling to surfaces like rust, and the occasional ghost-pale frond of a carnivorous hydroid that unfolds when something brushes it. The ecology is chemosynthetic, ancient, and self-contained. What grows here feeds on stone and heat.

And stone has grown into city.

The structures are the most unsettling part. They rise from the silted floor of the basin in dense, layered blocks — towers, arches, curving walls, half-collapsed galleries — all formed by the slow labor of uncountable generations of tube-worms, clams, glass sponges, and mineral precipitation. Over tens of thousands of years they have fused, died, been overgrown, eroded, and fused again until the distinction between creature, skeleton, and architecture has vanished. The forms look intentional. Streets — genuine processional avenues wide enough for whale-sized creatures — run between districts of differing texture and color. One quarter is dominated by brittle white spires of calcite that have weathered into fluted columns; another is upholstered in overlapping black shells that form overlapping scales like the hide of some buried dragon. Bridges span the avenues, grown rather than built, their undersides dripping with slow curtains of stalactitic worm-casing.

Everything is in balance. Erosion has had so many ages to work that the sharpest edges are gone; every surface is rounded, beveled, polished by current into shapes that guide water rather than fight it. The entire lattice breathes with the slow tidal pulse that still reaches this deep. When the moon is right (somewhere unimaginably far above), the currents strengthen and the whole city hums — a low, organ-deep note that vibrates in your teeth. Then it quiets again for days.

Sound itself is mostly quiet, but never absent. It has simply changed register. The crackle of snapping shrimp is constant but distant, like rain on a roof you cannot see. Every few minutes a deep, sub-audible thrum rolls through the water as some leviathan sings to itself on the far side of the basin. Closer, you hear the soft ticking of claws on shell, the whisper of millions of tiny filter fans working in unison, the occasional hollow pop as a gas bubble escapes from a vent and rises into the dark.

The vents are the secret heart. Scattered across the low basin like the streetlamps of a forgotten civilization, they exhale warmth and hydrogen sulfide. Around each one the life is densest — rings of red-plumed tube worms (the only strong color in the entire world), mats of white crabs, forests of bone-white clams. The heat they release keeps the water at its peculiar temperature and drives the slow convection currents that keep the avenues clear of silt. The city maintains itself.

There is a distinct feeling that the place is *in motion*, even though nothing seems to be happening quickly. Predators glide along the avenues in predictable tides. Swarms rise and fall like breath. Sections of the lattice are growing; other sections are gently collapsing into new shapes. The entire submerged region is one slow, deliberate metabolic process spread across kilometers. It has been doing this since before any land animal learned to walk.

The weirdness is quiet but persistent. Sometimes the geometry of the spires and bridges aligns in ways that feel like language — not decoration, not coincidence, but something the eye insists must mean. Bioluminescent bacteria occasionally bloom across a wall in perfect geometric bands that last for an hour before they fade. Certain blind fish follow routes that never vary, as though tracing avenues that existed before the structures themselves. The place does not feel built by minds, exactly. It feels *remembered* by biology.

You float between two colossal shells that have become the walls of a canyon-street. Warm water moves past you in a slow, living current. A ribbon of silver shrimp glides by, so dense it momentarily blacks out what little light there is. Far below, something enormous and pale shifts its weight, sending a pressure wave that rocks you gently. The lattice answers with its low, tectonic hum.

This is not ruin.  
This is a city that was never anything but alive, and has simply learned how to wear the shape of civilization without ever needing tools, or hands, or eyes.

It has all the time in the world.  
And it is still building.
