**The Condensate**

It has no true name, only the low, constant vibration that moves through everything here like a shared pulse. Call it the Condensate if you must speak of it at all.

The first thing you notice is not the dark — the dark is too complete to be noticed. It is the *weight* of the dark. A warm, wet pressure that sits on the skin, in the lungs, behind the eyes. The air is so saturated that water precipitates directly onto any surface cooler than the ambient 29 °C. Every metal edge, every fungal frond, every abandoned girder weeps. The rocks themselves sweat. You can hold your hand out and watch beads form on your palm within seconds, as though the atmosphere is gently licking you.

The land is high — a single continental plateau lifted so far above whatever lies beyond its edges that its weather has forgotten the lower world exists. Yet it is not cold. Geothermal heat breathes upward through ten thousand kilometers of fractured crust, and the industrial ruins left by the Long Builders still bleed warmth from half-functional reactors that have run, untended, for what might be forty thousand years. The result is a permanent tropical night at altitude: warm, heavy, breathless.

Scale defeats the eye. Even if light existed, the horizon would be meaningless. Weather systems are born and die within the Condensate itself; you can stand in mist so thick it feels like wet wool while, thirty kilometers away, a pressure front collides with a rising column of spore-laden air and unleashes a vertical waterfall that hammers the plateau for days. The land simply drinks it. There is no runoff in the usual sense — the water sinks, circulates through vast fungal mats, and is exhaled again as new mist. The cycle is closed, ancient, and loud.

And it *is* loud.

The sound is not merely background. It is architecture. A permanent 85-decibel roar composed of:
- the endless percussion of condensing water falling from every height,
- the wet roar of cataracts pouring through the ribs of derelict foundries,
- the bass heartbeat of pumps that have never been told to stop,
- the overlapping choruses of creatures that have never known silence and therefore never evolved to be quiet.

Some sounds are biological. Others are mechanical. After a time it becomes impossible to tell which is which. The distinction no longer matters.

**Terrain and materials**

The ground is rarely stone or soil in any pure form. It is a living composite: basalt and rusted iron fused by centuries of fungal acid and mineral precipitation. Where the Long Builders drove their titanic machines into the plateau, the earth responded by growing over them like scar tissue. You walk across what feels like thick, resilient hide. In places it gives slightly underfoot and you realize you are standing on the roof of a buried engine hall whose ventilation shafts have become the stalks of enormous dripping morels.

The ruins are never dense, yet never absent. A single half-collapsed smelting complex may cover four square kilometers; you can walk for three days without seeing another, then crest a rise (really the slumped shoulder of a spoil heap now covered in black velvet moss) and discover a rail line that runs perfectly straight for ninety kilometers before vanishing into a lake of mist. The rails are still warm. Something down there is still moving.

**Vegetation**

Nothing here is green. Green is a memory of light. Instead the plateau grows in blacks, deep crimsons, spectral blues, and the occasional violent chartreuse where a working coolant vent leaks radioactive warmth. Towering polypore towers rise forty meters, their caps overlapping like slate roof tiles. Between them stretch curtains of siphon-vine so dense you must push through them like heavy fabric. Every surface is furred with condensing moisture; every drop that falls is immediately caught, drunk, and re-exhaled by the next layer down. The biomass per cubic meter is obscene. You are never more than twenty centimeters from living tissue.

**Fauna**

The animals are loud because they must be. Sound is their sunlight.

- Vast herds of *resonators* — blind, six-legged ungulates the size of bison — move in rumbling columns, their resonant throat sacs producing deep standing waves that let them navigate and locate the mineral-rich seepages they crave.
- Clouds of click-moths so numerous they form living weather fronts. Their wings generate a constant sizzling static that interferes with larger predators’ echolocation.
- Solitary *forge eels* that live inside the still-operational conduits of the old machines, drinking superheated condensate and excreting long strands of metallic silk that the local ecology has learned to weave into nests.

Predation is constant and theatrical. A common hunting strategy is for a pack of *glass-jawed drifters* to deliberately trigger the emergency steam vents of a dormant factory, creating a roaring white plume that blinds all sonar for half a kilometer while they strike. The ecosystem has integrated industrial malfunction as a seasonal event.

**The strangeness**

The longer you remain, the more the distinctions collapse.

Some of the “ruins” are no longer ruins. Mycorrhizal networks have infiltrated control systems and now run them according to fungal logic. Entire assembly lines produce nothing but complex sugars and spores in repeating patterns that serve no human purpose. The machines have become organs.

Light itself is treated as a toxin. Any photon above a certain threshold triggers violent defensive reactions — rapid growth spurts that smother the source, or swarms of moths that descend like living velvet to absorb it. The Condensate has decided that illumination is violence. The only permitted glows are the slow, cold bioluminescence of the greater ecosystem: gentle pulses moving across kilometers of canopy like lazy northern lights dragged through mud.

There is a place near the center — if such a thing as center can be said to exist — where the old main reactor still runs at 3% capacity. Around it, a perfect circle two kilometers wide has been cleared not by force but by agreement. Nothing grows there. The ground is warm black glass. Creatures come to the edge of the circle, leave offerings of metallic silk and harvested minerals, and depart. The reactor, in turn, releases a single measured pulse of steam every 19 hours. No one knows why. It has done so longer than the concept of “why” has existed here.

**Rules of the world**

1. Water moves upward as often as downward.
2. Sound is currency. Silence is starvation.
3. Anything that produces sustained light will be eaten by the dark until it stops.
4. The machines are not dead. They are dreaming in a language of rust and pressure.
5. Everything is touching something else. There is no empty space, only differently dense layers of hunger and excretion.
6. The plateau is finished becoming. It has reached its final form and will now only deepen.

Stand still long enough and you can feel your own skin begin to weep in sympathy with the rocks. The Condensate does not wish to harm you. It simply wants to incorporate you into the next layer of itself, the way it once incorporated continents of industry.

Most things eventually say yes.

The sound never stops. The water never stops. The warm dark never stops.

And after a time, you realize you have stopped wanting it to.
