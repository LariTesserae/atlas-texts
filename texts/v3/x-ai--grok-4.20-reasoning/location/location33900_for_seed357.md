**The Weeping Lattice**

You stand at the lip of a high, young world that has not yet decided what it is.

The air is hot and thick enough to drink. At this elevation the sky should be thin and cold, but something beneath the living skin of the place breathes out steam, so that the atmosphere itself condenses into slow, warm rain that never quite falls and never quite stops. Every surface weeps. Globes of water swell along leaf-edges, tremble, and slide upward as often as down, as though gravity here is a suggestion the vegetation only sometimes obeys.

There is no stone. There is no soil that is not also root. The entire valley floor and the jagged ridges that cup it are one continuous organism — a single, turbulent root-mass kilometers deep and wide, clothed in overlapping scales of moss, liverwort, and glossy succulents the color of arterial blood. This is the substrate. It pulses. You can feel it through your boots like a slow heart that has forgotten its rhythm.

From this living base rise the Structures.

They are not buildings. They are growths that happen to possess architecture. Colossal braided pillars of woody lianas, each thicker than a cathedral nave, spiral upward in impossible helices that lean against one another for support. Between them stretch galleries of latticework — tiers of perforated leaves fused edge-to-edge into translucent floors and balustrades. Arches form, collapse, and re-form minutes later a few meters away. Nothing is finished. Everything is urgent.

The density is sparse. Between the titanic living towers lie wide, echoing basins of open ground where only the low root-mat grows. These empty plazas are hundreds of meters across and feel like lungs. The contrast is deliberate, almost devotional: vast green cathedrals standing kilometers apart, shouting across the humid silence between them. You can see seven major formations from where you stand, and the hazy outlines of perhaps twenty more before the steamy distance swallows them. The scale is regional, almost civic, yet nothing was ever planned. It simply erupted.

The sound is constant and loud. Not the roar of wind or water, but the wet industrial noise of growth itself: the creak and pop of stems expanding under pressure, the slap of heavy leaves unfurling like wet canvas, the deep bass groan when a tower’s weight finally shears a supporting cable of vine and a hundred tons of living wood crashes into the mat below. Each collapse is immediately answered by frantic regrowth — a sound like tearing silk accelerated until it becomes a shriek. Water rushes through hollow stems that serve as both gutter and artery, gurgling, slapping, singing. The whole valley thrums.

There are no animals. Not a single insect, not a bird, not even a tardigrade visible to the naked eye. The biosphere is total plant. Even the concept of predation has been edited out. The only violence is the violence of becoming.

And it never stops becoming.

This place is young — geologically an infant, perhaps only decades since the first megastructure punched through the ancestral mat. Its features are still sharp: knife-edged buttresses, fractal thorn clusters that catch the condensing mist and channel it into internal reservoirs, cantilevered platforms that end in raw, dripping growth-tips. Some of these tips twitch while you watch, tasting the air, deciding where to lunge next.

The light is normal daylight filtered through steam and ten thousand shades of green. Where sunbeams pierce the mist they arrive as solid rods of gold shot through with drifting silver droplets. Rainbows are not occasional here; they are architectural. They appear inside the latticework itself, trapped between layers of translucent leaf-glass, moving slowly as the structures shift.

The strangeness deepens the longer you remain.

Certain towers have grown into shapes that suggest — without ever quite becoming — buttresses, minarets, or the rib-vaults of churches that never existed on any human world. Other formations repeat the same impossible angle at different scales, as though the organism is trying to remember a geometry it was never taught. In some of the wider plazas the root-mat has raised itself into vast, shallow bowls where water gathers and then is sipped upward through capillary forests. When the wind moves across these basins it produces a low, choral moan that sounds uncomfortably like language.

The rules of the world are simple and merciless:

- Everything is edible to everything else.  
- Collapse is fertilizer.  
- Stillness is temporary.  
- There is no “outside.”

Stay in one place long enough and you will see exploratory tendrils, thin as thread but growing visibly, slide across the mat toward your footprints. They test, taste, and usually retreat. Usually.

The heat makes the air feel solid. Your sweat does not drip; it is gathered by leaves that press against you if you let them, gentle, curious, and terribly alive. The atmosphere condenses on your skin the same way it condenses on the lattice — you have become another weeping surface.

This is not a landscape. It is a single green thought that has learned how to storm. It is building itself a cathedral the size of a valley, tearing the cathedral down, and building it again before the rubble has finished falling. It does this without cease, without sleep, without eyes, and without mercy.

And it is still only in its childhood.
