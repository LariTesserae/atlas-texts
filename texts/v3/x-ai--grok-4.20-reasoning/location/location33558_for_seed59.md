**The Breathing Hollow**

You step into it as if the world had folded one of its pockets inward. The place is no larger than a cathedral and its close precincts, yet it contains a entire living town suspended inside a single, hyper-saturated organism. The locals call it simply *the Hollow*, or sometimes *the Loom*.

The ground is not ground. It is a three-metre-thick mat of interlaced roots, mosses, liverworts, and the rubbery black rhizomes of a dominant species the people call *bloodvine*. Walk on it and your boots sink a finger’s depth; the surface yields, then pushes back with slow vegetable stubbornness. Every footprint fills with water within seconds. The water is everywhere—seeping, dripping, beading, condensing. It weeps from the undersides of leaves the size of blankets. It runs in braided threads along woody stems that have been trained into gutters and downspouts. It gathers in cupped bromeliads big enough to bathe a child, then spills over in perpetual silver chains. The air itself sweats. On cool days (every day is cool up here at this altitude) the mist thickens until the difference between breath and cloud disappears.

Light falls normally but is immediately captured, fractured, and slowed. Sun arrives as a kind of pale, trembling broth filtered through ten thousand layers of translucent green, russet, and wine-red foliage. Shafts appear and vanish as leaves shift; nothing is static long enough for a true shadow to settle.

The architecture is not *in* the vegetation. The vegetation *is* the architecture. Over uncounted generations the people have coaxed, braided, pruned, and hollowed the bloodvine and its companion species into tunnels, platforms, staircases, balconies, and chambers. Great living arches form the nave of the central “square”—a vertical space tall enough for three stacked storeys of walkways, all of them quivering with growth. Houses are knot-holes and hollowed stems lined with woven reed and cured moss. Doors are curtains of living tendrils that residents tie back each morning and release at night so the plant can feed. Bridges are single trained branches thick as a man’s thigh, kept supple by constant trimming. Everything is damp, everything is warm to the touch, everything is moving a little.

And the sound—God, the sound. It is loud enough that newcomers feel it as pressure on the eardrums. A constant oceanic roar composed of ten thousand layered voices: the wet clicking of ten million insect legs, the metallic ringing of tree-frogs, the wet slap of wings, the low universal creak of stems lengthening by the hour, the chattering of the human inhabitants bargaining, singing, arguing, sharpening stone adzes, hammering wooden pegs into living wood to direct new growth. Waterfalls no taller than a man produce a surprising deep bass because there are so many of them. The overall effect is that of a perpetual riot inside a greenhouse.

The animals are not visitors. They are citizens. Iridescent beetles the size of walnuts stream along designated root highways. Clouds of midges rise and fall like breathing. Bright orange frogs no bigger than thumbnails guard bromeliad cisterns and will leap onto your shoulder without hesitation. Small furred gliders—half squirrel, half bat—launch from one balcony to another, squeaking. Every surface is speckled, crawling, humming. The density is almost obscene; you cannot put a hand down without touching something alive. Yet nothing feels out of balance. The system has been here long enough (centuries at least) that every niche is claimed, every waste product immediately devoured and turned back into new growth. The erosion parameter has reached equilibrium: the Hollow is ancient and still furiously young at the same time.

The people are few—perhaps four hundred souls—but because the space is so tightly woven their presence feels like thousands. They wear clothes of felted moss and bark-cloth dyed with plant resins. Their tools are stone, bone, fire-hardened wood, and the occasional piece of meteoric iron traded from lower valleys. They speak in voices pitched to cut through the insect din. Their lives are a continuous negotiation with the Loom: prune here or it will swallow your house; encourage growth there or the bridge will collapse under its own weight. Every adult carries a curved pruning knife and a small pot of rooting hormone made from crushed grubs and fermented sap. The town is never finished. It is never the same two days running. Walls thicken overnight. New rooms appear where vines have been patiently trained for a decade. Old chambers seal themselves if neglected. The people accept this the way sailors accept the tide.

At the centre, where the light is brightest and the din somehow loudest, stands the *Heartbole*—a single colossal stem eight metres across whose interior has been hollowed into a spiralling meeting hall. Its walls pulse with slow sap. Water trickles down them in permanent tears. The floor is a living mosaic of different moss species kept cropped by a herd of hand-sized snails that the children tend like sheep. Here the town council meets, here marriages are knotted, here the annual cull of excess animal life is ritually blessed before the bodies are composted back into the Loom.

There is no edge. The Hollow simply becomes denser and darker as you move outward until the living mass becomes impenetrable. Beyond that green wall the high plateau continues, cool and windswept, but inside this one sheltered crater the conditions are perfect and the vegetation has gone completely, gloriously mad. The rules are simple and absolute:

- Nothing is allowed to stop growing.
- Nothing is allowed to stop being eaten.
- Nothing is allowed to become still.

Stand in the middle of the Heartbole at midday and you can feel the whole place respiring around you—wet, loud, cool, turbulent, ancient, newborn. A cathedral made of hunger and water and ten million beating wings. A town that is also a single green body. A place that has achieved perfect imbalance and called it home.
