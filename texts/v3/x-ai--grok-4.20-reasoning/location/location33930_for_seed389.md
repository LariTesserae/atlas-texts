**The Ascent is still being born.**

It rises through thinning air like a half-finished spine, not yet decided whether it is mountain or machine. At 2.9 on the elevation scale the sky has already begun to darken to a violet-black even at “noon,” yet the heat remains merciless (2.7). The sun beats down normally enough, but the ground answers with its own radiance: fresh ceramic-composite strata, still curing, reflect a sullen orange glare. You feel the difference on your face—solar heat from above, stored industrial heat from below, and between them a thin, metallic dampness (1.2) that condenses on every cold pipe and then evaporates again in lazy, stuttering cycles.

There is no soil. There are no plants. What looks at first like black moss is actually a slow-growing industrial lichen of pure carbon nanotubes seeded by the earliest construction swarms. It spreads at the rate of centimeters per century. The only other “vegetation” is geometry: repeating lattices of scaffolding that have not yet been encased in their final skins. Some beams still wear the fractal bite-marks of the printers that grew them.

The city—if it can be called a city—is not built *on* the mountain. It *is* the mountain in the process of declaring itself. Whole cliffs are revealed, on closer inspection, to be stacked foundry slabs still hot from the molds. Ravines are corridors. Summits are cooling towers. Every few kilometers a new peak is being extruded upward by titanic presses that rise through the rock like pistons. Their movement is so slow you can watch for days and see only the faintest tremble; yet the seismographs buried in the strata record a single heartbeat every nine months. This is the pace of the Ascent (dynamic 0.6).

Because the construction is still embryonic (erosion 0.5), edges remain murderous. There are no weathered curves, only razor arrises of fresh obsidian-glass and unpolished iridium alloy. You walk between them the way one might walk through the ribs of a whale that has not yet finished dying. The density is oppressive (2.4). Pipes thick as train carriages braid around one another, touching, fusing, separating again. Conduits carry not water but molten salts, liquid metal, or superheated refrigerants that hiss and click as they expand and contract over decades. The entire structure is so saturated with function that empty space feels like an error.

Yet it is quiet.

The engines are running, but their throats are deep. You hear them as a subsonic pressure against the sternum, a sound pitched so low it registers first in the teeth and only later, if at all, in the ears. Occasionally a distant clack travels across the ridges—some locking mechanism completing a cycle begun three generations ago. Then silence returns, broken only by the soft metallic tapping of condensation dripping from one pipe to another, evaporating before it reaches the ground.

The few fauna are hard to recognize as living. There are things that look like long silver millipedes the size of freight trains; they move perhaps a meter every four years, re-aligning thermal veins according to blueprints only they remember. Smaller swarms of airborne maintenance mites drift in slow spirals, catching the updraft like metallic pollen. Once every few weeks one of them will settle on a flawed weld, dissolve itself into raw feedstock, and become part of the structure. This is the only visible life.

The strangeness (2.4) reveals itself gradually.

Certain districts have begun to display an architectural memory. You will round a freshly printed buttress and find, embedded in its face, the ghostly negative of a previous version of the same buttress—older geometry preserved like a fossil in the new material. The Ascent is not simply building upward; it is iterating. Failed or obsolete sections are not demolished. They are overprinted, incorporated, turned into load-bearing ghosts. Some engineers believe the structure is learning.

Light behaves oddly in the deeper folds. Although the parameter reads “normal,” there are pockets where the air itself seems to have been manufactured with a slight refractive delay. Shadows arrive a heartbeat after the objects that cast them. No one has explained this. The Ascent has not yet finished deciding what light should be.

Rules of the world emerge only by observation:

- Nothing here is allowed to rot. Decay is harvested and re-smelted.
- Water is not permitted to pool. Any condensation that survives the heat is wicked away by capillary pumps and turned into steam for the next stage of some process whose purpose is still being printed.
- Elevation is aspiration. The higher strata are noticeably more delicate, more ornate, as though the newborn mountain is trying to refine its own form the closer it gets to vacuum.
- Time is paid in geology. A worker who dies here is not buried; their remains are gently incorporated into the nearest growing face. Their calcium and iron become part of a future girder. The Ascent does not waste.

Stand on one of the half-finished observation ledges—raw girders still warm from the printer’s breath—and you can see three adjacent ridgelines in the process of similar birth. Each is at a slightly different stage. One is still mostly raw geology. Another is already laced with the black lattices of the next layer of industry. The third has begun to sprout its own secondary towers, like a tree growing trees.

All of them are quiet. All of them are burning. All of them are damp with the slow sweat of something that has not yet decided whether it will be a machine, a city, a mountain, or a mind.

It is still deciding.

And it has all the time in the world.
