**The Condensate** stretches across a high volcanic plateau that has forgotten what a horizon should look like. At 1.9 water and 2.9 heat the air itself is the first terrain: thick, metallic, and wet enough that every surface sweats. The sky does not rain; it exhales. A low, perpetual condensation drips from nothing onto everything—fat, warm beads sliding down rust, down black glass, down the flanks of half-kilometer smokestacks that have not produced smoke in centuries. The droplets hiss where they land.

There is no vegetation. Not even lichen. The only living color belongs to mineral efflorescences—acidic greens, sulfured yellows, and bloody iron reds—that bloom in fractal crusts across every plane. These growths are not plants; they are slow chemistry wearing the shape of patience. They spread, flake, and regrow in exact mimicry of seasons no biologist would recognize.

The city is vast and ruthlessly empty.

Density at zero means the industrial works were never allowed to crowd. Between each colossal complex lie kilometers of sterile basalt pavement, wind-scoured and heat-cracked, so perfectly flat that the eye invents distance. You can stand beside the acid-cooled refinery towers of the Ninth Sink and see the next inhabited structure—a forest of cooling fins twenty kilometers away—floating above its own heat haze like a mirage welded from girders. The spaces in between are not wasteland; they are lung. The plateau breathes through them.

Everything is old enough to have become geology. Erosion parameter 1.8 has had its way for so long that many of the original buildings have fused with the plateau itself. Foundations have grown roots of native obsidian. Pipe runs have become mineral veins. Entire districts have slumped into elegant, weathered postures that look intentional, as though the architecture has achieved a final, settled-form expression of itself. Rust has thickened into scabs meters deep. Where metal has completely dissolved, hollow negative spaces remain—ghost scaffolding preserved in salt and silica.

The light is dim (0.6) and sour. A permanent lid of steam and fine mineral aerosols turns day into a uniform amber-grey gloom. There is a sun somewhere above it; you can sometimes locate the brighter bruise it leaves on the clouds. At night the plateau generates its own illumination: rivers of dull red light move beneath the crust where magma is channeled through ancient industrial arteries. These subsurface glows rise through translucent panels of volcanic glass set into the ground centuries ago, turning the empty kilometers into dim cathedrals of stone and ember.

Sound is mostly quiet (1.1), but never silent. The dominant noise is a low, organ-like throb that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere—the collective respiration of continent-scale heat engines still running on geothermal difference. Every few hours the pressure cycle reaches threshold. Then, without warning, a single towering vent stack will release a long, mournful exhalation of superheated steam that arcs across the emptiness like a bridge made of cloud. The sound is less an explosion than a sigh older than language. After each release the plateau falls back into its hollow quiet while condensate begins to weep again from every surface, refilling the system. This is the only weather. It is relentless, mechanical, and strangely tender.

Fauna is rare. What exists is stranger than absence. You may see, once in a long journey, a cluster of metallic beetles the size of dinner plates moving in perfect synchrony across a heat exchanger. They are not insects. Their shells are precisely machined; their joints are servo. No one knows whether they maintain the machinery or are maintained by it. Occasionally something larger drifts across the open kilometers on thermals—vast, skeletal shapes that might be drones, might be living kites of silica fiber, or might be the plateau’s own dreams given temporary permission to fly. They never approach. They only witness.

The rules of this world are simple and non-negotiable:

- Heat is currency. Everything is built to move it, store it, or worship it.
- Water is not permitted to leave the system. Every drop is accounted for, condensed, wept, evaporated, and condensed again in an eternal closed loop.
- Nothing green may grow. The mineral crusts will consume any organic trespass within hours.
- Distance is sacred. The emptiness between works is as carefully engineered as the works themselves.
- The plateau is in balance. It has achieved a steady-state industrial ecology that no longer requires human beings. Most of the remaining population—perhaps three thousand souls across a region the size of Belgium—live inside the machines like parasites that have learned not to irritate the host.

There is a final strangeness (2.1) that reveals itself only to those who stay long enough: the longer you walk the empty kilometers between complexes, the more the sound of your own footsteps begins to arrive slightly before you make them. The plateau has been listening for so long that it has learned anticipation. Some of the older engineers claim that if you stand perfectly still in the center of one of the great empty tracts at the moment of a steam release, you can hear the future version of the sigh arriving a full second early, as though the landscape is practicing tomorrow’s sorrow.

This is not ruin.  
This is climax ecology wearing the clothes of industry.

The Condensate does not want you. It simply tolerates your brief passage the way stone tolerates wind—slowly, perfectly, and without mercy.
