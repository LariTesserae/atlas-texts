**The Submerged Hollow**

You are inside a single chamber no larger than a cathedral, entirely filled with water. The ceiling arches perhaps forty meters above the floor; the walls stand eighty meters apart at their widest. Everything is cool—cold enough that the water feels silky and heavy against the skin, yet not so frigid as to bite. A clear, even, blue-white light suffuses the entire volume, strong enough to read by, yet no lamps, no cracks, no glowing organisms can be found. The light simply exists, as though the water itself has learned how to remember daylight.

The space has been flooded so long that the distinction between architecture and geology has dissolved. Great cylindrical forms rise from the silt like petrified smokestacks, their surfaces no longer metal or concrete but a single seamless skin of mineral accretion—smooth in some places, rippled in others, the way old candle wax settles. What were once girders have become pale, fluted columns veined with rust the color of dried blood. Pipes thicker than a man have fused with the ceiling and now hang as thick, irregular stalactites, their original angles softened by centuries of calcium and manganese plating. Every edge has been rounded, every joint blurred, until the industrial skeleton reads as a kind of stony cartilage.

There is almost nothing else.

Vegetation is reduced to faint, ghostly films—thin bacterial mats the color of cigarette ash—clinging only to the most sheltered hollows. They are so sparse they barely register as life. No fronds, no weeds, no color. The water is chemically poised at a point where nothing more complex than these films can persist.

No animals. Not one. No blind fish, no amphipods, no microbial clouds. The sterility is absolute and ancient. Even the smallest motes of organic matter seem to have been winnowed out long ago, leaving the water glass-clear for dozens of meters in every direction.

Sound is nearly extinct. There is a low, almost sub-audible thrum—less a noise than a pressure against the eardrums—that might be the memory of machinery still dreaming in the stone. Occasionally, once every few hours, a single mineral flake detaches from somewhere high above and falls, ticking faintly against a pipe before vanishing into the silt. That is all. The silence between these events is so complete it feels architectural.

The floor is a plain of fine, dove-gray silt, utterly level, broken only by the occasional blunt shoulder of buried machinery rising like a whale’s back. Between these shapes the open water feels enormous. The chamber is small by planetary standards, yet inside it the emptiness is vast; every object feels isolated, floating in its own cathedral of distance.

Nothing changes.

Temperature, chemistry, light, pressure—every gradient flattened to zero long ago. The water is in perfect hydrostatic equilibrium. Even if you swim vigorously, the currents you create die within seconds, swallowed by the density and stillness as though the chamber itself refuses disturbance. This is not dormancy. It is completion. The place has reached its final form and simply stopped.

There is a strange rule here, felt rather than spoken: the longer you remain, the more the boundary between your body and the water seems to soften. Not physically—your limbs do not dissolve—but perceptually. The cool, mineral-saturated water begins to feel like an extension of thought. After a time it becomes difficult to remember why anything should ever need to move again.

This is the Hollow: a small, submerged, industrial reliquary that has outlived every purpose, every worker, every war, every species that might have cared about it. What was built has been un-built by time and water until only the echo of function remains, frozen into stone and silence. It will look exactly like this a thousand years from now. Ten thousand. The equilibrium is older than history and younger than regret.

You can drift between the colossal, half-dissolved engines forever and never find an exit that matters. There is only the cool light, the silt plain, the petrified pipes, and the low, eternal note that may or may not be the last heartbeat of a machine that forgot it was ever supposed to stop.
