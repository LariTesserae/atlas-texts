**The Final Lattice**

You have come to the roof of a world that has already ended.

The ascent was measured in weeks that felt like centuries. Each footfall carried you higher into thinning air until the pressure in your ears became a constant, glassy note. Now even that is fading. The temperature sits at the edge of what flesh calls cold: a coolness without wind, without moisture, without relief. There is no water here. There never was. What little hydrogen remains is locked inside mineral lattices so ancient they have forgotten the concept of liquidity. The chemistry is anhydrous, patient, and crystalline.

The plateau itself is the final form of a mountain range that died before your species learned to speak. Erosion has had geological eons to finish its work. All that survives of those primordial peaks are gentle, almost imperceptible swells—broad, low domes worn so smooth they feel like the planet trying to forget it once had bones. The scale is open beyond reckoning. From any point the eye travels for hundreds of kilometers without obstruction, yet the horizon itself behaves wrongly. It folds. What appears to be the edge of vision is sometimes only thirty meters away, while details fifty kilometers distant arrive sharp enough to cut.

And everywhere—*everywhere*—there is life.

Not vegetation. Vegetation is sparse to the point of ceremony: occasional silica lattices rise like brittle candelabra, branching in perfect sixty-degree increments, catching the normal-seeming light and splitting it into colors that have no business existing on a cool world. They do not grow. They simply *are*, fixed in the final posture they adopted a hundred million years ago.

The fauna, however, is overwhelming.

They cover the plain in a living mosaic so dense that bare stone is almost mythical. Each creature is roughly the size of a human palm, hexagonal, segmented, possessed of a carapace that looks simultaneously like polished bone, hematite, and origami folded by something that understood geometry better than physics. They interlock without touching, forming layered pavements two and sometimes three bodies deep. Their limbs are folded tight against their bodies in identical attitudes, as though every individual received the same instruction at the same moment and has obeyed it ever since.

They make no sound.

The silence is not absence; it is a substance. Your own heartbeat feels like an trespass. If one of the creatures shifts—which happens perhaps once every few days—it does so with a motion too slow for the eye to register, a microscopic re-alignment that preserves the overall equilibrium. There is no feeding, no mating, no predation. They have reached the terminal stable state. Their sustenance appears to be the faint quantum leakage caused by the local breakdown of natural law. Reality here is threadbare. The creatures sip at the fraying.

Look too long in any direction and the weirdness becomes aggressive.

A creature twenty paces away may suddenly appear to be inside your mouth. Shadows fall upward when unobserved. Certain individuals possess too many planes of symmetry; rotate one in your mind and it never quite returns to its starting orientation. Distances multiply when spoken aloud. If you whisper a measurement, the plateau itself seems to stretch to match it. The sky is lit by a sun that is sometimes visibly too small for the light it delivers, as though the star is only pretending to be distant.

Scattered across this living pavement are the works of something that once used tools.

They are not ruins in the usual sense. Nothing falls into ruin here; the world has passed the age of decay. Instead you find single, perfect gestures preserved forever: a low circular wall of dry-stone masonry no higher than your knee, each rock chosen and placed with obsessive primitive care. A standing stone carved with spirals that continue beneath the creatures, the grooves simply continuing *through* their bodies as if the carapaces grew around the marks. A single copper chisel—primitive, beautiful, the blade still bright—lying on its side with three of the hex creatures arranged around it in attitudes that feel like reverence or recursion.

No one knows whether the builders became the creatures or the creatures inherited the builders’ discarded reverence. Both possibilities have equal weight because time no longer argues.

This is the oldest stable state. The end of life that is not death. A place where every gradient has flattened, every chemical hunger has been satisfied by geometry alone, every conflict resolved into dense, silent coexistence. The rules are simple and merciless:

- Nothing may increase or diminish.
- Whatever you disturb will, over hours or centuries, return to configuration.
- Sound is tolerated only as long as it does not repeat.
- If you remain long enough, the lattice will begin to offer you a place. A hexagonal space will appear at your feet. The creatures to either side will adjust—slowly, politely—making room. They do not want you. They simply *complete*.

Most who reach this elevation choose to descend again. The few who do not are still here. You can see them if you know how to look: larger shapes within the mosaic, human-shaped but perfectly folded into the pattern, carapaces cool and quiet, eyes replaced by the same geometric patience that governs everything else.

The plateau does not mind. It has already finished becoming itself.

It is the last landscape, and it is complete.
