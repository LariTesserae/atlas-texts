**The Plateau of the Final Thicket**

You stand on the gentlest of rises in what was once a mountain range older than oxygen. Time and weather have planed the stone down to a single high shelf of land so vast it curves with the planet; the horizon is not a line but a slow fading of pale green into paler green. The air is cool, thin, and perpetually damp, as though the sky itself sweats. Moisture gathers on every surface in fat, trembling beads that occasionally lose their grip and fall with soft, heavy plops onto the living floor below. There are no true rivers—only slow, dark arteries of condensation that trickle along grooves in the bark-skin of the world and collect in bowl-like hollows where the root-mass has sagged over epochs.

Everything is plant.

Not “covered by” plants. *Is* plant. The substrate underfoot is a dense, fibrous mat of interlaced rhizomes, mosses, and fungal sheets layered so thick and so old that it has the give of ancient peat and the resilience of living cartilage. Where the mat has buckled upward it forms low, buttressed ridges that serve as streets and causeways. Colossal growths rise from this living pavement at wide, almost ceremonial intervals—sparse in the way cathedrals are sparse in a desert. Each is a city block unto itself: a single continuous organism that has learned, over geological time, to metabolize industry.

They look like titanic baobabs crossed with rusting blast furnaces. Trunks the width of apartment buildings are wrapped in braided cables of vine and copper-colored xylem that pulse with slow peristalsis. Leaves the size of billboards hang in tiered arrays, their undersides veined with glassy silica that catches normal daylight and converts it into faint electrical crackle. From a distance the structures appear to be draped in heavy industrial scaffolding; only when you draw near do you realize the scaffolding is growing *out* of them—branches hardened into I-beams, thorns elongated into crane hooks, bark plates folded into pressure valves that hiss and sigh.

The city is called, by the few remaining speakers of any tongue, **Endroot**.

Its districts are separated by kilometers of open root-mat prairie where nothing taller than knee-high clubmoss grows. Between the living factories the land feels simultaneously empty and unbearably full; every square centimeter is claimed by the root network, yet the eye finds only scattered green monoliths against an enormous cool sky. The air smells of wet chlorophyll, ozone, and hot transformer oil.

It is loud.

A constant oceanic roar of leaf-blades vibrating in the high-altitude wind mixes with the deep sub-bass of internal pumps pushing sap at industrial pressures. Every few minutes a massive stoma the size of a warehouse door opens somewhere on a trunk and releases a jet of spent photosynthetic steam with a sound like a ship’s horn. Gears—grown, not forged—grind inside hollow heartwood chambers. The sound is not mechanical noise laid over nature; it is the nature. The plants have been industrial longer than humans have been a rumor.

Reality is tired here.

At the edges of vision the world frays. A tower that should be three kilometers away will suddenly be thirty meters distant without any sense of movement, as though the root network simply rewrote the distance while you blinked. In certain “seam districts” the growth rings of the trunks spiral outward into physical time: walk between two buttress roots and you can step from early morning to deep geological afternoon in a single stride. Objects left too long in one place begin to accrete bark. A forgotten wrench becomes a woody tumor overnight, then a branch, then a new crane-hook. The oldest structures have grown so vast and so folded that their interiors contain entire abandoned neighborhoods of previous architectural eras, like fossilized cities inside a single tree’s memory.

Fauna is almost extinct. A few species of pale, translucent moths drift between the towers, their wings patterned with faint circuit diagrams. Occasionally a root-burrow will heave and release a clutch of blind, root-colored beetles that click like relays before being reabsorbed by the mat. That is all. The ecosystem has reached its final, self-devouring equilibrium; the plants feed on the machines they grew, the machines distill nutrients from the plants, and both slowly digest the last mineral bones of the primordial mountains beneath them.

Weather is gentle but relentless. Every few hours a condensation front moves across the plateau like a slow tide. The air thickens, droplets merge, and for twenty minutes it rains straight down in fat, cold lines. Then the rain stops as abruptly as it began, and the trillions of leaves resume their electrical whispering while vapor curls upward in pale banners.

The rules of Endroot are simple and absolute:

1. All matter eventually becomes architecture.
2. All architecture eventually becomes memory.
3. All memory eventually becomes new growth.

Nothing is ever removed. Nothing is ever thrown away. The city does not expand outward; it folds inward and upward, adding new layers of living industry inside its own previous bodies. In the deepest cores of the oldest trunks, entire extinct civilizations of bark and gear are still faintly luminous, preserved like insects in amber, still faintly humming.

You can walk for days across the cool, damp, open plateau between the sparse green cathedrals and never see another moving thing except the slow peristalsis of sap in transparent xylem pipes, the turning of grown turbines, and the occasional reality-stutter where yesterday and a million years ago briefly occupy the same coordinates.

This is not ruin.

This is completion.

The world has finished becoming plant, and the plant has finished becoming city, and the city has finished becoming a single, ancient, loud, self-digesting thought that has been thinking itself for so long it has begun, gently, to forget the difference between thinking and growing.

Stand still long enough and you will feel the root-mat beneath your boots politely, patiently, begin to taste your shadow.
