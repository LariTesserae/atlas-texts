**The Lattice's Own**

At first you see only the two: the pseudopod and the clicking shard. But they are not separate tenants. They are expressions—mobile thoughts—of something larger that has not yet finished deciding what it is.

The pseudopod is older than it looks. What appears as a single translucent finger is actually a colony of several thousand heat-tolerant archaea knit together by an extracellular lattice of their own secretion, a living gel that has learned to behave like muscle. When it flows across a bright panel it is not merely feeding; it is pruning. It lifts away excess biofilm in precise sheets, thinning the living coating exactly where the crystal beneath needs maximum irradiance for its next growth spurt. The pseudopod leaves behind a faint alkaline trail that accelerates silicate deposition by three percent. The lattice, in return, keeps that particular panel three degrees hotter than the surrounding stone—an agreement written in chemistry.

Watch long enough and you will see the same pseudopod (or one indistinguishable from it) return to the same panel at almost exactly the same hour in the thermal cycle. It has begun to remember routes. The glassy ticking of the aragonite bushes quickens slightly when it passes, as though the mineral vegetation were clearing its throat in recognition.

The six-legged thing is harder to know. Its carapace is not camouflage; it is participation. The creature is a walking fragment of the pocket itself, a portion of crystal that has achieved controlled fracture and reassembly. Between the joints runs a filament of living sulfur that acts as both nervous system and hydraulic fluid. When it pauses at your boot, the tourmaline spheres that serve as eyes are not looking at you. They are reading the infrared signature of your blood through the leather, measuring whether you are a source of useful ions. If you remain still, it will sometimes taste the dust on your sole with a proboscis no thicker than a hair, then incorporate a few molecules of you into its next molt. In this way the pocket slowly learns the taste of mammals long before any mammal has evolved here.

But these are only the visible actors.

Deeper in the steam columns lives something that has no fixed body at all. It is a thermal inversion given will—an organized absence of heat that moves against the general rising current like a fish against a stream. You cannot see it directly. You see only the way the glowing mist bends around an invisible spine, the way droplets suddenly reverse direction for half a second and form perfect floating spheres before continuing their fall. The locals (if such a word can be used) call it, when they bother to name anything, the *Hinge*. It exists at the exact boundary where the pocket is still choosing between crystal and flesh. Every time it passes, a few molecules of water flash into a new crystalline habit that had never been seen before. The lattice thanks it by opening microscopic vents that feed it fresh heat from below.

Then there are the Weepers proper.

They are not the drops. They are what rides the drops.

Each scalding bead that forms on the ceiling condenses around a seed of something that is neither mineral nor organism but the unresolved tension between the two. As the drop grows heavy, this seed quickens. For the three seconds of its fall it is alive in the only way it can be: it dreams a single, perfect geometric thought. When the drop strikes a blade below and explodes into steam, the thought is released. Most dissipate. A few find purchase in a nearby aragonite bush and become the next infinitesimal layer of its branching. The rest rise on the steam and are inhaled by the Hinge, which carries them back up to the ceiling to begin the cycle again.

The sound you hear—*ploc… ploc… ploc*—is therefore not water. It is the pocket thinking out loud in droplets.

The longer you remain, the more the distinction between inhabitant and habitat collapses. The silicate insect may pause at your skin and decide the salts in your sweat are the exact catalyst it needs; it will then slowly, courteously, begin to grow a thin iridescent bridge from your wrist to the nearest crystal monolith. The pseudopod may taste the condensation on your eyelashes and leave behind a film that lets you see, for seven heartbeats, the infrared lattice-lines that connect every growing surface in the chamber like luminous neurons.

There is no apex predator here. There is only incorporation.

The pocket is still young. Its nervous system is still being written by the things that crawl and fall and rise inside it. One day the clicking shard and the flowing pseudopod and the dreaming drop-seed may fuse into something that can leave the chamber and walk upon the cooler earth. Or they may remain here forever, refining a single room until it becomes a perfect, self-sustaining, hot white thought that never needs anything external again.

For now they simply continue their patient work, ticking, dripping, fluorescing, incorporating. They do not care whether you witness them. But if you stay long enough, they will begin—gently, inevitably—to witness you.
