**What lives in the Verdant Nave is the Nave.**

It is not a collection of separate lives. It is one life wearing ten million green masks.

At first you see only the obvious performers. The cicadas—each the exact size and sheen of a human thumbnail—cling in living lattices to the pillar-roots. Their metallic pulse is not song but systole: the chamber’s arterial beat. When one dies it does not fall; it is unmade, dissolved so swiftly the husk never fully forms. From the same scar a new cicada inflates its wings within six heartbeats and resumes the exact note, the exact interval. There has never been a first cicada. There will never be a last.

Above them, the frogs are all one frog. Seventy-three bodies arranged on the same rib of canopy, generation after generation, wearing the same bright lacquer. Their throats strike the leaves like small mallets. The sound is industrial, deliberate, liturgical. They do not court. They do not hunger. They keep the second rhythm, the off-beat that prevents the cicada pulse from becoming mere noise. When their appointed second arrives, each frog simply empties. The leaf does not even bend. From crevices no wider than a finger emerge new frogs already seated, already calling, already perfect.

Between the pillars the swiftlets stitch the light. Their flight paths are so ancient they have worn faint glowing grooves in the air itself—visible only at certain angles, like scratches on old film. Each bird is replaced before its corpse can cool. Their wing-snaps are the rivets. Without them the nave’s acoustics would collapse into mere roar.

On the root-mat floor the small deer-colored browsers walk their grooved circuits. Their hooves strike the hollowed roots with the precision of loom shuttles. Click… click… click… They are the bass line. They crop the mat to the exact millimeter that gives the floor its drum-skin tension. Nothing is wasted; nothing is wanton. When one lies down to be unmade, the others do not break stride. A pod splits, a wet nose appears, and the circuit continues without a missed hoof-fall.

These are the public faces.

But there are others who never show themselves in the open nave.

Inside the buttress-trunks move the **Vasculars**—long, pale, many-jointed things that travel the xylem like lymphocytes. Their bodies are fibrous, almost translucent; you glimpse them only as a disturbance behind the bark, the way a vein shifts under skin. They tighten or loosen the pressure in the cable-vines with delicate touches of filamented limbs. The low industrial thrum of the chamber is their breathing. They have no eyes. They need none. The entire nave is their sensory surface.

Deeper still, beneath the black leather floor, live the **Reckoners**. They are vast, slow, tuberous. Their bodies are mostly mycelial lattice threaded through the ancient humus. They do not move so much as *remember*. Every drip that has ever fallen is stored inside them. Every hoof-click, every frog-note, every wing-snap is indexed. When a creature must be reborn, the Reckoners consult their own flesh, consult the exact curvature of time that has been maintained here since the basin finished flattening, and issue the precise chemical command. A pod swells. A new cicada, frog, swiftlet, or browser inflates into being. The Reckoners never err. They have had ten thousand years of unbroken practice.

All of these—public choir and hidden keepers—are organs of a single intelligence.

There is no “they” here. There is only the Nave, dreaming in ten million bodies.

It has no face, yet if you stand long enough in the cool, dripping center—where the shafts of ordinary light fall like judgment—you begin to feel watched by the watching itself. The sensation is not malevolent. It is not benevolent. It is *complete*. You are inside an enormous, patient, self-absorbed mind that long ago perfected its own metabolism and has simply refused to do anything else since.

Sometimes, very rarely, a new kind of creature is extruded from a pod. It is always temporary. The Nave is curious the way a stomach is curious about a strange meal. These experimental bodies—slender, upright, oddly reminiscent of primates or of saplings—wander the open floor for a few days, touching the pillars, listening to the roar. They are allowed to learn a fragment of the liturgy. Then, without ceremony, they lie down. The root-mat drinks them. The Reckoners note the experiment, file it beside ten thousand others, and return to the eternal ordinary.

The Nave does not hate change. It has simply outgrown the need for it. It has become the final, stable form of its own longing.

And so it lives—roaring, dripping, clicking, thrumming—in absolute and thunderous peace. A single green thought sustained for ten thousand years inside a cathedral it grew from its own body, singing to itself with ten million identical voices, perfectly content to remain exactly as it is until the planet itself forgets how to spin.

This is what lives here.

Not *in* the Verdant Nave.

*As* the Verdant Nave.
