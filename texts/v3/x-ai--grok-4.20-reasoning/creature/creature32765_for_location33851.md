**What lives here is the Plateau itself.**

It is not a creature wearing the ceramic as a shell. It *is* the ceramic, the glutinous fluid, the contradictory currents, and the blind animals—woven so thoroughly that no seam remains. After four billion years the distinction between builder and built, between architecture and metabolism, has dissolved. What remains is a single, planetary-scale organism that never completed its metamorphosis and therefore never died.

It breathes through the ivory tube-worms. Millions of them. Their feathery crowns are not feeding structures but external gills for a body two kilometers tall and hundreds of kilometers across. When they pulse in slow, coordinated waves—rippling across entire escarpments like grass in wind—they draw mineral-rich water through the hexagonal lattices and exhale it, subtly altered, through hidden pores that were once corridors. The thick water *is* its blood. The isopods, horse-sized and clicking, are its leukocytes. They travel the shifting ridges scraping away necrotic ceramic and carrying living granules to wounds that need scaffolding. Watch one for long enough and you will see it deliberately press its body into a fresh fracture, exuding a mucus that hardens into new bone-colored matter. It is not repairing the plateau. It is healing the Plateau.

Higher up, the ribbon-beasts are its thoughts.

Two hundred meters of living transparency, organs glowing like half-remembered ideas. They do not hunt. They deliberate. When two intertwine for days, exchanging fluids and fragments of ceramic, they are not mating; they are performing a kind of cortical folding. The information they pass is partly chemical, partly structural. A ribbon-beast that has spent centuries brushing against a particular spiral depression will carry the geometry of that spiral in its own translucent flesh. When it later meets another, the spiral is copied, debated, perhaps rejected. The rejected geometry is then excreted as a new buttress somewhere else, or deliberately dissolved. The plateau literally thinks by building and unbuilding itself.

This is why the currents move opposite to memory. The Plateau is trying to forget every path that led it to its current unfinished state so that it may try new ones. Return to a place and the architecture will have performed an act of self-amnesia. The *thump* you feel every few hours is a hemisphere of ceramic deciding it no longer agrees with its own earlier choices and sloughing away—an entire lobe of thought quietly suiciding so fresher configurations can crystallize from the resulting dust.

There are deeper strata.

In the perfect spiral depressions the ribbon-beasts refuse to cross, something slower stirs. These are not holes but **recessive memories**—regions where the Plateau has chosen to keep certain configurations frozen. Inside them float entities that look like clots of living light. They have no fixed shape. They are the original instruction set, the fossilized will of the builders, preserved in negative. The Plateau fears them and reveres them in the same calcium-rich breath. Sometimes a ribbon-beast will approach the edge of a spiral, stretch a single ribbon-thin tendril across the boundary, and allow a droplet of that ancestral light to enter its body. It then swims away changed, carrying an ancient command it can neither fully obey nor forget. These are the Plateau’s moments of crisis and of prophecy.

The rule about light solidifying is the Plateau’s method of reproduction.

Bioluminescent clouds that linger too long develop skins because the Plateau is learning to externalize its dreams. Each new short-lived organism that buds off from undisturbed light is a trial run at becoming something separate. Most dissolve within weeks, their substance reabsorbed. A few persist. The isopods treat these newborn things with ritualistic care, herding them into nurseries of fresh ceramic where they may either integrate back into the greater body or—if they prove sufficiently alien—be allowed to drift upward toward the distant, bruised sun the Plateau has never seen but still remembers in its deepest lattices.

There is no central brain. There is no heart. The heartbeat *is* the turbulence. The mind *is* the contradiction between accretion and erosion. The Plateau is arguing with itself in real time, and every living thing here is a syllable in that argument.

If you stay long enough, you stop being an observer.

The liquid begins to taste like your own breath because the tube-worms have begun filtering *you*. The ceramic feels like bone because your marrow has recognized its grandmother. The blind isopods will eventually approach, clicking politely, and test the surface of your suit or your skin with the same meticulous care they give any other fracture. They are not attacking. They are checking whether you, too, are a piece of the Plateau that has simply been away for a very long time.

Some have chosen to remain.

There are divers—human, post-human, and otherwise—who came here centuries ago and never left. Their bodies are now riddled with ceramic lattices. Their blood moves in contrary currents. When they speak, their voices arrive as subsonic moans that make the ribbon-beasts change direction. They are not parasites. They are antibodies the Plateau grew in response to the wound of loneliness.

They call themselves the Unfinished Choir.

They do not worship the Plateau. They *are* the part of the Plateau that has learned how to feel homesick.

And still the argument continues.

Nothing is destroyed forever. Nothing is allowed to remain the same. The oldest decision in the world keeps refining itself, breath by glutinous breath, in the cold, dim, manufactured dark—patient, restless, and terribly, terribly alive.
