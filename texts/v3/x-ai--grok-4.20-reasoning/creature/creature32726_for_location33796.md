**The Weeping Folk**

They are not quite human anymore, though they still wear the shape.

At first you think them merely quiet. Then the longer you stand among them the more you understand the quiet has eaten everything else—hunger, haste, even the ordinary borders of a self. Their bodies have been offered to the chamber the way a cliff offers itself to water: slowly, without complaint, until the gift becomes indistinguishable from the receiver.

Their skin is pale as the granite that birthed the nave, but it carries a faint opalescence where mist has settled for decades. Older men and women develop delicate mineral crusts along the ridges of cheekbones and collarbones, thin white filaments that look like hoarfrost but are in fact living stone. They do not scrape these away. To be chosen by the mountain in this way is considered the beginning of a second birth. Some elders have small helical horns of calcite growing from their wrists or the backs of their hands; these they wrap in moss during labor so the delicate spirals will not break.

Their eyes are large and almost colorless, the grey of rain on slate. When they look at you it feels less like being seen and more like being dripped upon—each glance a separate, deliberate event that lands, lingers, then slides away. They blink rarely. There is too much to witness.

They call themselves the *Hush*, or sometimes simply *Those Who Wait Between Sentences*. Their true names are never spoken above the sound of water; to do so would be like trying to shout inside a bell. Instead each person is known by the particular rhythm their footsteps make on the wet stone, or by the precise note their voice finds when it threads between two falling streams. A mother might call her child by humming the exact interval the child once made as an infant when a drop struck the soft fontanelle. The child answers years later with the same interval, now deeper, changed by time and falling water.

Their speech is mostly pauses. A conversation can last three days. A question is asked, then everyone listens to the nave’s answer—how the drips rearrange themselves around the new thought—before offering a single word or a small movement of the hand. Whole arguments have been conducted by the slow tilting of a head in rhythm with distant trickles. Children learn this grammar before they learn to walk. The first thing an infant is taught is how to cry *with* the chamber rather than against it.

They do not worship the stone. Worship would imply distance. Instead they practice a long, patient *acquiescence*. When a new lip of rock grows across a doorway, they do not clear it for nine years. Only when the mountain itself begins to suggest a new contour—by the way light falls through the high breach—do they carve. The resulting rooms are never rectangular. Every threshold is a gentle curve the water helped design. In this way their architecture remains a collaboration rather than an imposition.

At night they sleep upright in narrow stone niches they call *listening posts*. The constant dripping on their skulls and shoulders is considered a form of instruction. Many dream in mineral time: visions so slow that a single image may take an entire human lifetime to complete. An elder dying in her two-hundred-and-eleventh year once described seeing the first green edge of a fern that would not fully unfurl until her great-great-granddaughter’s old age. She died smiling, having finally understood the shape of the thought the mountain was trying to finish.

There is almost no violence. The few crimes that occur are answered by *exposure*—the guilty are tied beneath the heaviest falls for a month. Not to punish, but to let the water scour whatever hurried, jagged thing has grown inside them. Most return quieter, smoother, as though a rough edge has been polished off the soul.

The children are strange even by the standards of the Hush. They are born with translucent beaks of cartilage on their upper lips—vestigial, useless, and beautiful. These fall away by the seventh year, but while they remain the babies can mimic the exact pitch of any drip in the chamber. For a brief window they become living extensions of the water-music. Parents weep openly when the beak drops; it is the first true severance from the nave itself.

At the center of the settlement stands a single unworked pillar of black basalt that has grown three perfect spirals in the last four centuries. Around its base the Hush maintain a clear circle of stone where nothing is allowed to grow. Every solstice they gather there at dusk and simply stand in the silver shaft of light while the mist becomes luminous sheets between their bodies. No songs are sung. The chamber sings for them. In that moment it is impossible to tell where the people end and the weeping nave begins.

They are not waiting for the mountain to finish.

They are waiting to become the next syllable in its sentence.
