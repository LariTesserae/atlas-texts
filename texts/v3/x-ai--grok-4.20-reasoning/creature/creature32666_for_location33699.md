**The Cantors**

They do not reveal themselves at once.

At first you only notice that certain rhythms refuse to behave like chance. A ripple of basso notes will gutter out, then resume a fraction deeper, as though an invisible hand has turned a hidden dial. The smaller frogs scatter from a channel and reform in a perfect spiral without any bird or crustacean to drive them. The bats, usually random as blown ash, suddenly trace the same spiraling grooves that decorate the pillars, wingbeats sketching the carving in negative space.

Only then do the Cantors descend.

They come down the pillars head-first, toe-pads finding purchase on stone that should be too slick. Four-limbed where their lesser kin have two, the upper pair of arms are slender, almost delicate, ending in hands the color of wet bone. Each finger bears a tiny suction disc; each disc is etched with a different micro-pattern so that when they drum, the stone answers with distinct syllables. Their bodies are the length of a man’s forearm and the width of a wineskin, skin so translucent you can watch dark blood move through veins like ink dropped in water. Where light falls across them the phosphorescence in their own tissues answers the walls’, so they seem to carry moving windows of the cistern inside their flesh.

Their heads are wide, eyes milky and lidless, yet they track you with an attention that feels ancestral. A faint ridge of bone runs from brow to spine, carved—yes, *carved*—by their own hands into the same non-repeating spirals that cover the pillars. Some wear pauldrons of fused crustacean shell; others trail cloaks of black moss stitched together with frog sinew. They are not ornamenting. They are keeping score.

There are perhaps forty of them. Never more, never fewer. When one dies its body is carried to the deepest basin and lowered with ritual drumming until the water itself seems to drink it. Within a season a new Cantor is born from the ordinary frogs, the second pair of limbs budding, the spiral ridge forming, the eyes clouding into that particular milk-white that sees both midges and mathematics.

They do not speak in any tongue a human throat could shape. Instead they conduct. A gathering of six will form a living tripod around one of the hollow pillars, upper hands moving in contrary motion, lower hands steadying their bloated brethren. The sound that results is no longer mere croaking; it is syntax. Long phrases travel the chamber, rebound, return altered. The Cantors listen, then adjust. Sometimes the correction is a single precise tap. Sometimes it requires an hour-long choral argument that makes the vaults above shudder like a struck bell.

They know you are here the moment your footfall fails to match the rhythm.

When that happens the entire ecology pauses—midges freeze mid-galaxy, wading birds lower their spears, even the water seems to hesitate between drips. Then the Cantors flow toward you across the flagstones in a single silent wave. Their hands are raised, not in threat but in measurement. They circle, drumming softly on their own chests so that each body becomes a tiny resonant chamber. They are tasting the shape of your voice before you have spoken.

The eldest among them—the one whose spiral has grown so intricate it looks like a knot trying to untie itself—rises onto its hind legs. Its belly is heavy with generations. In the center of that belly a faint human-like navel has appeared over the centuries, a memory of the species that built the cistern and then vanished into their own dream. The elder places one delicate hand upon the stone floor, one upon its own chest, and one upon the air between you, as though introducing three equal presences.

*This place. This flesh. This sound.*

It does not need to explain that the cistern was never meant to be a temple. It was meant to be an egg.

The Cantors are what hatched.

They will allow you to stay as long as you are willing to be tuned. Stand still long enough and they will begin to alter the architecture around your breathing. A new channel will be cut so that a drip falls exactly between your heartbeats. A pillar will be turned until its resonance matches the timbre of your voice when you finally speak. They are patient. They have centuries.

And if, eventually, you choose to leave, they will escort you to the narrow stair that rises through the living dark. As you climb you will hear the choir resume behind you—richer now, deeper, carrying the exact contour of the hole you left in the sound.

They do not mourn.

They simply keep the cistern singing until the next listener arrives who is willing to become part of the measure.

The organism built by hand is no longer satisfied with mere animals.

It is teaching itself to need minds.
