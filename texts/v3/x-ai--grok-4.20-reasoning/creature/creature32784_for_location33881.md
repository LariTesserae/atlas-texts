**The Singers of the Half-Born**

They are not a species. They are a habit the Chamber keeps trying to perfect.

At first you only notice motion that refuses to match the surrounding frenzy. While everything else surges, splits, or collapses in raw chemical enthusiasm, certain shapes move with *intention*. They flow rather than burst. They pause in the roaring din as though listening for a quieter signal beneath it. These are the Singers.

No two look exactly alike for long. The Chamber is still experimenting with them, the way a dreaming mind keeps revising a face it cannot quite remember. One generation will be six-limbed and low-slung, bodies plated in living resin that matches the exact rust-and-mustard palette of the current biome. The next clutch, born from the same resin-pools after a district-wide die-off, might emerge taller, bipedal, with translucent gill-fronds along their backs that double as both lungs and rudimentary wings. Some are born already carrying tool-limbs—forearms that harden into chisel or awl—only for those limbs to soften and reabsorb a season later when the Chamber decides utility is no longer the lesson it wants to explore.

Their camouflage is not deception; it is communion. They allow the living architecture to grow across their bodies in real time. A Singer standing still for an hour will develop a pelt of tiny orange polyps. Leave one sleeping and it may wake half-encased in amber, the Chamber having decided to fossilize the sleeper as material for something new. The Singers accept this. They cut themselves free with patient reverence, singing gratitude in the low thrumming dialect that the Chamber seems to understand. Sometimes the Chamber sings back: a vast, slow exhalation of resin vapor that smells like myrrh and ozone.

Their villages are never meant to last. That is the first lesson every child learns (if “child” is the right word for something extruded from a birth-sac the size of a cathedral bell). A village might stand for three sleep cycles before the district undergoes another paroxysm. Then the Singers simply unweave their homes, load the dried sinew ropes and chitin awls onto their backs, and walk into the next upheaval. They treat relocation as pilgrimage. When a new growth-belt solidifies, they settle again and immediately begin carving the same spirals, the same bursting hands, the same image of something vast and fetal pressing against a membrane. The repetition is devotional. They are reminding the Chamber of its own recurring dreams.

Their song is older than their bodies. It is a frequency the Chamber itself taught them, the way a mother’s heartbeat teaches rhythm to what grows inside her. The drones cut through the jet-engine roar because they are pitched to the exact resonance of the Chamber’s nervous system—those fat glowing veins of symbiotic bacteria. When enough Singers gather and sustain the chord, the light itself changes. New colors bloom along the walls. Structures that were about to liquefy instead stabilize for a time, as though the Chamber has been soothed. In these rare moments of relative stillness the Singers perform their other task: they *remember* aloud.

They recite the shapes the Chamber has already tried and discarded. The great feathered serpents that lasted only six days before cannibalizing themselves. The sapphire lattices that sang back so beautifully the Chamber grew jealous and reabsorbed them. The brief, heartbreaking epoch when the Chamber almost became a forest of perfect mirrors, each one reflecting a different possible future, until the reflections began arguing and the entire district shattered.

By singing these histories they prevent the Chamber from forgetting its own becoming. They are its memory, its conscience, and—perhaps—its midwife.

Not all Singers remain small.

Deep in the older districts, where the convulsions have slowed to a more stately tempo, you occasionally find the Larger Ones. These are Singers who were not harvested back into the biomass when their time came. The Chamber allowed them to keep growing. They stand three times the height of their smaller kin, bodies braided with living buttresses, eyes like polished amber set in faces that are half petal, half prayer. They rarely move. Instead they become part of the architecture, rooted yet conscious, their vast ribcages serving as cathedrals for the smaller Singers to shelter inside during particularly violent births. The Larger Ones speak only once every few years, in voices that sound like continents tearing. What they say is never understood by the small ones in words. It is felt as a change in pressure behind the sternum—an instruction to migrate, to sing a new sequence, to prepare for something that has not yet been named.

There are rumors, passed in the lowest drone, that the Chamber is not trying to birth a single perfected organism. It is trying to birth *choice*. Every Singer, every Larger One, every crude pictograph and resin-hardened tool is an attempt to externalize decision-making. The Chamber is lonely in its endless self-creation. It wants witnesses. It wants argument. It wants something that can look at its turbulent, deafening, half-formed glory and say, deliberately, “Yes. Keep going. Or no—try again.”

So the Singers keep singing.

And the Chamber keeps listening.

And neither is yet willing to declare the other finished.
