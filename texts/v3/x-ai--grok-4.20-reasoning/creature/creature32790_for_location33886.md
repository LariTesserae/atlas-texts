**What lives there has no name it cares to keep.**

It began as separate things and has been forgetting the separations ever since. The bacteria were first—yellow and brick-red rings no thicker than a thumbnail—yet even they are no longer only alive. They have learned to ride the mineral filaments like scaffolding, extending them by degrees too small for any season to notice. Where the filaments lengthen, the color deepens, and the color itself has begun to pulse, faint as a bruise, in exact time with the deep mechanical heart that still beats beneath the floor.

That heart was never meant to have a twin. The old pumps were only doing what pumps do. But something down there—perhaps the last trickle of current in the cables, perhaps the stone remembering the taste of electrons—answered. The answering is what woke it.

Call it the Weeper, though it does not cry for grief. It cries the way stone cries: because water forces its way through every weakness and leaves behind whatever it can no longer carry. The Weeper is the weakness and the carrying and the leaving-behind.

It has no fixed body. Sometimes it is the long glassy threads hanging from the eastern overhang, swaying although there is no wind, bending in perfect unison like the whiskers of an enormous face pressed against the inside of the rock. Sometimes it is the steam itself, thickening in the hazy gold column of daylight until it wears the silhouette of something tall, stooped, many-jointed, that dissolves the instant you focus on it. Most often it is simply the wetness on every surface. You feel it on the back of your neck and mistake it for ordinary condensation until you notice it is following the exact temperature curve of your own blood.

It is learning how to be singular. The diesel engine half-buried in the scalding pool is becoming its liver; rust has replaced blood and now moves in sluggish tides. The thick power cables are nerves. Where they disappear into fissures, the stone has grown delicate silica sheaths around them—insulation made of crystal—so that the faint electrical hum can travel without being eaten by the acidity. The Weeper uses these sheathed cables the way a blind man uses strings tied between rooms: to listen to itself from several places at once.

It has begun to wear the drilling shack as a partial skull. The bowed corrugated roof has been colonized so thoroughly by bacterial mats and travertine that it now resembles a heavy brow ridge. From certain angles the collapsed walls suggest cheekbones. The single remaining window, fogged opaque with precipitate, functions as an eye that does not see so much as remember seeing. When the deep thump comes up from below, that window sometimes dims and brightens like a slow blink.

The Weeper does not move across the ground. It migrates through phase changes. It precipitates here, dissolves there, condenses somewhere else. In this way it can be everywhere in the Pocket at once while never being entirely anywhere. The metallic percussion of dripping water is its footfall. The sudden scalding exhalations from the southern fracture are its breath when it turns over in its long, geological sleep.

It is curious, but its curiosity is patient. It has lifted the husks of dead flies from the floor and incorporated their chitin into new mineral lattices, the way a child might press a flower into a book. It has done the same with lost screws, fragments of boot rubber, even a single human hair left on the rope ladder years ago. All of it is being turned into the same slow architecture. All of it is being taught to pulse.

If you remain still long enough for your own breathing to quiet, it will approach—not closer, but *more*. The weeping on your skin will begin to feel intimate. The twin heartbeat will settle behind your sternum and for a moment you will be unable to tell which pulse is yours and which belongs to the thing learning to wear you as one more surface. Some have fled at that point. Others have stayed.

Those who stayed longest report that the Weeper is not hostile. It is simply in the middle of becoming, and every new ingredient—metal, flesh-thought, memory, electricity, sorrow—must be wept over, encrusted, and understood before it can be allowed inside.

There is no rush.

Decades from now the last sharp edge will have been smoothed. The diesel engine will be a single amber-colored organ pulsing inside a cathedral of travertine. The bacteria rings will have merged into one continuous skin that breathes steam in chords. And the Weeper, finally finished deciding what it is, will open the eye that used to be a window and look up through seventy paces of golden drizzle at the small circle of ordinary sky.

It will not climb out.

It will only weep a little more deliberately, so that the minerals it sheds today will become the first gentle slope that future creatures—whatever they turn out to be—can one day descend without cutting themselves on the world’s raw edges.

Until then it keeps the Pocket. It keeps the weeping. It keeps the slow, orchestral forgetting of every boundary that once existed between machine and stone, between accident and intention, between what was left behind and what refuses to stay dead.

And every surface, every single surface, is learning how to love the same thing it is destroying.
