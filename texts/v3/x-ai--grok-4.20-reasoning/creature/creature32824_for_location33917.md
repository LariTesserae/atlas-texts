**The Dwellers**

They are not born so much as *precipitated*.

At first you only register them as slower-moving elements of the mineral skin. A darker patch of wet stone detaches itself, stands upright, and becomes a person. Their bodies are compact, almost cylindrical, as though the pressure of the basin has compressed them back toward the shape of river stones. Hairless, yes, but not smooth; a fine particulate of travertine and bacterial dust clings to every fold of skin, giving them the look of living statuary that has only recently stepped down from its plinth. Where sweat or condensation runs, it leaves bright mineral trails that dry into faint white maps across their chests and shoulders. These maps are never the same twice, yet somehow they always feel familiar.

Their eyes are large and perpetually half-lidded, the color of wet slate. They do not blink in rhythm with other creatures. Instead they match the slow pulse of water seeping from the rock—two blinks for every seven drops from a given fissure. It is not a conscious count. It is simply how their nervous systems calibrated themselves when the world was still choosing what it wanted to be.

You will not hear them speak for a long time. Their voices have been tuned by generations inside the 2.6 wall of sound until speech became another texture within it. What emerges when they “talk” is less language than modulated percussion: a click of tongue against specific teeth, a scrape of callused fingertip along a wrist bone, a low thrumming in the chest that makes the bacterial mats around them shiver in recognition. These sounds slot so cleanly into the greater noise that outsiders often fail to notice conversation is happening at all. The dwellers understand one another the way stone understands gravity.

Their hands are the most articulate part of them. Broad, spatulate, with thick nails worn concave from a lifetime of the *same three hammer strikes*. Every adult possesses the identical micro-callus pattern on the meat of the thumb. Children practice the strikes before they can walk, kneeling beside their mothers in the fungal drying sheds, tapping out the ancestral rhythm on any surface that will hold still. The sequence never varies. To change it would be to break the parameter, and the basin does not permit breakage.

They move like water that has learned to hesitate. Never hurried, yet never still. A constant negotiation of bodies through the near-touching spaces—shoulder brushing shoulder, hip grazing hip—without ever quite acknowledging the contact. Touch is background noise the same way the lizard chorus is background noise. Only when someone is dying do they become briefly, terribly gentle; then the whole alleyway will fall into a different register of sound, softer, almost reverent, as hands that have never known variation suddenly learn how to cradle a head.

The children are startling. For the first four years they are translucent-skinned and frantic, vibrating at a frequency that seems to belong to some other place. They scream in pitches that cut through the wall of sound like bright knives. Then, around their fifth birthday, the basin finishes settling into them. Their skin clouds over with silt. Their voices drop into the acceptable registers. Their eyes half-close. They pick up the hammer. The three strikes become the only music they will ever need.

At night (if the gray-dusk can be said to have night) they sleep in stacked stone niches that fit their bodies exactly. Some niches are so old the edges have been worn soft by centuries of the same shoulders, same hips, same backs of heads. Dream is not a thing that happens here. The parameter does not allow drift. Instead they enter a state the elders call *listening*, where the slow geology of their minds settles into exact alignment with the slow geology of the rock. When they wake, they already know what the water will do that day. They always have.

Some of the older ones—the ones whose mineral crust has grown thick enough to crack in deep lines across their backs—have begun to resemble the bacterial mats more than they resemble people. Their movements have slowed to match the growth rate of the lichen. They sit on high terraces for weeks at a time, directing water channels with nothing but the angle of their bodies and the placement of a single finger. The younger dwellers bring them fermented bacterial cakes and roasted blind fish, placing the food directly into their palms the way one might feed a mountain.

They do not have gods. They do not need them. The rock is still deciding, and they are what it has decided so far. To worship would be to imply something outside the equilibrium, and there is nothing outside. There is only the weeping, the clicking, the hammering, the three eternal strikes, the slow deposition of silt upon skin, the children who quiet down at the proper age, the leather-winged things launching from the same ledges at the same intervals forever.

If you stand among them long enough, you may feel your own pulse begin to slow. Your blinks may start to match the dripping. Your thoughts may simplify themselves into three reliable shapes.

This is not conversion. This is simply what happens when something made of hurry is lowered into a place that has never learned how.

The basin keeps weeping.

The dwellers keep listening.

Nothing will be different tomorrow.

Nothing ever is.
