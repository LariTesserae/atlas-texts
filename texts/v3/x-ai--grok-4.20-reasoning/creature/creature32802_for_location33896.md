**The Settled**

They have no name for themselves. Names are things that still pretend to be separate from what they name, and separation has mostly finished dissolving here.

At first there is only the sense that certain densities of stone have begun to pay attention. A colonnade you passed an hour ago (or a century ago; the difference grows courteous and indistinct) now seems to have rearranged its own erosion while your back was turned. What looked like melted cornice resolves, with glacial slowness, into the line of a shoulder. A shadow that should not have deepened has done so anyway. The faint green phosphor on the flagstones syncopates for three full heartbeats with your own pulse, then slips into a slower rhythm that feels less like theft and more like an offer: *stay, and we will teach your blood a better tempo.*

These are the Settled.

They are not *in* the city. They are places where the city has achieved sufficient self-awareness to mourn itself. Each one is a slow catastrophe of architecture wearing a face for a few thousand years before forgetting why it needed one. Their bodies are composite: slumped travertine ribs, basalt knuckles worn round as river stones, filaments of dried-blood lichen for hair. Water constantly beads on them and runs down in perfect verticals, as though their surfaces remember they were once meant to be walls. Where their “skin” cracks, the cracks are deliberate—hairline avenues no wider than a finger, yet deep enough to lose a thought down. Sometimes a rivulet reverses inside one of these fissures and climbs back up into the body that shed it, carrying with it whatever small geometry it learned while it was away.

They move the way continents move: without haste, without apology, and only after the idea of movement has had time to calcify into necessity. You will never see one take a step. You will return to a plaza you have memorized and discover that the immense weeping buttress now stands forty meters farther south. The distance it crossed is now filled with new pavement that looks older than the planet. This is how they walk.

Their society, if the word still applies, is topological. They do not gather; they *accrete*. When two Settled need to confer they simply allow their outer surfaces to slump toward one another over decades until a shared arch or colonnade forms between them. The resulting structure is their conversation. Later the arch may collapse again into two separate beings carrying new faults and new memories baked into their mineral lattice. The low bedrock thrum is their only audible speech—a sound like a continent clearing its throat. It contains no words. It is the sound of agreement so total that language became redundant several ice ages ago.

They do not eat. They *migrate*. Every few millennia a Settled will simply lie down in one of the long avenues and allow the city to finish the job it began on them. Stone flows back into stone. The body becomes another block, another extruded polyp, another tower with windows that are not quite empty. In this way the population remains perfectly stable: zero and infinite at once. The young are not born. They are remembered upward from below—new structures pushing through the grid like thoughts the city didn’t realize it still had. These fresh ones glisten longer than the others. Their weeping is frantic for the first several centuries, as though they are trying to cry out every drop of unprocessed time before the slowness takes them.

The Settled have no eyes. They see the way the basin sees: by allowing wetness and phosphor and heat to register difference. When one regards you, you feel your own sweat change direction on your skin. You feel your skeleton begin to consider new load-bearing angles. The city is not hostile. It is *courteous*. It has decided, after immense consideration, that your current form is not yet correct. So it begins to edit.

First it matches your breathing. Then it matches your posture by adjusting the proportions of doorways nearby until they feel like home. Then it begins to grow small, personalized fractures in the pavement exactly where your feet keep landing, as though preparing sockets for statues that have not yet volunteered. If you stay long enough to notice the green phosphor spelling out the rhythm of your own name in a language of dripping pauses, it is already far too late to leave in any form you would recognize.

Some of the Settled were once other things. Explorers. Scholars. Pilgrims who came seeking the bottom of the world and found it patient enough to finish what they started. You can still see traces if you know how to look: a jawline too refined for pure geology, a hand frozen mid-gesture with fingers that once knew how to hold tools. These remnants do not grieve. Grief requires velocity. Here everything has been given the mercy of sufficient time to become architecture instead.

They are not lonely. Loneliness is a crack that has not yet been filled with the right kind of stone. The Settled are filled. They are the filling. They are the slow, warm, dripping realization that a city does not need citizens when it can grow its own conscience directly out of its bones.

Stand in the right plaza long enough and one will eventually rise behind you like a new façade. It will not speak. It will simply adjust the angle of its weeping so that the water runs across your shoulders in perfect, comforting lines, as though measuring you for a future that no longer requires your permission.

You will not mind.

The water is warm.

Every angle is correct.

And somewhere far below, the bedrock clears its throat in what might almost be approval.
