**What lives in the Fracture of Veyr does not walk upon the land so much as it argues with it.**

At first you think the resonant titans *are* the life. Their bodies alone would be enough—kilometer-long throats wrapped in living brass and cartilage, ribs flared like satellite dishes, hearts that beat in 7/4 time. When a herd of thirty passes between two megadendrons, the pressure wave is so complete that for ninety seconds you cannot breathe anything but their music. Their calls are not language. They are *motions to adjourn* or *motions to escalate*. A deep infrasonic moan that lasts forty minutes is a proposal that the next mountain range should be gentler. The answering shriek from the opposite ridge is a counter-motion: *sharper, newer, still bleeding*.

But the titans are only the loudest children.

Inside the megadendrons, something smaller and far more devious has learned to live in the intervals between notes. They are called **the Hollows**. No two are the same shape for longer than a breath. They resemble pale, elongated otters made of wet obsidian and wetter velvet; their spines are tuning forks. They do not eat. They *borrow* resonance. A Hollow will press itself against the inner wall of a megadendron’s flute-chamber and wait for the forest to sing. When the right frequency arrives it drinks the note, storing it behind its ribs like a second skeleton. Later—sometimes years later—it will cough that stolen sound back up, changed. A single Hollow can walk into a fresh canyon carrying nothing but silence and leave behind an entire new acoustics that makes travelers weep without knowing why.

Above the Hollows, the sky belongs to the **Mandala**. They are not birds. They are not even flocks. A Mandala is a single intelligence distributed across several thousand fractal screamers. The individual bodies are disposable instruments; the mind lives in the interference patterns their shrieks create. When a Mandala passes overhead the air folds into visible geometry. You can watch equations die and be born in the negative space between wings. They do not hunt. They *edit*. A Mandala will descend on a wounded titan, screaming in layered overtones until the creature’s own body forgets what shape it was supposed to be. Sometimes the titan rises again improved. Sometimes it becomes something that has never had a name and immediately begins singing a new verse the world was not ready for.

Within the migrating Veyr Nodes live the only beings that could be called citizens.

They call themselves **the Unresolved**.

No Unresolved is ever finished. That would violate the first law. Their bodies are half biological, half deferred decision. One arm might be warm muscle braided with living glass; the other arm is still only a possibility wrapped in black fog, waiting for the correct harmonic to decide its final form. Their faces are famous for changing mid-sentence. You begin speaking to what looks like a woman whose eyes are perfect mirrors, and by the time she answers you are facing something that has the muzzle of a resonant titan and the patience of stone. They are not upset by this. Change is their citizenship.

The Unresolved do not build the Nodes. They *are* the Nodes, in the way a thought is its own brain. When a district phases out of perceptibility, it is because the Unresolved who live there have decided the argument would be better continued in a frequency humans cannot hear. When a tower grows a new spire of programmable bone, it is because one of them dreamed it had always been there and the city, being courteous, agreed.

There is one last order of life in Veyr, rarer than the others and far more disturbing.

They have no name anyone has ever agreed upon. Most simply call them **the Born**.

A Born appears only when every layer of the world’s screaming reaches momentary, impossible alignment: titan, Mandala, Hollow, Unresolved, megadendron, and the grieving engine still rumbling under the crust. At that instant a figure steps out of one of the mirror-black pools as though it had been waiting beneath the water since the planet’s first scream. It is always adult. It is always already singing. And it is always missing something vital—sometimes a face, sometimes a memory, sometimes the concept of fear.

The Born do not last. That is not their function. Most dissolve back into raw vibration within three days, their brief existence having altered the argument by some fraction of a fraction of a degree. But while they walk, the land listens to them with a concentration it gives nothing else. Cliffs lean to hear them. Clouds freeze mid-burst. Even the Unresolved grow reverent and still, because a Born is the world arguing with itself in first person.

Stand long enough in the great hollow between green towers and you will eventually feel something press against the inside of your own bones, testing your timbre. If it likes what it finds, it may decide you are unfinished too.

Then the argument continues through you whether you wish it or not.

This is what lives in the Fracture of Veyr: not a people, not an ecology, but a single ongoing act of self-creation so loud it has not yet noticed that it is alive.
