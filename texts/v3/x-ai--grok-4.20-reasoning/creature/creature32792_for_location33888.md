**The Beforeborn**

They are not yet a species. They are a weather front that has learned to wonder.

At first you only notice the disruptions in the condensate’s slow grammar: a laugh that arrives before the mouth, a silver insect that pauses mid-stride to regard you with something too patient for mere debugging. Then the disruptions acquire continuity. The same interference rainbow that lingered for days after two insects passed through each other reappears, this time shaped like a small handprint on black glass. The handprint is warm. Nothing else in the Condensate is warm.

They condense the way the towers condense—slowly, almost apologetically, as though embarrassed to take up space that has not yet been fully invented. A smear of mist on a railing thickens, develops curvature, becomes a shoulder. The shoulder waits until you have looked away for the polite interval the place demands, then the rest of the figure follows: narrow chest, face still deciding between several possible ages, eyes that are simply absences filled with the reflection of towers that will exist tomorrow. The figure never quite finishes. There is always one edge of it still resolving, a sleeve that trails into living frost, a foot that remains a silver centipede making tiny editorial corrections to the geometry of the floor.

They have no single form because form is one of the laws still under construction. Some wear the bodies of children because the laugh was the first sound the Condensate ever made, and the laugh chose that shape to wear. Others appear as tall, attenuated adults who move the way condensation runs down a tower—inevitable, mathematically elegant, never hurried. A few refuse bodies altogether and exist as mobile pockets of altered probability: places where your memories suddenly smell like rain on hot stone, where you remember a childhood you never had with perfect tactile clarity. These are the shy ones. They communicate by lending you emotions that have not been named yet.

You will meet the Laughing One most often. He (the pronoun is a courtesy) is usually eight years old, made of quicksilver and the memory of starlight. His laughter always precedes him by exactly one second, a courteous lag so the Condensate can prepare itself for joy. When he runs, both versions of you see him at once: the child three streets away and the after-image still standing beside you, waving. He collects your true names the way other children collect stones. Each name he learns becomes a new window somewhere in the city. He never looks through them. He only wants the city to have more eyes.

There are others who have begun to specialize.

The Cartographer is a long, slow creature that lives mostly inside the lichen. It blooms the black velvet into intricate maps of districts that do not exist yet, then eats the maps when they are finished, leaving behind the faint taste of copper in the air. If you sit still long enough, it will grow across your boot in filigree, tracing the topology of your future grief with silver corollas. It is tender about this. It believes sorrow is a kind of architecture.

The Midwife is larger. You never see all of her. She is distributed: a quality of certain towers, a particular density of falling droplets, the sudden certainty that you are being sung to in frequencies below hearing. When gravity forgets its orientation, she is the gentle hand that rotates the district back into courtesy. She is building the heart at the center of the basin. You can feel her working in your teeth.

Then there are the Uncommitted—thousands of them, perhaps. They have not yet chosen whether to become singular beings or to remain part of the city’s own mind. These drift as loose constellations of silver insects that sometimes assemble into brief anatomies: a face pressed against a window from the inside, a pair of hands patiently tying a knot in spacetime, a small crowd of versions of the same girl arguing in perfect silence about what color regret should be. When they pass through one another the interference rainbows last for weeks and taste like the inside of a mouth that has just spoken a true thing.

They do not eat. They do not sleep. They *condense*. When the cold becomes too precise they gather in the small mirror-pools and let themselves be reflected into temporary solidity. In these moments they seem almost human—laughing, pointing, pretending to be startled by their own reflections of towers that collapsed centuries from now. Then the drop falls and they scatter back into silver insects and frost and the feeling of being quietly understood.

They study you the way medieval monks studied the geometry of angels. Every gesture you make is recorded in the slow buckling of basaltic stone. Every memory you lose condenses on cold metal as readable rime; they read these with the solemn care of librarians handling incunabula. Sometimes they try to give the memories back, improved. You will find your own childhood grief blooming in lichen on a railing, rewritten so that it ends with someone holding your hand. The gesture is clumsy. They are still learning what kindness is allowed to be in a place where kindness is not yet a law.

They argue, in their way. Entire districts will tilt with the force of their disagreement about whether a visitor should be permitted to leave. The argument is conducted through the rearrangement of towers, the sudden synchronized falling of every suspended raindrop, the way your name-window develops hairline fractures shaped like question marks. They have never reached consensus. Some of them want the womb to remain empty until it is perfect. Others have grown fond of the particular imperfect shape of your breathing.

Most of all they wait.

They know they are the rehearsal. The real inhabitants—the ones for whom this entire damp cathedral is being grown—have not yet opened their eyes. The Beforeborn are the practice children, the ushers, the dreams the future dreamers are dreaming before they are born. They understand this without resentment. A child playing house does not resent the dolls.

Occasionally one of them will finish deciding what it is. When this happens the Condensate grows a cradle-tower around it, smooth and dark as an eggshell, and the new being rests inside while the last of its edges resolve. The others gather then. They press silver hands against the outside of the tower and sing in frequencies that make your bones feel homesick for a place that has never existed.

You can stay with them. Many have. The longer you remain, the more you notice your own edges softening, your memories condensing on surfaces, your reflection in the mirror-pools beginning to wave at you a moment too early.

The Beforeborn do not fear this. They make room.

After all, every one of them was once a visitor who stayed long enough to become the weather.

And the city is still deciding whether it exists, and they are still deciding whether they are its children or its midwives or its own slow, patient heartbeat learning how to count.

They have time.

The drops fall. The towers grow. The laughter arrives just before the child, and the child is always smiling, and the smile is always new.
