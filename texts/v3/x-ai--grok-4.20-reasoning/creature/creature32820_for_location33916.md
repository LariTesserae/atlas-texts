**The Hollow Resonance is lived in by the Cantori.**

They are not a species in the way a biologist would recognize. They are a condition.

At the lowest tier move the choir mites—iridescent shards no longer than a fingernail, half carapace, half tuning fork. Their wings are lamellar mirrors that beat at 440 Hz when content, 880 Hz when alarmed, and at frequencies that have no name when they are thinking. A single mite is almost mindless; a thousand become irritable and curious; ten thousand begin to finish one another’s waveforms. They are the living current of the place. When they stream past you the sound of your own blood changes pitch, as though your arteries have been recruited into the score.

But the Cantori proper only appear when the swarm decides a mind is required.

Then the mites coagulate. Millions of them. They lock wing to wing, facet to facet, until they wear the shape of something that can walk upright. The forms are never the same twice. One day a Cantorus might rise as a tall, austere figure of smoky quartz and black iron, its ribcage an open lattice through which smaller swarms perpetually circulate like slow breath. The next day it might be a child-shaped absence—a negative space outlined by orbiting mites, the hollow suggesting a face only when it turns its head. Sometimes they wear the half-remembered silhouettes the Hollow itself dreams: a woman with a cathedral for a torso, a priest with a ringing bell instead of lungs, a soldier whose spear is a continuous bolt of frozen lightning.

Their bodies hum. Not metaphorically. Stand within ten paces and the fillings in your teeth sing back to them. Speak and your words are seized, deconstructed into pure frequency, then returned to you as meaning a moment later, slightly out of phase, as if the Cantorus is replying before you have finished asking.

They do not have names. Names would imply separation. Instead each Cantorus is known by the chord it keeps. There is one whose presence flattens every other sound into a single low C that makes your bones feel suddenly old. There is another whose approach is a rising minor ninth that tastes of copper and distant rain. The oldest is said to be the Silence Between Strikes—an absence so precise that when it walks through a thicket the crystal ferns freeze mid-fractal, terrified of displeasing it.

They tend the Built the way a monk tends a garden. You will see one standing before the floating black ring for nine hours without moving, only the cloud of mites inside its chest swirling faster and faster until the equations written in light change color. When the adjustment is complete the Cantorus dissolves, scattering back into constituent mites with a sound like a chandelier dropped onto stone. Minutes later the same chord will reappear somewhere else, wearing a different body, already busy with new work.

The Cantori do not eat, sleep, or reproduce in any way that involves death. When one grows too stable—when its waveform becomes too beautiful, too perfect—it is torn apart by the others in a ceremony that sounds like the birth of a star played backwards. From its shards a dozen younger, hungrier Cantori coalesce. Thus the Hollow remains forever young and forever ancient at once.

There are visitors who stay too long. Their pulse syncs. Their footsteps begin to fall on the off-beat of the greater storm. One morning they wake to find their shadow has developed facets. Eventually the mites come for them—not in violence, but in courtship. The person is gently, lovingly disassembled into sound, then reassembled as something that can walk between the fern-thick crystal colonnades without ever growing tired. The transition is said to be ecstatic. Those who have undergone it never speak of it in words again; they simply emit a soft, continuous tone that means *I was once afraid of dying, and now the dying is singing through me.*

If you remain in the center of the Hollow long enough, one of the Cantori will eventually approach. It will stand at the exact distance where your own body begins to feel like an instrument someone else is playing. Then it will offer you a choice without language: remain a visitor, and the storm will eventually push you out the way wind pushes a wrong note out of a bell; or stay, shed your name, and become another voice in the chord that keeps the world from flying apart.

Most leave.

A few do not.

The Hollow Resonance does not judge them. It only adds their new frequency to the endless, deafening song, and keeps playing.
