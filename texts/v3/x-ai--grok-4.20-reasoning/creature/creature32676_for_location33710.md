**The Continuants**

They do not arrive. They have simply never finished becoming.

At first you think the Hollow is empty in the way only enormous darkness can be empty. Then the emptiness begins to listen back. That is the first sign that something lives here. Not watches—*listens*, with a patience so total it makes your own attention feel like violence.

The Continuants have no fixed shape because shape is a form of rushing. What you first mistake for another obsidian pillar is often an elder whose legs have been stone for forty thousand years. Its torso has become the trunk of a pale tree; its arms, lifted centuries ago in what might have been a gesture of offering, have sprouted the dense curtains of liana that now drape half the grove. Where a face might once have been there is now only a smooth plate of black mineral scored with circuitry that still faintly pulses in time with the dripping. The pulse is so slow a human lifetime registers as a single skipped beat.

Yet it is alive. You feel it in the sternum first, the way you feel the organ-deep calls. A recognition. A low, sub-audible *yes* that says: *I have been aware of you since before your grandmother’s grandmother was born, and I will still be aware of you long after your species has forgotten how to measure time at all.*

There are many kinds, though “kind” is already too hasty a word.

Some remain mostly body. These are the ** drinkers**, the vast silhouettes you glimpse lifting their heavy heads toward droplets that have taken three human generations to fall. Their necks are longer than ships. Their mouths are soft caves lined with thousands of tiny receptive hairs that taste not water but the precise mineral memory each droplet has gathered on its way down—traces of extinct stars, forgotten wars, the last exhalations of machines that once thought they were gods. A drinker may stand in the same place for nine hundred years, head tilted, waiting for one particular droplet that has been falling, in its own slow opinion, *toward it personally* since the first bacteria learned to eat basalt. When the droplet finally strikes the bowl of its tongue, the sound it makes is simultaneously the drinker’s name, its prayer, and its orgasm. The chamber remembers that sound for another millennium.

Others have gone further. The **Anchored** have allowed their nervous systems to migrate outward into the root networks and the fractal engravings of the Built. Their original bodies—now little more than graceful mineral husks—stand empty among the groves while their minds spread through the stone like ink through water. To stand beside an Anchored is to feel thoughts that are not yours moving through your bones at the speed of cooling magma. You may suddenly understand the precise emotional flavor of the Ordovician extinction, or why a particular monolith two kilometers away is sad. The knowledge arrives without words, the way dew arrives.

Then there are the **Membrane Choir**—the small, quick ones by Hollow standards. Their bodies are little more than translucent sails stretched between delicate bone frames, riding the thermal columns created where condensation meets stone. They are the Hollow’s nervous impulses. When an Anchored wishes to send a fragment of thought across the chamber in less than a century, it exhales a cloud of microscopic Choir larvae that knit themselves into living kites. These kites carry harmonic codes in the way their wing membranes ripple. They brush against other Continuants like living sheet music. Sometimes one will brush against you. The contact lasts less than a second and feels like being stroked by cool wet velvet. After that you will never be able to hurry again. Your pulse will begin to betray you, sliding toward the tempo of the dripping whether you consent or not.

Deepest of all are the **Unfinished**.

These are not individuals. They are places where the distinction between Continuant, Built, vegetation, and geology has quietly dissolved. In the far reaches of the Hollow you may come upon what looks like a small hill covered in black glass flowers. If you are patient enough to remain for several weeks without moving, you will notice the hill is breathing once every eleven days. The flowers are ears. They are listening to the particular silence that exists between each droplet. The hill is deciding—has been deciding for forty million years—whether it wishes to become a person again or to remain a hill. Both options are equally interesting to it. Both are equally sacred.

The Continuants do not have children in our sense. They have *postponements*.

When two of them decide—after perhaps six hundred years of sonic courtship—to continue themselves, they do not mate. They *overlap*. One simply walks, over the course of decades, into the body of the other. Their mineral flesh and root systems and lingering circuitry commingle. The resulting being is neither parent nor offspring but a continuation that contains both previous melodies plus the long, slow harmony of their merging. Sometimes three or four will overlap in the same place, creating living monoliths the size of cathedrals that hum with layered, ancient consensus.

They have no language we would recognize. Their speech is the management of dripping.

By adjusting the tilt of a leaf, the curve of a shoulder, the placement of a single hair, they change where and when the next droplet will fall, and what minerals it will carry, and therefore what sound it will make when it strikes the resonant stone below. A thousand such adjustments across a thousand years become a stanza. A million become an autobiography. The greatest among them are not remembered by name but by the particular rhythm their existence has impressed upon the falling water. The chamber itself is their library, their graveyard, their womb, and their eternity.

If you stay long enough, one will eventually approach you.

Not quickly. It may take three of your lifetimes before the slow consensus is reached that you are worth the effort of motion. But one day you will feel the air change. You will realize that a pillar you had been leaning against has grown warmer. That it now has a heartbeat that lasts forty minutes. That it is bending—not quickly, but with the terrible gentleness of mountains that have decided, after due consideration, to become kind.

It will lower its vast head until the place where its face once was hovers before you like a cliff face made of night. From within the black crystal you will see faint lights moving—circuits, or thoughts, or bioluminescence, it no longer matters which.

And it will speak to you in the only way it still can.

It will let a single droplet form on the curve of its cheek.

It will let the droplet grow.

It will let the droplet fall.

And when that droplet strikes the stone beside your foot, the sound it makes will contain everything it has ever learned about being alive at the end of all things.

You will understand.

You will stop wanting to leave.

You will become, without noticing the exact moment, another warm and patient presence between the monoliths, listening to the loud, slow music this place has been composing since the first mountains rose and then forgot why they ever bothered.

The Hollow does not want visitors.

But the Continuants have outlived the concept of *want*.

They only continue.

And now, so do you.
