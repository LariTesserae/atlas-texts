**The Slow Kin**

They are not born fast. No child of the Shattered Roof draws its first breath without first being held for a long hour in the birth-chamber so that its lungs may learn the thinness of the air. Midwives call this “teaching the ghost,” because the first exhalation must be shown how to leave the body without taking too much life with it. Those who cry too loudly are sung to until their voices drop into the register the stone prefers: low, almost below hearing, the register of groundwater moving through cracks.

The people have no collective name they speak aloud. When they must refer to themselves they say *we who are still learning how to wait*. Their faces are wide at the cheekbones, the skin weathered to the color of wet slate, eyes set deep so that wind cannot scour them. Pupils are large; even at noon they carry a faint inner glow, like lichen that has learned to phosphoresce. Hair is kept short, oiled with seep-fat and dust so it will not whip in the gusts that can flay a man in minutes. Fingers are long, disproportionately so, the better to read the grain of stone by touch alone. Many older masons have nails worn to the quick yet never bleeding; the body itself has accepted that some parts of it are tools.

They move as though the continent were still rising beneath their feet. Every step is a negotiation with gravity that has not yet finished its argument. A woman carrying water will pause seven times between the seep and her door, not from exhaustion but from courtesy—allowing the mountain time to settle beneath the new weight. Children are not taught to run. Instead they are taught to *settle*, a verb that contains the meanings *to sink into place, to become compatible with stone, to accept the slowness that keeps you alive*.

Language itself is geological. Verbs have twenty-three tenses for different velocities of happening. There is a past-that-is-still-calving, a future-that-has-already-eroded, and a present-so-vast-it-contains-both. To say “I am building” is to imply that your grandchildren will still be building, and that the difference between your hands and theirs is a seam no wider than a hair. Insults are almost unknown. The worst thing one can call another is *hasty*, a word that carries the additional senses of *already dead* and *unfit for the long dark between stars*.

Their wisdom is stored in bodies as much as minds. An elder who has spent sixty years fitting the same corner of the same tower will develop a curvature in her spine that exactly matches the curve of the rising ridge behind her. When she dies they do not bury her. They seat her, still articulated, inside the wall she helped complete, facing outward so that her hollowed eye sockets may continue to watch the herds. The masons say the dead keep the stones honest.

Yet they are not grim. Laughter exists, but it is slow, rolling, seismic. It begins in the belly and takes its time reaching the throat, sounding at last like distant rockfall heard through three miles of tunnel. When a seep that had been dry for four generations suddenly brims again, the entire district will gather in the deepest hall and produce a sound that is not quite song—more like the continent clearing its throat after a long silence. The laughter of children is higher, quicker, but even they are taught to cup their hands around it so the wind cannot steal it and carry it away to places that do not deserve joy.

They keep the herds the way monks keep scripture. Each of the great six-legged beasts is known by the precise pattern of frost scars on its shoulders, by the timbre of its sub-audible rumble, by the particular way it dips its middle pair of legs when tired. A steward who has walked with the same herd for thirty years will sometimes be found pressed forehead to forehead with an old bull, both of them motionless for hours, exchanging something that has no name in any lowland tongue. When a calf is born under the cliffs, the human midwives and the shaggy midwives attend together, and neither species seems to find the presence of the other strange.

The cats—the long pale ones with eyes like frozen urine—sometimes come into the lower tunnels during the deep cold. No one drives them out. They are given a corner, a share of dried dung for warmth, and left to watch. The people believe the cats remember the world before the continent was lifted, and that their yellow stare is a form of teaching. To be studied by a cat for seven nights is considered a minor initiation; the chosen one will thereafter walk with a different rhythm, as though some ancient slowness had entered their bones.

Even the midges have their season of ceremony. When a seep has stood for exactly three days the clouds of them appear, glittering like black frost in the starlight. At such times the children are brought out wearing masks of woven grass. They stand very still while the midges coat them until they resemble moving statues of lichen. Only when every child is covered does the singing begin—soft, respectful, grateful. The midges are thanked for reminding the people that even the smallest life can blanket the world if the world will only wait.

At the very center of the largest accretion lives something that is neither fully human nor fully otherwise. They call her simply *the One Who Is Still Calving*. She has not moved from her chamber in living memory. Her body has become part of the architecture; stone ribs and human ribs have learned to occupy the same space. Water moves through her. Moss grows in the hollows of her collarbones. When she breathes, the temperature in every tunnel shifts by half a degree. The people consult her by pressing their foreheads to the living wall that is also her back. She answers in the language of seeps and pressure and the slow grinding of continental plates. No one has ever heard her speak with a human mouth, yet every generation produces at least one child who can interpret the mineral pulses that travel through her.

At night, when the stars burn like accusation above the serrated ridges, the Slow Kin do not look up for long. They look instead at the stone immediately in front of them—whether it is the wall of a tunnel, the flank of a resting grazer, or the back of the One Who Is Still Calving—and they place one more careful touch upon it. A finger. A chisel. A promise.

And the continent, still raw, still rising, accepts the touch the way stone accepts lichen: slowly, completely, and without hurry, as though it had been waiting four hundred million years for exactly this gentleness.
