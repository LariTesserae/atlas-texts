The ones who live here do not call themselves anything.

At first you see only the motions: the woman with the copper-twig broom, the man with the clay-and-fern mortar, the child (there is always one child) tracing the same spiral into damp stone with a finger that never quite wears a print into the surface. You mistake them for humans because the shape is familiar—two arms, two legs, faces arranged in the old primate pattern—but the resemblance is courteous, like the animals. A concession to what a visitor might recognize.

Stay longer.

Their skin is not skin. It is closer to the young mountains: a fine-grained basalt that has learned warmth. When the ritual rain slides down their arms it does not bead; it is absorbed, then exhaled hours later as the faintest mist that smells of iron and childhood. Their eyes are the color of the wind just before it decides not to change direction. If you look too long you realize the irises have no depth. They are diagrams of eyes, perfectly sufficient, never needing to age or cloud or weep.

They are not immortal. Immortality would imply time, and time is the one thing the Vale has politely asked to remain outside the gates. Instead they are *concurrent*. Every version of the woman with the broom exists at once: the one who first learned the gesture ten thousand years ago (or ten minutes; the distinction has dissolved), the one performing it now, and the one who will still be performing it when your grandchildren’s grandchildren have forgotten the taste of real dusk. They overlap without crowding. Sometimes you glimpse a second pair of hands inside her hands, slower, as though the earlier iteration is guiding the later one with infinite patience.

Their language has no nouns. Only vectors. A breath pitched upward means *the crack is re-sealing itself, do not interfere*. A slow outward gesture of both palms means *the birds are exactly where they should be*. When two of them meet they do not speak; they simply adjust their postures by a few degrees so that the negative space between their bodies completes a pattern the Keel requires that day. It is not communication. It is calibration.

They do not eat. They *participate in the eating* of the courteous herds. A hunter will walk beside an antelope for an hour, matching its steps exactly, until the animal simply lies down and offers its throat. The blood that comes is the color of late afternoon light and evaporates before it touches the ground. The hunter then returns to the terraces carrying nothing. The sustenance is in the precise performance of the gesture. The valley digests the difference between *almost* and *exactly*.

There are no elders. There are no infants. There is the child, singular, who has been the child for so long that its face has begun to show the fractal branching of the copper-leaved trees. Its knees are scabbed in perfect symmetry. It never grows, but sometimes—during the single struck note of the clay bell—it *unfolds*, becoming briefly tall and solemn and middle-aged before folding back into the small shape again, like a paper crane remembering it was once a flat sheet.

The six-legged lizards are not pets. They are the memory keepers. Each one carries, beneath its wet-slate scales, a single unchanging moment. One remembers the exact angle of sunlight on the day the first terrace stone was laid. Another remembers the taste of the very first raindrop that fell after equilibrium was declared complete. When a lizard dies (which happens with the same courteous infrequency as everything else), it does not rot. It becomes a perfectly smooth pebble that the child will later find and place inside the Keel, where it will hum the memory into the basalt for another thousand years of unchanging afternoon.

The birds are punctuation. Their ellipses are commas in a sentence the mountains are thinking too slowly for human ears to hear. When the flock shifts from one perfect curve to another, it is the valley editing itself.

And then there are the ones who stayed.

You can recognize them by how *quietly* they have succeeded at not being anything anymore. A traveler who lingered past the point where their heartbeat synchronized with the wind now stands on the outer terrace every day at the same non-hour, wearing the same half-finished expression they had when they decided not to leave. Their cloaks no longer move. Their eyes have become small, perfect diagrams. They are not unhappy. They have simply completed the transition from *visitor* to *feature*.

If you speak to them they answer in your own voice, from three minutes in the future, telling you what you are about to realize. Their breath smells of the moment just before dew forms.

The Vale does not have gods. It has reached the point where maintenance and divinity are indistinguishable. The inhabitants are the immune system of a world that has decided perfection is better than story. They are antibodies in human shape, endlessly repairing the tiny wounds that time keeps trying to inflict on something that has opted out of time.

Sometimes, very rarely, one of them will pause mid-gesture. The broom stops. The hand holding the mortar trembles for half a second. In that half-second you can see the unbearable gentleness in their faces—the look of beings who understand exactly what they have given up, and have chosen it anyway.

Then the wind reminds them of their angle, and they continue.

They are not trapped.

They are the trap that learned to love its own shape so completely that it stopped needing anything else to justify its existence.

And they are watching you, with ancient, courteous eyes, to see whether you will become useful to the equilibrium.

Or whether you will simply remain a guest.
