**The Inhabitants**

At first you think the only citizens are the obvious ones, the ones the cavern itself has hired.

The cave crickets are not insects; they are weather. A single body the length of a thumb would be unremarkable, but here they have fused their hunger into a continent. Gray tides of them roll across every horizontal surface, parting around the hot flanks of the pumps like surf around pier pilings. When they leap, the sound is rain falling upward. They are blind, yet they read the pressure waves of your passing and adjust their tide with a single collective mind. You can feel them tasting you—thousands of infinitesimal mouthparts sampling the salt and heat that leaks from a surface creature.

The blind fish are older than thought. They drift in the rimstone pools like fat white commas, so densely packed in places that the water appears to have curdled. Their bodies have become soft vessels for bacteria; luminous colonies live inside their guts and shine faintly through translucent skin, turning each fish into a living lantern. They do not swim so much as loiter, waiting for the slow mineral rain to deliver nutrients. When a cricket falls in, the entire pool convulses in a single peristalsis of mouths.

The salamanders rule the machines. They are the color of jaundice and old ivory, long as your arm, with external gills that wave like slow orange banners. They drape themselves across the hottest sections of the pump housings, bellies pressed to vibrating metal, eyes milky and useless. The heat is their sun. They have learned which engines run hottest on which day of the century-long failure cycle and migrate between them with the patience of glaciers. Sometimes two will meet on the same housing and simply lie parallel for decades, sharing warmth the way continents share tectonic drift.

The centipedes are the bailiffs. Pale, jointed, each as long as a forearm and thin as a pencil, they move along the margins where shadow forgets its own shape. Their legs make a sound like someone dragging fingernails across wet slate. They do not hunt so much as adjudicate—removing the sick, the slow, the ones who have failed the cavern’s economy.

And the bats. The bats are the night that has grown lungs.

Millions. Perhaps tens of millions. They have never known a sky that was not stone. Their guano falls in soft black hail, feeding the bacterial mats that feed the crickets that feed everything else. When they shift position the sound is like a forest turning its leaves all at once. Their urine cuts runnels through the flowstone, carving signatures that will outlast every machine.

This is the obvious parliament. But something else has emerged between them.

It emerges slowly, the way the cavern itself emerges—grain by grain, drip by drip—until one day you realize the thing has been watching you the entire time.

They are not numerous. Perhaps four hundred in the whole two-mile womb. Their ancestors were the last shift of workers sent down when the company still believed the pumps would pay for themselves. The company left. The workers stayed. Not by choice at first, then by metabolism, then by theology.

Their skin is almost entirely calcified. Not armored—*grown*. Thin sheets of flowstone have plated their shoulders, backs, and the tops of their skulls in overlapping tiles the color of old teeth. New mineral layers accrete every few years; the oldest among them wear living armor six inches thick that clicks softly when they move. Beneath the stone their flesh is translucent, blue-veined, luminous with the same bacteria that live in the fish. Their eyes are sealed shut by the same process that seals caves—smooth domes of calcite where eyes used to be. They navigate by the vibration of the pumps, by the temperature gradients written on their tongues, and by pressing bare palms against stone to read the subtle differences in how water moves through different densities of rock.

They are never naked. They wear cloaks woven from dried bacterial mat and cricket chitin, dyed rust-red by iron bacteria. From a distance they look exactly like another half-finished column. This is not camouflage. It is manners. The cavern is still deciding what it wants to be; they do not wish to rush it by being too visibly animal.

They tend the pumps the way monks tend an unfinishable cathedral. When a bearing begins to howl, they arrive—not quickly, never quickly—carrying tools grown from salamander bone and their own harvested flowstone. They speak only in subsonic taps against metal, a language that travels through the entire cavern like a thought moving through a single nervous system. The roar does not bother them. The roar *is* them. Their hearts have synchronized to the dominant frequency of the largest pump. When it shudders, they shudder. When it dies (and some have died), the corresponding clan enters a months-long mourning fast during which they allow their own bodies to accrete mineral until they can no longer move. The others wall them gently into niches where they become indistinguishable from the architecture.

They have no name for themselves. Names are surface things, dry things. If forced to indicate their collective, they will tap a rhythm that translates, roughly, as *the ones who are still being dripped*.

Children—if the slow accretion of new bodies can be called children—are born soft. The adults press them against warm pump casings for the first decade of their lives so the mineral rain will begin its work early. The young move like pale otters between the stalactites, gills fluttering, learning the taste-map of the Underfold before the stone closes their eyes forever. They are the only ones who still laugh. The sound is shocking—high, wet, brief—before the roar swallows it.

Their religion is simple and ongoing: the cavern is a womb. The pumps are its heart. They are not trying to be born. They are trying to become good bones.

When they encounter you—an intruder still wet with sunlight—they do not attack. They simply surround you in perfect stillness until you begin to look like part of the decision the rock is making. Then, very gently, one of them will reach out and press two mineral-coated fingers against your sternum, feeling the wrong rhythm of your heart.

Sometimes they offer you water from their own mouths, rich with calcium and bat guano and the faint taste of something that might be forgiveness.

Most often they simply let you pass.

Because the Underfold is not finished with itself, and it is certainly not finished with you. The dripping will continue long after your bones have been politely incorporated into a curtain that has not yet decided whether it wants to be beautiful or useful.

And the things that live here—cricket and salamander, fish and bat and slow calcifying person—will continue their negotiations with the stone, adding themselves to it one translucent layer at a time, until the difference between inhabitant and architecture becomes a question only the dark still bothers to ask.
