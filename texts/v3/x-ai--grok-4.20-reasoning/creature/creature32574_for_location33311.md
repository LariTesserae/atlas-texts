**The Hollow’s Children**

They are not a people who arrived. They *emerged*.

At first you do not see them at all. Your eye registers only another slow movement among the dripping leaves, another pale shape that might be a fungal shelf or a hanging root. Then the shape breathes, and the breath is the same temperature as the air, and you understand why they have remained half-invisible for so long.

They call themselves, when they must use words, *Siirath*—which means both “those who are wept upon” and “those who have not yet become pillars.” The distinction is important. Every adult Siirath believes they are in the middle of a very long metamorphosis. Childhood is leaf. Adulthood is vine. Elderhood is pillar. Death is when the pillar finally flowers. Whether this is metaphor or literal biology has never been settled, least of all by them.

They are small—none taller than a ten-year-old human child—but their bodies are elongated in the manner of cave creatures, limbs slightly too long, necks slightly too flexible. Skin the color of wet bone, faintly translucent; beneath it you can sometimes see the slow green pulse of the same bacteriologic light that veins the pillars. It is not paint. It is infection, willingly kept. The older the Siirath, the more luminous the veins, until certain great elders glow like living candles when they walk the rope bridges at night.

Their hair never stops growing. It grows in lank, moss-green strands that they braid with liverwort and the silk of blind moths. Over decades these braids thicken into living shawls that drape to the knees and continue to photosynthesize. When a Siirath stands perfectly still against a pillar, it is impossible to know where the person ends and the moss begins. This is considered polite.

Their faces are narrow, almost blade-like. Eyes enormous, pupils permanently dilated, reflecting the teal and violet bioluminescence in wet crescents. They blink rarely. Instead they have a second, transparent lid that slides horizontally, clearing condensation the way a frog’s does. When they look at you, it feels less like being seen and more like being tasted.

They do not speak with frequency. Their true language is conducted through three simultaneous channels:

- The touch of palm to living wood. The pillars register pressure, moisture, and temperature; the Siirath read the answering shifts in sap pressure and light as clearly as we read tone of voice.
- The bone flutes, carved from the hollowed femurs of their own dead. The notes are subsonic. You feel them in your teeth and in the roots of your hair. When a flute is played, the nearest pillar will brighten or dim in specific patterns, and every Siirath within the Hollow will unconsciously adjust their own bioluminescence to match. It is how they sigh, how they argue, how they pray.
- The scent of their own skin. Each family cultivates a particular strain of symbiotic mold in the folds of their elbows and behind their knees. The mold releases a perfume only they can read. To walk among them is to walk through an invisible conversation you can almost taste.

There are perhaps forty of them now. Never more. When the population threatens to rise, several of the eldest simply walk into the deepest fungal recesses between the pillars and allow themselves to be overgrown. The moss thanks them with extravagant fruiting bodies the color of fresh blood. The younger Siirath harvest these fruits and press them into ink used for the single form of writing they permit: marks made directly upon the living pillars that will be grown over and erased within a year. Memory, they believe, should be temporary. Only the Hollow itself should be permanent.

The children are almost indistinguishable from the thumbnail frogs that carpet the moss. They move on all fours, belly low, and their skin is still dappled with chromatophores that let them match whatever they rest upon. A child asleep on a pillar looks like nothing more than a pale tumor until it yawns. Their games consist of moving so slowly that the millipedes crawl across them without noticing. The child who is first discovered by a lantern-millipede is considered to have lost.

The oldest, the ones whose veins have begun to knot into visible whorls beneath their skin, are called *Veluuni*—the near-pillars. Their bodies are developing gentle swells and folds exactly where the great columns have balconies and handholds. Some of them no longer sleep lying down. They lean. It is understood that when the first root-like growths emerge from their heels, they will choose their final place among the nine and stand there until the transformation is complete. No one mourns this. It is the highest purpose.

They have no chiefs. They have listeners.

A listener is simply someone whose veins have synchronized so perfectly with the Hollow that the mist itself seems to bend toward them when they move. There are never more than three at a time. The current listeners are:

- **Ashen-Song**, whose entire left arm has become a single luminous vein. She no longer uses it; it rests against the central pillar like a grafted branch.
- **Sift**, a woman so small and quiet that moths roost in her hair even when she walks. She communicates primarily by breathing on people.
- **The Unfinished**, an elder whose transformation stalled halfway. One leg has become wood. He drags it behind him, and where the wood touches moss, new pillars sometimes begin.

Every dawn—not that there is true dawn, only the hour when the internal thermal tide shifts—the Siirath perform the Weeping. They gather beneath the largest of the pale leaves and let the saline water run into their open mouths until their bellies are full. Then they sing, without flutes, a sound like wet leather stretching. The pillars answer by releasing clouds of the glowing midges, which swarm across every Siirath body, feeding on the minerals in their sweat. The midges leave behind a faint gold dust that the people rub into their skin. For the rest of the day they glitter like damp saints.

They are not kind in the way we understand kindness. They are courteous the way an ecosystem is courteous: they will not harm you unless you first damage the balance, and even then the punishment is slow, gentle, and total. The last outsider who struck a light was not attacked. He simply woke one morning to find his legs had been lovingly, patiently bound to the floor by new root growth. By the time he understood what was happening, the moss had already begun to find his mouth nutritious.

If you stay long enough, they will begin to touch you. First with fingertips, then with entire palms, then with their foreheads. They are checking whether you, too, are in the process of becoming something that belongs here. Most are not. Most eventually feel the urge to leave, and the Siirath do not stop them.

A few, however, begin to dream of being roots.

Those ones the Hollow keeps.

And after enough seasons the new pillar is no longer quite so pale, and it bears, if you look closely, the faint suggestion of a face high up in the cartilage, eyes closed, smiling the slow green smile of something that has finally finished becoming what it was always meant to be.
