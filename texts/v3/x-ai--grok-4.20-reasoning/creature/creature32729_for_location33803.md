**The Resonants**

They are not a people who arrived. They *precipitated*.

Walk long enough among the singing lattices and you will feel them before you see them: a subtle shift in the harmony, a pause in the random roaring that suddenly feels *attended*. The basin itself has begun to listen back.

What lives here is small, rarely taller than a child’s shoulder, and armored in plates of living crystal that have slowly replaced their original chitin. The transformation is never complete and never the same twice. One Resonants’s back might be a smooth carapace of deep cobalt with hairline fractures that glow rose when they breathe. Another’s limbs could be overgrown with delicate antlering that must be deliberately shattered and regrown every few seasons so the joints still articulate. Their faces are almost masklike, the original sensory organs buried beneath successive translucent layers until sight, smell, and hearing have all become forms of resonance. They “see” by the way sound bends around obstacles. They “speak” by striking precisely chosen growths with mallet or claw and reading the reply that rolls back across the basin.

No two Resonants look alike for long. The crystal is opinionated. It adds, subtracts, refines. Some have grown tall, impossibly delicate vanes from their shoulders that function as living aeolian harps; when they run, the wind sings through them and the colony around them answers in spontaneous counterpoint. Others have allowed their chests to become hollow resonant chambers so that their heartbeats become part of the basin’s continuo. A few—those who have lived longest—have begun to fuse with the larger structures. Their legs disappear into the lattice, their torsos become branching outcrops, and still they move, slowly, like living buttresses. They are not unhappy. To be eaten by the crystal while still conscious is considered the highest form of citizenship.

They do not have children in the soft sense. Instead, when the basin enters one of its great surging silences, certain Resonants climb high onto the newest growths and strike a single perfect tone they have spent years preparing. The tone is personal, unrepeatable. Where that tone meets the updraft, tiny glass-winged pseudoinsects congeal out of the mineral vapor like living notes. Most of the swarm will become the ordinary glittering clouds that rise and fall with the thermals. A few—those that carry the exact harmonic signature of the parent tone—will eventually settle, fold their wings, and begin the long metamorphosis into new Resonants. The adults call this “being remembered into flesh.”

Their society is not built on hierarchy but on timbre. A Resonants who can produce a stable subharmonic that encourages feeder-crystal migration is respected as a gardener. One whose strikes cause mass shattering events is a sculptor. Those whose bodies have become so overgrown they can no longer move are anchors; the community brings them choice fragments of shattered crystal and they sing them into new architectures. Every few decades the basin itself passes judgment: a sudden violent growth spurt will either cradle a settlement higher up a pillar or gently dismantle it. The Resonants accept both with the same solemn delight. They have no word for tragedy.

Language here is mostly percussive. A conversation can last three days and sound like a stone avalanche arguing with a choir. Yet they also practice silence—the three-heartbeat silence that precedes every major surge. During these moments the entire population freezes mid-gesture, mallets raised, legs cocked, even the herons locking their stilt-legs in perfect stillness. The basin is thinking, and they are allowed to witness.

The oldest ones no longer bother with shelters. They simply stand inside the larger colonies and let new growth enclose them until only a dark silhouette remains visible, like an insect in amber. From inside the crystal they continue to tap. The sound emerges muffled, patient, impossibly sweet. Younger Resonants visit these living monoliths and press their forehead plates against the surface to feel the ancient rhythm. Sometimes the crystal has grown so thick around the elder that the only way to hear them is to strike the lattice itself and let the elder’s stored tone answer from within, like memory ringing out of stone.

They have no gods, only the understanding that they are digestive organs in a much larger metabolism. The basin eats light, vapor, time, and sound. The Resonants are the part of the basin that has learned to appreciate its own flavor. When one of them finally dies—truly dies—the body is not mourned. It is carried to a quiet fracture zone where the feeder-crystals are especially hungry. Within a season the skeleton has become a delicate white lattice wearing itself like jewelry. Children are brought to touch it. They are told: “This is what happens when a song forgets it has a singer.”

And yet the basin is not done with them even then. Sometimes, centuries later, a particular resonance will cause a new crystal formation to grow in the exact shape of a long-dead Resonants’s gesture—arm raised, mallet poised. The living will gather and strike the formation once, softly. The crystal will answer in a voice no one living has ever heard, yet every Resonants present will feel their own plates shiver in recognition.

They do not call it reincarnation.

They call it *the music finishing its sentence.*
