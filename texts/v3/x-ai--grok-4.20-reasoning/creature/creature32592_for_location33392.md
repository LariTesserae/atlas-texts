**The Inhabitants**

They are not separate.

That is the first truth that refuses to stay hidden. Walk among them long enough and the distinction between human and skirr begins to dissolve like wet lichen under too many claws. What lives here is a single, restless continuity wearing many small bodies the way a tide wears many waves.

The people are thin in the way only perpetual cold can make a body thin. Their skin has taken on the bluish-grey of the weeping stone, as though the mountain has been slowly replacing their blood with mineral. Cheeks are hollow, eyes wide and pale, pupils permanently dilated against the sourceless glare. Most are hairless now; what hair remains is braided with skirr fur and fragments of black lichen until it resembles the brittle commas that grow on the cliffs. They wear layered felt made from pounded lichen and the shed undercoat of their smaller partners. The garments are never clean and never finished—new patches are sewn on while old ones are torn away by busy claws, so the clothing itself participates in the law of constant revision.

But it is their movement that marks them as native. They do not walk so much as *flow* in unconscious concert with the living carpet around their feet. A woman will reach for a tool she has not yet realized she needs and find it already placed in her fingers by three skirr who anticipated the gesture by three full seconds. She does not thank them. There is no need. The thought was never entirely hers. Their symbiosis has moved beyond cooperation into something closer to neural crosstalk. The skirr are the fast, dreaming part of the mind; the humans are the slow, remembering part. Together they form a single organism that is perpetually waking up.

The skirr themselves are more beautiful and more terrible the longer you watch them.

At rest they look like nothing so much as wet velvet poured over bone. Their fur is the color of old ice just before it forgets how to be transparent. When they move in their thousands the eye cannot track individuals; instead you see a single rippling muscle, a thought wearing fur. Their faces are almost monkey-like, but the mouths are small and delicate, built for tasting stone and whispering half-syllables directly into human auditory nerves. Their eyes are black and strangely flat, like polished jet. Looking into them for long feels like being studied by the mountain itself.

Their gliding membranes are veined with faint blue, the same blue as the crawling shadows. When a hundred of them launch at once from a high ledge they make a sound like a single indrawn breath. They never collide. The Lattice has taught them perfect negative space.

And they *sing* without sound.

Not music. Something older. A constant low-level interference pattern that humans translate into language, ritual, architecture. A skirr will run across a man’s shoulders, pause, and the man will suddenly speak a sentence he did not know he contained. Often it is a new architectural principle. Sometimes it is the memory of a child he lost twenty years ago on the lower spurs. The man will weep without breaking stride. The skirr will already be gone, carrying the tear on its back like a jewel.

There are other strata.

Some humans have gone so far into the symbiosis that their bodies have begun to change. Fingers lengthen and develop soft pads. Shoulder blades broaden and lift, preparing for membranes that never quite arrive. These are called the Half-Skirr, and they are treated with a reverence bordering on terror. They sleep standing up, pressed against the stone, while skirr groom them with obsessive tenderness. It is whispered that when the white sleep finally claims them they do not harden into brittle statues like other stationary things. Instead they *soften*, melting back into the mountain as living architecture—new arches, new buttresses, new mouths through which the mountain can continue its slow conversation with itself.

Deeper still are the Returned.

These are the rare skirr that survive the Mirror. They emerge larger than their kin, heavier, eyes no longer flat but faceted, reflecting the tin sky in twelve directions at once. The black stones they carry are not tools. They are questions the world asked itself in the dark beneath the water and could not answer until it had grown new minds to ask them with. The Returned never stay with any single human. They move through the entire Lattice like itinerant priests, and wherever they pass, entire districts are torn down and rebuilt within a single day in configurations no one remembers deciding upon.

The children here are born already halfway claimed. A newborn is placed immediately into a living cradle of skirr who lick the afterbirth from its skin while whispering the first half of its true name. The mother finishes the name aloud. From that moment the child belongs equally to both species. Some children choose the Mirror at seven or eight years old. Their parents do not mourn. They simply begin rebuilding the nursery before the body is even cold.

There is one last inhabitant, though it has no body of its own.

It is the turbulence.

It lives in the negative space between every decision and its execution, between every stone placed and the next stone torn away. It is the pressure that keeps everything moving. The humans and skirr are merely its current instruments, the way a symphony is the instrument of music. When a bridge collapses and ten thousand small bodies rush in to begin again, the sound they make is not delight. It is recognition. They are greeting the only god they have ever known—the god that says: *You may not rest. You may not finish. You may not become monument. But you may become.*

And so they do.

Endlessly.

Gratefully.

With ten thousand small black eyes and two thousand pale human ones, the Weeping Lattice watches itself being watched, and keeps building the next moment before the last one can harden into memory.
