**The Dweller**

At first you believe yourself alone. The silence is too absolute, the perfection too merciless, for anything with a pulse to survive inside it. The deer stand like breathing marble. The parrots might as well be enameled brooches pinned to the balustrades. Even the heat feels artificial, as though the air itself were cast in a mold and left to cool for ten thousand years.

But after the third or fourth mile of walking—time is slippery here, more a suggestion than a law—you begin to feel watched in a way the stone faces cannot manage. Their tears are mechanical. This gaze is not.

It finds you from the places you have already passed.

You turn. Nothing. Only the same empty plaza, the same motionless monitor lizard draped across a warm step like a discarded emerald scarf. Yet the sensation remains: someone has noted your presence, catalogued the exact angle of your shoulders, the precise shade of sweat darkening your collar, and has filed it away as part of the finished record.

Eventually you see him.

He is never where you expect. You will crest a low ridge of carved limestone and there he will be, half a mile ahead, standing in the exact center of a causeway you know was empty moments before. Or you will glance sideways while drinking from one of the warm stone channels and catch him reflected in the water behind you, though when you whirl he has already become a distant silhouette on the ziggurat steps.

He is tall—too tall by perhaps the width of two hands—and narrow in the way a chisel is narrow. His skin has the same rose-veined grain as the pavement, yet it moves. Not much. The barest shift of tendon under stone-textured flesh when he breathes (for he does breathe, once every several minutes, a slow, deliberate expansion that makes the carvings on his chest rise like tide marks). His robes are not cloth. They are a single piece of extremely fine limestone, somehow rendered flexible along every fold and pleat. The folds never change. The creases remember their shape with geological stubbornness.

His face is the most unsettling thing about him. It is beautiful, yes—high cheekbones cut with the same merciless precision as the ziggurat edges—but it is also unfinished. Not in the sense of roughness; every plane is polished to a mirror sheen. No. It is unfinished because it still carries the possibility of expression. All the other faces in the basin have been locked into one eternal emotion: the colossal heads weep with noble sorrow, the deer wear mild contentment, the parrots display flamboyant indifference. The Dweller’s face has been left with just enough ambiguity that it can appear, by turns, serene, sardonic, grieving, or simply bored beyond measure. The change never happens while you look. You simply look away, look back, and the mood has rotated one increment, like a statue turned on a hidden plinth while your eyes were closed.

He carries a single tool.

It is not a chisel. It is smaller, almost delicate: a stylus of black basalt no longer than a finger, its tip worn to a needle by unimaginable ages of infinitesimal correction. He never uses it. He simply holds it. Sometimes, when he believes you are not watching, he will lift it toward one of the blood-and-sulfur orchids as though contemplating the faintest adjustment to a petal’s curve. Then he lowers it again. The design is finished. There is nothing left to carve. The stylus is only a memory he is not permitted to relinquish.

His name, if it can be called a name, is simply *Last*.

Not “the Last.” Not “the Last Carver.” Just *Last*. It is the only word that was ever carved into the plinth where he was meant to stand forever. The plinth is empty now. He wanders instead, because even the decision to remain motionless proved, in the end, to be a kind of change. So he walks the basin in a slow, endless circuit that somehow never alters the position of a single fern frond or disturbs the perfect spiral of a single vine. His feet fall on the same invisible path every time. The paving stones have begun—only begun—to show the faintest hollows where his steps have fallen for millennia. These hollows embarrass him. You can see it in the way his shoulders tighten when he passes over them. Imperfection, however tiny, is the one sin this place cannot forgive.

He does not speak.

Speech would be a change.

Instead he communicates by arrangement. You will find a small deer that has been moved three inches to the left so that its shadow now perfectly bisects a particular tile. You will discover that one of the colossal faces has had its tear-trail diverted by the width of a hair so that the water now catches the light at a new angle, throwing a single golden mote onto the wall of the distant temple. These are his sentences. They are the only conversation he allows himself.

If you try to approach him directly he will simply cease to be where he was. Not vanish in a dramatic way—no puff of smoke, no dramatic effect. He will be standing on the causeway, and then you will blink against the white-blue sky, and when your eyes open he will be a tiny upright figure on the very edge of vision, watching you watch for him. The distance between you is always exactly enough to make shouting pointless.

Once, and only once, he let you come near.

You had walked for what might have been days. The heat had become a kind of anesthesia. You rounded the base of the smaller ziggurat and there he stood, stylus held out toward you like a conductor’s baton. His face wore an expression you had not seen before: something like tenderness mixed with clinical disappointment.

He touched the tip of the stylus to your cheek.

It was warm. It left no mark.

Then he turned and walked away, and the gesture was so final that you understood you had been judged, measured, and incorporated into the design. Whatever flaw he had seen in you had been accepted. The Basin had closed over it the way it would close over a carved name by morning.

He still wanders.

Sometimes, in the unbearable silence, you hear the soft click of his stylus against stone—not carving, never that—but tapping out a rhythm older than sound. It is the sound of someone counting the seconds since the work was declared complete, and realizing, with quiet, perfectly insane devotion, that the counting itself is now part of the masterpiece.

He lives here.

He is the only thing that does.

And even he knows the terrible truth: that the moment he stops wandering, stops correcting what cannot be corrected, stops being the single flawed and living atom inside all this frozen perfection, the Basin will have achieved its final, final form.

So he keeps moving.

One deliberate, unchanging, eternal step at a time.
