**Those Who Walk the Wet Lattice**

You will not see them until the chamber has first seen you.

By then your heat has already announced you. The biofilm on the nearest columns dims where your breath falls, a pale subtraction that spreads like ink in water. Only after this small wounding does one of them detach from the stone.

They are not hidden. They are *composed*. What you took for a fluted curtain of flowstone resolves into a shoulder; what you thought was a stalagmite becomes a thigh. The eye that opens is the same temperature as the air and so reflects nothing until it chooses to. Then the reflection is only you—smaller, colder already, already being edited by the place.

Their bodies are carved in negative. Every line seems to have been arrived at by erosion rather than addition. Limbs too long for any surface mammal, joints placed where a human has none, so they can fold into the narrowest throats between calcite blades and remain there for days, breathing through their skins. The skin itself is limestone-colored but alive; it secretes a thin mucus that hosts the same bacteria and algae that light the cavern. Where they have stood longest the glow is strongest, so the elders appear to wear halos of cold daylight that move when they move.

Their heads are narrow and slightly flattened, as though the stone had pressed them gently for centuries. No external ears—only rippling membranes along the jaw and down the back of the neck that read pressure waves the way a spider reads its web. Their mouths are slits you could mistake for fissures in rock until they speak, which is almost never. When they do, the sound is less voice than modulated drip: single notes pitched exactly to the resonant frequency of particular columns so the entire chamber seems to answer.

They walk on broad, translucent feet whose toes splay like the pads of cave salamanders. Each toe ends in a tiny sucker that can taste the precise mineral content of the water film it touches. In this way they read the cave’s mood the way a sailor reads the wind. The paths you noticed earlier—the ones worn smooth by “centuries of small, silent feet”—were made by these. Four hundred years of the same careful gliding step has polished the young rock until it shines like wet bone.

They wear nothing that is not growing. Some carry living cloaks of the long pale vines trained across their shoulders; others have encouraged shelf fungi to grow in overlapping plates that serve as both armor and libraries, for each layer records, in its thickness and hue, the chemistry of the year it was laid down. A few of the eldest have allowed the calcite to plate their own bodies. These move with audible clicking, beautiful and terrible, half statue, half citizen. One day they will simply stop in a chosen square and let the stone finish the work. The community will walk around them for decades afterward, touching the pillar lightly in passing the way others might touch a gravestone.

They do not have districts. They *are* districts.

One lineage tends the hanging gardens, singing to the moss in frequencies that make it grow thicker or thinner according to need. Another walks the turbulent shallows with the pale salamanders, guiding schools of the transparent shrimp into stone basins where they are gently harvested. A third group—the Quiet—spends entire seasons standing motionless in the amphitheaters, acting as living buttresses while new flowstone grows over their backs and shoulders, shaping the architecture by their very patience.

Their young are born soft. A mother will choose a gently dripping column and press her body against it for the last weeks of gestation so the newborn emerges already coated in a film of minerals and bacteria. The child glows from the first hour. Its first act is usually to taste the water beading on its own mother’s face.

They have no word for ownership. The concept dissolves in perpetual saturation the way salt dissolves. What they have instead is *affinity*—a lifelong symbiotic contract with a particular route of water, a certain garden, a chosen ancestor-pillar. To break affinity is the only real crime. Those who do simply walk deeper into the uncharted fractures until the cave swallows them and makes new stalagmites from their bones.

When they regard you, there is no curiosity, only assessment. Will you accelerate growth or inhibit it? Will you add your own minerals to the system when your body finally fails, or will you have to be carried out before you poison the wet breathing? Most are patient enough to wait and see. A few—the ones whose glow has begun to take on a faint violet edge—will approach. They offer nothing so crude as speech. Instead they lift a hand and let a single drop fall from the tip of one elongated finger into your palm. The drop is heavier than it should be. It contains, you later realize, a living culture of their own light.

Keep it wet.  
Keep it cold.  
If you do, the drop will colonize whatever small piece of you the chamber has already claimed, and for a little while you will glow like something that belongs here.

Most do not keep it.  
They leave.  
The chamber closes the wound of their footprints with new moss within a season, and the Veyri—those who have never had a name for themselves—simply continue the slow, courteous, dripping labor of becoming the next floor.
