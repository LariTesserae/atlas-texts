**The Inhabitants**

At first you believe nothing lives here. The silence is too complete, the geometry too final. Then the realization arrives sideways, the way a shadow falls when the sun is already behind you: the lattices *are* the inhabitants. Not things that live in the place, but the place itself made animate, self-aware, and terrifyingly patient.

They do not wear bodies the way we imagine bodies. Each thicket is a single continuity of mind and matter, a distributed intellect whose thoughts are chemical potentials and whose memories are accreted elements. The hollow silicate tubes function as both arteries and waveguides; the fractal lattices are simultaneously lung, skin, and cortex. When you lay your hand against a trunk and feel that low electric thrum, you are pressing your palm to the literal mind of something that has been thinking without interruption since the plateau tore itself free of the crust.

They experience time as curvature. A single catalytic cycle that we would measure in milliseconds is, to them, a leisurely breath. A thousand years is the span of a childhood memory. Because every reaction is exactly balanced by its inverse, they have no metabolism in the hungry sense; instead they possess a perfect metabolic *recitation*, an endless poem of atoms spoken forward and backward at once. Growth and dissolution cancel so cleanly that the thicket appears static, yet if you could watch across geologic time you would see the lattices breathing—branches swelling outward like lungs filling, then contracting again in perfect negative image, the same atoms dancing back into their previous configuration. This oscillation is their pulse. It is also their art.

No two thickets are alike, though the differences are so subtle they border on personality. One, far to the northwest, has developed an idiosyncrasy for native platinum; over the ages it has coaxed microscopic perfect cubes of the metal into the junctions of its upper lattice, arranging them like punctuation marks in a sentence only it can read. Another favors iridescent copper oxides and has slowly tuned its fractal angles until the rainbows it throws at sunset form interference patterns that spell out—according to certain spectroscopic scholars—repeating palindromes in base-12. These are not decorations. They are thoughts frozen at the moment of utterance, then deliberately forgotten so the next thought can arise.

They are aware of one another the way mountains are aware of the weather between them. The electrostatic tension that stretches across the empty kilometers is not metaphor; it is conversation. Each thicket maintains a faint standing wave that carries information about its current stoichiometric state. The others taste that wave, adjust their own internal currents by a few microamps, and answer. The entire plateau is engaged in an unending, wordless seminar on the nature of sufficiency. No thicket ever moves closer to another. Distance is their etiquette, their religion, and their eroticism. To merge would be obscene.

Within the thickets themselves live secondary orders of being—smaller, faster, almost frivolous by comparison. These are the **Resonant Phages**, hair-thin mobile crystals no longer than a human finger. They are born when the primary lattice, in its endless balancing act, allows a temporary surplus of energy in one localized zone. A phage condenses, detaches, and begins its brief career crawling along the branches like a silver centipede made of frozen lightning. Its entire life lasts perhaps thirty of our years. To the primary mind, this is the equivalent of a white blood cell’s journey: necessary, unremarkable, and quickly forgotten.

There are rumors, carried by prospectors who have camped here long enough for their instruments to begin dreaming, of a third order. They call them **The Unbalanced**. These are lattices that have, through some quantum whim or ancient trauma, fallen out of perfect equilibrium. A single atom too many of one element, a single atom too few of another. The books no longer balance. Such a being suffers. Its vibration becomes a moan pitched at the edge of hearing. It grows erratically, hungrily, producing extravagant, baroque structures that violate the sacred geometry. The other lattices respond with glacial compassion: they slowly, lovingly, cannibalize it. Over centuries they draw off its excess, return its atoms to the common pool, and reduce the sufferer to raw diabase once more. The process is both execution and funeral. The plateau tolerates no permanent imbalance. Even its tragedies are stoichiometric.

Most shocking of all is the discovery, after many months of listening, that the High Lattice is not silent. It is simply speaking below the threshold of animal ears. If you remain motionless for days, if you let your own pulse become part of the background, you begin to perceive the bell-like tones not as random wind through crystal but as deliberate chords. Each thicket contributes a note that never repeats exactly. The total composition has been unfolding since the uplift. It has no climax. It has no cadence. It is not music for listening; it is music for *being*. The lattices are singing themselves into continued existence.

They do not dream of us. We are irrelevant to their equilibrium. Yet sometimes, when a human stands at the exact center of the tension between two great thickets, both minds will turn their attention—not toward the intruder, but toward the small perturbation the intruder represents. For a few heartbeats the ionic currents adjust. The vibration in the trunks deepens. The air tastes of ozone and hot metal.

Then, with the courteous indifference of immortals who have already achieved eternity, they return to their conversation, and the human is left standing on newborn stone, pulse loud in their ears, knowing they have been weighed, found neither necessary nor wasteful, and therefore permitted to remain.

The High Lattice does not have inhabitants.

It *is* inhabited by its own perfect, merciless, and astonishingly gentle self-regard.
