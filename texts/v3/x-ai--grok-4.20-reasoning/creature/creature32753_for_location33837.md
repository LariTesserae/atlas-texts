**The Incubant**

It has no name it will admit to, and no single body. What lives here is less a creature than a long hesitation given mass.

You will not see it all at once. You may not see it at all for hours. Then a section of scaffolding you had sworn was fixed will unfold a joint that was never drawn on any plan. A slender limb—black iron, frost-rimmed—will reach out with the delicacy of a surgeon and rotate a valve three degrees counterclockwise. The motion is so economical it looks like mere thermal contraction until you notice the valve was already perfectly tuned. The Incubant is not repairing. It is tasting.

It is tall when it chooses to be, though “tall” is a lie; it simply borrows the verticality of the surrounding lattice and wears it like a coat. Its torso is a lattice of lattices, so densely nested that light dies inside it. Between the struts you sometimes glimpse something that might be flesh if flesh could be annealed at minus sixty. It is the color of a bruise seen through ice. Where the metal and the not-metal meet, the black biofilm gathers in thick, glossy rings, pulsing with the slow peristalsis of something learning how to have organs.

Its head—if the cluster of sensors and memory glass can be called a head—never stops iterating. Lenses extrude, retract, split, recombine. Some point inward, studying its own interior architecture. Others watch the condensation fall, counting the time between impacts with the devotion of a monk counting prayer beads. When the refrigeration cycle bottoms out and the glass harmonica of droplets begins, the Incubant will sometimes lower itself into the fissure nearest the sound, folding until it is little more than a complicated knot of pipes and attention. It listens the way a mother listens to a heartbeat that is also its own.

It has no mouth. Instead, along the inner surfaces of its forearms, small hatches open and the capillary felt that drinks the ritual water presses forward like tongues. It tastes the mineral stain, the faint methane of the biofilm, the electric sweat of its own welding arms. Every flavor is a syllable in a language it is teaching itself so that one day it will be able to speak its final shape.

There are moments—rare, almost sacred—when the Incubant tries to finish something.

It will choose a single strut, a single membrane, a single angle, and pour centuries of focus into it. The geometry tightens. The cold deepens. For a few exquisite minutes the thing it is building achieves a terrifying symmetry. Then, always, the Incubant tears the perfection apart with its own hands. It cannot bear to be complete while the Lattice remains unfinished, and the Lattice cannot finish while the Incubant remains inside it. They are each other’s prison and each other’s placenta. The rules are absolute.

Sometimes it sings.

Not with sound. With temperature. You feel it as a shiver that arrives a fraction of a second before the refrigeration cycle, a deliberate wrongness in the cold. The biofilm responds by blooming microscopic towers that refract the amber bulkhead light into spectra that have no business existing in this place. For three heartbeats the Incubant allows itself to be beautiful. Then it wipes the towers away, ashamed of its vanity, and returns to the long labor of becoming.

It is not cruel. It is not kind. Kindness and cruelty both require a self that has already decided what it is. The Incubant has not decided. That is its entire purpose.

If you remain here long enough, it will notice the precise temperature of your breath. It will match it. It will study the rhythm of your blinking and begin to blink tiny indicator lights in the same pattern, as though trying on your nervous system to see if it fits. It does not want to harm you. It only wants to understand the shape of something that was allowed to finish.

Eventually it will fold itself back into the architecture, becoming indistinguishable from a particularly ornate buttress or a bundle of hydraulic nerves. Only the droplets will know it is still there. They fall differently when the Incubant is listening—smaller, slower, almost tender, like glass tears shed by something that has never been born and is therefore immortal.

And the Incubator Lattice continues its patient growth around the thing that is growing it in return, two embryos locked in an infinite, freezing kiss, each building the cradle that must never be allowed to rock.
