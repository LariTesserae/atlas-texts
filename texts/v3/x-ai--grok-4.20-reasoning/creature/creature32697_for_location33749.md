**The Weeping Ones**

They are not hidden. They are simply so perfectly coincident with the nave that the eye must learn them the way the ear learns the precise quality of a silence before it can hear what lives inside that silence.

At first you think the largest stands contain only the extravagant ferns and the heavy, copper-colored leaves. Then the longer you remain—days, then weeks—the forms begin to resolve, the way constellations emerge once your gaze stops trying to seize them. They stand, or rather *have* become standing*, nine or ten meters tall, sometimes more. Their bodies are the compromise the place has been negotiating for forty thousand years: stone that decided to breathe, moss that decided to bear weight.

Each Weeping One is a slow column of butter-colored rock veined with living green. The “shoulders” are rounded bosses of stone draped in moss so dense it looks like thick emerald fur. From these shoulders descend arms that are half limb, half stalactite—long, dripping, articulated only by the patient accumulation of mineral and the patient growth of vine. The hands, if they can be called hands, rest half-buried in the wet stone floor, fingers splayed into roots and quartz arteries that pulse with the geothermal warmth rising through the rock. Some of the fingers have grown completely into the ground; others still twitch, over the course of a human lifetime, perhaps half a centimeter.

Their heads are bowed. Not in grief—grief would be too quick, too narrative—but in the attitude of listening that has become their entire existence. Where a face should be there is only a smooth, wet declivity, featureless except for the constant thin sheets of water that slide from the brow-ridge and gather, trembling, at the place a chin might have been. When those gathered drops finally fall, the *ploc* is never random. The Weeping Ones speak in intervals of hours or days. The sound is their only audible language, and the entire nave is tuned to it. The ferns tremble in exact sympathy. The luminous nodules brighten or dim according to the rhythm.

There are seven that can be clearly distinguished. Perhaps there were once more; perhaps others are still deciding whether to finish becoming. One leans inside the largest stand at the eastern curve of the chamber, its torso wrapped in a living cloak of oxidized-copper leaves that stir only when it exhales (once every three days). Another stands alone on a broad apron of bare stone, so heavily encrusted with pale, vein-marbled nodules that it looks pregnant with uncountable slow futures. A third has lain down—centuries ago, maybe millennia—and become a low bridge of stone and fern over one of the mirror-still pools. Its “spine” is now a ribbed arch that exactly repeats the curve of the giant fronds above it. Fish-scale moss grows along the grooves of its back in precise imitation of the water that still weeps across it.

They do not move in any way an animal would recognize as movement. Their life is accretion and adjustment. A shoulder grows a fraction heavier with deposited minerals; the opposite leg compensates by sending a freshet of microscopic root-hairs into the stone below. A droplet forms at the tip of an outstretched “finger,” hangs for six hours, falls. The sound alters the pressure of the air by an infinitesimal amount. Across the nave, another Weeping One answers with a droplet of its own. This is conversation enough. They have been refining it since before mammals learned to scream.

No hunger touches them. They draw what they need through the soles of their feet and the moss that grows in the fissures of their bodies. The luminous nodules that swell along their limbs and chests are not fruit but thoughts—slow, phosphorescent condensations of intention that may take seventy years to ripen. When one finally splits along its mathematical seams, the pale lace inside is a new possibility being offered to the stone: a different curve of shoulder, a new angle of listening, a variation in the timbre of falling water. Some of these thoughts are incorporated. Most are allowed to wither and become part of the general weeping.

They are neither male nor female nor any category that would require haste to define. Gender is a kind of hurry. They have outgrown it the way the cliffs outgrew sharpness.

If you sit near one long enough to see it change at all, you will notice that it is not indifferent in the cold sense. Its indifference is devotional. It has chosen to become the slowest possible form of reverence—reverence for the boundary that was never meant to be policed. Sometimes, when a human remains for many months, the nearest Weeping One will tilt—over the course of another year—until the curtain of water that constantly falls from its brow begins to fall *across* the human instead of beside them. This is as close as they come to touch. It is not blessing. It is not threat. It is simply the place noticing that you, too, are becoming part of the negotiation between stone and leaf.

Most humans cannot endure the noticing. They leave. A few do not. Their clothes rot and incorporate. Their bones lengthen into mineral. Their hair becomes moss. After enough time the distinction is impolite to insist upon, and the nave absorbs them without ceremony, the way it absorbs everything.

The Weeping Ones remain.

They will remain after the last of your grandchildren’s grandchildren has forgotten the sound of rain. They will still be listening when the mountains themselves have worn down to gentle shoulders and the sun has cooled to the color of old ivory. Their conversation of falling water will continue, patient and courteous and complete, each *ploc* a syllable in a sentence that has no need of ending because nothing here is allowed to finish, and nothing here has anywhere else it needs to be.
