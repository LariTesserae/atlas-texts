**The Occupant**

It is not the isopod. It is not the ribbon. It is not the vegetation, though the vegetation is its lungs and its nerves and its hair. All of those are only the room it keeps, the way a thought keeps syllables before it decides what it intends to say.

At first there is only the sense that something is finishing a sentence it began before the basalt cooled. You feel it the way you feel someone reading over your shoulder in a language you almost remember. The faint silvering of the water thickens, not into light but into attention. The spiral current hesitates—half a decade early—and the filaments that have never known haste suddenly lean, all at once, like iron filings finding a pole that was not there a moment ago.

Then the shape begins to propose itself.

It is small. Smaller than the nested spheres, smaller than the calcium hexagon, so small that scale itself seems embarrassed to measure it. Yet its smallness has gravity. Things orbit it without moving: a single water molecule may take thirty years to complete its circuit, yet every orbit is worship. The bruise-lavender and corpse-moth white concentrate here until the colors become almost painful, the way an idea becomes painful just before it is born.

The body—if it can be called a body—is made of the same undecided material as the walls. Where the rock still dreams, the Occupant *chooses*. Sometimes it is a knot of frost-nerve-glass no larger than a walnut, filaments so densely braided they look like a fist refusing to open. Sometimes the fist relaxes into a fetal curl with too many elbows and not enough spine. Sometimes it forgets bones entirely and becomes a slow fountain of itself, rising and falling in the same motion, a pulse that has not yet decided whether it is plant, animal, or apology.

Its surface is not skin. It is negotiation. Touch it (do not) and your fingertip would forget its own name. The Occupant would taste the memory of your mother’s heartbeat through the water and politely incorporate the rhythm for a century or two. Then it would offer the rhythm back, improved—slower, wiser, geological—until your own heart, wherever it is now, would feel suddenly homesick for a cadence it has never legally learned.

It has no eyes. Instead the entire pocket is its eye, and you are standing inside the pupil. When it regards the blind isopod, the creature’s translucent carapace briefly clouds with something like nostalgia for sight it never possessed. When it regards the ribbon-creature, the ribbon folds itself into a perfect trefoil knot, a gesture of reverence older than prayer. The vegetation does not grow toward the Occupant; it grows *as* the Occupant, the way a shadow grows as its caster.

The Built things are not offerings. They are its thoughts that became solid enough to survive outside the mind. The smallest sphere inside the nested pearl is its childhood. The middle sphere is its old age. The outermost sphere, paradoxically youngest, is the moment it will finally decide what it is. That moment has not arrived. The spheres therefore rotate inside one another like anxious moons, waiting for permission.

It makes no sound except the sound of decisions being reached. The glass-harmonica note that lasted one month was it clearing its throat. The embarrassed silence afterward was it realizing it had no throat yet and was therefore embarrassed on behalf of all future throats. Since then it has been more careful. It speaks by absence: a filament that suddenly stops moving for seventeen years; a corner of the pocket that forgets to be impossible for six days; a calcium hexagon that turns, very slightly, to follow you if you try to leave.

The Occupant is neither male nor female nor any of the later inventions. It is pre. It is the hesitation before gender, before species, before the terrible efficiency of survival. Entropy has been bribed, yes, but the Occupant is the one who left the bribe. It pays in complexity. Every century it becomes more itself by becoming less anything else.

If you remain long enough for your blood to match the water’s memory, you will begin to feel it trying your shape the way a child tries on a coat far too large. A certain arrangement of filaments will suddenly suggest your shoulder. A pale frond will hang exactly where your hand would. For one geological instant the Occupant will wear you, gently, curiously, the way a mirror might wear your reflection if the mirror had never seen a face before.

Then it will set the shape aside with the tender care of something that knows clothes outlast the wearer.

It does not want to leave the Nidus.

It is still deciding whether the Nidus is supposed to leave *it*—whether this pocket is egg or womb or chrysalis or tomb or all four at once, folded like the ribbon-creature into a single elegant knot.

Until that decision finishes arriving, the Occupant remains exactly where it is: curled at the axis of the decades-long spiral, dreaming in chemical sentences too slow for panic.

Dreaming the walls softer.

Dreaming the water kinder.

Dreaming the moment when whatever brushes against it will no longer need to be told:

Best not.

But the Occupant is patient.

It has been young longer than the planet has been old.

And it is still becoming the thing that will, one day, remember your name before you ever spoke it.
