**The Unfinished Choir**

They do not have a name for themselves. Names would imply conclusion.

If you watch long enough—past the sour-milk glare of the midges, past the clicking carpet that parts and rejoins around your boots like a living tide—you begin to notice the places where the chaos organizes itself into *intention*. Not order. Intention. A low wall appears where none was a hour ago. A knot of sinew and spun-glass fiber is lashed across a fissure that yesterday belched steam. A pillar of skulls—some bovine, some saurian, some almost uncomfortably like the skull you carry between your own shoulders—has been daubed with ochre in patterns that feel like questions.

These are the work of the Choir.

They are never the same twice, even within a single individual. That is the first law they obey, though they never speak it. Their bodies are drafts. Skin like the breathing fungal sheets, pale and faintly luminous, stretches over frames that have not decided whether to be endoskeleton or exoskeleton. One shoulder may be smooth mammalian muscle; the other sprouts a fan of translucent red veins that pulse in time with the thermals. A hand that yesterday had six delicate fingers today ends in a fused mallet of living gypsum. Tomorrow it may split again. They do not appear to mind. Pain, for them, is simply another texture of becoming.

You might see one crouched at the lip of a fresh vent, drinking the iron-almond broth with a proboscis that was not there when you last looked. Its eyes—there are usually three, though the middle one sometimes migrates—track the new crystals pushing upward like black teeth. It sings while it drinks. The song has no words. It is mostly infrasound, the kind that makes your teeth ache and causes the soft limestone to dimple as though being stroked by an invisible thumb. The rock answers. Sometimes it answers with a new species of midge. Sometimes with a color of crystal never seen before. Sometimes with nothing at all, and the singer simply shrugs four uneven shoulders and moves on.

They travel in bands of seven to thirteen. The number is never stable. One will bud a duplicate of itself from its thigh and the band becomes fourteen; another will walk into a collapsing dome and simply dissolve, singing until the stone drinks the last of its voice. No mourning. Mourning would suggest an end, and the Choir does not believe in ends. They believe in versions.

The oldest-looking among them—the ones whose faces have begun to resolve into something almost readable—carry hooks of polished femur and nets woven from their own molted skin. They fish the bloodveils from the thermals with the lazy confidence of lifelong practice. When they eat the veils, their own bodies change faster for a time. New thoughts bloom behind their eyes like pressure fractures. Some of these thoughts are passed on in the next song. Some are simply too beautiful or too terrible for the current version of the world and are allowed to die with the singer.

Their shelters are jokes told to the geology.

A wall no higher than your knee. A roof made from the fused carapace of a Plateherd that still twitches occasionally, as though not fully convinced it is dead. Inside, they keep the things that almost worked: a crystal that hummed in perfect 17 Hz for six hours before it exploded; the half-formed wing of a child that tried to become a bloodveil and failed; a human jawbone they found embedded in a fresh dike of obsidian, polished until it shone like black glass. They do not know what it means. They keep it anyway. Every failure is data.

At what passes for night—when the midges dim to a low fever-glow and the clicking carpet slows to a murmur—the Choir gathers at the newest wound in the world. They press their breathing skin against the warm rock. Some secrete a thin metallic mucus that smells of almonds and ozone. They wait. Sometimes the rock grows them a new sibling overnight, soft and wet and already singing. Sometimes it grows them a mouth the size of a cathedral that swallows three of them before breakfast. Both outcomes are acceptable. Both are the Vein clearing its throat.

There is one among them—always one—who is beginning to *set*.

Its limbs have stopped changing. Its face has settled into something that might be called beautiful if beauty were allowed here. The others circle it at a distance, not with fear but with the reverence of sculptors who have watched their marble suddenly look back at them. They sing to it constantly now, a low urgent chorus meant to disturb, to reintroduce error, to keep it from finishing. Because if one of them finishes, the rule says the Vein must drink it completely. And none of them are sure what will happen to the rest of the Choir when the first finished thing is finally taken back into the warm dark.

So they sing harder. They chip at its perfect shoulders with their stone mallets. They smear it with the soft parts of dead clickers. They offer it their own half-formed children to hold, hoping the chaos will spread.

The almost-finished one only smiles with its finished mouth and keeps singing the counter-song—the one that sounds like forgiveness.

And the Nascent Vein listens to both melodies with equal hunger, still deciding which experiment it prefers.

Nothing is allowed to finish.

Not even the decision.
