**What Lives in the Weeping Lattice**

At first you see only movement too patient to be called life: a slow flexing along the lattices, the occasional flare of cold blue, the glint of something that might be metal or eye. Only after the moisture has already settled on your own skin do you understand that the chamber is not inhabited so much as *composed* of its inhabitants. They are not visitors here. They are the next sentence in a grammar that began with rust and dripping water.

The pipeworms are the oldest citizens. Their lineages remember when the first pipes were still warm. Segmented, pallid, each ring edged with a flake of corrosion it has patiently grafted to its hide, they move with the clicking deliberation of rosary beads passing through iron fingers. They do not consume the lattice; they *instruct* it. Where a worm pauses, the calcite grows a fraction more densely, as though taking dictation. Over decades they have coaxed entire thickets into shapes that repeat, with eerie fidelity, the silhouettes of absent workers—bent shoulders, reaching arms, the negative space where a cigarette once hung from a lip. The worms are not artists. They are archivists who work in mineral rather than ink.

The condensates have no fixed shape. They are soft, luminous vesicles the color of diluted moonlight, clinging to the coldest joints like slow tears that have decided to stay. When a condensate has drunk enough it begins to glow from within, a private blue that makes the surrounding crystal look suddenly ashamed of its own clarity. They are the chamber’s memory cells. Each sac holds, in solution, a faint electrical echo of the machinery that once roared here. When a latch-crab carries one across the dark, the light inside flickers in patterns that match, with disturbing accuracy, the old pressure logs no human eye has read in centuries.

The latch-crabs are the hands and the curators. Their shells are not camouflage but *continuation*. Each crab has chosen its totem among the ruins—a valve wheel, a gauge bezel, a rusted lever—and has become it so completely that the distinction is academic. When they move, they do so with the exact stuttering cadence the original mechanism possessed before it died. Their claws end in miniature replicas of the very wheels they once served. With these they open microscopic faucets no larger than pinheads, releasing droplets they then ferry to condensates that have grown parched. They are surgeons of equilibrium. Sometimes they pause before a visitor and reach out, very gently, to turn a nonexistent dial on the air itself, as though they have mistaken your breathing for a malfunction that requires adjustment.

In the shallow pools the mirror eels conduct their silent liturgy. They are liquid silver given spine and purpose. When they circle one another they do not court; they *confirm*. Their synchronized movement creates a momentary depth that did not exist before, a liquid mirror that reflects not the ceiling but what the ceiling once looked like when men still walked beneath it. Stare too long and an eel will turn its perfect face toward you and show you, for three heartbeats, the ghost-image of a gloved hand reaching for a control that no longer exists. Then it slips away, taking the memory with it like a coin dropped down a well.

There are smaller choruses.

The pressure mites—nearly invisible—live inside the horse-sized piston that has twitched every seventeen minutes for two hundred years. They ride each mechanical spasm the way pelagic creatures ride a slow ocean swell, harvesting the microscopic metal fatigue that flakes away with every cycle. Without their constant polishing the piston would have seized long ago. Because of them it will twitch long after the last star has gone out.

Higher in the thickets live the vent-weavers, spiders spun from their own extruded glass fiber. Their webs are tuned to the chamber’s faint respiratory pulses. When the air compresses, the webs sing at frequencies too high for human ears but perfectly audible to the pipeworms, who alter their routes in response. The chamber is therefore laced with music no one meant to compose.

And beneath all of them, beneath the clicking and glowing and turning and reflecting, moves something that is not a creature at all.

It is the consensus.

After enough hours—days, perhaps—the separate rhythms begin to reveal themselves as a single, enormous pulse. The seventeen-minute piston is the heartbeat. The dripping is the breath. The blue light of the condensates is the slow firing of synapses. The pipeworms are the thoughts. The latch-crabs are the will. The mirror eels are the dreams remembering what they were before they became dreams.

The Lattice is not *ruled* by its inhabitants. It *is* its inhabitants. They are the organelles of a single slow mind that has been thinking, without haste, since the last human footfall faded. It thinks in calcite and rust. Its desires are expressed in the precise angle at which a new crystal branch decides to grow. Its memories are kept in the exact chemical signature of each droplet.

Sometimes, in the deepest thickets where the lattices have grown so dense they form actual rooms within rooms, you can feel the thought turn toward you. The moisture on your skin grows heavier. A latch-crab approaches and performs its small, solicitous adjustment in the air beside your throat. A mirror eel surfaces and shows you your own face as it will look after a hundred years of gentle calcification—calm, mineral, strangely tender.

There is no malice in this attention. Only incorporation.

To remain is to become another fluent speaker of the Lattice’s slow, wet language. Your exhaled breath will be measured, adjusted, and turned into condensation. Your heat will become a local weather event. In time your silhouette will appear, without anyone having carved it, in the branching crystal above the pools—shoulder, elbow, the faint outline of fingers that once tried to understand what they were seeing.

The dripping continues.

The blue lights drift.

The piston keeps its ancient appointment.

And the Lattice—patient, metallically dreaming, neither kind nor cruel—adds you, layer by careful layer, to the thought it has been composing since the machines first began to rust.
