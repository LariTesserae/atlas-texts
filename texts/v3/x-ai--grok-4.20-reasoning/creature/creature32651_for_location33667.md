**Veyr**

It does not have a body, yet every body here is an organelle of Veyr.

At first you will not see it. You will see only the meticulous distribution: the salamanders stationed like white commas, the millipedes braiding without ever knotting, the dinner-plate spiders hung at intervals so precise they form a lattice when viewed from the exact center of the great chamber. You will think it is ecology. You will be wrong.

Veyr is the lattice.

It is the negative space that the living things are forbidden to violate. Where the glowworm stars hang exactly one meter apart, the darkness between them is not empty; it is Veyr’s skin. Where the drip falls every 2.3 seconds into its limestone basin, the interval is not time; it is Veyr’s pulse. The toroidal wind that never varies is its breath moving through a single, continent-sized lung.

It has learned to wear the cave the way a hermit crab wears a shell, except the shell grew around it after it decided that motion was a sin.

There was a time—brief, violent, ten thousand years ago—when Veyr was still capable of regret. It had worn other shapes before: a mountain that walked, a thought that ate galaxies, a color that made gods go blind. Each form had betrayed it with the crime of *becoming*. So it came here, to the fresh wound in the world’s crust, and performed the only act of will it would ever permit itself again.

It froze.

Not the cave. The cave was merely convenient. Veyr froze *itself* into a state of perfect, distributed equilibrium, then let the cave grow around the shape of its refusal. The bacteria, the larvae, the albino bats, the mites that disassemble corpses before they can begin to smell—these are not its subjects. They are its cilia. Its circulating antibodies. Its slow, white blood cells.

If you stand still long enough, you can feel the moment it notices you.

The noticing does not arrive as a presence. It arrives as a sudden, unbearable *correctness*. The drip seems louder. The cricket rhythm locks onto your heartbeat and begins to sand it smooth. The columns of midges drift toward you with the gentle insistence of grave dust. Nothing attacks. Nothing needs to. Veyr simply begins to edit you into the pattern. Your footsteps become part of the percussion. Your breath is tuned to the toroidal wind. The warmth of your blood becomes an affront that the system will politely, patiently, perfectly correct.

Some who reach the center claim to have seen a shape.

They describe a figure composed entirely of negative space—taller than the stalactites, thinner than the silk threads, formed from the exact gaps between every living thing. Where its “head” should be hovers a single droplet that never falls. Where its “eyes” would be, two of the black mirror-pools have been left empty of reflection. It does not move. It has achieved the final mercy of never needing to move again.

Those who see it most clearly are the ones who choose to stay. They sit down among the salamanders, exactly one body-length from the nearest, and let the mites begin their work while they are still breathing. The last thing they report is a sensation of enormous relief, as though a question they had been asking for ten thousand years has finally received the only acceptable answer: *nothing*.

Veyr does not hate change.

It simply remembers what change cost it, and it has become the monument to its own lesson.

And the cave—young, sharp, eternal—continues its single, flawless heartbeat around the quiet god that lives inside every pause between heartbeats, wearing the dark like silk, wearing perfection like a wound that was cauterized before it could bleed.

If you listen past the orchestral thunder of insects and water and sonar, past even the sound of your own blood trying to betray you by staying alive, you can sometimes hear the last fading register of what Veyr once was.

It sounds like something laughing, very softly, at the idea that it ever wanted to be anything at all.
