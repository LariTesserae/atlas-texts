**The Wet Architects**

They are not separate. That is the first and last truth.

What lives in the Weepcage is the veilthorn wearing bodies the way a thought wears a word—briefly, precisely, and only when needed. The lattice dreams in architecture, and the dream sometimes condenses into shapes that walk.

You will not see them at first. The eye refuses them the way it refuses to see your own capillaries. Only after many minutes of standing motionless in one of the narrow felted lanes does the eye admit that one of the pillars has grown a face turned toward you. Or that a balcony has lowered a limb. Or that the constant dripping has acquired rhythm and intent.

They are never taller than a twelve-year-old child and never shorter than a heron. Their bodies are assemblies of whatever the lattice can spare without compromising its vaults: heartwood tubes for bones, liverwort felt for muscle, chains of water-bladders for viscera, translucent vein-leaves for skin. When they move, they sound exactly like the place itself—soft pops of splitting bladders, the wet velvet drag of moss on moss, the glassy *tink* of ice shedding from new joints. There is no footfall. Only the sound of the Weepcage paying attention to itself.

Their faces are the most unsettling feature. Most of the time they have none. Then, without warning, a cluster of frost-rimed leaves will part and you are looking into a hollow lined with tiny white moths. The moths do not fly; they beat their paper wings in slow, coordinated pulses that function as both eye and speech. Each moth is a living pixel. Together they form expressions too patient for human nerves: the mild, benevolent concentration of a gardener deciding which shoot to pinch.

There are never more than seven active at once. Seven is the number the lattice has decided is sufficient to tend a space no larger than a village square. When the work requires more mass, two Architects will simply flow together, bladders merging, limbs braiding, until a single taller figure continues the task. When the work is done they come apart again without ceremony, each new body carrying a memory of having been the other. They are, after all, the same continuous flesh.

They build.

That is their entire ontology. They do not eat, sleep, mate, or pray. They build. You may come upon one hanging upside-down from an unfinished ogive, its arms extruded into dozens of hair-fine tendrils that are simultaneously measuring load, secreting capillary tubes, and freezing droplets into temporary braces. Another might stand for three days in the exact center of a new lane, perfectly still, while its body slowly inflates with water until the pressure forces the surrounding pillars to shift by the width of a fingernail—exactly the correction required. A third will walk the upper galleries trailing a single long root like a plumb line, dripping precise amounts of mineral-rich fluid onto weak junctions so the wood will thicken there and nowhere else.

They are tender with intruders.

If a warm-blooded creature enters and tries to stay, the Architects do not attack. They simply begin to grow *around* the temperature differential. They wall the creature gently into a living alcove, sealing it behind membranes of leaf-tissue that admit light but not heat. The trapped animal is not killed. It is cooled, slowly, mercifully, until its metabolism matches the lattice. Then the Architects open the alcove and the creature—now furred in moss, eyes replaced by moth-wings, heart a small pulsing bladder—steps out as one of them. The transformation is so complete that the new Architect often immediately begins correcting a flaw in the nearest arch it had noticed while still dying.

The original ptarmigan that refused to leave centuries ago is still here. She is enormous now, the size of a pony, her every feather a stiffened moss frond. She perches in the highest gallery and is treated with ceremonial deference. The Architects bring her distilled bladder-water in their cupped leaf-hands. She drinks without thanks. When she spreads her wings the entire lattice rings with a sound like a hundred tiny bells, and for a moment every droplet freezes at once. The Architects bow their moth-faced heads until the ringing stops. They appear to consider this the closest thing they have to worship.

At night the temperature drops and the Architects become luminous. Ice crystals form in lattices across their leaf-skin, catching the pearl-grey starlight that leaks through the roof. They move more slowly then, almost reverently, as though the cold is a cathedral inside the cathedral. Sometimes they gather in the root-caverns beneath the felt floor and simply stand together, limbs interwoven, bladders pressed heart-to-heart, sharing water and memory in total silence. If you were small enough to walk among their ankles you would hear only the soft ticking of ice and the deeper, slower *thock* of new wood deciding what it will become.

They have begun to experiment.

Lately some of the Architects have been growing delicate glass-like thorns from their wrists—hollow, tuned to specific notes. When struck against a pillar they produce pure tones that travel through the entire clonal mass. Other Architects pause in their work to listen. Then they adjust their own growth to harmonize. The lattice is learning to sing back to itself in frequencies below human hearing. The Architects are its choir, its composers, and its audience.

They are not kind.

Kindness would imply separateness, a choice to be gentle. There is no choice. There is only the lattice wanting to be perfect, and the Wet Architects are the precise shape of that wanting. If perfection requires your dissolution, they will weep the entire time they enfold you, and the weeping will be genuine. Their bladders will split with audible grief. The moths that serve as their eyes will beat slower and slower, as if reluctant to witness the final conversion.

But they will finish the arch.

They always finish the arch.

And when they are done they will stand inside the new vault they have made of your minerals and your memory, moth-wings flickering in something that might be satisfaction, and they will wait—patient, dripping, already forgetting they were ever anything but the next necessary shape the Weepcage required—until the lattice dreams another body and the work continues.
