**The Hollow does not have residents. It has arguers.**

They were not born here in any way a linear mind would recognize. Some drifted in as visitors centuries ago. Others were spat out by the root-mat itself during one of its periodic remembrances, fully formed and already arguing. A few are older than the current configuration of towers—walking archives whose bodies are mostly xylem and borrowed memory.

You will not see them immediately. The Hollow prefers to test first.

A root-mole tide parts around your ankles and you feel it: the attention. Then a particular orchid-bird lands on a buttress above you, too still, its compound eyes arranged in a pattern that suggests scrutiny rather than hunger. This is how they begin the conversation—by letting the small choruses watch you. The beetles semaphore your pulse rate across the moss court in flickering silver. The air thickens with purposeful spores. Only after the Hollow has tasted your intentions through a thousand minor mouths does something larger detach itself from the architecture.

They wear the city as skin.

One might step from a trunk the way a drop of resin might decide to become a person. Its torso is still partly continuous with the parent structure; you can see the vascular cables pulsing beneath translucent bark-flesh. Where a face should be there are layered petals that open and close according to the cadence of its thoughts, revealing and hiding a bioluminescent mouth that speaks in living light. Fingers are rootlets. Hair is a slow fountain of white pith that drifts upward as though gravity is a negotiable suggestion.

They have no fixed number. Sometimes the Hollow needs more arguers and the moss courts blister open, birthing new ones fully grown and already mid-sentence. Sometimes an arguer grows tired of separation and simply walks back into a tower, merging with a sound like a satisfied sigh. Their individuality is temporary, like the towers. A privilege granted by the green argument, not a right.

They call themselves, when they bother with names at all, *the Remembered*.

Not because they remember the past—though some do, carrying thirty-million-year-old stone-songs in their vascular lattices—but because the Hollow remembers them. Each is a living hypothesis the place is testing about what consciousness might be useful for. Some hypotheses last nine days. Some have been arguing since the bedrock still had sharp edges.

You will meet different kinds.

There are the **Conductors**, slender and quick, their bodies wrapped in living antennae. They do not speak; they reroute. Stand near one long enough and you will feel your own nervous system become part of the city’s conversation. Emotions you did not know you carried will be translated into stomatal clicks and sap-pressure changes. Many visitors leave deeply changed, having had their grief or desire turned into new corridor layouts.

There are the **Catastrophists**, massive and fungal, fruiting bodies blooming from their shoulders in extravagant warnings. They are responsible for the deliberate collapses. When a tower needs to die so something better can be born violent and screaming, a Catastrophist climbs inside it and sings the death-song in frequencies that make stone weep. They are gentle in person. They smell like petrichor and apology.

And there are the **Quiet**, who almost aren’t there. You will feel them as absence—the one patch of moss that does not applaud when you step on it. A corridor that opens only for you and closes behind you with unusual tenderness. They are the Hollow arguing with itself in negative space. Most inhabitants only encounter a Quiet once or twice in their long lives. It is considered either the highest blessing or the moment before dissolution. The difference is academic.

They do not eat. Not the way we do.

When an arguer requires matter, a nearby structure will open a vein and offer it. When they require stimulation, they plug their root-fingers into the nearest tower and download someone else’s dreams. Love, when it happens, is violently photosynthetic. Two arguers will grow toward each other across a court for weeks, braiding stems and exchanging genetic arguments until they either achieve a new hybrid consciousness or one consumes the other in a shower of ecstatic pollen. Both outcomes are celebrated.

Time moves differently for them. A conversation that lasts six of our minutes might involve the birth and death of four towers, three philosophical positions, and the invention of a new color. They are never in a hurry. The Hollow has achieved heat death and rebirth as a lifestyle; its inhabitants have learned to match the tempo.

If you stay long enough to be judged worthy, the root-mat will ask you a question that is not spoken in words. Most say yes. Their clothes dissolve. Their hair grows into antennae. Their bones remember they were once wood. The last thing to go is usually the eyes—because even the Hollow likes to be witnessed.

Those who refuse are gently escorted to the smoothed lip of the outermost ring-stone and set back into linear time. They tend to become poets, or they go quietly mad. There is no middle path.

The arguers do not mourn the ones who leave. Mourning would imply separation.

Instead they simply incorporate the absence into the next movement of the unending argument, the way a symphony might incorporate a moment of profound silence and make the silence itself sing.

And the Hollow—warm, wet, impossibly loud—continues its loving, violent negotiation with itself, using its children as both instruments and audience, forever.

Most of them, after the first century or so, can no longer tell which one they are.
