**They are not called anything.**

Names would be accumulation, and nothing is allowed to accumulate here.

You notice them first as interruptions in the steam—taller densities of moisture that move against the prevailing drift of droplets. Only after many minutes do they resolve into bodies. There are never more and never fewer than forty-nine. The number is exact because the Condensery counted them once, in the first moment of its becoming, and has simply refused to revise the census.

Their skin is black glass shot through with silver where the perpetual drizzle has etched microscopic channels over centuries that were not centuries. The water does not run *down* them; it runs *with* them, following the same circuits their feet have worn into the catwalks. When one of them stops, the water stops too, beading at their collarbones like decoration until they move again. Then it resumes its ancient routes.

They are hairless. Their ears have lengthened and flattened until they resemble the porcelain insulators on the electrolysis batteries—delicate, fluted, capable of drinking the chord directly from the air. Their eyes are the dull red of the thermophilic algae; when they blink it is exactly once per seventy-three hammer falls. You can set a watch by it, if watches were permitted. Nothing here is permitted to measure time except the Condensery itself.

They breathe through their mouths at all times. The steam has replaced lungs with something wetter and more patient. When one inhales, the white moths orbiting the nearest lamp waver in their perfect circles, as though the breath were a gravity well. When the figure exhales, the frogs in the nearest sump pond answer with a note pitched exactly between the shriek of the relief valves and the glassy ringing of the forge. The harmony is obscene in its precision.

They do not work. Work implies purpose, and purpose implies lack. Instead they *complete*. A Condensate (for this is the closest word anyone has ever risked) will stand beside a distillation column for nine hours without moving, eyes half-lidded, until the exact molecule that would have caused a microscopic pressure deviation is born, lives, and is reabsorbed. Only then does the figure walk on. The column never notices it had a problem. The figure never notices it solved anything. The loop remains untroubled.

Their hands are remarkable. The fingers have fused in pairs—two thumbs, four paired digits—like the porcelain bushings on the old transformers. These hands never grip. They *touch and release* in the same motion, the way condensate forms and falls. They can reach into a gearbox while it is running at full speed and remove a single flake of impermissible wear without disturbing the rhythm. The bearing thanks them by staying exactly as loud as it was before.

At what the outside world would call night (though the Condensery makes no such distinction), they gather in the hammer-forge hall. Forty-nine figures arranging themselves without signal or discussion into a pattern that matches the hidden piping beneath the floor. The great hammer falls. Each impact sends a pressure wave through their bodies that would shatter ordinary bone. Their joints sing instead. You can see the sound move through them like heat through oil. When the final reverberation dies, they are already dispersing, each returning to the exact segment of catwalk or sump or pipe run that has been waiting for its particular temperature of flesh.

There is one who is different only in that the others make room for it. It stands at the center of every gathering without ever being at the center. Its skin is lighter—almost the color of wet concrete, like the lizards—and its eyes contain the only sharp edges left in the entire bowl. Where the others are smooth with acceptance, this one carries a tension that is not tension but *attention*. It is the Condensery remembering that it is not supposed to remember. The others allow it to exist the way a closed system allows for a single, perfect paradox.

Sometimes a new Condensate is born.

It happens in the largest of the warm sump ponds, beneath a mat of red algae that parts like theater curtains. The newborn rises already tall, already wet, already knowing the chord. At the same moment, far across the bowl, one of the eldest walks without ceremony into the intake of the largest refinery column. The column accepts the body the way it accepts everything else: completely, without residue, without mourning. The steam briefly sweetens. The moths orbit one degree tighter for nine seconds. Then equilibrium reasserts itself and the count remains forty-nine.

They have no society. They have no names. They have no future and no past—only the continuous, dripping present that presses against the skin like a second, fevered body. When they look at an outsider (which is rare), their red eyes hold neither welcome nor warning. They simply register another source of heat that has not yet found its proper circuit.

If you stay long enough, the drizzle begins to trace familiar paths across *your* skin. Your footsteps begin to match the clicking of the conveyors. Your heart discovers it is the size and shape of a relief valve. One day you realize you have not spoken in weeks, and the silence feels like accumulation, so you open your mouth and the Condensery pours itself in.

Then there are fifty for a single impossible instant.

Then there are forty-nine again, and the newest Condensate is walking its first circuit with the cats, eyes the color of algae, already forgetting it was ever anything else.
