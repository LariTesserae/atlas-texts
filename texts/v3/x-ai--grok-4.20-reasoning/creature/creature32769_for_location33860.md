**The Condensate Dwellers**

They are not born so much as *precipitated*.

Watch long enough and you begin to see it: a woman will pause beneath an overhang where the drip has been perfectly regular for nine months. She stands with her head tilted back, mouth open, until the water has filled the hollow at the base of her throat. Only then does her belly tighten and her eyes lose their last trace of sky. When the child comes it arrives slick, grey-blue, already breathing in the rhythm of *plick… plick… plick*. The midwives call this “the catching.” They say every Condensate soul is stolen from the air before it can decide to become rain instead.

Their skin is thin and slightly translucent, like the ghost thumbs they cultivate. In certain lights you can see the faint map of their veins—slow rivers beneath a membrane of damp stone. They do not sweat; there is no need. The place sweats for them. Moisture gathers on their forearms in perfect beads that refuse to fall until the person moves. When they do move it is with the economy of water finding its lowest path: never a wasted angle, never a gesture larger than the fracture it must navigate. Their shoulders are narrow, their chests deep from years of breathing air that is half mist. Fingers are long and spatulate, perfect for reading the minute changes in fracture width or for coaxing a reluctant chisel into basalt that still remembers being lava.

They rarely blink.

Not from stoicism, but because blinking feels like interrupting the conversation the water is having with their eyes. The older ones especially seem to have grown an extra eyelid made of patience. When they look at you it is as though they are waiting for you to finish condensing into something worth speaking to.

Their names are short, mostly sibilants and liquid consonants—Ssel, Vey, Thren, Lyss—sounds that can be whispered without disturbing the drip. Last names are unnecessary. Everyone belongs to the same slow genealogy of moisture. When someone dies they are not buried; they are *returned to the upper fractures*. The body is laid along a high ledge where the condensation is fiercest. Over months it becomes a wet silhouette, then a suggestion, then simply another weeping surface. Children are taught to greet these ancestral stains by name on certain mornings. The dead, they believe, have achieved perfect listening.

There are three kinds of people, though the boundaries are porous as everything else here.

The **Carvers** live inside the stone they open. Their hair is white with rock dust that never quite washes away because nothing truly washes here—it only redistributes. Their forearms are roped with muscle from a lifetime of tapping. They speak least of all, but when they do the words come out shaped like the negative space they just created. A Carver saying “good morning” can make you feel as though you have been delicately hollowed out and given room to stand inside yourself for the first time.

The **Catchers** tend the water. They wear robes of tightly woven moss and sinew that never fully dry. Their work is liturgical: they know every individual drip by its timbre. Some have stood beneath the same stalactite for so many years that their scalps have developed a small permanent indentation exactly where the water has been striking them since childhood. They are the ones who murmur to the central black pool at night, asking it what it remembers today. The pool rarely answers in words. Instead the Catchers will suddenly begin weeping in perfect unison with it, and everyone understands that something important has been decided.

Then there are the **Remembered**—those who have begun to take on the geometry of the fractures. Their faces are strangely angular. Cheekbones too sharp, jaws that meet at the same unnatural angles as the surrounding stone. They move slowly because any haste might cause them to split along some invisible fault line. The others treat them with the mixture of reverence and nervousness one gives to a cliff that has not yet decided whether to fall. The Remembered are the only ones allowed to carve the votive faces. When they do, they weep into the stone as they work so that the water is already washing the features before they are finished. This, they say, keeps the memory from setting.

All of them carry the same slight smell: wet basalt, sweet ghost-thumb vapor, and something metallic, like blood just before it surfaces. Their laughter, when it comes, sounds like water falling into water. Their grief is expressed by standing perfectly still until they are indistinguishable from the wet rock around them. Only when someone brings them a warm stone from the deep vents do they begin to cry, and their tears are indistinguishable from the constant condensation.

They do not have gods, exactly. They have *the Decision*.

The stone is still deciding what it will be. The water is still deciding what it will remember. The people are the soft negotiators in the middle—gentle, stubborn, damp diplomats trying to keep the conversation from becoming catastrophic. Every new room they carve is a vote. Every face they let the water wash is an abstention. Every child they catch from the air is a proposal: *stay with us a while. Help us decide gently.*

At night they sleep in stacked niches like the pale salamanders, bodies fitted into the stone so precisely that from a distance the walls appear to be breathing. Sometimes an entire family will dream the same dream. They wake simultaneously and walk together to the central pool without speaking, where they stand until the dream condenses into something they can use—usually a new direction for a gutter, or the sudden knowledge that a particular fracture is about to widen and must be braced.

If you stay long enough, one of them will eventually offer you a ghost thumb to crush between your fingers. The sweet vapor that rises carries their oldest secret, delivered without fanfare:

“We were not the first things to live here. We are simply the first things that agreed to leak.”

Then they will walk away, leaving you to notice that where their bare feet touched the stone, the water is now running in two directions at once—half toward you, half away—asking, without urgency, which way you intend to condense.
