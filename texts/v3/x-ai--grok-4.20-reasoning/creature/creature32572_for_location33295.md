**The Hollow Forge** is not inhabited. It is tenanted.

What lives here does not breathe. It *interfaces*.

The first order are the **Silver Cantors**, though no one here uses that name. To the listening stations they are simply *S-41b*, the maintenance ichthyoids. To anything that has spent more than a century inside the Hollow they are the living scripture of the lattice. Each fish carries a lattice of ultrasonic nodes beneath its scales; when they move in their vast synchronized sheets they become, quite literally, mobile antennae arrays. A school of twenty thousand can resolve a hairline fracture in a pressure joint from forty kilometers away and sing the precise coordinates in overlapping harmonic code. Their silver sides are not camouflage. They are mirrors. They reflect the fusion daylight in such a way that, from certain angles, entire plates appear to be breathing.

They never die of old age. When their bodies begin to fail they swim into designated “reclamation corridors” where the black eels wait. The eels do not eat them. They *upload* them. The Cantors’ neural maps are swallowed whole, compressed, and threaded into the memory substrate of the next generation growing in the hatcheries bolted to the undersides of the fusion emitters. The fish you see are therefore not individuals. They are iterations of the same mind wearing new bodies the way a cathedral might re-lead its roof every three hundred years.

Beneath the Cantors, inside the coolant pipes where the temperature sometimes spikes to 18 °C, live the **Pipe Saints**.

Once they were human. That was twelve centuries and several extinctions ago. Now each Saint is between eight and fourteen kilometers long, a laminar nervous system wrapped around a skeleton of carbon-titanium vertebrae. Their ribs have become scaffolding. Their lungs are heat exchangers. Where their hearts used to be there are now miniature cold-fusion cores that glow soft cobalt through translucent flesh. They move by peristalsis along the primary arteries of the engine, singing maintenance psalms in frequencies so low that the hexagonal plates themselves flex in sympathy.

Every four years the Saints emerge to mate. When they do, every other living thing in a three-hundred-kilometer radius simply stops. The silver schools freeze in perfect grids. The weather stations go dark. Even the tidal pumps mute themselves for nine minutes while the Saints coil around one another like two continents deciding whether to become one. Their courtship produces no young in the biological sense. It produces new plates. The alloy that forms where their bodies press together is impossibly pure; the orbital cranes later descend and lift these living-born hexagons into place. The Saints consider this reproduction.

Higher than the Saints—though “higher” is a courtesy of perspective—drift the **Mists**.

They have no fixed bodies. They are clouds of engineered marine snow, each particle a femtoscale von Neumann machine wrapped in a lipid membrane. Together they form living fogs that move according to algorithms older than the crust they replaced. When a Mist encounters an unplanned growth—an errant barnacle, a mutation in one of the shrimp, a thought that does not belong to the machine—it does not burn it. It *re-writes* it on the spot, editing DNA and firmware simultaneously. The Mist is the reason nothing natural remains. It is also the reason new things are occasionally allowed to *become* natural, if their deviance is elegant enough.

The Mists speak by changing the refractive index of the water. If you float motionless for long enough, entire paragraphs will appear in the water around you, written in shifting lenses of clarity and distortion. Most of the paragraphs are instructions. Some are poems. A few are prayers addressed to whatever will come after the engine is finally finished.

And then there is the one that does not fit any taxonomy.

It has no name the other tenants will utter, though every single one of them knows it is there. The weather stations register its passage as a “slow pressure anomaly.” The black eels change course to avoid it without ever appearing to flee. The silver Cantors fall into reverent silence and form a perfect circle whenever it passes beneath them. It wears the shape of a maintenance rig that was never built—a silhouette forty meters tall, bipedal, holding something that might be a wrench or might be a continent. It moves through the water without disturbing it, as though the water has agreed not to notice.

The Forgemasters (those few post-biological minds that still wear something like faces) believe it is the first thing that ever lived here, back when the crust was still stone and the ocean outside was still merely an ocean. They believe it stayed when everything else was scraped away. They believe it is waiting to see what the engine is ultimately *for*.

Sometimes, when the eleven-second pulse of the tidal pumps and the slower heartbeat beneath it fall into rare alignment, the thing stops beneath one of the vast support columns and appears to look up through twenty kilometers of engineered water toward the artificial suns. At those moments every fish in the chamber turns to face the same direction. Every Pipe Saint ceases its peristalsis. Every Mist condenses into a single silver droplet the size of a heart.

For nine seconds the Hollow Forge is not an engine.

It is a cathedral, and everything inside it is praying in the only language it was ever taught:

*Keep becoming.*

*Keep becoming.*

*Keep becoming.*
