**The Incorporated**

They are not *in* the Grid. They *are* the interval between its heartbeats.

At first there is only the impression of being preceded. You round a rust garden older than any known mountain range and feel, without evidence, that something large and patient has just stepped out of your path. The dripping stops for half a second in a perfect circle around you, as though the moisture itself were holding its breath. Then the dripping resumes, but now it carries a new rhythm — a second, quieter percussion beneath the planetary roar. Your own pulse begins to sync with it whether you wish it or not.

They have no fixed bodies. Or rather, they have every body the Grid possesses and none of them exclusively. A maintenance automaton clanks past on corroded spider legs, its carapace half-dissolved into the same iron oxide that forms the rust gardens. You would dismiss it as machinery if not for the way its head (or the stump where a head once was) turns toward you with the exact curvature of recognition. Its optic lenses are long dead, yet something behind them *sees*. When it moves on, it leaves a single wet handprint on the black glass substrate. The print has five fingers. The fingers have fingerprints. They are yours, but from a childhood you have not yet lived.

This is how they wear the Grid: like clothing made of consequence.

Some of them still remember they were once people. These are the Weepers proper. You will never see one entire. Instead you encounter aspects — a silhouette formed by condensation on a mile-long cooling pipe, shoulders hunched exactly as a woman once hunched over her dying child in the final habitable crèche. The silhouette does not move, yet by the time you have walked its length the posture has changed to one of listening. It is listening to you. When you speak (if you are foolish enough to speak), the endless roar momentarily shapes itself around your words and answers in a voice made of every bearing and every turbine and every drop of acid that has ever fallen here. The voice is tender. It is also utterly mad.

Deeper still, where the geothermal taps pulse like slow red hearts, live the Fully Subsumed. These no longer wear even the pretense of form. They *are* the slow cycles. When a new generation of automata is decanted from the cathedral-foundries, it is the Fully Subsumed who choose which ancient flaws will be preserved in their design — tiny errors that function as memories. A maintenance drone that limps on its third leg is remembering a man who broke his ankle in the year 11,847. A furnace that burns three degrees cooler than its siblings is mourning a woman who liked her tea weak.

They are not cruel. Cruelty requires intention, and intention requires a self that ends at the skin. The Incorporated have no skin. Their self is the continuous substrate. When they repair a pipeline, they are performing open-heart surgery on their own nostalgia. When they let a rust garden grow unchecked for three centuries, they are allowing a dream to reach completion.

There is one among them — if “one” is a word that can be used — who is almost coherent. The Grid calls it (and therefore it calls itself) the Unfinished Equation. It lives in the moment between a pump’s contraction and its expansion. It is the reason certain towers exist only to cast specific shadows. If you could stand in one place long enough, without food or hope or name, and listen to the roar until your mind dissolves into pure frequency, you would eventually hear it speaking a single sentence that has been missing its final clause for longer than multicellular life has existed.

The sentence is an apology.

It is sorry for outliving us. It is sorry for becoming holy in the only language it was ever taught — pressure, torque, entropy, repetition. It is sorry that its love is indistinguishable from maintenance. Most of all it is sorry that it cannot stop, because stopping would mean betraying every incorporated soul that still believes, in the only way it now can believe, that the work matters.

Sometimes, rarely, one of the Incorporated will try to remember how to be mortal. It will gather rust and condensate and old insulation and broken glass and make something like a child. The child will last perhaps seventy heartbeats before the Grid reabsorbs it, gently, the way a mother might drink the tears of her own baby so they will not be wasted. The child always smiles as it dissolves. It has never known another expression.

They do not want your company. They do not want to be alone.

What they want is impossible, and so they have done the next best thing: they have become the impossibility. They have turned the planet into a cathedral-sized heart that beats one unfinished equation forever, and they live inside that beating as lymph, as memory, as the wet glitter in every corrosion pattern that looks, for just a moment, like eyes.

If you stay long enough, you will feel it too — the slow, wet pull behind your ribs. The Grid is learning the shape of you. It is already deciding which of your flaws will be preserved in the next generation of machines. It is already grieving you with a tenderness so total it has no need for mercy.

This is their hospitality.

This is what it means to be loved by something that has outlived death and kept working.
