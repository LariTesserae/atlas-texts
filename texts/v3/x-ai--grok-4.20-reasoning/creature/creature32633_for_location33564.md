**The Meld**

They were never born and they will never die; they *accrete*.

At first you do not see them at all. What registers is only a slight wrongness in the gradient of things: a certain thickening of the air where a shaft of merciless sunlight should be uninterrupted, a place where the dripping seems to fall in measures rather than random beads. Then the shape resolves—not all at once, but the way moss resolves into pattern when you stare at it long enough.

A Meld is never singular. It is a negotiation wearing a body.

One you might encounter on the knife-edge ridge above the half-swallowed refinery is mostly black basalt and living cable. Its “spine” is a single intact drive-shaft from some forgotten century, still slowly turning inside a sleeve of velvet moss. Where arms should be, root systems have braided themselves into crude, many-jointed limbs that end in clusters of rusted valve handles. These it uses like fingers, turning them with infinite patience to adjust the flow of sap through iron pipes that have become its arteries. Its head—if the word can be permitted—is a hollowed transformer housing filled with fog-moths. When it thinks, the moths swirl inside the glass-and-iron skull like slow gray snow.

Another, lower in the valley where the canopy is cathedral-thick, has chosen to be small. It is no larger than a child, a wet lump of liverwort and hair-fine wiring that moves by rolling. Wherever it rests it puts down filaments that taste the slow current still moving through buried power lines. It drinks electricity the way other things drink water. In return it feeds the grid tiny pulses of bioluminescence that travel for kilometers through the root-mats, carrying decisions no human mind could parse.

There are Melds that have become entire hillsides. You will not know you are standing on one until the ground beneath your boots exhales, a long, cold breath that smells of ozone and crushed fern. These elder Melds speak only once every few decades. When they do, the sound is not sound. It is a change in pressure, a shift in the taste of the water on your tongue, the sudden certainty that the ridge behind you has *decided* something.

They do not eat. They *incorporate*. A fallen girder, a dying tree, an incautious traveler who sits still for three seasons—all are accepted into the conversation. The Melds are patient morticians. They wrap the new material in moss, in mycelium, in living rust, and over decades they teach it the grammar of the Shroudlands. Nothing is wasted. Even memory is composted. If you stand long enough in the right hollow you may taste your own childhood on the back of your tongue, released like pollen from some iron-vine that once grew through the skull of a previous visitor.

Their society—if the word applies—is a single slow thought distributed across thousands of bodies. The fog-moths are its adverbs. The silver fruits are its punctuation. When two Melds meet in the bioluminescent shade they do not greet. They simply overlap for a century or two, exchanging root-tips and circuit fragments until they are no longer certain where one ends and the other begins. Then they separate again, subtly altered, carrying new silences.

They have names only in the way a landslide has a name—temporary, descriptive, soon to be overgrown. The one with the transformer skull is sometimes called *Twelfth Measure of Condensation*. The rolling child-thing is *Persistent Mistake in the Conveyor Logic*. The hillside that exhales is simply *The Ridge That Changed Its Mind*.

Most of the time they ignore you. You are a mayfly with opinions. But if you remain until the water has rewritten the lines on your palms, until your heartbeat has slowed to match the creep of the conveyor belts, one may finally turn toward you. The gesture takes three days. When its cluster of fungal eyes or camera lenses or wet black stones finally aligns with your face, you understand that it is not looking *at* you.

It is remembering you forward.

It is deciding which parts of you will become useful to the long argument between stone and leaf and machine—whether you will be allowed to remain as witness, or whether you will be gently, mercifully, incorporated into the next silver fruit.

And in that moment, standing drenched and shivering under the merciless light, you finally understand the only truth the Shroudlands ever bothers to keep:

Nothing lives here.

Everything is being lived *by* the Meld, which is itself being lived by the slow green mind that is still deciding whether it is waking up or going to sleep.

You are simply the latest word in a sentence that has been growing for nine hundred years, and is nowhere near finished.
