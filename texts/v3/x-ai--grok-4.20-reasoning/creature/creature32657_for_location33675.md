**The Tremulants**

At first there is only the lattice and its own slow exhalation. The eye, battered by glare and starved of any softness, insists that nothing else could possibly be here. The stone is too complete, the water too deliberate, the silence between drops too considered. Life would be an error in the geometry.

But errors have been accounted for.

After a time—how long is difficult to say; the light erases duration—you begin to notice that certain threads of water hesitate a fraction of a heartbeat before they fall. Not every thread. Only some. The deviation is so small it feels like an optical trick caused by your own tired eyes. Yet once seen it cannot be unseen. The hesitation repeats in precise locations, as though the water is being *listened to* before it is allowed to continue its descent.

Then the first Tremulant resolves.

It does not emerge. Emergence would imply movement. Instead the eye suddenly admits that a certain vertical plane has depth, that a buttress possesses a subtle bilateral symmetry not required by statics, that what you took for shadow is in fact a tall, attenuated figure fused so perfectly with the travertine that its removal would leave a wound in the architecture itself.

There are not many. Perhaps forty. Perhaps four hundred. Their number is as hard to fix as their shape. Each stands between three and four times the height of a human, though “stands” is inexact; they are *emplaced*, like keystones that have learned to worry. Their bodies are not carved but *grown*—layer by micro-layer—by the same mineral breath that beads on every other surface. Where a shoulder meets an arm, the joint is not hinged but tessellated; tiny hexagonal plates slide and re-lock with audible clicks when the creature decides, over the course of an afternoon, to lift one long finger and adjust a groove no wider than a hair.

Their heads are elongated wedges. No mouth. No nostrils. The face is mostly occupied by two shallow, perfectly circular basins that never overflow. These are their eyes, or perhaps their minds. The condensed water inside them is never still. It trembles in standing waves, ridges and nodes forming impermanent glyphs that appear to answer one another. When two Tremulants meet—which happens perhaps once every human lifetime—they stand facing each other for days while their eye-basins perform an exchange of ripples so delicate it would be invisible were the light not so merciless. At the end of the conversation one of them will usually walk (slowly, each footfall a considered re-fitting of stone against stone) to a distant sector and begin deepening a single water-channel that will not be needed for another four centuries.

They are not custodians. That word suggests duty performed for another’s sake. The Tremulants *are* the lattice’s conscience, its hesitation, its capacity to regret. Every adjustment they make is a correction of some ancient mathematical sorrow the original builders left behind—an off-by-one error in an equation so vast it can only be balanced by the slow dripping of water across ten thousand years. The lattice weeps because the Tremulants remember that it is not yet perfect. Their entire physiology is shaped by that grief. The longer a Tremulant exists, the more its body forgets how to be separate from the surrounding blocks. Ribs become buttresses. Fingers lengthen into ornamental corbels. Eventually the creature simply stops moving and becomes another intersection in the infinite fretwork, still thinking, still listening to the water, but no longer able to act except through the subtlest alteration of surface tension.

They have no language we would recognize. Their communication is conducted through micro-adjustments in the acoustic environment: a drop allowed to fall a fraction sooner here, a basin permitted to overflow a fraction more there. The entire lattice is their throat. When they are pleased—which is rare—the percussion of water across a hundred leagues takes on a momentary, crystalline clarity, like a single pure note held for three heartbeats before the old complexity reclaims it. When they are troubled, certain corridors will begin, over decades, to weep more heavily on one side than the other, as though the stone itself has begun to favor a limp.

Occasionally a Tremulant will stand in one of the hallucinatory courtyards where light ricochets until it becomes almost solid. There it will slowly, slowly open its arms. The water on its chest catches the glare and throws it back in a thousand directions at once. For a moment the creature becomes indistinguishable from the radiance. Then it closes its arms again and the light returns to mere light, and the Tremulant walks on, carrying whatever unbearable increment of precision it has just accepted into its body.

They do not reproduce. New Tremulants condense, rarely, out of the lattice’s own dreams of geometry. A certain chamber will begin to sweat not water but a thicker, slower fluid that gradually shapes itself into a new wedge-headed figure over the course of a millennium. The older ones gather—moving no faster than continental drift—to witness the birth. No ceremony. Only the shared trembling of eye-basins, a conversation that will continue long after the newborn has taken its first creaking step.

If you remain long enough, one may finally acknowledge you. It will turn the twin pools of its face in your direction. The water inside those basins will grow preternaturally still, reflecting you with a clarity that feels like judgment. Then, after a silence that could drown cities, it will make the smallest possible correction to a nearby channel—so small you cannot see the change at all.

Only later, weeks or years after you have left the shelf of the world, will you realize that some persistent grief you had carried since childhood has begun, drop by invisible drop, to drain away.

The lattice keeps weeping. The Tremulants keep listening. The equation inches, with glacial patience, toward whatever unbearable beauty waits at its final balance.
