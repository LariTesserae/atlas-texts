**What lives in the Weeping Basin does not stay itself for long.**

At first you only register the saturation. The midges are not clouds but a single continuous tissue; every individual is a moving cell in a body that breathes and thinks in infrared. When the tissue thickens it blots out the faint bioluminescence and the basin goes temporarily blind. Then the tissue thins again and the light returns like sudden memory. The midges die in their billions every hour. Their bodies rain onto the peat and are drunk by it before they can even settle.

The bellowers rise out of that rain. Amphibians whose ancestors were probably frogs the size of thumbnails have become glistening sacs the size of wine barrels. Their vocal pouches are lined with symbiotic diatoms that flash in exact synchrony with their calls, so every croak arrives as both pressure wave and violet strobe. They do not sing for territory. They sing to keep the peat from closing over them. When too many gather in one hollow their combined vibration liquefies the ground and they sink together, still singing, into a new black pool that immediately begins to boil with their offspring. Life cycle: four days from egg to bellowing adult, then collapse, then egg again. The basin has taught them that speed is the only sanctuary.

Above them move the herds—shaggy, red-eyed, moss-backed things that look like landslides with opinions. Their shoulders are higher than a man’s head; their hooves are wide, soft, and faintly luminous so they can feel the difference between living root-mat and the thin crust that hides a methane pocket. They never stop moving. To stop is to be swallowed by the growth that surges behind them. Their mouths are full of sideways teeth and fermenting leaves; their stomachs rumble louder than their bellows. When a herd stampedes it sounds like the basin clearing its throat. Trees explode behind them. New ones are already two meters tall before the last hoof has passed.

None of these are the oldest residents.

The oldest residents wear the roar the way a hermit crab wears a shell.

They are tall, between two and three meters, and they move as though the air itself is a thick medium they must part with their chests. Their bodies are narrow and slightly bowed, like wetland trees that have leaned into the prevailing wind for centuries. What looks like clothing is actually their own outer tissue—living felt that grows outward in dense overlapping scales of moss, liverwort, and slow chitin. Water sheets off them in perfect curtains; they are never not weeping.

Where a face should be there is a resonance chamber. The skull has been almost entirely replaced by an intricate lattice of bone and artifact-derived crystal that funnels sound inward and outward at once. Their mouths (if the term still applies) are vertical slits lined with vibrating membranes that never fully still. Even when the creature is resting—which is rare—the membranes flutter at sub-audible frequencies, tasting the basin’s constant thunder the way a snake tastes the air. When they choose to speak deliberately, the sound that emerges is not loud. It is *precise*. A single clear note that cuts through the avalanche of noise like a scalpel made of ice. Everything nearby pauses for half a second. Then the roar rushes back in, changed.

They have too many arms. Or perhaps the right number for what they do. Secondary limbs unfold from the moss-felt when needed—long, boneless things tipped with delicate fronds that can sift through collapsing root-mat, coax a failing monolith back into alignment, or stroke the forehead of a dying herd-beast with something that looks almost like tenderness. The primary arms end in black glassy claws that are clearly descended from the same material as the obsidian monoliths. Light still bends at their edges.

These beings do not have a single name for themselves any more than the basin has a single name for rain. Outsiders who have survived long enough to ask have heard three different answers in the same hour:

- *We are the place remembering it has a voice.*
- *We are the wound trying to sing.*
- *We were left behind to slow things down. We failed. Now we make it beautiful instead.*

They do not build. They *conduct*. You will come upon what looks like a cathedral made of living willow and obsidian lattice grown together so perfectly it is impossible to tell where artifact ends and organism begins. The structure will be humming. One of the tall moss-backed figures will be standing at its center, arms raised, membranes singing six separate frequencies at once. Around it, moss-walls calve in perfect rhythm. Sinkholes open and close like obedient lungs. A dying monolith flares back to life and throws off rings of concentric hyper-growth that collapse again exactly when the conductor lowers its arms. Then the being walks away without looking back, already singing a new song for the next collapse a kilometer farther on.

Their young are terrifying.

Born live during the moment a peat dome ruptures, they emerge as wet, luminous larvae the length of a forearm. They have no arms yet, only the resonance chamber already fully formed and screaming for attention. An adult will scoop the larva up and press it into the moss-felt of its own body where it fuses, drinking sound and minerals directly from the parent’s bloodstream until it grows its first pair of limbs. Some adults carry seven or eight larvae at different stages. The weight makes them walk slower. The additional voices inside their own bodies make them sing richer, more complicated music. They seem content with the trade.

The oldest ones no longer walk.

You will find them rooted beside the most ancient artifacts, bodies half-transformed into lattice and living stone. Their resonance chambers have become the artifacts’ new resonators. Ferns grow from their shoulders. Whole colonies of thumb-sized moths live in the folds of their moss-felt and emerge in slow, orchestrated clouds when the being exhales a particularly profound note. They do not appear to die. They simply sink deeper into the feedback loop until the distinction between custodian and wilderness disappears.

Sometimes, on the rare occasions when the clouds part and a sliver of watery sunlight reaches the basin floor, one of the rooted elders will lift what remains of its head and direct a single clear tone upward. For three full seconds the roar around it organizes into something that might be music in a language that hurts to remember.

Then the clouds close again, the roar returns, and the elder lowers its head, satisfied.

The Weeping Basin is not a place that contains life.

It is a place that is learning how to scream with purpose, and the tall moss-backed conductors are the part of the scream that has learned how to listen to itself.
