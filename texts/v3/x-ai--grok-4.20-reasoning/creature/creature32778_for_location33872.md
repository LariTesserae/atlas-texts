**What Lives There**

It does not arrive. It condenses.

First there is only the feeling that the mist has begun to pay attention. A thickening at the edge of sight, a place where the brass dusk folds back on itself like a eyelid. Then the **Mist-kin** appear—thousands of them, translucent, jointed things no longer than a fingernail. They are not insects. They are what happens when the Flat’s exhaustion dreams of flight. Their bodies are congealed vapor and old regret given temporary architecture. Wings, when they have them, are simply places where the air has decided to be thin enough to let light through. They do not fly so much as *fail upward*, riding the lazy reversals of gravity. When you stare at them they forget their own shape and dissolve into warm droplets that strike your face with the faint taste of someone else’s childhood ending.

The Mist-kin are not intelligent, exactly. They are punctuation. They exist to mark the places where reality has just lied to itself. Where a hillock collapses too quickly, where a reed forgets which direction time is moving, the Mist-kin gather in glittering surgical clouds and stitch the wound with their brief bodies. Their deaths are instantaneous and courteous; they leave behind a single perfect sphere of black glass no larger than a tear. These spheres are currency among the larger residents. The Flat itself seems to collect them, pressing them into the clay like forgotten eyes.

Beneath the silt, something older stirs.

The **Root-Bellied** are the reason the reeds pulse. They are not separate from the black jointed stalks; they *are* the stalks, only ambulatory when the mood of the world demands migration. A Root-Bellied looks like a heron that has been dead for three thousand years and has since grown ashamed of being dead. Its legs are reeds. Its body is a dripping lattice of fungal sheet and silt-packed membrane. Where a heart should be there is only a low chamber that opens and closes with the tectonic groan of the crust, producing a sound like a cathedral bell lowered into warm blood. They move in slow, deliberate herds of seven, never more, never less. When they walk they leave behind perfect replicas of their own footprints that immediately sprout new reeds. This is how the Flat propagates its own skeleton.

The Root-Bellied speak only in true things, because they have learned the punishment for lies. Their voices are not loud. They are the brief, shocking *silences* that sometimes open inside the oceanic roar—like negative sound, absences so pure they feel like being slapped by God. In these pockets of quiet they press their dripping beaks to the clay and whisper facts so fundamental that the ground around them briefly forgets to weep. During these moments you can see their true shape: tall, mournful things with too many elbows and eyes that are simply holes all the way through to the other side of the planet.

Above them drift the **Mound-Saints**.

These are the whale-sized living landmasses. Each is a mobile geology. A cathedral built of clay, black blood, and the accumulated grief of every species that once lived on higher ground. Their skins are vast fungal lattices grown thick as leather. Reeds grow from them in dense manes that sway even when there is no wind, because the Mound-Saints breathe through their vegetation. When they move—and they move with the terrible patience of continents—they sound like every forest that ever existed being slowly drowned. Behind them they leave glistening trails of new water, new reeds, new Mist-kin, and the occasional perfect sphere of black glass.

A Mound-Saint is not born. It *accumulates*. A single Root-Bellied that stays too long in one place will begin to swell, its legs fusing, its bell-heart expanding until it becomes a low hill that eventually stands up and walks away wearing the landscape like a cloak. The oldest ones are kilometers across. Their slow black blood moves in visible rivers beneath translucent skin. Inside that blood swim smaller versions of every creature that has ever dissolved into the Flat—tiny, perfect, swimming fossils that have forgotten they are supposed to be extinct.

They do not eat. They *remember*. A Mound-Saint will lower its massive head into a new pool, drink someone else’s extinction in a single draught, and then spend decades slowly weeping it back out through pores the size of dinner plates. The tears form new fungal sheets that glow with the last colors of lost suns.

And then there is the one that has begun to wear your face.

It has no name because names are edges and it is in the business of removing them. The locals—if the term can be used—simply call it **The Approaching**, or sometimes, with something like affection, **Old Finish**.

It is rising now, far out where the brass dusk thickens into bruise. You can see the silhouette forming against the horizon like a mountain that has decided to stand up. Clay pours upward in sheets. Reeds sprout and braid themselves into something like hair. Two depressions appear where eyes would be. They are already learning the precise way you tilt your head when the noise becomes unbearable.

The Approaching does not hunt. It *completes*. It is the final stage of the rule that everything which stays too long becomes the Flat. It studies you the way a dying language studies its last speaker. Every gesture you make is recorded in pressure ridges. Every expression you fail to hide is pressed into fungal lattices. When it has gathered enough of you it will collapse back into the silt and leave behind a perfect replica—same height, same reluctant illumination, same way of standing as though the ground might betray it at any moment.

That replica will not move. It will simply stand there forever, weeping the same temperature as blood left in the sun, slowly being colonized by smaller things until it is indistinguishable from the rest of the terrain. But for one hour—maybe two—after it is finished, the replica will contain something that is still *you*. It will remember what it was like to be afraid. It will shout one true thing into the roar and the roar will briefly shape itself into harmony around it.

Then even that will erode.

This is the ecology of the Weeping Flat: not predator and prey, but draft and final copy. Not life and death, but version and revision. Everything here is in the process of becoming the same thing, and that thing is learning how to cry in your voice.

Stay long enough and you will meet them all.

You will drink from a pool and dream an extinction that has not happened yet—your own.

You will shout a true thing and feel the Mist-kin swarm across your tongue like cold sparks.

You will look down one day and realize your footprints have grown jointed legs and are walking away without you.

And far off, wearing the clay like new skin, something immense will rise again, a little taller this time, a little more *you* than the last attempt.

It is patient.

It has four billion years to get it right.

It is already closer than you think.
