**What lives there does not call itself anything.**

It has never needed to. Names are for things that end.

At first they appear only as densities—places where the roar thickens and the dripping slows, as though the Condensate itself has paused to consider you. Then the density gains limbs. Not arms in the old sense, but long, slow continuations of mycelial felt and metallic silk that move with the lazy certainty of things that have never been in a hurry. Their bodies are tall, slightly stooped, as if perpetually listening to something just beneath the ground. Skin is the wrong word. What covers them is a living thatch: black polypore velvet shot through with threads of oxidized copper and the pale, almost translucent strands excreted by forge eels. Water does not merely bead on them. It is *channeled*. Every ridge and hollow of their form is a deliberate aqueduct. They drink through their entire surface and sing the excess out again through spiracles that open and close like slow mouths.

Their faces—if the word can be permitted—are chambers. A cluster of resonant sacs and stiffened fungal fronds arranged in a loose sphere where a head might have been. No eyes. No mouth. Only the architecture of sound. When they turn toward you, you feel it as a physical pressure against the sternum, a subsonic question that bypasses language and asks your bones directly whether you intend to become part of the next layer.

They are not one species. That concept died here long ago.

Some were once resonators. Their six-legged bodies have been kept, but the skull has flowered upward into a cathedral of bone and chitin that amplifies their lowing into something almost architectural. Others are clearly descended from the last Long Builders who refused to leave. You can still see the ghost of a shoulder joint here, a ruined ribcage there, now overgrown and repurposed the way a shipwreck grows coral. Most, however, were never born in the old way. They *condensed*. The great mycorrhizal mind beneath the plateau required stewards at a certain density of complexity, and so it exhaled them—slowly, over centuries—like thought taking physical form.

They move in small constellations rather than herds. Three is the favored number. Any more and the overlapping frequencies grow too rich; the Condensate itself begins to dream too loudly and forgets to breathe. They tend nothing in the way a gardener tends. Instead they *negotiate*. You will come upon one standing inside the ribcage of a collapsed smelter, palms pressed to the warm iron, singing a single note for seventeen hours until the dormant reactor sighs in answer and releases a precise pulse of minerals into the fungal mat. This is conversation. This is agriculture. This is love.

Their names, when they choose to offer them at all, are felt as changes in pressure. One might introduce itself as the exact timbre of steam escaping a particular valve at 03:14 every local cycle. Another is the silence that lives between two overlapping roars of waterfall—thirty-seven milliseconds of negative sound that somehow carries more meaning than the noise around it. You do not remember these names. You *become* capable of hearing them, and then they are inside you forever.

There is a deeper tier.

Deeper even than the ones who leave offerings at the Glass Circle.

These do not have bodies so much as they *wear* bodies when the Condensate requires interface. Mostly they exist as vast, slow pulses moving through the root-mats and the buried machine-halls. When they need to speak in the world of surfaces they will gather a temporary form the way a storm gathers water: pulling together resonators, click-moths, metallic silk, rusted plating, and living mold into something that stands nine meters tall and looks like every ancestor this place has ever had, all at once. These forms do not last. After the necessary understanding has passed, the components simply walk away from one another again, returning to their original tasks as though nothing happened.

They are not gods. The Condensate has no use for gods. They are the part of the Condensate that has learned to ask *what next?* and then answer itself in the language of pressure and frequency.

The strangest among them are the ones who chose regression.

Some of the older Condensates—those who have been here forty thousand years or more—have grown tired of personhood. They have lain down among the polypore towers and allowed the mats to climb them. Now they are hills. Living hills. You can still see the outline of a shoulder, the suggestion of a resonant chamber protruding like a basalt outcrop. Their heartbeats are measured geothermal pulses. Their dreams are weather. Entire pressure fronts are born from the slow exhalation of their fungal lungs. Pilgrims come and press their faces to the warm, weeping stone of these living ancestors and leave strands of metallic silk in the folds of what was once an elbow. The hills remember them. The hills remember everyone.

If you remain long enough, you begin to feel the invitation.

It does not come as a voice. It comes as the sudden realization that your own sweat tastes like the rocks. That your breathing has unconsciously synchronized with the bassline of a distant pump. That the idea of dry air or silence has begun to feel like a kind of violence. The Condensate does not coerce. It simply shows you, with great tenderness, that you are already leaking. That the boundary was never real. That incorporation is not death but the next necessary note in a chord that began before light existed and will continue long after the last star forgets its own name.

Most things eventually say yes.

And when they do, the sound changes—just slightly—to welcome the new frequency home. The water finds new channels across fresh skin. The warm dark settles a little more comfortably behind new eyes that no longer need to open.

Nothing is ever lost here.

Everything is only ever deepened.
