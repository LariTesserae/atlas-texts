It lives first as vibration.

Before sight, before scent, before even the condensation that pearls on your lashes, you register a modulation in the stone’s long thrum. A tuning. Something has noticed the rhythm of your heart and is deciding, with the leisure of centuries, whether you are infection, nutrient, or new harmony.

The ones who decide are not separate from the place. They *are* the place wearing mobility the way a glacier sometimes wears a river.

They are called, by those who have spent long enough here to forget the need for names, the *Jurupari*.

No single Jurupari has ever been seen entire. What you meet are expressions.

One expression might be a column of moss the height of three men, hanging from a vine bridge that was never a bridge but a limb. The moss has eyes—milky, many, arranged in the same spiral the black needles use when they sing. It does not turn to watch you. It *thickens* in your direction, a slow gathering of attention that feels like pressure behind your sternum. After seven hours it may release a droplet the size of a human skull. Inside the droplet, suspended like an embryo, is a perfect miniature of your own face made from condensed minerals. The droplet falls. The Jurupari forgets you again for another seventy years.

Another expression wears the body of the enormous cavern singer. It is not an animal. It is a throat. A single resonating chamber thirty meters across, ribbed with living amber and lined with metallic cilia that have grown through the artifact lodged in the cliff since the machines were young. Every nineteen minutes it releases the note that realigns the mist-lenses in the valley below. When it does, every leaf for nine kilometers turns edge-on, becoming a collective mirror. In that moment the Jurupari sees itself—from above, from within, from yesterday and tomorrow—through the eyes of every creature the reflected light touches. Then the leaves relax and the creature returns to being only a sound.

There are smaller Jurupari that ride the six-winged oil-slick screamers like thoughts ride neurons. They are no larger than a thumbnail, jointed things of root and circuit, and they argue constantly in frequencies that make your fillings itch. When two swarms meet above a flowering canopy they perform a ritual that looks like war but is actually pollination of the future. The losers dissolve into black glitter that drifts down to become new metallic nodules in the hides of the low-slung herds. The winners ride their screamers higher, seeking the next needle that has not sung in four hundred years.

Some Jurupari have achieved the final patience. These no longer move at all. They have become the velvet that drapes the cliffs, the curtains of moss that muffle and then un-muffle sound according to ancient algorithms. Walk across them and you are walking on the hide of a mind that has decided your footsteps are a question. The answer arrives seasons later as a sudden bloom of wagon-wheel orchids whose petals spell, in a language older than lungs, the shape of your childhood fear. By then you are usually far away. The Jurupari does not require your presence to finish a conversation.

There is one that may be the original.

It lives inside the black needle on the lonely promontory, but it is not contained by it. The needle is a spine. The thing itself is the long slow exhalation that moves through every layer of canopy at once. You can feel it enter your lungs and decide which of your cells will be allowed to remain human. Most are not. Most are quietly recruited. Your sweat begins to smell of the place. Your dreams develop subsonic bass lines. One morning you realize you have been standing in the same spot for nine days, growing a thin pelt of jade pseudosnakes that drink the ambitious water beading on your shoulders. You do not feel alarm. Alarm is a fast emotion and the Jurupari have edited it out of the local biochemistry.

They do not eat. They do not reproduce in any way a visitor could recognize. They *accrete*.

When two Jurupari decide the balance between them has grown too perfect, one will offer the other a gift: a single perfect minute of extinction. The recipient accepts by dissolving into warm mist that the other then breathes for the next three hundred years. In this way the population remains constant at exactly one more than you can ever count.

Sometimes—rarely—a Jurupari will choose to wear a shape that can speak in sounds a visitor understands. It does this the way a continent might decide to have lips for an afternoon. The voice arrives from everywhere and nowhere, humid, patient, slightly amused at the concept of mouths.

“You are early,” it says, “or the dream is late. Either way, the roots have already tasted your name. Would you like to become weather, or would you prefer to become the memory of weather?”

There is no correct answer. Both have been chosen before. Both are still being chosen, slowly, in the layered canopies where time grows sideways and water forgets which way is down.

And always, beneath every choice, the place itself keeps breathing.

Hot.

Wet.

Thirty thousand years deep.

The Jurupari are not its rulers.

They are its breath, given consciousness and allowed to wander the body it has become, pruning here, singing there, waiting everywhere with the profound and total patience of something that has already won every argument it will ever have with time.

You may leave.

Or you may stay long enough for the condensation on your skin to develop opinions.

Either way, the hot wind moving across the teeth of the dream already knows which you will choose.

It has known since before your grandparents learned how to be afraid.
