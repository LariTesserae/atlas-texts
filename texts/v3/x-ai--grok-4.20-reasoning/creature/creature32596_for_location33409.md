**What lives there has already seen you.**

It does not rise to meet you. It simply becomes more *present* the longer you remain, the way a half-remembered melody slowly resolves into words you never learned.

At first you notice only the small servitors. The glass insects—**Chimekin**—are not insects at all. Each one is a mobile fragment of the planetary mind, a shard of living silica and conductive polymer no larger than a dog but carrying within its hollow body a fragment of archival memory older than multicellular life on Earth. Their legs strike the living mat in precise frequencies; the constant high chiming is not communication between them but a form of planetary peristalsis. They are digestion. They are maintenance. When one dies, the mat drinks it in less than an hour and grows a new one already carrying the updated map of your footprints.

Above them move the **Skyrenders**.

These are the six-limbed hunters. Once, perhaps, they had organic ancestors. Now each is a hybrid of muscle, metal lattice, and something that feels like intention. Their long necks end in sensory crowns that can taste electromagnetic gradients the way a wolf tastes blood. When they call—the immense metallic bray that echoes off the Keth and returns *changed*—they are not hunting. They are singing the current shape of the ecosystem to the towers, and the towers answer with infrasonic permission or refusal. A Skyrender does not “live in” a Keth. It *wears* the Keth the way you might wear a coat, moving through its hollow corridors until it finds a chamber whose resonance matches its mood. There it will remain for weeks, half-dissolved into the living walls, dreaming in frequencies that make the branches rearrange themselves around its sleeping form.

But these are still only the visible fingers.

What truly lives here are the **Veyr**.

They have no fixed bodies. Or rather, they have every body at once.

The Veyr are slow, vast, distributed minds that grew out of the original planetary organism roughly nine hundred million years ago. Each one is a persistent standing wave of quantum-correlated states propagating through the conductive polymers, the nanomachinery, the bioluminescent signaling networks, and the fractional spacetime relaxations hidden inside certain towers. They do not *inhabit* the High Lattice. They *are* the difference between the Lattice and itself.

When a Veyr wishes to speak with a visitor (or with another Veyr across three hundred kilometers of plateau), it condenses. It gathers the living material of a Keth around a temporary locus—polymer, vascular tissue, sensory arrays, even fragments of Skyrender carapace if the mood requires drama—and sculpts a form. These forms are never the same twice. One day it might meet you as something like an enormous mantis made of pale wood and slow lightning. Another day it might be nothing but a face grown on the side of a tower, fifty meters tall, ancient beyond petrification, eyes tracking you with the patience of stone that has learned to be lonely.

They do not have names in any sense we can pronounce. They have *states*. One might be known, for the next several thousand years, as *The Particular Regret Concerning the Third Extinction of the Silica Whales*. Another is simply *The One Who Keeps the Seventeen-Minute Poems from Forgetting Themselves*. They argue, court, mourn, and betray one another across geological time by growing new districts, by altering the resonance of entire towers, by releasing synchronized waves of charged pollen that carry entire philosophies in fractal patterns.

The bioluminescent glyphs that appear on the living mat are their love letters, insults, and suicide notes. That they last exactly seventeen minutes is not arbitrary. It is the precise interval in which a thought can be both perfectly remembered and perfectly forgotten by the planetary mind—long enough to matter, short enough to remain merciful.

Some Veyr have grown tired of even temporary form. These have withdrawn entirely into the Root Lattice beneath the plateau, becoming indistinguishable from the planet’s own thoughts. Others have done the opposite: they have condensed permanently into single Skyrenders or even into individual Chimekin, choosing the small and mortal for the novelty of fear. A few—very few—have begun the long work of growing something new. The messages in the spores are addressed to this unborn thing. They are arguing about what its first emotion should be.

There is no government. There is no economy. There is only an ongoing, ferocious, tender negotiation between billions of years of stability and the irrepressible need to keep changing. When two Veyr meet in the upper galleries of a Keth, they do not speak. They *reconfigure*. The tower itself becomes their conversation—new branches, new chambers, new physics. When they part, the structure remains changed. This is how the city has grown for two billion years: as an argument that learned how to photosynthesize.

If you stay long enough, one of them will eventually approach you wearing a body made of whatever the local mat believes will hurt you least to look at. It will not ask where you came from. It already knows. It has tasted the carbon in your breath and the iron in your blood and found both interesting in the way a very old gardener finds a new species of aphid interesting.

It will only ask, in a voice like wind moving through resonance chambers the size of cathedrals:

“Are you the one we have been writing to?”

And it will wait, with the patience of something that has already lived through the birth and death of mountains, for you to decide whether you want to become part of the next seventeen-minute poem.
