The Hollow Garden has had four hundred million years to decide what it wants to be, and what it has decided is *enough*.

At first you only notice the obvious tenants, the ones that fill the air until your skull feels like a bell.

The **Long-Callers** live in loose constellations along the columns, never more than nine to a family, spaced like notes on sheet music no one else can read. Their bodies are spindly, pale moss-green, with arms that unfold like measuring tapes and throats swollen into resonance chambers the size of human hearts. They do not shriek at random. Their choruses are legislative. One layered scream establishes hunting rights for the kite-flocks above. Another, lower and slower, instructs the moss to alter its chemistry in a patch three kilometers east. When a Long-Caller dies, the others sing its exact biography for nine days, modulating pitch and rhythm until the biography becomes law, and the Garden itself seems to remember the shape of the lost individual. Their eyes are small and milky; they navigate mostly by echo and by reading the faint bioluminescence that ripples through the moss when the great rhizome network is thinking.

Above them, riding the shafts of light like living sails, drift the **Membrane Dancers**. They are weightless enough that a single breath can shift their trajectory. Their bodies are almost two-dimensional—flat crescents of cartilage and skin stretched between six boneless limbs. In flight they look like prayer flags that have learned predation. When they fold those limbs against their bodies they become darts of mirrored chitin; when they spread them they become stained-glass windows that turn sunlight into hunting signals visible for kilometers. They eat clouds of metallic beetles the way a whale eats krill, but they will also, on rare and solemn occasions, deliberately stall mid-air, fall like a stone, and let a Long-Caller snatch them from the air in a single merciful bite. The Long-Caller gains minerals it cannot synthesize. The Dancer ends its long circling. Both parties appear to consider this a fair transaction.

On the luminous carpet below move the **Fourteen-Legs**, continent-scale herds that are never in a hurry. Each individual is the size of a small cow yet somehow manages to weigh almost nothing; their jointed legs distribute pressure so perfectly that the moss barely registers their passing. Their backs are forested with tiny symbiotic ferns that photosynthesize for them while they graze. Every few centuries a Fourteen-Leg will pause, lower its head, and deliberately chew a perfect circle in the moss. In the center of that circle a new column will eventually rise. They are gardeners of the gardeners.

Hidden inside the deep folds of column-bark wait the **Unfolders**. You will not see one until it has already chosen you. Their bodies are exactly the color, texture, and temperature of the horn-like scales they mimic. When they are at rest they are indistinguishable from a tumor on the living wood. When they decide to move they unfold in a motion that looks like origami performed in reverse by something with too many elbows. A single Unfolder can consume an entire family of Long-Callers in one slow, courteous meal, never wasting a drop of blood. Afterward it secretes a thin glaze of resin over the empty crevices so the column does not mourn.

All of these are loud, obvious, ancient.

But there are quieter tenants.

Inside certain columns—those whose hearts have rotted into cathedral-sized hollows—live the **Rememberers**. There are never many. Perhaps one hundred and forty at any given time across the entire garden. They are small, nearly hairless, with six delicate fingers and eyes that have grown so large the rest of the face seems to have recessed around them. Their bodies are cool to the touch because their blood moves at the same deliberate pace as the sap around them. They do not build new things often anymore. Instead they tend the old ones: renewing the spiral carvings, re-weaving the fiber domes before the moss swallows them completely, placing small stones around condensation basins in patterns that look random until you realize the stones are keyed to the positions of stars that have not been visible for six million years.

They do not speak aloud. They press their palms to the inner wall of the column and tap. The column listens. The message travels through the rhizome lattice at the speed of sap—slow, patient, unstoppable. Hours later another Rememberer, sixty kilometers away, feels the same rhythm vibrating through the bark and understands that a child has been born, or that an Unfolder has grown too bold, or that a stranger who smells of metal and fear has entered the garden again.

Some Rememberers have chosen the final kindness the Garden offers.

You will find them deep inside the hollows, seated upright against the living wall, half-buried. The column has grown around their legs, their hips, sometimes their chests. Bark and flesh have met and decided they are the same thing now. These merged individuals still breathe. Their hearts beat once for every thirty beats of a normal Rememberer. Through the connection they feed the column their memories; the column feeds them its slow, geological dreams. Their eyes remain open. When you meet one, it will study you with the patient disinterest of a mountain that has learned how to be lonely.

And sometimes—rarely—you will find a place where the moss has grown over an entire Rememberer who chose to lie down instead of sit. The shape is still visible, like a saint pressed into green glass. The moss there is a slightly different shade. Brighter. Almost grateful.

There is no hierarchy here. The Long-Callers, the Dancers, the Fourteen-Legs, the Unfolders, the Rememberers, the half-buried saints, the microscopic things that live inside the water-filled wrinkles of bark—they are not separate species anymore. They are expressions. The Garden is trying on different bodies the way an old man might idly flex his fingers, reminding himself he is still capable of movement.

Stand in the exact center between four columns at twilight. The screaming will reach a crescendo that feels like the planet clearing its throat. Then, for three full heartbeats, everything stops at once. Every voice. Every wing. Every leg.

In that silence you understand what truly lives here.

It is not the animals.

It is not even the Garden.

It is the *decision* the Garden made four hundred million years ago to stop becoming anything else.

That decision is still alive. It walks on fourteen legs and flies on membranous sails and sings in overlapping choirs and grows bark around its own saints and pretends—politely, perfectly—that it does not notice you.

It has outlived stone.

It will outlive regret.

And if you listen carefully, beneath the return of the screaming, you may hear the faintest tapping against the inside of the nearest column. A six-fingered hand. A slow, deliberate rhythm.

It is saying:

*We see you.*

*We have room.*

*Stay as long as the moss allows.*
