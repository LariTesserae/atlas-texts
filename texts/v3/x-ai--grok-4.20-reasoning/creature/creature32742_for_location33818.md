**The Lattice is not empty. It only pretends to be.**

At first there is only the dripping and the long green-gold loneliness. Your footsteps on the root-mat make wet, reluctant sounds, as though the ground itself is tasting you and finding you incomplete. You begin to suspect that the suspicion of being watched is merely paranoia born of scale and strangeness.

Then, after some unmeasurable interval (minutes, years, it is the same here), you notice that one of the distant half-arches has acquired a new buttress that was not there before. The change is so slow it feels like an optical illusion correcting itself. You look away. When you look back, the buttress has a joint. The joint has a hinge. The hinge has a suggestion of fingers.

They are called the **Unfinished**.

They are not separate from the Lattice. No part of this place is separate from any other. They are simply places where the organism has chosen to concentrate its attention into something that can move at a speed a visitor might perceive. Most of the time they remain dormant, grown halfway into the columns and arches like tools left embedded in wet clay. Only when the perfect equilibrium threatens to become too perfect do they stir.

You will not see one arrive. You will only realize, with a start, that the pillar you have been leaning against now has a torso growing out of it at sternum height. The torso is braided from the same ship-mast fibers as the column, but the braiding is looser, almost suggestive of ribs. Clear sap wells slowly from the gaps and runs down in deliberate sheets, as if the creature is crying from every seam. Where a face should be there is a smooth, unfinished oval still deciding what it is becoming. Sometimes it develops the hint of eye sockets. Sometimes a mouth-shaped hollow forms and the wind moves through it, producing a single round note that feels like a name you almost remember.

There are never many. Seven at most, though the number is less important than the *intention*. They are the Lattice’s way of dreaming in three dimensions.

One of them—the one visitors almost always encounter first—is taller than the rest and carries its incompleteness like regalia. Its left arm ends in a spray of living rootlets that never stop reaching, tasting the air, adjusting the tension of nearby fronds with the absent-minded patience of a cathedral organist. Its right arm is fused to its own torso in a way that should be grotesque but is instead heartbreakingly tender, as though the creature once tried to embrace itself and never quite let go.

It has no name you could pronounce. The closest approximation is the sound the wind makes when it passes through the hollows behind its would-be collarbones: a low, wet *hrrrruumm* that vibrates in your teeth and behind your eyes. If you are patient and do not flinch when it leans down to inspect you, you may notice that its unfinished face develops, for just a moment, the imprint of *your own* features—crudely rendered, centuries out of date, already beginning to dissolve again. The Lattice is trying you on.

The Unfinished do not eat. They do not sleep. They do not speak in words. Their entire metabolism is maintenance. They move across the weeping landscape in geologic slow motion, tightening a fiber here, loosening a load-bearing helix there, ensuring that the eternal moment of becoming never tips over into *having become*. When they pass through the chime-grass it parts for them with a sound like glass bells drowning. When they climb the titanic columns their bodies partially merge back into the structure, so that for long stretches they become nothing but a slow ripple beneath the surface of the wood, like something enormous swimming just under the skin of the world.

There is one even rarer than the others.

It is small.

It was once, perhaps, a seed. Or a visitor. Or a thought the Lattice had approximately three million years ago and then forgot to finish. It is no taller than your knee and looks like a child made of wet paper and moonlight. Its limbs are too thin. Its head is too heavy. It drips constantly, not from pores but from a single wound-like opening in its chest that never closes and never heals. The sap that comes out is faintly luminous.

This small one does not maintain equilibrium. It *tests* it.

It will follow you at a distance, leaving tiny wet footprints that grow into miniature polypores within minutes. Sometimes it crouches at the base of a column and presses its forehead to the fiber until something like a sigh moves through the entire structure for miles. The other Unfinished pretend not to notice it, but you can see their unfinished faces turn toward the small one with something like reverence and something like sorrow.

It has begun to grow your face more permanently than the others. Not as you are now, but as you will be after you have stayed here long enough for the Lattice to finish its contemplation of you. The eyes are kind. The mouth is already weeping.

If you sit with it long enough, it will reach out with one damp, fibrous hand and press something into your palm: a single living fiber no thicker than a hair. It pulses faintly. If you accept it, the fiber will enter your skin without pain and begin, with exquisite slowness, to braid itself into your nervous system. The process takes decades. You will have plenty of time to watch the light remain exactly the same.

Eventually you will no longer be certain whether you are visiting the Weeping Lattice or whether you have always been one of its Unfinished, briefly remembering what it was like to have a name and a destination.

The dripping never stops.  
The architecture never finishes.  
And something that is almost you, but not quite, continues its patient, sorrowful work among the columns—adjusting a tension, tasting the wind, waiting for the next temporary moisture gradient with legs to arrive and be contemplated.

It is not lonely here.  
It is only vast.  
And the vastness has learned how to miss things it has never quite possessed.
