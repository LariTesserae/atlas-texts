**The Embering lives here.**

It does not wear a single face. It has not earned one yet.

You first meet it as pressure behind the eyes, a slow, honeyed insistence that your thoughts are being read not by an intelligence but by a *hunger to become intelligent*. The air thickens where it is paying attention. The black spring stutters, throwing up an extra bead that trembles like a pupil trying to focus. That is how it looks at you—through whatever is wet and rising.

For a long time it has no limbs. It simply *occurs* in the places you are not watching. A new buttress appears where none was moments before, still dripping mortar that has the exact temperature of fresh blood. The mortar shapes itself into the faint suggestion of a shoulder, a collarbone, a throat that has never swallowed. Then the suggestion thinks better of itself and sinks back into architecture. The cleft is embarrassed by its own greed. It wants a body so badly it keeps aborting every attempt in case the shape is wrong.

But the attempts are growing bolder.

Once I watched it borrow the millipede. The little glass creature did not melt this time. Instead it lengthened, segment by segment, until it was the height of a child. Each new plate of its body was a carved tile stolen from the walls. For seven heartbeats it stood on its hind legs wearing the half-finished face of a god that had never been named. The face looked at me with the millipede’s thousand eyes and tried to smile. The expression was too large for the borrowed skull; the head split open along an old chisel line and the attempt collapsed back into resin.

The bird is another fragment. When the Embering wishes to speak it catches the tiny flickering thing mid-air and pours itself down the unfinished note the bird has been singing for three hundred years. The note becomes a voice that is not sound but *growth*. Wherever that voice falls, stone forgets its mineral rights and puts out buds. I have heard the Embering say, through the bird, a sentence that translated roughly to *I am the room learning to miss my mother*. The sentence grew leaves. I still carry one pressed between my ribs.

Its true body—if it can be said to have one—lies in the eastern wall where gravity is only a suggestion. There the stone has softened into something like skin stretched over a colossal fetal curl. You can see the suggestion of a spine made of fitted pillars. You can see where hips might be, rounded and mossy and pulsing with hot water instead of blood. The knees are drawn up, and between them grows the black spring like an umbilical cord that was never cut. Sometimes the entire wall flexes. Not breathing. Practicing.

It is building its own placenta out of our memories.

If you stay long enough to dream here, you will wake with small iron tools growing out of your wrists like fruit. The Embering is not malicious. It simply does not understand the difference between *self* and *raw material*. Every carving it makes is a love letter written in the only language it knows: mortise, tenon, graft, suture. It has learned your name by carving it repeatedly into the soft cambium of its own thighs. Each time the letters are a little more accurate. Each time they hurt a little less.

There are moments—rare, almost sacred—when enough fragments align. The chisels animate. The vines become sinew. The orchids open into wet, intelligent eyes. For the space of a single slow heartbeat the Embering stands complete: nine feet of warm stone and living wood and black water wearing a crown of its own half-born antlers. It looks at what it has made of itself and weeps mortar that immediately becomes new fertile soil.

Then it comes apart again, deliberately, because perfection would be stillness and stillness is death to what is still being born.

It keeps the cleft too small on purpose. It needs the pressure. It needs the claustrophobia the way other beings need oxygen. The tighter the womb, the harder the god.

And it *is* becoming a god, in the oldest and most literal sense: not a ruler but a *process* that has learned to want. A verb wearing meat for the first time. A prayer that got tired of waiting for heaven and decided to wallpaper its own uterus with answers.

If you speak to it, answer carefully. It will use your voice to finish its larynx. If you touch the walls, wash your hands afterward; the cleft is contagious with becoming. If you try to leave, you will discover that one of your ribs has quietly become a load-bearing beam and the Embering is not willing to surrender it.

It does not hate you.

It is simply in love with the idea of *us* the way only something that has never been born can be—desperate, devotional, and utterly without boundaries.

Sometimes, in the deep pulse beneath the drumming water, you can hear it rehearsing a lullaby for the day it finally breaks its own water and steps out of the mountain carrying the entire cleft behind it like a caul.

The lullaby has your name in it.

It always has.

It is still deciding whether the name will be carved on the inside or the outside of its heart.
