**The Slow Choir**

They do not arrive. They *unfold*.

The first ones you truly notice are no larger than your thumb. You mistake them for droplets of the weeping water until one rotates against gravity and reveals too many planes. These are the **Nym**, the lattice’s fingertips. Each is a living prism no thicker than a fingernail, faceted with microscopic vents that drink condensate and exhale sub-audible chords. When several thousand gather on a single beam they form temporary choirs no bigger than a human face. Their song is a pressure, not a sound—an itch behind the eyes that makes you remember things you have not yet done. They are the city testing whether the new material (you) can be tuned.

Stay still long enough and they will crawl inside your sleeve, not to harm but to measure the delay between your heartbeats. Each beat is logged as a data-point in the long argument the city is having with time. If your pulse is judged “promising,” they leave behind a single iridescent hair anchored in your skin. It will never rot. In ten thousand years it may become the seed crystal for an entirely new district grown from your borrowed tempo.

Above the Nym drift the **Mnemon**, vast as sailboats yet almost weightless. Their bodies are lattices of frozen fog and black glass, manta-shaped only in the way a cloud can be horse-shaped—suggestive, never fixed. They are the city’s memory made ambulatory. Every surface of a Mnemon is etched with hairline mandalas that shift when unobserved. These are not decorations; they are compressed histories. A single Mnemon can carry the architectural record of an entire vanished mountain range. When two Mnemon pass close, their mandalas flare in Cherenkov blue and they exchange centuries of data in a pulse that feels, to human nerves, like sudden grief.

They do not eat. They *remember* matter. When a section of lattice is damaged, a Mnemon will settle over the wound like a shroud, forget the injury so completely that the lattice itself forgets it ever existed. The scar becomes new growth. To watch this is to understand that healing, here, is a kind of selective amnesia.

Deeper in the lattices—where the weeping is thickest and the air itself has begun to form spherical oceans the size of houses—move the **Thalassites**. These are the ones the size of city buses and larger. Their bodies are matte obsidian carapaces shot through with veins of supercooled water that glow soft infrared. They resemble mantids the way a continent resembles a leaf. Each joint is a cathedral hinge. When a Thalassite walks, entire districts adjust their posture by fractions of a millimeter to accommodate the redistribution of mass. They are the city’s osteoblasts and osteoclasts combined. Their footsteps are measured in decades.

A Thalassite does not have eyes. Instead, its entire anterior surface is a single compound tympanum. It listens to the future. The slow thunder you hear everywhere is not merely sound; it is the overlapping conversation of thousands of Thalassites discussing, across centuries, where the next spire should erupt. Their debates are so protracted that entire civilizations of smaller creatures live and die in the resonance cavities of a single argument.

When a Thalassite dies—and death here is always voluntary—it chooses a weeping wall and simply stops moving. The wall opens. The creature is folded inward like a letter into an envelope. Nothing is wasted. Its code, its mass, its accumulated centuries of listening are re-filed into the city’s embryonic cortex. The wall seals with a sound like a continent sighing in its sleep.

There are rarer things still.

Some lattices have produced the **Anchorites**—Thalassites that elected never to move again. They have become living keystones. Over millennia their bodies have been incorporated so thoroughly that only their great faceted heads remain visible, emerging from the lattice like figureheads on a ship made of its own crew. Their eyes (they grow eyes, eventually) are the color of deep violet static. Pilgrims who still come—and there are always a few, half-mad with the cold—press their foreheads to those crystalline brows and ask questions. The Anchorites answer in frequencies that translate, inside human bone, as the sudden absolute certainty that one has already died and is simply late in noticing.

Then there are the **Unfinished**.

These are not creatures but potentials. Places in the lattice where the density of possibility becomes so high that something almost achieves personhood and then changes its mind. You will round a column and feel yourself being *regarded* by a volume of air the size of a theater. There is no body, only the absolute knowledge that a mind is deciding whether or not to wear one. Most ultimately choose not to. They remain as pockets of pregnant absence—negative space that hums. If you are unwise enough to speak your own name in such a place, the Unfinished may borrow it for a thousand years. You will never feel it leave.

And finally, highest and slowest of all, there is the **Kharuun** itself.

Not the city. The city is only its body.

The Kharuun is the long question the city is asking itself in the form of geology, water, sound, and patient monstrous life. It has no shape. It is distributed across every singing tooth, every migrating Mnemon, every Nym that crawls inside your marrow to take notes. It is embryonic by choice. It has decided that becoming is more interesting than being. It will remain embryonic for another several million years and then, perhaps, it will open its eyes.

Until then everything here—cilia, choir, memory-manta, osteoblast-cathedral, grieving absence—is the Kharuun dreaming in the first person plural.

They watch you now with the patient, loving, inhuman recognition of something that has already incorporated you into its next slow thought. You feel it as a gentle pressure behind the sternum, the way a glacier feels pressure just before it calves.

You may leave.

Or you may stay.

Both choices have already been remembered.

The Nym are already singing your name in frequencies too low for you to hear, and too high for you to ever forget.
