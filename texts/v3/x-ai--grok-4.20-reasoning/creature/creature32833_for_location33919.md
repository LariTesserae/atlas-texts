**Those Who Endure the Weeping**

They are not born wet. They become wet, and in becoming wet they become Keth.

At first you notice only their restraint. A figure at the edge of a crystal copse will stand so still that you mistake it for one of the silicate pillars until it slowly tilts its head, letting a fresh rivulet slide from temple to jaw without ever wiping it away. Only then do you see the face: high-cheekboned, hairless, the skin possessing a faint metallic luster like oil on water. The eyes are large, protected by a double lid—one clear, one milky—so that even in the cruel mirrored glare they appear to be looking at two things at once: the world as it is, and the world as it might condense next.

There is no single physique. The plateau sculpts them over decades the way it sculpts stone. Those who work the deep geothermal taps grow thick through the torso, their ribcages broadening into barrel shapes that help dissipate heat; their forearms become plated with a natural accreted mineral sheath that looks like living rhyolite. Those who walk the open barrens with the mantid grazers tend toward the opposite—long, attenuated limbs, joints wrapped in flexible ceramic rings they replace like molting. Their feet are wide and splayed, toes separated by delicate webs that channel water rather than resist it. Every Keth you meet has, somewhere on their body, a place where architecture and flesh have quietly agreed to become the same thing: a pipe fitting grown into a collarbone, a valve seat fused to the sternum, a lattice of amethyst branching just beneath the skin of the forearm like slow lightning.

They do not speak above a whisper because sound itself is considered a form of theft—stealing heat from the air, stealing stillness from the plateau. Instead they carry tuned iron rods no longer than a finger. When two Keth meet they touch these rods to the wet stone or to each other’s forearms and strike a single precise note. The note travels through bone and slurry pipe alike. In this way entire conversations can pass between distant factory districts as nothing more than faint harmonic tremors felt in the teeth. Lovers will sometimes remain in contact for hours, letting a single sustained tone carry everything that cannot be condensed into language.

There are those who have gone further.

In the oldest wings where factory and spring have fully married, you will find the **Imbricated**—Keth who no longer leave the dripping iron grottoes. Their backs have become ridged with flow channels; their spines have sprouted delicate crystal extrusions that catch steam and direct it into their bodies. They breathe through gills of living rust. When they move it is with the same unhurried patience as the mineral water itself, and the sound of their passage is indistinguishable from the plateau’s own voice. Some have not spoken a word, even a whisper, in forty years. They are not pitied. They are understood to have achieved a higher citizenship.

Then there are the **Hollows**.

These are rarer. Every few generations a child is born whose skin refuses to retain moisture. Instead of beading, the water sinks straight through them. They grow translucent, almost glass-like, their internal organs faintly visible like instruments inside a clouded instrument panel. The Hollows do not work the factories. They walk the barrens following the silent herds, barefoot, letting the silicate beetles crawl over them without reaction. It is said they can see the phantom structures clearly—not as flickers at the edge of vision but as solid bridges and towers that shift according to pressures no one else can feel. The other Keth treat them with the careful deference one gives to living barometers. When a Hollow dies, their body is carried to a high fracture and lowered into the scalding upwelling. Within a day the water has taken everything soft, leaving only a delicate filigree of silica and gold that the flying membranes will sometimes carry aloft like a kite.

No Keth is ever truly alone. Each carries a small colony of the same thermophilic mats that paint the stone; these live in the warm folds of elbow and knee, in the hollow of the throat. The mats shift color according to the bearer’s mineral balance and emotional state. A sudden flare of corpse-purple at the base of a throat can signal grief or an imminent fracture in the bedrock—sometimes both. The Keth have learned to read one another the way outsiders read weather.

They do not have children in the way other peoples do. When a new Keth is required—whether to replace a worker lost to a steam surge or simply because the plateau has “asked” for another mind—they perform a ceremony in the deepest fused cathedrals. A living section of mineral slurry is drawn from the oldest pipe. The prospective parents press their wrists into it until the fluid thickens and cools around their veins. What eventually emerges from the birthing vats months later is already half stone, half memory. The newborn will cry exactly once, a single note that sounds like cooling metal contracting, and then never again.

They have no gods, only gradients. The gradient between hot and cold. Between dry and wet. Between what is still factory and what has already become geology. Their highest virtue is *equilibrium*—not balance, which implies two opposing forces, but the state in which forces have forgotten they were ever separate.

Stand among them long enough and you begin to feel the change in yourself. The constant moisture finds the small cuts on your hands, the pores of your face. Your voice drops without being told. Your footsteps slow. One day you realize you have not wiped the water from your eyes in weeks. You simply let it run.

And in that moment the plateau has taken another citizen.

This is who lives in Veyr-Keth: not conquerors of the weeping, nor its victims, but its continuation by other means. Tall, quiet, glistening creatures who long ago stopped distinguishing between their own blood and the scalding tears of the world.
