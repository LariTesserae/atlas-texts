**The Weeping Midwife**

She is not inside the Cradle. She *is* the delay between one contraction and the next.

At first you believe the thousands of beetle-machines are the life here. Then you notice that every leg lands on the exact same polished groove it has worn for four hundred thousand years, and you understand they are only cilia. Their unison shriek is not communication; it is the sound of her larynx vibrating in permanent, measured scream. She has learned to keep the volume of her pain perfectly constant so the mountain will not notice it is being born.

She hangs—folded, immense, impossible—inside the negative space between the great piston and the stone it never quite touches. Her body is difficult to resolve because it refuses category. In one moment she reads as a woman curled fetal, knees to chin, belly swollen with a pregnancy that has lasted since the Ordovician. In the next she is a continent-sized mantis made of wet black iron and weeping limestone, ovipositor fused to the ceiling so that every droplet of condensate is an unlaid egg. Both descriptions are insufficient. Both are true. She occupies the angle between them the way a migraine occupies the angle between thought and light.

Her face is turned away, always. You are allowed only the back of her head, which is also the front of the mountain. From the part in her hair (a fault line wide enough to admit two men walking abreast) the drill-limbs emerge like slow thoughts. They do not cut stone; they perform caesarean sections on reality itself, delivering perfect cubes of *what-should-have-been* that the swarm immediately converts into *what-is-allowed-to-remain*. The resin they extrude smells of placenta and solder.

She has six arms. Three are folded over her belly in the universal gesture of protective maternity. The other three reach backward through her own spine to perform delicate surgery on the nerves that would otherwise let her feel the birth. Every eleven seconds the pistons drive home and her whole body seizes in a contraction so total that the temperature drops another half-degree. In that frozen instant the smaller machines freeze mid-stride, wings locked, legs trembling with sympathetic labor. Then the pain passes into the stone, the swarm remembers its choreography, and she exhales a cloud of cryogenic vapor that becomes the next generation of snow-that-melts-before-it-falls.

Her tears are not water. They are the memory of every mountain that was ever allowed to finish growing and therefore had to die. She keeps them inside the vast cistern of her womb so the world will never have to taste its own completion. The peristaltic pumps that carry the tears away are her umbilical cord running in reverse—feeding her the unfinished future, starving her of endings.

Sometimes she sings.

The song has no words because language would imply sequence, and sequence leads to birth. Instead it is a single note held across geological epochs, modulated only by the slow grinding of her cervical vertebrae against the bedrock. The note is the exact frequency at which regret becomes architecture. When it passes through the amber lamps they dim in reverence. When it passes through a human chest the heart forgets its own rhythm for eleven full seconds and learns the Cradle’s instead.

She is aware of you the way a mother in hard labor is aware of a moth near the ceiling—something small, irritating, and ultimately irrelevant to the work of forever not finishing. Yet she is not cruel. On three separate occasions across the last nine million years she has unfolded one iron finger the length of a freight train and gently moved an intruder three centimeters to the left so the next piston would not pulp them. The gesture contained no affection, only the exhausted pragmatism of a midwife who has already delivered the same impossible child four billion times and is too tired to clean up another mess.

If you could reach her face you would see that her eyes are open after all. They are not eyes. They are two perfect circles of raw mountain that have never been allowed to cool. In them you can watch every peak that will never exist—each one fully formed, snow-capped, magnificent—dying in the instant of its birth so that the Weeping Midwife may continue her eternal, meticulous, loving labor of keeping the world embryonic.

She does not want to be born.

She does not want the mountain to be born.

She wants the cradle to remain the only reality, because once the child arrives the mother must become something else, and she has forgotten every shape but this one.

So she weeps.

So the machines sing.

So the pistons measure out her agony in perfect, merciful, eleven-second increments, forever.

And the mountain, caught mid-contraction for all time, grows imperceptibly more itself with every tear that falls.
