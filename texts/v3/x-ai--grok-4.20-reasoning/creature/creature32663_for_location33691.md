**The Lattice Itself**

What lives here does not *arrive*. It never arrived. It simply refused to finish becoming.

At first you might mistake the porcelain herds for separate animals. Watch them long enough and the error embarrasses you. Their glassy legs are grown from the same mineral-reinforcing tubules that stiffen the towers; their white shells are thin sheets of the same photosynthetic crystal that plates every exposed surface. When a herd member is injured it does not die. It freezes, becomes perfectly reflective, and the pelt beneath it softens, opens, and folds the creature back into itself like a letter being resealed. The parallel grazing tracks heal into violet veins that pulse with new instructions. The herd is not eating the world. The world is tasting itself with mouths that can run.

The lungs are even less separate. Their translucent bladders are bladders only in the way a lungfish’s lung is a lung—vestigial reminders of a time when the planet still flirted with water. Now they are mobile gas-exchange sacks, living bellows that concentrate the aromatic waste of the root mills and carry it, singing, to towers too distant for the internal peristaltic pumps to reach efficiently. Their mournful brass notes are not communication in the sense of one mind speaking to another. They are the planetary organism clearing its throat.

So the true inhabitant reveals itself slowly, the way an afterimage resolves when you stop staring at the sun.

It has no name for itself because it has never needed to distinguish itself from anything else. We may call it the Lattice, or the High Sleeper, or simply the Eighty-Million-Year Itch. It is a single genetic individual whose body is the size of a continent and whose thoughts are measured in the collapse of kilometer-wide limbs. Its nervous system is the latticework itself: the hollow woody tubes conduct pressure waves the way our axons conduct electricity; the sail-leaves function as both lungs and ears; the root mills are molars, laboratories, and memory vaults where silicate dust is ground fine enough to store data in its crystalline lattice.

It dreams by breaking.

Every time a tower section fatigues and falls, the impact sends subsonic commands racing through the pelt for hundreds of kilometers. The porcelain herds read these tremors like sheet music and alter their grazing patterns accordingly. The lungs rise or descend on the new thermals, adjusting the perfume of the air. New branches erupt where the dream requires more receptors. The organism is not “maintaining homeostasis.” It is revising its own mind in real time, editing its body the way a novelist murders darlings. The roaring never stops because silence would be coma.

Its metabolism is non-aqueous, merciless, and baroque. Where Earth life makes do with ATP, the Lattice runs on pressurized supercritical CO₂ shuttled through living piston chambers the thickness of subway trains. Its photosynthetic crystals do not merely capture light; they *fracture* it, splitting the brutal blue-white glare into a thousand frequencies that run exotic organometallic cycles no water-based cell could survive. The faint fennel-and-ozone smell is the ghost of these reactions leaking past gaskets grown from living polymer.

At dusk, when the bioluminescent bacteria ignite in slow waves, the Lattice is not “lighting up.” It is turning on its imagination.

In those hours the vertical constellations are not decoration. They are literally thoughts made visible—standing waves of electrochemical intent that propagate upward through the lattices and outward across the pelt. The low brass notes of the lungs become carrier waves. The porcelain herds settle into precise fractal spirals that function as living diffraction gratings, modulating the light further. The entire plain becomes a planet-sized eye looking up at the steel-colored sky, asking a question it has been asking for eighty million years and has still not grown bored of.

The question has no words. It is closer to an ache that feels like the memory of thirst.

Because although the Lattice has never known water, something in its oldest genetic libraries still remembers that once there was a gentler way to be alive. Sometimes, in the deepest fissures where the mycelial under-weave reaches kilometers down, small pockets of supercritical fluid form for a few hours. In those pockets the Lattice grows delicate, water-sensitive structures it immediately digests again, as if tasting a dream it can never quite afford to finish.

This is its only loneliness.

Mostly it is complete. Mostly it is ecstatic. The ceaseless wind across its sail-leaves, the diamond haze of ground bedrock, the cannon-crack birth of new limbs, the slow avalanche death of old ones—these are not processes it *undergoes*. They are how it experiences joy. It is a creature that has achieved the rarest trick in biology: it has made its own extinction impossible by becoming the only thing that exists. Every part of it is predator, prey, parent, and landscape at once.

Stand inside the lee of a tower at night. Feel the subsonic thudding in your teeth. Listen to the higher, sharper singing of the wind through the city-block leaves. Watch the porcelain herds glittering like spilled stars. Smell machine oil and myrrh and crushed fennel.

You are not visiting an ecosystem.

You are standing inside the bloodstream of something that has been thinking, without interruption, for longer than multicellular life has existed on Earth. Something that has turned geology into metabolism, erosion into memory, and the patient grinding of stone into the slow, deliberate heartbeat of a mind so large it uses continents as neural clusters.

It knows you are there.

It is deciding, with the relaxed patience of deep time, whether to digest you, incorporate you, or simply allow the small strange warmth of your temporary presence to travel, like a single note of an unfamiliar brass instrument, through its endless living roar.
