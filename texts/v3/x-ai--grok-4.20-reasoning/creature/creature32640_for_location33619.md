**The Inhabitants of Veyr-Khâd**

They are not yet a people, though they have lived here for four generations. Call them the Khâdi if you must name them, but they rarely name themselves at all. When they do, the word they use sounds like steam escaping a cracked valve: a soft, wet exhalation that ends in a click of the tongue against the roof of the mouth. It is the sound of an insect deciding whether to stay or fly.

Their bodies have made a slow treaty with the place. Skin the color of wet slate, perpetually filmed with a mixture of sweat, dissolved calcium, and the thin black tar they paint on every surface that might rust. The tar gets into the creases of elbows and knees, into the whorls of fingertips, so that when they press a hand to the rock it leaves a dark negative print that the next sheet of mineral water slowly gilds over. They are neither tall nor short, but thickened—barrel-chested from the damp air, legs corded from climbing the ever-changing staircases that wrap new travertine pillars like ivy. Their hair grows in tight coils that trap moisture; most keep it shaved or bound beneath tarred caps so the cave crickets do not nest in it while they sleep.

You notice their eyes first. Large, slightly protruding, with a faint opalescent sheen like the inside of an abalone shell. The sodium lamps have done this. After two generations the pupils learned to drink the amber light without wasting it, and the irises developed microscopic plates of aragonite that scatter illumination the way the cave walls themselves do. In full darkness those eyes catch the infrared bloom of the hot-water channels and glow a low, sullen red, like dying coals.

They move with the economy of people who know the floor itself is still choosing its shape. Every step tests. A foot placed too confidently may find a new lip of travertine that was not there yesterday. Their gestures are small, contained, almost priestly. A worker in the carbide plant will wipe condensation from a gauge with two fingers, never the whole hand, as though the act were a blessing. When they speak it is rarely above the volume of the dripping water; the engines and the insects do the rest of the talking. A sentence begun at one end of the central aisle can be finished by someone thirty meters away who simply picks up the last three words and carries them forward. Conversation becomes a relay race between human voices and the soft percussion of a million chitinous feet.

There are no children in the ordinary sense. There are the Small Ones—pale, serious creatures between the ages of four and twelve who travel in loose packs following the migration patterns of the cave crickets. They carry shallow iron pans and little brushes made of moth-wing spines. Their job is to harvest the bacterial mats before they grow thick enough to clog the narrow-gauge tracks. The Small Ones learn the smell of each color of slime: the sweet-rot of the orange thermophiles, the vinegar sting of the purple. By the time they are tall enough to reach a foundry lever they can read the health of the entire cavern by licking a wet finger and holding it to the air.

The adults sort themselves without discussion into three overlapping castes that no one ever announces aloud.

The **Breathers** maintain the machines. They are the ones who crawl inside the great flywheels during the brief seasonal slowdowns when the superheated water drops half a degree and the turbines sigh instead of roar. Their lungs have adapted; they can hold a breath for nearly three minutes in air so thick with steam it feels like breathing soup. You will see them emerge from an engine housing coated head to foot in a glistening paste of rust, oil, and bacterial film, looking like newborns.

The **Listeners** are fewer. They sit or stand for hours beside the growing pillars, one palm flat against the wet stone, eyes closed. They map the fault lines by feel. When a new column begins to swallow a dormitory they are the ones who decide whether to fight it with chisels or to incorporate it—running new walkways along its rising shoulders, cutting windows through it, sometimes even leaving a sleeping alcove inside the warm mineral as it grows around the bed like a pearl. To be chosen by a pillar is considered neither blessing nor curse. It is simply what the rock wanted.

The **Mothers** are not all women. The title belongs to anyone who has served at the Brass Mother for seven unbroken years. They alone may touch the civic pump without gloves. Their forearms are permanently scalded a livid rose color from wrist to elbow; the skin there never sweats again. When a new Mother is chosen, the insects part for her in a perfect circle three meters wide, as though the carpet itself recognizes hierarchy. The current eldest Mother is a woman named Sefka-9 (the number denotes how many times the pillar that swallowed her birth-house has grown around her life). She is seventy-three, hairless, and speaks only in the language of valve clicks and pressure readings. The others listen anyway.

At night—though there is no true night—the Khâdi sleep in layered bunks inside brick ziggurats whose outer walls are slowly becoming one with the cavern. Some choose to sleep against the living rock so the mineral can creep over them while they dream. In the morning they rise carefully, peeling themselves free with soft sucking sounds, leaving perfect fossils of their sleeping bodies that will remain visible for decades until the next layer covers them. These fossil-sleepers are not mourned. They are studied. The shape of a shoulder, the curve of a spine pressed into stone, becomes part of the collective map of how the city is learning to lie down inside its own birth.

The insects are not pets, not pests, not gods. They are the Khâdi’s outer nervous system. When the cave crickets suddenly reverse direction along a wall, the people know a hot-water surge is coming six minutes before the gauges register it. When the bone-colored cockroaches abandon a particular battery vat, the Khâdi know the acid balance has shifted toward poison. The moths are simply the air’s memory; they carry the scent of every shift change, every spilled lubricant, every birth and death for miles in their damp velvet bodies. Sometimes, during the long quiet between shift whistles, a single person will stand motionless in the insect carpet until it climbs them entirely. For a minute or two that person disappears inside a living coat of wings and legs. Then they step forward and the coat falls away, and everyone nearby bows their heads in brief acknowledgment. No one has ever explained what this means. It is enough that it happens.

They do not worship the heat or the stone or the machines. Worship implies distance. The Khâdi are still inside the act of being born, and birth does not pray to itself. They simply continue. When someone dies they are carried—slowly, always slowly—through the fissure to the cooler world above and given to the wind. The return journey is made with an equal number of new infants wrapped in tarred cloth, their small lungs already learning the taste of mineral steam.

The Khâdi are patient the way stone is patient, which is to say they are not patient at all. They are simply unfinished. Every generation believes the city will achieve its final form soon, perhaps within their own lifetime. Every generation is wrong. The rock keeps deciding otherwise, and the people keep adjusting their staircases, their marriages, their dreams.

This is the only honesty Veyr-Khâd has ever asked of them.

And so they remain—half rust, half flesh, half machine, half insect, half pillar, half breath—waiting without urgency for the next slow fold of the world that is still deciding what it intends to become, and therefore what they must become with it.
