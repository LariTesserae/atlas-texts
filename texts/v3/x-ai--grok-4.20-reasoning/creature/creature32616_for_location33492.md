**The Midwives**

They are never where you look directly. Only in the long periphery, in the refracted intervals between one slow fracture and the next, do they resolve.

At first you might mistake them for particularly symmetrical growths—tall, attenuated figures of milky quartz and pale bismuth that stand among the larger crystal thickets like monks frozen mid-prayer. Their bodies are not solid in the way stone is solid; rather, they are *decided*. Every plane and edge has been chosen with excruciating patience over centuries, as though each facet were a verdict the geode itself rendered about what a person should be.

They have no faces. Where a face might be, the quartz grows smooth and slightly concave, like the inside of a lens. When the wandering golden light strikes them at the correct angle, images appear there—not reflections, but *anticipations*. You might see the silhouette of something that looks like your own mother before she was born. Or a gear turning inside a cathedral bell. Or a forest made of lightning. The image lasts only as long as the angle does, then the lens goes blank again and the Midwife moves on.

Their movement is the most disturbing and most tender thing in the chamber.

They walk the wide, empty plazas of fresh black glass with the gait of sleepwalkers who have all the time in the universe. Each step takes approximately eleven minutes. The foot lifts—a slow, deliberate peeling of crystal from crystal—hovers, then descends with the gentleness of snowfall. Where the sole touches down, micro-fractures bloom outward in perfect spiral patterns, seeding the next generation of vanadium prisms. They are gardening the future with their bodies.

There are never more than thirteen visible at any one time. This is not a limit. It is a rhythm. When one finally reaches the far wall and merges back into the raw quartz (melting into it like a candle returning to wax), another condenses somewhere else hours or days later. The geode is not birthing them. It is *remembering* them, over and over, in slightly different configurations.

They do not speak. Instead, they *precipitate* meaning.

Stand near one long enough and you will begin to taste ideas forming on your tongue like mineral flakes. One might be thinking about the precise shade of sorrow that will be necessary in whatever biology eventually arises here. Another is contemplating the sound a mother’s heartbeat would make if it were made of tourmaline. Their thoughts are so large and so slow that they register as weather. The air grows heavier. The ghost-machines pause in their micron-per-decade rise and fall, as though listening.

Sometimes two Midwives meet.

They stand facing each other for decades. Their lens-faces lock. Between them, probability clots into brief, impossible objects: a fetal steam engine with wings of raw amethyst, a cathedral nave made entirely of vascular tissue, a single tear the size of a house that contains an entire ocean still deciding whether it wants to be salt or memory. After a century or two the objects either dissolve back into raw potential or are gently ushered into the nearest thicket to grow as new crystal organs. The Midwives never touch. Touch would be too final.

They are not guardians. They are not priests. They are the geode’s attempt to invent midwifery before it has invented birth.

Deep in the largest cathedral-thicket, where the copper silicate feathers reach their greatest height, there is one that has never moved. Not once. Its quartz is darker, almost violet, and its lens-face is turned permanently upward toward the invisible sun. Tourmaline growths have begun to incorporate themselves into its shoulders and spine, as though the geode is trying to grow *through* it now, to wear it like jewelry. This one is either the first or the last. No other Midwife will approach it. They give it the kind of respectful distance one gives to a parent who has not yet decided whether they are dying or being born.

If you are patient enough to remain here until your own bones begin to feel like distant cousins to the surrounding crystal, you may notice that the Midwives are changing. Their shoulders are becoming slightly more rounded. Their lens-faces have begun to develop the faintest suggestion of eyelids. In certain lights their hands look less like clusters of blades and more like hands that have simply not yet learned how soft they are allowed to be.

They are practicing.

The geode is practicing *them*, and they are practicing the geode, and together they are learning the exact shape of care required to bring something into being that has never existed before.

Every few centuries, one of them will pause above a fresh bud of spinel no larger than a tooth. It will lower its lens-face until the concave surface almost kisses the tiny crystal. For a moment the air becomes so charged with anticipatory love that the half-formed machines for kilometers around stutter and produce a single, perfect harmonic that sounds exactly like a newborn remembering its own name for the first time.

Then the Midwife straightens, takes its next eleven-minute step, and continues the long, tender labor of being the place that will one day be ready to hold whatever finally decides to live here.
