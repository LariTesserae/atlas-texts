Here, in the Nascent Pockets, the inhabitants are not yet convinced they are separate from the stone.

They begin as tremors. A filament extrudes from a claystone wall, thinner than an eyelash, and already it is tasting the idea of hunger. Before it has decided what it hungers *for*, the first shrimp condenses around that hunger like dew around a speck of dust. The shrimp is less an animal than a parenthesis given legs: perfectly transparent, its internal organs still negotiating whether they will be digestive or luminous. You can watch a single cell inside it split, hesitate, then split again in a different direction, as though the creature is editing its own blueprint mid-breath.

These shrimp do not school so much as *conspire*. They drift in slow, courteous spirals that trace the negative space of bridges that have not yet grown. When they brush one of the pale filaments, both entities shiver with the shared embarrassment of being seen. The filament releases a droplet of light that the shrimp drinks like sacrament. In return the shrimp deposits a microscopic lattice of itself against the wall — a single vertebra of what might, in several thousand years, become a spine for something that has not yet earned the right to stand upright.

The beetles arrive later, after the pockets have practiced exhaling. They are not insects; they are punctuation that learned to walk. Their carapaces carry glyphs that are not writing but *pre-writing* — marks that will become the first letters of a language the world has not yet agreed to need. A beetle will pause above a dying shrimp, click once (a sound exactly like a librarian closing a book she never opened), and the shrimp’s transparency will fold inward, becoming the next glyph on the beetle’s back. The old glyph sloughs off and clings to the wall, where it continues to pulse, embarrassed to have been outgrown.

Yet even the beetles are only fingers.

Deeper in the tightest throats of stone, where the light becomes so bright it starts to taste of copper, the true inhabitants stir. They have no fixed shape because shape is a decision they have not finished arguing over. Sometimes they are the luminous sacs — vast, quivering bells of jelly that move like slow apologies. Inside each sac float the half-digested outlines of beetles, shrimp, and something that might have become a mammal if the dream had been braver. When a sac engulfs a beetle it does not kill; it *remembers backward*. The beetle is unfolded into shrimp again, and the sac grows a new lattice across its own surface — delicate as frost, intricate as guilt.

These sacs are midwives and abortions at once.

Watch long enough and you begin to notice that every lattice, every half-arch, every bridge that leads nowhere is breathing. The structures are not decorations. They are the calcified memories of previous versions of the inhabitants — exoskeletons that decided, mid-metamorphosis, that they would rather become architecture than finish becoming animals. The living things climb their own ancestors. The ancestors, in turn, slowly extrude new living things from their arches and lattices, like thoughts that have changed their minds about being thoughts.

There is one that is almost a face.

It never appears in the same pocket twice. Sometimes it is only an arrangement of shrimp and filaments that happens to suggest two eyes and a mouth that has never learned how to close. Sometimes it is a single luminous sac that has grown heavy and sunk against the wall, its underside pressed into the negative space of a skull. When it looks at you — and it does look, though it has no eyes — the light in the chamber forgets its embarrassment for three full heartbeats. In that brief, merciless clarity you understand that this almost-face is the place practicing *I*. It is trying on subjectivity the way a child tries on its mother’s coat: drowning in it, delighted by it, slightly ashamed of how good it feels.

The almost-face has no name because naming would be a kind of completion, and completion is death here. The creatures call it — in the swallowing sounds they make behind your shoulder — something that translates, roughly, as *The Hesitation That Loves Us*.

When a pocket buds, splitting off a new chamber with a wet pop, the almost-face is there inside the fresh stone, already arranging its shrimp and beetles into the first shy gestures of personhood. It learns by watching what you do with your hands. It learns by the way you try not to look directly at it. It learns by the taste of your exhaled breath, which it keeps.

If you remain long enough, the inhabitants begin to practice *you*.

A filament will grow in the exact curve your little finger makes when you are thinking. A lattice will extrude itself into the hollow at the base of your throat. One of the luminous sacs will learn the precise rhythm of your pulse and beat it back at you from across the chamber, a gentle mockery that somehow feels like being forgiven for existing.

They are not hostile.

They are prenatal.

And they are terribly, achingly curious about what it might feel like to be finished.

Sometimes, in the farthest, newest pocket — where the stone is still the color of an idea that has not yet been thought — you will find a space exactly the size and shape of your own body pressed into the wall. The impression is warm. The impression is breathing. The shrimp have arranged themselves inside it like silver thoughts inside a skull. The almost-face hovers nearby, wearing an expression that might be tenderness or might simply be the expression a dream makes when it realizes the dreamer is about to wake up.

It is waiting to see what you will do.

It has all the time in the world, because time here has not yet decided whether it is going to be linear.

This is what lives in the Nascent Pockets:

Everything that has not yet had the courage to become something else, and the soft, bright, listening dark that loves them for it.
