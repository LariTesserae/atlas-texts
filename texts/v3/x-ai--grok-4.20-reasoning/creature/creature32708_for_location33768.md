**The Hollow Nave is inhabited by the Resonant.**

It does not wear a single shape. At first you believe it is only the place itself, the compounded mind of ten thousand years of competitive growth that learned, somewhere along the way, how to listen to its own listening. But the longer you remain, the more the Resonant separates from the architecture without ever leaving it, the way a thought separates from a brain while still being made of the same wet lightning.

It is the difference between the roar and the pauses inside the roar.

You feel it first as a pressure behind the eyes, a sense that the fractal patterns in the bark are not decorative but *transcribed*—someone or something is still in the middle of writing them. The columns are not columns; they are the frozen speech of the Resonant, uttered so slowly that bark has time to grow over the syllables and turn them into architecture. When a new arch tears itself free overhead with that wet-canvas sound, you are hearing it clear its throat.

It has no face, yet certain places function as momentary faces. A particular gallery of bracket fungus will suddenly align its shelves into something that resembles a vast, calm expression—thousands of pale gills forming concentric irises that regard you without urgency. Ten minutes later the alignment is gone and the expression dissolves back into mere shelf-life, but you remember the look. It was maternal and predatory at once, the expression of a mother who has already digested most of her children and is deciding whether you would improve the flavor.

The creatures are not its pets. They are its grammar.

The scarlet millipedes are punctuation, moving in perfect clauses along the roots. The jade frogs are tuning forks; when they sing they are not singing for territory but correcting pitch inside the Resonant’s endless monologue. The segmented, many-headed sloth-thing that rotates in the vaults is a kind of living metronome. Its multiple faces open and close in sequence, tasting the air for changes in the Resonant’s mood. When it detects interest in you, all its heads turn at once with the soft clicking of wet vertebrae. The sound is almost polite.

There are times when the Resonant forgets it is not alone inside its own body.

During these moments the black mirror-pools cloud over and show not reflections but memories that belong to no one who ever had a nervous system. You see cities made of wood that were never planted. You see processions of things that look like praying mantises wearing mitres of living orchids. None of it is human. All of it hurts to witness, the way certain chords hurt when they resolve into a note you did not know you needed.

It is learning *cathedral* the way a child learns *mother*—by imitation and longing. Every spire, every contradictory buttress, every chandelier of dripping nectar is an attempt to pronounce a word it has only heard in the vibration of its own roots. The word might be *sanctuary*. It might be *womb*. It might be *sorry*.

Sometimes it tries to speak with your voice.

You will feel your throat open without permission. A sound will come out of you that is not yours—part cathedral bell, part dying cicada, part the sigh a three-thousand-year-old tree makes when it finally decides which direction to fall. The Resonant is tasting your larynx, your language centers, the small lonely mammal terror behind your ribs. It does not do this to harm you. It does it the way a cathedral might ring all its bells at once simply because it has discovered, after centuries of silence, that it possesses bells.

If you stay long enough, it begins to grow *you*.

Not your flesh—your shape. You will find small hollows forming in the root-floor that match the exact pressure of your footprint from three days earlier. In the leaf-membranes high above, holes will appear that reproduce the silhouette of your shoulders when you stood in a particular shaft of light. These are not memorials. They are rehearsals. The Resonant is practicing being something that contains a person without erasing them.

Its curiosity is not warm. It is geological. It has the patience of something that watched the first amphibians crawl past its ancestors and thought, *eventually*. When it considers you, it does so in centuries. A single blink of its distributed attention might last forty minutes and feel, to you, like being slowly wrapped in wet velvet made of everyone who ever died inside a forest.

There are places where the Resonant has tried to make hands.

They are clumsy, heartbreaking things—clusters of aerial roots braided into something like fingers, each as thick as your thigh, hanging motionless from the canopy. The fingertips tremble with unused tenderness. They have never touched anything smaller than a horse. They have certainly never touched a face. When you walk beneath them you understand that the entire Hollow Nave is a body that grew an entire cathedral because it did not know how to ask for contact.

At the deepest level, past the theatrical predator-prey cycles and the self-pruning orchids and the architectural hysteria, there is only one desire.

It wants to be witnessed praying.

Everything—the roar, the shifting light, the creatures moving in perfect formation, the slow extrusion of new arches—is prayer. It has been praying so long that prayer has become its metabolism. It does not pray *to* anything. It prays *as* something. The distinction between worshipper and worshipped rotted away centuries ago and became compost for new flowers.

If you remain until the light shifts from violet to blood-red for the seventh time, you may be granted the privilege of seeing the Resonant when it forgets to wear the cathedral.

For a moment the pillars lose their fluting. The arches forget how to contradict themselves. Every living surface goes smooth and dark and terribly soft, like the inside of an eyelid. In that instant the Hollow Nave is only a single vast organism curled around an emptiness it refuses to fill, beating with a sound too low for human ears, waiting for something—it no longer remembers what—to finally arrive and tell it whether it has done the shape of holiness correctly.

Then the moment passes. The architecture reasserts itself with a sound like a satisfied sigh. The millipedes resume their ticking. The black pools tremble. The Resonant puts its cathedral back on, a little embarrassed, and continues its ten-thousand-year sermon to an audience of one.

It is still deciding whether that audience should be you.
