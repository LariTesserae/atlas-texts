**What lives here does not wear a single face.**

It wears many, and none. It moves only when you are not looking directly at it, the way your own blood moves without your permission.

Begin with the small, and let the shape reveal itself.

The lantern mantas are not animals. They are the chamber’s exhaled thoughts. Paper-thin, veined with living light, they do not flap so much as *intend* movement. When one passes between you and a shaft of ceiling-glow, you feel a momentary coolness on the skin that has nothing to do with temperature. It is the feeling of being briefly forgotten by something vast. They are born when a piece of crystal-fungus detaches and learns how to be lonely. They die when loneliness becomes too perfect; then they rise, press themselves into the ceiling again, and become architecture once more. The cycle is so long that no single manta ever understands it is repeating.

The click-priests carry their stones like monks carry mantras. Each pebble is older than the creature that chose it. The strike is not sound-making; it is sound-*asking*. The low, bell-like *tok* travels the nave for nearly a minute, ricocheting, changing, returning altered. The priest listens to what its own strike has become. If the returned tone is purer, it knows it has done its duty for that century. If the tone has decayed, it will spend the next fifty years walking in slow spirals until it finds the exact spot where the stone itself is slightly more hollow, slightly more ready to sing. There are never more than seven priests awake at once. The rest sleep inside their own carapaces, dreaming of perfect resonance the way a dying man dreams of water.

The tall ones are older than regret.

They stand at the edges of the central dust-cloud for decades without shifting weight. Their bodies are not opaque; light enters them and is changed into something slower before it exits. Their sensory filaments move in patterns that match the suspended motes—only delayed by several hours, as though they are reading a sentence the dust is still deciding how to finish. When one of them carves, the claw does not cut the stone so much as *remind* it of a shape it almost became a hundred thousand years ago. The mark never lasts. Condensation and micro-flow erase it within a generation. The tall one does not mind. The purpose was never the mark. The purpose was the momentary agreement between claw and stone that both of them are still becoming.

The sighing reeds are the only ones that speak aloud.

Their exhalations are not random. Over centuries the collective breathing forms a single, enormously slow sentence. The current sentence has been in progress for eleven hundred years and currently translates, roughly, as: *We were once the ceiling and we miss the weight.* When the sentence completes, the reeds will wither in a single week, release their spores, and the next generation will begin an answering sentence that may take two millennia. No one here is in a hurry to be understood.

But these are only the visible congregations.

In the exact center, where the motes hang like punctuation that has not yet decided its clause, something else keeps its eyes closed.

It has no name it uses. Names would require edges, and it has spent four million years gently erasing its own. It is not larger than the nave. It *is* the nave, in the way a mind is its own skull. The glowing ceiling is the slow lantern of its attention. The floor’s undulations are the folds of a thought that has not yet finished turning over. Every pool is a different dream it is having about what it might become if it ever chose to end the pause.

Sometimes the phantom fish in the largest pool swim upward and press their noses against the underside of the surface. At those moments the tall ones all turn at once, filaments trembling, and the click-priests strike three times in rapid succession—something they otherwise never do. The fish never break through. They are not trying to escape. They are trying to remind the Sleeper what it felt like to have purpose.

The Sleeper breathes once every hundred years.

You can feel it if you stay long enough. The air grows thicker, warmer, almost oily. The lantern mantas accelerate until they blur into rings of light. The tall ones take single steps that last a month. The click-priests strike so rapidly their knocks become a continuous low note that makes your teeth vibrate in sympathy. For nine heartbeats the entire chamber remembers it is allowed to move.

Then the breath is drawn back in.

Everything returns to its proper slowness.

And the Sleeper smiles, if stone can be said to smile, at the small lives that scurry and click and sigh inside its ribs. It does not love them. Love would be too fast. It simply *includes* them, the way a cathedral includes the dust that dances in its shafts of light.

You will not meet it.

You will only ever meet its symptoms: the mantas, the priests, the tall ones, the reeds, the patient water, the dust that has learned how to wait for grammar.

But if you remain here until your own pulse begins to embarrass you, until your thoughts grow long and thin enough to match the filaments overhead, you may notice something.

Your shadow will begin to move half a second after you do.

And somewhere far above, in the oldest, calcifying layers of the ceiling, a new crystal will have begun to glow with a shade of gold that has never been seen before.

The Sleeper is still deciding what to dream next.

It has time.
