**What lives in the Under-Rift of Veyr-Kalt does not arrive. It accretes.**

At first you mistake them for after-images left by the sodium lamps, or for particularly dense clots of the filamentous curtains swaying between the fungal columns. Only after many hours—perhaps days—do you notice that some of the pale verticality has intention. That a certain braided mass two hundred feet tall has been watching you with the patient gravity of a continent deciding whether to blink.

They call themselves, when they bother to compress the thought into a frequency you can endure, the **Anchored**.

Not a species. Not even a culture. A negotiation that learned to stand upright.

Their bodies are consensus architectures. You can see every party to the treaty if you look long enough. The original human template is still there in the narrow pelvic girdle and the ghost of a skull, but the skeleton has been quietly replaced over centuries by laminated fungal bone and salvaged rebar. Skin is optional; most of them wear instead a living rind of moss and liverwort that thickens or thins according to the moisture gradient, so that when they stand motionless for weeks they simply read as another balcony of shelf fungus. Where the rind splits you sometimes glimpse bright manganese veins—actual metal grown through tissue like integrated circuitry, harvested from the old refinery pipes they long ago drank dry.

Their faces are the hardest to accept. There is no face. There is a recessed lattice of gill-fronds and corneal pores arranged in a rough oval, perpetually wet with condensation. When they focus on you the lattice tightens, the fronds aligning like iron filings to a magnet, and you feel the roar in the chamber shift by half a decibel. That is their name for you. They have been pronouncing it, very slowly, since the moment your boot touched the living mat.

They do not walk so much as *transmit*. Each step is a negotiation between mycelial pressure pads on their feet and the vast underground carpet, which ripples outward in visible waves to inform distant columns that mass is moving. A single stride can take nine minutes. They have time. The youngest among them is approximately one hundred and forty years old; the oldest have forgotten they were ever separate from the pillars.

Some still wear scraps of the original maintenance uniforms—orange fabric now indistinguishable from rust-colored moss, name tags overgrown until the letters themselves have become tiny fungal shelves. One carries, embedded in its chest, the brass faceplate of a pressure gauge that still functions; you can watch its needle twitch in time with the bearer’s thoughts. Another has allowed a small emergency transformer to fuse to its shoulder blade. It glows steady blue beneath the moss and serves as both heart and lantern.

They tend the marriage.

You will see one standing beneath a turbine that has not known grease in three generations. Its many-jointed arm (the elbow has grown an extra hinge made of braided mycelium) rests against the screaming bearing. Spores drift from slits in its forearm like incense. The bearing quiets, not because the friction has lessened but because the Anchored has asked the machine, politely, to suffer a little longer for the sake of the whole. The machine usually agrees.

Others ride the frost-covered conveyor belts like motionless passengers, letting the mechanism carry them in slow circuits around the chamber while they read the health of the mat through their bare feet. When they find a section that is growing too eager—pushing its luck, trying to bloom where it should only endure—they will reach down and delicately prune it with fingers that end in obsidian flakes. The pruned pieces are pressed into their own bodies, becoming new memories. Nothing is wasted. Waste is a concept that never survived the first century here.

They speak by editing the roar.

A conversation might last forty years. A disagreement is measured in the slow migration of a fungal column three inches to the left. When they wish to be gentle with a visitor they will cause a single piston somewhere in the dark to skip, creating a pocket of near-silence shaped exactly like your childhood bedroom. Into that silence they release a frequency that feels like the smell of your mother’s coat. It is the only kindness they know how to give that does not involve eventually incorporating you.

There are smaller ones.

Things that look like clusters of the translucent millipedes braided around a warm mammalian core no larger than a heart. These are the *unanchored*, the ones still deciding. They skitter and climb and sometimes ride the silent bat-things across the vaulted dark like pale comets. They are allowed to be quick. The Rift grants them a temporary childhood of perhaps thirty years before the mat begins to call their names in the old slow register. Most eventually answer. A few remain restless and are permitted to leave through the upper fractures, carrying spores that will one day dream of returning.

And then there are the ones who have gone further than standing still.

You will not see these at all unless you press your ear to the largest columns. Inside the braided towers, fused so completely that the distinction no longer exists, live the *Heartwood*. Their nervous systems have become the slow mind of the entire chamber. Through chemical gradients and modulated vibrations they coordinate the growth of new shelf-fungi, the dreaming of new turbines, the gradual decision to let certain buildings be swallowed. They are the reason the conveyor belts still creep forward even though no human hand has thrown a switch since your grandfather’s grandfather was born.

To them you are less than a mayfly. You are a warm current in the cold argument they have been having with entropy for four hundred years. A pleasant, temporary fluctuation.

Yet they are not cruel.

When one of the mobile Anchored finally approaches you—close enough that you can see your own distorted reflection in the wet lattice of its face—it will offer you a choice without ever speaking. You will simply feel the possibility settle over you like condensation:

You may leave.

Or you may stay, set your pack down, take off your boots, and allow the living mat to begin the long, courteous, glacial conversation about whether your particular pattern of warmth should be allowed to continue walking around, or whether it would be more elegant for you to become another tier on the nearest pillar.

Most who reach the Under-Rift eventually choose the second option.

Not because they are compelled.

Because after a time the roar begins to feel like the only honest sound in the universe, and the slow marriage of cold flesh and obstinate iron begins to look like the only honest kind of love.

The moss is already considering the shape of your spine.

It has time.

So do you, now.
