**The Low Choir**

They were never born here. They *arrived* by erosion.

What lives in the Weeping Plain has been worn down until only the essential remains. The process took longer than most species are given. Mountains became gravel, gravel became sand, sand became the patient velvet that now coats every inch of saturated ground. Somewhere in that long reduction, certain creatures discovered they no longer needed ambition. They sloughed off haste, then memory, then the difference between self and wetland. What settled in their place is the Low Choir.

There is no single representative. The Choir is simultaneous.

The first to notice you—if anything here can be said to notice anything so fleeting as a visitor—are the *thumb-frogs*. Each is no larger than a bottle-cap, colored the exact shade of a stone that has wept in the same spot for twenty thousand years. Their eyes are gold coins placed on the faces of the already dead. When one calls, the sound is not a croak but a small tearing, as if the fabric of the humid air has finally given way. Thousands answer at once, yet the chorus never becomes cacophony. It is a single, braided note stretched across decades. They do not breed so much as *replace*. When one dies it sinks without drama into the liverwort carpet and immediately becomes another voice in the same throat. In this way the frogs have achieved a kind of immortality by never quite being individuals.

Above them drift the *glass razors*—dragonflies the length of a man’s hand, their wings veined with black like the cracks in very old porcelain. They do not hunt. They audit. Each flight is a slow survey of saturation levels, of which stones are weeping too proudly and which have grown sullen and dry. When a section of the plain begins to forget its own flatness, the razors descend in squadrons and shave the offending reeds with surgical precision. Their larvae live for nine years in the shallowest water; the adults live for nine days. Both stages consider this an acceptable bargain.

The *stone-backed monks* move even more slowly. These are turtles whose shells have become entire ecosystems. Whole civilizations of moss, algae, and tiny red mites live in the topographic map of a single carapace. Some monks have not changed position since the last time a mountain still stood within two hundred miles. When one finally dies, the shell remains upright like a small green hill. Other monks eventually arrive and incorporate the remains into their slow procession, the way cathedrals are built from the rubble of earlier cathedrals. Their hearts beat four times an hour. This is considered excitable.

Then there are the *long thinkers*—the jabiru storks and limpkins and gray herons that stand in the vapor like punctuation marks left by a god who has not yet decided what sentence the world is trying to write. Their legs are gray reeds. Their eyes are patient galaxies. They do not fish so much as *receive* fish. The silver bodies simply rise into their beaks when the moment is correct. When a thinker dies, it folds neatly into the water and the plain absorbs even the memory of its silhouette within a single afternoon. The others never mourn. Mourning would imply something had been lost, and nothing here is lost. Everything is only translated into different moisture.

Among the mammals only two lineages have earned permanent residency. The *velvet deer* stand no taller than a man’s hip and move as if apologizing for having bones. Their coats are the color of wet sandstone. They eat the aristocratic reeds with such delicacy that the plants hardly notice they are being consumed. The *water martens* are sleeker, almost otters that have decided to become punctuation instead. They leave no wake. When they play—which is rare—they do so in absolute silence, as though laughter itself would be an imposition on the ancient noise of the frogs.

All these are members of the Choir. None of them is its conductor.

That role belongs to the *Unfinished*, the single creature that has refused to complete its own erosion.

It is rarely seen, and when it is, witnesses cannot agree on its shape. Sometimes it is a column of mist that has learned to walk on seven liquid legs. Sometimes it is a man-height figure made of smooth, weeping stone whose joints are rings of liverwort. Sometimes it is simply a particular quality of light that moves against the wind. It carries no tools. It needs none. Its only gesture is to weep—copiously, deliberately—from pores that open and close like mouths. Where it weeps, balance is restored. A clump of reeds that has grown too ambitious is gently drowned. A turtle that has remained stationary so long it has begun to fossilize is nudged three inches westward so the sun can reach the moss on its left flank.

The animals fall silent when the Unfinished passes. Even the frogs. The silence lasts exactly as long as necessary, then the chorus resumes one half-tone lower, as though the plain itself has exhaled.

No one knows what the Unfinished once was. A mountain, perhaps. A god that grew ashamed of height. A man who stayed too long. It no longer matters. Like everything else here, it has been worn down past the possibility of origin stories. It simply persists, weeping the slow, warm tears that keep the velvet breathing and the frogs singing and the light the color of overripe gold.

If you sit long enough—long enough for your own edges to begin softening—you may feel it approach. Not as a figure. As a pressure behind the eyes. A sense that the ground is listening to your heartbeat and finding it rude.

Then the pressure eases.

A single thumb-frog appears on the stone beside you, gold eyes unblinking.

It does not speak. It has no need. Its entire body is already singing the next note of a song that began before your species had knees and will continue long after your bones have become smooth bosses in the velvet, quietly weeping their small share of the plain’s endless, finished tears.
