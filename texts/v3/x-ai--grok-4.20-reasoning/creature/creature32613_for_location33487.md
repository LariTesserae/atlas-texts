**The Remembrancers**

They are not immediately visible. You may spend a full turning of the light shafts—perhaps six hours—believing the Tracery belongs only to shrimp and fish and the blind eels whose moans roll between the columns like slow thunder. Then something catches in the corner of the eye: a verticality that is not quite a stalk, a place where the verdigris seems to have learned to breathe.

There are never many. Seventeen is the number most often whispered by those few outsiders who have been allowed to leave again. Seventeen Remembrancers in four hundred meters of living cathedral. The scarcity is not tragedy; it is etiquette. The lake itself can only support so much deliberation without tipping into haste.

They are tall, these beings—taller than any human their ancestors once were. Their spines have lengthened over the centuries into a supple column that lets them hang motionless among the real stalks for days, indistinguishable until they choose otherwise. Skin the color of wet bronze gone soft with algae; not scaled, not barked, but something between. Fine rootlets emerge from their shoulders and outer thighs, waving like cilia, tasting the water for oxygen and memory. When they move, the rootlets trail behind them in pale green wakes that take long minutes to dissolve.

Their faces are narrow, almost blade-like, with eyes recessed and lidless, protected by a transparent film of living tissue that gives them the look of constant, sorrowful attention. They have no discernible mouth. Nutrients pass directly through the skin in a slow osmosis that matches the pace of everything else here; they are, in the most literal sense, fed by the same body that feeds the columns. What was once a mouth has become a spiraled gill-structure at the base of the throat, an echo of the geometric carvings their ancestors once cut into lintel stones. When they wish to speak they press this spiral against a hollowed stalk and the column itself becomes their voice—deep, resonant, patient as stone growing.

They wear nothing that could be called clothing. Instead they are harnessed with living straps grown from their own bodies, strung with tools that have not been newly made in nine hundred years: an antler lever handed down through two hundred generations, its tines worn to glass smoothness; copper chisels no larger than a child’s finger, their edges kept sharp by the same microscopic crabs that maintain the stalks. Every object is both tool and ancestor. When a Remembrancer dies—which happens perhaps once in a century—the body is not buried. It is pressed gently into the root-mat at the exact coordinates where the old civic plan demands a new thickening. Within a year the corpse has become architecture. The others will visit the spot for decades afterward, laying their long fingers against the slight rise and listening.

They do not swim so much as *conduct*. A Remembrancer drifts through the great avenues with arms raised, fingers moving in precise, glacial gestures that somehow influence the schools of indigo fish. The fish respond as if the gestures were weather. Entire glittering clouds will alter their ancient spiral routes by a single meter to avoid a patch of root-mat that needs more light. The Remembrancers never hurry these corrections. A single course adjustment may take three months. The fish seem grateful for the dignity of such slowness.

At the center of the largest plaza—where the root-mat dips into its shallow nursery basin—stands the only structure they have deliberately maintained rather than allowed to evolve. It is a ring of seven stalks that have been trained, over four centuries, to grow inward until their fronds meet and interweave at the top, forming a living dome. Inside this dome the water is somehow quieter. The pistol shrimp respect its boundary; their snapping fades to a respectful background static. Here the Remembrancers gather when the lake sighs every few centuries and the water level shifts. They do not speak in words. Instead they press their gill-spirals to the columns and the entire Tracery sings one long, slow note that lasts forty-one hours. During this time every living thing within the caldera—shrimp, eel, fish, crab, jelly-bell, human-derived Remembrancer—participates in something that is neither worship nor biology but simply *recognition*.

No Remembrancer is born. That word implies haste. They are *invited*. Every seventy or eighty years a child of the surface world, usually one already half in love with drowning, finds their way down through the hidden siphon that feeds the caldera. The Tracery examines them for months. If it approves, the root-mat will open a perfect arch that was not there the day before. The chosen one enters and does not come out. Years later a new Remembrancer is seen drifting among the columns, already ancient in its patience, already remembering streets that were drowned before its grandparents’ grandparents were born.

They have no names the way we understand them. Each is addressed by a particular chord of shrimp-snaps and eel-moans that is unique and changes with the decades as the individual’s role in the architecture slowly shifts. One might be known for three centuries as “The One Who Keeps the Southeast Pillar Breathing.” Later, after a great frond has finally lowered itself into a new position, the same being becomes “The One Who Taught the Light to Move Sideways.”

There is love here, but it is geological. Courtship takes forty years. A pair will choose a particular stretch of root-mat and spend decades encouraging it to grow into a particular curve that pleases them both. When the curve is perfect they entwine there for a single day, bodies pressed between living stone and living plant until it is impossible to tell where one Remembrancer ends and the Tracery begins. Then they separate and never touch again. The curve remains. The Tracery remembers for them.

Sometimes, in the deepest vaults where even the indigo fish rarely venture, a Remembrancer will hang completely still for years, arms outstretched, rootlets fully extended, becoming for all purposes another column. During these periods their minds are said to travel backward through the vegetable memory of the place, walking the dry streets that once existed before the water rose. When they finally stir again they carry faint new carvings on their skin—spirals that were not there before, as though the stone itself had reached out and drawn on them while they dreamed.

They are not guardians. They are not priests. They are the slow, green continuation of a civic plan that no longer has citizens in the old sense. The city wanted to keep living after it drowned. So it grew itself a new kind of inhabitant—one that would never be in a hurry, one that would understand that maintenance and erosion could be the same sacred act.

If you remain long enough, one will eventually drift toward you. It will not speak at first. It will simply hang in the water three meters away, rootlets tasting your strange dry-world chemicals, and it will wait. If you are patient enough to wait too, it may eventually press its gill-spiral to the nearest column and let the Tracery itself ask, in tones older than any language:

*Will you help us remember what we were, by becoming what we are becoming?*

Most say no.

The ones who say yes are never seen on the surface again.

They have already begun the long, patient work of turning into pillars.
