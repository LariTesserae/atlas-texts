**The People of the Unfinished Chamber**

They are not yet named, not fully. They call themselves the *Veylari*, the “those who answer the teeth,” but the word feels provisional on their tongues, the way everything here does. When they speak of themselves in the low, wet clicks of their language, it sounds like water finding a new crack in stone.

You notice first how their bodies have made peace with compression. They are compact, almost stunted by surface standards, yet muscled in ropes and bundles from a lifetime of levering stone that has not decided to stay still. Shoulders are broad from carrying freshly split slabs; calves thick from climbing the bone ladders that sway in the dripping dark. Their skin is the color of wet limestone left too long in torch-smoke—grey-white with faint ochre undertures where the marrow-lamps have kissed them for generations. Scars map their arms like fault lines: chisel slips, falling shards, the clean burns from fire-setting the rock. Many bear deliberate ritual lines cut into the forearms—shallow parallel grooves that catch condensation so the water runs along their skin like living veins.

Their eyes are the strangest feature. Large, dark, almost liquid. In the thick orange light they seem to drink the glow rather than reflect it, storing it the way the pale fungi store moisture. In the true dark beyond the last lamp they can navigate by sound and the faintest gradients of temperature on their faces. Children born in the newest wet allotments sometimes have a faint blue sheen to the whites of their eyes, like the inside of a mussel shell. The midwives call these “cave eyes” and consider them lucky.

They smell of tallow, wet calcite, and something sharper—old mushrooms and the iron tang of constant minor wounds that never quite dry. Their hair is kept short or braided tight against the head so it cannot catch on the low ceilings of new passages. What little ornament they allow themselves is bone and tooth: incisors of cave-bears long extinct on the surface, carved into tiny repeating patterns of dripping water. A woman’s status can be read in the number of stalactite needles threaded through her earlobes; a man’s in the weight of the polished hammer he carries on his hip, its head handed down through six generations of the same unfinished wall.

They are patient the way stone is patient, but their patience has teeth.

You see it in the surveyors—lean figures who return from the northern dark with water still running from their hair, eyes bright with the particular madness of those who have heard the mountain dreaming new rooms. They speak in rapid clicks about “the next heartbeats of Veyl,” as though the cave were a living thing learning to breathe. Behind them come the *Kneelers*, the caste that tends the embryonic stalagmites. They crawl on hands and knees among the infant stone teeth, whispering to them, stroking their wet heads with oiled rags so they will grow straight and not crack the floor when they finally join their counterparts above. It is half maintenance, half prayer.

There are the *Rememberers* who sit in the driest upper lofts and recite the history of every fracture. Their memories are geological; they can tell you which exact year a particular pillar first revealed the seam of harder chert that now serves as the town’s main load-bearing spine. When they die, their skulls are cleaned, inlaid with tiny calcite crystals, and placed into the *Wall of First Drippings* so their empty sockets can continue watching the ceiling.

And then there are the ones who live closest to the unborn river at the downstream dark. These are quieter still. Some say they have listened too long to the water moving under the arch. Their voices develop an extra click, a sound like two drops striking at slightly different intervals. Children dare one another to spend a night on the last dry ledge before the river disappears. Those who do come back changed—calmer, slower, as though some part of them has already been dissolved and carried downstream into whatever comes next.

They do not have gods in the surface sense. Veyl is not worshipped; Veyl *is occurring*. To be Veylari is to be an event in the long sentence of stone becoming itself. Their birth rituals involve placing the newborn directly onto a fresh fracture plane so the first thing it feels is the unfinished world. Their dead are not buried. They are wedged upright into niches in the growing walls, mortared in with clay and fungi so that as the cave continues to open, the ancestors will be revealed again in new and unexpected places, like fossils that have learned to wait with intention.

At night—though night is only a matter of lamps being dimmed—the sound of the chamber changes. The hammers stop. The scrapes stop. Only the dripping remains, and beneath it the low, rhythmic breathing of four thousand people learning to exhale in unison with their birthplace. Sometimes, in that collective breath, you can almost hear the mountain answering: a deeper *tok* from far above as another flake of ceiling lets go. When that happens, the Veylari smile in their sleep. The sound means the conversation is not over.

The place is still being born.

And so are they.
