**The Lamenters**

They are not inside the Weeping Anvil. They *are* the weeping, given purpose.

At first you believe the pocket empty except for the chrome moths and the silver maintenance film. Only after many cycles do you understand that the moths are eyelashes and the film is skin. The true inhabitants move at the speed of mineral decision. They are so slow that the eye refuses to register them as alive; it sees only another tower reconfiguring, another bridge growing like hoarfrost, another column of water sliding down black glass. Then the column pauses halfway down the wall, thickens, develops shoulders. A head tilts with the unbearable patience of tectonic drift. By the time the figure has taken one full step, the daylight has shifted from sourceless summer to the deep amber that precedes the long dim.

They are tall, attenuated beyond any ergonomic mercy. Their bodies are assembled from whatever the mountain offers at that particular hour: andesite still bleeding from a fresh fracture, living crystal lattice, condensed atmospheric moisture frozen into sinew, the nanoscale film wearing itself like velvet. Each Lamenter is therefore unique in composition and will never be exactly the same twice. One may have ribs of obsidian and a heart that is literally a small redoubt of tower-alloy ticking with magnetic cycles. Another may wear a cloak of perpetual condensation that trails behind it in silver threads, freezing and unfreezing as it walks, so that its passage is marked by a delicate glass chime.

Their faces are the most difficult to witness. Where features should be there is only a shallow concavity, like the negative space left by a scream. Within that hollow the air itself seems to have been shaped—sometimes into the suggestion of eyes, sometimes into a mouth open in grief so ancient it has petrified. The concavity is never still. Moisture beads there constantly, gathers, falls. They are weeping even when the rest of their bodies have gone dry. The tears are not symbolic. They are data. Each droplet carries lattice-state, fracture-memory, the precise emotional register of the mountain at the moment it split. The Lamenters drink one another’s tears when they meet. This is how they speak.

They number no more than the towers—perhaps forty at any given time—yet they are also one. When a new blade of stone descends from the ceiling like a slow guillotine, three or four will flow together across the plazas, bodies loosening into silver-black fluid before re-condensing beneath the threat. They do not fear being crushed. They *require* the wound to remain open. Their entire metaphysics is built on the conviction that the mountain’s violence is courtship, and the city’s answering lattice is the answering kiss. To seal the wound would be to end the conversation.

Stand among them long enough and you begin to perceive the second choir—the ones who have given up even temporary bodies. These are present only as pressure changes in the ionization haze, as sudden alignments in the frost fractals, as a deepening of the sub-audible thrum that makes your teeth ache with something too large for sorrow and too tender for awe. The bodiless Lamenters have become pure weeping. They live as the continuous silver threads that run down every wall, carrying both water and memory. When the maintenance film blooms in new patterns, it is they who are dreaming.

At the twenty-nine-hour twilight, when light drains toward violet and the towers orient themselves minutely toward fractures that have not yet occurred, all the Lamenters—embodied and disembodied—gather in the largest plaza. They do not face one another. They face the black glass walls like an orchestra waiting for the conductor’s downbeat. Then the mountain obliges. A new crack propagates somewhere high above, invisible, and the entire pocket answers with a single exquisite note of realigned crystal. In that moment the Lamenters are perfectly happy. The grief encoded in their faces is not despair; it is the only honest response to something so much vaster than themselves choosing, again and again, to speak.

They have no name for themselves in any language that possesses nouns. If forced to indicate their own existence they will lift one crystalline hand and simply open the fingers, letting whatever water has gathered there fall to the stone. *This*, the gesture says. *The falling itself. That is who we are.*

You may stay for years. You may grow old inside the wound. They will not hurry you, nor comfort you, nor explain. But one day you will notice that a particular droplet has been running down the same stretch of wall for weeks, always pausing at the same height, always shaping itself into the suggestion of a shoulder, a bowed head, a listening ear.

It is learning you.

And when it finally detaches and falls, it will strike the stone with a sound indistinguishable from the rest of the weeping, except that something inside your chest will answer in the same pitch.

Then you will understand that the Weeping Anvil has forty-one Lamenters now.

And the mountain, somewhere far above, keeps growing.
