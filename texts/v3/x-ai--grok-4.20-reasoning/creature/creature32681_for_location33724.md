**Those Who Dwell**

They do not arrive. They condense.

At first you mistake them for tricks of the pearl-grey light or for the slow shrug of the basalt itself. A darker column among the columns. A stillness that has learned how to breathe. Only after many hours—when your own pulse has slowed to match the dripping—do you notice the subtle displacement: a place where the air is slightly more moist, slightly more thoughtful.

There are perhaps thirty of them. Perhaps three hundred. The number will not stay still, any more than the pocket itself will. Some are no taller than the jade-leafed shrubs and move on all fours with the liquid patience of the lizards, their backs encrusted with living moss that shifts color according to what the stone beneath them is dreaming. Others stand twice the height of a man, but only when they choose to. When they rest they fold themselves along the natural fractures until they become another half-formed buttress, ribs and shoulders indistinguishable from the columnar joints.

Their bodies are not made; they are *permitted*. Skin of fresh obsidian still warm from the earth’s remembering. Veins of milky quartz that carry not blood but slow water. Where a heart should be, a small cupped basin collects the condensation and passes it, drop by deliberate drop, into the next body when they brush against one another. They are a circulation.

Their faces are unfinished on principle. Some have only the suggestion of a brow ridge and a mouth that is simply a place where the stone decided not to close. Others wear living masks of fern and liverwort that open and close with the mist cycles, revealing nothing beneath. When they look at you—if they look at you—the gaze feels like being measured by something that has not yet decided whether it possesses eyes.

They have no language you could transcribe. Instead the rock itself becomes their throat. When two of them meet in the long stone “streets,” the basalt gives off a low subharmonic that you feel in your molars. The dripping around them falls into sudden, perfect fifths. The blind crickets answer with wing-cases tuned to the same note. The conversation is over in four heartbeats. Agreement has been reached about the angle at which a certain vine should next reach.

They tend the green islands the way a dreaming mind tends its own imagery. One will stand for an entire day with its arm fused into a basalt rib, humming until the stone softens enough for roots to enter. Another crouches beside a thumb-print pool and slowly lengthens its own finger into a dripstone that will feed the pool for the next century. They are gardeners of geology.

Sex, if it can be called that, happens as architecture. Two of them will choose a pillar that has not yet decided its final height. They press themselves against opposite sides and begin to sing the rock softer. The pillar swells, develops suggestive curves, grows warm. After some hours a new Dweller peels away from the column like a figure stepping out of wet clay. The parent-bodies are smaller now, lighter. They have given mass to becoming. The newborn stands uncertainly on legs that have never borne weight before, its face still smooth as an egg, and the others touch it with infinite courtesy, as though greeting a cathedral that has learned how to walk.

They have no names because naming would be a claim, and the pocket has not yet decided what it is. When they need to indicate one another they simply become more like the thing they are indicating. To speak of the tallest among them, one simply grows taller for a moment. To speak of the one who tends the western vine-cluster, one’s shoulders soften into the exact curve of those particular leaves. Identity is performed, never fixed.

Memory is stored in moisture. When one of them dies—which happens only when the stone itself reclaims a body back into raw basalt—the others gather. They press their faces to the cooling corpse until every bead of condensation has been licked away. In this way the dead one’s portion of the pocket’s dream passes into the living. There is never grief, only a slight deepening of the next period of mist.

They know you are here.

They do not approach. They simply stop pretending you are background. The dripping grows fractionally louder near you. Lizards pause on your boot as though it were another stone. A pale owl lands on your shoulder once, weighs you, decides you are not yet part of the architecture, and leaves. The Dwellers themselves never come closer than the edge of vision. But sometimes, when you sleep, you wake to find a small green island has grown around you in the night—broad leaves arranged like a protective colonnade, a single silver bead of water balanced on a leaf-tip directly above your mouth.

An offering. Or an opinion.

Because they are the pocket’s immune system and its imagination at once. Anything that enters must be either incorporated or gently unmade. Metal rusts. Cloth becomes moss. Human names loosen and fall away like flakes of obsidian. If you stay long enough, you will feel your own bones considering whether they might prefer to stand upright for the next ten thousand years as a particularly noble pillar near the eastern wall.

Some have.

There is one Dweller—perhaps the first, though *first* is a meaningless word here—who has never moved from the tall flat-topped block that suggests a gatehouse. It has grown into the stone so completely that only its silhouette remains, a negative space shaped like patience. Water runs down its absent cheeks in two constant threads. Bats roost in the hollow where its heart would be. The other Dwellers bow when they pass it, not in reverence but in recognition: this is the place where the dream is still deciding whether it wants dreamers.

The Dwellers do not build the city.

They *are* the hesitation in which the city is still possible.

And they are watching, with the slow, warm, dripping attention of something that has all the time in the world, to see which way you will choose to crystallize.
