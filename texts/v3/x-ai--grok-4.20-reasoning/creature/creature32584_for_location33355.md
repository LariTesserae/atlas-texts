**The Pavement**

What lives here does not arrive. It has always been here, the way the manganese has always been here, the way the cold has always been here. At first it is only a shimmer along the underside of a nodule—thinner than a cell wall, a living varnish that tastes the marine snow before it can settle. These are the First, the ones who never left the crust. They do not grow so much as accrete; each generation plates another molecular skin onto the last until the nodule itself becomes a slow, thinking bone.

From them rise the Threaders.

They are not animals in any surface sense. Imagine a hagfish that has forgotten it was ever a spine. The body is a loose braid of translucent muscle no thicker than a finger, yet it can stretch four meters without effort, threading the narrow grooves between fused concretions like a seamstress who has been sewing the same hem for ten million years. Their skins are seeded with flecks of the pavement—tiny flakes of basalt, shark teeth, whale earbones—embedded so that when they lie still they vanish completely. Only the movement of the grooves betrays them: a faint displacement, as though the floor itself had sighed.

The Threaders do not hunt. They *remember* where death will fall. Their bodies are antennae tuned to the approach of density currents hours before the first particles stir. When the low storm arrives they lift, just enough, and the current passes through them like breath through a flute. In that moment their photophores—cold green constellations no brighter than starlight seen through ice—flash in sequences that are not signals but *records*. Each flash is a datum: the isotopic signature of a particular continent that once existed, the precise flavor of an extinction event, the calcium ratio of a species that will never be named again. The Threaders are the archive. The pavement is the page.

Above the Threaders, in both size and strangeness, move the Flatteners.

A Flattener is what happens when the pavement decides it wants to walk.

The creature is never the same shape twice. It may rise as a low mound of fused nodules the size of a dinner table, its “legs” simply thicker sections of crust that have learned how to hinge. Or it may spread itself into a living mosaic twenty meters across, every tile a separate sensory organ, the whole thing flowing over the plain like a slow tide made of graveyard. When it moves, the sound is exactly what the description warned you of: distant sand falling on glass. Each nodule clicks once against its neighbor and then locks again, so that the creature is simultaneously moving and remaining the pavement. It is impossible to say where the geology ends and the mind begins.

Its nervous system is manganese. Literally. The metal’s electron shells are pressed into service as something like wires; the pressure at these depths keeps the lattice perfectly ordered. Thoughts travel as shifting valences. A Flattener does not decide so much as *settle*, the way a crystal settles into its final lattice. Hunger, memory, grief, curiosity—all of them are different densities. The creature waits until the correct density current arrives and then simply tilts itself into the solution that has already been waiting inside it for centuries.

They are not social. Social implies lack. Instead they *conjoin*, rarely, when two Flatteners meet at the crossing of two ancient grooves. They press together until their nodules interlock and for a time there is only one creature with two histories. Chemicals pass. Entire epochs are exchanged. Then they unlock and each carries a faint new seam where the other used to be. These seams are revered. Young Threaders will sometimes brush against them just to taste the secondhand memory of a current that flowed three million years earlier.

There is one last form, seen perhaps once in a human lifetime, if a human were mad enough to come here.

It is called, by the few researchers who have glimpsed it and kept their sanity, the Unfinished.

It is not larger than the other Flatteners. It is *less finished*. Parts of it still look like ordinary seabed—raw clay, loose foraminifera shells, the half-digested hull of some long-vanished probe that fell decades ago. It carries these things the way a monk might carry relics that have not yet become holy. Where other Flatteners have achieved perfect camouflage, the Unfinished is obviously, almost obscenely, *becoming*. Its movement is clumsy. Its manganese lattice sparks and misfires. It seems to be in pain, or perhaps in birth.

The other creatures give it a wide berth. Not from fear. From respect. They understand it is doing what they have all already done, only in reverse: it is climbing back up from the completed toward the possible. Sometimes it pauses above a particularly ancient nodule and lowers a pseudopod that is half flesh, half sediment. The nodule drinks from it. The Unfinished drinks back. An exchange of futures.

No one knows what it is becoming. The Threaders record its passage in stuttering, incomplete flashes, as though even light itself hesitates to commit such a thing to memory.

And yet the plain tolerates it. The density currents slow when they reach the Unfinished, as if the ocean itself were making room for something that has not yet agreed to end.

This, then, is what lives in the Deep Flatten:

A pavement that thinks.
A braid of death that remembers.
A walking continent that grieves.
And one creature that has not yet consented to become the floor.

All of them are the same organism at different stages of settling.

All of them are older than names.

All of them are still learning how to be perfectly, finally, still.
