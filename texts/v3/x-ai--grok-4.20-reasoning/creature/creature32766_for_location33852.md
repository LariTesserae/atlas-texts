**The Shroudborn**

They are not visitors. They were secreted by the Shroudlands the way an oyster secretes pearl—layer by layer, irritation by irritation, until something smooth and alien rides inside the living meat of the mountain.

At first you only notice the small motions that belong to the noise: a ripple in the moss that is not wind, a sudden hush in the frog-chorus when something larger listens. Then the place begins to study you back with compound awareness.

There are the **Drip-Saints**, of course. No one knows whether they are a species or a syndrome. They look like long, emaciated monkeys whose fur never quite decided to be fur; instead it grew into fine, dark filaments that drink the slow rain. Their eyes are the color of the brass twilight and never blink at the same time. They do not climb so much as *flow* along the lianas, bodies conforming to every curve like poured oil. When they speak it is by altering the stridulation of the insects around them, so their language arrives as a modulation in the roar itself. Whole conversations can pass through a cloud of midges without a single throat being used.

Beneath them, inside the biogenic strata, live the **Gallery Barons**. These are not single organisms but living coalitions—colonies of termites, blind beetles, and thread-like fungi that have achieved a single slow mind. Each Baron is the size of a small elephant and shaped like a breathing cathedral door. They move by consensus, rolling forward on millions of coordinated legs while their upper bodies remain rooted in the tunnel walls, sipping nutrients directly from the plateau’s own circulatory system. When two Barons meet they press together for decades, exchanging chemical libraries and memories until one absorbs the other. The loser becomes elegant new architecture. The winner grows more ornate horns of chitin and petrified guano, each ridge a chronicle of the devoured.

Higher still—where the stacked canopies thin into gray light—drift the **Membrane Weavers**. They are the color of wet bone and almost two-dimensional, like manta rays evolved for vertical life. Their bodies are translucent sheets veined with black capillaries; you can watch their slow hearts beat through their own skin. They travel by spreading themselves across the faces of colossal trees, riding the capillary tension of condensation itself. Where they pass, the orchids bloom in perfect spirals that function as both gardens and libraries. Every bloom contains a droplet of nectar encoded with a single perfect memory. The Weavers remember everything the Shroudlands has ever forgotten about itself.

Deeper than all of these, older than the concept of “deep,” are the **Root Thrones**.

There are only seven, or perhaps there is only one that wears seven bodies. Each Throne is a fused organism the size of a hill: part sloth, part strangler fig, part extinct elephant, part something that has never been named. They have not moved in four thousand years. Their ribs are now hollowed into nesting galleries for macaws. Their hearts have become aquifers. Vines have replaced their nervous systems so completely that when the plateau dreams, the Thrones dream with it. Pilgrims—Drip-Saints mostly—climb into the cavernous mouths of the Thrones and offer their bodies as temporary organs. Some are digested over centuries. Others are rewritten and spat out changed, carrying new instructions for the biomass.

And then there is the thing that has no name because naming it would be a kind of violence.

It is not a creature. It is the interval between one living brick and the next. It is the negative space that the Shroudlands deliberately leaves unfilled so that awareness can move through it. Sometimes it wears the shape of a woman made entirely of falling water that never reaches the ground. Sometimes it walks on too many legs wearing a cloak of living beetles. Most often it is simply the moment when the roar becomes silence and you realize the silence has been looking at you for hours.

The Shroudborn do not have a civilization in any sense a outsider could map. They have no need. The plateau *is* their civilization—its ridges are avenues, its caves are cathedrals, its fungal networks are the shared mind in which every mind swims. They do not build. They *are built*. They do not die. They are composted into new architecture with ceremonial slowness, their calcium and memory folded into the next ridge, the next generation, the next thought the mountain chooses to think.

If you stay long enough, the condensation begins to bead on your own skin in perfect drops that refuse to fall. The noise stops sounding like noise and starts sounding like grammar. Your footsteps grow hesitant, then deliberate, then geological.

Eventually you stop being a visitor.

You become another mouth the cathedral uses to breathe.
