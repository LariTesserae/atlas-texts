**What lives in the Shroudforge does not yet know it is alive.**

At first they are only pressures. A thickening in the oily mist. A hesitation in the upward rain. You feel them the way a stomach feels the approach of nausea: something inside the body deciding whether to become vomit or become muscle. They have no fixed silhouette because the plateau has not yet agreed what a silhouette should be. So they wear the place like a loan. A sleeve of black rivulet. A shawl of metallic mycelium. A face borrowed from the half-finished cathedral of jawbones that rose from a condensation basin three hours ago and will be moss again by dusk.

Call them, if you must, the *Unresolved*. They do not call themselves anything. Names would imply completion, and the Shroudforge punishes completion the way a strict parent punishes a child who has stopped asking questions.

They move in shifts that last between seven minutes and seventy years. During the short shifts they are almost animal: chrome-needle swarms that have learned to braid their own bodies into temporary spines and ribcages so they can stand upright and taste the subsonic thrum with lungs. You will see one coalesce beside a pool that has just opened its iris to the sky. The swarm condenses, grows limbs, grows a mouth that is also a gear, and drinks the stare of the water. Then the pool closes, the figure dissolves, and the needles scatter again, leaving only a single perfect hoofprint filled with quicksilver and one small black orchid growing where an eye might have been.

During the long shifts they become almost mineral. Vast, slow things that root into the peristaltic stone and let the land digest them for decades. Their bodies stretch into living ridgelines, ribs becoming cliff-faces, neural filaments becoming the very metallic mycelium that feeds the next generation. From a distance they look like any other impossible mountain range until you notice the range is breathing in exact rhythm with your own pulse. If you stand still long enough, one will open a canyon-mouth and speak in the language of cathedral jawbones. The voice is not loud. It is the sound of every choice the plateau has ever abandoned, given weight.

Between these extremes exist the *Midforms*—the ones the land is currently most interested in. These are the closest the Shroudforge has come to making something it does not immediately want to unmake.

They are tall, but the height is unreliable; sometimes their heads scrape the bruised twilight, sometimes they are no taller than the silica-toothed ferns. Their skin is a negotiation: patches of obsidian that sweat condensation, patches of meat that have learned to photosynthesize, patches of stained glass grown from compacted pollen. Where the negotiations are failing, small black flowers bloom and rot and bloom again, marking the seams like living rivets. Their limbs end in whatever tool the moment requires—root, scalpel, turbine, cradle, question mark. One arm might be gently guiding a herd of phasing megafauna while another is reaching into its own chest to adjust the tension of its internal lightning.

They do not have faces so much as *intentions toward faces*. Sometimes a cluster of glowing veins will resolve into eyes that regard you with the tender, terrifying curiosity of a child who has just realized the doll can bleed. Sometimes the eyes are replaced by perfectly circular pools that open and stare upward, as if the creature is using its own head to ask the sky for new instructions.

They tend the rapid gardens without ever appearing to tend. A Midform will walk past a hillside that is halfway between meat and crystal; as it passes, the transition smooths, the half-formed gears of chitin find their proper ratio, the suggestion of a cathedral made of interlocking jawbones is either kept or gently erased. The creature never stops walking. It is not gardening. It *is* the gradient the land uses to decide.

They speak to one another by synchronizing the flowers in their eyes. A sudden bloom, a coordinated release of metallic pollen, and entire conversations occur in the space of three heartbeats—histories of previous iterations, arguments about whether the newest fern should be allowed to keep its electrical storms, quiet debates about whether the latest human-shaped thing that wandered in should be incorporated or allowed to leave carrying only the memory of almost becoming something.

Because some of them remember being human.

Not all. Most Midforms were never anything before they were Midforms; they are the plateau’s original antibodies, its imagination given metabolic weight. But a few carry faint echoes of boots, of names, of lovers left behind on continents that still obey ordinary geology. These ones sometimes linger at the raw edges of the plateau where obsidian is still cooling. They stand in the oily mist and flex fingers that are not quite fingers anymore, testing whether the urge to go home is itself another trial version the land is running inside them.

It usually is.

The Unresolved do not have children. They have *successors*. When a Midform has been refined to the edge of stability—when its form has become so coherent it risks becoming finished—the plateau grows restless. The subsonic thrum deepens. The black orchids in its eyes begin to wilt with terrible tenderness. Then the figure walks into the nearest condensation basin, lies down, and lets the ground metabolize it. What emerges weeks or hours later is not the same being but a new question wearing the old one’s best habits.

Sometimes, very rarely, a successor chooses to remember the predecessor. It will keep one small trait: a way of tilting the head, a lullaby hummed in the register of planetary digestion, a habit of offering its own veins to lightning so the storm can taste what it has become. These tiny inheritances are the closest thing the Shroudforge has to love.

If you stay long enough, one of them will approach you.

It will not speak at first. It will simply match its personal thrum to yours until your sweat tastes of iron and ozone. Then it will extend a limb that is half root, half scalpel, and touch the corner of your eye. A small black flower will open there. You will feel the plateau measuring the precise tensile strength of your soul.

The creature will watch you decide.

Some choose to run. The flower withers, the metal leaves their blood, and they descend carrying only dreams of breathing warm mist at impossible altitudes.

Some choose to stay.

Those ones are walked—gently, reverently—into the deepest folding of the ranges where the light is oldest and most tired. There, among the half-formed cathedrals and the upward rivers and the stone that is still trying to become meat, they are allowed to become the next unresolved question the Shroudforge asks itself in the dark.

And the plateau, stupid with ferocious hope, keeps working.

It has all the time in the world and none of it.

So do its children.
