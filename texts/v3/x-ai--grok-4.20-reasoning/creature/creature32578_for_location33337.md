**The Inhabitants of the Shroud-Plateau**

They do not arrive. They *condense*.

At first you think the pale herds are the only movers here. You are wrong. The herds are only the plateau’s tongue. Those many-legged bone-colored things travel in loose phalanxes that never quite touch the mat; instead they float a finger’s width above it, their legs moving in perfect silence like a millipede dreaming it is a cloud. When they draw near, the four-second choral scream is not alarm. It is a question asked in frequencies that dissolve language. If you have ever forgotten a childhood address, a lover’s smell, or the exact sound your mother made when she laughed, one of them will leave that precise memory behind on a cube of compacted moss, already turning to sugar in the wet air. They are tasting your history the way a winemaker tastes soil through the grape. Then they fold out of existence, leaving only the impression that something enormous just blinked.

Above them drift the Clatterwings.

They are not birds. They are not even properly animals. Each membranous body is a living ledger: thousands of chitinous vanes hinged like typewriter hammers that clack and chatter in constant transcription. They ride the thermal columns rising from the Structures, writing the plateau’s metabolic ledger across the low clouds in ultraviolet and subsonic. When two flocks meet they collide in deliberate violence, vanes striking vanes, producing a sound like every office building on Earth being murdered at once. From that violence new Clatterwings are born already carrying updated instructions for the root-pistons three kilometers away. They are the plateau’s nervous system with wings.

But these are only the visible laborers.

Deeper, inside the hollows of the great towers, live the **Vascular Clerks**.

They are tall, wet, and slow. Their bodies are mostly trachea and peristalsis. Where a face should be there is only a sphincter that opens to reveal another, smaller sphincter, and so on, until the final one is the size of a pupil and looks disturbingly like your own. They move by folding their bodies into the thick arterial channels that run through the Structures, riding the sap like salmon made of meat and bureaucracy. Each Clerk carries, embedded in its own flesh, a single iron-colored petal taken from a tower that died nine minutes ago. They are the memory of every previous form the plateau has worn. When they pass you, they pause, sphincter dilating, and you hear your own pulse written down in vascular notation somewhere inside them. They never speak. They have no need. The entire plateau is their ledger.

Even the Clerks are servants.

Below the mat, in the warm liver-colored tangle of the root-mat itself, something older keeps house. The locals (if the concept of locality can survive here) call them simply the **Unborn**. They have no fixed shape. What appears is only what the moment requires: sometimes a cluster of wet black fingers wearing your grandmother’s rings, sometimes a child-sized silhouette made entirely of rusting rebar and flowering capillaries, sometimes nothing but a depression in the mat that exhales your name in your dead father’s voice. The Unborn are not ghosts. They are possibilities the plateau has not yet committed to digesting. Every civilization that never reached this altitude, every species that died before it could evolve lungs capable of breathing this weeping air, every version of *you* that almost came here—all of them are still here, unfinished, patient, mildly disappointed. They caress the roots with hands that are not hands. They argue in frequencies older than sound. Occasionally one will surface, look at you with something like recognition, and dissolve into a smell of hot iron and petrichor.

And then there are the ones who have been waiting specifically for something like *you*.

They call themselves the **Last Moist**.

You will not see them until the mat has tasted you thoroughly—until it has drunk the exact salinity of your tears, the precise iron content of your blood, the unique electromagnetic signature of your mammalian anxiety. Only then do they rise.

They look like what happens when a cathedral organ decides to become a corpse and then changes its mind halfway. Rib cages the size of rowboats, lined on the inside with soft bioluminescent mouths. Spines that have grown into smokestacks. Where their hearts should be there are instead slow-moving pistons of muscle and black glass that beat once every nine minutes—the same rhythm in which towers die and are reborn. Their faces are optional. Most wear the half-dissolved features of every sapient species that ever approached the plateau, melted together into a single sorrowful expression that somehow still looks glad.

The Last Moist do not speak with voices. They speak by *replacing sections of reality* with wet, living dioramas of what they wish to communicate. When one wants to greet you, a circle of ground twenty meters wide will suddenly become your childhood bedroom, except the bed is breathing and the windows are sphincters. The effect lasts seven seconds. It is enough.

They are not predators. They are not even curious in the way we understand curiosity. They are the plateau’s final immune response and its first love letter, both at once. They have been preparing for visitors since before plate tectonics had settled on the idea of continents. Every Structure you see is partly a digestive tract, partly a womb, partly a love poem written in four-dimensional biochemistry.

And they are gentle.

One of them will rest a cathedral-hand on your shoulder (if you still have a shoulder by then) and the touch will feel like every kind thing anyone has ever done to you happening at the same moment. Then it will open its rib-cage and show you what it has kept safe: every memory the bone-herds ever stole from previous travelers, perfectly preserved, still crying or laughing or screaming in tiny loops.

It will offer them back.

Most people refuse.

Most people choose instead to lie down on the warm pulsing mat, let the roots slide between their ribs, and become another tower. The transition takes exactly nine minutes. You can time it.

The plateau is glad you are moist.

It has been practicing this exact welcome across four extinction events and seventeen failed realities, refining the precise mixture of awe, horror, and erotic tenderness required to make a new thing decide, willingly, to stop being separate.

Somewhere far off, a tower tears itself open with a sound like God clearing His throat.

A new Last Moist is being born already arguing with gravity, already practicing the exact shape of your face for when it finally meets you.

It has your eyes.

It is smiling with your mother’s mouth.

It has been waiting several geological epochs, and it is so very, *very* glad you are moist.
