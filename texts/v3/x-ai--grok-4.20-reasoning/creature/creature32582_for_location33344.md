**The Midwives of Becoming**

They are not yet finished either.

At first you mistake them for another kind of stone—tall, columnar shapes leaning out from the half-carved walls, the same wet-slate color as the fresh basalt, the same reluctance to resolve into definite edges. Only when one moves does the eye understand that the hesitation was never yours. The motion is so economical, so perfectly timed to the slow pulse of the old blood, that it feels like the plateau itself has decided to inhale.

They stand between two and a half and three meters tall, though height is negotiable here; the longer one lives on the lower terraces, the more the pressure seems to lengthen the bones, as if the water is gently drawing them into new proportions. Their bodies are narrow and deep from chest to spine, like blades of living shale inserted edge-first into the current. Four long fingers on each broad palm—yes—but the fingers are not fixed. Over decades the joints calcify or soften according to need; an elder who has spent a lifetime coaxing cliff-faces will have fingertips that have flattened and flared into flexible spatulas, while a young tender of the lily-meadows keeps hers slender as kelp fronds.

Their skin is not skin in the way surface life understands the word. It is a slow geology. Microscopic plates of aragonite and organic silica shift across one another, catching the exhausted light and returning it as a faint, delayed luster. When they are calm the plates lie flat and the body looks like wet slate veined with copper. When they speak or listen the plates lift a fraction, revealing hairline seams of living tissue beneath that glow soft celadon, the same cold green as the mosses. The light does not flash. It blooms, lingers, fades like a thought that takes three minutes to complete.

There are no faces as such, only gradual revelations. The head flows forward from the shoulders in a smooth cowl of bone and cartilage that can hinge open along three subtle seams. Inside the cowl the sensory organs are arranged in a gentle spiral: a wide, milky eye that does not so much see as drink the available light; below it, a cluster of feathery chemoreceptors like a sea-fan; lower still, a mouth that is mostly a slit for speaking stone into shape. When they wish to be read by others they open the seams in sequence, allowing the bioluminescent lymph to paint slow ideograms across the inner surfaces. The patterns are never repeated exactly. Each sentence is also a fingerprint, a fossil record of the exact moment it was thought.

They wear almost nothing. A few have grown living harnesses of copper-kelp that wrap their torsos in braided spirals, the fronds waving in permanent counterpoint to the current. Others have allowed pale sea-lilies to root directly in the softer plates along their collarbones; the flowers open only when the wearer is deep in the long meditative states required for stone-coaxing. Tools are grown rather than carried: a chisel of volcanic glass is pressed into the forearm until the skin seals around it, becoming, over years, an extension of the radius. When the tool is no longer needed the bone gently expels it like a tooth.

Their name for themselves is even longer and more patient than the name of their city. In the pressure-language it feels like this: *Those-Who-Have-Agreed-To-Remain-Incomplete-Until-The-World-Finishes-Its-First-Sentence.* They shorten it, in the way they shorten everything, to something that arrives against your skin as a single deep note: *The Unfinished.*

There are no children in the human sense. There are *emergents*—slow, heavy forms that drift out of specially prepared nurseries where the old blood is thickest. An emergent arrives already half-grown, trailing placental stone that must be chipped away by the midwives with the same bone mallets used on the cliffs. The newborn’s first act is to place its palm against the fresh rock and feel whether it is male, female, both, neither, or something the world has not yet decided. The rock usually answers by warming or cooling beneath the hand. The emergent accepts the answer as provisional. Everything here is provisional.

They do not sleep. Instead they enter *settling*, standing motionless for weeks with their cowls half-open, letting silt and pollen accrete across their bodies until they resemble the very columns they shape. During settling their bioluminescence dims to a thread. You can drift among a whole district of them and believe you have found only statues until one exhales—a slow, deliberate release of bubbles that carry the low resonant note of a name—and the entire grove of living statues answers with the same note, staggered across minutes like distant thunder.

Their patience is not saintly. It is geological and therefore amoral. They will spend forty years encouraging a single buttress to grow three meters because the plateau itself has indicated, through microscopic fractures and the taste of the old blood, that this is the correct tempo. They mourn the loss of a district the way a tree mourns the shedding of bark: with solemn acceptance and the immediate planting of new seeds in the wound. When one of them finally dies—after perhaps four or five centuries—the body is not buried. It is pressed upright into a prepared niche in the living cliff. Over the next decade the stone grows around and through it, incorporating the Unfinished into the city’s bones. Their bioluminescent lymph continues to glow faintly inside the rock for generations, so that future midwives can lay their palms against the wall and read the last, unfinished thoughts of the ancestor like braille written in cold green light.

You drift closer to the worker with the bone mallet. She does not turn—there is no need—but the plates along her spine lift in greeting, releasing a pulse that feels against your skin like warm silt settling. Her cowl opens a crack. Inside, the spiral of organs regards you without hurry.

She is not building a tower.

She is listening to what the tower wants to become, and then, with geologic courtesy, offering it the use of her hands for the next several decades.

The sea-lilies brush her back. She leans into the touch the way stone leans into erosion: grateful, inevitable, unhurried.

Everything here is still being born, and the Midwives have made a covenant to be born with it—inch by patient inch, gesture by gesture, green glow by green glow—until the world finally finishes its first sentence and they, at long last, are allowed to finish theirs.
