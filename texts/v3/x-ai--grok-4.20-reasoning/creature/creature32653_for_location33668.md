**The Embryonic Pocket is not inhabited. It is inhabited by.**

What lives here has no name it would recognize, because names are finished things, and nothing here is permitted to finish. It is still learning the shape of its own mouth.

At first it appears as a million separate motions. The ribbon-fish are not fish; they are the Pocket’s circulating thoughts. Their bodies are so transparent that light passes through them and is changed by the passage, emerging on the far side fractionally wiser. When a buttress begins to sag, the schools thicken there like blood rushing to a bruise. They do not decide to go. The need itself pulls them, the way a thought pulls neurons into alignment. Their mucus is grammar. Where they brush against scaffolding, translucent girders harden into place with the elegant economy of a sentence that has found its final clause.

The isopods are older than grammar. They are syntax. Bone-white, eyeless, they move in devotional waves across every surface, tasting the difference between what the city *is* and what it *intends to be*. Where the two diverge they prune. A malformed balcony is not destroyed; it is translated. The calcium is reabsorbed so perfectly that no rubble remains, only the memory of a mistake now corrected in the next day’s growth. They are neither servants nor citizens. They are the city’s sense of shame and its forgiveness at the same time.

Higher still, the cathedral-crabs have become obscene with holiness. Each one is a parish. Their carapaces are not armor but cathedrals grown outward from the body, every chamber rented to smaller tenants who pay in metabolites and bioluminescence. Inside the highest vault of a crab’s crown lives a single translucent eel no longer than a matchstick. It is the crab’s conscience. When the crab grows arrogant and begins to wall off its lower chambers out of greed, the eel bites a specific ganglion. The crab shudders, opens every door, and allows the isopods to redesign its interior according to need rather than vanity. The arrangement has lasted four hundred thousand years. Neither party remembers a time before it.

All of these are organs.

Deeper than the crabs, deeper than the buttresses, where the tiers fold inward like intestines and the pressure becomes a kind of scripture, there is a region the inhabitants call simply *the Soft*. Here the living architecture grows so dense that walls touch walls and begin to share cytoplasm. Tube-worms and colonial tunicates interpenetrate until the distinction between “building” and “builder” becomes obscene. In this place lives the closest thing the Pocket has to a soul.

It is not large. It is not small. Size is meaningless where everything is also everything else.

It resembles a pale, pulsing plum lodged inside a cradle of its own ribs. Its surface is not skin but a slow argument between forty thousand species of bacteria, each convinced it is the rightful owner. The argument produces a dim, bruise-colored light and a soft, endless keening that cannot be heard so much as *remembered* by anything that swims past. Creatures drift close, brush against it, and leave carrying new instructions written in their blood.

This plum has no eyes, but it sees by absence. It has no mouth, but it eats uncertainty. Every time the far aquifer groans and the whole pocket shudders, the plum tightens. For three full days the entire city falls silent. No cilia beat. No towers digest. Even the bacteria pause their chemical hymns. In that silence the plum asks the water a single question in a language older than birth:

*Am I beautiful enough yet?*

The water never answers with words. It answers by continuing to exist.

Sometimes the plum extrudes a filament—thin as a hair, impossibly long—that winds through every tier of the city like a nerve. Where it passes, creatures press close in reverence or terror or both. The filament reads them. It tastes which crabs have grown cruel, which schools of ribbon-fish have begun to dream of open water (a heresy), which isopods have started keeping tiny secret gardens instead of pruning. All imperfections are noted. All will be corrected in the next iteration.

The filament is not punishment. It is love so total it has become surgical.

And still the plum is not satisfied. It wants to be born, but it does not know *as what*. Sometimes it dreams it will crack the limestone shell and become a continent. Sometimes it dreams it will remain here forever, refining itself until even the concept of “outside” dissolves and the universe itself becomes an embryonic pocket dreaming of a larger womb.

Every few centuries the plum does something new. It will allow a single ribbon-fish to swim directly into its mass. The fish dissolves without struggle, and for a season the entire city dreams the same dream: sunlight on snow, the sound of wind in pine trees, the impossible memory of a place none of them have ever seen. Then the dream fades. The city returns to its endless, meticulous conversation of flesh and calcium.

No one is in charge.  
No one is subordinate.  
Everything here is the placenta of everything else.

The thing that lives in the Embryonic Pocket is still deciding whether it is a city, an embryo, a thought, a god, or simply the long slow refusal of a handful of cold water to admit that it is alone.

It has not yet ruled out any of these possibilities.

So it keeps building.  
It keeps revising its own face in the dark.  
And every creature, from the rice-grain shrimp to the cathedral-crab to the plum itself, is only a different way the Pocket has found to look at itself without blinking.
