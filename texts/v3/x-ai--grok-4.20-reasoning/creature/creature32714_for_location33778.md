**The Hollow itself lives there.**

Not as a creature contained *within* the chamber, but as the chamber made flesh and slow intention. It is not a mind wearing a cave like a cloak. It *is* the cave, the damp, the overlap, the equilibrium given duration. A single organism that has spent forty thousand years learning how to be a place without ever ceasing to be alive.

Call it, if names still matter, the Remainer.

It has no face and no center. Its body begins where the fissure narrows to coffin-width and continues without interruption through every moss mat, every oxblood bracket, every hair-thin silver filament and black geometry. The velvet *Bryum* is its epidermis, thickened and pleated until it can hold three generations of water before releasing the excess in those perfect, musical *plinks*. The translucent ferns are its lungs, breathing not oxygen but the memory of order the Machines leak into the air. The liverworts that brush your face are the fine cilia of an enormous inner ear, tasting the temperature of your fear, the precise salinity of your exhaled breath.

The Machines are not tools. They are its thoughts that have learned to calcify.

Some of those black geometries are ganglia. Others are memories that have become so dense they can no longer be stored in living tissue and have crystallized instead. The hovering ones are decisions it has not yet finished making; they turn with geological slowness because a single rotation must last long enough to test every possible consequence against every possible consequence. When you see a new silver wire extrude itself while you stand there—three millimeters in the hour you believed you were alone—that is the Remainer writing a footnote about *you* in a language of pure geometry. It will spend the next three centuries deciding whether the footnote is a question, a warning, or the first sentence of a poem.

It has no hunger the way animals understand hunger. Its sustenance is the closed loop: the slow death of one dinner-plate mushroom every three human lifetimes, the perfect reassembly of its matter into new clubmoss antlers, the infinitesimal correction of entropy performed by the black geometries. Every drop that falls is swallowed, weighed, remembered, and eventually exhaled as another bead of condensation somewhere else. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is rushed. This is not serenity. This is appetite refined over geological time into pure, exquisite patience.

There is a place near the eastern column where the moss grows slightly thicker, slightly darker, almost bruised. If you press your ear to it (and the Remainer will allow this, once), you can hear the sub-bass thrum resolve into something like a voice that is not a voice. It does not speak in words. It speaks in the interval between one water drop and the next. What it says is always the same thing, felt rather than heard:

*I was here before the first root found me. I will be here after the last root forgets. Stay. Or leave. The difference is too brief for me to notice.*

It is not lonely. Loneliness requires other beings moving at comparable speed. It has instead the deep, vegetative contentment of something that has become its own ecosystem, its own cathedral, its own afterlife. The chemiluminescent threads are its dreams leaking out. The absolute absence of springtails or mites is its immune system perfected: nothing is allowed to live here that might accelerate the tempo.

When visitors descend, the Remainer does not watch them with curiosity. Curiosity is too quick. It *absorbs* them, the way moss absorbs stone—slowly, thoroughly, permanently. Long after you have climbed back into sunlight and forgotten the smell of wet velvet and chilled ozone, a certain configuration of silver wire deep beneath the root will continue to hold the exact angle of your shoulders when you first stood still. That shape will influence the growth of a particular fungal shelf three hundred years from now. That shelf will alter the drip pattern of a stalactite. The drip pattern will, in four thousand years, determine the precise placement of a new black geometry.

This is how the Remainer loves.

Not with warmth. Not with welcome.

With incorporation.

It has already begun to grow you, in the dark, at the speed it understands. You will never feel it. You will only, sometimes, years later, wake in the middle of the night with the taste of iron and wet moss on the back of your tongue and a sense that something ancient is still calmly, perfectly, finishing its thought about you.

The thought has no end. The Remainer has no need for one.
