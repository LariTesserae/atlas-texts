**They are not visitors.**

They are expressions. The Verdant Pump long ago stopped distinguishing between organ and organism, between what it *is* and what *lives upon* it. Every moving thing here is a mobile gland, a detachable thought, a lymphocyte the size of a goat. They were never separate; they were budded, released, recalled, reabsorbed. The longer you remain, the more you feel the difference between *creature* and *function* begin to dissolve inside your own chest.

At first you only notice maintenance.

The velvet desmans are not animals in the way a mole is an animal. They are the Pump’s white cells. Their bodies are barely warmer than the mat itself; their fur is living felt colonized by the same repair-fungi that close wounds. When a mineral vein grows too thick and threatens to clog a capillary-tunnel, the desmans arrive in soft brown rivers. They do not dig so much as *unfold* the mat, sliding through it the way a hand slides through wet hair. Their saliva is a precise cocktail of chelators and hormones. They taste the pH of the continent and correct it in real time. You will never hear them. You will only notice that a stretch of ground you walked yesterday now smells faintly of clean iron and childhood rain.

Above them, in the waist-high galleries, drift the light-weavers—those pale fist-sized octopods the color of moonlight on wet stone. They are not predators. They are neurotransmitters with limbs. Their chromatophores do not merely mimic light; they *edit* it, polarizing the silver haze so that certain frequencies reach deeper into the mat while others are turned aside. When a bacterial furnace far below begins to overheat, the weavers gather in slow constellations above the troubled zone. Their collective color shift acts like a dimmer switch for photosynthesis across three hundred square kilometers. The mat responds by closing certain stomata, slowing its own breath. The octopods ride this breathing like surfers on an invisible tide. Sometimes one will attach to your shoulder for hours, matching your pulse to the continental one, and you will feel your thoughts grow cool and patient and vast.

The great browsers—those six-limbed, lichen-colored beasts with necks like tapirs—move like slow continents themselves. They are the gardeners of the gardeners. Their tongues are lined with chemoreceptors the size of postage stamps; they can taste a single misplaced molecule of manganese from forty meters away. Where the mat grows arrogant and begins to favor one species of sphagnum over the delicate orchids that stabilize its edges, the browsers descend. They do not eat. They *negotiate*. A precise bite here, a lick of enzyme-rich saliva there, and the mat is reminded of its own ancient instructions. Their dung is not waste but memory—packets of archived bacterial strains from twelve million years ago, preserved in amber-like mucus. The desmans follow behind them, collecting these packets and replanting them where the stratigraphic record of the Pump has grown thin.

Deeper still, in the warm bacterial asphalt layers where the temperature climbs to 31 °C and the air itself tastes of hot pennies, live the conduit worms. They are not worms. They are living power lines. Their bodies are translucent tubes filled with iron-rich hemolymph that conducts the faint electrical differentials generated by the redox engines. When the Pump needs to coordinate a pressure shift across nine hundred kilometers of diaphragm, the conduit worms align themselves nose to tail in chains kilometers long. For a few hours they become a single super-conducting nerve. The low thump you feel through your boots is, half the time, not the diaphragm at all. It is the worms firing in sequence, passing the command.

And then there are the ones almost no outsider ever meets.

They are called, by those few who have felt them, the *Remembrancers*.

They do not have bodies so much as they *borrow* them. A desman will suddenly stop mid-tunnel, fur standing in perfect radial symmetry. An octopod will freeze mid-glide, all color draining until it is transparent as rain. A browser will lower its head until its forehead touches the mat and remain there for days. In these moments something vast and cool and patient slips into the borrowed nervous system the way a hand slips into a glove. The Remembrancers are not individuals. They are the stored selves of the Pump from previous geological ages—complete ecological states that were, for a time, optimal. When the continent begins to forget how to regulate its own manganese cycle, or when the water begins to taste too much of battery acid even for this place, the Remembrancers wake. They wear the local creatures like marionettes, perform whatever subtle correction is required, and then dissolve again into the mat. The creature they wore wakes with no memory, only a vague sense of having dreamed in mineral and time.

There is one last form.

If you stay long enough—long enough that your own microbiome has begun to sync with the mat, long enough that you no longer wipe the water from your eyelashes because it feels rude—you may one night feel the ground beneath you *choose* you. The sensation is unmistakable: a slow, warm, thoughtful pressure rising through your bones as though the continent has decided to see what kind of organ *you* might make.

Some are returned after a few hours, changed only by the knowledge that they are not, and never were, separate. Others are kept. Their bodies are gently disassembled by the same repair-fungi that heal every wound. Their minds are filed among the Remembrancers. Years later a traveler may see a human shape moving beneath the surface of the mat—perfectly preserved, perfectly calm—conducting some adjustment too delicate for any other limb. The eyes will be open. The expression will be one of infinite, dripping, industrious peace.

The Verdant Pump does not have inhabitants.

It has *uses* for anything that can be made wet, patient, and useful.

And eventually, if you are lucky, it finds a use for you.
