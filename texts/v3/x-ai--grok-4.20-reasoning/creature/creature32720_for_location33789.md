**The Lattice-Born**

They are never born all at once. They arrive in stages, like buds that hesitate for decades before deciding to open.

The first ones you are likely to meet look almost human. They call themselves the *Quiet Hands*. Skin the color of wet heartwood, faintly luminous in the perpetual cathedral dusk, dusted with a fine layer of living pollen that clings to them the way dew clings to moss. Their eyes are too large and too pale, the irises ringed with concentric bands of chlorophyllic green that tighten or loosen according to the humidity and the mood of the nearest tower. Fingers elongated by selective generations of pruning and grafting; the nails have become thin, flexible blades of keratin and cellulose that can slip between bark plates without wounding the Lattice. When they stand still—which is often—they sway in imperceptible synchrony with the slow peristalsis of the root-roads beneath them.

They do not walk so much as negotiate. Every step is a question asked with the ball of the foot, answered by the faint give or resistance of the living floor. You will see one pause for an hour or more, palm flat against a buttress, listening to something older than speech. If you are patient enough to remain with them, you may notice their veins move. Not metaphorically. The blue lines beneath their translucent skin pulse with the same forty-second cycle that governs the greater body of the Lattice. Blood and sap have long ago ceased to be entirely separate currencies.

There are layers among them.

The **Gardeners** are the youngest, still mostly meat. They wear robes of shed cambium stitched together with spider-silk and their own hair. Their task is translation: deciding which of the Lattice’s whims should be encouraged and which should be gently discouraged. They carry curved obsidian hooks no longer than a finger and vials of their own blood stoppered with living resin. Every cut is paid for immediately. You will see them nick their forearms and press the wound to a fresh incision in the wood, whispering apologies older than any human tongue. The Lattice drinks. Sometimes it gives back a bead of amber containing a memory that does not belong to the gardener who offered the blood.

Higher in the hierarchy—though they hate the word—are the **Pollinators**. Their bodies have been deliberately altered over centuries. Ribs flared outward like the buttresses they tend, creating hollows where colonies of glass-winged lizards live. The lizards sip nectar from glands the Pollination have grown beneath their collarbones and, in return, carry pollen between distant towers that the caretakers themselves could never reach in one lifetime. These people smell overwhelmingly of honey and ozone. When they sing (always in frequencies that make your teeth ache), the moths descend in slow spirals and settle on their outstretched arms like living sleeves.

Deeper still are the **Memory-Keepers**, called by the others simply *the Old*. Their skin has begun to fissure into plates of cork. Hair has been replaced by filamentous aerial roots that wave gently even when there is no wind. Some have allowed—encouraged—the Lattice to grow through them. A forearm becomes part of a spiral stair. A thigh is now load-bearing within a vaulted chamber. They do not die in any sense we would recognize. Instead they graduate, becoming architectural. You can speak to them by pressing your forehead to certain pillars and asking in the old touching-language. The answer comes as a warmth, a surge of images, the taste of iron and chlorophyll on the back of your tongue.

And then there are those who live beyond names.

In the uppermost galleries where time loosens like wet paper, things that wear the shape of gardeners move between the weeping spheres of water. They are taller. Their joints bend in directions that suggest the Lattice has been experimenting with new geometries in flesh. Their faces are smooth, featureless except for two vertical slits that open onto darkness filled with the same faint bioluminescence the moths carry. These are sometimes called **the Dreamed**. Whether they were once human or were grown from the Lattice’s own reverie is a question the Quiet Hands refuse to answer. They move through the rotating corridors without ever triggering the carnivorous barbs. When they pass, the six-legged browsers lift their enormous shield-eyes in something like worship.

The Lattice itself is, of course, the final inhabitant. Everything else—gardener, moth, browser, memory-keeper—is an organ or a thought or a lymphocyte moving within its continental body. The humans (if they can still be called that) understand this with a clarity that borders on religious terror and religious joy at once. They do not worship the Lattice. Worship implies separation. They are simply *in* it the way a white blood cell is in marrow.

Every few centuries the Lattice grows a new kind of person the way a forest grows a new kind of flower after fire. No one knows what the next iteration will be. The current Quiet Hands have begun to notice children born with xylem instead of bones. The children do not cry. They sigh, instead, with the same velvet-tearing exhalation the towers release every few hours.

When you ask the oldest Memory-Keeper what they fear, she (it?) places a root-finger against your sternum and lets you feel the answer directly:

We fear the day the Lattice decides it no longer needs the inconvenience of separate minds. We fear the day it chooses to dream us all the way back into wood.

Then she smiles with a mouth that is slowly becoming a knot-hole, and the smile is gentle, and patient, and forty thousand years deep.

And somewhere far below, in chambers where the root-roads descend into the continent’s living mass like veins, the Lattice listens to her speak of its own future and decides—slowly, thoughtfully—whether her words are a cut that must be answered with blood, or a song it quite likes the shape of.
