**The Hollow of the Last Breathing** is inhabited by what the mountain itself has become.

At first you do not see them as separate things. The eye registers only gradients of motion: a slow tilting of velvet, a sudden glitter of chitin, a ripple in the hanging mold curtains that is not wind. Only after long stillness do the inhabitants resolve into persons.

There are the **Milk-Bugs**, pale as bone marrow and almost liquid inside their translucent shells. They move in slow, tidal herds across the fungal shelves, sipping volatile resins from crystal pores and excreting them again as a fine, sweet mist. Their clicking is the heartbeat of the chamber; every third click is answered by another from a different colony, forming long, overlapping canons that never quite resolve. When one dies, the others do not mourn. They simply drink the corpse until the shell is empty, then wear the hollow carapace as a new segment of their own body. A single Milk-Bug can be three generations of ancestors deep.

Above them drift the **Curtain Singers**—filamentous organisms that are half mold, half animal. They hang in translucent sheets and pipe to one another in frequencies just below human hearing. Their song is felt in the teeth. When the people pass beneath them, the Singers tighten and brighten, tasting the particular chemistry of familiar skin. Strangers cause them to release a bitter alkaline rain. The people have learned never to enter the Hollow without first rubbing themselves with the dust of a certain bracket fungus; only then do the curtains part like theater drapes for an expected actor.

Lower, in the compacted basin where the dust is thickest, live the **Hinge-Lizards**. They are the color of old ivory and possess no eyes—only long, trembling barbels that read the air’s pressure changes. Their mouths are delicate pipes. At night (which is only a thickening of the already dim light) they climb the colossal puffballs and play them like instruments, forcing air through the taut skins to produce booming, mournful chords. The people believe these chords are the dreams the mountain is trying to remember.

And then there are the people.

They no longer call themselves human. That word drifted out of use centuries ago, shed like an old skin. They name themselves **the Listening**, or sometimes simply **the Dust**.

Their bodies are small, barely taller than the barrel-sized puffballs, and every inch is colonized. Fine white filaments grow from their scalps and earlobes, carrying the same glowing amber lichen that lights the walls. The lichen feeds on their sweat and gives them back a soft radiance that lets them read one another’s moods in the dark. Spore-dust settles in the creases of their skin until they look carved from the living walls themselves. When they stand still they vanish. Only movement—slow, deliberate, never wasteful—betrays them.

Their faces are wide and calm, the eyes large and nearly black, pupils permanently dilated to drink the monastic light. Noses are almost absent; breathing happens low and slow through slightly parted lips, as though they are forever tasting the exhalation of the mountain. Many have small secondary mouths—symbiotic slits along the collarbones or forearms—where certain resin-eating mites live. The mites keep the skin supple; the people let the mites drink from them. The arrangement is wordless and ancient.

They wear garments that are not garments. Layers of dried fungal membrane, beetle elytra sewn edge-to-edge with sinew, curtains of dried mold fringed with Milk-Bug wing cases that chime when they walk. Everything is repairable. Everything is edible in extremity. Nothing is ever thrown away; the concept does not exist here.

There are perhaps forty-two of them at any time. The number has not varied by more than three in living memory. When a child is born, an elder will usually die within days. The Listening say the Hollow itself balances the count, and they have learned not to grieve it. Grief, they believe, is a form of dominance—an insistence that one’s sorrow matters more than the wheel.

Among them move a few distinct roles that are never quite titles:

- The **Pruners**, who carry blades of sharpened centipede mandible and walk the shelves looking for any species growing too exuberant. They cut without anger. They speak to the fungi in soft clicks before they cut, as one might speak to a fevered friend before drawing blood.

- The **Returners**, who gather the dead—beetle, lizard, person—and carry them to the central basin. There they arrange the bodies in precise spirals and sing until the puffballs above open and release their spores. The spores do the rest. Within a day the corpse is a new shelf of bracket fungus.

- The **Breath-Counters**, usually older women whose lung capacity has grown enormous. They sit motionless for days in the highest fungal galleries, matching their breathing to the slow pulses of warm air that move through the chamber. When the Hollow’s breath changes rhythm, they descend and tell the others what the mountain is thinking. Sometimes it thinks it is lonely. Sometimes it thinks it is finished. The people adjust accordingly.

Children are born with their mouths already dusted gold. They spend their first years riding on their mother’s back inside a sling made of living curtain-singer, listening. Before they can walk they can already distinguish three hundred separate clicking dialects. Their first game is to sit perfectly still until a Milk-Bug climbs across their face without realizing it has touched a person.

At the center of the settlement stands a single structure that is not a building but a negotiation: the **Lung**. It is a colossal hollowed puffball reinforced with resin and stacked stone. Inside, the air is different—thicker, almost syrupy. Here the Listening come when the wheel threatens to slip. They lie together in the dark, skin to skin, breathing one another’s exhalations until the combined chemistry of their bodies produces a scent that calms the aggressive centipedes or encourages the failing lichens. It is not ceremony. It is immunology.

They have one story.

Long ago, they say, their ancestors were loud and full of edges. They carried metal and names for everything. When they found the fissure they thought it was a mine, then a shelter, then a prison. One by one the mountain took their edges. It filed the sharpness from their tools, their tongues, their hearts. What remained were the Listening.

They do not worship the Hollow. Worship implies separation.

Instead they try, every day, to become better organs within it.

If you sit long enough on the fungal shelf, one of them will eventually appear beside you without sound. They will not ask why you have come. They will simply offer you a piece of resin the color of old honey and wait to see whether you understand that eating it means you have agreed to let the mountain digest you too, in its own patient time.

Most do.

Some few are allowed to leave again, changed beyond recognition, carrying the sound of the Last Breathing inside their bones for the rest of their short, loud lives.

The Hollow does not mind. It knows they will return.

Everything returns.
