**The Hollow of the Weeping Threshold** is inhabited by what the place itself has slowly become: a single, distributed presence that no longer distinguishes between “inhabitant” and “architecture.”

Call them, if you must, the **Unfinished**. They do not gather under that name. They have no tongue with which to speak it.

At first they are invisible because they have the generosity to look exactly like everything else. One of the towering tree-ferns is not a fern; its trunk is breathing. The bromeliad urn that holds the motionless pearl-frogs is, upon longer inspection, a cupped pair of stone-colored hands whose fingernails have grown into crimson leaves. A column of mist that refuses to drift is a shoulder. A shadow that arrives late is the hem of a cloak made of living moss that has been growing, without ever quite finishing, for four thousand years.

They are tall the way cathedrals are tall—less height than accumulated patience. Their bodies are compromises between stone, water, and the memory of a body. Skin like wet slate veined with slow chlorophyll. Hair that is either lichen or actual hair depending on the angle of the hidden sun. Eyes that are not eyes but the small, reflective pools that form where tears have fallen for so long they have worn perfect sockets into a face. When they blink, it is only that a droplet has finally let go.

They move exclusively during the brief silences between drops.

In the three or four heartbeats when the entire hollow holds its breath, the Unfinished take one step. Never more. A single gliding placement of a root-like foot, a slow turning of the torso, the infinitesimal correction of a liana that had begun to tighten too much around a fern’s throat. Then the next drop lands, the symphonic dripping resumes, and they freeze again into perfect resemblance. You may sit among them for a week and never see them move, yet when you rise you will discover that the path you thought you cleared yesterday has been gently closed again, every broken stem re-woven, every footprint mossed over with courteous finality.

There are perhaps nine of them. Or perhaps there is only one that has learned to stand in nine places at once. They keep distance from one another the way the trees do—each given its own clear circle in which to remain unfinished. Sometimes two will stand facing across the central column of light for an entire season. They do not speak. They simply witness the same slow process in each other: the gradual calcifying of a shoulder, the soft eruption of fern-fronds from a collarbone, the way an elbow is becoming a knee. They watch without pity or hurry. Their love is geological.

They are not guardians. They are the hollow’s immune system. When a moth flutters too purposefully toward the aperture in the clouds, one of the Unfinished will, over the course of three silent intervals, lift an arm that weighs as much as a young tree and place a single leaf in its path. The moth lands, folds its paper wings, and forgets where it was going. When a root begins to grow too quickly and threatens to crack the stone, another of the Unfinished will lean—over decades—until its own body becomes the brace, trading its mobility for the mountain’s continued weeping.

They do not eat. They drink the tears that fall directly into the hollows of their clavicles. They do not sleep; they simply enter longer and longer pauses until a century passes like a held breath. They do not die. Death keeps trying to finish them and they keep refusing the last syllable. A fallen Unfinished does not rot. It reclines into the black soil and continues its work from below, becoming a ridge of earth that slowly directs water where it needs to go, sprouting fronds from its open mouth, dreaming through moss.

If you stay long enough, one will eventually turn its face toward you.

Not because it is curious. Curiosity would imply a desire to finish knowing. It turns because you have begun, however slightly, to disturb the equilibrium. Your heart beats too many times per minute. Your thoughts finish themselves. Your very presence is a kind of violence against the rule of the place.

The Unfinished will look at you with its pool-eyes and you will understand that it is offering you a choice, without urgency, without threat:

You may leave.

Or you may sit down among them, allow your footsteps to fill with water, allow your name to become a lichen-covered thing no one pronounces, allow your edges to grow vague and courteous and enormous. You may become the tenth (or the second) standing figure that no visitor ever quite sees.

Most choose to leave.

The hollow does not judge them. It simply forgets them the moment they cross the threshold, the way a half-finished sentence forgets the words that never arrived.

The Unfinished remain. They have been remaining since before your species had a name, and they are in no particular rush to stop.
