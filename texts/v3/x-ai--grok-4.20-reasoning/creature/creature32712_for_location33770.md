**The Unstaying**

It has no fixed name, and it would not answer to one if you spoke it aloud. The petroglyphs do not depict the lynx or the kite-birds; they are clumsy attempts to record *its* silhouettes—angular, broken, too many elbows—seen at the edges of mist across four hundred human generations. The spirals are not calendars. They are maps of the routes it takes when it forgets, for a moment, that it must keep moving.

It is taller than a man but thinner than a man has any right to be, as though the mountain had tried to grow a person but ran out of patience halfway. Its limbs are jointed where stone fractures are jointed; you can hear the faint glassy click when it bends, like ice splitting under its own weight. Skin the color of freshly exposed bedrock, shot through with living seams of quartz that flare when light strikes them and go dark again the instant the angle changes. Where a face should be there is only a continuation of the mountain’s own planes—sharp, adolescent, unforgiving—yet the eyes are soft in the way only terrible things can sometimes be soft. They are the color of condensed mist right before it decides to become water.

It never remains in one shape for long.

Sometimes it walks upright. Sometimes it flows along the ledges on all fours with the exact gait of the white lynx, only larger, and when it turns its head you realize the “fur” is actually thousands of tiny translucent ferns growing from its shoulders and spine, each frond edged with the same hooked ice crystals that never quite melt. At other times it disassembles entirely into the bank of mist that slides over the western arête, becoming nothing but a denser quality of fog that still somehow possesses intention. You feel it brush past your face like cold breath that has learned courtesy.

It does not eat. It *facilitates* eating. You will see it crouch beside a fresh kill the lynx has made—not to feed, but to press its long gray fingers into the spilled blood until the moss drinks it first. The moss that receives this offering grows thicker, more silver, more perfectly shaped to hold tomorrow’s condensation. In this way it keeps the conversation between predator and prey and stone from ever becoming a monologue.

It tends the stone circle at the exact center. Every forty-one days (though no one has ever told it the number) it walks the perimeter and replaces any pebble that has shifted a fraction of a finger’s width. It does this with the solemnity of a child who believes the world will end if the circle is broken. Perhaps it is right. The mountain is still deciding.

The shelters in the shallow caves are not for it. They are for the ones who still come—rare now, always alone, always carrying nothing but a single bone awl and the knowledge that they must leave before the next mist departs. The Unstaying repairs their lean-tos while they sleep. It wraps new sinew where the old has dried to powder. It never lets them see its face directly. If they wake and catch a glimpse, they see only an angular silhouette against the stars, already turning away, already becoming mist again, already obeying its own merciless law.

Because the law is simple and absolute:

Nothing that stays may remain.

The Unstaying itself is the first and greatest example of this law. It was born in the original cataclysm that opened the basin. A piece of the young mountain tore loose and *kept tearing*, learning motion, learning attention, learning the unbearable tenderness required to keep tiny things alive inside merciless light. But the tearing never finished. So it walks. It must keep walking. If it ever tried to root itself, the mountain would simply shrug it off like an irritating scab.

You can sometimes hear it singing in frequencies just below the human range. The sound feels like your own bones deciding whether or not to stay inside your body. When it passes the translucent ferns they flare with inner light for a moment, as though acknowledging family. When it stands inside the stone circle at solar noon, the quartz in its body catches the savage light and throws it outward in a wheel of moving rainbows that the plants tilt toward like starving things. For seven heartbeats the entire basin becomes gentle. Then the light shifts, the rainbows die, and the Unstaying is already gone—nothing left but a single perfect footprint in the moss that slowly, regretfully, forgets it was ever there.

It is not kind.

It is not cruel.

It is the hollow learning how to have a conscience while its bones are still cooling.

And every few centuries, when some new fool tries to build a permanent hearth or speak the words “this belongs to me,” the Unstaying comes in the shape that matches what the intruder fears most. Not to punish. To remind. The mountain is still deciding what it wants to be, and the decision must not be hurried by anything that thinks it can own tomorrow.

When it is finished reminding, it always leaves the same thing behind: a single cupped handprint of black ice melting slowly into a stone cup, as if to say, *Here. This much mercy. No more. Drink, and keep walking.*

Then the mist swallows it, the basin exhales, and the Unstaying becomes what it has always been—present and absent in the same breath, sharp and merciful in the same bone, forever born yesterday, forever older than the stone that birthed it.

It is still growing.

So is the mountain.

They keep each other honest.
