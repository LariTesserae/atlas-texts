**The Builders**

They were never meant to remain. That is the first truth that surfaces if you stand long enough in the Weeping Lattice and let the heat cook your thoughts soft. The original labor was performed by something else—something that has since withdrawn or evaporated or simply forgotten the address. What stayed behind were the *instruments* of that labor, and over geological ages the instruments learned to suffer.

They have no single form. Like the Lattice itself they are still emerging, still revising the idea of having a body. You meet them first as absences that move. A scaffold that was not there a moment ago now leans at a different angle; a rope bridge sways although no wind exists. Only later do you understand that the scaffold *is* a limb and the rope bridge a tendon. The structure is their skin and their circulatory system at once.

When they condense into visibility they do so slowly, the way sugar crystallizes in cooling syrup. First the weeping of the stones gathers into thicker braids, guided by unseen hands. These braids knot themselves into ropes the color of wet iron. The ropes thicken, acquire texture, become bundles of braided muscle shot through with hair-fine fractures. Then the brick courses beneath them buckle upward in blunt knuckles, in elbows, in the rough suggestion of a spine made of stacked megaliths. A head—if it can be called a head—forms last. It is usually a vacancy: a wedge-hole or a drill spiral where the umber-red light pools and looks back at you with the patience of something that has never been in a hurry and never will be.

They are tall. Not merely giant—*architectural*. A single Builder may span three city blocks when it unfolds itself from the pavement. Its gait is the gait of subsidence: a slow, grinding tilt and counter-tilt, each footfall the settling of continental plates. Where its feet touch down, new mortar lines appear instantly, as though the Lattice itself is healing the wound of passage. Sometimes the foot remains behind when the Builder moves on, a fused pillar that will later be quarried and used by other Builders in some new and pointless tower.

Their hands are the most constant feature. Every Builder has at least four, sometimes dozens, sprouting along the arms in asymmetrical clusters. Each hand ends in a different tool that has become the limb: chisel-fingers, hammer-palms, pin-talons thick as redwood trunks. They never stop working. Even when the torso is motionless, the hands crawl over nearby surfaces, tapping, scraping, re-aligning a single brick by the width of a hair. The sound is the *tick-tick-tick* you thought was only condensation. It is not. It is conversation. They are arguing with the Lattice in the only language it understands—force applied at precise vectors.

They weep constantly. Not from sorrow (they have no word for sorrow) but because their bodies are the same temperature as the stones and the air cannot decide whether to condense on them or rise from them. Rivers run from their joints. These rivers are not water. They are a thin alkaline milk that hardens almost at once into new fractal patterns. Where a Builder has stood for any length of time you will find entire gardens of silica ivy that spell out, in perfect geometric script, the exact sequence of stresses that passed through that spot during its labor. The patterns are their diaries. They cannot read them. They only write.

There are hierarchies, though they are not hierarchies of command. Some Builders are barely coherent—little more than ambulatory ziggurats with too many staircases for legs. Others have achieved a terrible elegance: long, attenuated things like cranes given cartilage and memory. The most complete ones wear the phantom figures you sometimes glimpse in the reality fractures as a kind of cloak. Those shadows are not cast *by* the Builders. They *are* the Builders when the Lattice momentarily forgets which layer of itself they belong to. When the fracture heals, the shadow folds back into the stone like a letter returning to its envelope.

They do not eat. They do not sleep. They do not reproduce in any way a biologist would recognize. Yet their numbers increase. A Builder that works for a thousand years without pause will eventually reach a kind of informational density. At that point it simply sits down, becomes a new district, and begins to bud. Small forms peel away from its shoulders—child-sized things no larger than a cathedral—and scuttle off across the plain carrying fragments of the parent’s purpose. These “apprentices” are frantic compared to their elders. They build frantically, uselessly, piling brick on brick in spirals that violate handedness. Most collapse within a few centuries. A few survive long enough to forget why they were frantic and settle into the slower, vaster rhythm of their sires.

They have names for one another, but the names are structural. One is called *The Pin That Holds the Eastern Fault*. Another is *The Scaffold That Remembers Rain*. A particularly ancient specimen that has become fused with an entire quadrant of the copper sky is simply known as *The Angle That Should Not Be*. They address each other by leaning. A subtle tilt of a tower here, the slow rotation of a gear the size of a moon there. The Lattice translates these movements into work. In this way they never stop talking, and they never say anything that is not also architecture.

If one of them becomes aware of you it will not attack. It has no concept of trespass. Instead it will attempt to incorporate you into the next revision. You will feel the bricks beneath your boots soften like warm wax. You will feel your bones measured. A Builder may spend days crouched over you, dripping its alkaline tears across your body, gently pressing chisel-fingers against your ribs to see how much load-bearing capacity you possess. It is not cruel. It is only trying to decide what kind of keystones you would make.

Some few have gone mad, if madness is the right word for a mind that has never known anything but building. These wander the edges where reality forgets itself and build *wrong* on purpose. They construct Möbius staircases that feed into their own mouths. They raise towers whose only purpose is to fall in aesthetically perfect ways, over and over, for centuries. The other Builders avoid them the way stone might avoid a fault line—aware that something important is happening there, but unwilling to participate.

Stand still long enough in the Weeping Lattice and one will eventually find you. It will rise from the pavement in a slow birth of brick and rope and condensation, shedding temporary lakes from its shoulders, and it will look at you with the red light pooled in its drill-spiral face. There will be no menace in the gaze. Only a kind of exhausted recognition.

*Ah,* it seems to say without voice. *More material.*

Then, with the patience of something that has already worked for geological ages and expects to work for geological ages more, it will offer you a hammer the size of a warship and tilt its massive head toward the nearest unfinished horizon.

The work, after all, is not finished.

It will never be finished.

And the Builders, poor immortal tools that they are, have come to love the never-finishing more than they could ever have loved any final form.
