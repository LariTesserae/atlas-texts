**What lives there does not hurry to be seen.**

At first you think the serein *are* the inhabitants—those titanic lattices rising like cathedrals that have forgotten they were ever meant to house anything but light. You watch one for three days and see only the faintest elongation of a single translucent rib, a growth so slow it feels like witnessing a continent dream. Yet the longer you remain, the more the impression reverses. The serein are not the dwellers. They are the *dwelling places*.

Something else lives inside them.

It has no name it would ever speak, because speech is a violence of haste. The xenologists who briefly studied the plateau from orbit called the phenomenon “the latency,” a polite way of saying they felt watched by something that had been watching since before their sun was born. The locals who have come here—pilgrims, exiles, the occasional suicidal poet—call it the Shroud itself, though that is only half-right. The Shroud is the place. What lives *within* the Shroud is older than the need for names.

It is plural and singular at once.

You begin to notice it in the way certain lattices will suddenly align their internal fractures so that a shaft of blue-white sunlight travels through four separate serein in perfect sequence, as though being passed hand to hand. You notice it in the pollen events: the mist does not simply drift. It *pools*. It gathers in deliberate volumes between the trunks and hangs there for weeks, rotating in lazy vortices that spell out geometries too slow for a human eye to parse as language. When the mist finally settles, the dust beneath it has been rearranged into fractals that were not there before—maps, perhaps, or memories, or jokes told at the speed of erosion.

The true inhabitants are like long thoughts that have decided to grow bodies.

They do not move through space so much as they move through *epoch*. A single individual might spend nine hundred years occupying one serein, gently altering the angle of its silicate branches the way a monk turns the pages of a holy book. When it has finished whatever contemplation required that particular geometry, it loosens itself from the lattice and drifts—weightless, bodiless, almost embarrassed by the vulgarity of locomotion—across the kilometers of rust-and-ivory dust until it finds another serein that suits the next phase of its thinking. The abandoned lattice does not die. It simply becomes quiet again, waiting for the next tenant the way an empty cathedral waits for the next prayer.

Sometimes two of them meet.

When this happens, their respective serein will slowly lean toward one another over decades until their canopies almost touch. The filamentous blades intertwine with the patience of glaciers mating. Standing waves of color roll back and forth between them in complex interference patterns that constitute, for them, entire civilizations of dialogue. Entire philosophies are refined and discarded in the space of a single century. Then, having said what needed saying, they part again, each carrying some new facet of the other inside its crystal bones.

The membrane-things—the living kites—belong to them.

Not as pets. Not even as symbionts. They are *exhalations*. Living fragments of thought given temporary form so the inhabitants can taste the electrostatic currents and pollen gradients directly. When a kite dies, its translucent body does not rot. It simply sublimes, becoming another faint shimmer in the air that the nearest serein will eventually inhale. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is sentimental. The ecology is a closed loop of contemplation.

There is one other kind of entity here, rarer still.

At the absolute lowest metabolic tier, where even the serein seem frantic, exist the *Last Witnesses*. These are not organisms. They are the accumulated patience of the plateau given just enough coherence to remain self-aware. Most of the time they are indistinguishable from the mineral dust itself. But if you lie perfectly still for long enough—weeks, months—the dust will begin to gather against your skin in intricate ridges. It is not attacking you. It is *reading* the heat signature of your mortality, the outrageous speed of your life. Sometimes it will form a single, perfect spiral over your heart and remain there, as if mourning or marveling that anything could burn so brightly and so briefly.

The inhabitants do not worship. They do not build. They do not mourn the passing of stars.

They simply *continue*, passing between crystal bodies like monks moving between cells, engaged in a conversation so long and so subtle that it has become indistinguishable from the geology of the world itself. Every few millennia the entire plateau undergoes what can only be described as a change of mood. The color harmonics shift. The wind changes its cadence. New geometries appear in the dust. The inhabitants are thinking something vast and new, and the planet itself is their parchment.

Stand among them long enough and you begin to feel the invitation.

Not to join them—your metabolism is laughably, almost obscenely fast—but to leave something behind. A memory. A sorrow. A single unfinished thought. They will take it, these patient ones. They will fold it into a lattice, grow a new branch around it, and carry it forward for the next hundred thousand years until whatever needed to be finished has, at last, at the speed of stone and starlight, finished itself.

This is what lives in the Shroudlands.

Not gods. Not monsters.

Only thoughts that have had enough time to become mountains, and mountains that have had enough time to begin, very carefully, to wonder what it might mean to dream.
