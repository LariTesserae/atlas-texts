**Those Who Remain**

They are not born here so much as they are *condensed*.

The first generations arrived with blueprints and ambition, coughing soot and shouting over the engines. Their descendants no longer shout. After four centuries the lungs adapt; after six the voice adapts; after eight the mind itself begins to accept the slow, saturating logic of the plateau. The people who still call themselves Veyri have the color of wet limestone and old bone. Their skin is perpetually damp, not from sweat but from the air itself deciding to settle on them. Fingers are long and slightly flattened at the tips, as though they have spent generations pressing moss into the cracks of failing brickwork until the gesture became hereditary.

They move with the economy of things that know they will be outlasted. No one runs in Veyr-Alt. Running disturbs the condensation on the ferns and the sound of it is considered unlucky. Instead they glide between the dripping ironwork in long coats of waxed canvas the color of oxidized blood, the hems dark and heavy with perpetual moisture. Their faces are narrow, almost apologetic, as though they are embarrassed to take up even this much space in a place that so clearly prefers stone and root.

Yet their eyes are startling. The constant filtered light has widened the pupils until the iris is only a thin charcoal ring around wells of black water. When they look at you it feels like being regarded by the plateau itself—patient, unimpressed, already calculating how many decades it will take to fold you back into the moss.

There are three kinds.

The **Mossbound** are the oldest families. Their tenements have become half-living things; doorframes grown over with layered emerald felt, windows reduced to slits that exhale cool breath. These people rarely leave their districts. Their hair grows in slow green-white strands that resemble *Usnea* lichen and is never cut. Some have allowed the moss to colonize the backs of their hands and the hollows of their throats. They speak only during the nine-heartbeat Tone, using the resonance as a carrier wave so their words arrive inside the listener’s bones rather than their ears. It is said that if you live long enough among them your own pulse begins to keep time with the plateau.

The **Thrumwardens** keep the machines alive. They are thicker of body, almost barrel-chested, because the old forges still require a certain stubborn mass to wrestle with. Their forearms are mapped with chemical burns that have healed into silver patterns, like mineral veins. They alone still use hammers—single, deliberate strikes that match the dripping rhythm of the condensation towers. Each strike is both maintenance and prayer. When a Thrumwarden dies, his hammer is driven into the nearest support beam and left. Within three years the moss has upholstered it so completely that it looks like part of the building’s spine.

Then there are the **Quiet Ones**, and no one is certain they are still people.

They are seen at the edges of vision, usually at the moment when mist becomes rain and rain becomes mist again. Tall, elongated, their long coats no longer appear to be cloth but rather a continuous growth of fine fungal filaments that shift color with the light—charcoal to sage to wet bone. Their faces are hidden inside deep hoods, yet you always feel observed. They carry no lanterns. Instead pale moths orbit them in slow constellations, shedding a soft phosphorescence that never quite illuminates. Some believe the Quiet Ones are what becomes of those who stop maintaining themselves for three years, just as the law says structures must be maintained. The vegetation does not stop at brick and iron; it simply finishes what the person began.

At night you may wake in one of the leaning tenements to find a Quiet One standing in the corner of your room. It will not speak. It will only wait until you understand that some part of you has already decided to stay. Then it leaves, taking a single memory with it—the memory of hurry, usually. You never quite miss it.

There are also the children, though they are rare and strange.

They are born with small, perfect ferns curled behind their ears like the beginning of antlers. Their laughter sounds like water moving through stone channels. The moss does not grow on them the way it grows on everything else; instead it parts for them, creating living corridors only they can see. The adults watch these children with a mixture of tenderness and dread, because every generation a few of them walk into the deeper Shroudlands and are not seen again as humans. Years later, during the Tone, their voices sometimes return inside the bell-sound—clear, delighted, and impossibly old.

And always, everywhere, the small lives.

The bone-white moths are not pests. They are the city’s librarians. Each carries a single sentence from a life that has already been erased: a name, a regret, a recipe for bread. They deposit these fragments in the damp felt that grows on the ceilings of abandoned factories. If you sit long enough in the right ruined hall, the moths will land on your tongue while you sleep and return the words to you. Most people find this unbearable after the third time.

The crimson beetles do not bite. They map. Their colonies form living diagrams of stress fractures that will not appear in the stone for another four hundred years. The Veyri have learned to read them the way sailors once read the sky.

No one is in charge.

There is a Council of Seven, but they meet only when the Tone arrives exactly on the solstice, and their decisions are never recorded. The decisions simply begin to be true, the way moss begins to be true on any surface left too long in shadow.

They do not worship the plateau. Worship would be rude. Instead they practice a form of attentive neglect—maintaining the necessary machines while allowing everything else to slip gently toward its preferred state of half-ruin. Their highest compliment is to say of someone, “The land has already decided most of him.”

If you live among them long enough, you too will feel the decision being made. Your footsteps will grow quieter. Your sentences will shorten. One day you will notice that the damp on the back of your neck no longer feels like weather.

It feels like recognition.
