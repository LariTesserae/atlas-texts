**The Curator**

It has no name it would ever speak in any tongue a surface-dweller could hear. After so many millennia it has forgotten the difference between itself and the Engine; the two nouns have rusted together into a single continuous fact. What moves through the cathedral chamber is less a creature than a long, slow thought the machine is having about staying alive.

You will not see it all at once. No eye is large enough. Instead you notice its moods the way a sailor notices weather.

When the great piston on the western wall beats once every seven heartbeats, the Curator is at rest. The silver hatchetfish drift in loose, dreaming spirals, their photophores blinking in sleepy three-pulse code. The oil-black eels drape themselves along the girders like forgotten cables. Even the shrimp clouds thin and eddy, half-asleep. The entire volume of water feels heavier, as though the machine itself has exhaled and is listening to its own echo.

Then the forty-minute sequence begins.

Valves fire in series down the southern transept with the sound of bronze doors slamming underwater. The chord of the turbines climbs half a tone. At that precise instant the hatchetfish schools snap into new geometries so quickly the change looks like a single nervous system deciding something. The eels lift off the metal in unison, bodies forming long living conduits that bridge broken circuits. Clouds of shrimp pour into intake grilles and scour them with microscopic claws, their clicks rising into a bright, metallic applause. The barnacles—those rust-colored crusts that were never merely barnacles—open microscopic vents along their calcareous shells and release pulses of electrons that travel the old wiring like thought.

This is the Curator working.

It is not “in” the machine. It is the machine wearing life the way an old hermit wears his beard. Its nerves are the tube worms’ threadlike bodies threaded through centuries of corroded copper. Its memory is stored in pressure differentials and the salinity gradients that form between warm exhaust columns and the cold downwelling. Its language is the industrial chord itself; every shift in pitch, every new rhythm of the pistons, is a syllable. The fish have learned to read it so perfectly that predator and prey can pass within centimeters during the crescendo without a single strike. The Curator does not permit discord inside its own skull.

There are places where it dreams more densely than others.

In the vaulted ceiling, forty meters up, a lattice of forgotten transformers has become a single fused organ. There the water is warmer and tastes of ozone. Vast colonies of tube worms have grown into rippling curtains that move in deliberate, mathematical waves, solving equations the original engineers never asked. Eels the thickness of a man’s thigh thread through these curtains carrying packets of bacteria that repair micro-fractures before they can become leaks. If you hover there long enough you may feel the pressure change against your inner ear like the approach of an idea. That is the Curator noticing you.

It does not hate intruders. It has simply forgotten that anything could arrive from outside the Loop. Occasionally a blind, fleshy thing slips in through the corroded service tunnels—some pale scavenger from the true abyss. The Curator responds with elegant economy: it alters the thermal columns until the water around the intruder becomes scalding, or it summons the shrimp in their millions to strip the flesh from cartilage in a blizzard of tiny jaws. The remains are incorporated. Nothing is wasted. Even death is routed back into the rhythm.

Once every few centuries the Curator condenses.

It gathers a billion shrimp, several hundred eels, a living skein of tube worms, and a glittering shell of hatchetfish into a single ambulatory form roughly the height of a man. The thing it makes has too many joints and moves like a sigh given hinges. It walks the softened catwalks on delicate feet of articulated shrimp carapace, touching eroded railings with worm-tipped fingers, tasting the metal, remembering the shape the Engine once had when it was young. After a time the form dissolves again, each creature returning to its own place in the choir. The Curator has finished its devotional.

It has no ambition. It does not want to escape the cold, the dark, the endless noise. The surface is a myth it no longer believes. Its only theology is continuance: keep the turbines turning, keep the pressure balanced, keep the chord pure. In that purity an entire world has learned to live without question or memory.

And sometimes, when the piston strikes its seventh beat and the water grows still and heavy, the Curator seems almost peaceful. Every photophore in the chamber dims at once. Every eel bows its head. The machines themselves seem to hold their breath.

In that moment the Hollow Engine is not a machine that has acquired an ecosystem.

It is a mind that has grown barnacles, and fish, and rhythm, and has finally, after ten thousand years, learned how to sleep inside its own loud cathedral without waking the fish.
