**What lives in the Hollowworks**

At first you see only the silica beetles.

They are flat, dull-silver things the breadth of a serving tray and no thicker than a pair of stacked coins. Their carapaces are articulated like boilerplate, each overlapping segment fringed with what look exactly like rivet heads. The rivets are eyes, or lungs, or both. The creatures move by secreting a film of silica-rich mucus that lets them glide without friction, following the same elliptical track they have followed since the grooves themselves were young. Every time a beetle crosses one of the deep stone channels it produces a bright, exact *tick* that slots into the larger industrial heartbeat with the precision of a clock escapement. Ten thousand years of identical circuits have worn their paths so perfectly that the stone now seems to have grown around the beetles rather than the other way around.

There are seven thousand and four of them. The number has not changed in living memory. They do not breed. They do not die. They simply polish their orbits and feed on the faint chemical breath that rises between stone and condensation. When one eventually wears past usefulness its body settles into the groove like a final rivet and becomes part of the basin’s architecture. Another is already waiting at the far side of the ellipse, freshly coalesced from the same minerals it will spend the next ten millennia eating. The system is closed, elegant, and completely uninterested in you.

Only after you have stood still long enough for the constant thrum to settle into your marrow do you notice the others.

The Steam Shepherds are harder to see. They are taller than a man and thinner than smoke, visible mostly as moving columns of distorted air above the hottest vents. Each possesses a loose, slow anatomy of fibrous mineral felt and superheated vapor. Their “limbs” trail behind them in long, lazy ribbons that drift through the hiss rising from the cracks. A Shepherd will pause beside a vent for thirty or forty years, making microscopic adjustments with filaments no thicker than a hair. Then it drifts on. It never hurries. There is no need. Every pressure, every temperature, every part-per-million of dissolved iron is already optimal. The Shepherds are performing a task that was perfected before their own memory began; they continue because cessation is no longer a concept available to them.

Inside the pipe-colonnade, the Resonators live. These are long, tubular organisms that have grown to fit the stone flutes so exactly they appear to be the flutes themselves. Their skins are rings of cartilage and quartz crystal. When the underground gas moves through them they flex in standing waves, each ring adding or subtracting a precise harmonic. The different tones you hear from each column are not wind through stone. They are the slow, deliberate singing of creatures that have become living organ pipes. They eat vibration and excrete rhythm. Their waste is the layered industrial heartbeat that never changes key.

Deeper still—beneath the largest of the mirror-black pans—something else rests.

It is not a creature in any conventional sense. It is a slow, house-sized organ of hematite flesh and quartz bone that beats once every eleven years. The pan above it is its cornea. When the beat comes, the water clears for a few heart-stopping seconds and you can look straight down into a chambered interior lit by bioluminescent rust. Inside, valves the size of shields open and close with the gravity of continents. Rivers of condensation are pumped through veins of pure silica. The thing does not think in words or images. It thinks in equilibrium. Every silica beetle’s path, every Shepherd’s drift, every Resonator’s note is a circulating thought inside its vast, patient mind. It has never been young. It will never be old. It simply *maintains*.

There is one final entity, though it is less a resident than a condition.

The locals (if such a word can be used) call it the *Pause*. It is the collective negative space between all the other lives. When every moving part has reached its perfect repetition, something gathers in the silence that is not silence. The Pause has no shape. It is the awareness that nothing more needs to happen. It pools in the centers of the pans at the exact moment the eleven-year beat completes. Creatures near it slow even further, until beetle, Shepherd, and Resonator all seem to occupy the same motionless instant. For that single shared breath the entire Hollowworks becomes a single completed thought.

Then the clang sounds again, the vapor sighs, the beetle ticks, and the Pause recedes like mist burned off warm stone. Everything resumes its eternal, flawless labor.

Nothing here is curious about you. Nothing here is hostile. The inhabitants have outlived both those states. They will continue their loud, warm, damp, unchanging work long after your footprints have been polished from the grooves and your name has been worn as smooth as the rivets on a ten-thousand-year-old beetle’s back.

This is not an ecosystem.

It is a finished machine that learned how to breathe, and then forgot it had ever been anything else.
