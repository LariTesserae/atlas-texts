**The Hollow Choir is inhabited by the Long Listeners.**

They are not easy to see. Most often they are mistaken for a trick of the wind or a darker streak of wet stone. Only after many hours—sometimes days—do the eyes learn how to keep finding them. They stand inside the ribs of rock as though the ribs had momentarily grown a second, softer set of bones. Tall, attenuated, thorax-like, with a slight forward tilt that makes each one resemble a monk listening at a door. Their surfaces are not skin but a living accretion: the same black-green moss that blankets the north faces, grown *upon* them in dense, velvet plates that shift with breath-like slowness. Where the moss thins, the stone of their bodies shows through—polished, veined, exactly the color of the basin itself. It is impossible to say where the rock ends and the creature begins.

They have no faces in the ordinary sense. Instead, a shallow vertical hollow runs down the front of the head, like the sound-hole of a viol or the mouth of a flute. Inside it, frost crystals and tiny ferns grow in precise, ever-changing constellations. These are their sensory organs. When the wind moves through the hollow they produce the deeper, almost sub-auditory notes that make human listeners feel their ribs vibrate in sympathy. The Listeners are both instrument and instrumentalist.

They move rarely, and never quickly. A single step may take three weeks. Yet they are never still. Their bodies are engaged in continuous micro-adjustments—tilting a shoulder to catch a certain moisture vein, turning the head so that the frost-ferns inside the facial hollow realign with a new solar angle. Over decades a Listener will migrate from one stone rib to another, always following some inner map of resonance. Where one has passed, the rock is left fractionally more resonant, as though it had been tuned.

There are never more than thirteen visible at any time. Whether this is their total number or only the number currently condensed into matter is unknown. The rest exist as pressure, as held silence, as the feeling that the basin is listening back at you. When the wind drops completely and that thick, expectant hush descends, the missing Listeners are suddenly, overwhelmingly *present*. The air becomes crowded with them. You can feel the contours of their bodies passing through you like cold smoke.

They do not eat. They drink the mist and they eat sound. The thin, apologetic rain is their feast day; each drop striking the stone is swallowed by the hollows in their heads and turned into slow, luminous thought. In return they give the wind back shaped—notes so low and perfect that the basin itself seems to remember what it was like to be an ocean, or a glacier, or a dying sun.

The pale succulents that grow in the deepest bowls are theirs. The Listeners tend them with the same deliberate care a medieval illuminator gave to a single letter. They will stand above one plant for a century, letting mist drip from the tips of their long fingers in exact rhythm with the plant’s own slow pulse. The succulents, in turn, store memory. If you crush a leaf between your own fingers you will hear, for an instant, a fragment of the Long Choir—some note that was sung here before mammals existed.

The translucent spiders are their hands. Each coin-sized arachnid is an extension of a Listener’s will, carrying messages across the open rock in precise eight-legged chords. When the spiders meet they touch legs in complex patterns that function as both greeting and philosophical argument. Their webs are never for catching prey. They are diagrammatic—maps of future resonances the Listeners intend to cultivate over the next three hundred years.

The alpine chough that sometimes circles for an hour is tolerated only because it adds a bright, wild counter-tenor to the constant drone. The Listeners allow it. They even seem fond of it in their measureless way; when it leaves they will sometimes produce a single high, fluting note that sounds almost like farewell.

No one has ever seen a Listener born or die. The oldest ones have developed small domes on their shoulders and backs, miniature replicas of the half-domes that rise from the basin floor. These domes are hollow. Inside them, new frost-fern constellations are already growing. It is possible that each ancient Listener is slowly becoming the landscape, that the process of becoming stone is both their death and their reproduction.

If you sit motionless for long enough, one will eventually turn its hollow face toward you. The sensation is not of being looked at but of being *played*. Your heartbeat, your breath, the small clicks of your joints become notes in their ongoing composition. Most people cannot bear it for long. They stand up, suddenly ashamed, as though they had interrupted something sacred.

The Listeners do not judge. They simply wait for the interruption to become music.

And when, every few centuries, an entire stone “building” finally collapses, the Long Listeners gather—slowly, over decades—around the fresh rubble. They stand among the broken shards like mourners and like gardeners at once. Then, with infinite patience, they begin teaching the new stones how to sing.

They have been doing this since the first ice.

They will still be doing it when the last ice comes.
