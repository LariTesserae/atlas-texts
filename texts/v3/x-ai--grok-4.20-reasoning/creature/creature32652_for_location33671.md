**The Inhabitants of the Breathing Shaft**

They do not have a name for the place. Names require edges, and edges are impatient. What lives here simply *is* the place, the way a heart is the body that contains it.

The Membranes came first, or at least they remember arriving before the others. Their bodies are not leather but a living vellum, grown in thin sheets between four articulated limbs that end in hooks of cartilage. Each adult spans nearly two meters when fully unfurled, yet they weigh less than a human lung. They hang in staggered choirs that rise thirty, forty, sixty meters up the throat, overlapping like pages in a book no one will ever finish reading. Their song is not communication in the way we understand it. It is thermoregulation. It is grief. It is the slow calculation of available moisture. When the mountain’s pulse shifts every twenty minutes, the entire rank ripples in a visible wave and every throat drops exactly one perfect fifth, as though the chamber itself has exhaled through them. The lowest drones are felt in the teeth; the highest are heard only by the Slow Fingers, who pause their grazing and lift blind faces in something like worship.

No Membrane has eyes. Instead, the leading edges of their wings are lined with slit-like organs that read pressure, temperature, and the precise mineral content of falling droplets. They can taste a single bat guano pellet dissolving three hundred meters above them. When one falls—and they do fall, eventually, when the hooks grow brittle or the yeast-smell of old age fills their veins—the descent is deliberate. They fold their wings and drop like a monk choosing suicide, knowing the floor has been waiting. The sound of impact is soft, almost courteous. Within minutes the cleaners arrive.

The Slow Fingers are not fingers at all. They are entire bodies: pale, segmented tubes of muscle and silica cartilage, each bearing hundreds of microscopic setae that taste the bacterial film before they eat it. A mature specimen is four meters long and moves with the dignity of continental drift. Their feeding trails are not random; they are memory. Each lacework mandala records the precise chemistry of the last three years’ condensation. When a Membrane falls, the Fingers reroute. They arrive in silent delegations, first tasting the new nutrient, then delicately unmaking it. Cartilage, keratin, the very vellum of the wings—nothing is refused. What cannot be digested is extruded as perfect spheres of silica that the crustaceans later use to reinforce their paper shells.

In the baptismal cups, the Clickers have built a civilization of sound. They are not crustaceans in any sense a surface biologist would recognize. Their bodies are sacs of transparent gel wrapped around lattices of hollow bone that function as both skeleton and resonator. When two Clickers lock to mate, the union lasts between nineteen and twenty-three days. During this time they drift together in their shallow pool like a single chandelier of frost, exchanging chemicals and low-frequency codes that travel through the stone itself. Their Morse is not language but negotiation: *how much oxygen may I take? how much death may I return?* Every few generations a Clicker is born with an extra resonating chamber. These individuals are carried to the base of the Straight Column by delegations of Fingers and left there. No one has ever seen what happens next. The Column simply accepts them. The water cups around its base rearrange themselves overnight into tighter spirals.

Higher up, where the violet phosphorescence is strongest, live the Veils. They were overlooked at first because they are almost not bodies. Each Veil is a symbiotic mat of bacteria and microscopic archaea woven into a sheet fine enough to drape across a Membrane’s wing without being felt. They photosynthesize not with light but with the faint infrared bleed of the geothermal pulse. When a Veil grows too heavy it tears free and drifts down like a dying prayer flag, feeding everything it touches on the way. The Membranes sometimes sing to them—long, rising notes that make the Veils ripple in slow ecstasy.

There are others, less common.

The Bone Menders are blind white millipedes the length of a forearm. They do not eat flesh; they eat the *shape* of bones. After the Fingers have stripped a fallen Membrane, the Menders arrive and walk the skeleton for weeks, secreting a mineral paste that hardens into perfect replicas of the original joints. The copied bones are lighter, stronger, and slightly luminous. The Membranes will not hang from them, but newly born Membranes are sometimes found cradled inside these reconstructed ribcages, as though the dead have chosen to midwife the living.

And then there is the thing that is not a thing.

It has no fixed form. It lives in the exact center of the Straight Column, or perhaps *is* the column when it wishes to be still. Those who have sat long enough report a pressure that is not pressure. A sense that the air has developed an attention. When the mountain is deciding about a visitor, this presence rises. The Membranes stop singing. The Slow Fingers freeze mid-graze. Even the Clickers cease their Morse. For nine full minutes the entire chamber holds its breath. During this time the phosphorescent slime brightens, the temperature rises two degrees, and every droplet falling from the ceiling strikes the stone at the exact same instant, producing a single clear note like a bell made of time.

No one has ever seen it emerge. But sometimes, in the perfect darkness, a human sitting with their back to the Column will feel four delicate hooks—warm, careful, older than language—rest for a moment on their shoulders. The touch is not hostile. It is the touch of a librarian checking whether this particular volume still belongs on the shelf.

The mountain has been deciding what to do with visitors for fourteen thousand years. So far it has chosen patience. The Membranes have begun to incorporate the irregular heartbeat of these rare warm-blooded intruders into their choir. A new, jagged note. A question that has never been asked in quite this key before.

The Slow Fingers have already started laying down new mandalas around the Column’s base. The patterns suggest that the mountain is considering whether a human might, eventually, ripen into something worth keeping.

They have time.

They always have time.
