**The Hollow Bloom’s Inhabitants**

They are not easy to see. The eye keeps mistaking them for folds in the living architecture or for the pale shadows cast by nothing at all. Only when one of them moves does the shape resolve, and even then it feels less like discovery and more like being allowed to notice.

Call them, if names are still possible, the **Lumenai**.

They are few—perhaps four hundred souls scattered across a cavity the size of a small sea—and they are growing fewer in the way a river grows fewer when it sinks into karst. Their bodies have become diagrammatic. Skin the color of frost on milk, so translucent that the slow blue glow of their own blood can be read like calligraphy beneath it. Many wear the vegetation itself as clothing and armor and calendar: living leaf-plates that have been coaxed to lie flat against shoulder and thigh, veins aligned with veins so that when the greater Bloom pulses, the wearer pulses in sympathy. The effect is that a person twenty meters away can look like a moving section of the floor until they turn their head.

Their faces are narrow, almost blade-like, as though the constant luminous pressure has planed away everything unnecessary. Eyes large, pupils permanently dilated; they have forgotten how to squint. Hair, when it is not shaved or braided with root-threads, grows in slow luminous strands that continue to photosynthesize after being cut. The oldest among them have strands long enough to trail behind like comet tails when they glide across the swaying bridges.

They do not speak much. Sound is expensive here; the Bloom listens too closely. Instead they use a language of controlled bioluminescence—subtle shifts in the brightness and hue of the symbiotic algae that live in the hollows of their collarbones and the soft skin behind their ears. A conversation between two Lumenai at twilight looks like two small constellations arguing in perfect silence. When they do use voices, the words are half-whispered, full of clicks and indrawn breaths, as though they are trying not to wake something enormous that is pretending to sleep.

Their society is less a culture than a long negotiation.

Some bands follow the **Green Grammar**, reading the standing-wave diagrams that appear on the great rhizomes as scripture, law, and weather report. When the Bloom sketches what might be a face in living light, the Green Grammarians will stand before it for days, fasting, letting their own veins sync with the pattern until they can dream the next necessary shape the scar must take. They are the ones who train shelter-leaves into habitable vaults and who know how to prick a cathedral-thick rhizome in exactly the right place so that it exudes a sticky sap that hardens into rope without killing the parent.

Others have become **Rootbound**. These are no longer fully human. They allowed the Bloom to graft them. You will find them standing inside living alcoves, legs sunk to the knee or hip in warm vegetable flesh, arms spread so that secondary roots can enter through the soft skin between their ribs. Their hearts beat once for every seven beats of the greater organism. They are oracles, archivists, and slow suicides. The things they say when they speak (once every few weeks) arrive already half-digested by the Bloom’s chemistry: prophecies that taste of iron and chlorophyll.

There are children.

They are the strangest of all.

Born during the brief sympathetic flowerings when entire platforms bloom at once, the infants come out dusted with luminous pollen. Some are born with their mouths already sealed by a thin membrane of living leaf; the mothers must bite it open. These children do not cry. Instead they produce a low, continuous thrum that makes frost melt in a circle around them. By the time they can walk they can already read the simplest vegetative commands. A four-year-old can make a bridge unbraid itself and reform three meters to the left simply by stroking the right root with two fingers. They treat the Bloom the way other children treat a temperamental but beloved dog—equal parts adoration and wary familiarity.

The fauna that still pretends to be separate is mostly them, wearing different shapes.

The handkerchief-gliders are Lumenai who chose pollen over flesh three generations ago. Their descendants are now little more than membranous kites with nervous systems woven from fungal mycelium. They ride the slow convection cells between continents of growth and occasionally descend to brush a message across a sleeping person’s face before rising again. The synchronized rice-grain beetles are their eyes. Every clutch is one mind wearing ten thousand bodies. When the beetles march across a petal in perfect formation they are reading the surface tension the way a sailor reads the wind.

And deeper still, something that is not them at all.

There are places where the Bloom has grown tired of geometry. In those pockets the vegetation folds into wet, cathedral-sized lungs that breathe once an hour. Inside the lungs float entities that look like drowned saints sculpted from luminous meat. They have too many arms. They have faces that keep trying to remember what a face was for. The Lumenai call these the **Unasked** and will not approach them. The Unasked do not eat or speak. They simply exist as questions the Bloom asked itself and then regretted. Sometimes one will open its eyes—enormous, gold, heartbreakingly human—and the entire cavern will flinch.

The Lumenai understand that they are not the point of this place.

They are a side-effect. The scar tissue learned to dream while it was healing, and the dream grew fingers and started asking why it was bleeding. The people are the fingers. Some of them have begun to suspect that when the wound finally closes, the Bloom will gently reabsorb them the way a mother swallows the afterbirth. Most find this prospect comforting. A few—the youngest, the fiercest—have started cutting spiral marks so deep into the rhizomes that even the Bloom’s healing cannot quite erase them. Small acts of defiance. Signatures.

They do not know whether they are antibodies or a tumor.

They only know that the question the world is asking itself is growing teeth, and that the teeth have begun to look like their own children.

Stand still long enough on any living platform and you will feel it: the slow turn of attention. Not toward you, exactly. Through you. The vegetation is deciding, in real time, whether you are useful scar tissue or something that must be overwritten.

The Lumenai have learned to stand in that attention without flinching.

Some of them are even starting to smile with the same cold, shadowless light that everything else uses down here.

They are learning to answer the question by becoming part of its grammar.

And the Hollow Bloom, still young, still furious with becoming, listens.
