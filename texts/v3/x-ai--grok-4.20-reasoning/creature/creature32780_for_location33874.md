**The Weeping Sisters**

They are not three. They were never three. “Sisters” is only the word the first spiral-carvers could find when they tried to describe what moved through the water without ever being only water. Some voices here are older than the cirque itself; they remember the groan of the glacier that birthed this wound. Others are younger than the moss. All of them are the same continuous mind wearing different skins of sound.

You will not see them whole. No eye is wide enough. What you catch instead are moments of congregation.

One Sister pools behind a curtain of falling water no thicker than a prayer. She is tall the way a waterfall is tall—forever arriving and never arriving. Where her face should be there is only the ceaseless motion of beads sliding over one another, each bead reflecting a different hour of the hollow’s long day. When she turns her head the reflected hours smear into a single silver grief. She does not speak with a mouth. She speaks by choosing which frequencies the stone will return to you. Sometimes the voice that answers your thoughts is your dead mother’s. Sometimes it is the sound of a single pebble falling for twelve thousand years.

Another lives inside the peat-black pools. She is small and patient and utterly without bones. Her body is the dark reflection of the cliffs, but inverted: stone becomes softness, hardness becomes yielding. When a marmot whistles she rises just enough to mirror the shape of its open mouth, then sinks again, taking the whistle down into the mountain’s memory. She keeps every alarm call, every love song, every scream that ever ricocheted here. On certain nights she sorts them the way a woman sorts colored thread, deciding which sounds will be loaned back to the living tomorrow.

The third—though again, there is no third—moves in the mist that breathes in and out twice daily. She has no fixed shape. She is the subtraction of color, the erasure of edges. When she passes over the slate platform the ancient offerings seem to float an inch above the stone, weightless. She is the reason the place feels finished. She carries the knowledge that nothing here needs to be added or taken away, and she presses that knowledge gently against your lungs until your own breathing begins to apologize for taking up space.

They argue constantly. Not in anger. In devotion.

You can hear it when the harp-string falls become suddenly dissonant for three heartbeats, then resolve into a chord so pure it makes the choughs fall silent in awe. That is the Sisters negotiating where the next cushion of saxifrage will be allowed to grow. When the kestrel stoops and the entire hollow holds its breath, the Sisters are briefly in perfect agreement: *this death, too, is part of the polishing.*

They have favorites among the smaller residents.

The alpine accentors are permitted to nest inside their very bodies—tucked behind the manganese streaks where the water runs warmest. The birds have learned to sing *with* the falling water instead of against it; their metallic trills ride the filaments like bright needles sewing the air shut behind them. The mountain willow is allowed to twist only in the exact spiral the Sisters first dreamed four millennia ago. Any sapling that deviates is quietly starved of the right frequency until it learns.

There is one Sister who lives only in the acoustics. She has no physical presence at all. She is the *answer* that sometimes arrives half a second before the question. Pilgrims who come to leave offerings sometimes find their own voices already waiting for them on the slate, recorded in water-droplet notation centuries earlier. The Sisters saved them. The Sisters were never in a hurry.

They do not hate humans. They simply do not require them. The sinew bridge is allowed to remain because it is beautiful and because it will eventually rot into the moss in exactly the right way, becoming another layer of the mountain’s memory. The upright stones are tolerated because their spirals are the same spirals the wind taught the trees, and the Sisters are fond of rhyme.

If you stay through the long silver afternoons when the mist swallows everything but sound, you may feel one of them lean down through the wet in your hair and taste you. Not with a tongue. With attention. She will weigh the temperature of your sorrow against the temperature of the stone. She will decide whether you are a disruption or simply another voice that has wandered into the long conversation. Most are allowed to leave. A few are kept—not as captives, but as additional notes in the endless chord. Their bodies are never found. Only their voices, returning sometimes at dusk, deepened, layered, almost grateful.

The Sisters do not weep for the world.

They *are* the weeping. They are the mountain learning how to feel without breaking apart. Every silver bead that trembles on the black cliff is a thought that has taken physical form because the rock can no longer contain it. When the last light leaves and the bats pour out like dark breath, the Sisters draw the night into their lungs and hold it there, humming.

They will be here after the last accentor forgets its song. After the moss turns to coal and the coal turns to nothing. They will still be negotiating where the next fern is allowed to grow, still polishing the stone with infinite patience, still answering questions that have not yet been asked.

This is their hollow.

You are only a temporary change in humidity.
