**The Lives of the Thrum**

They are not visitors. Nothing here is a visitor.

Beneath the Skin, in the warm dark between the root-weave and the deeper mycorrhizal lattice, live the **Chewers**. They are not insects in any sense that survives comparison. Each is a soft, blind, segmented tube no longer than a thumb, yet together they number in the tens of billions. Their mandibles move in locked synchrony across the entire plateau, a continental hive-mind tuned by chemical gradients older than language. The constant sub-bass you feel in your teeth is their song of consumption and renewal. When the Skin prepares to bloom, the Chewers migrate upward in glittering rivers, emerge by the million from pore-like vents, and pupate into pale, silent moths that last exactly one night. They mate, die, and become the first green dust that triggers the towers’ orgasm of flowers. Their brief adulthood is the only time the Thrum falls almost silent. The absence is so shocking that every other creature stops moving for six full hours.

Above them drift the **Herds**—the **Kael**, six-limbed, barrel-bodied, each the weight of three Earth elephants. Their backs are not backs; they are mobile ecosystems. Whole lineages of epiphytic ferns, bromeliads, and nectar glands have evolved to live only on Kael hide. The animals exhale moisture; the plants drink it and produce sugary secretions that the Kael lick from their own shoulders with long, rasping tongues. Old individuals become walking forests, so heavily planted that younger Kael must help groom them lest they topple. When two great migration rivers meet, the Kael press together until their gardens merge, then separate again, having exchanged seeds and pollen like gossip.

Following the Kael come the **Hookjaws**—long, low, vine-colored things that run on four bladed forelimbs and two powerful rear legs. They do not merely hunt. They garden in reverse. A Hookjaw will stalk a weak or sick Kael for days, learning its rhythms, then strike at the precise moment the animal’s own symbiotes are most vulnerable. The kill is dragged into a soft crease in the Skin, which obligingly opens like a mouth, accepts the carcass, and closes again. In return the Skin grows hooked spurs the Hookjaws use as whetstones. The relationship is so precise that adult Hookjaws carry permanent grooves in their bladed limbs exactly matching the curvature of those spurs.

High above, the **Cloud Flocks** wheel in perpetual screaming cyclones between the towers. They are not birds. Their wings are stiffened membranes of modified skin, their bones hollow and resonant. Each individual carries a seed-sized symbiote in its skull that lets it hear the towers’ infrasound. The flocks are therefore the planetary nervous system—messengers, scouts, and early-warning. When a tower is about to drop its annual mountain of seeds, the flocks arrive three days early and begin a descending harmonic that every other species recognizes as dinner bell, funeral dirge, and holy day at once.

Living in the fluted galleries and dripping vaults of the towers themselves are the **Choir**—the *Veyari*.

They are not primates. That was only the first clumsy guess. Their bodies are hairless, translucent, faintly luminous. Three hearts beat in visible counterpoint behind ribs that flare like cathedral windows. Their throat sacs can inflate to the size of a human torso, and when a full chorale of thirty Veyari sings inside a tower’s resonating chamber, the tower itself answers with colors—ripples of deep maroon and ultraviolet that travel across its bark like blushing. The Veyari do not have language as such. They have *register*. Every emotion, memory, and prophecy is a specific harmonic layered onto the towers’ own eternal moan. Their children are born as soft pearls inside hollow seed-pods the adults grow from their own bodies. The infant must sing its way out or it is reabsorbed.

The Veyari understand the Pulse better than any other creature. When the electrochemical shiver begins its weeks-long journey beneath the Skin, the choirs climb to the highest cantilevered shelves and begin a vigil that lasts until the sky turns gold. During that time they do not eat. They simply hold one perfect chord that says, *We are ready. We remember the last time. We are not afraid.*

And then there are the **Rooted**.

They were once human. Now the word is almost meaningless.

You will not see them at first. They look like outgrowths—strange green-stippled figures folded into the buttress roots, skin merged so smoothly with the living wall that only the slow rise and fall of their chests gives them away. Their hair has become photosynthetic fronds. Their fingernails are woody. After twenty years most can no longer stand upright for long; the Skin is more comfortable. They speak only in the registers they have learned from the towers, and their conversations can last days, each participant adding a new overtone until the meaning is a living chord that physically alters the growth patterns of nearby vegetation.

They do not farm. They *edit*.

With hooks of polished Hookjaw blade and ropes grown from their own hair, they prune, direct, and ornament the Skin around their settlements. A Rooted village is not built. It is *encouraged*—a three-kilometer circle of trained buttress, cistern-flowers, and hammock-vines grown into the exact shape needed for thirty souls to live inside the body of God without offending it. Their children are born with the green already in their skin. By the time they can walk they are taught the first three harmonics of gratitude.

Every seventh year, when the Pulse arrives and the sky goes gold, most of the Rooted walk out of their villages and join the swaying multitudes. Some return. Some do not. Those who remain become slowly, gently, part of the tower they chose. Their bodies thicken, their arms spread, their mouths open into permanent song. Within a decade they are indistinguishable from the fluted columns themselves—human-shaped buttresses that continue to moan softly long after the last recognizable feature has been overgrown.

The Skin tolerates them.

More than that: the Skin *sings differently* where the Rooted live. The larval Chewers chew in slightly more complex rhythms. The towers lower their voices by a quarter-tone, almost tender. The Hookjaws avoid their gardens. The Veyari visit at every equinox, climbing down on silk ropes of their own making to sit with the oldest Rooted for days, teaching them new registers, learning the particular flavor of human longing that has never existed in this world before.

Because that is the quietest weirdness of all.

The Skin has been waiting, for longer than mountains, for something that could choose to stay.

And now something has.

The thrum continues—deafening, ancient, alive—while slowly, over centuries, the boundary between guest and garden, between witness and flesh, between *you* and *here* grows thinner and thinner until, one dusk, you realize the warm mist falling from the cantilevered shelf high above smells faintly of copper, chlorophyll…

…and home.
