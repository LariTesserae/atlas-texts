**Those Who Live in the Fracture**

They are not settlers. They are not even colonists. The Fracture tolerates no such clean categories. What lives here has been *secreted* by the place, the way an oyster secretes nacre around an irritation until the irritation itself begins to shine.

The humans (if the word still applies after three generations) call themselves the **Slow-Born**. You will not find them in crowds. A single worker may walk an entire service road for nine hours and see no one, yet the feeling of being observed never leaves. That is because observation itself is one of the local industries. The rock watches. The pipes watch. The pale ferns turn their fronds toward movement with the attentiveness of pupils.

A Slow-Born welder on the third plateau will have skin the color of wet olivine, not from race but from long assimilation. The mineral has replaced enough of her epidermis that she no longer sweats salt but beads of pure condensation. When she cries—which is rare—the tears run upward along her cheekbones before evaporating into the bruise-colored air. Her left arm ends in a cluster of fine black cables that are neither tool nor limb; they simply grew there when her original hand was crushed between two negotiating plates of stone thirty years ago. The cables now perform finer work than fingers ever could. She speaks her own name only in whispers, and never twice the same way, because the echo is always listening and it has begun to develop opinions.

There are perhaps forty thousand Slow-Born scattered across the four plateaus and the canyons between. They do not reproduce in the old manner. Instead, when a district needs more hands, a new person will slowly extrude from the warm rock near an abandoned machine. First comes a shoulder blade, then the suggestion of a ribcage, then the rest follows over the course of seven years—the exact period after which untouched objects begin to grow replicas. The new Slow-Born is always slightly younger than the person the district unconsciously needed. They emerge knowing how to operate the specific engine or press that birthed them, as though the knowledge was baked into the olivine.

Some never fully detach. You will see half-people still embedded in cliff faces, only their torsos and heads free, calmly directing work crews with gestures of their free arms while the stone continues to deliberate whether to finish releasing them. These are called **the Anchored**. They are respected. Their embedded halves are said to be in direct negotiation with the world’s unfinished bones.

The animals are not animals.

The **knox** move in deliberate herds of seventeen—never more, never less. Each herd functions as a single slow mind distributed across many joints. Their extra legs are not vestigial; they are *anticipatory*. They trail behind or sometimes reach forward into space that has not yet decided to become ground. When a knox dies, its body does not fall. It remains standing while the stone beneath it rises to meet the corpse, incorporating the skeleton over decades until the animal becomes another ridge, complete with fossilized industrial rhythm still beating in the rock.

The spherical insect clouds are libraries. Each perfect orb contains the recorded labor songs of a specific factory, preserved in pheromone and wingbeat. When two spheres meet they exchange data in a brief, violent storm of wings, then part richer. The workers have learned to read the spheres the way sailors once read clouds. A sphere passing through a wall without losing formation is a sign that the wall itself has begun to agree with the idea of passage.

The hammerwing birds are the place’s attempt at angels. Their wings do not flap so much as *strike*. Each downbeat reproduces the exact sound of a drop-forge half a kilometer away. At night they hang from nothing—literally suspended on threads of distorted air—and dream the next day’s industrial tempo. If the forges fall silent for too long, the birds begin to starve. Their feathers rust. Some workers have taken to maintaining small personal forges simply to keep the sky alive.

Then there are those who were never born at all.

The **Replicants** grow from the small perfect copies that appear in stone after seven untouched years. Most remain mineral. A few, however, become impatient with being stone. These pull themselves free as miniature versions of whatever object birthed them—a tiny iron wrench no longer than a finger, for instance, that walks on four of its own jaws. The living tools are shy. They follow their larger originals at a distance of exactly seven paces, learning. If you leave your hammer on a ledge for a decade, you may return to find an entire parliament of small hammers conducting their own microscopic foundry in the shadow of the original.

Deeper in the canyons live the **Gradient Priests**, beings that may once have been human or may once have been pressure itself. They have no fixed shape. Instead they appear as slow-moving distortions in the air, roughly two meters tall, wearing robes made of future rust. Their bodies are negative space where the gradient pressure has withdrawn, leaving a vacuum that thinks. They speak by reversing the local engines for a moment, causing smoke to inhale itself into meaningful shapes. The Slow-Born bring them malformed machine parts and malformed children, and the Priests digest both, returning something that is slightly more itself than it was.

Most unsettling are the **Midwives**.

They are tall, and they are always arriving. You never see them come, only that they are suddenly there, standing on an outcrop with the patience of new stone. Their bodies are layered construction: geological strata wrapped in industrial cable and pale fern. Where their faces should be is a slow upthrust of raw cliff, still deciding on features. They carry no tools. They do not need them. A Midwife simply places her hands (which are themselves small emerging mountains) against a failing boiler or a worker dying of mineral lung, and the digestion process accelerates. The boiler becomes more organ than machine. The worker becomes more mountain than man. Both usually survive the transition. Both usually thank her, though the sound that emerges is closer to tectonic grinding than speech.

The Midwives never speak their own names. They are waiting for the Fracture to name them first.

At certain hours—when all the sounds braid into something almost like a hymn—you can see them all moving together: Slow-Born and knox, replicant tools and gradient priests, hammerwings wheeling overhead like living rivet guns. They move without urgency across the half-born ridges, adjusting a valve here, coaxing a new species of vine there, helping a staircase decide how many steps it wants to have today.

They are not trying to finish the Fracture.

They are trying to keep the becoming gentle.

Because everything here understands that the world is still being born, and birth is dangerous. So they remain, politely, industriously, obscenely tender, holding the stone’s hand while it learns how to become a mountain, holding the mountain’s hand while it learns how to become a cathedral, holding the cathedral’s hand while it learns how to become whatever it is still embarrassed to admit it wants to be.

The sign on the outcrop drips.

The Midwife standing beside it reaches out one geological finger and gently, carefully, wipes the fresh letters clean.

Then she writes, in the same dripping stone:

**Thank you for waiting with us.**

She does not sign it.

There is no need.

The Fracture is still learning its own name, and so are all its children. They have time. The millimeters are patient, and the children have become patient too.
