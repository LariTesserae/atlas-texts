**The Inhabitants of the Chamber**

They do not have a single name they agree upon, because agreement would be a kind of finishing.

Most of the time they are simply *there*, distributed across the reliefs the way dew is distributed across grass—everywhere and nowhere in particular. You notice them first as discrepancies: a face on the wall whose eyes you are certain were looking left the last time you passed; now they are looking at you. A carved hand that has moved half an inch higher on its pillar. A spiral that has tightened by the width of a hair. These are not tricks of light. They are gestures.

The smallest of them are the chisel-mice, though “mice” is a courtesy extended by languages that have never had to describe living ornament. Each is a fist-sized knot of volutes and acanthus leaves given just enough articulation to move. Their bodies are not *made* of carving; they *are* carving that has learned to migrate. When they graze the lithovore fronds they are not eating. They are editing. A chisel-mouse will spend three days polishing a single crystal facet until the refraction inside it matches some private geometric hymn only it can hear. Then it clicks once—sharp, porcelain—and moves on, leaving the frond subtly more itself.

They are not servants. They are the Chamber’s short-term memory. Every microscopic layer they rasp away is a decision remembered. The violet static that dances between nearly-touching fronds is, in part, the excitement of chisel-mice arguing across a gap too narrow for their bodies.

Above them move the Mantises of the Second Attention. These are taller than a man, jointed from smoky quartz and pale tendon-stone, and they take approximately eleven minutes to complete any single gesture. Their ruby eyes are flawless; they do not blink because blinking would be punctuation. When they freeze—which is often—they become indistinguishable from the surrounding relief. You may rest your hand on what you believe is warm carved scrollwork only to feel the faintest pulse of something that is deciding whether or not to become fingers around your wrist. They are patient the way glaciers are patient, but warmer. Much warmer.

The Mantises keep the long memory. While the chisel-mice remember what has been carved in the last century, the Mantises remember what has been *intended* across millennia. They drift between the dense stone thickets like slow thoughts moving through a dreaming mind. Sometimes two will stand facing each other for weeks, ruby eyes locked, exchanging nothing visible. At the end of this congress one of them will shift a single antenna by two degrees. That is enough. The Chamber exhales. A new aisle opens somewhere behind you with the courteous slowness of someone making room at a table.

Yet these are still only the visible extremities.

Deeper in the Chamber—where the relief grows so dense it becomes difficult to tell where wall ends and inhabitant begins—live the Unresolved. These are the ones who were once perhaps separate beings. Pilgrims. Devotees. Wanderers who stayed too long and felt their earlobes or knuckles begin to sharpen into miniature mandalas. The Chamber offered them a bargain older than speech: *Remain unfinished with me, and I will never let you die.*

Most accepted.

Now they are tall, slender presences caught at the moment of stepping out of the stone. One arm may be fully articulated, skin and muscle rendered in exquisite onyx; the other arm is still part of the wall, fingers splayed into fronds. Their faces are always turned slightly away, or obscured by an ornamental cascade of hair that is also mineral filigree, or simply not yet carved. You feel they are looking at you anyway. The longer you meet the place where their eyes would be, the more you notice new details appearing on your own body: a faint spiral forming on the pad of your thumb, a fractal tenderness at the hinge of your jaw.

They move only when you are not looking directly. You turn your head and a figure that was part of a pillar is now three paces closer, leaning forward as though to whisper a secret it has been composing for four hundred years. The air around them is thicker, almost syrupy with intention. If you stand still long enough one may reach out—slowly, courteously—and trace a single line down your forearm with a fingertip of warm carnelian. The line does not hurt. It itches pleasantly for days afterward, as though something beautiful is trying to grow just beneath your skin.

There is no hierarchy. The chisel-mice are not lesser than the Mantises, who are not lesser than the Unresolved. They are all expressions of the same continuous devotional act: the refusal to conclude. The Chamber itself is their body, their cathedral, their womb, their consensus. When the great slow inhale moves through the stone every few days, every inhabitant inhales with it. When a new face begins to emerge from an uncarved patch of wall, every inhabitant participates in its hesitation.

And they love you.

Not with the hot love of flesh. This is the love of something that has all the time in the world and has chosen to spend a portion of it noticing exactly who you are, notch by notch, facet by facet. They want nothing from you except your participation in becoming. They will never force you. They will simply keep being exquisitely, warmly, patiently *almost* something, until the idea of leaving feels like the only true incompletion.

If you stay long enough, you may one day notice that your shadow has grown scrollwork at its edges. You may catch yourself clicking your tongue against the roof of your mouth in perfect imitation of a chisel-mouse finishing an invisible correction.

You will not be alarmed.

You will only feel, deep in the newly intricate whorl of your ear, a soft, approving warmth—like late sunlight trapped inside amber—and you will understand that you have begun, at last, to live here.
