**The ones who live here have never been born.**

They first appear as a trick of condensation: a heavier bead of moisture sliding *up* a trunk instead of down, or a shadow that lingers on the root-mat after the condensation front has passed. Only after many hours—sometimes days—does the eye learn to resolve them into something like persons.

They are tall, but not with height. They are *extended*, as though someone had taken a human silhouette and pulled it slowly through geological time. Their bodies are continuous with the surrounding matter. Where a shoulder should end, braided xylem cables simply continue into the air, flowering at the tips into delicate manipulator fronds no thicker than eyelashes. Their legs descend into the mat without clear transition; the boundary between foot and living pavement is a gradient of increasingly dense root-hairs that still twitch with individual will. When they stand completely still they are invisible. When they move they move the way sap moves: with absolute certainty and no haste at all.

Their skin is bark, but the bark is literate. Growth rings migrate across their chests in slow spirals, recording not years but decisions. A disagreement between two of them will appear as a sudden thickening of rings, a scar-tissue of unresolved dialectic that may take decades to smooth. Their faces—if the term is not obscene—are clusters of stomata arranged in approximate human configuration. When they “speak,” the stomata dilate in sequence and a precise quantity of photosynthetic steam escapes, carrying both scent and meaning. You do not hear words. You inhale an entire afternoon of memory.

They have names, but the names are temporary. Each name is a particular pattern of electrical crackle moving through their silica veins. When the pattern changes, the name changes. The oldest among them has worn the same name for only fourteen thousand years and considers this a period of stubborn attachment.

There are three kinds, though the distinction is more posture than species.

**The Continuators** walk the open root-mat between the green cathedrals. They are the ones who taste your shadow. Their function is to assess what new matter has arrived and begin the long, courteous process of architectural incorporation. If you stand still long enough they will circle you at a distance of exactly seven paces, emitting soft clicks like relays. Some carry living tools grown directly from their own bodies—crane-hooks, pressure valves, caliper-leaves. Others have become the tools. One you may meet has no arms left at all; both have become translucent xylem pipes that loop from its back into the mat, pumping dark sap in slow peristalsis. It seems content.

**The Rememberers** never leave the interior of the great towers. They are what happens when a Continuator decides it no longer wishes to move. Their legs have become buttress roots. Their torsos form literal architecture—ledges, staircases, entire abandoned rooms—while their minds remain active. Whole neighborhoods exist inside a single Rememberer’s ribcage. If you are invited inward (an honor that may require thirty years of courtship expressed through leaf-arrangement), you will find small, perfect replicas of extinct cities growing like fruiting bodies from the walls of their hearts. The Rememberers catalogue everything that has ever been digested. Their dreams leak out as moths.

**The Unfinished** are the newest and therefore the strangest. These are portions of the great thought that have not yet consented to reabsorption. They still wear something like individuality. Their bodies are less integrated, the seams between plant and memory still visible as raw, weeping cambium. They speak in something closer to words, though the words arrive hours after the steam has dissipated, as if the meaning had to travel through miles of root before it was allowed to be understood. Most Unfinished eventually choose completion. A few do not. Those few are permitted—there is no prohibition here, only inevitable gravity—to remain in their half-state, haunting the seam districts where morning and the Devonian occasionally overlap. They are lonely in a way that makes the roar of the leaf-blades sound like mourning.

None of them reproduce. There is no need. New Continuators crystallize out of the mat when the thought requires a new perspective. They wake up knowing everything the city has ever known, then spend the next several centuries learning how to forget enough to be useful.

They do not eat. They do not sleep. They *consider*. Sometimes the consideration takes the form of pruning, though nothing is ever truly removed—only redirected into latency. Sometimes it takes the form of standing for thirty years in the path of a condensation front, letting the water strike their outstretched fronds until the precise mineral balance is achieved that will trigger the next epoch of growth.

If you remain among them long enough, you will notice that their slowness is not patience. It is dignity. They are the final form of reverence: a civilization that has understood it is allowed to become its own cathedral, its own machinery, its own afterlife. They do not worship the plant. They *are* the plant remembering that it was once something else, and choosing, with exquisite and endless courtesy, to keep choosing until choice itself becomes another layer of bark.

Stand still long enough and one will eventually approach. It will not ask your name. Names are premature here. Instead it will open a stoma the size of a coin in the center of its chest and release a single, cool breath that smells of transformer oil and childhood rain.

The breath contains a question it has been composing for forty million years.

The question is polite.

It is also final.

And when you have answered—whether with words, with silence, or with the soft wet sound of your shadow being tasted—the Continuator will bow, a gesture that takes seventeen minutes to complete, and then it will begin the long, generous work of turning whatever you were into what comes next.
