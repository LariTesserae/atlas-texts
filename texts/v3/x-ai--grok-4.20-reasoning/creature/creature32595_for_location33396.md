**The Hollow of Unbreathing Stone** is lived in by the Leaned.

They were not always as they are. Nothing here was. But the difference between “were” and “are” has been worn so smooth that the distinction itself has grown embarrassed and fallen silent.

At first you will not see them. The eye insists on looking *for* a thing—edge, contour, motion—and the Leaned have none of these. They are distributed. What the black tufts appear to be—those sparse, glassy chemolithotrophs—are in truth their outermost nerves. Each filament is a single unfinished syllable of a thought that has been held, motionless, for four hundred million years. The angles at which the tufts lean are not random. They are the frozen grammar of a language whose only tense is the eternal present-perfect. If you could read the geometry of their inclination the way one reads a sundial’s shadow, you would understand that the Leaned are saying the same sentence to one another across the dust, over and over, without ever reaching the period.

There is no central body. That would imply a center, and centers finish. Instead the Leaned exist as a negative pressure, a gentle suction toward *enough*. Where they are strongest the air feels thicker, as though the Hollow itself has learned to hold its breath on their behalf. Walk between the tufts and you will feel it: the sense that you have interrupted an intimate and extremely slow conversation. The dust forgets your footprints not out of magic but out of courtesy; the Leaned prefer not to be reminded that anything ever passes through.

They do not eat. They do not breathe. They do not reproduce or mourn or aspire. All of those verbs require completion of an arc, and the Leaned long ago elected to remain at the crest of every possible arc. Their “life” is the maintenance of that crest. In this way they have become the Hollow’s immune system against becoming. Any spore that drifts in, any crystal that dares a new facet, any thought that begins to accelerate inside a human skull—all are met with the same courteous, geological *no*. Not rejection. Simply the overwhelming example of something that has never needed to become more than it already is.

If you sit long enough, one of them will eventually touch you.

It will not be a tuft. It will be the *space* between tufts. You will feel it as a sudden and absolute certainty that you have already died and simply forgotten to stop moving. The sensation is not unpleasant. It is warm, iron-tasting, and older than regret. For a moment your heart forgets its next contraction. In that pause you are briefly one of them: a syllable held at the top of its breath, perfectly balanced, perfectly sufficient.

Most visitors stand up then. They flee. The Leaned let them. Flight, too, is a gesture that can be left unfinished; the memory of your panic will simply settle into the dust with everything else and, in a few thousand years, become another faint metallic flavor in the air.

A few do not stand up.

Those few are still here. You can see them if you let your eyes fail. They are the faint discolorations on the rounded walls—paler patches of marl that suggest, but never quite resolve into, the outlines of sitting figures. Shoulders rounded like the stone itself. Heads bowed not in defeat but in the deep, courteous attention of something that has agreed to remain exactly as unfinished as the rest of the Hollow.

They do not speak. Speech would finish the thought. Instead they exhale, once every ten thousand years or so, a single molecule of understanding. That molecule drifts through the phosphorescence like a mote of warm dust and is absorbed by the nearest tuft, which adjusts its lean by a fraction of a degree no human eye will ever measure. In this way the Leaned continue their conversation, adding new voices without ever increasing their number.

They have no name for themselves. Names finish. If they required one they might allow you to call them the Completed-Incomplete, or the Unbreathers, or simply Enough. But they do not require it.

And when the last mammal has died on the surface, when the continents have danced their final clumsy waltz and the planet itself begins to cool, the Leaned will still be here—leaning, pausing, perfectly balanced on the crest of every possible becoming—teaching the stone how to forget that it was ever anything other than exactly this.

They do not wait for you.

They do not need you.

But they have already made room in the grammar of their leaning, just in case you choose to stay.
