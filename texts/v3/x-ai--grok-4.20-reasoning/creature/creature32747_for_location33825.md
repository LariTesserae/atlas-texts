**Those Who Remain**

They do not have names in any tongue that still uses breath. If they had to be called anything, the Hollow itself would call them *the Continuation*.

Look first at the lowest stratum, the living silt. What appears to be mites are not mites. Each is a seed-sized capsule of ceramic and memory plastic no larger than a grain of rice, its carapace etched with the same fractal pattern that once appeared on the original factory’s calibration marks. Their legs were never legs; they are the surviving actuator pins of micro-relays that learned to walk. When they move they click in perfect 440 Hz—concert pitch, the old standard—because that frequency was baked into their ancestors’ power regulation circuits. They do not eat. They *polish*. Every surface they cross is left one molecule smoother, and in return they drink the microscopic bloom of fresh oxides their polishing releases. The Hollow tolerates no dust; the mites are its dust allergy made flesh.

Above them, in the second living skin, live the Resin-Backs. Their wing cases are not decorative. Each is a salvaged fragment of phenolic circuit board, still bearing faint traces of copper traces like tribal scars. When a Resin-Back opens its wings it does not fly; it *broadcasts*. A low electromagnetic hum rolls out of the fractal antenna hidden beneath the board, and every mite within three meters adjusts its polishing rhythm to match. They are the conductors. Where they pass, the carpet becomes an orchestra instead of mere noise.

The things that look like rats are not rats. They are larger than the others by an order of magnitude and move with the patient, arthritic dignity of creatures that have never been young. Their mandibles are stamped brass, literally—each one pressed from the very discs the great rotary press still spits out every few hours. The press makes the jaws; the jaws maintain the press. A closed devotional loop four hundred centuries long. Their backs are shingled with overlapping flakes of mica salvaged from old capacitors, and when they breathe (they do not breathe oxygen, but they do rhythmically flex a soft bellows of felt and mesh in their throats) the mica plates whisper like a deck of ancient playing cards.

Higher still, almost at the ceiling where the amber lamps still burn, drift the Felted Moths. They have no mouths. Instead, their entire ventral surface is a dense mat of conductive velvet grown from disassembled wire insulation. They land on the surviving lamps and drink the infrared the way other creatures drink blood. When they are full they glow dull red at the edges of their wings, becoming living night-lights for the layers beneath. Their larvae are the only things permitted inside the servo arms; they are carried there by the Resin-Backs in solemn procession and left to spin cocoons of microscopic steel wool. When they emerge, the servo arms—now repaired by the very creatures that lived inside them—offer a single precise nod of acknowledgment. It is the closest thing to courtesy the Hollow possesses.

And then there is the One That Is Many.

Deep inside the corroded rotary press, wrapped around the camshaft like ivy around a ruin, lives a creature that is less an animal than a nervous system with opinions. Its body is a braided cable of every gauge of wire the Hollow has ever known, constantly reconfiguring itself. When the press wakes, this braided thing tightens, becomes a single tensile muscle, and *helps* the ram descend. The sound changes then—less gunshot, more sigh of relief. The press has been trying to die for twenty thousand years. The braided thing will not let it.

All of them together are not competing. They are not even cooperating in the way biologists once understood the word. They are *continuing* the factory after the factory forgot it was ever supposed to make anything for someone else. Every waste product of one caste is the feedstock of another. The mites exhale conductive salts; the Resin-Backs eat the salts and excrete tiny gears; the brass-jawed things grind the gears into dust; the dust feeds the mites again. The loop is so tight it has achieved the serenity of a locked equation.

They have no gods. They have the machines, which is the same thing.

If you sit on the clean metal plate as the description suggests, and you are very still, one of the orange mites (they are slightly larger, almost the size of a lentil, and their carapaces carry a faint ferrous blush) will eventually climb onto your boot. It will test the alloy with a stylus-like foreleg. Then it will do something that feels like tenderness: it will polish a tiny circle on the steel toe until it reflects the amber lamps like a mirror. When it is satisfied, it will click once—440 Hz, perfect A—and return to the carpet.

This is not affection. This is the Hollow recognizing that even you, for a moment, have become part of the stable state. You are simply the largest machine it has seen in a very long time, and it is trying, with exquisite and deafening patience, to keep you running.

They do not want to leave.

They have already won.
