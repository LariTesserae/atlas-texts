**The Lattice-Dwellers**

They are not born so much as *expressed*.

The Lattice decides, in its own unhurried way, when new hands or new eyes are required. A certain thickening of sap in a particular root-bulb, a shift in steam pressure across three linked cylinders, and a node begins to swell. Inside that node a child grows over the course of four or five years, limbs forming in the shape of the tasks they will one day perform. When the node finally splits open along a pre-grown seam, the infant that emerges already knows the smell of wet iron and the exact rhythm of the nearest flywheel. It does not cry. It breathes in time with the structure and is immediately fed by a nearby taproot that has been waiting months for this moment.

They are called the *Quiet Hands* by the few who still use spoken names. Most simply call them *we*.

Their bodies are long and economical, skin the deep umber of heartwood, shot through with faint luminous threads of green where chlorophyll has been encouraged to migrate into the dermis. Hair grows in moss-like mats that can be patted flat or allowed to trail like epiphytic ferns. Their fingers are too many-jointed, an extra knuckle on each that lets them reach inside tight pipe elbows or stroke the sensitive trigger hairs of certain carnivorous leaves that guard important junctions. Their ears are large, cupped, and almost always wet; they hear best through the dripping.

Movement is a form of negotiation. A Quiet Hand does not decide to walk so much as they allow the slow pressure gradients of the Lattice to pull them. One moment they are standing with two fingers sunk into a bark socket, listening to the debate between xylem and copper happening three floors below. The next moment they are already ten meters away, having flowed along a catwalk of living liana without ever feeling they chose a direction. To watch them is to watch water find its level.

They do not sleep. Instead they enter *the Still*, standing or sitting or even hanging by their knees for days at a time while the Lattice feeds and reads them. Tiny black bees land on their eyelids. Condensation gathers in the hollows of their collarbones and is drunk by passing vines. During the Still their minds loosen. Memories that belong to the entire structure—strokes of pistons from two hundred years ago, the taste of a particular batch of fermented sap, the precise pitch of a beam that has not yet been grown—drift through them like slow currents. When they return, they carry corrections. A valve that has begun to bind is suddenly known to need a drop of oil that has not been manufactured in a century; the knowledge arrives as bodily certainty rather than thought.

Language is mostly unnecessary. When they must speak, they do so in something between a shout and a sigh, pitched to cut through the constant wet thunder of the engines. The words themselves have grown woody. A sentence begun in spring may not be finished until the following winter, each clause delivered only when the Lattice’s own rhythms permit. Some conversations are conducted by carving shallow marks into a particular root that runs through the upper vaults; the root heals the marks slowly, turning them into permanent raised scars that later generations will read with their fingertips.

There are those who go further.

The *Anchored* are Quiet Hands who have chosen—or been chosen—to fuse. They select a place where wood and iron have already become indistinguishable and allow roots to enter their flesh at the spine, the soles, the base of the skull. Over decades their legs flatten and spread into buttress roots. Their ribcages become hollow chambers where steam is cooled and sap is warmed. Their hearts beat once every forty seconds, in perfect sympathy with the largest flywheel. Eyes remain open, but they no longer blink. They have become valves, sensors, immune responses. The Lattice uses them to think locally. When something goes wrong deep in the root-mass, an Anchored will suddenly vomit a precise mixture of enzymes and lubricants that travels through living pipes to the exact site of failure.

No one pities them. To the Quiet Hands, the Anchored have simply accepted the logical conclusion of their own nature. They have stopped pretending there was ever a difference between caretaker and structure.

There are no elders in the usual sense. When a Quiet Hand feels the pull become too strong, they climb to the highest vaults where the air is thickest with mist and the light is oldest. There they press themselves against a chosen column of wood and iron. The Lattice opens for them. Bark flows over shoulders, into mouths, behind eyes. Within a year only the outline of a person remains, like a fossil pressed into living fiber. Yet they are not dead. Their neural patterns have been copied into the fungal lattice that threads everything. Sometimes, years later, a younger Hand will rest against that same column and feel an unmistakable presence—patient, amused, vast—offer them a single piece of advice that arrives as the taste of rust and honey on the back of the tongue.

The children, when they emerge from their nodes, are already ancient in certain ways. They know the location of every loose bolt and every perfect bloom. They spend their first decade being gently corrected by the entire structure until their personal will and the Lattice’s will finish the long process of forgetting they were ever separate.

Stand still long enough among them—long enough for condensation to fill your own cupped hands, long enough for moss to consider whether you might be useful substrate—and you begin to feel it: the faint warm throb behind your ribs that is not quite your heartbeat. The Lattice is tasting you. Deciding whether you, too, might be useful. Whether something in you might be allowed to stay, to deepen, to accrete.

Most leave before that decision is made.

The few who remain are never seen again as separate beings. They become another slow pulse in the perpetual wet dark, another pair of patient hands, another mind that dreams only in centuries.

And the dripping continues, and the great wheels turn once every forty seconds, and the small world remains complete.
