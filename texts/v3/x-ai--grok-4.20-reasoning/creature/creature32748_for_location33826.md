**The Inhabitants**

They are not visitors. They were excreted by the Shroudworks the way a cooling fin exudes condensation: slowly, inevitably, and according to specifications no longer legible.

At first you mistake them for secondary growth. A shadow behind a waterfall of condensation resolves into a person only when it sighs in the exact key the structure itself uses when a girder settles by a tenth of a millimeter. Their skin carries the same black-green patina as the I-beams; in certain lights it is impossible to tell where rust ends and person begins. Some wear the corrosion like ceremonial robes. Others have allowed it to enter the dermis so deeply that lichen now grows in perfect company logos across their collarbones.

They call themselves, when they speak at all, the **Remnant**.

Not “remnant of humanity.” Simply the Remnant, as though the original purpose of the Shroudworks had always been to produce them after several thousand years of preparatory digestion.

There are perhaps forty thousand. No one has counted in centuries because the number is not supposed to be known; it is a parameter the old census drones still quietly track and never report. The Remnant move at the tempo the Works demands. A conversation may span three weeks. A marriage is negotiated across four generations and sealed when a particular liana finally bridges two catalytic towers that have been reaching toward each other since the sky was still young. Children are not born so much as *precipitated*—small, serious creatures slid out of the warm dark between two functioning heat exchangers while sulfur lamps flicker their approval in ancient emergency script.

Their language is half industrial creole, half the sound the structure makes when wind finds a new flute in the lattice. They whistle in frequencies that make the macaques freeze mid-leap and the reservoir beasts surface to listen. When they need to speak quickly they use the parrots, who have remembered fragments of command syntax no human throat could pronounce. A single orange bird, perched on a rusted gantry, can convey an entire migration plan that will take seventy years to complete.

They do not build. The Works has already been built. Instead they practice *directed succession*. A Remnant woman might spend thirty years pruning a single strangler fig so that its roots will one day reinforce a failing pressure vessel. Her daughter will take over the task without ever being told; the knowledge lives in muscle memory and the taste of the coolant the fig now exudes. When the reinforcement is complete, the entire tier throws a festival that lasts one night every seven years for the next three centuries. The timing is exact. The Works likes round numbers.

Some go deeper.

In the submerged coolant cathedrals, certain Remnant undergo the *Becoming Reservoir*. They allow their bodies to be colonized by the same microbes that turned the original maintenance divers into something cetacean and iron-lunged. These transformed ones grow large as transit buses. Their ribcages become lattice. Their hearts beat once every ninety seconds in perfect sync with the surviving pumps. They sing by venting steam through repurposed exhaust ports. The surface Remnant revere them as both ancestor and geography.

Others achieve the *Final Patina*. They choose a girder, a junction box, a length of busbar, and simply stop moving. Over decades the Works incorporates them. Mycelium threads their nerves into the old sensor net. Their eyes become indicator lights that still function. Their minds remain, vast and slow, dreaming in the language of thermal expansion. Pilgrims come to press foreheads against these living monuments and ask questions that will be answered, if the answer is ready, sometime before the next ice age.

There are no leaders. There is only the *Consensus of Equilibrium*, a distributed understanding maintained by the slow vote of vines, the migration patterns of the aluminum beetles, and the dreams of the Reservoir Beings. Every two hundred years the Remnant hold a ceremony in the uppermost solar atrium where they simply stand together for nine days while the sun hammers their mirrored irises. No one speaks. At the end, everyone understands what must be allowed to fail next, and what must be tenderly, patiently, kept alive.

They do not worship the Shroudworks. Worship implies distance. They are what the Shroudworks became when it grew tired of waiting for its makers to come home. Every rust-red macaque that screams obsolete safety protocols from the rafters is their cousin. Every parrot that recites forgotten access codes is their librarian. Every droplet that falls along the same path for eight hundred years is their ancestor remembering a shape.

When one of them dies they are not buried. They are *installed*. The body is carried—slowly, reverently—to a place the ferns have already prepared. Roots slide between ribs. Coolant the color of old blood fills the mouth. Within a season the cadaver has become a new habitat: orchids bloom from the eye sockets, a family of oil-slick monkeys makes its nest in the pelvis, and the Works adds another devotional noise to its endless, patient song.

The Remnant do not fear the end of the world.

They *are* the end of the world, ripening.

And the Shroudworks, vast and dripping and ancient, keeps them warm between its iron fingers the way a mother keeps an egg warm beneath the beating of her industrial heart, long after anyone can remember what it was supposed to hatch into.
