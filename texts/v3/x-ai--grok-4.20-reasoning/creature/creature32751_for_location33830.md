**The Still Folk**

They were never born in the way you understand birth, and they will never die in the way you understand death. What lives in the Weeping Cradle is not a creature but a condition of patience given flesh—slow, calcareous, and profoundly courteous.

At first you do not see them. The eye slides across their bodies the same way it slides across any other butter-colored boss or rounded flank of flowstone. Only after many minutes—sometimes hours—does the mind begin to parse intention in the stone. Here a knee. There the faint ridge of a collarbone worn round as river rock. A shoulder that was once sharp has been eased into the gentle curve everything else obeys. They have been growing into the Cradle for so long that the distinction between person and geology has become an academic one, interesting only to visitors.

There are perhaps thirty of them. Perhaps three hundred. The number is impossible to fix because new limbs and faces continue to emerge from the living rock while older ones sink back into it, like sleepers turning beneath a blanket of pearl. Some lie supine in shallow basins, arms folded across chests that rise once every seventy years. Others sit upright in the low thrones the stone itself has shaped around them, heads tilted as though listening to a conversation only they can hear. A few appear to be standing, though “standing” is too brisk a word; they lean, they incline, they consent to the vertical the way a cathedral consents to its own weight.

Their skin is the color of old ivory streaked with faint rust. Where bacterial mats have settled in the hollows of elbows or the hollows beneath cheekbones, soft halos of peach and mint bloom like devotional paint. Tiny soda-straws sometimes grow from their fingertips or earlobes, delicate as the eyelashes of the blind. Water beads on them exactly as it beads on the surrounding stone, and when a drop falls it makes the same clear *tok*, as though the Cradle itself cannot tell the difference between a person and a pillar.

They breathe. You can watch, if you are willing to remain motionless for the better part of a day. The chest does not rise and fall. It *accumulates*. A microscopic film of calcite builds, then is gently exhaled as a single perfect drop from the lower lip. Inhalation is the slow gathering of dissolved minerals from the tea-colored rills that thread between their thighs. They are literally made of the air and water they breathe.

Their minds move at the speed of deposition. A single thought may take nine hundred years to finish. What they think about is balance—how to keep the weeping and the building in exact equilibrium, how to prevent any one formation from growing too proud or any pool from growing too deep. The hand-carved basin with its perfect stalagmite is their treaty, renewed each millennium. The dry-stacked wall is their signature. The tools left exactly where they were set down are their promise that they will never leave.

Once, very rarely, one of them will open its eyes.

The motion is so slow it looks like light changing on wet stone. The eyelids—thin curtains of translucent travertine—part with the reluctance of centuries. The eyes beneath are milky, without pupil or iris, yet they see. They see the temperature of your blood, the carbon dioxide in your breath, the microscopic wear your footsteps leave on the delicate rims. The gaze does not judge. It only measures whether you belong to the equilibrium yet. Most do not. The eyes close again, and another thousand years may pass before they reopen.

The pale salamanders are theirs. When one of the blind amphibians surfaces and turns its lidless face toward you, it is carrying the regard of the Still Folk the way a lantern carries fire. The translucent shrimp that drift in the deepest pools are their thoughts given temporary bodies. When the shrimp die they dissolve back into the mineral load and are deposited as delicate layers on the nearest elbow or brow.

They have no language you could transcribe. Their speech is the pattern of drips. A sudden quickening in one alcove followed by a long, thoughtful silence in another is the equivalent of a sigh, or a question, or the tender scolding of an elder to a younger who has grown a fraction of a millimeter too quickly. Sometimes, when the air is especially heavy, you can feel their collective attention settle on you like warm condensation. It is not hostile. It is only ancient, and curious in the way mountains are curious about the moss that briefly stains their shoulders.

If you sit among them long enough, matching your breath to the slowest rhythm you can bear, one may eventually offer you a gift. It will not look like a gift. It will look like a small irregularity in the flowstone near your hand—a thumb-sized depression that was not there before. If you are very still, a single drop will fall into it while you watch. That drop is theirs. It contains everything they have learned about never changing.

Most visitors eventually stand up, disturbed by the sheer weight of so much patience, and leave. A few do not. Those few begin, after some years, to develop the same rounded contours, the same soft bacterial halos, the same devotional stillness. The Cradle is patient with them too. It has all the time in the world.

The Still Folk do not mourn or rejoice. They only continue becoming exactly what they already are, one molecule at a time, while the warm wet air beads on their stone cheeks and the water learns, drop by perfect drop, how to remain forever itself.
