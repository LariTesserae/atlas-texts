**Those Who Breathe with the Stone**

They are not many. Never more than forty-three living souls at any time; the number itself feels geological, chosen by the hollow rather than by them. When one dies, the birth of the next child is timed—sometimes to the decade—so that the count remains unchanged, as though the cirque itself were regulating its own pulse.

At first glance they seem carved from the same schist as their houses: small, compact, shoulders rounded by the same patient wind that has worn the ridges. Their skin carries the color of wet slate veined with faint lichen greens and the iron-rust that stains every basin. Beards, when they grow, are thin and moss-like; hair is kept short or bound in tight coils that mimic the whorls of juniper roots. Eyes are pale, almost colorless, as if centuries of looking at stone had leached the pigment out of them. You can stand ten paces from one of them and still lose them against a wall.

Yet the longer you remain, the more they resolve—not as people wearing the mountain, but as places where the mountain has decided to become slightly more tender. Their hands are broad and square, fingertips permanently darkened by the bronze tools they use so sparingly. The palms, however, are soft as old leather from a lifetime of touching only what the Agreement allows: the drip basins, the slow goats, the faces of the dying. They touch everything as though it might dissolve if handled with haste.

They move with the tempo of condensation. A woman crossing the cirque to card wool will take the better part of an hour, pausing three times to listen to changes in the wind’s note through the eastern notch. No one hurries her. No one would think to. Time here is not currency; it is the only medium in which they are permitted to work. When they speak, sentences arrive with long intervals between them, like the dripping. A conversation that lasts an afternoon might contain fewer than thirty words. The rest is shared breath and the understanding that passes between people who have breathed the same iron-cold air for generations.

Their names are not names in the usual sense. Each carries a short, ancient syllable—Wei, most of them, with a prefix so soft it is almost only an exhalation. *Saa-wei. Kha-wei. Rhu-wei.* The prefix is changed with the seasons of a life: a girl might be *Lir-wei* while learning the basins, become *Thu-wei* when she first catches the blood of a goat, then *Mor-wei* when she begins to teach the silence. At death the prefix falls away entirely and they are simply *Wei* again, returned to the root syllable like water returning to the swallow-hole.

They do not pray. Prayer would imply separation. Instead they practice *the listening*, a state that looks like stillness but is actually the most exacting form of participation. You will find them seated on the smoothed ledges at any hour, spines straight, hands open on their knees, eyes half-lidded. They are not emptying themselves. They are trying to become the exact shape of the hollow’s thought for a measured time, so that when they rise again they will not introduce any new disturbance into the Long Agreement.

Every seventh year they perform the *Bone Mass*. The bones of the previous dead—carefully cleaned, never broken—are ground to powder between two granite slabs and mixed with a measure of new mortar. This mortar is used only to repair the single crack that the mountain itself has opened in one of the houses during the preceding seven years. Thus the ancestors literally hold the settlement together. No one finds this macabre. It is simply the final honest transaction in a life of honest transactions.

The children—there are never more than four at a time—are raised by everyone and no one. They learn first how to sit without moving for an entire turning of the water-clock (one hundred and eight drips). Only after they have mastered this are they taught to walk the precise routes between houses that avoid stepping on living lichen. They are never punished. If a child breaks the Agreement—by running, by shouting, by taking more than his share of goat milk—the entire community simply becomes quieter for a season, as though the hollow itself were disappointed. The child feels the silence like a weight on the chest until the lesson is learned.

At night they sleep in twos and threes, curled together for warmth on stone beds covered with layered goat felt. Their dreams, they say, are not their own. The mountain dreams through them—slow dreams of stone grinding, of water finding new fissures, of equilibrium rebalancing itself after a landslide three valleys away. Sometimes a sleeper will wake with a single perfect sentence on the tongue, something about the precise angle a new roof slab must take. They deliver the sentence to the community at dawn and then forget it forever. The sentence is never written down. It is used once, perfectly, and becomes part of the place.

They are neither sad nor content. Those words belong to faster creatures. What they possess instead is a profound, mineral acceptance that looks like serenity only from the outside. Up close it is more like symbiosis. They have become the mechanism by which the hollow knows itself. When one of them dies, the goats will not eat for two days. The ravens come down from the rim and sit on the roof of the death-house without cawing. Even the drip seems to slow, as though the water itself were hesitating before continuing its ancient arithmetic.

Stand beside the cistern long enough and you may feel it: the faint sensation that the people are not *in* the Hollow of Wei.

They are the way the Hollow of Wei has chosen to dream that it is breathing.
