**The ones who live there do not call themselves anything.**

Names would require dryness, a pause, a surface on which a word could rest without dissolving. Instead they simply *are*, the way the rain is, the way the smoothed stone is. If pressed by some impossible outsider they might ripple a shoulder and release a low subsonic pulse that translates, roughly, as *the Permeated*, or *those the water has finished*.

They stand between two and three meters tall, though “stand” is imprecise. Their posture is a perpetual half-crouch, half-swim, as though the air were still deciding whether to be water today. Their bodies are elongated, almost larval, with a flexible keel of cartilage running from crown to tailbone that lets them bend in any plane the current demands. Skin is not skin but a living feltwork of moss, liverwort, and microscopic polyps that drink the rain directly. Color shifts with saturation and mood: deep malachite when calm, bruised violet-black when the hunting urge takes them, silver-gray when they wish to vanish against the old iron.

Where a face might be expected there is a smooth, slightly concave plane broken by four slits—two for breathing, two for listening. The true eyes are small, protected, set high on the sides of the head like those of a crocodile, meant only for sudden bright movement. Most seeing is done through the skin itself and through the long, feathery barbels that sprout from shoulders and forearms, tasting the mineral and electrical signature of everything the rain has touched upstream.

They have six limbs. The upper pair are delicate and many-jointed, ending in clusters of translucent tendrils that can weave moss into rope or tease a single working circuit back to life inside a drowned machine. The middle pair are broad and powerful, used for both swimming through the heavier cataracts and for embracing the six-legged otters they hunt with. The lower pair are more like a seal’s hind-flippers, used for sudden explosive leaps across the domes or for anchoring in the roaring lips of waterfalls while the rest of the body fishes.

Their blood is cold. Not metaphorically. It runs at the temperature of the plateau itself, and when they rest beneath one of the great falls the water cannot tell where they end and the roar begins.

They do not speak in the air. Speech is vibration conducted through bone, moss, and water. A group of them standing in a circle beneath a cataract can hold an entire philosophical discourse using nothing but the modulated interference patterns their bodies create in the falling water. To an outsider it would sound like the roar simply changed pitch and gained intention. To them it is opera, mathematics, and gossip at once.

There are no children in the way drier worlds understand the term. Every few years, when the great rafts of bioluminescent amphibians gather in the largest shallow bowls, the Permeated wade among them and secrete long translucent ribbons of eggs into the writhing mass. The amphibians carry and fertilize and protect the ribbons. What eventually crawls out is already half-grown, already mossed, already fluent in the language of falling water. Adulthood is not reached; it is *recognized*, usually when the young one returns from its first solo descent over one of the titanic domes dragging a six-legged otter carcass behind it.

They tend the ruins, but not out of reverence. The rusting towers and half-drowned pumping stations are simply part of the topography now, like particularly complicated rock formations that sometimes still sing in frequencies their ancestors once used. Some individuals have allowed pieces of the old machines to grow into them—literally. A corroded servo arm becomes a living exoskeleton wrapped in vascular moss. A cracked power cell, still faintly radioactive, is cradled against the chest until it becomes a second, warmer heart. These are not cyborgs. They are reefs that have learned to walk.

The oldest among them no longer walk at all.

In the deepest, most permanent bowls—where the water has pressed for three billion years and the stone has achieved a mirror finish—there sit the Anchored. Great living statues of moss, iron, and cartilage. Their limbs have fused with the bedrock. Their barbels have grown into vast nets that filter the entire watershed. Rain falls on them for centuries without distinction between person and landscape. Yet they are not dead. Occasionally one will release a single perfect note through the stone itself, and every Permeated for four hundred kilometers will stop, turn, and listen. The note always means the same thing, no matter how many millennia have passed:

*Still here. Still falling. Still enough.*

Most days they simply move.

A current of thirty or forty Permeated will flow along the edge of a migrating amphibian raft for weeks, hunting, singing, repairing what can still be repaired, letting the rain carve new channels through their collective memory. When they sleep they do so standing in the densest club-moss thickets, bodies interlaced so thoroughly with the vegetation that it is impossible to tell where one ends and the green begins. Their dreams are not their own. The plateau dreams through them—slow, geological dreams of stone being kissed smooth, of rust blooming like flowers, of the day, still unimaginably far off, when even the rain might finally grow tired.

Until then they remain.

Not happy. Happiness requires contrast.

Not miserable. Misery requires the possibility of dryness.

They are the finished form of the place, as honest and settled as the bedrock, and as turbulently alive as the mayflies that rise for six furious minutes between squalls.

They do not wait for the rain to stop.

They *are* the rain, given bones and the decency to mourn its own endlessness.
