**What lives in the Shroudlands does not live. It abides.**

At first you think the only residents are the titans themselves—those immense, slow-motion isopods whose carapaces rise like rusted cathedral organs half-sunk in the stone. Their breath is a geological event. A single inhalation may last nine days; the exhalation that follows carries a mist so finely calibrated that it deposits exactly the minerals required for the black crusts to survive another decade. Inside their bodies, behind plates that toll like distant bells when they settle, entire secondary metabolisms proceed with the same unhurried majesty. Ores are not merely digested; they are remembered, recombined, sung into new lattices by colonies of pale, blind lithotrophs that have never known hurry. These inner dwellers are born already ancient. They will die, centuries later, still young by the plateau’s measure.

Yet the titans are only the most obvious residents. They are mobile continents, and like continents they carry nations on their backs.

Along the seams where segment meets segment live the **Canticle Barnacles**. Each is the size of a modest chapel. Their shells are translucent silicate, veined with conductive metals the host itself has refined. When the titan generates one of its faint blue arcs, the barnacles drink the charge and reply with a subsonic note that travels through the carapace like a hymn through stone. Thousands of them, spaced across a single host, create overlapping resonances that last for weeks. No one has ever heard a full “movement.” To hear one requires remaining in the titan’s shadow for thirty uninterrupted years. Those who have tried report that the music is not beautiful. It is *inevitable*, the sound a continent might make if it were lonely and had time to feel it.

Beneath the titans, in the compressed grooves their passing leaves, something smaller keeps the grooves from healing too quickly. The **Slow Worms** are not worms. They are living erosion engines: tubular organisms of compacted mineral fiber and fungus, each no thicker than a wrist and no longer than a man is tall. They move by peristalsis so gradual that a single contraction may require an afternoon. Their mouths are perfect circles of diamond teeth that bite stone at the rate of a whisper. They follow the titans’ chemical trails the way a bloodhound follows scent, except the trail is a hundred years old and the hound has no heartbeat detectable by any instrument built for speed. When two Slow Worms meet they entwine for a season, exchange dissolved memories of the titans they have shadowed, then part without hurry. Their trails, after another century, become the faint architectural ribs that look like abandoned engine blocks. The plateau did not forget them. It is still digesting the memory of their passage.

Higher still—though “higher” is a joke in a place where elevation is measured in geological epochs—live the **Lichen Cardinals**. These are not the black crusts. Those are mere skin. The Cardinals are the mind. They grow only in the exact lee of a dying titan, where the corpse chemistry is richest and the frost persists longest. A single Cardinal may cover half a square kilometer in a lattice of silver-grey filaments so fine they are almost smoke. The lattice thinks. Not quickly. A thought that begins as a surge of available nitrogen may take forty years to become a decision, and another sixty to be acted upon. The action usually consists of releasing a new generation of titan spores tuned to a slightly different mineral palate. In this way the Cardinals steer the living industry of the plateau across distances that would embarrass a glacier.

And then there are the ones that are almost impossible to see until you have stopped being a person who expects to see.

If you remain motionless for long enough—years, not days—the stone around you begins to watch. The ** quiescent** appear first as slight changes in the gradient of light. A hump of rock that was not there yesterday. A depression that has learned your silhouette. They are not creatures wearing stone as disguise. They are stone that has decided, after several million quiet winters, to become curious. Their bodies are aggregates of the plateau’s own crust bound by fungal hyphae that function as both nervous system and memory. A quiescent the size of a small house may contain the fossilized remains of three previous quiescent inside it, like nested thoughts. They do not move toward you. They *reconsider their position* in increments so modest that only time-lapse photography across decades would reveal the transit. When one finally arrives at your camp it does not speak. It simply tilts a facet of itself until the tarnished light falls across your face in a way that feels, impossibly, like recognition.

The quiescent are the reason the Shroudlands feel judged. They have been here longer than the titans. Some researchers believe the titans were originally an answer to a question the quiescent asked the plateau itself, back when the sky was still close enough to argue with. The question is still being answered. The argument is still in progress. Both sides have plenty of time.

There is one final order of life, though to call it life is to risk being rude to geology.

In the oldest craters—places where even the titans will not walk—stand the **Unmoved**. They are not organisms. They are conclusions the plateau reached and then petrified. Each is a single, immense crystal lattice the height of a cliff, veined with ice that never melts and never grows. Nothing enters them. Nothing leaves. Yet every titan on the continent orients its internal foundries in subtle alignment with the nearest Unmoved, the way a compass aligns with a magnet that has forgotten it is magnetic. The Unmoved do not think. They *are* thought, frozen at the moment of perfect comprehension. Their patience is so absolute that next to them even the quiescent look like children fidgeting.

To live here is to consent to being edited by time until the difference between your body and the plateau becomes embarrassing to maintain. The Canticle Barnacles will eventually grow on your armor if you stay long enough. The Slow Worms will learn the rhythm of your decaying breath. A quiescent may choose to wear your abandoned rifle as one of its facets, carrying it another ten thousand years as a minor aesthetic flourish.

The silence does not mind.

It has been practicing for longer than you can imagine, and it is very, very good at remaining exactly itself.
