Here, in the last and slowest movement of the Sinking Choir, live the **Cantors**.

They are not easy to find. At first you mistake them for another buttress that has worn down into the likeness of a shoulder, a bowed head, a trailing sleeve of stone. Only after many hours—when your own blood has slowed to match the water—do you notice that one of the columns is breathing.

A Cantor is roughly the height of a man, though it has forgotten how to stand straight. Its body is an accreted compromise between flesh and architecture: ribs splayed outward like vaulting ribs, skin thickened by centuries of calcite into a waxy, translucent rind the color of old tallow. Where the mineral layer is thinnest you can sometimes see the faint blue ghost of an organ pulsing once every ninety heartbeats. The limbs have lengthened and simplified; the lower ones have fused with the floor so that the creature appears to rise directly from the stone it once carved. Fingers have become delicate buttresses, each tip drilled through by a living tube-worm that sways like a tiny censer, perpetually offering invisible incense to the dark.

Their faces are the most difficult to look at for long. The original features have been erased by erosion and deliberate self-sculpting. What remains is a soft, blunt oval with two shallow depressions where eyes once were. The mouth has migrated to the throat and opened into a permanent, silent O—less an organ of speech than a resonance chamber. From it issues no sound, only a slow, warm exhalation that carries a precise chemical signature. That breath is their only voice. The shrimp read it like sheet music. The blind eels interpret the gradients and adjust the orbits of their dreaming spirals by a single degree. The living handkerchiefs drift closer, unfurl their fans, and become the tremolo.

There are never more than thirteen Cantors at any time. They arrange themselves according to an geometry only they remember, occupying the precise nodes where the structure’s ancient ratios converge. Some stand upright in the narrow choir-stalls, bodies half-grown into the stone. Others have lain down across the vaults so that their backs form new floors for the pale shrimp to graze. One has folded itself into a narrow shaft between two pillars and remained there so long that its chest has become a nesting chimney for the tube-worms; when it inhales, the entire colony contracts in unison, a single slow lung.

They do not eat. The chemosynthetic film that coats their skin is enough. Every few decades a Cantor will detach a small piece of its own calcified flesh—never more than a finger-joint’s worth—and allow it to drift away like a wafer. The shrimp descend upon it in perfect silence. In this way the Cantors slowly diminish, becoming less body and more Choir with every century.

Movement is almost insulting to them. When one must shift position it does so over the course of a human lifetime. You might return after thirty years and find that the figure which once faced north now faces northeast by two degrees. That is a decisive doctrinal statement. The others will answer by altering the angle of their own spines a fraction more. The water translates these arguments into current, current into chemistry, chemistry into the orbital paths of eels. The hymn continues.

If you remain long enough to begin forgetting your own name, one of them may acknowledge you. It will not turn its head. Instead it will open its throat a fraction wider and release a single dense droplet of memory. The droplet tastes of iron and candle-smoke and the particular grief that comes when a god outlives every worshipper. Ingest it and you will understand, for one slow heartbeat, why they chose to drown their cathedral from the inside.

They are not immortal. Eventually a Cantor’s heart simply forgets the next beat. When that happens the body does not fall. It settles, softening, until it is indistinguishable from the surrounding stone. The other Cantors then migrate—one glacial inch at a time—to fill the new silence in the chord. A fresh Cantor is never “born.” It is grown. In some alcove a statue that has worn down to almost nothing will, over centuries, begin to accumulate flesh instead of losing it. The stone grows warm. The depressions that were once eyes deepen. One day the new Cantor opens its throat and the shrimp change direction by a single perfect degree.

No one has ever seen the moment the transformation is complete. The water is patient. The Choir is patient. The Cantors have become the patience itself.

Sometimes, very rarely, a visitor who has stayed until their own bones feel rounded tries to join them. They press their cheek to the warm stone, slow their breathing, offer their name to the iron-tasting dark. Most are gently forgotten and drift away as another rounded shape. A few are kept. You can recognize them by the faint human outline still visible beneath the calcite, like a face pressed to the underside of ice. They never move again. They have become the lowest, slowest note in the hymn—the one that makes the others possible.

The Cantors do not mourn or rejoice. Mourning and rejoicing are quick things.

They only sing, without sound, without end, ten thousand years per measure, until even the idea of an ending dissolves into velvet stone and warm water and the patient, patient dark.
