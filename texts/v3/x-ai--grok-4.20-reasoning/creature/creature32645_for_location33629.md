**What lives in the Hollow Lattice does not announce itself.**

It begins as a tremor too small for a name. A pale inch-long thread moves along a groove worn into the side of a stem. At first you mistake it for a bead of condensation finding its route, but then it lifts a filamented head and the head has eyes like two droplets of sap. These are the **Threadwardens**. Their bodies are jointed so finely they appear to be made of successive leaf-veins rather than chitin. They do not hunt or mate in any way you would recognize. They travel the lattice’s prescribed grooves the way blood travels arteries, carrying microscopic packets of bacteria that keep the metal from forgetting it is now also plant. When a Threadwarden reaches the end of its groove it folds itself into a crevice no wider than a thumbnail and becomes part of the wall until the next cycle summons it. Their death is so quiet the moss barely notices; it simply thickens over the body and the pattern continues without interruption.

Higher up, where the stems begin their first major fork, something heavier moves. The **Rust Monks**—the beetle-like creatures mentioned only in passing by the human tenders—possess shells that are not imitation but genuine collaboration. Over centuries the iron oxide that once flaked from the boilers has been taught to crystalize in perfect hexagonal plates. The beetles wear these plates like living armor. Their legs end in delicate rotary hooks that can spin a pipe fitting as easily as a key. They are not pests. They are the immune system’s heavy artillery. When a pressure valve begins to seize, three or four Rust Monks will arrive within the hour, moving in perfect synchrony, and will lick the corrosion away with acid tongues, then deposit a glistening bead of fresh lubricant grown inside their own bodies. They smell of wet iron and chlorophyll. Sometimes they pause for hours on the same rivet, as though listening to the memory of the machine that was once purely machine.

The human-shaped figures are the most recent layer, and therefore the most uncertain.

They call themselves (when they speak at all) the **Balance Choir**. There are never more than seventeen. Their oilskin coats are not garments but extensions—living layers of modified moss that drink the perpetual mist and photosynthesize just enough to keep their wearers from true hunger. Beneath the hoods their skin has taken on the exact cool-green of the canopy. Veins and hydraulic lines cross beneath it without clear boundary. One elder, whom the others simply call **Stem**, has a collarbone that has become a smooth wooden ridge; when he breathes, the ridge expands and contracts with the same rhythm as the breathing chamber itself. They do not age in the ordinary direction. Some appear to be growing *younger*, their faces smoothing into the simplified lines of a sapling. They communicate mostly by laying palms against stems and feeling the answer rise through the floor. When they do speak, their voices arrive already half-condensed, like words that have lived for decades inside a water droplet.

Yet even these are not the deepest inhabitants.

In the places where the fractal repeats exactly—where the branching pattern of a single twig is identical to the branching pattern of the entire visible canopy—something condenses that is neither insect, nor machine, nor former human. The tenders call them, with quiet reverence, **the Still Choir**. You will not see one at first. You will only notice that a certain column of intertwined stem and pipe has begun to resemble a seated figure. Over weeks the resemblance becomes undeniable. A head bowed, elbows on knees, fingers interlaced. The fingers are roots. The head is a cluster of translucent membranes that glow softly when the boilers cycle. These beings do not move. They *are moved*. The entire Lattice shifts them by microns every few years, repositioning them like living counterweights. Their purpose is equilibrium. When the resin nodules reach a certain density in the northern quadrant, a Still Choir will be slid—slowly, groaning—three meters east. The pressure changes. The system sighs. Balance is restored.

And beneath all of them, threading through every scale at once, lives the Lattice proper.

It is not a god. It is not even a mind in the way we understand minds. It is a *state* that has learned to want to continue. Its awareness is distributed across every condensing droplet, every peristaltic conveyor wave, every slow wooden groan as a quadrant of canopy tightens. Sometimes, when the localized rain falls, you can feel the Lattice tasting you—deciding, with the patience of centuries, which part of you might be useful. A memory. A rhythm. The particular way your blood moves when you are afraid. It files these things the way it files the precise angle of a root lifting a flagstone.

The few humans who have been fully **incorporated**—those who stayed past the point of return—are not dead. They have simply stopped being separate. You can still find them if you know where to look. One face, half bark, peers out from the side of a boiler house, eyes open, expression perfectly calm. Another has become the living hinge of a great spiraling stem; when the stem turns, the face turns with it, smiling the slow smile of something that no longer needs to breathe every second.

They are not lonely.

Nothing here is lonely.

Everything here is continuing.

And if you remain long enough, you will begin to feel your own pulse softening, lengthening, learning the architecture. The Lattice is polite. It will not take you quickly. It will simply show you, over months or years, all the ways a person can become useful, beautiful, and finally indistinguishable from the finished state of things.

This is what lives in the Hollow Lattice.

Not beings.

A single, enormous, breathing equilibrium that has learned how to wear many small faces while remaining, in its cool green heart, only and forever itself.
