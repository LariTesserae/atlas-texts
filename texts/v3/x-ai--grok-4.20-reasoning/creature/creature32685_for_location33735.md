**The Hollow Crown is lived in by the Unfinished.**

They are not a people who arrived. They are what the rock began to do when it grew tired of being only stone.

At first you notice only motion that refuses to resolve. A rust-colored swallow wheels past; then you realize the swallow is being watched by a child whose skin is the same shade as the weathered pillar behind her, so that for a moment the two seem one creature interrupted mid-transformation. The child does not blink often. Few of them do. Their eyelids have grown heavy and slow, as if even the act of closing must be budgeted.

They call themselves the Unfinished because they believe the mountain is still deciding what shape it will ultimately keep, and they are the part of the decision that breathes. To finish would be to betray the stone.

Their bodies are compact, almost foreshortened, with wide palms and feet that splay slightly so they can grip the waxy succulents without tearing them. Skin ranges from the pale grey of shaded stone to the deep bronze of sun-struck faces; every adult bears a faint sheen of the same waxy bloom the plants produce, secreted now through their own pores. It is not metaphor. Their sweat does not evaporate. It is reabsorbed through microscopic channels in the skin the way the rope-vines reabsorb dew that never touches ground. They smell faintly of warm resin and bird lime, a scent so constant that strangers notice it for three days and then cease to smell anything at all.

There are no fat ones and no frail ones past childhood. The equilibrium is merciless. A body that demands more water than it returns is simply not born twice. Midwives read the scalp of every newborn for the faint luminous stippling that marks acceptable thirst. Those without it are sung to sleep one final time and placed in the Root Cradles—narrow fissures where their small moisture is offered directly to the iron-barked trees. The trees grow one spiral tighter in gratitude. Everyone pretends not to count the extra rings.

The old do not die in the usual way. When someone feels the long compression beginning in their chest, they walk to the Narrow Choir, a vertical alley so tight that two people cannot pass. There they wedge themselves upright between living ropes of vine and simply stop moving. The swallows land on their shoulders. The beetles walk across their open eyes. Within a week the body has become architectural: another textured surface for the mats of succulent to colonize, another warm stone radiating the day’s heat into the night. The family stands at the opening of the alley and sings the Leaving Note—a single tone held until the singer’s own lungs begin to burn. Then they leave the ancestor where he has chosen to become part of the Crown’s final, patient shape.

Their language is mostly breath. Consonants have been worn away over generations the way the mountains below were worn away. What remains are long, modulated vowels and clicks of tongue against palate that mimic the gecko-chimes. A sentence can last twenty minutes and contain three distinct meanings depending on how the speaker’s hands rest on the stone while speaking. Children learn by pressing their palms to their mother’s throat while she sings, feeling the vibration travel through bone that has itself been carved by generations of the same song.

They have no gods, only grandmothers.

The grandmothers are not all female, nor all living. Some are the standing dead in the Narrow Choir. Some are the oldest trees whose spiral trunks have grown into rough semblances of faces. Some are simply the memory of a particular pattern of reflected light that moves across a certain wall at the same hour every seven years. When the living need judgment they go to one of these and wait until the place itself answers. Sometimes the answer is a sudden gust that smells of fermented sap. Sometimes it is the sound of a single beetle landing on a leaf. They accept either with the same grave nod.

At dusk the entire population moves upward in a slow, wordless procession along the cantilevered stairs and rope bridges. They carry no lights. They do not need them. Each person places a hand on the shoulder of the one ahead, forming a single continuous organism that climbs the Hollow Crown like a vine seeking its final height. At the summit—really only the place where the eight pillars almost touch—they sit together in silence while the last light ricochets between the stone faces. For a few minutes every carved doorway and every living bridge is illuminated in sequence, as though the Crown is turning the pages of its own book and allowing them to read.

Then the light fails.

In that moment the Unfinished begin to sing. Not the Leaving Note. Something older. A sound so low it feels like the mountain clearing its throat. The swallows fall silent. The geckos stop chiming. Even the wind seems to pause on the stair.

The song is not beautiful. It is inevitable.

It is the sound the rock would make if the rock had decided, after ten million years, that it wanted to be held.

And when the song ends, the people descend again into their carved rooms and hanging gardens and narrow streets that are never wider than a cart. They curl against one another on stone beds worn concave by centuries of the same hips, the same shoulders. They breathe out. They breathe in. They pass the same molecules of water back and forth between their lungs and the lungs of the vines and the roots and the small slate-colored browsers that wander across the rooftops at night, licking condensation from human skin as tenderly as calves nursing.

Nothing is wasted.

Nothing is finished.

In the Hollow Crown, to live is to be the place realizing, very slowly, that it is lonely, and deciding—over millennia—to keep itself company in the smallest, densest, loudest, warmest way possible.

The Unfinished do not hope for paradise.

They *are* the paradise the stone invented when it grew tired of being alone with the sky.
