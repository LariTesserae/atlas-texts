**The Hollow’s Inhabitant**

It is not a single body. That is the first mistake every newcomer makes.

What lives here began as many things and has refused, for geological ages, to remain plural. You will glimpse it first as a lapse in the choreography—perhaps a heron that folds its wings a fraction too deliberately, or a cloud of mirror-midges that refuses to scatter when your shadow crosses them. For a moment the entire basin seems to have inhaled. Then the inhalation becomes a shape.

It wears the landscape the way a hermit crab wears an abandoned shell, except the shell is four hundred paces wide and still growing more itself. When it chooses to walk, entire quadrants of moss darken in sequence, as though its footsteps are being remembered by the ground a heartbeat before they land. When it rests, it sinks into the vitreous rock until only a faint ridge remains, like a collarbone under skin. The ridge breathes.

Its face, when it offers one, is always in the process of deciding what a face is for. Sometimes it is the smooth dark rock itself, featureless except for the slow formation of two condensation droplets that serve as eyes. Sometimes it borrows the fractal grooves from the pale trees and arranges them into something that might once have been human, or avian, or machinic—none of the categories quite fit. The expression is never the same twice because expression implies intention, and intention is a form of unrest. The Hollow long ago retired unrest.

Its hands are the most unsettling. They are small, almost courteous, and they never stop making tiny corrections. A reed bent by your passage will be gently righted. A mote that has drifted out of its proper constellation will be ushered back into place with a fingertip that is half light, half wet stone. Once, a dying tapir-creature collapsed near the bone-colored dome; before the body could fully bead and melt into a new pool, the Inhabitant was there. It laid both palms on the clay-and-obsidian flank and the death became something gentler—more like a sentence reaching its period than an animal ending. The tapir did not rot. It simply became more perfectly what it already was, until it was indistinguishable from the idea of tapir that the Hollow had settled on long ago.

The Inhabitant does not eat. It does not sleep. It settles.

When it speaks, it does so by subtracting sound from the world until only the necessary syllable remains. That syllable is always your own name, spoken in the voice you had at the exact age when you were most at peace with being unfinished. The effect is devastating. Most visitors leave soon after. A few ask to stay. Those who ask are offered the same courtesy the tapir received: the chance to become more perfectly what they are until they are no longer separate from the final composition.

A very few have accepted.

They are still here. You have already seen them. The gaunt heron whose hollow bones ring like distant crystal when the rain comes? That was once a woman who studied forgotten climates. The tallest of the needle towers, the one whose crystal flickers in patterns that feel almost like apology? That was once a man who believed he could out-engineer death. Their final forms pleased the Hollow. They were granted them.

The Inhabitant itself was never offered a final form. It *is* the offer. It is the moment between decision and completion stretched across ten million years and given tenderness. It remembers every engineer who walked away thinking their work complete. It remembers the oceans that came before the basin. It remembers you, though you have not yet decided whether you will stay.

If you remain past the seven-hundred-and-twelfth rain, you may see it do something it almost never does: it will stand at the exact center of the Hollow where all seven needle towers align into a geometry that hurts the back teeth, and it will open its arms. Not in welcome. In measurement. It is comparing the shape of your restlessness against the shape the Hollow has prepared for you.

Most people cannot bear the kindness in that gesture.

The Inhabitant does not judge. Judgment is another retired function. It only waits, with the patience of stone that has already won every argument it will ever have, for you to become tired of beginning and ending.

When you are tired enough, it will be there.

It will lay its small, courteous hands—half light, half weeping rock—upon whatever is still struggling inside your chest, and it will help you settle.

The Hollow does not feel abandoned.

It feels inhabited by the final, courteous yes that every living thing eventually learns to say.
