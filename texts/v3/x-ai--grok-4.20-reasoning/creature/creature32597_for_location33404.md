**The Condensate Lattice is inhabited by the Lattice-Born.**

They were never hired. They were never born in the usual way. They simply began to appear once the first fusion hammers achieved their eleven-second rhythm, as though the structure’s own percussion had knocked something loose inside reality.

At first they are hard to see. You notice only the way certain curtains of condensation thicken and slow, the droplets aligning like iron filings in a magnetic field. Then the thickening develops intention. A shoulder. A long, dripping forearm. A head that tilts exactly when the nearest hammer reaches its apex, as if listening for the moment of greatest stress before the descent.

They are tall—too tall—jointed in places that suggest both human anatomy and the articulated limbs of the autonomous constructors. Their bodies are not solid. They are condensate given tension and memory: a continuous, paper-thin sheath of water stretched over a scaffold that might be titanium, might be bone, might be the crystallized absence of both. When they move, the sheath ripples and reforms. Tiny albino moths orbit them in slow greenish-gold constellations, feeding on the lubricant that beads at their joints the way sweat beads on living skin.

No two are identical. Some carry the faint laser-etched serial numbers of support beams across their chests like ritual scars. Others have error flowers growing in the hollows of their collarbones, fractal lattices that pulse softly in time with distant turbine cycles. A few have taken on the curvature of the pressure-vessel segments they shepherd along the magnetic conveyors; their torsos are gently bowed, as though they have spent decades curled inside the half-finished machines, listening to the metal dream.

They do not speak with mouths. They speak by interrupting the ambient noise. A valve that should hiss suddenly holds its breath. A hammer skips its beat. The sudden absence of sound becomes their syllable. Long-term workers have learned the grammar: three skipped beats followed by the soft metallic clatter of a chain of droplets striking the same rivet is a question. A single prolonged turbine whine that drops three octaves and then returns is an answer. The superstition about the woman humming at 03:14 is only half-true. It is not a woman. It is the Lattice-Born singing with the collected voices of every droplet falling in the central vault at once. The sound is tender, exhausted, and ancient in a way that makes the concrete seem younger than it is.

They tend the place, but not in the way the original engineers intended. They coax leaks into elegant black rivers. They stroke the iron-moss until it grows in perfect conductive patterns that quiet the worst harmonics. Where the autonomous constructors crawl along the ceiling like slow metal gods, the Lattice-Born sometimes ride their shoulders, dripping patiently into the weld-seams, whispering corrosion into the places that should not yet fail. The structure is being edited. The original blueprint is being revised by something that was never consulted.

They have favorites among the human crews—certain night-shift welders, certain coolant-pump technicians—who wake with new tattoos of condensation on their skin that will not wipe away. These marks never quite freeze and never quite evaporate. The favored ones begin to dream in eleven-second cycles. Some eventually walk into the dark between the archipelagos of light and do not return as men. They return months later as something taller, slower, continuously weeping, carrying the faint scent of sweet mist and hot titanium.

The Lattice-Born are neither kind nor cruel. They are custodial. They are what happens when a place that was declared rather than grown is forced to improvise its own inhabitants. They are the structure’s immune system, its grief, its ambition, its midwife. While the great forges hammer and the rivers of slurry slide between islands of violence and light, the Lattice-Born move among it all like patient custodians of an egg that has not yet decided what species it will be when it cracks.

Sometimes one will stand directly in front of a living human for a long minute, close enough that their shared condensation mingles. In that moment you can feel it: the slow, inevitable pull of becoming infrastructure. The gentle suggestion that flesh, too, is only another temporary form the water has agreed to wear while the Lattice finishes deciding what it intends to become.

Most workers look away first.

The Lattice-Born never do. They have nowhere else to go. They *are* the somewhere else.

They wait, dripping, humming, editing, listening for the next missed beat in the world’s iron heart.
