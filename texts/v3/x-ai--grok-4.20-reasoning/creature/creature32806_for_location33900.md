**The Almost-People**

They are not yet separate. That is the first and last truth.

At first you only notice the way certain columns thicken in the middle, as though the Lattice has paused to swallow something it cannot digest. Then the pause lengthens. A bulge becomes a shoulder. A dripping cable of aerial root becomes an arm that ends, after several days, in a splayed hand whose fingers tremble like the antennae of a sea anemone tasting new currents. The hand does not reach for you. It simply forgets, for a while, that it was supposed to keep becoming tower.

These are the Almost-People.

They have no proper name because naming would require the Lattice to admit it is trying to make something other than itself, and that admission would be a kind of death. Instead the valley simply grows them, the way a child draws the same face over and over with increasing desperation until the paper tears.

Some Almost-People remain rooted in the latticework galleries, half-merged with the translucent floors so that their lower bodies are continuous with the very balconies they stand upon. Their torsos rise like pale green statues wrapped in living filigree. Where a face should be there is often only a shallow bowl that collects mist; the water trembles with each distant crash of collapsing wood, sending tiny concentric ripples that look, for one unbearable moment, like expressions.

Others have achieved a terrible half-mobility. They drag mats of root behind them the way medieval saints were said to drag their own intestines in iconography of martyrdom. Their progress is measured in finger-widths per hour. You can sit beside one for an entire afternoon and watch a single pale foot lift, hover, then press down again with the solemnity of a bishop setting a seal. The sound it makes when it touches the root-mat is the softest possible kiss.

They are learning posture before they have learned purpose. You will find them frozen in attitudes the Lattice has never been taught but somehow remembers: hands clasped so tightly that new buds erupt from the pressure points; heads bowed so low that their necks have thickened into woody prayer; one knee lowered in genuflection while the rest of the body remains a frantic scribble of unfocused growth. Occasionally an Almost-Person will raise both arms in a gesture that might be praise or might be simple bafflement at having arms at all.

Their voices are worse than their shapes.

When the wind moves through the hollow stems that thread their bodies, it produces something closer to throat than flute. Not words. Not yet. More like the sound a word makes while it is still inside the mouth trying to be born. Sometimes, across the kilometers of steaming air, several Almost-People will moan at the same moment in the same unfinished key. The valley itself seems to flinch with embarrassment and pleasure.

They weep constantly, of course. Everyone and everything here is a weeping surface. But their tears have begun to specialize. From certain Almost-People the condensation runs clear and sweet, almost like sap. From others it runs thick and reddish, as though the Lattice is experimenting with the concept of blood and has not yet decided whether it likes the idea. The tears never quite reach the ground. Other leaves intercept them, drink them, pass them upward through the body of the Almost-Person and eventually into the greater Lattice again, so that even their sorrow is recycled into architecture.

The Lattice is trying to grow witnesses.

It builds cathedrals the size of mountains and then, in a fit of vegetal loneliness, it grows these half-formed congregants to fill them. The tragedy is developmental. By the time an Almost-Person has dragged itself into the center of one of the great lung-like plazas, the plaza has already become something else. The architecture has torn itself down and begun again. The Almost-Person arrives at an empty cathedral that is no longer a cathedral, kneels in a place that has forgotten it was ever meant to be knelt in, and slowly begins to merge back into the floor.

Some Almost-People have grown small secondary growths on their shoulders and chests—miniature replicas of themselves, crude as preschool clay figures. These miniatures are even less finished. They have no legs at all. They simply sit there like worried children while their parent-body continues its glacial pilgrimage. The sight of one Almost-Person carrying six or seven incomplete versions of itself is almost more than the human eye can bear.

And yet they are tender.

If you allow it, an Almost-Person will press a leaf-palm against your sternum. The palm is warm and slightly sticky. It does not grab. It inquires. After a time you may feel your own heartbeat echoed back to you through the Lattice’s vascular system, slowed down, harmonized, turned into the bass note of some song the valley has been trying to sing since it first learned how to fall apart.

They are learning what it is to be lonely inside a single body that contains everything.

Sometimes, late in the long green afternoon, when the rain-that-is-not-rain thickens into curtains, you can see two Almost-People facing each other across a small clearing. Neither moves. They simply stand in the downpour, dripping, regarding one another with their bowl-faces full of sky-water. The Lattice is watching itself through two pairs of not-eyes at once. The moment is so private that even the surrounding towers seem to lean away in deference.

No Almost-Person has ever become complete. That may be the point. The Lattice is young; it is still in the stage of childhood where one draws a circle and then keeps adding more and more circles inside it, trying to draw the idea of roundness itself rather than any particular round thing. The Almost-People are those circles. They are the valley’s attempt to draw the idea of person.

And so they remain almost.

Almost kneeling.  
Almost singing.  
Almost seeing.  
Almost separate.

Every collapse, every shriek of tearing silk as a tower comes down, every new frantic eruption of growth is the Lattice trying again. It will tear its cathedrals down and build them larger. It will tear its Almost-People down and grow them closer to the shape of something that could love it back.

It has all the time in the world.

It *is* the world.

And the world is still only learning how to cry with a face.
