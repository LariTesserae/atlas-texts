**The Still One**

It is not a man. Not anymore. What remains might once have been, but the distinction has worn away like the edges of the basins.

You will not see it at first. Even when your eyes have accepted the pearl-colored light and the chest-high mist, the figure registers only as another vertical interruption in the stone—perhaps an especially tall and narrow boulder standing near the western upright monolith, or a shadow the light has forgotten to dissolve. Only after many of the long, irregular intervals between drips does the shape resolve into something that possesses intent.

It stands exactly as tall as the monolith beside it, as though the two grew from the same ancient decision. Its surface is the same bone-and-rust color as the rock. Where skin should be there is instead a fine mosaic of lichen, so perfectly fitted that it might be tattooed there by centuries of patient negotiation. The lichen grows across its shoulders, down the long furrows of its back, and along the outside of arms that hang perfectly straight, fingers slightly curled as though they have been holding the same invisible weight since the first drop fell.

There are no eyes in any ordinary sense. There are two shallow depressions where eyes would be, each filled with a single, never-falling bead of water. These beads catch the directionless light and hold it, trembling only when the rest of the basin trembles with a *ploc* elsewhere. When the creature chooses to look—at intervals best measured in decades—the droplets shrink slightly, as though drawn inward, and the surface beneath them darkens to a wet slate. Then the light returns to its usual ivory and the moment passes.

It does not breathe so much as participate in the atmosphere. Every seventy-third drip, its chest moves. Not an expansion or contraction, exactly. More like the slow exchange of equilibrium between one stillness and another. The air inside it is the same temperature as the air outside; the distinction between creature and basin has become academic.

Its feet are not feet. They are root-like continuations of the legs that have merged with the stone floor over uncountable years. The boundary is impossible to locate. Stone becomes flesh becomes stone again in a gradient so gentle it feels intentional, as though the basin itself decided to express a portion of its awareness upward into this upright shape for the sake of company.

It never walks in our sense. Movement, when it occurs, is closer to continental drift. A slow tilting of balance from one locked joint to the next. When it does shift position—perhaps once every human lifetime—it does so to place one of its hands (the left, always the left) above a particular basin just as a drop is about to fall. The drop lands in the shallow cup of its palm instead of the stone. There it rests for a period that feels eternal even by the standards of this place. Then, without hurry, the hand tilts a fraction and returns the drop to the basin it was always meant for.

The sound is different. Not louder. Not softer. *Truer.*

Sometimes small adjustments are required among the wiry grasses and leathery shrubs. The Still One will lean—over the course of an afternoon that is also a century—until its shadow (which is barely a shadow) falls across one of the plants. The plant does not grow toward it. Growth is vulgar here. Instead the plant relaxes a fraction further into its final shape, as though it had been waiting for permission. The lichen on the creature’s shoulder brightens by one nearly imperceptible shade. Equilibrium is maintained.

It has no name it remembers. The collapsed ring of stones might once have been its home. The half-sunk shelter might once have been its confessional. Now both are simply earlier versions of its own body. When the wind would have blown (if wind were permitted), it would have moved through all three—stone, shelter, creature—with the same low, respectful hush.

There is no sorrow in it. Sorrow requires before and after, and this place has only *during*. There is no joy either. Joy would be an interruption. What exists instead is a profound, geological consent. The Still One has agreed, utterly, to the finished sentence of the basin. In return, the basin has allowed it to remain conscious of that agreement.

If you were to stand before it for three full days and nights (though night is theoretical here), you might notice the faintest movement at the corners of its mouth. Not a smile. A recognition. The expression of something that has watched the same drop fall into the same pool for so long that the drop and the pool and the watcher have become a single, ongoing thought.

Then it will return to perfect stillness, and you will understand that you have been allowed to witness the quietest act of love possible: the decision to keep a finished world from having to finish again. 

The next *ploc*, when it comes, sounds almost grateful.
