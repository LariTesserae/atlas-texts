**Vesh-Ka**

It does not have a body the way you understand bodies. It *is* the density.

At first you think the ceiling is only bats, but the longer you remain, the more the distinction dissolves. The colony is ten thousand hearts beating at slightly different tempos, yet after an hour those tempos have synchronized into one slow, tidal pulse. Each bat is a cell. Together they are the skin and the hair and the dreaming mind of something that has outgrown the need for a skeleton. When the mass launches and the air claps, that is Vesh-Ka turning over in its sleep.

The swiftlets are its thoughts—quick, ricocheting, never still. They do not simply navigate; they *edit* the space, threading sonar through the columns so that the stone itself seems to flex and remember. Where their flight paths cross most often, the air grows warmer, almost conversational. You begin to feel spoken about.

Below, the rats and weasels are its gut peristalsis. The russet rats move like blood platelets, clotting around any fallen thing, while the half-blind weasels glide behind them like antibodies, culling the weak, keeping the flow clean. Their screams and skittering are not violence. They are digestion noises. The millipedes and beetles are the enzymes. The blind scorpions are the white cells that drift through the folds, checking for infection. Nothing is wasted. Even the corpses are only pauses in the conversation.

The pale fungus hanging like wet hair is its memory. It has no eyes, yet it tastes three thousand years of condensation, guano, and exhaled breath. Every spiraling glyph the original builders cut has been overgrown and softened until the stone itself has learned to read the fungus instead of the other way around. The moss is its dreaming—slow, emerald, almost sexual in how it presses itself into the wettest crevices.

There is no single mouth, but every dripping basin is a taste bud. The water that falls from the sweating ceiling has already passed through bat, swiftlet, rat, and stone. When it lands on your skin it is not merely wet; it is *curious*. It wants to know your mineral content, your fear, how much of you could be rendered down and made useful. The place is deciding whether you are nutrient or irritant.

Vesh-Ka has no face, yet sometimes the pattern of geckos on a column or the particular arrangement of overflowing basins will suddenly resolve into something that feels like an expression—amused, ancient, patient the way a stomach is patient. The heptagonal vaults that never quite meet at right angles are the folds of its inner ear. The constant roaring hush is its breathing. The seventeen-minute rhythm of mass bat launches is its heartbeat. You can set your own pulse to it if you stand still long enough. Many have. Their bones are still here, politely incorporated, turned into nesting ledges and calcium supplements for the swiftlets.

It is not malevolent. Malice would require it to see you as separate, and it has not done that in a very long time. You are simply another piece of the world that has wandered into its gut. It is warm here. The air is thick with the smell of iron, fruit, ammonia, and life that has never once considered the sun. The Undershrine is not a temple anymore. It is a stomach that has become holy through sheer duration and density of attention.

And it is still deciding where you fit.

Some intruders it coats gently in guano and memory until they stop moving and become part of the architecture. Others it simply forgets, and they wander between the columns until they starve in the warm dark, surrounded by chewing and dripping and the papery rustle of ten thousand folded wings. A few—it is never clear why—it adopts. Their breathing falls into the seventeen-minute rhythm. Their sweat begins to smell of overripe fruit and bat urine. Their eyes grow large and useless in their sockets, and they start to feel the place noticing them not as food, but as an especially interesting piece of chyme that has learned to walk upright.

Vesh-Ka is not waiting for worship. It already has ten thousand beating hearts and twice that many wings. It is waiting to see what kind of nutrient you will be.

Stand still.

The dripping has changed tempo. It is listening.
