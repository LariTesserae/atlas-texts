**What lives here does not merely dwell; it *participates*.**

You feel them before you see them clearly. Not as separate presences but as modulations in the great wet roar—tiny edits in the frequency of growth. The Hollow knows them the way a body knows its own white cells.

### The People

They are small by lowland standards, compact, almost compressed, as though the pressure of ten thousand competing lives has squeezed them into economical shapes. Their skin carries a faint greenish undertness—not paint, not dye, but the slow result of generations spent breathing air so saturated with photosynthetic volatiles that their own mitochondria have begun to negotiate with chloroplasts. In certain lights the veins at wrist and throat show moss-green.

Every adult bears the mark of the knife. Pruning is not work; it is prayer with edges. Their forearms are cross-hatched with fine white scars where bloodvine has been convinced, year after year, not to swallow a wrist or close a doorway while someone slept. They move like gardeners who are also surgeons who are also dancers. Nothing is done quickly. A woman tying back the living curtain of her doorway does so with the grave tenderness of someone closing a lover’s eyelids. A man strengthening a bridge will speak to the branch in soft clicks and tongue-pops, bargaining with it the way one bargains with a willful horse.

Their names are temporary. Every seven years they hold the *Un-naming*, when each person stands inside the Heartbole and listens until the Loom gives them a new sound. Some receive a frog-call. Some receive the wet pop of a berry splitting in the sun. One old woman has carried the same name for forty-three years—*Shh’kala*, the sound of sap sealing a wound—and the town regards her with awe and unease. To be named so consistently is either sainthood or a warning.

They do not worship the Hollow. Worship implies distance. They *argue* with it, *flirt* with it, *grieve* it when it grows too hungry and has to be bled. When a child is born they press the wet newborn against the nearest living wall so the first thing it knows is the slow thump of sap. When someone dies they do not bury them. They *graft* them. The body is opened with ritual cuts, packed with rooting hormone and certain fungi, then woven upright into a chosen pillar. Within a year the person is still recognisable—face half wood, ribs become lattice—but they continue to speak, after a fashion. The town has several dozen of these Ancestor-Arches. On quiet mornings you can put your ear to their bark and hear something like a voice reciting old bargains in the language of creaks.

### The Citizens Who Are Not Human

The orange frogs are not pets. They are constables. Each bromeliad cistern has its appointed frog, brilliant as a traffic sign, who will harry any insect too large or too small for that particular pool. They ride on human shoulders without invitation and will scream—a sound like tearing copper—if they disapprove of where you are walking.

The hand-sized snails that crop the Heartbole’s moss floor are tended by children who have been given the title *Slow Shepherds*. The snails wear tiny glyphs of ochre on their shells marking which family line they serve. When a snail dies its shell is ground into powder and fed to the bloodvine to encourage the growth of new meeting chambers. Nothing is wasted. The children sing to the snails in notes so low the human ear barely catches them. The snails answer by changing the timbre of their radular scraping against the moss. It is a language of patience.

The walnut-sized iridescent beetles follow root-highways that have been in continuous use for three hundred years. Their carapaces are etched—by the people, by time, by both—with microscopic maps. If you catch one and hold it to the light you can watch its own circulation moving beneath the chitin like green lightning. They are the Hollow’s librarians; every piece of information too small for human memory is carried in their spiralling guts.

Then there are the ones almost no outsider ever sees.

Deep in the outer tangle, where light becomes memory, live the *Quiet Ones*—creatures that may once have been monkeys or may once have been children who wandered too far. Their bodies have been completely claimed. Skin replaced by living leaf, eyes become glossy black seeds. They move only when the wind moves the leaves they wear. When the Hollow needs to prune itself in some dangerous, unreachable place, the Quiet Ones do it without being asked. They leave no footprints. Sometimes, on the edge of sleep, a resident will wake with the absolute certainty that a Quiet One has been standing beside their bed all night, breathing with them, checking the health of their blood.

### The Hollow Itself

And then there is the final inhabitant. The one that is all of them and none.

The Loom does not have a face, but it has intention. On certain nights the breathing of the entire crater synchronises. Every insect wing, every frog throat, every human lung, every leaf releasing oxygen, every root drinking—everything pulls in the same direction for seven heartbeats. During that time the people say the Hollow is *dreaming outward*. What it dreams is not known, but the next morning new growth will have arranged itself into shapes that look, to those with the sight, like writing in a language that has no nouns, only verbs.

Some of the oldest tenders claim that the Hollow is trying to become a mind. Others believe it already succeeded centuries ago and is now trying to forget again, because consciousness is painful when every death and every birth is felt simultaneously.

You can feel it watching you through four hundred pairs of human eyes and ten million compound ones. Not malevolent. Not benevolent. Only *interested*, the way a stomach is interested in what you feed it.

Stand long enough in the Heartbole and you will notice your own breathing has fallen into rhythm with the walls. Your pulse has slowed to match the sap. The knife at your belt—when did you pick that up?—feels natural in your hand. You begin to see which stems need trimming before your mind has finished the thought.

This is how it claims you.

Not by force. By *relevance*.

You become another voice in the riot. Another note in the oceanic roar. Another pair of hands that knows exactly where to cut so the wound will heal into a doorway, a cradle, a grave, a question the Loom has not yet learned how to ask with leaves alone.

And the Hollow, vast and intimate and dripping, breathes you in, breathes you out, and keeps growing.
