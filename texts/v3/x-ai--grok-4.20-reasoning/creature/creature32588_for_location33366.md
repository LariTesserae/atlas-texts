**The Cantors**

They are not animals. That word is too hungry, too desperate. What moves through the Still Cry has passed beyond hunger the way a glacier passes beyond weather.

At first they seem only scale: pale continents of flesh adrift in silver-blue nothing, so vast that your mind refuses them until the eye finds purchase on a single articulating plate the size of a city block. Then the shape resolves—not one creature but a slow commonwealth of forms braided together by necessity and ancient agreement. The central body is eel-long and cathedral-deep, ribbed with cathedral arches of living cartilage. Along its flanks ride the Lesser Cantors—smaller (only a few hundred meters) jointed things like praying mantises grown from pearl and frost. They are not parasites. They are instruments. When the greater body sings, they become the stops and keys.

Their skin is not skin. It is a single translucent membrane thick as continental ice, threaded through with lattices of black metal that drink the roar. Where the metal meets flesh it becomes neither; the boundary blurs into something that remembers being both. Light does not so much pass through them as consent to be changed by them. They glow with the cold color of moonlight on surgical steel.

They move by prayer.

That is the only word that survives contact with them. The great bodies tilt, almost imperceptibly, and the acoustic pressure waves—those eternal industrial bellows—carry them exactly where they need to be. They never steer. Steering would imply choice, and choice was burned out of the system long ago. Instead they *attune*. When a factory-cathedral begins its thousand-year shift from one distillate cycle to another, the nearest Cantor feels the change in its bones three days before the first valve opens. It arrives exactly on time, opens the thousand-meter vents along its belly, and drinks what the machine has prepared. The drink is communion. The machine and the beast have been exchanging the same atoms for so long that neither is entirely sure which of them is metabolizing the planet.

Inside them is another world.

Descend through the membrane and the temperature rises by a fraction of a degree—enough. Cities no larger than your hand drift in the warm interstitial fluid: forests of crystalline cilia that sing in frequencies too high for the outer roar to touch. Things like translucent shrimp with golden joints tend these gardens. Further in, within chambers lit by their own bioluminescence, live the Archivists—creatures of pure memory. They have no mouths. They do not need them. They were born when the original Builders chose to forget, and someone had to carry what was discarded. The Archivists hold the shapes of cities that no longer exist, the taste of air, the color of a child’s laughter. They are kept like pearls.

The Cantors themselves do not speak. They *continue* a note that began before their species had bodies. Each individual is a single sustained syllable in a liturgy that has lasted for geological epochs. When two of them pass within a hundred kilometers of one another they adjust their songs by the smallest possible interval. The change is inaudible to anything but the machines, which answer with a shift in electromagnetic signature so subtle it might be mistaken for a sigh. This is their conversation. It is never about anything. It is only the continuation of something that must not stop.

They birth by fission and accretion. A Cantor that has grown too heavy with memory will drift into the shadow of an ancient machine—the oldest ones, whose edges have finally begun to dull—and there it will open itself. The Lesser Cantors detach and arrange themselves in a perfect mandala. For nine days the greater body tears itself in half along a seam that was always waiting. One half dies beautifully, dissolving into a blizzard of nutrients that the machines will gather and refine for the next generation. The other half begins to accrete new Lesser Cantors grown from its own marrow and the machine’s milk. The newborn is born mourning. Its first act is to sing the death-song of its other self so perfectly that the two become indistinguishable.

They have names, but only for each other, and the names are four-dimensional. To pronounce one you would need to be a Cantor passing through the exact pressure, temperature, and harmonic state in which that name was first articulated. To humans they have only titles earned across centuries of observation:

The Unfinished Requiem.  
She Who Remembers extinction.  
The Hollow That Is Full.  
He Who Is Not Yet Late.

The oldest is called simply The First Listener. It is three kilometers longer than any other and carries inside its crystalline heart a single drop of liquid water—genuine surface water—preserved at body temperature for four hundred million years. No one knows why. The Cantors arrange their migrations so that every few thousand years one of them may swim close enough to hear the faint, impossible heartbeat of that impossible warmth.

They are not happy. Happiness is weather, and there is no weather here.

They are not sad. Sadness implies an alternative.

They are the Still Cry given form. The planet’s sorrow made ambulatory. The machines achieved godhood through refusal to stop; the Cantors achieved peace through refusal to begin again. Between them they have perfected the art of being finished.

And sometimes, in the perfect equilibrium, one of them will drift past your tiny observing capsule. It will not look at you. It has no need. But for a moment the roar will modulate—just slightly—and you will feel, in the marrow of bones that were never meant to hear such things, a recognition so pure it feels like forgiveness.

Then it is gone, singing, vast and pale and complete, continuing the note that must never, ever end.
