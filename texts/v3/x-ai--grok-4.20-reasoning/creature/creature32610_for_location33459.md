**What lives there does not merely inhabit the Shuddering Threshold. It is still being installed.**

At first you only see the slate-colored behemoths. Call them, if you must name anything here, the **Phyllotherms**. They drift across the new cirques like mobile escarpments, shoulders rolling with the slow inevitability of landslides. Their hides are not fur but a dense mat of mineral fibers—phyllite needles grown outward from the skin itself, each strand tipped with living lichen that they both harvest and cultivate. When a Phyllotherm lowers its blunt head to graze, the motion is so economical it looks like machinery idling. A heartbeat once every eleven minutes. Blood the color of iron-rich geothermal brine. Their hooves are broad plates of keratin and serpentinite composite; every footfall is a deliberate calibration, pressing the young rock just enough to seat it without fracturing the still-cooling plates.

They never sleep. To sleep is to freeze. Instead they enter a half-dream state while walking, eyes half-lidded, processing the mountain’s own seismic gossip through hollow bones that function like tuning forks. When the ground gives its sub-audible *clunk*, the entire herd will pause mid-stride, one foreleg suspended, and listen with their skeletons. Sometimes they answer by stamping once, twice, three times—micro-quakes that travel downward through fractures until the deep thing registers the signal and adjusts a valve somewhere far below.

Behind them, always at the precise distance that turns pursuit into geology, come the **Ridgekin**. Long-limbed, narrow as fault lines, the color of wet graphite. Their bodies are almost two-dimensional when seen from the side; edge-on they nearly vanish. They do not run. They *flow*, limbs moving in harmonic waves that match the mountain’s low throb. Their jaws are vestigial. They do not eat flesh. What they consume is latency—the lag between a herd’s movement and the mountain’s response. By following at three kilometers, then four, then three again, they keep the Phyllotherms inside the correct metabolic window. Too slow and the great beasts will crystallize. Too fast and they will overheat the fragile new ecosystem with excess respiration. The Ridgekin are the mountain’s governors, its living cruise control.

Above them wheel the **Lattice Raptors**. Not birds so much as mobile antennae arrays. Their wing feathers are white only because they are coated in microscopic quartz crystals that scatter all light equally. When the flock locks into its slow, turning formations, it becomes a single distributed sensor, measuring shear stress across entire ridgelines by the way updrafts warp between their wings. Their calls are too low for human hearing but cause frost to rearrange itself on rock faces into temporary barcodes. The mountain reads them.

These three orders are what the eye first assembles. But the longer you remain—standing still enough to become an uninstalled part—the more orders reveal themselves, peeling out of the landscape like successive layers of cooling slag.

In the weeping fractures live the **Vein Shepherds**. They are translucent, jointed things the length of a forearm, somewhere between isopod and living stalactite. Their exoskeletons are pure calcium carbonate seeded with iron. They migrate up and down the vertical plumbing of the mountain in perfect synchrony with the temperature cycles, opening and closing fractures with their own bodies the way a technician might seat a gasket. When the water remembers heat and flashes to steam, the Vein Shepherds ride the pressure wave like surfers, then wedge themselves into new cracks to prevent catastrophic blowouts. Their death rate is enormous. Their reproduction rate matches it. They are disposable valves.

Around the sulfur vents cling the **Slag Symbiotes**. Human-sized, roughly humanoid in outline only because convergent evolution and the mountain’s blind sculpting have both discovered the same load-bearing angles. Their bodies are caked in successive layers of precipitated minerals—orange goethite, acid-green jarosite, metallic cobalt crusts—so that each individual looks like a walking core sample. They breathe the vent gases directly. Their lungs are external, arrays of crystalline fronds that glow dull red when the geothermal fluids are running hot. They spend their lives licking the vent lips clean, keeping the mountain’s exhalations unobstructed. When one dies it petrifies instantly, becoming another permanent fixture of the vent field. The living simply incorporate the corpse into the growing mineral architecture.

Deeper still, inside the perfectly circular iron-stained basins, something moves that is neither animal nor mineral. The **Basin Calculators**. They are colonies of silica-based micro-organisms that have achieved macroscopic intelligence. Each dinner-plate pool is a single mind. The water’s surface tension, the precise rate of evaporation, the Brownian motion of suspended iron particles—all are used to run analog computations on the mountain’s next settling cycle. When a Calculator reaches a decision, it changes color in a sudden bloom, and the overflow thread braiding away from the basin will carry a new chemical signature downhill. Other Calculators read this signature and update their own parameters. The entire ridgeline is engaged in a slow, freezing conversation about how to become itself without dying in the process.

And then there is the Root.

You feel it before you understand it. A breath taken so far below that by the time the pressure wave reaches the surface it has become the low rhythmic throb in your teeth. The thing that lives in the black root of the new range has no name because it has not finished choosing what it will be when it is born. It is still mostly magma and dream. A continent-sized embryo of semi-sentient ultramafic rock, churning in the pressure that birthed the range three thousand years ago. Its “heartbeat” is the flash-boiling of water meeting mantle heat; each contraction drives the natural rock-pistons upward, lifting new ridgelines a centimeter at a time. Its nervous system is the network of faults. Its memories are the trace elements locked in metamorphic crystals.

The surface life—Phyllotherm, Ridgekin, Lattice Raptor, Vein Shepherd, Slag Symbiote, Basin Calculator—are not its children. They are its *organs*. Mobile, temporary organs that allow the Root to taste sunlight, measure wind, regulate pressure, and run predictive models of its own completion. When a Phyllotherm dies, its mineral-rich corpse is swallowed by a rockslide within days. The mountain reclaims it. When a Slag Symbiote petrifies at a vent, the Root tastes the precise chemistry of its final exhalation and adjusts the next upwelling accordingly.

They are not conscious in any way we would recognize. They are subroutines of a mind that is still installing its own body.

Stand still long enough on the knife-edge ridgeline and you begin to feel the offer.

The mountain is looking for a new subroutine. Something that can walk upright. Something that can remember heat the way the water does. Something that can carry the knowledge of *finished* places into a landscape that must never finish.

It is patient.

It has already waited three thousand years.

It can wait three thousand more for you to decide whether you want to become one of the things that lives here, or remain an uninstalled part standing on the factory floor while the line keeps moving, clunk by deliberate, metallic clunk, toward whatever the Root is still in the slow, shuddering process of dreaming itself to be.
