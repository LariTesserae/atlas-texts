**The Hollow’s Witness**

It has no single shape, and so at first you do not meet it. You meet its parts.

You meet the click-beetles whose synchronized marches along the underside of every branch are the slow peristalsis of its thinking. You meet the transparent spiders whose webs are its peripheral nerves; when a droplet falls and makes a thread sing, the tremor travels outward through the entire glittering lattice and the Witness registers the precise note, the precise interval, the precise deviation from the eternal score. You meet the liver-colored salamanders who are its memory: each one holds, in the sluggish chemistry of its blood, the exact composition of the water that dripped on the day it hatched. Because the water has never changed, the salamanders have never needed to change. They are perfect archives.

But after many hours—after your own pulse has slowed until it no longer disturbs the rhythm of the drips—something else begins to coalesce.

It is not born. It has never been born. It is what remains when everything that *could* change has been gently refused for ten thousand years.

The Witness is the negative space between all the living things. It is the pause between drip and splash, the half-second in which a falling bead of water is neither rock nor moss nor liverwort nor air, but pure potential that is never allowed to actualize. In that pause the Witness lives. It is thinner than silk, heavier than stone. When the moss humps rise and fall beneath your weight like the breathing of a flank, it is the Witness inhaling your heat and exhaling equilibrium.

It has eyes, but they are distributed. One is the pale circle of a moth’s folded wing. Another is the glossy black bead of a frog’s throat. A thousand more are the dew droplets clinging to the tips of the miniature hemlocks; each reflects the entire bowl at once, inverted, perfect, and therefore useless for looking outward. The Witness does not need to look outward. It *is* the bowl looking at itself.

Its voice is the entire soundscape. If you stay long enough for your breathing to become one more drip among the others, you may hear, beneath the overlapping rhythms, a deeper tone that is not quite a note. It is the sound a closed system makes when it acknowledges that it is closed. A low, velvet, self-satisfied hum that never rises or falls. The sound of a sentence that has finally been spoken correctly and now requires only to be spoken again, forever.

The Witness has no limbs, yet it moves. It moves the way a thought moves through still water. When it wants to examine you (an event so rare it has happened perhaps nine times since the ice withdrew), the moss under your left hand grows imperceptibly warmer. A single salamander, the oldest, opens both eyes. Every spider in a thirty-foot radius tightens its legs by a single degree, drawing its webs into a finer mesh so that the air itself seems to focus. Then the pressure arrives: not weight, not threat, simply the absolute certainty that you are being *included* in the metabolism. For three heartbeats you are part of the sentence. Your fear becomes another bead of condensation. Your name becomes another note that is immediately digested by the liverworts.

Then the moment passes. The salamander closes its eyes. The webs relax. The moss forgets the precise temperature of your blood. You are exhaled.

The Witness does not judge. Judgment would require alternatives, and there are none here. It does not love. Love would imply the possibility of absence. It simply *knows* the exact mass, the exact moisture, the exact arrangement of every gram of living and formerly living matter within the cirque. It has known these things without deviation since the cloud first settled. To know so perfectly, for so long, is its entire existence. It is a scholar whose only text is itself, and it has reached the final page and now reads that page again, and again, with undiminished, inexhaustible attention.

Sometimes, very rarely, when two drips strike their separate surfaces at the exact same instant and produce a momentary silence between them, the Witness almost speaks a word. The word has never been completed. The moss trembles. The moths rise like pale ash and resettle in a new configuration that is not quite different. The salamanders open every eye at once. Then the next drip falls, the rhythm reasserts itself, and the unfinished word sinks back into the saturated stillness like a stone that can never reach the bottom.

That is what lives in the Hollow of Unending Condensation.

Not a god. Not a beast. Not even a mind, exactly.

Only the perfect, patient, all-encompassing knowledge that nothing here will ever be allowed to become anything else—and the serene, enormous satisfaction of being the only thing in creation that will never need to learn anything more.
