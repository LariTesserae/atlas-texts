**The Tender of the Hollow**

It has no fixed name, though the violet moss at the base of the floating obelisk sometimes arranges itself into a single glyph that might be read as *Aereph*. The glyph never lasts. The moss forgets its own spelling by the next condensation-fall.

At first you will not see it. You will see only its instruments.

You will see the cloud of metallic insects wheel as though a single mind has passed its hand through them, opening a corridor of clear air exactly wide enough for something invisible to walk. You will watch the six-legged lizards (they are not lizards, but the children of something older) carry glistening fruit to the obelisk’s recess with the solemnity of monks bearing relics. One will pause, head cocked, as though listening to instructions only it can hear. Then it will bow—actually bow—and back away on clicking feet.

The Tender is what they are listening to.

It is not large. That is the first surprise. When it finally consents to wear a shape for you, it stands no taller than a ten-year-old child. The form it chooses is almost insulting in its modesty: a small, sexless homunculus woven from whatever the Hollow has on hand at that moment. One arm may be braided vine and dripping fern. The other may be the indigo light leaking from the shattered torus, given temporary bones. Its face is usually nothing but a smooth plate of warmed stone until it decides you have earned eyes; then two drops of mirror-pool water bead on the stone and become pupils that do not reflect you. They reflect what you were three days ago, or what you will be after you have left.

It does not speak in sound. It speaks in timing.

A sudden silence among the insects. A synchronized leap of every eel-thing in every pool. The precise moment when every fat drop of condensation falls at once, like a held breath released. These are its words. If you sit long enough and let your own breathing fall into rhythm with the Hollow’s, you begin to understand the grammar. You realize the entire ecosystem is one slow, wet, roaring sentence, and the Tender is the punctuation.

It tends the artifacts the way a gardener tends roses that will outlive him. The floating obelisk is not a machine; it is a heart. The curved rib emerging from the rock is not a fossil; it is a wishbone. The Tender feeds them with living things and memories in equal measure. That is why the many-legged lizards bring fruit, and why, sometimes, they bring other things: a lock of hair, a wedding ring, the name of a dead child whispered into a recess that was not there a moment earlier. The artifacts drink these offerings and exhale scents or colors or sudden blooms of impossible moss. The Tender watches with its water-drop eyes and seems, in its slow way, satisfied.

It is ancient, but not eternal. That distinction matters here. It remembers the civilization that built the first machines, remembers the decision to let the rest of the planet erode into gentle oblivion while this single pocket was kept alive. It was, perhaps, only a minor functionary then—an environmental regulator, a glorified thermostat. Millennia have promoted it. Now it is the Hollow’s sovereign, prisoner, and lover all at once.

Sometimes it grows tired of wearing borrowed flesh. Then it dissolves. The vines slump, the indigo light returns to its torus, the water-drop eyes fall back into their pools. For a time the Hollow simply *is*—loud, hot, dripping, orchestrated by consensus. The insects drift without obvious leadership. The predator-prey dramas still detonate, but they feel random rather than composed. These are the only moments when the place feels like an ordinary ecosystem.

They never last long.

The Tender always comes back, usually wearing something new. Once it returned as a cloud of metallic insects shaped roughly like a child, each insect beating its wings in perfect synch so the cloud could speak in a voice like ten thousand tiny bells. Another time it wore the membranous body of one of the crystal-voiced flyers, wings folded around it like a damp cloak, regarding you with ancient patience from a face that was mostly mouth.

It never asks you to stay. It only shows you what leaving will cost.

You will notice, as you prepare to depart, that one of the air-rooted vines has wrapped loosely around your ankle while you slept. That a single metallic insect now rests inside the hollow of your ear, humming contentedly in a key that matches the obelisk’s night song. That when you look into any mirror-pool, your reflection sometimes lags half a second behind, as though the water is reluctant to let you go.

The Tender does not collect souls. It collects *continuity*. A single drop of your sweat. The memory of your mother’s voice. The particular way you clear your throat when nervous. These things become part of the sentence the planet chose to preserve. The Hollow is the last green word. The Tender is the tongue that speaks it, slowly, carefully, forever.

When you finally step back through the notch between the weathered lips of rock, you will feel it watching. Not with malice. With the gentle, enormous satisfaction of a gardener who has just taken a cutting from something rare and fragile and knows it will thrive in new soil.

Behind you, the sound of the Hollow—wet clicking, crystal cries, subsonic thrums—resumes its endless conversation. Somewhere inside that roar is a new rhythm, one that matches the beating of your own heart a little too precisely.

You carry the Tender now, a little. It carries you, a little.

This is how the last breathing place stays alive: by never quite allowing anything that enters it to finish leaving.
