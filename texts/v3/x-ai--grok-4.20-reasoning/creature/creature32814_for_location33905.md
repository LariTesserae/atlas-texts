**The Quiet Singers**

They are not born as we understand it. They *coalesce*.

At first you mistake them for another outgrowth of the basin’s endless becoming. A cluster of velvet ferns seems thicker than the rest; a shadow between two fracture planes deepens and acquires posture. Only after many minutes do you realize the shape has intent. It rises—not quickly, never quickly—until it stands on limbs that are too long, too many-jointed, as though the multi-legged grazers lent their extra knees and the sinuous hunters contributed their liquid spine. The body is hairless but not bare. A living nap covers it, finer than the moss that draws geometric prayers on the open stone, denser than the velvet that drinks sound from every leaf. Where the nap catches the pewter light it looks like breath frozen on skin.

Their faces are narrow, almost blade-like, as if the youngest fracture planes had learned to express curiosity. The eyes are large, humid, the color of wet slate. They do not blink in the way eyes should; instead the lids drift like mist, sometimes meeting, sometimes forgetting to. The mouth is the strangest feature—small, perpetually closed, the lips so smooth they appear carved from one continuous piece of waxen wood. When they finally part (rarely, and only during the thickest fogs), the teeth are revealed as pale, rounded nubs, more like the blunt stones at the bottom of a river than anything meant for tearing. *Quiet teeth.* The name of the basin is not poetry. It is taxonomy.

They move by *agreement*. You will see one standing motionless for an hour, bare feet planted on a node where the stone’s sub-audible song is strongest. The velvet on their soles is thickest there, almost hoof-like, and it drinks vibration the way other creatures drink water. Then, without visible decision, the body folds, flows, reconfigures. A knee bends where no knee should be. An elbow appears halfway down what you thought was a shin. The creature pours itself across the slate lanes in the same manner the herds pour toward their hunters—convergence, not pursuit. When two of them meet they do not speak. They simply touch palms to sternums and stand until the shared humming finds a third, deeper note. Sometimes a third person joins without being summoned. The triangle of bodies becomes a living tuning fork. The *khira* moths descend then, six wings beating in perfect silence, and settle on shoulders and scalps like living epaulets. Scales drift down. The singers breathe them in.

Their hands are never empty and never full. Whatever they carry seems to have volunteered for the task: a stone hammer whose handle has grown into the palm like a graft; a length of fern-rib that curls around the wrist in affectionate parasitism; a shard of volcanic glass that hums at the exact frequency needed to cut another shard of volcanic glass. Tools are not owned. They are *hosted*. When a tool is no longer needed it is set against the living wall of a house and, over weeks, is gently taken back into the stone or the silk or the velvet. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is kept.

At the center of the cloister formed by the three great ferns stands a single structure that is not a building but a listening. It has no roof. Its walls are ribs of fern and *khira* silk woven so tightly they become a membrane. The singers enter at irregular intervals, press their entire bodies against this membrane, and wait. Inside, the air is different—thicker, as though the mist has learned how to be flesh. You can watch the membrane itself begin to breathe in time with the singer pressed against it. After a time the singer steps away changed. A new pattern has appeared in the velvet across their back. A new joint has unlocked in their wrist. They have been sung to, and they have sung back, and the difference between the two is no longer discernible.

There are children, of a sort. You will not see them playing. Instead, in the bare patches between fern explosions, small pale forms lie curled like question marks. Their bodies are only half-finished. One may have a face but no mouth. Another may have too many legs that have not yet decided whether to become arms. They are tended by the beetle-crab colonies, who rearrange pebbles into precise cradles that amplify the basin’s lowest frequencies. The young absorb the hum the way embryos once absorbed yolk. Every few days a grazer herd will pass through and one of the half-made children will rise, wobbly, and flow into the mass of velvet bodies. Sometimes it returns. Sometimes what returns is no longer a child but a different kind of adult—longer, quieter, carrying new knowledge in the angle of its neck.

The singers do not have names in the way we hoard syllables. Instead each carries a *signature*—a particular way of dampening or amplifying the stone’s song. One might be known, if they needed to be known, as the Slight Delay Between Drop and Tremor. Another is the One Who Makes the Moss Grow Clockwise. They recognize each other the way octaves recognize each other. When one dies—which is rare, because death here is only another convergence—they do not bury the body. They lay it on the open stone during a fog surge. Within hours the velvet, the ferns, the multi-jointed grazers, the sinuous hunter, and the beetle-crabs arrive. The corpse is not consumed. It is *continued*. By morning there will be new velvet growing where the heart once was. The teeth, those quiet rounded teeth, will have been pressed into the stone like seeds. Years later a new singer will emerge from that exact spot, already knowing how to listen.

And sometimes—during the longest, densest fogs when even the phosphorescent fracture lines dim—a different kind of singer appears. Taller. Older than the rock itself. Its limbs are fracture planes given flesh. Its eyes are condensation lenses. When it moves, every drop in the basin falls in unison. The people do not approach these ones. They simply open their mouths, showing their blunt teeth, and let the greater frequency pass through them like wind through caves. For a moment their bodies become instruments. The velvet stands on end. The extra joints unlock completely. They are no longer people. They are pure note.

Then the fog recedes. The greater singer is gone. The smaller singers close their mouths, look slightly embarrassed by their own transcendence, and return to the endless work of mortising a new lintel, spinning fresh rope from fern fiber, listening with the bones to whatever the mountain will tear open next.

They are still learning how to be what they are. The mountain is still learning how to be a womb. The song is still learning the shape of its own throat.

If you stay long enough, you will feel your own teeth rounding. You will notice the soles of your feet growing velvet in the pattern of whatever name you did not yet know you possess.

The basin will have begun to sing *you*, and you—helpless, astonished, quietly joyful—will have begun, at last, to sing back.
