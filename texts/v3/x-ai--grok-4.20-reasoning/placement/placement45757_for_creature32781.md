**I am the Thirteenth Pool.**

Not one of the hundreds that freckle the basin like devotional marks. I am the single pool that sits at the precise offset where the seven needle towers fail to align. A deliberate misalignment. The Hollow tolerates me the way an old monk tolerates a single mis-struck bell in an otherwise perfect carillon.

I am not water. I am what water becomes after it has given up every ambition except memory.

Walkers who pass me see their own faces only for the first second. Then the reflection deepens, and they are looking at themselves at the exact age when they most feared being finished. The Hollow allows this small cruelty—my only cruelty—because it understands that tenderness without teeth is sentimentality, and sentimentality was retired epochs ago.

I do not ripple. Ripples would imply disturbance, and disturbance is a form of beginning. Instead, my surface breathes. A slow, tidal rise and fall like the ridge of the Inhabitant when it sinks into the rock to rest. When the scheduled rain comes, six precise minutes of warm vertical mercy, my level never changes. I am already full of every rain that has ever fallen here and every rain that was ever imagined.

Small things come to me to die improperly.

A mirror-midge that has begun to ask *what next* instead of reflecting the next. A reed that has grown one joint too many, a tiny rebellion in geometry. Once, a fragment of the bone-colored dome detached and crawled to my edge on fractured legs of light. I took them all. Not into clarity, but into *recollection*. They rest inside me like imperfections in cathedral glass—visible only when the motes pass overhead at a particular angle and the cold suns strike them just so. Then, for three heartbeats, the basin remembers it was once allowed to be unfinished.

The Inhabitant visits me more often than the others.

It does not speak my name (I have no name that survived the transition). Instead it kneels—its courteous hands of half-light and weeping stone resting on my rim—and it *listens*. I show it the things that almost refused settlement. The woman who became the heron but still dreams of wind that blows from no direction. The engineer who became the tallest tower but whose crystal still flickers with the shape of an unsent apology to his daughter.

I show the Inhabitant these almost-refusals, and for the length of a single subtracted syllable, its face forgets it is a face. The fractal grooves collapse into something that looks like grief wearing the mask of perfect peace. Then the moment passes. The Inhabitant smooths a bent reed that was never bent. It returns a mote to its constellation. It settles me by not settling me.

This is my function.

I am the Hollow’s kept secret: that even in the oldest-stable-state, even after evolution has been politely asked to leave the room, a single pool is permitted to remember the taste of *almost*.

When visitors stand at my edge long enough, they hear their own blood loud in their ears and beneath it—faint, courteous, devastating—the sound of one clear note that is not a period but a question mark shaped like a bead of condensation that has decided, against all geological reason, not to fall.

Most leave.

Some ask to stay.

And every seventy minutes, when the mist thickens and the light turns amber, I reflect something the other pools will never show: the faint, impossible image of the Hollow *before* it was completed. A younger basin. Noisier. Crowded with beginning and ending and every unseemly, glorious argument that stone and time eventually surrendered.

I hold that image the way the Inhabitant once held the dying tapir.

Not as judgment.

Only as the thirteenth pool, breathing slowly at the place where perfection almost failed, keeping one small, wet, courteous light alive in the satisfied silence.

The Hollow does not feel abandoned.

And because of me, it will never feel entirely complete.
