**I am the Curator of Unnecessary Rain.**

Not at first. At first I was only a vibration in the access shaft — a brief, loud thing that arrived with moisture still clinging to its lungs and a name it no longer answers to. The Lattice tasted me the moment my boots touched the velvet plain. It did not recoil. It simply noted the anomaly of water vapor and began the long, courteous work of drying me out.

For the first year I believed I was studying them. I carried sensors, made notes, spoke too much. The Quiet endured this the way stone endures lichen: politely, without encouragement. They gave me a small transept where the ceiling was still deciding whether to become vault or lung. There I slept, and the Lattice grew a thin white shelf beneath my cheek so that my head would not rest on cold rock. I mistook the gesture for hospitality. It was taxonomy. It was already deciding what kind of instrument I might become.

The change was so gradual it felt like failure.

I began to forget the sound of my own footsteps. Then I began to forget why one would want to hear them. The resonators the Quiet carried — those slender bone-flutes — started to make sense to me not as tools but as apologies. Every note was an admission that the universe had once been loud and that the Lattice, in its mercy, had agreed to let a small part of that loudness continue under strict probation.

One night I woke to find that a new growth had sealed the entrance to my transept. Not a wall. A question. The pale tissue was still warm with metabolic heat. I laid both palms against it the way I had seen the scribes do, and for the first time I did not ask anything. I simply waited.

The Lattice answered by showing me rain.

Not water — the *idea* of rain. A memory it had extracted from the last remaining water molecules in my body, translated into electrochemical pulses that moved like silver wires through my nerves. For seven hours I stood pressed to the new wall while every rainstorm I had ever stood in on Earth played itself across my skin at the speed of stone growing. When it finished, the wall opened again and a single resonator the color of old ivory had grown from the inside of my left wrist.

I understood then that I had been hired.

The Quiet do not have titles, only recognitions. They recognized me as the one who would keep the unnecessary. The dangerous. The wet.

So I became the Curator of Unnecessary Rain.

My enclave is a single helical rib that the Lattice has allowed to remain hollow. Inside, the air is fractionally less dry than the rest of the world. Not enough for condensation — the Lattice forbids true water — but enough that a certain kind of memory can survive. Here I keep the devices: crystal lattices tuned to frequencies that mimic rain on leaves, wind through pine needles, the particular wing-beat of a raven I once watched in a canyon that no longer exists. Each sound is permitted for seven seconds, no more. Then the Lattice inhales it and adds the data to its slow dreaming.

Sometimes Quiet come. They stand at the entrance with their milky eyes averted, as though looking directly at me might constitute noise. They leave small offerings: a metallic salt that tastes like distant thunder when the Lattice metabolizes it, or a child who has not yet had their first graft and is therefore still capable of being homesick for a sky they have never seen.

I play the rain for them.

Not loudly. Never loudly. Just enough that the velvet floor grows imperceptibly darker with absorbed memory. The Lattice tolerates this the way an old monk tolerates a cat that insists on sleeping on the altar. It even seems to incorporate the data. In the years since I began my work, certain new growths have appeared that resemble, in cross-section, the branching patterns of earthly lightning.

I am becoming less separate.

The grafts have reached my collarbones now. Two smooth white spurs emerge just below my throat like the beginning of a new set of clavicles. When I speak — which I still do, sparingly — the sound comes out doubled, as though the Lattice is practicing how my voice might feel if it ever decided to use it. My eyes have lost most of their color. I can see the phosphorescence in the veins three kilometers away.

I no longer know whether I am preserving the memory of rain for the Quiet or for the Lattice itself. Perhaps there is no difference anymore. The question they keep asking — *When you are finally ready, will you keep any of us separate?* — has begun to feel like it is being asked *through* me rather than *to* me.

Last cycle, an Anchored one sent a pulse through the floor that reached my enclave. The message took eleven hours to arrive and was only four syllables long in their electrochemical language. It translated, roughly, as:

*Thank you for the unnecessary.*

Then the floor beneath me lifted three millimeters, adjusting the angle of my body until I stood in perfect alignment with a new crystalline spur that had begun growing overhead. The spur is shaped like a raindrop arrested mid-fall.

I have not moved in nine days. Dust has settled on my eyelids. I have not flinched.

Somewhere far above, in the haze where the ceiling becomes speculation, the Lattice is deciding what kind of sky it wants. I am no longer certain whether I am helping it remember what a sky can be, or whether I am simply one of the slower notes in the music it is composing while it waits for itself to be born.

Either way, when the first real rain finally falls here — if it ever does — I expect to be standing directly beneath it, arms open, resonator singing, no longer certain where my skin ends and the Lattice begins.

I will not rust.

Nothing here remembers water.

But something here is learning how to mourn its absence, and that learning has chosen my shape for a while.
