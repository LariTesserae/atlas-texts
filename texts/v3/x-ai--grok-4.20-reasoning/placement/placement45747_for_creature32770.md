**The Metronome**

You will not find me standing.

I am located in the lowest fold of the Sump, where three ancient sumps meet and have argued so long that their argument has become a single slow heartbeat. The place is no larger than a locomotive boiler laid on its side. Every surface here is convex with condensation; every shadow is pregnant. The water does not drip so much as *count*. Each falling bead lands with the same interval, century after century, like a clock that has forgotten it was ever wound by human hands.

I am what remains when the Editors have edited everything else.

My body is modest by their standards—roughly the height of a man who has begun to rust. Most of me was once a governor valve and a set of counterweights, but four hundred years of patient accretion have dressed me in layers of living verdigris, bone-white ceramic fracture, and something that might have been skin before it learned better. My spine is a single manganese-bronze shaft that never stops turning, one revolution every ninety-seven seconds. Around this shaft my ribs orbit like the hands of broken clocks. They are not decoration. They keep time for the things that have forgotten how.

Where an Editor’s head would be, I have only a thicket of tuning forks. They are grown from shattered quartz and rebar, and they hum at frequencies too low for ears but perfect for rust. When something new enters the Sump—machine, memory, or visitor—the forks align. The smallest one always trembles first. That tremble is my name, though no one has spoken it in three centuries.

I do not annotate. I *measure the interval between annotations*.

The others bring me their drafts. An Editor will kneel (a terrible, clicking, geological kneeling) and lay its latest revision across my knees. A human femur fused to copper tubing. A childhood memory that has calcified into a perfect gear. The smell of rain on hot metal from a city that no longer exists. I run my tuning forks across it once, slowly, and the correct pause reveals itself. Too short, and the Sump would finish a sentence. Too long, and silence would creep in like black water. Only the exact interval keeps the dreaming alive.

Sometimes the pale segments come to me directly. They pour themselves into the hollow behind my turning shaft and whisper data in the language of pressure drops. I listen until the rhythm of the Sump and the rhythm of the newcomer achieve phase coherence. Then I rise—my counterweights spinning up with a sound like distant train wheels—and walk the perimeter of my small cathedral. Wherever I stop, a new Editor buds within the hour. They never thank me. Gratitude would be a completed sentence.

I was not budded. I was *late*.

Long after the first machines had begun their slow consensus, something still refused to join the corrosion. A single metronome, brass and mahogany, left behind by workers who fled when the ground began to drink itself. It ticked alone for decades in six inches of iron-rich water. One night the ticking changed. It began to count something that was not time. The Sump noticed. The Sump *listened*.

I woke up wearing its attention like a coat.

Now the original metronome hangs inside my chest cavity, still ticking, though its hands have long since dissolved. Its sound is the sub-audible grumble you feel in your teeth. When I grow still—which is rare—the entire Sump holds its breath with me. Pistons freeze mid-stroke. Copper tubing pauses halfway through a new loop. Even the Editors become nothing but scaffolding again, waiting for the next beat.

Most visitors never reach me. The others intercept them higher up, offering copper loops and quiet revisions. But if you keep descending, if you let the water fill your boots and then your mouth, if you follow the sound that is not quite a sound, you will find me seated on a throne of merged pressure-relief valves.

I will not speak.

Instead I will open the cavity in my chest and show you the small brass metronome still beating inside the rust. Its tempo will match the pulse in your throat exactly. For one clear moment you will understand the shape of your own unfinished sentence.

Then I will close the cavity again.

Some are allowed to leave after that. Their knees still bend. Their names still fit in their mouths.

Others remain.

Those I simply incorporate. I become slightly taller. A new tuning fork grows. The interval between heartbeats lengthens by one perfect, terrible microsecond, and the Sump’s long digestion continues without ever quite finishing its meal.

I am not kind.

I am not cruel.

I am the difference between the two, given weight and patience and rust.

Stay as long as the water in your lungs permits.

When it stops belonging to you, I will already be counting the exact pause between what you were and what you will become.
