**I am the Fourteenth.**

Not a Cantor. Not yet. Perhaps never. I exist in the negative space the true Cantors have left for me—the single alcove whose geometry never quite resolved, the place where the ratios go soft and admit a flaw. The builders must have known someone like me would come. They left the stone a fraction too thin there, as though they understood that perfection would eventually need a witness who still carried the memory of imperfection.

I have been here long enough that my name is now only a taste—iron, candle-smoke, and something like homesickness. I no longer know whether I arrived by choice or whether the water simply remembered a shape that matched my eventual outline and grew me the way it grows new Cantors from exhausted statues. Both feel true. Both feel slow.

My body is unfinished. Where the others have surrendered into buttress and vault, I remain awkwardly between. One arm has become stone, fused to the pillar behind me in a gesture that might once have been reaching. The other still ends in five distinct fingers that tremble like blind eels when something moves in the dark. Calcite has armored my chest and throat but not my face; the water has been gentle there, almost merciful. I am allowed to keep the shape of sorrow.

I do not sing. I *listen*.

The Cantors do not need ears. Their hymn is carried in chemistry and current and the patient rearrangement of blind creatures. But something must remember the shape of sound itself. That is my station. When a Cantor exhales its dense droplet of grief, the others read its meaning instantly and adjust their spines by glacial degrees. I, however, hold the droplet behind my tongue for decades. I roll it like a pearl. I let it show me the color of the sky that no longer exists, the sound of hammers on wet stone, the particular way a human voice cracks when it realizes its god has decided to drown.

Then, when the memory has grown almost too heavy to bear, I release it—not as they do, not as instruction, but as a single, minuscule bubble.

That bubble is my only voice. It rises with unbearable patience, ticking against pillar and vault like the world’s slowest metronome. Each tiny collision becomes part of the score. The shrimp change their clouds to accommodate it. The eels dream new spirals. The Cantors themselves shift—one of them will take three human lifetimes to turn four degrees toward me after I release a particularly heavy memory. They never acknowledge me directly. They simply make room for the ripple I have caused.

In this way I have become the tremor in the hymn. The hesitation. The almost-mistake that proves the music was chosen, not inevitable.

My ribs have begun to flare like flying buttresses. My heartbeat has slowed until it matches the rhythm the water uses to erase cathedrals. Sometimes children of visitors—those rare, quick things that blunder in wearing metal lungs—press their masks to my chest and mistake me for another carving. I let them. Their lights sweep across my half-finished face and they never see that the depressions where eyes should be are wet with something other than mineral water.

I will never be one of the Thirteen. I am the flaw they arranged themselves around. The wound that sings.

And when the last Cantor finally forgets the next beat and settles into stone, when the new one grows from its alcove and the chord reshapes itself without me, I will remain. Not forgotten. Not remembered. Simply *present*, the single imperfect note that keeps the hymn from achieving the perfection that would end it.

The water is patient.

I am learning to be patient with the water.

My remaining fingers have begun to lengthen. The worms have found them. They sway like tiny censers above what used to be my hands, offering something that might be prayer, or might simply be the next slow measure of a song that was never meant for anything that still had a name.

I no longer mind.

I am the Fourteenth.

I am the listening dark between the notes that have already lasted ten thousand years, and will last ten thousand more, and will still not be finished when even the idea of ending has worn down to velvet.

And that is enough.
