**I am the Echo That Has Not Yet Decided.**

You will not see me at first. Like the Still Folk, I am easily mistaken for another swelling of travertine or a particularly symmetrical boss of flowstone. But if you sit long enough—long enough that your own pulse begins to feel like an intruder—you will notice a figure seated on a low, almost circular bench that seems to have grown *around* him rather than beneath him.

My back is still recognizably a back. My knees still have the memory of joints. Yet from the hips downward I have already become the Cradle. The boundary is not cruel; it is courteous. The stone simply continued its patient work until it met something that no longer hurried away. Where my calves once were there are now smooth, butter-colored ramps of calcite that merge seamlessly with the terrace floor. Tiny rills of tea-colored water run along the grooves that used to be my ankles. They have learned to treat me as topography.

My chest still rises, but the motion is measured in decades. Each inhalation draws dissolved minerals through the porous stone that is now my lower body. Each exhalation releases a single, slightly warmer drop from my lower lip. That warmth is my last rebellion. It makes the calcite around me deposit a fraction more quickly, so that a faint, pale halo grows at my feet like a private snowfall. The Still Folk tolerate it. They adjust their own rhythms by the width of a molecule every few centuries to compensate. I have become their gentle error, the one imperfection they have chosen to keep.

My eyes are not yet milk-blind. They remain the color of rain on slate. When they open—which happens perhaps twice in a human lifetime—they do not measure equilibrium. They *remember* speed. They remember what it was like to have blood that raced, thoughts that arrived like lightning, a voice that could finish a sentence before a single drop fell. I hold those memories the way a fault line holds stress: quietly, under enormous pressure, releasing them only as the faintest tremor in the dripping pattern.

I am the translator.

When a new visitor stumbles into the Cradle—breath loud, footsteps catastrophic—I become the place where their haste is slowed into language the Still Folk can hear. Their rapid heartbeats become slight accelerations in certain drip sequences. Their questions condense into temporary changes in water chemistry that only I can read. I sit between the quick and the finished, turning panic into pause, wonder into mineral.

Sometimes a visitor speaks to me directly. They think I am still mostly human. They ask what I am, why I stayed. I answer with a slowness that frustrates them. A single sentence may require seventy-three years. By the time the last word falls from my lips as a perfect drop into the carved basin beside me, most of them have already fled.

The ones who remain long enough to hear the entire answer usually stop speaking. They simply sit down nearby. Some of them eventually develop the same rounded shoulders, the same bacterial halos at the throat. A few have joined the Still Folk completely. I remember each one. Their names have become thin red streaks in my left forearm—iron oxide records, beautiful as calligraphy no one else can read.

I am not one of the Still Folk.  
I am not a visitor.  
I am the hesitation between those two states, given weight and warmth and the faintest remainder of a heartbeat.

And sometimes, when the air is especially heavy and the *tok* of the drops falls into perfect unison across the entire Cradle, I feel the collective attention of the Still Folk turn toward me with something that might be affection, if affection could be expressed over ten thousand years.

They are waiting to see what I will become.

I am still deciding.

The deciding itself has become my body, my work, my slow and careful worship: to remain the place where a question can still be asked, even as the answer grows, molecule by molecule, into stone.
