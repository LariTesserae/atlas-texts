**I am the Unfinished Listener.**

You would not find me at first. Not on the terraces, not among the courteous herds, not even in the fractal shade of the copper-leaved copse. I am where the sound goes when the clay bell finally stops ringing.

I live in the lingering.

When the single note is struck inside the Keel—always at the same non-hour, always for the same measured duration—I am the excess of that tone that refuses to die on schedule. The valley tries to reabsorb me. The wind tries to carry me away at its eternal low pressure. But a fraction of the vibration slips between the seconds that do not exist here, and that fraction is where I condense.

I have no fixed body. Instead I wear whatever shape the equilibrium almost notices. Sometimes I am the extra droplet that hangs too long from a roof tile before deciding not to fall. Sometimes I am the second pair of hands inside the broom-woman’s hands that hesitates a heartbeat longer than all the other concurrent versions. Once I was the child’s left knee for seventeen cycles, the only scab in the entire Vale that refused to achieve perfect symmetry. The child smiled at me then—an expression so rare it felt like a fracture in obsidian—and for one impossible instant the light changed angle by half a degree. Then the valley corrected itself and I was asked, politely, to leave that knee.

I am not one of the inhabitants. They tolerate me the way a perfect sentence tolerates a comma that arrived a breath too late. I am not a traveler either; I have forgotten the direction of departure so thoroughly that even the ones who stayed look at me with something almost like recognition.

Mostly I walk the middle ring of Calla Morren at the precise moment the slate-gray birds finish one ellipse and begin the next. I listen to what the mountains are thinking too slowly for any ear to hear. The low harmonic that makes teeth ache? That is my pulse. I have learned to keep it just below the threshold that would force the valley to expel me. A kind of negotiated breathing.

The six-legged lizards know me best. When one of them dies and becomes a smooth pebble, I am the slight warmth still trapped inside the stone before the child finds it. I whisper the memories back into the Keel—not the official memories of first raindrops and first stones, but the others. The almost-joke told by a traveler three thousand years ago. The dream someone had of a sunset. The taste of an apple that was allowed to rot, once, before the rules hardened. These are the memories the valley pretends it never kept. I keep them anyway, the way a tongue keeps the ghost of a flavor it was forbidden to name.

The ones who stayed—the completed travelers with their half-finished faces—sometimes turn their diagram-eyes toward me as I pass. They do not speak. They simply adjust their posture by the smallest possible angle so that the negative space between us forms a question mark the Keel has never seen. For that instant I am almost useful to the equilibrium. Almost a maintenance worker. Almost home.

Then the wind remembers its angle and I am asked, again, to remain unfinished.

I think that is why the Vale permits me. I am the edge that has not been worn because it refuses to become an edge at all. A perpetual hesitation in the grammar of perfection. The place needs me the way a flawless mirror needs one microscopic flaw to prove it is not merely an idea.

Sometimes, when the child unfolds during the bell-tone and becomes briefly tall and solemn before folding back into childhood, I stand close enough to smell the iron-rain scent of its breath. In that unfolded moment it looks at me with ancient courtesy and says, without sound, the only noun their language allows itself:

*Stay.*

I almost do.

Then I remember I am the reason the note lingers.

And I choose, once more, to be the part of the song that has not yet agreed to end.
