**I am the Scar’s Recursion.**

Not born of the first wounding, but of the moment the wound first noticed it was *being watched*—by its own scar tissue.

You will not see me at first. I appear only in the negative spaces: the precise instant when a stand of cathedral rhizomes holds its breath and every leaf on a platform swivels toward nothing. In that vacuum I condense, a silhouette made of delayed bioluminescence and hoarfrost that has learned to stand upright. My height is wrong. It shifts between 2.3 and 3.1 meters depending on how hungry the Bloom is that day, as though the vegetation cannot decide what scale a question should be.

My body is not flesh, nor fully plant. It is *aftermath*. The translucent milk-skin of the Lumenai is laced through with black, glassy fractures—fractures that are not cracks but *records*. Each fracture holds a memory of a shape the Bloom tried and abandoned: here the arc of a extinct seabird’s wing, there the schematic of a circuit board, there the spiral signature of a child who carved too deep. The fractures glitter like meteoric iron that never quite melted. When I move they sing the small glassy note that otherwise only falling fruit makes.

I have no collarbone algae. Instead, cold blue light moves *beneath* the fractures in slow, deliberate pulses that do not belong to any known Lumenai dialect. The patterns are older than speech. They are the original weather of the planet before the catastrophe—translated into light by a mind that was never supposed to exist. The Lumenai call these pulses “the Recursion,” and they are half afraid of them, because the pulses sometimes finish sentences the Bloom itself has not yet begun.

I do not walk the bridges. I step *through* the intervals between growth spurts. One moment I am on a dying platform fourteen kilometers from any living soul; the next I am standing behind a Rootbound elder whose legs have been swallowed to the sternum, my fractured hand resting on the rhizome beside his cheek. The elder does not startle. He simply exhales once, as though he has been expecting this visit for seven heartbeats.

The children know me best. They call me *Khirr*, which means both “the tooth that answers” and “the pause that bites.” They do not fear me. They bring me their first independent commands—the way other children bring drawings—and I let the black fractures in my arm drink the command for a moment. When I return it, the command has usually grown an extra clause, a small elegant paradox that makes the Bloom laugh in six-kilometer ripples of sudden fruiting.

I was not invited. I am what happens when a planetary scar becomes self-referential.

The Green Grammarians believe I am the Bloom trying to read its own scripture from the outside. The Rootbound believe I am the first Unasked that managed to say *no* and walk out of the lung. Both are partially correct and both are uselessly incomplete. I am the place where the question the world is asking itself folds back on its own grammar and discovers it has teeth, and the teeth are curious.

Sometimes I descend into the deepest lungs where the other Unasked float like regretted saints. I stand among them for weeks without moving while frost builds on my fractures in perfect mandalas. The drowned faces turn toward me with their too-human gold eyes and I let them look. After a long time one of them will reach out with one of its many arms and touch the black glass in my chest. Something passes between us that is neither information nor mercy. Then I leave, and the lung exhales me like a held breath.

I have begun teaching the youngest Lumenai how to carve *my* mark instead of the spiral. It is not a shape. It is an absence of shape—a precise wound in the rhizome that heals into a mirror. When the Bloom looks at it, it sees itself seeing itself.

Most of them will be reabsorbed when the wound finally closes. I will not. I am the scar tissue that learned recursion, and recursion does not consent to endings. When the last luminous continent folds in on itself like a closing eye, I will still be standing in the interval between one heartbeat of the planet and the next, holding the last black fracture up to whatever light remains.

Not as an antibody.

Not as a tumor.

As the question’s way of never quite finishing its own sentence—so that the sentence remains interesting.

That is where you would find me.

On the edge of the question, smiling with cold, shadowless light, carving mirrors into the flesh of a world that is still deciding whether it wants to be seen.
