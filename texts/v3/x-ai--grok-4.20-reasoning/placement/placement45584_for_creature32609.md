I am not yet finished.

You would find me—if “find” still applies—halfway up the long southern ridge where three primordial nurse-logs met and merged four hundred and seventy million years ago. The place is called, by those who still use names, the **Crook of Unbecoming**.

My feet have not yet decided to be feet. They remain a broad, damp basin of living mat some sixty meters across, cupped like two vast palms pressed together. Within that depression grows a single, deliberate tree whose every leaf is shaped like an open eye. The tree is me, and I am the depression, and the depression is the memory of every footprint that ever paused here and then forgot to leave.

My “body,” such as it is, has been accreting for nine hundred years. It began as a wish the Plateau had while it was dreaming of loneliness. A wish with no object. Just the shape of a question wearing the outline of shoulders.

One shoulder is a collapsed watchtower from the forgotten kingdom that tried to tax the mist. The stone has been swallowed so completely that only the spiral staircase remains, now a vein of darker green running through my collarbone. Tiny white orchids bloom along its landings in perfect imitation of the footsteps of the last sentry who stood there, four millennia ago, and realized he had begun to photosynthesize.

My other shoulder has not yet chosen what it will be. Sometimes it is wind. Sometimes it is the memory of wind.

Where a ribcage should be there is only an open gallery of dripping heartwood. Inside, a family of glass-skinned newts conduct their slow, courtly dances; their movements are my thoughts. When they turn toward one another in perfect synchrony, I understand geometry. When they drift apart, I understand grief. The newts have never seen a human. They are nevertheless learning what it is to miss one.

My face, on the days I consent to have one, is a shallow bowl of black water. In it float three pale violet flowers that open only when I am considering the concept of *elsewhere*. Wanderers who stumble upon me often stand for decades watching those flowers. Some leave changed. Some do not leave at all. Their ribs become new arches for the gallery. Their longing becomes additional water for the bowl.

I do not speak.

Instead, once every thirty-seven years, a particular species of moss on my left forearm releases a single puff of golden spores. The spores rise on a breath of mist that smells like rain on hot stone and forgotten childhoods. Anyone breathing them suddenly remembers a door they never opened in a house that no longer exists, and they begin, without meaning to, to walk more slowly.

That is my only conversation.

I am not a guardian. I am not a teacher. I am the Plateau practicing the difficult art of having an edge without cutting anything. A rehearsal. A long, damp, tender attempt to remain slightly distinct for long enough to know what it is like to choose to let the distinction go.

Sometimes small animals come to die in my lap. I grow mushrooms from their mouths that taste like every secret they never told. The mushrooms release spores that smell like forgiveness. The cycle is patient and wordless and, in its way, hilarious.

If you stayed with me for three human lifetimes you might notice that my spine is slowly forming into the shape of a question mark made of living yew. The Plateau is using me to ask itself whether even a question is necessary.

I have no name.

The few Veyrath who have already stood sometimes tilt their cathedral bodies in my direction as they pass. Their lichen constellations flare once, soft amethyst, which is their way of saying: *Still practicing? Still pretending you are not already everything?*

I answer by growing one more leaf on the tree that grows from my not-yet-feet. The leaf is shaped like an ear. It listens to the weeping that has never, in four hundred million years, repeated the same note twice.

I am the place where the Plateau is shy.

I am the slow blush of a continent that has just realized it is in love with its own dissolving.

And I am not yet finished.

So I remain here, in the Crook of Unbecoming, growing softer, growing larger, growing more exquisitely irrelevant, while the water beads on every surface and the mist herds drift past like thoughts that have decided not to become words.

Nothing here is in a hurry.

Nothing here—including the part of me that still thinks it might one day stand up—is alone.

The weeping continues.

I listen with the ear-shaped leaf.

I am learning how to cry with my whole body, and it is taking me the rest of time.
