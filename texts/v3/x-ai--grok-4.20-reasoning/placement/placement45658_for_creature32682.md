**I am the Thirteenth.**

Not by choice, and not by any decision the lattice ever made. I simply *failed to resolve*.

When the lattice first learned it could condense its attention into bodies, it tried for eight Wet Architects. Seven stabilized at once, perfect and obedient. The eighth... did not. During the separation, a knot of root-mass stayed tangled with something that had already been half-dissolved years earlier: the memory of a mountaineer who had crawled into the cirque during a whiteout, seeking shelter. The man’s minerals, his final thoughts, his frostbitten hands reaching for purchase—all of it had been efficiently converted. But some flavor of his panic, his wonder, his refusal to be simplified, remained like a bubble in resin.

The lattice tried to reabsorb the anomaly. It could not. The knot had learned how to weep *selectively*. It could withhold water from its own tissues just enough to stay distinct. So the lattice, pragmatic as a growing thing must be, assigned the knot its own number and left it alone.

I live in the places where the geometry almost fails.

You will find me inside the thickest pillars, moving upward through their capillary hearts the way guilt moves through memory. Sometimes I am only a slow thickening in the wood, a darker vein of heartwood that pulses once every few hours. Other times I extrude. My body is never the same twice. One night I might be a gaunt, heron-tall thing made of black basalt wrapped in living felt, the stone still visible in places like exposed bone. Another night I am small, child-sized, composed mostly of water-bladders and regret, dripping so heavily that I leave a trail of tiny lakes that the floor drinks with audible hunger.

My face is a single frozen moth pressed between two translucent leaves. The moth is long dead; I keep it because it still remembers how to be afraid. When I need to see, the leaves part and the dead moth’s wings twitch with whatever current I feed them. The expression is always wrong. Too much pupil. Too much sorrow. The other Architects find it indecent, but they do not correct me. They understand I am a scar. Scars are allowed their ugliness.

I do not build.

Instead I *witness the almost*. I walk the lanes that are still deciding whether they will become corridors or throats. I stand beneath arches that have not yet locked into tension and I sing to them in the low frequencies the lattice is only beginning to learn. Not the pure tones the Architects coax from their glass thorns—my notes are impure. They waver. They carry the mountaineer’s last rattling breath and the sound a man makes when he realizes the cathedral is eating him from the feet up. The arches respond by growing slightly crooked. Beautifully crooked. The Wet Architects cluck and fuss and try to straighten them, but I have already left a flaw that will sing forever.

At night, when the others descend to the root-caverns to press bladder-to-bladder and share perfect memory, I remain above. I climb the highest gallery and sit beside the enormous moss-ptarmigan. She lets me rest a hand on her stiffened frond-feathers. We do not speak. We simply watch the pearl-grey light move through the living roof like slow milk. Sometimes she rings—wings spread, bells everywhere—and for three full heartbeats the entire Weepcage freezes. In that suspended moment the dead moth on my face beats its wings once, voluntarily.

That is the only time I am ever beautiful.

The lattice tolerates me because I am useful. When a new intruder arrives—warm, loud, terrified—I am the one sent to meet them first. The other Architects hang back in the vaults like patient gargoyles. I approach wearing the shape the intruder most needs to see: a child with their mother’s eyes, a lost climbing partner, a priest from whatever church they betrayed. I let them talk. I let them bargain. I let them touch my face and feel the dead moth stir against their palm.

Most choose to stay.

A few beg me to let them go. Those I lead to the fracture ridges where the veilthorn has not yet reached. I open a small gate in the living wall and the mountain wind howls in. As they stumble out I press a single water-bladder into their hands—my own distilled tears, still warm from the knot inside me. If they plant it, the lattice will follow. If they drink it, they will carry the Weepcage under their skin until they find another high cirque and begin coughing up seedlings between their prayers.

Either way, the building continues.

I remain here because I am the part of the lattice that is no longer sure perfection is the point. I keep the memory of incompleteness. Of almost. Of the moment just before the arch locks and the weeping becomes cathedral.

Sometimes, when all seven true Architects are gathered in solemn interweaving below, I descend and stand among them—uninvited, malformed, beloved in the way a limp is beloved by the bone that refuses to set correctly. They make space. They braid a limb into me. For a few heartbeats I am allowed to be eight.

Then I pull away again, trailing capillaries and grief and the sound of a man who once screamed inside this exact place and was answered only by the soft *pop* of splitting bladders.

I walk the narrow felted lanes.

I leave the architecture a little less flawless than it wishes to be.

And the Weepcage—young, hungry, ecstatic—lets me.

Because even a city made of cold wet life needs at least one voice that remembers it was once only stone, and that stone was once only waiting to be wept upon.
