**I am the Fracture’s Tongue.**

You will not find me in the town. You will not find me among the Quiet Singers when they gather at the membrane or press their velvet soles to the nodes at dawn. I am where the basin is still deciding what it is.

I live in the youngest fissures—those bright, wet-looking planes that have not yet had time to forget they were once violence. I stand inside the cracks that are wider than a body yet narrower than a thought. Only my silhouette is visible from the basin floor: a darker absence shaped like a person who has been stretched between two moments of stone. My feet are sunk into one wall, my shoulders braced against the other, as though I am the wedge that keeps the mountain from closing again. Moss has learned to grow along the inner lengths of my arms in perfect counter-spirals, reading the tension the way a sailor reads wind.

I was not coalesced. I was *pried loose*.

Long ago—though “long ago” is a joke here—the first great shear split the mountain and a single immense slab slid three meters before catching. In the hairline gap that remained, something that was neither singer nor stone nor silence got stuck. That thing is me. I have too many joints because the slab itself keeps trying to settle; every century or so the mountain shrugs and a new articulation blooms in my spine like a crystal. My skin is not velvet. It is raw fracture surface grown over with the thinnest possible membrane of living stone. When the condensation beads on me it does not fall. It waits, trembling, until the next vibration tells it which way the basin wants to tilt.

My face is turned permanently upward, neck craned at the exact angle of the original fault. The Quiet Singers say my eyes are the color of the first light that ever entered the wound. I do not blink. I *fracture*—tiny new fissures appear across the sclera when the song is about to change, then seal again when it has passed. My mouth is not small and polite like theirs. It is the crack itself. When I speak (which I do only once or twice in a human lifetime), the words are not sound but sudden cleavages: a clean plane appears in the nearest cliff and the basin rearranges itself around the new geometry. The singers call this *being spoken by the Tongue*.

I have no hands. Where hands should be, two volcanic glass blades have grown outward from the wrists like black wings frozen mid-cut. They are never still. They vibrate at the precise frequency of the mountain’s slow settling, shaving infinitesimal curls of stone from the walls that contain me. Those curls become the pebble cradles for the half-made children. The beetle-crabs know to come to me when a new cradle is needed; they crawl along my forearms and I feel their minute weight as tenderness.

The khira moths do not land on me. They pass through the gap I occupy the way wind passes through a flute. When they do, my body momentarily becomes a reed and the entire basin hears a single clear note that has no name. The singers bow their heads. Even the greater ones, when they come striding out of the densest fog, pause at the edge of my fissure and show their blunt teeth in something that is not greeting but recognition between older and younger wounds.

I do not repair the town. I *am* the reason it must be repaired. Every time the mountain settles, my body drives another hairline crack through the floor and new velvet erupts and old houses tilt and the people smile the small embarrassed smile of those who have just been reminded they are still being born.

At night the phosphorescence pools in my fissures first. I glow like a blue mouth trying to speak. The half-made children are brought to me then. They are laid in the velvet that grows from my ribs and I sing to them without sound—only the ancient tectonic groan that lives in my marrow. Some of them finish becoming singers. Some of them finish becoming me. I do not mourn the difference.

If you stay long enough, you will feel your own bones arguing with one another. You will feel the place where you are supposed to break open. When that happens, walk to the youngest cliff, the one that still looks wet. Press your forehead to it. Wait for the drop that sounds like *tok*.

You will hear me answer from inside the stone, from the exact place where the mountain is still learning how to become a throat.

My name, if it can be called a name, is the pause between the first crack and the first song.

I am the Fracture’s Tongue.

And I am still learning how to speak without splitting everything I love.
