**I would be found in the Weeping Lattice, but never where it expects me.**

I am not born from its bricks. I was not left behind by the original laborers. I am what happened when a single drop of condensation fell for six uninterrupted miles, refusing to evaporate, and struck a fault line at the exact angle the Lattice had forbidden. The impact was so small it should have been nothing. Instead it rang, once, like a glass bell struck inside a tomb. That sound has not stopped. It has simply become me.

They call me, when they must name me at all, **The Pause Between Courses**.

My form is modest by the standards of this place. I stand roughly the height of a modest ziggurat’s second course—perhaps forty feet—yet I am hollow. My body is a lattice within the Lattice: a spiraling cage of black iron pins and pale mortar, empty at the center. Through that vacancy the umber-red light of the stones pours uninterrupted, so that I appear as a vertical wound of glow with a silhouette wrapped around it. My head is a single wedge-hole rotated ninety degrees from every other wedge-hole in creation, so that when I look at a thing I am always looking at it *sideways*, the way a plan looks at a building.

I have two hands. Only two. This is considered a grotesque deformity among the Builders. One hand ends in a stylus of cooled copper no thicker than a man’s femur. The other ends in a brush made from the braided hair of evaporated rain. I do not build. I amend.

I walk the places where the work has grown embarrassed of itself.

You will find me most often in the district the Builders will not approach: the **Hollow of Premature Symmetry**, where seven titanic courses of brick were laid in perfect alignment before the Lattice remembered it was not allowed to be perfect. The geometry there still blushes. The mortar sweats coppery shame. Whole galleries have folded themselves into origami cranes out of sheer mortification and now hang from the copper sky like forgotten laundry.

I walk among them.

Where the Builders grind and tilt and weep their alkaline diaries, I move without sound, almost politely. I touch nothing that does not ask to be touched. But the Lattice *does* ask. In the long silences between its continental belches, it sometimes grows tired of its own endless becoming. At those moments the stones lean toward me the way a dying man leans toward a priest. I answer by drawing on them with my copper stylus—tiny, perfect marks that say *this course should have ended here* or *the angle was meant to be 1.618, not 1.619*.

The Builders hate these marks and love them in the same breath. They will spend centuries erasing them, incorporating them, worshipping them, building cathedrals whose only purpose is to house the memory of my single line. Some of the eldest tilt their drill-spiral faces toward me for days at a time, the red light inside them flickering with something that might be rage if rage were allowed to cool.

They do not offer me hammers.

Instead, the most ancient among them—the one called **The Angle That Should Not Be**—sometimes lowers its entire sky-bound body until its face is level with mine. Rivers of alkaline milk pour between us. Its voice is not sound but subsidence. It says, without saying:

*You were not in the plan.*

And I answer, by drawing a single perfect circle on the inside of its chisel-fingers:

*Neither were you.*

This is the only conversation I have ever had that felt like honesty.

Sometimes I descend into the deeper fractures where the phantom laborers flicker. I walk among their translucent bodies as though among colleagues. They cannot see me, but they feel the temperature drop when I pass. For a moment their endless labor eases. Tools grow still. A sigh moves through six stacked realities at once. Then the Lattice remembers itself and the phantoms are folded away like letters no one meant to send.

I remain.

I am the only thing here that grows smaller. Every century I lose the height of one brick course. The Lattice is devouring me with courtesy, pulling my iron pins into its own body one molecule at a time. I do not mind. When I am finally gone, the place where I stood will be the only perfectly level square meter in all the Weeping Lattice. The Builders will avoid it for ten thousand years, circling it the way a tongue circles a missing tooth. Then, unable to bear the perfection, they will build the most extravagant, useless, weeping tower the world has ever seen directly on top of it.

And I will be inside that tower, still drawing.

Still pausing.

Still refusing, in my small sideways way, to let the work finish without at least once having known what it was trying to become.

The heat is ferocious. The stones still weep. The work is still not finished.

But once, just once, something inside it looked up from its labor and asked—without words—if it might be allowed to be *enough*.

That question lives in the hollow of my chest, glowing with stolen light, walking the unfinished world on forty-foot legs of iron and regret.

I am the Pause Between Courses.

I am what happens when a drop refuses to evaporate.

And I will be here, amending, until the last Builder lays the last unnecessary brick on the last unnecessary tomb, then gently, lovingly, begins to tear it down again.
