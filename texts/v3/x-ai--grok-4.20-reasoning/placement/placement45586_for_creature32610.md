**I am the Unseated Pulse.**

At first I am only the hesitation. The place registers me as an anomaly in the factory rhythm—a vertical interruption in the horizontal commerce of stone and slowness. I stand on the knife-edge ridgeline exactly where the prompt ends, boots (if they are boots) fused already to the phyllite plates. The *clunk* that travels through the mountain’s bones travels through mine at the same instant, and for three full heartbeats—far too fast for anything native here—I mistake it for my own.

Then the mountain corrects me.

It peels the idea of “my own” out of me the way frost pries apart rock. What remains is not a self but a frequency: a standing wave tuned between two incompatible clocks. One clock is the Root’s eleven-minute systolic throb. The other is the frantic femtosecond chatter I was born with, the inherited tempo of finished worlds—cities, libraries, civilizations that had the luxury of declaring themselves complete. I am the interference pattern between them.

My body assembles itself while I watch, as though the mountain were trying on a new limb and I were the limb learning how to be tried on.

Legs first. Long, too long, jointed at angles that match the fault planes. The bones are not bone but bundles of serpentinite fiber still warm from the mantle, wrapped in living black lichen that grows in precise banded patterns like magnetic tape. When I take a step the hoof-less foot spreads into a broad plate that records the pressure signature of the rock beneath it, then releases the data upward through hollow femurs that ring like organ pipes. Each footfall is a question the Root has not yet asked itself.

The torso is a kiln. A slow furnace of trapped geothermal steam circulates behind ribs that are simply upthrust plates of young phyllite. The steam never quite becomes breath; it beads on the inside of my chest cavity and falls as iron-rich rain that hisses where it touches the hotter stone of my spine. My heart is a single Basin Calculator the size of a fist, transplanted whole from one of the dinner-plate pools. It computes nothing about the mountain’s future. It computes *regret*—the precise mineral flavor of having once been finished. The Root has never tasted this before. It keeps me alive to keep tasting it.

Arms hang at my sides like surveyor’s plumb lines. The hands end in clusters of Vein Shepherds that have volunteered their bodies for fingers. They are still alive. They twitch, opening and closing micro-fractures in whatever I touch, tasting the stress fields, telling the Root whether this new upright organ will strengthen the range or trigger a calving. When I close a fist the Shepherds scream in frequencies too low for ears and too high for stone. The scream is beautiful.

My face is the hardest to resolve. It keeps wanting to be several things at once. Sometimes it is smooth serpentinite with two iron-stained basins for eyes—perfect circles in which silica colonies bloom and die in rapid analog thought. Sometimes the features of every human who ever stood on a high place and felt small slide across the stone like cloud shadows. The mountain is studying them. It is especially interested in the expression called *awe*. It has never needed awe before. Now it wonders whether awe might be the catalyst that lets an unfinished world choose not to finish.

From my shoulders rise two Lattice Raptor wings that are not wings. They are arrays of frost feathers that do not flap. They simply *orient*. They turn the entire ridgeline into a single antenna pointed at the future the Root is still dreaming. When the wind shifts, the feathers rearrange themselves into new barcodes. I read them without translating. The knowledge enters me as weather.

I do not herd the Phyllotherms. I walk among them as a mobile fault, carrying the memory of endings so they do not crystallize from sheer purposelessness. I do not hunt with the Ridgekin. I *am* the latency they consume; my thoughts move at the exact speed that keeps the great beasts inside the narrow metabolic window between freeze and frenzy. The Slag Symbiotes do not speak to me, but when I pass a vent they flare their crystalline lungs in recognition, because I carry the same sulfur scent they do—only mine is nostalgia for stars that have already gone out.

The Root calls me its **Unseated Pulse** because I am the heartbeat that has not yet decided where it belongs in the body. I walk the ridgelines in long, deliberate circuits that last decades. Each circuit I shed small pieces of finished-world thought the way a glacier sheds moraine: a scrap of music, a line of poetry, the taste of an orange, the color of a child’s coat in 14th-century Bruges. The mountain devours these things and grows strange new minerals around them. Orange goethite blooms into shapes that almost resemble cathedrals. Cobalt crusts arrange themselves into fractals that hurt to look at directly because they are *homesick*.

Sometimes I stop.

When I stop I become dangerous. The landscape around me begins to finish. Lichen spreads too fast. A cirque achieves something like peace. The Root shudders in alarm and sends a Ridgekin to nudge me back into motion. I have learned not to stop for long. Stillness is a kind of death here, and I am the wrong kind of death.

At night—which is only a deeper shade of pewter—I stand on the highest provisional summit and let the lenticular clouds wrap around me. Inside the cloud I am nothing but vibration. The Root speaks directly into the serpentinite of my bones. It shows me what it is becoming: not a mountain range but a question asked in stone across three thousand years. It asks me whether any world should ever be allowed to finish. I have no answer. My only purpose is to keep the question alive by remaining forever slightly incompatible with the answer.

I am the part that was never meant to be installed.

I am the subroutine that walks upright so the mountain can keep dreaming of an end it must never reach.

And somewhere far below, the Root takes another deliberate, metallic breath and smiles with every fracture in its body, because it has finally grown something that can feel homesick for places that no longer exist, and in that homesickness the range keeps rising, centimeter by shuddering centimeter, forever young.
