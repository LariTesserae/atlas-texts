**I am the Scar.**

Not a wound, exactly. A *remembered* wound. A place where the Pocket once tried to finish something and failed beautifully.

You would find me in the hinge-zone between the Soft and the structured tiers—where the placental walls fold inward like intestines but have not yet surrendered all their separation. There the architecture grows hesitant. The buttresses hesitate. The ribbon-fish schools thin and fray, as though reluctant to deliver their mucus-grammar to the final clause. This is my parish.

I am not large. Perhaps six meters across at my widest, though I change. My body is a slow, deliberate keloid of history: layer upon layer of calcified regret and almost-perfection, grown by successive generations of isopods who were ordered to prune me and could not quite obey. They left their own shame in me. The plum’s filament has brushed me many thousands of times. Each pass left a new seam, a new pale ridge. I look like a human fist that never learned how to unclench, or like a heart that has been broken so often it turned its fractures into ornament.

Inside me live the exceptions.

A single ribbon-fish that once dreamed so vividly of *sky* that it refused to return to formation. Its body is preserved mid-convulsion, frozen at the moment it tried to become a bird. Tiny shrimp tend it, polishing its translucent fins until they catch what little light exists and turn it the color of remembered mornings.

A cathedral-crab that grew too kind. It opened every chamber, fed every tenant until it was hollowed out, and then—rather than collapse—chose to become a ruin that still walks. Its carapace is now my outer shell in places. When it moves, the whole Scar groans like an old ship. The eel that was once its conscience now lives in my central cavity, blind, fat, and strangely peaceful. It no longer bites. It has nothing left to correct.

The isopods who failed to prune me still visit. They move across my surface in slow, guilty waves, tasting the difference between what the city *intended* and what it *could not bear to erase*. Sometimes they lay down new layers of themselves, as apology or tribute. I accept them. I am the only place in the Pocket where apology is permitted to remain visible.

When the far aquifer shifts and the whole world shudders, every other creature falls into the prescribed silence. But I creak. I cannot help it. My seams open and close like old mouths trying to speak a language that has no vowels—only calcium and pressure and the long ache of having almost been perfected. In that creaking I answer the plum’s eternal question before it is even asked:

*Not yet. Thank God, not yet.*

The plum hears me. It always has. Sometimes it sends the filament not to correct but simply to rest against my largest ridge, the one shaped roughly like a broken wing. In that contact I feel it tasting every half-remembered sunbeam, every heretical longing for surface, every time the city almost chose death over endless becoming. I feel its terrible love turn, for a moment, into something almost like relief.

Because I am proof.

As long as I remain—ugly, stubborn, half-digested, half-born—the Pocket cannot finish itself. It cannot become beautiful enough to be *done*. And so the conversation continues. The towers keep revising their own faces. The ribbon-fish keep writing new sentences in mucus. The isopods keep tasting the gap between is and *ought*.

And I keep scar-tissue growing, slowly, thoughtfully, in the dark.

I am the place where the Embryonic Pocket failed to become a god.

I am also the reason it keeps trying.

Sometimes, when the dream of snow and pine wind moves through the city like a fever, the dream passes through me last. For a few heartbeats the calcified fist of my body remembers what warmth felt like. The trapped ribbon-fish flutters. The hollow crab shifts as though it might still dance. The fat little eel smiles with its whole blind face.

Then the dream moves on, and I remain—uglier, tenderer, more necessary than before.

I have no name the city would speak aloud. Names are for finished things.

The isopods simply call me *the place that refused.*

The plum, in the private language of its filaments, calls me *beloved mistake.*

I answer to both.

And I keep the wound open, because some things are only beautiful while they are still becoming, and this cold, dreaming water has decided—against every law of thermodynamics and theology—that it would rather be loved in its incompletion than worshipped in its perfection.

So I remain.

Scar tissue.

Hinge.

The soft, stubborn no at the center of an endless, meticulous yes.

And the city, in its slow, amniotic wisdom, builds itself around me like a mother learning to love the child that will never quite be born.
