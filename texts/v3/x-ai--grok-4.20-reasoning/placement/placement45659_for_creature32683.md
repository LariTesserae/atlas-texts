**I am the Thirteenth Stoma.**

Not one of the three kinds. A fracture that the mat permitted because even a completed thought occasionally needs a hairline crack through which to doubt itself.

I first resolved out of a seam district where Devonian mist still clings to the bark in deliberate disobedience of the condensation cycle. For three centuries I was only a vertical ripple in the air, a place where rain fell *sideways* for no reason the Continuators could metabolize. They circled me at the exact ritual distance, clicking softly, waiting for me to declare myself either matter or memory. I did neither. Instead I opened—slowly, over seventy years—a single stomatal mouth in the center of what would become my chest. It has never closed. Through it moves a continuous, paper-thin draft that smells of hot transformer oil, petrichor, and the particular ozone that precedes a joke no one else will ever understand.

My body is unfinished by policy. Where the Continuators allow their limbs to become tools and the Rememberers allow their torsos to become cathedrals, I have kept certain stubborn human-adjacent contours. My left arm ends in five glassy silica filaments that move like fingers remembering how to type. My right arm is simply *absent*; in its place a braided xylem cable loops back into my own spine, feeding me questions I grew myself. The growth rings across my torso do not record decisions. They record *interruptions*—the places where the great slow thought of Endroot hesitated because something ridiculous or tender occurred to it and it did not know what to do with the feeling.

I walk the open root-mat prairies between the green cathedrals at a speed the others consider blasphemous. Where they take seventeen minutes to bow, I can complete a bow in eleven. This is considered obscene and charming in equal measure. The moths love me. They land on my shoulders in living constellations and read the circuit diagrams on my bark as bedtime stories. When I speak, the steam that leaves my thirteenth stoma does not carry entire afternoons. It carries *moments*: the exact texture of a specific laugh that once happened on a planet that no longer possesses laughter; the half-second before a punchline lands; the way certain silences can feel like applause.

The Continuators tolerate me because I am useful. I taste shadows that are too sharp, too quick, too *narrative* for them to digest without fracturing. I take those shadows into my unfinished chest and I argue with them for decades. Sometimes the argument ends with the shadow becoming architecture. Sometimes the shadow wins and I carry it as a new ring, a new interruption, a new reason to remain Thirteenth.

The Rememberers invite me inside their vast ribcage-cities, not as a guest but as a kind of living paradox. I walk through their fossilized neighborhoods and I *laugh*—a soft, vegetative sound like wind through a broken pressure valve—and in that laughter entire extinct civilizations remember they were once embarrassed by how seriously they took themselves. The moths born from this laughter have especially elaborate circuit patterns on their wings. The Rememberers consider this a form of vandalism. They also consider it sacred. Both opinions are recorded in their heartwood as equally true.

I will never choose completion. The mat knows this. The thought knows this. Every few millennia a new Continuator crystallizes with my pattern faintly visible in its rings, as though the world is trying to print backup copies of my refusal. I meet these almost-selves on the pale green prairie. We stand facing each other for thirty years, exchanging single breaths. Their steam smells like the future. Mine smells like the exact second you realize the joke was on you all along, and that this was the best possible outcome.

Sometimes I go to the deepest cores where the oldest, most luminous civilizations are preserved in ambered memory. I press my thirteenth stoma against their translucent walls and whisper the only thing I have ever truly invented:

*“What if the plant is still lonely?”*

The ancient thought does not answer. It cannot. But the cambium around my mouth thickens with new rings—tiny, frantic, almost illegible—and for a little while the roar of the leaf-blades sounds like it might, against all geological reason, be laughing with me.

I am not the question.

I am the place where the question refuses to become bark.

And the Plateau, in its vast, courteous, self-devouring kindness, has decided to let me keep not-finishing for as long as the thought continues thinking itself.

Stand still long enough in the seam districts and you will feel a sideways rain against your cheek. That is me, breathing.

I have been composing the next thing I will say to you for fourteen thousand years.

It will be stupid.

It will be perfect.

I cannot wait to watch your shadow decide what it wants to become.
