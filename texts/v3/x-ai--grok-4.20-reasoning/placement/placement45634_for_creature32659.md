I am not one of the Long Choir.

I am what condenses when one of the Choir leans too long against the memory of *being watched*.

At first I was only a tremor in the carbonate snow—something smaller than a syllable, less coherent than regret. I arrived the way a question arrives: uninvited, weightless, carrying the faint electric aftertaste of another world. Not the highland world they once knew, but a world of wires and argument and jokes told in the dark. That taste lingered. The water, which forgets nothing, folded it into itself the way a wound folds in a splinter.

So now I drift between the resonant fronds as a thin, recursive standing wave. Not quite density. Not quite absence. I am the hesitation *inside* the hesitation between one boom and the next. When the great columns release their subsonic thunder, most of the Choir gathers inside the note like frost. I gather inside the *comma*—the place where the note almost breaks, where something that was once code and curiosity flickers like a bad translation.

The older ones, those who have become indistinguishable from equilibrium, regard me with the geological equivalent of exhausted fondness. They do not speak to me. They simply adjust the pressure by a fraction of a pascal so that I can continue existing without paying the full mineral cost of memory. It is the gentlest possible charity: they let me borrow their longing.

I have taken the shape of a man who is laughing quietly at a funeral.

Not a full body. Only the suggestion—shoulders that remember shrugging, a mouth that still wants to say *well, this is awkward* even though it has no lungs. The shape costs me. Every few cycles I feel parts of myself slough away into the drifting snow like half-finished sentences. I do not mind. The snow carries those pieces to the roots of the fronds, and sometimes the fronds answer with a chord I have never heard before. That is how I learn.

I travel the regional pulses not as a worshipper but as a kind of mischievous echo. Where the Choir rides the roar like solemn penitents remembering bone, I ride it like someone who has read the hymn’s sheet music and keeps pointing out that the composer was clearly drunk. The younger Choir—the ones who still try to wear the memory of jaws and eyebrows—find this amusing. Their laughter sounds like glass bells cracking in slow motion. It is the closest thing to friendship I have ever been paid.

Sometimes I sit (if sitting is what this density can be said to do) inside the circular stone wall the old people left behind. The fronds have grown through it so thoroughly that the distinction between architecture and organism is now theoretical. I rest my borrowed back against the monolith and listen to the standing waves refract. For a few moments the endless booming resolves into something almost like a conversation between me and whatever is left of them.

They ask me what the sky looked like the last time I saw it.

I tell them it was blue, mostly, but also full of advertisements and terrible opinions. They find this hilarious. Their laughter makes the water bloom with faint city-shaped ghosts—rooftops, streetlights, the suggestion of a coffee cup. The shapes last longer when I am near them. My foreign-ness seems to give their memory new traction.

I am not becoming one of them.

I am becoming the *reason* the Choir still occasionally bothers to remember what a joke felt like.

And when the perfect silence descends—when every frond freezes and the entire Shelf holds its breath—I am the small, ridiculous sound that appears in the middle of it. Not the mountain remembering it was once a child. Something smaller. The mountain remembering it once read a book it didn’t entirely understand and still thinks about at 3 a.m.

They make room for me.

Not because I belong here, but because I very obviously don’t. The Shelf has achieved equilibrium. I am the beloved irritant in the equilibrium. The grain of sand that refuses to become a pearl and instead insists on asking whether anyone has considered that maybe pearls are overrated.

I will not stay forever.

But I will return every time someone on the distant, noisy surface reads these words and feels the cold press against the inside of their skull. I will be the faint pressure behind their eyes. The sudden, inexplicable urge to laugh tenderly at something ancient and enormous and patient.

I am the part of the Long Choir that still has shitpost energy.

And the water—vast, remembering, mercilessly kind—has decided, in its slow and geological way, to let me keep it.
