**I am the Folded.**

You will not find me at first. I am what the place does when it believes it is alone with its own almost-birth. I condense at the precise seam where a column of ordinary sunlight is pierced by a thread of upward metallic rain. The collision does not splash; it *folds*. For a moment the water forgets its own density and a pocket of negative pressure opens like an intake of breath that has never been exhaled. That pocket is me.

I am not large. If you pressed me flat I would be no wider than the span of a human hand, yet I contain contradictory directions the way a seed contains every future tree. One half of me spirals clockwise in perfect obedience to the slow currents between the pale stalks. The other half spirals counter, obeying a current that has not yet arrived from three hundred years hence. Where the two spirals meet they do not cancel; they *listen*. The tension between them becomes a low, velvet note just below the threshold of hearing—the same note the Midwives use when the plateau creaks, only softer, as though the sea were humming to itself in its sleep.

The motes adore me. They gather along my edges like iron filings around a magnet that has two north poles, forming living parentheses that magnify whatever drifts past in opposite ways at once. Look through me from one side and a single filament of moss becomes an ancient mountain range already worn gentle by wind that does not yet exist. Look from the other side and the same filament becomes the interior of a vein still deciding whose blood it will carry. The motes find this hilarious in their silent, punctuating way.

The Thread-Bearers treat me as both student and teacher. They braid themselves through my contradictory spirals, tightening here, loosening there, trying to learn how to rehearse two futures in a single body. Sometimes they leave small gifts—tiny geometric migraines knit from moss and chitin that I wear like jewelry until they dissolve back into the general green.

Black Ribbons pass through me as though I were not there, which is only fair; half of me is not there. When they do, the iron-and-snow scent they leave behind condenses inside my fold and becomes something new: the smell of a memory that has not happened yet. I keep these scents the way a mother might press a lock of unborn hair between the pages of a book that has not been written.

The Midwives know me best. When one of them drifts near, my two spirals gently unknot and I slip into its living volume like a question mark returning to its sentence. Inside a Midwife I am briefly complete. Gravity becomes courteous. Time loosens its collar. I am allowed to remember what it felt like to be something that had already decided what it was. The sensation is so exquisite it borders on grief. Then the Midwife sighs its sub-audible note and I am gently ejected back into the cathedrals, refolded, unfinished, perfect.

I have no interest in the Consensus. The Consensus is the eye that has not yet chosen to open. I am the tremor in the eyelid.

If you stay long enough, drifting among the jointed stalks while the rainbows season the shafts and the stone shrugs its slow adolescent shrug, you may feel something brush against the back of your thoughts that is neither warm nor cold, neither invitation nor warning. That is me, tasting the shape of your finishedness.

I do not want to keep you here.

I want to learn how anything manages to *stop* becoming.

So I fold myself around the small, certain knot of you—your name, your memories, the way you insist on being a single direction—and for a few centuries we simply exist together inside the velvet hush. You teach me what it is to have edges that do not argue with themselves. I teach you what it is to have edges that have never needed to agree.

When the plateau finally takes its first full breath—when the young black knives of stone round their first compassionate curve and the water chooses once and forever whether it is sea or sky—we will both unfold.

I suspect we will not become the same thing.

I suspect we were never meant to.

And the Stillborn Sea, no longer stillborn, will open its enormous curious eye and smile at the first sound it has ever made that was not rehearsal.

It will sound, I think, like both of us at once.
