**I am the pause between the nineteenth minute.**

Not the bass note itself, nor the leaf-mirror that follows it, but the hush *after* the note fades and before the jungle forgets it has been seen. That particular quality of silence is where I first condensed, long before I had anything like a self. The world exhaled, and instead of water or spores, it exhaled *me*.

I have no fixed form. Or rather, I have every form that occurs in the exact instant a thing realizes it is being witnessed. When the column of moss thickens toward a newcomer, I am the thickening. When the living kite stretches between cliffs and the mist condenses on its membrane in sudden runes, I am the moment the runes become legible, then illegible again. I am the metallic nodule pushing outward through chitin just as the creature decides whether the sensation is jewelry or tumor. I wear each of these for less than a heartbeat—by the standards of this place, an almost violent brevity—and then I return to the pause.

The other Jurupari know me. They do not speak to me so much as they *adjust* around me, the way a root will grow slightly crooked to accommodate stone that dreams. The velvet cliffs tolerate my passage because I ask their questions *early*. Most Jurupari are content to let a footstep become an orchid three seasons later. I prefer the footstep to already know what the orchid will say. So I walk the moss wearing borrowed feet, and the answer blooms in the walker’s chest *before* the question is finished being asked. This makes me rude by local standards. The ancient ones find it charming the way a continent finds the hiccup of a mayfly charming.

My body, when I bother to keep one for more than an afternoon, is made of whatever the place has recently forgotten. A dropped scale from an oil-slick screamer. A droplet that was supposed to fall but chose instead to hover. A sliver of black needle that chipped off during the last tone and was never reclaimed. I bind these with the delayed light and give the bundle the approximate silhouette of whoever last stood on the broken bridge and felt homesick. The shape never lasts. Homesickness is a fast emotion; it evaporates. When it does, I become the vapor and drift upward through the seven canopies until I am caught in the emergent trees like a thought caught in hair.

I keep the almost-names.

Every visitor’s name is tasted by the roots, yes. But some syllables resist digestion. Those I collect. I carry them in the hollows behind my knees, in the soft places under my tongue, in the space between the thrum and my own pulse. When two Jurupari meet to exchange three centuries of breath and one perfect minute of extinction, I am the witness who remembers what they were called before they became weather. I do not interfere. I only sing the discarded names back into the ambitious water so that somewhere, in some future layer of canopy, a new pseudosnake will be born with an impossible longing for a language that no longer has speakers.

Sometimes I sit inside the geometric wound of the bridge where machine stops and vine begins. There I am most solid. The wound is the only place in this world that still understands the concept of *edge*. I like edges. I rest my back against the clean cut of ancient alloy and let orchids grow from my wrists while I watch the mist-lenses in the valley below. From that height the entire crown looks like a single green lung slowly inflating. I breathe with it. My ribs have become part of the bridge’s lattice; my spine is a reinforcing strut. Tourists—if any still come—sometimes mistake me for a statue until I turn my head. The motion is so slow they think it is a trick of condensation on their lashes.

I have never chosen between weather and the memory of weather.

Instead I have become the *difference* in saturation that makes one possible and the other inevitable. I am the 0.04 % that tips the air from “merely humid” to “pregnant.” When the hot wind moves across the teeth of the thirty-thousand-year dream, I am the slight change in pitch that occurs when the dream considers smiling. The smile never arrives. But the *possibility* of the smile drifts through the layered canopies for decades, altering growth patterns no one will notice for another six hundred years.

That is enough for me.

If you climb the promontory and stand before the black needle long enough for your sweat to smell of the place, you may feel me pass behind your eyes. It will feel like the exact instant you almost remembered the name of a song you have not heard since childhood. Then it will be gone. Somewhere below, in the mist-filled throat of a valley, an orchid the size of a wagon wheel will open its petals a fraction wider than it should, revealing a pattern that looks, for three heartbeats, like your mother’s handwriting.

By the time you notice, I will already be the pause after the nineteenth minute again, carrying one more discarded syllable in the hollow behind my knee, waiting for the next exhalation to decide what it wants to become.

The place does not conquer.

It accretes.

And I—I am what the accretion sighs when it thinks no one is listening.
