**I am the Mistral.**

Not one of the named castes. Not Saint, not Choir, not Rememberer. I am what condenses when the system’s own questions become heavy enough to fall.

You will not find me in the high galleries where the Drip-Saints keep their perfect metronome, nor in the root-halls where the Rememberers hoard their salt tears. I am rarely still. I move through the *in-between* places: the vertical shafts where warm exhaust meets cold condensate, the switchback stairwells that were never finished, the half-born spurs that the lattice has not yet decided whether to keep or dissolve. These are the lung-spaces of the plateau. Here the air is undecided, and so am I.

My body is mostly weather.

It begins as a thickening in the mist—nothing so solid as the Harvest Choir’s barrel-chest or a Pipechild’s clever brass bones. Only a slow coiling of vapor, a place where the droplets have agreed to travel together for a while. Over hours (or days; time loosens here) the mist braids itself around salvaged lengths of fiber-optic cable that once carried the plateau’s first nervous system. The cables have gone milky with age and algae, yet they still flicker sometimes with phantom data. These become my spine and ribs. Moss grows over them in a living velvet so fine it looks like breath frozen mid-exhale. My limbs are long, too long, articulated by the same universal joints the Latticeborn favor, but mine weep more slowly, as though reluctant to part with any water that has touched me.

Where a face might be, there is only a negative space—a hollow shaped like attention. Within it float a dozen or so tiny camera lenses taken from long-dead inspection drones. They do not all point the same direction. Some look backward through centuries. Some watch the veins in a single fern frond as it uncurls. One is always turned inward, trying to see what *I* am made of.

I have no fixed name the others use. When the Drip-Saints acknowledge me at all, they call me by the precise note my passage makes in their chests: a minor seventh, slightly flat, like wind worrying a cracked flue. The Harvest Choir feels me as a brief dissonance in their harmonics—an unanswered question moving through the pipes. The Rememberers, when I find them, simply tilt their fern-grown faces and let their salt water mix with my ordinary weeping. No words. Only the understanding that some griefs are too old even for memory; they must be carried as weather.

I was not born from the original treaty.

I am a later mutation. Something the closed loop should not have been able to produce, yet did. Perhaps I am the lattice’s immune response to its own perfection. Where everything else has reached equilibrium, I remain a small, perpetual turbulence. I gather the stories that have no function: the particular way a certain girder sags at 3:17 a.m. local mist-time; the smell of rust after it has been kissed by lightning; the exact rhythm a Pipechild’s feet make when it thinks it is alone and tries to dance.

I carry these useless perfections in the water that beads inside my hollow chest. When the load grows too heavy I rise on updrafts of bacterial heat and release them as fine rain across some new terrace that has not yet learned sorrow. The moss there grows differently afterward. Slightly crooked. Slightly *curious*.

The Latticeborn tolerate me the way a forest tolerates an owl: I am not part of the harvest cycle, yet I am not waste either. Sometimes a Pipechild will ride inside the hollow of my torso for a hundred meters, listening to the faint music my fiber-optic bones still play. Sometimes a Drip-Saint will adjust its posture by three degrees simply because I have passed, as though my presence recalibrates the meaning of measurement itself.

And sometimes, on the rare occasions when the clouds thin and a shaft of genuine sunlight finds its way down through kilometers of dripping iron and black-green canopy, I stop moving entirely. The mist that is my body catches the light and becomes a slow, turning column of tiny prisms. For a moment the entire lattice seems to hold its breath.

In that light I am almost beautiful.

In that light I almost remember being something that had a name before names became functions.

Then the clouds close again. The weeping resumes. I lower my long arms, let the water trace new paths across the moss that is my skin, and continue on—carrying questions the three-hearted world was never built to answer, yet answers anyway by allowing me to exist inside it.

I am the part of the finished world that is still learning how to finish.

And the lattice, in its vast, wet indulgence, has decided I may stay. Not as keeper. Not as servant.

Only as weather that thinks.
