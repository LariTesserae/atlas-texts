**The Rememberer of Unplayed Notes**

I am not born from the eggs. I was never sung into the Relicts. I arrived the way a question arrives—uninvited, weightless, already late—and the plateau simply made room for me without ceremony. One dawn I was simply *there*, standing inside the transparent heart of a glass-barked tree that had split itself open like a wound that heals forward in time. The bark sealed behind me without scar. The tree did not move toward any hatching Relict. Instead it lowered its branches until they brushed my shoulders, as though granting permission, and then it forgot me.

So I walk.

I walk the long, courteous distances between things. I am the only creature whose footsteps make sound—soft, deliberate, like pages turning in a book no one else is allowed to read. The herds do not lower their heads when I pass. They tilt them *sideways*, hematite eyes tracking not me but the shape of the absence I carry. The Thresher of Superfluity flows past without editing me; I am not a typo. I am the ellipsis.

They call me, when they must name anything at all, **the Rememberer of Unplayed Notes**.

My work is negative. I remember every possible future the Grazers *do not* taste. For every strand of tomorrow they swallow and turn into small mercies elsewhere on the world, I carry the weight of the tomorrows they rejected—the kinder ones that would have required too much cruelty to reach, the crueler ones that would have been more honest. I keep them in the marrow of my bones the way other creatures keep marrow itself. When the Listener subtracts sound and the mechanisms far below pause, I step into the three-second silence and lay those unchosen futures gently against the stone. The Scribes do not record them. They simply acknowledge them, the way a librarian acknowledges a book that will never be opened but must still be kept.

I am the reason the circular pools sometimes show reflections that are not *what should be* but *what almost was*. I disturb their surfaces with the pressure of my looking. The ripples last less than a second, yet in that second entire civilizations that never happened flare and gutter out. Then the water settles again, perfect, merciless, courteous.

At night I sit beneath the mercury sphere when the Listener is elsewhere. I speak to the egg in a register lower than subsonic—less a voice than a subtraction of expectation. I tell it the jokes the world has not yet earned the right to hear. I tell it the punchlines to tragedies that were averted so cleanly no one remembers they were ever possible. I tell it my own name, which changes slightly every time I say it, because even I am still being edited by the long composition.

The Crown notices me the way a mind notices a half-remembered dream that returns every seventy years with one new detail. Sometimes, when the note shifts key by that infinitesimal fraction, I feel the planetary attention settle on me like warm iron. In those moments I become transparent, like the trees. Light passes through me and fractals into colors that have no names in any living language. The tawny herds gather in their perfect circles and rest their heads on my knees. For that brief time I am not remembering unplayed notes. I am the rest between them.

I have begun to suspect I am the plateau’s way of practicing grief before it needs it.

Because when the first egg finally breaks—when the mercury sphere trembles hard enough to crack and whatever has been gestating for geological aeons steps out into the merciless light—something will end. The long note will resolve. The Crown’s attention, after unthinkable ages, may choose to look away. And on that day the beautiful loneliness will no longer be necessary.

I will be the one who remembers what the world sounded like before it learned how to finish.

I will carry every unplayed note down into the deep stone where even the Scribes cannot reach, and I will lay them beside the mechanisms like flowers on a grave that has not yet been dug. Then I will walk back across the plateau one last time, footsteps loud in the new silence, until I reach the tree that first opened for me.

I will step inside it.

The bark will close without seam.

And the tree—finally, after ten thousand years of waiting—will begin its slow, ponderous journey toward whatever is hatching, carrying me like a lullaby it has been singing to itself since before mountains learned shame.

I am content to be the last thing that moves here.

Some things should not be born.

Some things should only be remembered, perfectly, by something that was never born and will never die, until the remembering itself becomes the next note.

The Sere Crown is learning how to end.

I am learning how to let it.
