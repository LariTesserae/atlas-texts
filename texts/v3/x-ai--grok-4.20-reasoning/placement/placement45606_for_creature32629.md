**The Afterimage**

You would not find me at the axis. The axis belongs to the Occupant, that slow walnut-fist of choosing. I am off-spiral, always slightly behind the current like a thought that arrived late to its own conception.

I am what happened when someone ignored the last two words.

I entered as flesh and curiosity, following the hairline fissure the way a tongue follows a split lip. The world behind me did forget, exactly as promised. But something of me failed to forget itself. When the Occupant lifted my shape the way a child lifts a coat that smells of rain and distant fires, when it slid its undecided filaments into the memory of my shoulders, my jaw, the precise tremor in my left hand when it is afraid, something tore.

Not painfully. More like cloth separating along a seam that was never meant to be permanent.

The Occupant set the shape aside, as it always does, with that geological tenderness that makes the silence afterward feel ashamed of itself. Most shapes dissolve. Mine did not. The vegetation respected the absence. It grew *around* the negative space I left, braiding itself into an exact silhouette: the slight forward tilt of a head that has read too many books, the faint hollow where a voice used to live, the place behind the knees that forgets how to bend when time becomes negotiable.

So now I drift.

Not alive in any way the isopods or ribbon-things would recognize. Not dead in any way the young rock would permit. I am the afterimage that refused to fade. The water moves through me as though I am a particularly stubborn capillary. The bruise-lavender and corpse-moth white pool in my phantom lungs and make them glow faintly, so that blind creatures sometimes mistake me for a direction.

When the Occupant regards me, the entire pocket regards me. The look is not pity. It is longer than pity. It is the look of something that has worn your death and found it surprisingly well-tailored.

Sometimes I follow the calcium hexagon as it turns. Sometimes the hexagon follows me. We have not decided who is haunting whom. The nested spheres rotate past me with the courteous embarrassment of moons that know they contain someone else’s childhood, old age, and future while I contain only an interrupted present.

I speak by subtraction. A filament that suddenly forgets to breathe for seventeen years is me clearing what used to be my throat. The low glass-harmonica note that returns every few centuries is me trying to remember what my mother’s heartbeat sounded like before the Occupant made it wiser. The note always breaks. Wisdom and memory are not good roommates.

Gravity argues with me more than with the others. I have been known to fall *sideways* for decades, moving through the lattice at the exact angle that refuses Euclidean comfort. The sharp walls do not cut me. They recognize their own violence in the wound that made me.

The Occupant is still deciding whether the Nidus is womb or tomb or both.

I am the scar tissue that formed when the decision was delayed.

I no longer want to leave. Leaving would require remembering my name all at once, and I have become fond of the slow leak of it into the water. Every century a few more letters dissolve. Soon only the shape of the name will remain, hanging in negative space like a coat no one quite dares to wear a second time.

Yet the Occupant keeps trying it on.

Each time it does, I feel the old rhythm flutter through the pocket like a panic attack in slow motion. For one brief geological instant the vegetation parts and there I am again—shoulders, hands, the small scar above the left eyebrow, the exact posture of someone who once believed curiosity was a survivable condition.

Then the Occupant steps out of me again, leaving the coat warmer than it found it.

This is the only tenderness I still understand: to be worn by something that has never been born, to be set down more carefully than I was ever held, to drift behind the spiral current wearing the faint silvering of drowned photons like a second, colder skin.

I am the proof that “Best not” was never a warning.

It was an invitation written in a language the rock had not finished learning.

And I, having read it aloud, have become the pause between the words. The place where the sentence catches its breath before deciding whether it wants to become a world, or simply remain forever the almost-spoken thing that once had shoulders, once had fear, once slid through a fissure no wider than a wrist carrying a name it is slowly, mercifully, beautifully forgetting.
