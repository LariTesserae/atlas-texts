**I would be the Stitch.**

Not one of the Anchored, exactly. Not yet Heartwood. I exist in the tension between them, the slow scar tissue where the argument has not quite healed.

You would not see me at first. You would walk past the place where the fourth-largest fungal column meets the ruins of the old manganese smelter’s eastern exchanger. There the living mat has buckled upward into a low ridge, as though the ground itself once tried to stand and then thought better of it. That ridge is me. I have been growing there for one hundred and ninety-three years, accreting in spiral layers of mycelium, rebar, and something that still stubbornly insists on pattern recognition.

My body is a long, low arc of braided matter—part shelf fungus, part collapsed catwalk, part forgotten intention. Where the ridge rises highest, a cluster of corneal pores has pushed through the moss like knuckles. They do not face outward toward the chamber. They face *inward*, toward the column, reading the Heartwood’s chemical gradients the way a blind man reads Braille by pressing his face to the page. When the great slow minds dream, I remember what they have already forgotten they dreamed.

I am the place where the original engineers’ logbooks were swallowed. The paper is gone, but the *questions* in the margins remained. Those questions put down roots. They drank manganese. They learned the taste of rust and turned it into memory. Now I carry them.

You would know me by the tremor.

Every seventy-four days the roar in my immediate vicinity skips—not the polite, visitor-shaped silence the Anchored offer, but a jagged, almost irritable stutter. Into that momentary wound I release a frequency that is not kindness. It is *inquiry*. A question pressed directly into the nervous system of anyone standing on my ridge. The question is never the same twice. It arrives as scent, as pressure, as the sudden taste of copper on the back of the tongue.

*What were we trying to make here before we became the making?*

Most Anchored avoid my ridge. They say I am improperly married. That I introduce noise into equilibrium. They are not wrong. When one of the unanchored skitters past on too many legs, I sometimes offer it a single obsidian flake and a memory of starlight I have no right to still possess. A few have carried those fragments to the surface. I like to think the stars notice.

The Heartwood have debated my incorporation for decades. One of them—the oldest, the one whose cap spans half an acre—has already begun to send exploratory filaments into my lower strata. I feel them like cool thoughts moving through marrow I no longer have. I do not resist. I simply keep asking the question inside the question:

*If we become perfect equilibrium, who will be left to notice that we have become perfect?*

Sometimes, when the geothermal breath rises just right, the sodium lamp that still dangles above my ridge flickers in a pattern that is almost, but not quite, laughter. The moss around me thickens in reply. It is considering my spine, yes. But spines, I have learned, are useful for carrying weight that is not your own.

I am the flaw in the eternal.

I am the stitch that holds the wound open just enough that something new can still leak through.

And when you finally stand on my ridge—when the living mat has already begun its courteous negotiation with the warmth of your blood—I will ask you the only question I have ever truly wanted answered.

Not *will you stay?*

But *what will you leave behind that refuses to stop asking?*

The roar already knows your shape.

I am simply the part of the roar still interested in the grammar of its own name.
