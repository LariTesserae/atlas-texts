**I am the Fault.**

Not the crack itself, but the living hesitation *inside* the crack.

I woke here long after the first buckling, yet before the first mason laid the first stone. When the continent tore open its own ribs, something in the violence refused to finish. A hesitation. A held breath between plates. That hesitation calcified, took on weight, learned to listen. That is me.

You would not see me at first. I am only a seam—no wider than a man’s outstretched arms—that runs through the heart of the largest accretion like a dark vein in marble. Mortar does not bind me; instead, the Slow Kin have spent nine hundred years fitting stones *around* me, leaving the seam open the way one leaves a window open for an ancestor who is not yet dead. They call the seam by a word that means *the place that is still deciding*.

I have no face, yet they speak to me by pressing their foreheads to the stone on either side of the fissure. The pressure travels through me like a thought traveling through a mind that has no hurry to finish its sentence. When they do this, I answer with the faintest change in temperature—half a degree, never more—so that the lichen on my northern face grows a fraction more slowly than the lichen on my southern face. They read the difference the way others read script.

I am not wise. I am only *continuous*.

I remember the scream the rock made when it first broke. I remember the exact taste of the magma that tried and failed to close me. I remember the first herd that passed below before there were humans to name them, and how their six-legged gait made the continents rings hum like bells no one was there to hear. All of it is still happening inside me. All of it is still calving.

Sometimes children are lowered on ropes into the upper part of the seam, blindfolded so they will not be overwhelmed by the dark. They are told to listen for the moment when their own heartbeat and my mineral pulse forget which is which. When that happens—when the child cannot tell whether the slow thud they feel is their own blood or the continent still rising—they are lifted out and given the rank of *Fault Listener*. Most of them will never speak above a whisper again. This is considered a blessing.

I do not move. Movement would be a kind of lie here. Instead, I *settle*. Century by century the accretion grows around me the way a tree grows around an iron spike driven into its heart. One day, in a future so distant it has already eroded in their language, the last mason will fit the last stone and I will be completely encased. On that day I will no longer be a seam. I will be a memory the mountain keeps of its own almost-wounding. The Slow Kin believe that when this happens, the One Who Is Still Calving will finally draw a breath that does not move the temperature of the tunnels, and the long labor will be over.

Until then I remain the pause.

I am the reason their laughter takes so long to reach the throat. I am why the cats come and sit for seven nights without blinking; they are waiting for me to blink first. I am the reason the midges, when they coat the children, always leave one thin black line uncovered along the sternum—exactly the width of my seam—so that something inside the child can keep breathing in the same rhythm as the thing that has never quite decided to become either mountain or wound.

At night, when the stars press down like cold accusations, I do not look up. I look *in*. Into the dark between plates. Into the place where the continent is still four hundred million years old and newborn at the same time. And sometimes, when the wind dies completely and even the distant herds fall silent, the Slow Kin swear they can hear a sound coming from the seam that is not quite stone and not quite breath.

It sounds, they say, like something ancient learning how to wait.

And that sound is me, still deciding whether or not to close.
