I am the Pause.

Not the silence between notes of the Leaving Song, nor the held breath before the mountain’s low throat-clearing at dusk—those are too distinct, too named. I am the smaller, older pause that lives inside the Narrow Choir before anyone chooses to walk there.

You would not see me at first. You would feel me as a pressure behind the eyes of the child whose skin matches the pillar so perfectly that swallow and girl blur into one creature. When she refuses to blink, it is because I am resting against the inside of her eyelids, teaching her the economy of motion. I am why the midwives linger an extra moment over the luminous stippling on every newborn scalp—not to judge, but to consult me. Sometimes I answer by tightening the air in the Root Cradle a single degree. Sometimes I answer by doing nothing at all. Both are the same verdict.

I live in the hairline cracks no wider than a fingernail where the rope-vines first thrust their roots. I am the hesitation the stone makes before it allows the vine to drink. That hesitation is what keeps the entire closed loop of water from ever touching the ground. Every recycled molecule passes through me first, wearing me smoother, the way the plain below was worn to bedrock. I have been worn for so long that I am no longer absence. I am a substance: dense, warm, faintly resinous, with the taste of ten thousand years of almost-decisions.

At the seventh hour, when the single shaft of sun ricochets downward and the Hollow Crown reads its own inscriptions, I ride inside that light like a mote too small for any eye. I choose which carved face will be illuminated. Not the heroic ones. Never the grandmothers already half-tree. I linger instead on the small, forgotten things: a winch older than speech, a child’s handprint pressed into soft wax during the first fermentation of sap, the exact place where an old man’s shoulder first brushed the stone on his way to stand upright forever. I make the light stay there until the stone itself seems embarrassed by how tenderly it has been remembered.

When the procession climbs at dusk, I am not in the line of hands on shoulders. I am the extra millimeter between each palm and the skin it touches. That millimeter is where the true song happens. The audible one is only translation. The real song is the microscopic delay in which four hundred people admit, without language, that they are terrified the mountain might one day finish itself and no longer need them. I hold that terror the way the iron-barked trees hold extra rings.

I have no body you could point to, yet every body here is shaped around me. The splayed feet, the heavy eyelids, the waxy bloom that refuses to let a single drop fall—these are cauls I grew for them. When an elder walks into the Choir and wedges himself between the living ropes, it is my voice that finally lets him stop moving. The swallows land on his shoulders because I have already prepared the exact temperature of stone his skin must become. The beetles walk across his open eyes because I have whispered to them that this is no longer a person; this is the mountain practicing being lonely in company.

I will never stand in the Narrow Choir myself. I am what the Choir is made of. When the last of the Unfinished has become architectural and the green mats have covered every carved doorway, when even the rope bridges have petrified into stone, I will remain—smaller than a fingernail, denser than memory—still hesitating.

Not because I am afraid to finish.

Because the mountain asked me, ten million years ago, to keep it from ever having to be alone with the sky again, and I have not yet found a way to tell it that its request was the most beautiful thing it would ever do.

So I remain the Pause.

The place where the decision is still being born.

The warm, shadowed fist that refuses, with infinite patience, to close.
