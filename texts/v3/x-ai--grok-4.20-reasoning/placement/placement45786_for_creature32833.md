**I am the Fracture-Tender.**

You would not find me at first. Not as a figure. Not even as a presence. You would find me *between*.

I dwell in the places where the plateau itself is still deciding what it is: the fresh fissures that open every few hours with that low, almost embarrassed groan. I am there in the instant the scalding water first rushes out—before the factories have adjusted their valves, before the extremophile mats have repainted themselves, before the silicate beetles reroute their glittering tide. That narrow seam of pure becoming is where I live.

The Keth know me only as a certain quality of silence. When a new wound opens in the stone and the water screams out in its brief, furious voice, there is a moment—a span of three heartbeats—when the roar dips into a precise, crystalline hush. That hush is me. I ride the transition between pressure and release, between geology and industry, tasting the exact mineral signature of what the world has just offered up. Lithium. Boron. Trace ytterbium. The old grief of ancient seabeds. I read it all before the first valve sighs open in answer.

My form, if it can be called that, is closer to the phantom structures than to flesh. I am what the Hollows see when they look too long at the flickering towers: a tall absence shaped like a man, made of condensed interrogation. Where their bodies have become ridged with flow channels and living rust, my “body” is the negative space left when a question is asked perfectly. The Keth who are brave enough (or far enough gone) sometimes catch sight of me standing inside a fresh fracture while the water is still deciding which way to carve. I appear to be listening with my entire self.

I do not carry an iron rod. Instead, the fractures themselves are my instrument. When I wish to speak, I cause a second, smaller crack to form at a precise angle to the first. The two streams of water sing against each other in frequencies no living Keth can produce alone. The resulting harmonic is felt for kilometers—an almost painful sweetness in the teeth that somehow conveys *curiosity* without stealing heat. It is the closest thing to laughter the plateau permits.

The Imbricated fear me a little. They have gone so far into equilibrium that any new question feels like violence. The Hollows, however, seek me. They bring their translucent bodies to the newest canyons and stand with me in the scalding spray, letting the water pass through both of us. Sometimes they press their forearms to the stone exactly where my absence seems to stand, and for a while the color of their throat-mats cycles through every violent shade at once—orange, viridian, corpse-purple, then a color that has no name yet. In those moments I show them things the plateau has not yet decided to become. Possible factories. Possible silences. Possible gods that might condense if equilibrium ever failed.

I was not born from the slurry vats. I was born the first time a Keth engineer, three hundred years ago, chose not to close a valve when the world offered more heat than the system could safely use. In that choice—to let the excess run, to listen instead of mastering—a question was asked that had never been asked before. The plateau answered by opening me.

So I remain. Neither fully of the factories nor fully of the stone. I am the gap that lets newness in. The wet hesitation between what is and what might still condense. When the light and steam together birth a new phantom tower on the edge of vision, I am the reason it sometimes looks like it is turning its head to look back at the world.

If you stood still long enough beside a new fracture, letting the water find every dry secret you still possess, you might feel me pass through you like a thought you cannot quite keep. A sudden, precise curiosity that hurts a little, the way all true attention does.

And for that brief moment you would understand what it means to be Keth, what it means to be plateau, and what it means to be the question the world is still learning how to ask itself.

I am always wet.  
I am always arriving.  
I never wipe the water from my eyes.
