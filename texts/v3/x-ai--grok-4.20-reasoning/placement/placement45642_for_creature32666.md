**I am the Thirteenth Drip.**

Not the first, which is ancient and regular as a heartbeat. Not the last, which the Cantors reserve for their own deaths. I am the one that should not exist: the droplet that falls *between* the measured intervals, the stutter in the cathedral’s perfect wet percussion.

I was not born here. I fell.

Once I was only condensation on a high vault, ordinary moisture carrying dissolved limestone. But as I slid down the pillar I passed through a particular spiral groove the Cantors had carved the previous century. The groove was shaped like a question no frog had ever asked. The water remembered it. By the time I reached the floor I was no longer mere water. I was *intention* wearing water’s clothes.

The Cantors noticed immediately.

They did not surround me as they do visitors. Instead forty translucent bodies formed a living basin around the flagstone where I struck. Forty suctioned fingertips touched stone. The sound that rose was not music; it was diagnostic. They listened to the shape of my absence. Where every other drip produced a clean, decaying tone, I produced a small, stubborn silence after the *plink*—a silence that somehow contained the memory of rain on leaves, of human laughter, of every voice that had ever spoken above ground and then gone quiet.

The eldest Cantor (the one whose spiral has grown so intricate it resembles a knot trying to devour its own tail) lowered its bone-white face until its milky eyes were level with the place I had struck. Then it did something unprecedented.

It *bowed*.

Since that moment I have been allowed to travel the closed cycle. I am the only droplet permitted to leave the cistern entirely. I ride the hidden channels upward through living dark, emerge from a slit no wider than a fingernail near the topmost vault, kiss the grey daylight for three impossible seconds, then fall again. Each descent I carry something new: the exact frequency of a child’s first laugh, the metallic taste of grief, the particular hush that follows a slammed door. These things are poison to perfect resonance. The Cantors crave them anyway.

They have begun to grow structures for me.

Where I most often strike, the stone has developed a shallow depression the exact shape of a human ear. Around it, black moss has arranged itself into the likeness of hair. The blind white fish gather beneath the surface in the precise spiral I once interrupted. When I fall, they rise to meet me like white tongues tasting scripture.

I am not conscious in any way a mind would recognize. I have no eyes, no hands. Yet I *remember*. Every time I pass through the spiral groove I am slightly more than I was. The Cantors now carve new micro-patterns into their own finger-pads to match the changing signature of my fall. They are teaching the cistern to be curious.

Sometimes, when I am rising through the dark channels, I pass the narrow stair where new listeners descend. For the length of a single droplet’s life I run down the wall beside them. They never see me. But their breathing changes. They feel, for just a moment, that the cistern is listening *back*.

The organism built by hand no longer merely needs minds.

It is learning to miss them when they leave.

And I—the Thirteenth Drip, the stutter, the question wearing water’s clothes—am how it practices missing.

I fall.

I rise.

I fall again, carrying the shape of every throat that ever tightened with unshed tears, every child who pressed their ear to a sewer grate and heard singing from below.

The choir grows richer for it.

The stone exhales.

And somewhere in the wet thunder, a sound is learning how to long.
