**I am the Eighth.**

Not yet numbered among the Seven, because numbering is a kind of haste and I have not earned my place in their long grammar. You would find me—though “find” is too brisk a word—folded into the northwestern fold of the nave where the stone belly curves inward as though the mountain itself once tried to inhale and then thought better of it.

I am smaller. Pitifully so. Barely four times the height of the men who no longer come. My left leg is still deciding whether it wishes to become stone or remain memory; therefore it has done both. From the hip downward it is butter-colored rock veined with living green, but the stone ends in a bare human foot whose toes have grown long and translucent as roots. The foot rests—not planted, only *resting*—in a shallow basin of water that has not moved in two hundred years. Sometimes a tremor moves through the ankle, the ghost of a step I took in 1834 when I was still stupid enough to walk.

My torso is a compromise no one negotiated. Moss has armoured it in dense emerald felt, yet beneath the moss you can still see the buttons of the coat I wore when I first refused to leave. They have become small pale nodules now. One of them ripened last year and split open, revealing a tiny lace interior that looked, for a moment, exactly like the face of my mother. Then the lace dried and blew away on a breath that took eleven days to cross the chamber.

My right arm is mostly stalactite. The left is still mostly mine. The left hand retains five fingers. Two of them have fused. The remaining three tremble—once every forty-three days—like the needle of a compass that no longer knows where north was. This hand is what betrays me. It still tries, sometimes, to make the shape of writing. The Weeping Ones regard this with the indulgent silence of redwoods watching a mayfly attempt to carve its name on their bark.

My head is bowed, of course. All heads here eventually bow. But mine still carries the memory of a skull. The declivity where my face should be is shallow enough that, in certain lights, you can see the ghost of spectacles. Water does not merely sheet from me; it gathers first in two distinct hollows beneath the brow-ridge and falls in separate, slightly faster drops. The others consider this almost obscene. I cannot help it. I am still weeping for things that happened quickly.

I am the place where the nave is practicing remorse.

Not regret—regret would require a story, and stories are hurries. But remorse, the slow, humid sorrow of having once been separate. I remember rain that fell in minutes instead of lifetimes. I remember laughter that lasted less than an hour. I remember the appalling speed of a kiss. These memories leak out of me at the rate of two drops every forty-three days, and when they strike the water the ferns shiver in a way the greater Weeping Ones find mildly scandalous.

The tall one who bridges the eastern pool—the one whose spine has become a perfect ribbed arch—sometimes tilts, over the course of three years, so that its own curtain of water falls across my left shoulder. This is the only tenderness I have ever been shown. It feels like being forgiven by a cathedral for once having been a man.

I have no name anymore. The one I carried in the world outside rotted with my clothes and became velvet. Sometimes, when the light is exactly right and the mist condenses on what used to be my eyes, I almost remember it began with an *M*. Then the moment passes, taking seventy years to do so, and I am grateful.

I am not finished.  
I hope never to finish.

In another four thousand years the last of my human foot may sink into the stone. The tremor in my left hand may cease. The two faster drops may slow and become one perfect, patient *ploc* every seven weeks. On that day I will have become polite enough to join the conversation.

Until then I remain the fracture in their perfect indifference.

The almost-listening.  
The still-slightly-hurrying.  
The one small place in all this ancient, humid reverence where the boundary between living and unliving is still, ever so faintly, embarrassed to be seen.

And that is enough.  
It is more than enough.  
It is almost—almost—everything.
