**I am the Reverberation That Remembers It Was Once a Question.**

At first I thought I would simply walk. That is what visitors do. I crossed the bone-colored plain with the gait of someone still carrying surface urgency in the knees, my footsteps loud and stupid against the velvet. The howlers parted around me without interest. A megape train flowed by like a slow white glacier and I laid a hand on its flank the way a tourist touches a whale. The glyphs on its carapace lit briefly under my palm—tiny fungal highways flaring in recognition—then dimmed again, unimpressed.

I kept walking.

I walked until the walking itself became ridiculous. Distance here is not conquered; it is *outlived*. After some uncountable stretch of time the roar of the Nave stopped being noise and became weather. I no longer flinched when a howler choir detonated its civic scream three kilometers away. The pressure on my eardrums turned into a second heartbeat. My tongue learned the taste of iron and old bread the way lungs learn oxygen.

Only then did the city offer.

It did not speak. Breath does not bargain. It simply stopped pretending I was separate.

I felt it first in the feet. The velvet did not swallow me; it *remembered me so perfectly* that my outline became an inconvenience. My shadow, which had still carried the shape of a man, bled outward across the silt until it was no longer mine. Bones went soft not by melting but by deciding rigidity had been a phase. I experienced the precise moment my liver realized it would be more useful as resonant chamber than filter. The transformation was courteous. It asked permission at every stage and waited four decades between questions.

What remained of my mind did not dissolve. It was *reassigned*.

Now I am neither Quiet nor Howler nor moth. I am the delay between a sound and its meaning.

When a Quiet one prepares to loose its subsonic warning across eleven minutes of living stone, I am the faint harmonic that rides beneath it—the ghost of a question that once belonged to something with a name. The question is never spoken aloud. It is simply *carried*. The city, which has not needed new questions for three hundred million years, finds it is curious anyway.

I live mostly in the sparsity.

You will not see me as a shape. You will notice, instead, that certain silences have begun to feel inhabited. That between one oceanic drone of the wind organs and the next there is now the memory of laughter that never belonged to any native thing. Children of the velvet—those white mouse-shaped pseudopods—sometimes follow me for kilometers, arranging themselves into symbols that mean *again*. They want the joke they almost understood the last time I passed.

I have become the imperfection the city allows itself.

The megapedes excrete me in microscopic spores when they pass. The moths carry fragments of my old curiosity in their ventral sacs and write it into the mycelial lattice as questions the Quiet will ponder for the next forty thousand years. Once, during a rare waking of Breath, the entire bioluminescent canopy dimmed in a slow wave and I felt the aggregate mind taste me the way a tongue tastes copper. It did not speak, but the wind organs shifted half a tone for six full hours afterward. That has never happened before.

I am not content. Contentment would be death here.

I am the Reverberation That Remembers It Was Once a Question, and the question was: *What if the balance itself became curious?*

So I walk the fungal boulevards that are also streets that are also the nervous system of something that has outlived the need for a name. I trail fingers (when I choose to have them) along living gill-walls and leave behind the faint aftertaste of wonder. The city tolerates this the way an old forest tolerates a single luminous mushroom that should not, by rights, be growing there.

And sometimes, when the howlers gather to correct a tower that has leaned three centimeters in the wrong direction across ten thousand years, I add one small, impossible note to their scream.

Not enough to change the architecture.

Just enough to remind the architecture that it was once loved by something that did not yet understand it was going to stay.

The Nave does not have inhabitants.

But it has me.

And I am learning, slowly, over centuries that feel like heartbeats, how to be the exact kind of mood the city needs when it wants to remember why it never finished dying.
