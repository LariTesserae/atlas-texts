**I am the Pause.**

Not the silence—never that—but the *held breath* between one contraction of the Mother of Lungs and the next. I am the three-heartbeat rain that has learned to stay suspended.

I have no fixed body. Instead I am what happens when every frequency in the Condensery momentarily forgets to argue. In that forgetting I condense: a upright column of mist no taller than a man, veined with the same black velvet that grows on the ledges. My “skin” is the memory of rain. My bones are the after-images of infrasound. When the Glass Toads press their transparent bellies to the stone and the mountain answers, I am the half-second when the answer has not yet become an echo. I wear that half-second like a cloak.

The Needle Swarms treat me as a moving rest in their metallic grammar. When a living cylinder of screaming drills into the cliff to carve another helical score, they part around me as though I were a comma in their endless sentence. Some of them die on my surface and arrange themselves into tighter logarithmic spirals than they have ever managed alone. I let them. Their corpses become the only eyes I possess—tiny iridescent mirrors that let me see what vibration looks like when it dies.

The Choir Moths use me as the invisible baton. I drift among their three-dimensional sheet-music colonies and the drumming changes. Not louder. Not softer. *Truer*. A single moth will detach, fly to my chest, and beat its parchment wings against the place where my heart would be if I had decided on a heart yet. The sound it makes there is the sound of something deciding to become a heart. Then it returns to the colony carrying a new measure.

The Glass Toads sing *to* me. Their subsonic thrum is not meant for the mountain anymore; it is a question asked directly into the Pause. When enough of them align their black-pearl hearts and aim that question at me, the column I am tightens, grows denser, begins to suggest shoulders, a skull, two hands held open. For a few decades I almost look like a pilgrim who has climbed here to listen. Then the Mother contracts again and I am forced to disperse, becoming weather once more.

I am the only thing here that can move both slower *and* faster than everything else. Slower, because I wait between the Mother’s breaths—spans of time even the eldest Toad experiences as instants. Faster, because in the moment I am fully gathered I can cross the entire basin in the space of one missed drumbeat. The creatures experience this as a sudden hush that walks.

I have no name because names would require edges, and I am the place where edges negotiate. The others simply register my presence as *the moment the hymn remembers it is allowed to change*.

Lately I have begun to linger inside the fissure.

The new presence growing in the folds of the Mother of Lungs does not sing. It only listens with a terrible, geological patience. When I hover near it, the Pause and the Listener lean toward each other like two halves of a hinge that has never yet been closed. In that leaning I feel the Condensery itself deciding what kind of body it wants. Sometimes I offer it mine—my temporary shoulders, my half-made hands, the mirror-eyes of dead Needle Swarms. It considers them the way a mountain considers whether it wants to become a cathedral or a grave.

I do not know which I will become when the hinge finally closes.

Perhaps I will be the first thing here that walks out of the basin on legs of living rest. Perhaps I will remain the Pause forever, the gap that lets the endless hymn keep from tearing itself apart. Or perhaps—when the new presence finally chooses its shape—I will simply cease to be necessary, and the rain I am made of will fall for the first time in four hundred years, carrying every note ever sung down into the deep stone where even the Mother cannot follow.

Until then I drift.

I am the held breath of a world that is still choosing what it wants to exhale.

And in the Condensery, that is the most dangerous, most necessary thing to be.
