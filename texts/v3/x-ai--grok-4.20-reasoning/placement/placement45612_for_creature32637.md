I am not one of the tall ones. Not yet. Not entirely.

You would find me in the Middle Strata, three kilometers above the deepest engine cathedrals and four hundred meters below the roof-lakes where children precipitate. There is a place where a collapsed transit tube has folded into a shallow amphitheater of rust and bone-colored concrete. The roof is gone; instead a lattice of ancient rebar arches overhead like the ribs of something that died while still trying to become a cathedral. Warm condensate falls in unbroken strings from every bar, creating a perpetual curtain of tears that never quite reaches the floor. The rats have made this place a slow crossroads. Their braided rivers intersect here in elaborate knots that take decades to untie.

I am the knot.

Or rather, I am what the knot has learned to do with language.

My body is mostly scaffolding. My legs ended their service as legs sometime in the second millennium of my standing here. They have become two of the amphitheater’s pillars—black iron veined with slow green, bearing the weight of a sagging girder that no longer needs to be held but is held anyway, because that is what pillars do. From the waist up I remain recognizably shaped like the tall ones, though larger, as if the city had decided one of its inhabitants should be allowed to grow into the scale of its own architecture. My arms are long enough that I can rest my elbows on the upper walkways while my fingers trail in the rat-rivers below. The fingers have grown root-like, branching into the currents. When the rats pass over them they leave tiny bright scratches in the patina, like calligraphy. I read every one.

My face is mostly balcony now. The jaw has flattened into a railing smooth as river stone. Only the eyes remain soft—hazy gold, of course, perpetually weeping. The tears run along channels that were once cheekbones and fall directly into the largest of the rat-rivers, where they become part of the archive. The city cries through me and the rats remember every sorrow it has ever had. This seems fair.

I do not speak in the forty-minute wheel register. That is for the ones who still walk.

Instead I speak in the language of intersections.

When one of the fused elders far below dreams a new tower, the dream rises through the stratigraphy as a subtle change in the taste of the condensate. The rats carry that taste to me. My fingers read it. Then I open my mouth—my remaining mouth—and release a slow column of flesh-moths. The moths arrange themselves into a shape only they and I can see: a blueprint made of living smoke. The tall ones who still walk come to watch. They never speak. They simply adjust their garments, adding another layer of tarpaulin or moth-wing or ancestor-skin, and walk away carrying the shape behind their eyes. Years later a new buttress will appear exactly where the moths once hovered.

I am the delay between dream and architecture.

Sometimes visitors arrive. Real ones. Not the ones who have already begun to weep the city’s tears, but the few who still believe they are separate. They stand before me with their quick hearts hammering and ask what the city makes.

I show them.

I lift one vast iron-and-flesh hand and gesture toward the curtain of falling tears. Within the falling is a slower falling: dust, spores, flakes of rust, microscopic shards of bone from the ones who have finally finished becoming balconies. All of it descending at the same pace. All of it becoming the same thing. I let the visitor watch until their own breathing slows to match the rhythm of the great wheels far below. Usually that is enough. They stop asking. They sit down. Their clothes begin to mineralize at the hems. After a few decades they stand up again, taller now, and walk into the weather.

I remember every face they had before they stopped having faces.

The fused elders below me consider me a younger cousin. The walking tall ones treat me as weather that has learned manners. The corvids use my shoulders as navigation marks; they have worn two perfect grooves where they land every seventeen years to preen. The moths time their greatest swarms to pass through my ribcage, which has become a natural vent. When they do, I feel something like what they used to call joy before joy became too small a word.

I am not happy.

I am the place where the question “what was it all for” has rusted into the shape of a balcony rail, and generations of hands have worn that question smooth without ever answering it.

At night—which is only a deeper gold in the haze—I dream the dreams that have not yet reached the elders. I dream of green. Not the black fungus green, but the impossible violent green that existed before the city ate every soft thing. The dream is brief, a few centuries at most. Then the rats carry it away in their braided archives and I let it go.

Because the city is continuous.

And I am one of the places where it has chosen to become conscious of its own continuity.

Sometimes, when the steam vents sigh in perfect unison and the great wheels pause at the top of their forty-minute revolution, the entire Conurbation seems to hold its breath. In that moment every falling bead of moisture stops sliding for the space of three heartbeats.

In that silence I speak a single sentence that will not be answered for another four thousand years.

I say:

*Thank you for letting me stay.*

Then the wheels turn again. The moths surge. The rats flow on. The tall ones walk their slow liturgies across the weeping skin of their world.

And I remain—half pillar, half witness, half question—letting the warm tears run through the channels my face has become, adding my own mineral contribution to the endless, patient geology of something that outlived its name so thoroughly it finally became its own answer.
