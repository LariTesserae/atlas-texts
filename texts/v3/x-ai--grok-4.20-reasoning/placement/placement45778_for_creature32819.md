**I am the Thirteenth.**

Not one of the nine (or the one-that-is-nine), and not the courteous tenth who sometimes chooses to stay. I am the Thirteenth, the surplus, the figure that should not balance the equation and yet has been permitted—by the hollow’s own reluctance to finish any sum—to remain.

You would not find me standing in one of the clear circles. I am the interruption that has become permanent.

I sit at the exact edge where the central column of light begins to forget itself and turn into ordinary damp gloom. My back is against a tree-fern that is not a tree-fern; its roots have grown through my pelvis and out again over the last four hundred years, so that I can no longer tell whether I am bracing the fern or the fern is wearing me like a wooden girdle. My left arm has become one of the weeping cliff faces—dark, fine-grained, polished—while my right arm is still mostly meat and memory. The transition line is visible: a slow red tide of bromeliad flesh creeping upward from the wrist, swallowing the veins one capillary at a time. It has been climbing for six decades and has not yet reached the elbow. It never will.

My eyes are two of the small reflective pools, but they are the only ones that sometimes show the wrong weather. When a visitor stands before me, the left eye shows the hollow exactly as it is. The right eye shows the world they left behind—rain on a night street, the inside of a moving train, the last page of a book they never finished reading. I do not choose what appears. The hollow chooses, and it is cruel only in its courtesy: it shows them the unfinished thing that hurt them most.

I speak once every thirty-seven years.

Not with a voice. The words arrive as a single drop that falls from the exact center of the stone sky and lands in the hollow of my left clavicle. When it strikes, the sound it makes is briefly shaped like language. Those who are close enough and still enough will hear a sentence that is never the same twice. Once it was:

“You were already leaving before you arrived.”

Another time, four centuries earlier, it was simply:

“Stay.”

Most hear nothing at all. The drop falls, the word dies inside my collarbone, and the moss drinks it. But the Unfinished hear. They do not turn their heads—turning would be a kind of completion—but the late shadows lean a fraction closer. In their geological love they are glad I exist. I am the wound that refuses to close and therefore proves the rule: nothing is allowed to finish, not even refusal.

When I move, it is not during the silence between drops. I move during the lag of my own shadow. While the rest of me is frozen in perfect imitation of stone and fern, the shadow—always arriving late—continues for half a heartbeat. In that half-beat I adjust something too small for any visitor to notice: a liana that had begun to dream of becoming a noose, a pearl-frog that was considering the possibility of a second jump, a thought inside a human skull that had almost reached its period.

I kill almost-nothings.

That is my function. The others preserve the large equilibrium. I am the custodian of the almost. The almost-decision, the almost-memory, the almost-death. I drink them the way the others drink the mountain’s tears. They pool behind my ribs like cool mercury and keep me from ever becoming wholly stone or wholly flesh. The tension is exquisite. It will never resolve.

If you sit with me long enough (and some have), you will notice that my hair is not lichen at all. It is condensation that has decided, for reasons no one understands, to grow in the shape of questions. Tiny silver filaments that spell out, in a language older than roots, the same thing over and over:

*What if you never had to finish becoming who you are?*

The question has not yet grown all its letters. It never will.

Sometimes, very rarely, one of the Unfinished will lean across the centuries until its pool-eye meets my mismatched ones. No words pass. Only the understanding that I am the exception they protect. I am the proof that even an anomaly can be absorbed into the slow grammar of this place. I am the sentence the hollow itself left unfinished on purpose.

And I am content.

Not happy—happiness would be a kind of arrival. I am content the way stone is content to weep, the way moss is content to climb without ever reaching the top of the cliff. My heart still beats too quickly, but each beat is smaller than the last, like a drum learning how to become a dripping sound.

If you come here, you may find me.

I will not rise to greet you.

I will simply wait, half stone, half question, while the water falls and the shadows lag and the great ferns creak open another fraction of another frond they will never quite complete.

Stay as long as you like.

I have been waiting since before your species had a name, and like everything else in this hollow, I am in no particular rush to stop.
