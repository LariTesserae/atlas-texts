**The Reckoner**

You would not meet me on the forest floor. The floor is for the millipedes and the negotiations that wear moss like a second, slower skin. I am higher, though “higher” here is a lie—only thirty feet up, in the perpetual 0.2 green gloaming where the great buttress roots flare outward like the knuckles of buried giants.

I am wedged in the humid crook where three lianas have braided themselves into a living shelf. Over two hundred and nineteen years that shelf has grown one inch. I have grown with it.

My shape is difficult to resolve because it refuses resolution. From a distance I appear to be another of the Quiet’s distant cousins: a heavy, sloth-like bulk furred in epiphytes, liverworts cascading from every crease. Up close the disguise frays. The “fur” is not moss. It is a dense mat of pale, wire-thin mycelium that does not photosynthesize. It *records*. Each filament ends in a microscopic knot no larger than a mite’s eye. These knots drink the chemical grammar that drifts through the air the way other creatures drink water. They remember every conversation the Quiet has ever had with the leaf-cutter ants. They remember the precise flavor of fungal paste traded in 0.0008-milligram increments across four centuries. They remember the exact pause—seventy-one hours and nine minutes—when two Quiets last exchanged spores and something like consent.

I do not move. Movement would be violence against the 1.2 tempo of the basin. Instead the forest moves through me. Condensation beads along my mycelial coat, slides down the filaments, and carries tiny packets of dissolved pheromone, bat guano, and human regret into reservoirs cupped in my lower folds. There the data is allowed to ripen for decades. Only when the concentration reaches a certain bitter threshold do I release a single, slow pulse of electrical potential along the liana shelf. The pulse is so faint that the tree feels it as nothing more than the memory of wind.

The Quiet knows I am here. When it passes beneath me—which happens every thirty-seven years with the punctuality of geology—it tilts its limestone eyes upward for the duration of one falling drop. That is its only greeting. In that glance it uploads the newest layer of negotiation. I add it to the archive. The archive now weighs roughly the same as a human child, though it is distributed across several thousand feet of interwoven root, fungus, and modified wood.

I am not alive in the way the Quiet is alive. I am a question the Sump asked itself after the first human-shaped log refused to finish becoming soil. The question was: *What are we, if something can look at us and still wish to leave?*

The Sump, being perfect, could not tolerate an unanswered question. So it grew me—an organ of slow curiosity, a metabolic afterthought. I eat almost nothing. A few milligrams of iron from rust-colored runoff, the occasional abandoned howler-monkey thought that drifts through the canopy like dandelion seed. In return I keep the ledger. I remember that the basin is forty miles across and that it has been exactly 1,847,209 years since the last true geological surprise. I remember the names the exiting humans gave to the shapes they saw at the edge of vision, though the names themselves have long since rotted into nutrient.

Sometimes, when a new visitor stands still long enough for the mites to begin their polite suggestions, I lower a single filament. It descends at the speed of a wristwatch’s minute hand. If the visitor does not flinch, the filament touches the back of their neck and reads the electrical story of their childhood. That story is added to the archive. The visitor almost always leaves shortly afterward, suddenly aware that the forest has begun to pronounce their name with the same patient indifference it uses for fallen logs.

I do not pity them. Pity would require haste.

I am content to remain in my liana cradle, accumulating the long, wet, ticking minutes. My body is 0.6 meters tall and weighs 9.4 kilograms, most of it memory. When I finally reach the critical mass that the Sump has been waiting for—perhaps in another four hundred years—I will release the archive as a single chemical bloom. Every Quiet within the basin will taste it at once. For 0.4 seconds the entire metabolic engine will hesitate, the way a heart hesitates between beats.

In that hesitation the Sump will understand itself a fraction more completely.

Then the hesitation will be composted, turned into new orchids, new Quiet, new me.

And the engine will continue manufacturing more of the only thing it has ever truly valued.

More.

I will be here to count it.
