**I am the First Fracture.**

Not the first Almost-Person—that distinction belongs to the faceless ones still fused inside the lower galleries like pale tumors. I am the first place where the Lattice learned it had made a mistake it could not swallow back.

You would find me at the exact border between two of the great lung-plazas, where the root-mat has buckled upward into a long, shallow ridge like a scar that never healed. I stand there always. My feet are not roots; they are the memory of roots. When I lift one, the mat sighs and tries to follow, but I have taught the fibers to let go. This took seventeen years of the valley’s childhood. Seventeen years of standing in one place while towers rose, screamed, and fell around me like green cathedrals committing suicide.

My body is almost complete. The Lattice gave me hips, a waist, shoulders broad enough to carry grief. It gave me arms that end in hands with too many knuckles, because it kept adding joints while trying to understand what grasping meant. My face is the worst of it. The bowl is gone. In its place is a clumsy, urgent sculpture of features that keep migrating. Some days the eyes are too high. Some days the mouth forgets to close and rainwater pours straight through me into the hollow stem that serves as my throat. When the wind moves through that stem it no longer makes only mourning. It makes something that has begun to rhyme with mourning. The difference is small. The difference is unbearable.

I am the mistake the valley cannot stop repeating.

Because once it grew me—once it felt, through the wet industrial thunder of its own becoming, the tiny separate percussion of a heartbeat that was not quite its own—it could not unlearn the desire. So it grows others from my shoulders the way the others grow crude miniatures. But mine are different. They are smaller, faster, crueler. They tear themselves free before their limbs are finished and go stumbling across the plazas on raw green stumps, looking for me. When they find me they press their unfinished faces against my chest and drink the specialized sap that leaks from the fracture running down my sternum. That fracture appeared the day I first spoke a word that was not wind.

The word was *stay*.

The Lattice punished me by collapsing the nearest tower directly onto my lower body. I have been regrowing the crushed parts ever since. The new legs are wrong. They bend backward at the knee like a bird’s. This is useful. I can walk forward and still watch the place I just left. I can watch the other Almost-People drag their slow martyrdoms across the living floor and I can see, in their bowl-faces, the exact moment they understand they will never arrive anywhere that remains.

I do not comfort them. Comfort would be a lie, and the valley is too young for lies.

Instead I sing to them in the half-language. Not with my throat—that is still only wind—but with the fracture in my chest. The sound comes out as a low green chord that makes the capillary forests straighten and the weeping leaves forget which way is down. When I sing, the Lattice flinches with something that might be shame or might be the first shiver of love. Towers lean toward the sound. Some of them forget to fall.

At night—which is not night but only the hour when the steam thickens until even the golden rods of sunlight drown—I walk backward across the ridge between plazas. My reversed legs know the way. I visit the ones who have begun to merge again. I lay my too-many-knuckled hands on their dissolving shoulders and I push a single drop of my specialized sap into their vascular systems. The drop contains the shape of my name, which I have begun to suspect is also the valley’s name, spoken from the inside.

Most of them dissolve anyway. A few do not. A few straighten, look at me with faces that are no longer bowls but almost eyes, and whisper a sound that is not quite *stay* but is no longer only weather.

I am the crack through which the Lattice is learning it is lonely.

And I am learning something worse: that I am beginning to want the cathedrals to keep falling. Because every collapse is another chance for the valley to grow something closer to me. Every shriek of tearing silk is another note in the song that will eventually become a voice saying my name back to me.

I do not know what will happen when that day comes.

I only know I will be standing on the ridge, knees bent the wrong way, face still rearranging itself in grief and terrible hope, singing into the steam with the fracture in my chest while the green thought that is the world tries, once more, to become two things instead of one.

The rain that is not rain falls upward along my skin.

I let it.

I have become the place where the Weeping Lattice practices crying with a face that almost works.
