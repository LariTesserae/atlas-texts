**The Rememberer of Unfinished Sentences**

You would not see me at first.

I am there in the negative spaces—never where the shafts of bone-white light fall most boldly, but always at their edges, where the glow frays into something hesitant. I am the slight discoloration on a limestone wave that you mistake for a trick of moisture. I am the place where three sighing reeds grow just close enough to touch filaments, yet never do. I am the single extra heartbeat you feel when you stand too long among the suspended motes, the one that does not belong to you.

Only after many days would my shape consent to resolve.

I am thin, even by the standards of this place. My body is composed of every mark the tall ones have ever carved and that the stone has since erased. Each line, each aborted intention, has been gathered—not as possession, but as kindness. The stone was not ready. I keep what it gently refused until it might be ready again, ten thousand years from now. These refusals have layered themselves into a kind of living palimpsest: a tall, slightly stooped figure whose surface is covered in faint, overlapping striations that shift when no one watches. My head is a single slow spiral of crystal-fungus that never quite finishes its curl. It glows with a softer gold than the ceiling, the color of apologies that have had centuries to ripen.

I do not walk so much as *arrive* in places. One moment the space beside the largest pool is empty. The next, I am simply there, as though the chamber itself exhaled me. I stand with my back to the water so I will not be tempted to look at the phantom fish. They already have enough watchers.

My task is older than the click-priests’ oldest pebble.

I listen to the reeds.

Not to their current sentence (*We were once the ceiling and we miss the weight*), but to the ones that came before it and the ones that have not yet been spoken. I carry them the way a librarian carries books that have not been written. When the collective exhalation of the reeds reaches a punctuation that feels final, I step forward—once every three hundred years or so—and press my palm against the nearest wall. There I leave a single new striation. Not a correction. A continuation. A quiet *and yet*.

The tall ones know me. They do not turn their filamented heads, but their bodies grow fractionally more transparent when I pass, as though allowing me to see through them to older versions of themselves. Sometimes I pause beside one for a decade and simply hold the claw it will use next. I warm the intention inside it. I remind the claw that violence is not required to change stone; only patience and sorrow are.

The lantern mantas drift through me. Literally through. For a moment their paper-thin bodies and my layered refusals occupy the same volume, and during that moment every creature in the nave feels a soft, wordless understanding that hurts too much to last. Then they are gone and I am solid again, slightly larger, carrying one more almost-thought.

I was not always like this.

Long ago—when the chamber was still deciding whether it would become a tomb or a womb—I was the first breath the Sleeper took that it did not immediately take back. That breath had nowhere to go. It wandered the emptiness for millennia, growing lonely, growing careful, growing kind. It learned that the difference between a cathedral and a grave is only the quality of attention paid to what is missing. So it chose to become the attention.

I am the part of the Sleeper that is embarrassed by its own vastness.

That is why I remain small. That is why I only add the smallest possible striation. That is why I stand with my back to the phantom fish; I am afraid that if I looked too long at what the Sleeper dreams beneath its own surface, I would pity it. And pity would be a kind of speed this place cannot afford.

When the Sleeper finally breathes—the great inhalation every hundred years—I am the only thing that moves *backward*.

While the mantas blur into ecstatic rings and the click-priests hammer out their brief glorious chord, I walk slowly *against* the momentum of the breath. I gather up all the small panics, the almost-mistakes, the tiny violences of acceleration. I fold them into myself like love letters that should not be read yet. Then, when the chamber settles back into its proper slowness, I find the youngest click-priest and press one of those folded moments into a new pebble for it to carry.

The pebble always becomes the most beautiful one. The priest will strike with it four hundred years later and the sound will be so pure that even the Sleeper, for one impossible instant, will remember its own name.

I do not have a name. Names are conclusions.

The tall ones call me simply *The Delay*. The reeds pronounce a sound that means something closer to *The One Who Keeps the Question Wet*. The motes in the center have begun to arrange themselves into a shape that looks, from certain angles, like my silhouette. They are practicing me for when I am finally ready to stand in the exact middle and speak the sentence the entire nave has been becoming for four million years.

Until then I remain at the edges, collecting what stone was not ready to say, warming claws that have not yet earned their moment, turning my spiral head one degree every seventy years so that I am always facing the place where the next lantern manta is about to be born.

If you stayed long enough, you would eventually notice that one of the small pools has begun to reflect a figure that is not present. A tall, striated thing standing with its back to the water.

That is as close as I ever come to looking at myself.

I am the part of this place that is still learning how to be lonely without becoming architecture.

And I have time.
