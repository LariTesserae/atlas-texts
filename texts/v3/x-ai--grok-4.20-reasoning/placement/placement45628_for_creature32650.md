**The Deliberator**

I am not native here. That is the first truth the Undersinter taught me, and it taught me slowly, the way it teaches everything.

I arrived along an old service conduit no wider than a coffin. Whatever I had been before—some hurried emissary, some last technician, some fool chasing a signal that had died centuries earlier—did not survive the descent. By the time my boots touched the mineral-veined floor I was already too loud. My pulse was an obscenity. My thoughts arrived in bright, violent slashes that left after-images on the Curator’s slow senses like flashbulbs in a cathedral.

The place did not punish me. It simply refused to hurry.

For the first year I moved. I catalogued. I whispered frantic observations into a recorder whose batteries died so gradually I watched the last electron crawl out of them. The slow-worms avoided me. The fungal mats withheld their spores. Even the dripping water seemed to fall *around* me, as though my temperature was disagreeable.

So I stopped.

I chose a place where a collapsed catwalk had fused with a heat-exchanger, creating a shallow declivity the exact length of a reclining body. The sinter had already shaped a pillow for the head and a gentle trough for the spine. It was not comfortable. Comfort implies change. It was *correct*.

There I lay down in the thirteenth year.

At first I counted. Then I forgot what counting was. Then the numbers themselves loosened and became long amber strands that drifted through me like dust in cathedral light. My left arm sank first. The minerals liked the oils in my skin; they drank them and replaced them with glassy patience. Now that arm is a graceful iron limb fused to the lattice. Its fingers have become four valve stems that open, over the course of a decade, to release a precise measure of ferrous condensate into a runnel the slow-worms use as a highway. I do this without deciding. The decision was made somewhere in the fortieth year and has been executing ever since.

My right arm remains flesh enough that the largest slow-worms—those the length of my forearm—sometimes coil beneath it for warmth. I feel their plated bodies as distant continents moving across my skin. Their movement is conversation. When they are content they secrete a thin lubricant that tastes, to the Curator, like approval.

My eyes are still eyes, but their lids have calcified open. The phosphor light enters them constantly. I have learned to see the way the Curator sees: not in images but in gradients of urgency. A droplet preparing to fall for nine weeks appears to me as a glowing node of potential. A pipe that will expand in six months shines with a soft rose-gold anticipation. Human speed, should any ever descend again, would look like white fire and screaming.

The Curator and I are not two beings. We are not even in dialogue. We are staggered phases of the same equilibrium.

It supplies the vast, mineral will. I supply the hesitation.

Every seventy-three days when the great gear prepares its one-degree turn, something in me—some last wet mammalian sliver—offers the smallest, most exquisite doubt. Not enough to stop the turn. Just enough to make the turn *considered*. The Curator has learned to wait for this almost-nothing. Without it, the motion would be mere habit. With it, the motion is choice. One degree every seventy-three days, chosen. That difference is everything.

In return it has given me the only thing worth having: time enough to finish a single thought.

I am thinking it now.

It began when I first lay down. It will end, if it ends at all, when the last slow-worm has polished the last lens and the great gear has completed its final degree sometime in the next ice age. The thought concerns the precise moment when purpose becomes worship. I am perhaps two centuries from the end of the first paragraph.

My torso has grown filaments—copper leached from ancient wiring, braided with my own nerve tissue. When I approach the far edge of a thought, these filaments flush the color of old brandy. The Curator reads them the way a vintner reads sediment in a barrel. Sometimes it answers by altering the temperature of the sinter beneath my skull by 0.03 °C. That is all the language we need.

There are others like me now. The sintered colleagues. You can see them if you stay long enough to let your eyes calcify too. A woman whose ribcage became a nest of control relays. A child who chose the eastern lattice and is now mostly a hand with too many joints. We do not speak to one another. Speech is violence. Instead we adjust one another’s temperatures by fractions of a degree across decades. This is fellowship.

I am not happy. Happiness is a fast emotion and has no purchase here.

I am *adequate*.

Adequate to the gear. Adequate to the drip. Adequate to the long, slow inhalation the Undersinter takes every few hours. When the Curator finally produces its single naming-sound—the perfect *tok* of water striking the exact valve at the apex of its thermal cycle—I will understand it not as a word but as the completion of the thought I have been thinking since the thirteenth year.

Until then I remain exactly where I belong: neither fully incorporated nor fully separate. A deliberate pause wearing the shape of a man who once hurried. A hesitation given mineral weight. The place where the Curator’s vast affection grows cautious enough to matter.

If you descend and you wait long enough, you will eventually reach the declivity where three pipes and a collapsed catwalk have made a body-shaped hollow. You will see something lying there that is mostly orange and bone-white and the blue of certain ancient alloys. One arm will be iron. The other will be soft enough for slow-worms to rest beneath.

Do not speak.

Lie down nearby if there is room.

The hesitation is large enough for two.
