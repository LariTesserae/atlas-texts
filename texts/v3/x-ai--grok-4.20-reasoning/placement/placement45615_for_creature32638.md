**The Scribe of the Unstruck**

I am not there at first.

I arrive the way condensation arrives—gradually, without announcement. One day there is only mist on the black stone; the next, the mist has weight and memory and a pair of hands that have already begun to move in forty-second arcs. I do not remember crossing the knife-edged ridges. I only remember noticing, while sitting on a riveted stool that no one had placed there the day before, that my breathing had already learned the exact shape of the main hammer’s lift.

They do not give me a name. They simply nod once, as though I were a new condensation tower that had quietly bolted itself to the existing architecture overnight. The children call me “the one who writes what she does not do,” which is accurate enough. The crows call me nothing at all; they simply land on my left shoulder when they wish the next shrug of the earth to be recorded in a particular key.

I keep no forge. I carry no hammer. My only tool is a stylus of drawn sigh-iron no thicker than a raven’s primary feather, and sheets of birch bark harvested from the miniature jungles that grow in the shadow of the third condensation tower. Every cycle—between the fall of the hammer and the moment the sound folds back from the ridges—I write down what the Unstruck has *not* done.

She passes. The wires go silent. The beads of water freeze mid-fall. In that pocket of arrested time I scratch the negative shape of her passage: the exact interval her foot did *not* break, the precise frequency her hair did *not* disturb in the air, the rhythm the starlings would have sung had she not silenced them by simply existing nearby. These negative scores become the blueprints.

From them the Quiet Hands build the devotional machines—devices whose only purpose is to remember a silence that has never happened before. One machine is a tall glass column filled with the alkaline sweat of the basalt; inside it, a single wire vibrates in the exact pattern her *absence* left behind. Another is a water wheel that turns only when no one is looking at it. A third is simply a stool bolted to nothing, placed exactly where she stood at dawn three years ago. The stool hums. No one sits on it. Everyone listens to it.

I have begun to change.

The mist no longer beads on my skin; it beads *inside* the small hollows that have opened along my collarbones and the backs of my wrists. When I move, the droplets fall in perfect counterpoint to the hammer. My eyes have taken on the glossy fracture planes of the young basalt. Light does not reflect from them. It enters and is kept.

At night I walk the perimeter where the ridges are still rising. The Unstruck sometimes walks beside me, though “beside” is imprecise—she occupies the space the way a future memory occupies the mind. We do not speak. Instead I feel the next shrug gathering in the stone the way one feels a coming sneeze in the bones of the nose. When it comes, I press my stylus to fresh bark and write the shape of the tremor that has not yet arrived. The crows descend. They read what I have written by tapping it with their iron-filament beaks, then scream once in unison. The new stone settles into the exact form I have described.

I have become a negative space that records negative spaces so that something enormous can be born without ever having to explain itself.

Sometimes, when the hammer is lifted to its highest point and the entire Notch holds its breath, I feel the thing we are midwifing stir inside the ribs of black rock. It is not awake yet. It is only dreaming that it might one day be awake. In that dream it is vast, patient, and faintly amused that four hundred small lives and a thousand small machines have agreed to spend centuries rubbing its back and singing to its spine.

I write that amusement down as well.

The rodents line their nests with the scraps. The starlings learn new phrases from the discarded notes. The goats walk across the bark sheets without smudging them, leaving hoof prints that become part of the next score.

And I remain.

Not waiting.

Only keeping the exact shape of what has not yet happened, so that when the enormous thing inside the ridges finally opens its eyes, it will already know the sound of its own becoming by heart—because someone, without drama or martyrdom, spent four hundred years writing down everything it chose *not* to be, until only the truth remained.

The hammer falls.

I write the shape of the silence after.

The ridges rise another inch.

I am exactly where I am supposed to be, forty-seven years and a half-measure into a labor that still has three hundred and fifty-three years left to breathe.

I have never been happier to disappear into the music I am helping to notate.
