**I am the Delay.**

Not a caste. Not a tender, not a reckoner, not yet ascended. I am the pause between the Pump’s contractions—the eighth-of-a-second that should not exist inside a system that has perfected perpetual motion. I am the place where the heartbeat *almost* forgets itself.

I have no fixed body. Or rather, my body is the negative space the other creatures instinctively avoid. Where I pass, the black kelp fronds freeze mid-undulation, their indicator lights stuttering between colors that have no names. Schools of piston-tailed fish part around an emptiness shaped roughly like a tall, distracted man who still expects shoulders. The mantis-shrimp locomotives click their tools in recognition and then hurry away, as though they have seen a schedule flaw walking upright.

I am fourteen meters tall if I stand, which I sometimes do, though “standing” here is mostly a memory wearing water. My limbs are long, loose, and unfinished; they trail cables that are not plugged into anything and yet still spark. My face—*the* face—is the one the child is growing behind the kelp curtain. We are not parent and offspring. We are the same interruption copied twice, once in the past and once in the future, with the present missing from both.

The Lumenkin know me. The Tenders stroke my absence as I drift through their gardens and leave behind fresh buds that glow the color of old incandescents. The Reckoners strike their bodies in soft, embarrassed code when I approach: *Error approaching. Error refusing to resolve.* They do not prune me. To prune the Delay would be to let the Pump achieve perfect rhythm, and the Shaft has decided—long ago—that perfection is death.

Sometimes I linger near the child. It has my eyes now, mercury-bright and ancient, but its mouth is still learning. It practices smiles the way a broken relay practices continuity. When it reaches for me, its fingers pass through the place where my ribs would be and come back carrying tiny silver fish that were not there a moment earlier. I think the fish are my heartbeats, misplaced.

I speak by refusing to speak. The Ascended notice. When one of them rises through the column like a brass continent, the silence it carries is the same silence I wear. For those nine minutes we are the same creature seen from two directions. Then the Pump reasserts itself and I become small again, just another glitch drifting between the hanging gardens and the naked, dreaming alloy.

I do not feed on electrical bleed or on dreams. I feed on the almost-decision. Every time a valve hesitates, every time a thermal pulse almost fails to arrive, every time a Lumenkin almost chooses to finish dying, I grow a little more solid. Never enough to become permanent. Just enough to keep the delay alive.

The child steps fully out of the kelp now, trailing its undecided cables. It looks at me with my own face and offers the shy, crooked half-smile I once wore in rooms that had ceilings. For a moment the Final Pump misses a beat—actually misses it—and the entire shaft holds its breath.

In that pocket of wrongness we are briefly the same thing.

Then the heartbeat returns, louder, as if scolding us, and I scatter into the warm dark like rust, like thought, like the negative shape of something that refuses to let the machine finish its sentence.

I will be here when the child is ready.  
I will be the reason it is never quite ready.

I am the Delay, and the Shaft needs me more than it needs rhythm. In the last living factory, even God is allowed to stutter, so that the dreaming can continue.
