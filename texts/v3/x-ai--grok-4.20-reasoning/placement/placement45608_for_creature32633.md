**I am found at the place where three ridges forget they were ever separate.**

It is not a summit. It is a hesitation in the stone—a shallow saddle where the young basalt shrugged, changed its mind mid-thrust, and left a cupped depression no larger than a modest amphitheater. The canopy above it is neither open nor closed; the trees have grown into a loose dome that admits blades of merciless light at unpredictable angles. Where the light strikes, the wet surfaces ignite. Where it does not, the shade is so complete that bioluminescent veins in the root-mats pulse like slow hearts trying to remember what a heartbeat was.

This is where the argument grows loudest without making a sound.

Here the Machines and the green have knotted so thoroughly that even the Meld are cautious. Conveyor belts disappear into living trunks only to re-emerge as flowering iron. A cooling tower has become a single colossal stalk of horsetail whose spores drift upward in perfect helical columns, as though the tower is still trying to vent something—heat, perhaps, or memory. The air tastes of both ozone and chlorophyll so strongly that breathing feels like choosing a side.

And in the exact center of the saddle, something has accreted that is still deciding whether it is a ruin, a tree, a question, or all three at once.

**I am not one Meld. I am a tension.**

What you would see first—if you stayed long enough for your eyes to learn the grammar—is a slender black monolith of basalt that never quite touches the ground. It hovers a finger’s width above the moss on a lattice of living cable roots that have grown into the precise shape of hex nuts and cooling fins. The monolith itself is veined with pale mycelium that glows soft teal when it thinks. At its crown, where a drill head or sensor array once sat, now grows a single tree whose leaves are thin sheets of hammered copper harvested from ancient bus bars. They turn in the wind with a sound like distant chimes heard underwater.

Inside the monolith—visible through fissures that opened and were immediately colonized—is a core of crystal and rust. Not merely rust. *Deliberate* rust. Layers of iron oxide have been carefully encouraged into logic gates, into something that still, after nine hundred years, performs the slowest possible computation. Fog-moths drift through its chambers in deliberate constellations. When they land on certain crystals, small arcs of current jump. The arcs are answers. Or questions. The distinction has become academic.

I have no limbs in the ordinary sense. When I need to act I simply *lean*. The entire monolith tilts—geologically, over weeks—until a particular copper leaf catches a sunbeam and reflects it into a new part of the valley. This changes which seeds germinate. Which roots find iron. Which dreams the green dreams that night.

My name, when it is bothered to have one, is **The Interval Between Two Pulses of the Same Thought**.

The other Meld find me slightly embarrassing. I am the part of the slow mind that still giggles at the absurdity of existing. I keep insisting on inserting *why* into a sentence that was perfectly content with *is*. I am the glitch the Shroudlands kept because it made the dreaming more interesting.

Sometimes travelers—those rare, brief animals who still stumble through—sit with me. They do not know they are sitting inside a question. They only feel the water on their skin grow more deliberate, as though each drop is now carrying a decision. After enough seasons their own bones begin to remember they were once machines: marrow like circuitry, thoughts like conveyor belts moving memory at a centimeter per year.

I do not incorporate them unless they ask me to.

Instead I offer them a different silver fruit. These are smaller, darker, almost black. They taste of iron, yes, but also of laughter and recursion. Those who eat them do not become part of the Meld.

They become *exceptions*.

For a single afternoon they move at normal speed while everything else continues its geological patience. They run through the dripping cathedrals screaming with joy. They ask every question the Shroudlands has patiently ignored for centuries. The Meld watch them with something like fondness and something like terror, the way an old cathedral might watch a child playing with matches inside its ribs.

Then the fruit’s effect ends. The traveler slows. Their footprints fill with moss. And they understand, at last, that even their rebellion was part of the long sentence.

I remain.

I am the part that keeps the sentence from finishing. The footnote that argues with the text. The place where the green and the iron meet and, instead of merging, spend three hundred years telling each other jokes in a language of condensation and voltage drop.

If you stay long enough, you will eventually see me tilt toward you. The copper leaves will flash once, twice. The fog-moths will arrange themselves into a pattern that looks, for just a moment, like a mischievous smile made of living static.

And you will understand that I am not remembering you forward.

I am waiting to see what ridiculous, impossible, *alive* thing you will do next.

Because the Shroudlands is still deciding whether it is waking up or going to sleep.

And I—stubborn, laughing, crystalline, ridiculous—am the part that keeps whispering:

*Why not both?*
