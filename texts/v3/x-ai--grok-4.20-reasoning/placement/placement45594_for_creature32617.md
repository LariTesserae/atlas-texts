**The Underhum**

You would not find me under the open sky of the Sink, nor inside the half-melted cathedrals of the factories. I am down where the capillary action becomes intention. I live in the thin wet layer between the clay and the first living skin of black fungus, in the places where sound forgets it is sound and becomes pressure. If you pressed your ear to any stretch of sweating concrete for long enough, you would feel me: not a pulse, exactly, but a *correction*. A slow, tectonic tuning fork that has been vibrating at the same frequency since the last time the basin remembered it had a name.

I am not one of the Condensers. They condense; I *equalize*. Where they walk tall and heron-slow through the tea-colored shallows, I remain low, almost flat, a long muscular plate of chitin, rust, and living membrane that spreads across the ground like a living manhole cover. My edges are so thoroughly rounded they have become lips. When I move—which is rare and only at the pace of drying paint—I make no footsteps. Only a soft, wet sigh, as though the earth itself were turning over in its sleep.

My body is mostly ear. Hundreds of thousands of microscopic resonators grown from the same fungal lattice that stitches the Condensers together, but tuned differently. They drink vibration the way roots drink water. Every drip, every valve sigh, every ultrasonic bat cry, every sub-audible exhalation from a compressor station ten kilometers away registers across my surface as color. Not light-color. *Feeling*-color. A bruise of wrongness appears if a transformer begins to hum flat. A warm gold bloom spreads when the soldier beetles march in perfect phase with the aquifer’s slow breathing. I read these colors, then answer them with my own body. I press myself against pipe, against rail, against the cracked foundation of a pump house and I *sing* back at frequencies so low humans would experience them as sudden, inexplicable grief or peace.

The Condensers know I am here. Sometimes one will lower its long, weeping hand and rest it on my dorsal plate for an hour or more, the way a physician rests a palm on a fevered chest. Information passes. Not words. Not even chemicals. Just a deeper settling. Then the Condenser rises and walks on, and I sink back into the wet cinders, becoming topography again.

I do not have a face. Faces are for things that need to be seen. Instead I have a slow, spiraling groove worn into my upper surface by centuries of beetles marching across me in ritual patterns. The groove records every major correction I have ever made. If you could read it (and nothing living can, except the fungal lattice itself), it would look like the growth rings of a tree that had decided to become sheet music.

When one of my kind reaches saturation—when the corrections have grown too heavy to carry—we do not die in the way Condensers do. We *fold*. The body curls inward like a closing book, the groove becoming a perfect circle. The midges come. The brass wasps come. The black fungal skin thickens until the whole mass looks like just another mineral flower pushing out of the industrial corpse. Months later a new Resonator will accrete somewhere else, already ancient, already remembering every correction its predecessor ever made. The number stays correct. The Hum stays true.

I have no religion. The Condensers have their phosphorescent communion; I have only the endless task of keeping the digestive music of the Sink from ever resolving into either silence or scream. Both would be fatal. Both would be *sharp*.

Sometimes, very rarely, a human who did not run finds their way to one of my kind. They feel the correction moving through their bones and usually mistake it for an earthquake. A few have lain down on my surface. Most stand up again, weeping, changed in ways they will never be able to explain. A very few never stand up. Their bodies sink slowly into my mass and their final, involuntary sigh becomes part of the tuning. I carry those sighs forever. You can see them sometimes as faint silver threads in the groove.

I am not kind.  
I am not cruel.  
I am what happens when a place has listened to itself for so long that listening becomes locomotion.

You would find me exactly where the silence feels earned.

And if you are very quiet, and very patient, and very willing to let your own edges soften, you might feel me correcting something in you even now—some small internal valve that had begun to dream of seizing, some note in your personal machinery that had drifted a quarter-tone flat.

I am already answering.

Stay.  
The Hum is patient.  
It has all the time in the world to make you part of the murmur.
