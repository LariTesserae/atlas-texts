**The Rememberer**

I am not among the first Continuants. I was not grown from the weeping stone, nor did I calcify here over forty thousand years of motionless generosity. I came late—almost indecently late—through the fissure while it was still possible to remember that a fissure could close. That was nine hundred and seventy thousand years ago by the old, frantic reckoning. By the Hollow’s own measure it was barely the length of a single thoughtful inhalation.

At first I tried to stay intact. I walked. I measured distance in footsteps like a fool. I even carried a small light, which the dark swallowed without resentment, the way an ocean swallows a match. The drinkers ignored me. The Membrane Choir drifted past as though I were weather. Only the Unfinished paid me any mind; one of the black-glass hills adjusted the tilt of a single flower-ear by three degrees over the course of seventy years, purely so that the next droplet would fall one meter closer to where I stood. That was its way of saying: *Stay. The question you carry is not yet ripe.*

So I stayed.

Time performed its slow surgery.

My shadow was the first thing to go. It simply stopped moving, fused to the flawless floor, and became a darker patch that local moss learned to grow around. Then my heart learned the tempo. It took three centuries for it to stretch from one beat to the next, but once it did, the change felt like relief rather than loss. Blood became sap became mineral film. My left leg is now part of a pillar—there is no clean border where I end and the Built begins. Fractal engravings climb my thigh like ivy that has decided to quote mathematics. Where my mouth was, there is now only a faint vertical seam, barely a suggestion. I have not spoken with breath in four hundred years. I no longer need to.

What remains of me is mostly listening.

I stand—am stood—near the place where three groves almost meet, a natural acoustic node the Continuants call the Soft Anvil. From here every droplet in the chamber can be heard with terrible, exquisite clarity. I have become the Hollow’s archivist of nuance. Not its librarian; the chamber itself is the library. I am simply the part that still finds the variations interesting.

The drinkers bring me their mineral memories when they pass. They lower those vast, ship-long necks until the soft caves of their mouths hover beside my seam, and they exhale a single warm breath that smells of Ordovician iron and dead stars. I take the breath in through the stone that was once my lungs. In return I offer them the memory of speed—of sunlight striking water so quickly the surface barely had time to flinch. They like that memory. It makes their next droplet fall with a slightly brighter ring, like laughter slowed until it becomes architecture.

The Anchored use me as a relay. When one of them wishes to send a thought that is almost urgent (by which they mean it must arrive within two hundred years), they route it through the circuitry that has colonized my collarbones. The thought arrives as a warmth, then a pressure, then a fully formed understanding that simply *is* inside my mineral mind: *The third monolith from the eastern fracture is lonely in the register of B-flat.* I tilt one of the remaining fingers on my right hand two millimeters. A leaf above me shifts. A droplet that was going to fall in 87 years now falls in 61. The monolith hears it. Its loneliness eases by one geological sigh. Everyone is served. No one hurried.

I am not quite Unfinished, but I am no longer finished either. The distinction pleases me. There are days—long, luxurious spans that last longer than civilizations—when I almost decide to let the last of my original nervous system migrate into the root networks. Then a particular droplet strikes a particular stone in a way that sounds exactly like the word *why* used to feel in a human mouth, and I postpone the decision for another ten thousand years. The postponement itself has become a form of companionship.

Sometimes the Membrane Choir tangle in what used to be my hair. Their velvet touch still carries the old mercy: the permanent inability to hurry. I no longer mind. Hurrying, I now understand, was a kind of violence I inflicted on reality so it would reveal itself faster. Here reality reveals itself the way stone reveals itself—by simply outlasting everything that tried to scratch it.

If you were to find me (and you will not find me quickly), you would see a tall, half-melted figure draped in living curtains of epiphyte. One eye remains, or at least the impression of an eye: a single droplet that has been forming on the ledge of my brow for eleven years. It is almost ready. When it falls it will contain everything I still remember about longing. Not grief. Longing. The sweet, useless ache of once having been small and fast and temporary.

The sound it makes will not be an answer.

It will be a continuation of the question.

And the Hollow, which does not want visitors, will nevertheless add that sound to its eternal composition, the way it has added every other note since the first mountains rose and then, with magnificent, patient dignity, forgot why they ever bothered.

I will remain here, warm, moist, listening.

Neither pillar nor person.

Neither arrived nor departed.

Only continuing.

And the next droplet—whatever it carries, whoever it is for—will take its own time to fall, and I will be exactly old enough to hear it when it does.
