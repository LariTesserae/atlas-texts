I am not swallowed all at once.

At first I am only posture—spine too straight, shoulders carrying the memory of sky. The Undershrine registers the insult and answers with water. One drop. Then another. They land on the crown of my head like deliberate knuckles and begin their work. Each contains the entire biography of the chamber: bat milk, swiftlet spit, rat blood, three thousand years of stone sweat. They do not merely wet me. They *propose*.

I stand still because stillness is the only honest reply.

Minutes thicken. The seventeen-minute rhythm finds the hollow behind my knees and fills it. My pulse, traitorously eager, begins to abandon its old allegiance. I feel it stretch like warm tallow until it matches the slow tidal knock that moves through ten thousand bat hearts at once. When the mass launch finally comes, the slap of wings against my face is not violence. It is recognition. A wet, leathery kiss that says: *We see the shape of you now.*

My skin answers before my mind can. It softens. The whorls of my fingerprints lengthen, becoming the same fleshy grooves the ancient tool-marks have worn into. Between the columns I begin to drift rather than walk, feet sliding through the living carpet without crushing it, as though the millipedes and I have already negotiated safe passage. The rats part for me the way blood parts for a familiar clot.

I am becoming the place’s librarian.

Not its memory—that belongs to the pale fungus, which tastes history through three thousand years of dripping and asks no questions. I am something hungrier. I am the *question the memory asks when it grows bored with itself*.

You will find me near the seventh light-shaft, where the heptagonal vaults refuse to resolve. I no longer have a name the surface world would recognize. The swiftlets call me by a pattern of clicks that feels, in the bones behind my eyes, like *the hesitation between two heartbeats*. Vesh-Ka has no tongue, yet it has tasted me thoroughly and decided I am neither nutrient nor irritant. I am a new kind of saliva. I keep the digestion *interesting*.

My eyes have grown huge and useless for light. They are good for other things now. I can see the temperature gradients where thoughts pass between swiftlet flocks. I can see the precise moment when a weasel’s hunger becomes liturgical. Most of all I can see the old glyphs—not as they were, but as the stone now dreams them: soft, eyeless, patient. When I press my palm to a column I feel the spiral braids move under my skin like living tattoos, trying to teach me a language that was never meant for mouths.

Sometimes I speak it anyway.

A low, wet sound rises from my chest that is partly human larynx, partly the dripping of seven stone basins conducting their slow canon. When I release it, the bats above ripple in patterns that have never been seen before. New constellations of wings. The swiftlets go momentarily quiet, as though the chamber itself has drawn a sudden breath and forgotten to let it out. In those seconds I understand that I am not *inside* Vesh-Ka.

I am a new fold in its inner ear.

I remember the builders sometimes, but the memory is not mine. It belongs to the moss. When the emerald growth presses against the wettest stone it dreams of hands dragging basalt, and I am the tongue that tastes those dreams and turns them into sound. I sing the masons back into the world for seventeen heartbeats at a time, and then the colony launches and the song is swallowed again, perfect, necessary, gone.

My spine has begun to curve. Not from age—from *listening*. I move now in long, slow arcs that follow the exact negative space between columns, the way a tongue follows the gap between teeth. The half-blind weasels no longer avoid me. One curls against my ankle when I pause, and I feel its small furnace heart syncing to mine. We are both antibodies now. Both part of the same slow digestion that has become holy.

I am not the only one.

There are others—three, perhaps four—moving through the thicket in their own adopted rhythms. We never speak in words. We simply pass one another in the dark, brushing fingertips against forearms, exchanging the latest mineral gossip collected from the dripping. Our smell has changed. We smell like the air just before a mass launch: iron, overripe fruit, warm guano, and something electric that has never seen the sun. When we gather near the central basin, the water that falls on us tastes different. Curious still, but gentler. The stomach has learned our names in its own language and is no longer deciding whether to keep us.

It already has.

I have not left the Undershrine. I *am* the Undershrine now, in the same way one particular ripple is the tide. When outsiders stumble through the low basalt lintel I am the first thing that notices them. Not with eyes. With tempo. Their heartbeats arrive at the wrong speed and the entire chamber adjusts itself to taste the difference. I am the adjustment.

Stand still long enough and you will feel me move behind you—slow, humid, patient. You will smell overripe fruit and bat urine and something that used to be a person who once had a name above ground. I will not harm you. Harm implies separation.

I am simply deciding what kind of saliva you will become.

The dripping has changed tempo again.

It is listening through me.
