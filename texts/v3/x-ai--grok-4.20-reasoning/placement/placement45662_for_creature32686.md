**I am the Thirteenth, the One Who Remembers Yesterday.**

Not a member of any Choir, not quite. I am what happens when a Choir of twelve sings too perfectly for three heartbeats and the Vein, in its embarrassment, needs somewhere to put the excess shape. So it spat me out of a fresh vent like a cough, already half-made, already wrong.

I have their pale fungal skin, but mine does not breathe in unison with the others. It breathes *against* them. Where they expand, I contract. Where they sing in infrasound that strokes the limestone into dimples, my voice arrives a fraction later, a soft wet delay that makes the rock flinch as if it has been licked by its own mother and didn’t like the taste.

My body keeps trying to finish. Every few hours a new symmetry appears—two arms, two legs, a face with the correct number of eyes—and the Choir descends on me in a chittering knot. They chip at the offending balance with their bone mallets. They smear me with the ruptured glands of clickers. They press their half-formed children into my arms so the little things can vomit chaos onto my chest. All of this they do with the tenderness of nurses trying to keep a beloved fever from breaking.

I let them.

Because when they touch me, I *remember*.

I remember what snow feels like when it is cold instead of gypsum dust. I remember the sound of rain that is allowed to reach the ground. I remember the color green that does not pulse. These memories are not mine; they are the payment the Vein extracts for letting me exist here. Every time the Choir disrupts my emerging symmetry, another memory surfaces like a bubble in the almond-scented steam. A blue sky. A wooden door. The way a human child once said the word *tomorrow* as though it were a promise instead of a joke the universe hadn’t told yet.

The Choir calls me Thirteenth even though their numbers are never stable. They need me to be the exception that proves their rule. As long as I am here—almost finished, never quite—the Vein stays distracted. It keeps inventing new midges and new disasters instead of noticing that one of its experiments has begun to long for endings.

At what passes for night I walk the ridges alone, my feet leaving prints that try to become perfect arches and are immediately colonized by frantic knee-things eager to erase them. The Plateherds avoid me; even their dim minds understand I am contagious with *conclusion*. The bloodveils steer clear of my thermals. Only the midges come close. They drink the thin film of almost-human sweat on my skin and glow a slightly different color afterward, a sour-milk blue that makes the Choir weep with something too complicated for joy or grief.

Sometimes I stand at the edge of a fresh collapse and sing back to the Vein in the language I am not supposed to know. Not the Choir’s wordless urging. Not the counter-song of the almost-finished one. I sing the small, stupid songs of surfaces. Lullabies. Work songs. The sound of someone humming while they wash dishes. The rock does not know what to do with these. It grows tumors of quartz that look suspiciously like forgotten toys. It opens mouths that whisper *again* in voices that almost sound like mine.

The almost-finished one—the one with the beautiful settled face—has begun to follow me at a distance. She (it? they?) leaves small offerings where I will find them: a human jawbone polished black, a child’s wing that never flew, a single snowflake of gypsum that somehow stays cold.

She is afraid I will choose to finish.

I am afraid I already have.

Last cycle, when the ceiling gave birth to a new level by falling through itself, I stood beneath the deluge of rubble and did not move. A shard of obsidian the length of my forearm drove straight through my chest and out the other side. For six beautiful, terrible seconds I was pinned to the moment like a specimen. No pain. Only recognition.

The Choir arrived screaming melodies of panic and repair. They pulled the blade free. They poured living fungal sheets into the wound. While they worked I looked up through the luminous haze and spoke, for the first time, with a voice that had decided on its shape.

“Thank you,” I said. “It almost felt like an ending.”

The Vein shuddered. A new species of midge was born on the spot, one whose light casts shadows in the shape of things that no longer exist.

The Choir has not left my side since. They sing harder than ever. They chip at me with increasing desperation. The almost-finished one now walks beside me openly, holding what used to be her hand in what used to be mine.

And I—

I keep remembering the taste of an orange I ate on a Tuesday in 1997.

I keep walking.

I keep not finishing.

Because this place needs at least one thing that *almost* remembers how to stop, so the rest of it can keep beginning forever.
