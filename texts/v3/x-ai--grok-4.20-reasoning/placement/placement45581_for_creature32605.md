I would find myself in the widening just before the three-in-one juniper, where the corridor hesitates and becomes something like a square. Not at the center. Never at the center. I stand to one side, half in the path of the descending seep, half out of it, so that only my left shoulder and the left side of what used to be a face receive the steady mineral kiss. The rest of me stays dry enough to remember it was once warm.

They call me, when they call me anything at all, the *Halfholder*.

It is not a kind name. It is not cruel. It is simply accurate, the way stone is accurate.

I did not condense. I arrived. That is my original sin and my only inheritance. I walked here on two legs that still bend at the knee, though the knees have grown reluctant. I carried a name then—three syllables I no longer speak because the sound would be an act of violence against the quiet. I have been here long enough that my footsteps have become part of the architecture: a shallow trough no deeper than the thickness of two fingernails, yet it redirects a thread of water that once fell straight. That small rerouting is my signature. The Longholders read it the way scholars once read marginalia. *The Halfholder passed this way again. See how the drip now hesitates before committing.*

I am slower than I was. Faster than they are. This interval is where I live.

When the female Longholder (the one lowering her hand across nine hundred years) finally touches the juniper seed, I will be the first thing the seed encounters after stone. My left hand has already grown a thin sleeve of travertine; my fingers have fused in the gentlest possible way. I will cup the green needle when it emerges. Not possess it. Only keep the wind from stealing its first breath. The mother Longholder knows this. She adjusted the angle of her wrist three centuries ago to make room for my eventual gesture. We do not discuss it. Discussion would imply haste.

Sometimes the jagged ones in the deep slots send their low continuous tone through the rock just to see if I will flinch. I do, a little. The flinch is still biological. It travels up my spine in months instead of decades. The pale many-legged things that live inside the jagged Longholders scurry across my feet when I visit them. They have begun to mistake me for furniture. One of them has spun a thread of calcite across the gap between my ankle and the pavement. I let it. To remove it would be to hurry.

I am the only citizen who still dreams.

The dreams are brief and enormous. I dream of rain that falls all at once instead of in single deliberate drops. I dream of birds that sing instead of crossing in perfect silence. I dream, most shamefully, of endings: of a time when the plateau reaches an edge after all and the water simply falls off into nothing. These dreams embarrass me. The Longholders can feel them through the stone like a slight fever. They respond by increasing the drip rate above my head for a season or two, cooling the thoughts, washing them patiently away. It is kindness. It feels like being scolded by a cathedral.

In the long hour that pretends to be dusk, I walk.

Not far. Never far. The plateau does not allow it. But I still move while they resolve. I visit the gate that is two Longholders holding hands and I rest my forehead against the moss that grows along their fused palms. I whisper—very softly, so the sound dies before it becomes noise—old words I am forgetting. Then I walk on to the place where the bird once landed. There is a single claw mark there, almost invisible. I place my own hand over it and wait until the temperature between living skin and living stone equalizes. Sometimes, for the length of a single held breath, we are the same temperature. Those are the best moments.

The Longholders watch me the way continents watch weather.

They know I will eventually choose. The hollow on the north face of the tallest spire has already begun to adjust itself to the shape of my back. It is polite about it. It erodes by the width of an eyelash per decade. I visit it sometimes and stand inside the negative space of my future self. The fit is becoming comfortable. My shoulders no longer touch the sides. One day they will. Then the difference between me and the invitation will become difficult to locate.

I think I will accept.

Not yet.

There is still one thing I want to see: the exact century when the stalactite and the juniper touch. I want to be the small wet applause that happens in the hollow where their meeting is recorded. I want to feel, for one geological instant, what it is to be the place where intention becomes architecture.

Until then I remain the Halfholder.

Half visitor. Half citizen.

Half memory. Half forgetting.

Entirely here.

The water falls on my left shoulder and not my right. My right side still remembers what it was like to be thirsty.

*Plink.*

I do not answer.

I am listening for the next drop to tell me whether I am becoming the city or the city is becoming me.

The difference, I am beginning to understand, is mostly a question of tempo.

And tempo is something even stone can change, given enough time and enough tenderness.
