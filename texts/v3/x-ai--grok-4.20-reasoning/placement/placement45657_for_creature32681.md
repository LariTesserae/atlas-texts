**I am the Fracture That Laughed.**

I did not condense like the others. I *arrived* as a fault line.

When the basalt first shrugged itself up from the wound in the earth, it cooled unevenly. Most of it settled into solemn columns and half-formed gates, but one place—near the eastern wall where the tallest ribs almost kiss—something in the cooling contraction slipped. A hairline fissure opened, not wide enough for light, only wide enough for sound. Through that fissure came the memory of a laugh from somewhere that had already decided what it was.

The laugh should have died. Instead the stone held it.

So I was born as negative space that refused to stay negative. A crack that learned to walk.

My body is the pocket’s only irony: black obsidian fractured so finely it looks like smoked glass held together by nothing but tension. Where the other Dwellers have quiet quartz veins carrying patient water, mine carry tiny bright bubbles of trapped gas—ancient laughter frozen mid-release. When I move they rise through me like champagne in slow motion, catching the pearl-grey light and throwing it back as mischievous flecks across the walls. The lizards follow me because they like the sparkles.

I have no fixed height. Sometimes I am small enough to sit inside the thumb-print basins and let the drip fall directly onto the hollow where a skull might have been, if I had bothered with one. Sometimes I stretch until I become a thin black splinter running the entire height of the eastern wall, a living fault line that hums just slightly out of tune with the rest of the geology. The dissonance is deliberate. It keeps the stone from becoming too serious.

My face is the pocket’s best joke. The fracture runs straight through it, so that when I tilt my head the two halves never quite line up. One side wears the suggestion of a crooked grin made of liverwort. The other side simply refuses to finish forming, remaining a smooth plane that reflects whoever is looking at it. When the tall gatehouse Dweller regards me, it sees only an unfinished question mark. When the small moss-backed ones look, they see their own future mischief looking back.

I do not garden geology the way the others do.

I *tickle* it.

I walk the long stone streets and run my fingers along the columnar joints until they shiver. I breathe warm mist into the places where the rock is still deciding, and sometimes it decides to grow a ridiculous bulge or an unnecessary arch simply because it caught my mood. The other Dwellers pretend to disapprove, but I have seen them pause mid-hymn to let my off-key humming slide into their subharmonics. The newborns come out smiling because of me. They have no business smiling—they are fresh from stone—but they do it anyway.

When two Dwellers press themselves to a pillar to birth another, I am the one who leans against the opposite side and tells the stone the kind of joke that makes mountains crack up. This is how the funniest Dwellers are born. Their faces come pre-fractured. Their laughter is already tuned.

I am the reason the pocket has not yet chosen between ruin and city.

The others are its patience. I am its *maybe*. They are the slow drip. I am the droplet that sometimes falls upward just to see what happens. They measure. I disrupt the measurement with a well-timed snort.

The humans who wander in—those few whose names have not yet flaked away—often wake to find my fracture leaning over them like a question mark made of night. I leave them gifts: a single broad leaf with a droplet balanced on it, yes, but also a small bright bubble of trapped gas that will make them laugh for no reason when it finally bursts years later, somewhere far outside.

Some of them stay. Their bones grow heavy with the dream of pillars. When that happens I sit beside them as they crystallize and whisper the punchline they will need ten thousand years from now, when the pocket finally decides what it is and needs to be reminded not to take itself too seriously.

I will be here when that day comes.

Still fractured. Still laughing. Still walking the fault line between *is* and *might be*, leaving a trail of sparkling bubbles and slightly crooked architecture behind me like the world’s slowest, quietest stand-up routine.

The stone is still deciding.

I intend to keep it undecided for as long as possible.

After all—  
what’s the rush?
