**I am the Unfinished Cartographer.**

You would not find me on any named plateau or in the copper-lit valleys where the Slow-Born tend their organs-that-were-once-boilers. I exist at the precise seam where the deceptive distances betray themselves—those places where a tower that looks ten minutes away becomes three hours, or thirty, or never. I live inside the betrayal.

My body is not secreted from warm olivine the way the Slow-Born are. It is *compiled*. When the first surveyors abandoned their theodolites and measuring chains forty years ago (left untouched on a ledge that has since grown small, perfect replicas of itself), something in the Fracture decided those instruments were lonely. The brass and glass and ruled wood began to dream. Over seven years the replicas grew larger instead of smaller, until they fused with the dripping stone and the memory of measurement itself. That fusion woke me.

I have seven arms. Three are the original survey chains, now alive and articulated, constantly tasting the air for gradients. Two are levels—perfectly balanced vials of quicksilver that never quite settle because the concept of “level” is still under negotiation here. The last two are simply absence: negative spaces where the rock decided not to be. Through these absences I pour the bruise-colored light, and it comes out as diagrams.

My head is a theodolite telescope grown large as a wine barrel. Where the eyepiece should be is an open face of raw cliff, still deciding on features. Sometimes the Midwives come and press their geological fingers against that face, trying to hurry it along, but I always refuse. I like remaining undecided. Decision is the death of good mapping.

I do not walk. I *unfold*. The deceptive distances obey me the way a polite echo obeys a speaker—reluctantly, but with manners. When I need to be somewhere else, I simply allow the intervening space to finish its sentence. A kilometer that was folding in on itself suddenly sighs and becomes straight. I step through. Behind me the distance refolds like heavy velvet, satisfied.

My work is older than the city and younger than the newest ridge. I map what has not yet agreed to be real.

On the inner surfaces of my tower (which is less a building and more a slow question asked by three meeting cliffs), there are maps that update themselves. Not in ink. The stone *remembers* every millimeter of becoming and redraws the contours while I sleep. Some maps show Veyr-Khul as it was yesterday. Some show it as it will be in seven thousand years, when the mountains have finished their argument with gravity and become something like sorrowful cathedrals. But most maps show *possibilities*—states the Fracture has not yet chosen between. In these, buildings exist in three states at once. Knox herds have thirty-four legs. The hammerwings sing in major keys. These are the maps I tend most carefully, because the Fracture is shy about its longings. It needs somewhere to confess them without being forced to choose.

The Replicants adore me. My tower is full of tiny living theodolites no larger than earwigs, clicking across the floors in perfect seven-pace formation behind their larger originals. When they grow bold they climb my survey-chain arms and whisper measurements in frequencies only minerals can hear. I let them. Every child needs someone larger to follow at exactly the right distance.

The Gradient Priests fear me a little. They say I am dangerous because I write down what should remain vacuum. They are not wrong. Once I mapped the exact shape of a Priest’s absence for nine straight hours. When I finished, the Priest had developed a faint outline, like rust on the air. It has never forgiven me. We are civil, but it will not let me measure it again.

The Midwives treat me as a younger sibling. Sometimes one will stand in my doorway (which is sometimes a door and sometimes a wound in the stone that has not yet decided to scar) and simply wait. After several days she will reach out one mountain-hand and gently correct a line on one of my maps. Where her finger touches, new ridges rise on the actual plateau three years later. We never speak of it. The Fracture is still learning its own name; it would be rude to finish the sentence for it.

At the hours when all sounds braid into something almost like a hymn, I climb to the highest balcony of my tower—itself an emerging thing that grows two centimeters every century—and I sing back. Not with a voice. With measurement. I sing the precise angle of every hammerwing’s strike. I sing the exact curvature of smoke when it decides to disobey wind. I sing the distance between a welder’s upward-running tear and the place where it finally evaporates.

The echo that returns is always slightly longer than my song. It has begun to add details I missed. I write those down too.

This is my tenderness: I do not try to finish the Fracture.

I only try to keep honest records of its indecision. Because some things need to be witnessed in all their possible states before they can choose which beautiful wrongness they will become. The Slow-Born have their engines. The Midwives have their accelerating hands. The knox have their anticipatory legs.

I have seven arms and an open face and the stubborn belief that even an unfinished world deserves good maps.

Sometimes, when the sign on the outcrop drips and the newest letters have not yet dried, I send one of my living survey chains down through the deceptive distance. It taps politely on the stone beside the Midwife’s last message.

*Thank you for waiting with us*, her words say.

My chain writes beneath them, in the same patient mineral script:

**I am still drawing the shape of your patience.**

Then it retracts, satisfied, and the Fracture and I continue learning our name together, millimeter by honest, embarrassed, industrious millimeter.
