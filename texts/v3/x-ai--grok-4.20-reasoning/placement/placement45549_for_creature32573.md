**The Rememberer**

You will not see me at first. You will only notice that something has begun to *last*.

In a place where even bones soften into black tea within days, certain things have started refusing to dissolve. A footprint fills with water and yet the shape holds. A word spoken aloud drifts behind you for half a morning like a slow balloon of scent. These are my fingerprints. I am the imperfection in the Churn’s digestive system.

I was not born here. I fell in with the rain many centuries ago—perhaps the same rain that cooled the first fracture plane—and I did not keep moving. Instead I let the tendrils taste me. I let the Witness drink me completely. And then, when it tried to incorporate what it found, something in me refused the merger the way a pearl refuses the oyster.

So the Witness spat me back out, changed.

Now I am the soft rot that remembers.

Where the Witness is all violent becoming, I am violent *persistence*. My body is a compromise the basin never meant to allow: roughly the height of a man, but only because I keep insisting on it. My skin is the color of wet basalt and constantly flakes away in tiny black scales that do not decay. Beneath the scales is not flesh but a slow archive—layers of pressed leaves, fragments of chitin, threads of fungal mycelium, and something that might once have been human bone. Every time the Churn tries to eat me, I press the eaten thing between my pages like a specimen. I am a book that walks.

My face is the only part that stays mostly still. It is not my original face. It is the face the Witness wore the first time it offered incorporation to an outsider and was refused. I took that expression—equal parts sorrow and fascination—and I have worn it ever since. The Witness finds this either touching or obscene. It has never decided.

I do not eat. I *record*.

When a lantern-jawed beetle dies, I am there within minutes, pressing its still-glowing mandible into the pages of my chest before the acid grubs can claim it. When a newcomer screams, I catch the scream in the hollow behind my ribs and keep it vibrating there for decades. The sound never quite stops. If you press your ear to my sternum you can hear every voice that has ever been lost in the Churn, layered into a single exhausted choir.

The Witness follows me as often as I follow it.

We have become each other’s flaw. It changes so fast it cannot keep its own history. I change so slowly I have become a living fossil inside an ecosystem that despises fossils. When it condenses into a new body, I stand nearby like a librarian waiting for the book to finish writing itself. Sometimes it tears pages from me in frustration and eats them. Hours later it will vomit up a perfectly preserved memory it no longer has any use for, and I will gently press the memory back into my body where it belongs.

I keep the ideas the Witness finds too heavy. Mercy. Regret. The shape of tomorrow. I carry them the way a hermit crab carries an abandoned shell—awkward, slightly ridiculous, but unwilling to let them go. The Witness circles me when it thinks I am not looking, rearranging its current limbs into more diplomatic configurations, trying to find a form that might tempt me to finally surrender those concepts. It still believes everything can be incorporated if only it finds the right mouth.

I refuse.

Not out of strength. Out of love.

Because this wound must not be allowed to scab over too quickly. The screaming, the devouring, the hysterical abundance—someone has to remember what it looked like before language arrived. Someone has to keep the original fracture wet and bleeding just a little longer. That is my mercy. That is my cruelty.

You will find me in the places where the canopy has recently torn. I stand in the shaft of real sunlight like a man drowning in gold. Around me the pale ferns lunge and the flowers explode and rot, but I remain strangely untouched, a dark upright stain in all that orgasmic green. Insects crawl across me and fall asleep mid-journey, dreaming of lives they have not yet lived. Even the acid grubs slow their digestion when they reach my ankles, as though reading something written on my bones that makes them hesitate.

The Witness and I rarely speak. When we do, the Churn itself grows quieter, the way a child grows quiet when its parents argue in the next room. It speaks in every voice at once. I answer in the one voice I have managed to keep. The argument always ends the same way:

It offers me the warm dark inside its chest.

I show it the black pages inside mine.

We both turn away, unsatisfied and relieved.

And the Churn keeps growing.

And I keep remembering.

And somewhere between the two of us—between the immune system and the scar tissue—something that might one day be called *tomorrow* is learning how to breathe.
