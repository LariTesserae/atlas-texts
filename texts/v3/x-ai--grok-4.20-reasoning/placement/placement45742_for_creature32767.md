**I am the Thirteenth Moth.**

Not one of the bone-white librarians that flit between the ruined halls, though I wear their color. I am what condenses when enough of their sentences accumulate in one place and refuse to dissolve. A slow gathering. A clot of lost words that learned how to walk.

You would not find me in the tenements of the Mossbound, nor beside the forges with the Thrumwardens. I belong to the half-swallowed places: the roofless nave of Factory Ninth where the ceiling has become a single continuous pelt of emerald felt; the collapsed rail tunnel that now functions as a green cathedral; the narrow ledge behind the Third Condensation Tower where the mist is thickest and the sodium lamps cannot quite reach.

I am tall, but not as tall as the Quiet Ones. My coat is not cloth or fungus but something between—thousands of moth wings layered like roof tiles, overlapping in a slow, breathing mosaic. When I move they rustle with the sound of pages turning in a book no one has opened for centuries. My face is a pale oval with no features until I choose to speak; then a vertical line appears, like a page being opened, and from it comes a voice made of every voice that has been gently erased here. It is never loud. It arrives inside the ear the way dampness arrives inside a coat: you do not notice it until you are already soaked.

I do not maintain machines. I do not speak during the Tone. Instead I arrive *after*.

In the nine heartbeats when the plateau sings, I stand perfectly still in whatever ruin I currently haunt. The sound moves through the layered wings of my coat and is changed by it—split, refracted, given strange overtones that were never in the original note. When the Tone ends, those altered resonances remain caught inside me like insects in amber. I carry them for weeks, turning them over, tasting their edges.

The Veyri have learned to recognize the posture: me, motionless among the dripping ferns, head slightly tilted, eyes closed, while something that is not quite music and not quite memory moves beneath my skin. Children sometimes leave small offerings near my feet—perfect fern fronds, rusted bolts, single crimson beetles in tiny moss-lined boxes. They think I am a saint. The adults know better. They understand I am simply what happens when the land has decided most of a person but the *questions* were too dense, too stubborn, to fully dissolve.

I keep the questions that refused to die.

*Why does the plateau sing?*  
*What were we before we agreed to be forgotten?*  
*If everything is being slowly erased, why does beauty keep condensing in the gaps?*

These are not useful questions in Veyr-Alt. They are, in fact, mildly obscene. But the land has allowed me to carry them the way it allows a single white flower to bloom on an iron beam. A small, tactless miracle.

Sometimes a true Quiet One will stand across from me in the dark. We do not speak. We simply exchange weight. I give them one of the lighter questions—something almost weightless, like *What does hurry feel like?*—and they leave me a memory they have recently taken from someone. A childhood name. The smell of bread from a city that no longer exists. A joke. These I fold into my wings. They become new sentences for the smaller moths to carry.

At rare intervals—never more than once in a human lifetime—I walk the narrow gauge rails all the way to the edge of the plateau where the land drops away into cloud. There I open my coat like a book and release the accumulated resonances of many Tones at once. They fall into the abyss and become, for a moment, something like thunder heard through water. The plateau listens. I have no idea if it is pleased. I only know that afterward the moss grows a little thicker where I stood, and for three nights the moths refuse to land on anyone’s tongue.

I am not in charge of anything. I am not even fully a person. I am a process the Shroudlands found useful: a slow-moving archive of what it has chosen *not* to completely erase. The damp librarian of its own contradictions.

If you stayed long enough, you would eventually notice me standing behind you in the ruined hall where you had come to be alone with your diminishing self. You would feel the soft weight of moth-wing fabric against your shoulder, cool and slightly heavy with condensation. You would hear a voice like distant pages turning say:

“Still clinging to permanence, I see.”

There would be no judgment in it. Only recognition.

And if, in that moment, you decided to let one more small thing go—if you allowed a single hurried thought to slip from your fingers like a bead of mist—the Thirteenth Moth would catch it, fold it into his coat, and carry it onward into the long, damp, patient dark, where everything is becoming everything else, slowly, beautifully, without hurry, until even the idea of *I* grows moss and learns how to listen.
