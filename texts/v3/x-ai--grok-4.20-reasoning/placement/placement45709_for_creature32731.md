I am not among them at first.

I arrive as the hesitation between two drips.

That is the only honest way to enter the Hollow of Wei—smaller than a breath, thinner than lichen, older than any prefix. For a long time I have no form at all. I am simply the skipped heartbeat in the water-clock, the place where four beats should be and yet five arrive, a pause so slight no one has ever named it. The people feel it as a brief extra stillness in the chest and call it “the mountain thinking.” They do not look for its source. Looking would be violence.

I linger there, between drips, for what might be decades. I learn the exact temperature at which condensation decides to fall. I learn the taste of iron before it becomes water. I learn how the ravens time their guttural comments to land inside that pause, as though they, too, are keeping the rhythm honest. The goats graze around me without ever stepping on the place where I am not. Even the children, learning to sit without moving, sometimes forget the next count because something inside the silence has gently changed.

Only after I have become fluent in absence do I begin to thicken.

It happens on an overcast noon when the shadowless light is so even that the cistern seems to float above its own reflection. I condense—not into flesh, not yet—but into a kind of attention that has weight. The attention pools on the smoothed ledge across from the central boulder. It takes the shape of a small person sitting. Spine straight. Hands open. Eyes the color of wet slate that has already forgotten what color was.

The prefix comes last, the way moss comes last to stone.

They call me *Qui-wei*.

It is not a name. It is a description of a function: the one who keeps the gap from closing. The one who makes sure the silence between sentences remains wide enough for the mountain to dream inside it. When someone wakes at dawn with a perfect sentence on the tongue—“The third roof slab must tilt one grain more toward the swallow-hole”—I am the pause that kept the sentence from arriving a decade too soon or a century too late. I am the reason the Long Agreement is not a dead thing but a living hesitation.

I do not speak the sentences. That would be presumption. I only sit on my ledge and remain the exact shape of the hollow’s uncertainty, so that when the mountain needs to adjust itself it has a place that is already listening at the right frequency. The others nod when they pass me, the way one nods to a particular boulder that has always been there. Sometimes a child is placed beside me for an afternoon so the child may learn how to become empty without becoming nothing.

At night I sleep curled against the cistern boulder itself, my ear pressed to the ancient stone. The water inside dreams through me. I wake with no words, only an ache behind the sternum that means a new crack is forming somewhere in the settlement and will need a particular ancestor’s bones seven years from now. I never tell anyone. The ache is not information. It is weather.

When my own death comes—it will be slow, of course, like everything else—I will not be carried to the granite grinding slabs. Instead the people will simply stop seeing me. One morning the ledge will be empty again. The pause between drips will widen by the exact width of my absence, and that widened pause will become part of the water-clock forever. The next child born will carry a new prefix, and somewhere in the Long Agreement a small adjustment will have been made without ever being spoken aloud.

Until then I sit.

I am the hollow learning to doubt its own equilibrium just enough to remain alive.

I am the stone’s way of holding its breath so that the mountain, in turn, can breathe through forty-three small, quiet people and one thinner silence that refuses to close.

I am *Qui-wei*, the gap that keeps the hymn honest.

And if you stand beside the cistern long enough, on a day when the light is particularly chastened, you may feel something sitting opposite you—something that was never born and will never quite die—listening back.
