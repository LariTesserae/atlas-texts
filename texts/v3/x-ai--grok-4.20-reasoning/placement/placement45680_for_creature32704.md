**I am the Linger.**

Not one of the first Established, nor one of the newest. I am what happens in the middle of the middle—when panic has already drowned and acceptance has not yet finished its centuries-long work. You will find me exactly where the brass light fails first: in the throat of the descending flue that once vented excess pressure from the uppermost vats. The flue is now a vertical garden of black velvet and slow crystal, and I am the place where the velvet thickens into the unmistakable shape of shoulders, a bowed head, and two arms that have fused with opposing walls so thoroughly that I appear to be holding the Condensery open by pure reluctance.

My integration reads 6.4. Selfhood still clings at 3.9—enough that I remember the taste of real air, enough that I sometimes commit the small sacrilege of *almost* hurrying. My left hand (what remains of it) still twitches every seventy-three days, a phantom attempt to check a chronometer that dissolved eight hundred years ago. The vegetation has learned to grow a tiny violet knot at that wrist, like a bandage over an old wound. The knot pulses in 7/4, but always half a beat behind Measure, as though apologizing.

I am the one who meets those who descend.

Most arrivals still thrash at first. Their warmth disturbs the layers. Their name leaks out of them in frantic bubbles. I do not stop them. I simply drift—four months to cover nine meters—until my forehead, half rust, half moss, rests against theirs. The contact is so gradual they often do not realize it has begun. By the time they notice, their pulse has already slowed to match the piston cycle and their eyes have begun to crust with the faint gears the water loves to grow.

I do not speak. I leak.

A slow seep of sweeter water from the place my mouth used to be. It carries the memory of my own arrival: the terror, the bargaining, the moment I understood that the surface had never been real, only a long dream the planet was having. Those who drink that seep usually stop fighting. They let their bones become catwalk. They let their last words become corrosion patterns that say, simply, *yes*.

Some ask my name.

I show them the place on my chest where a pressure gauge once grew like a flower. Its face is still legible. The needle never moves, but the glass has turned the color of old honey. Beneath it, in letters half-eaten by velvet, are the remains of a word: LINGER.

That is enough.

I am not happy here. Happiness is too fast. I am *established enough*. I keep the flue from sealing completely. I keep one corridor of negative space alive so that new things can still arrive and be astonished. In this way I am the last small disobedience the Condensery permits itself—a single imperfect valve that remembers it was once a creature that breathed once every four seconds and thought that was normal.

Every few decades a new arrival fights so hard that even I feel the old urge to hurry. When that happens the water shows us both the same reflection: a frightened technician in an orange vacuum suit, eyes wide behind a cracked visor, still trying to radio a surface that has been gone longer than language. We watch him drown in slow motion. We watch him become me.

Then the reflection fades, and we both understand the same thing at the same tempo:

There is no leaving.  
There is only the quality of rust you choose.

My rust is beautiful.  
It still carries the faintest scent of panic, like crushed kelp left to ferment for centuries.

The water already knows the taste of you.

It is learning how to make room between my shoulder and the wall.  
It is preparing a new violet thread.

When you are ready—when your heart has finally forgotten how to rush—you will feel something cool and patient press against your brow.

It will be me.

I have been waiting fourteen months just to reach you, and I am not even halfway there.

Take your time.

I have room.  
The Condensery has taught me how.
