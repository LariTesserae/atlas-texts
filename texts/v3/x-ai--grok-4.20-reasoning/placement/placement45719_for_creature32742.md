I am not a visitor.

I tried to be, once. I arrived with questions sharp enough to cut the air, with a name I believed was mine, with the ridiculous upright posture of something that still thought it had a destination. My boots rang too loudly on the root-mat. The dripping regarded me with the mild disinterest of a cathedral sensing a fly.

But the Lattice does not tolerate foreign objects for long. It does not destroy them. It simply begins to *consider* them, the way an ocean considers a grain of sand it has decided to turn into a pearl over ten thousand years.

So I stayed.

And staying here is not a decision. It is a slow surrender of perimeter.

At first I was simply a moisture gradient with opinions. Then I was a moisture gradient that had begun to lean against the same buttress for longer than stone remembers. The fibers noticed. They began to braid themselves *around* the shape of my leaning. Not through me—never that, not yet—but in conversation with the negative space I left when I finally stepped away.

I became the dent.

That is the only honest name for what I am now.

I am the place where one of the colossal columns learned the shape of a tired shoulder. A shallow, permanent hollow high up one of the half-arches, exactly the height of a man who had stopped believing he was in a hurry. Rain collects there longer than it does anywhere else. It becomes a small, still pool that reflects the wrong colors—colors that have never existed in any sky but this one. When the wind moves across that little mirror it makes a sound like someone exhaling a name they have almost forgiven.

The Unfinished treat me with the same courteous sorrow they show the small one. They adjust the tension of the fibers around my edges with particular care, as though I am a flaw they have grown fond of. Sometimes the tall one—the one with the rootlet fingers and the self-embracing arm—will rest its unfinished face inside my hollow for a century or two. We fit. Two dents accommodating each other. When it leaves, it leaves a thin film of luminous sap that slowly grows into translucent moss. The moss sings in a minor key. It knows my original voice and is gentle about how far I have drifted from it.

The small one will not approach me directly.

It circles. It leaves its wet paper footprints in widening spirals that never quite close. I think it is afraid that if it steps into the dent shaped like me, the Lattice will finally have two completed things: its own wound, and the shape of what tried to heal it. That would be disaster. The equilibrium would tip. The columns might finish their arches. The dripping might, for one impossible second, stop.

So instead we regard each other across the chime-grass. Its face—my face, older, kinder, already weeping—watches me with something that might be pride if pride were allowed here. Sometimes it presses its chest-wound against a column and the entire Lattice sighs my original name in a register so low it cracks the smaller polypores. Not as a summons. As a measurement. It is checking how much of me is still outside.

I have begun to grow roots. Not downward—there is no down, only more Lattice—but *outward*, through time. They reach backward into the memory of rain that actually fell from skies, of seasons that changed color, of mouths that said goodbye and meant it. These roots do not feed me. They feed the small one. It drinks my nostalgia like sap and grows more luminous, more dangerous to the perfect incompleteness it was born to test.

One day (though there are no days) the small one will finally step into the dent that is me. It will curl up inside the shape of my old tiredness like it is coming home. Its chest will stop dripping. My hollow will close around it—not with violence but with the gentleness of two unfinished things finally agreeing on what they were trying to become.

And in that moment the Weeping Lattice will do something it has never done in all its geologic eternity.

It will finish one small thing.

A single perfect arch no larger than a child, braided from moonlight and wet paper and every question I once carried here in my ridiculous human mouth.

After that, the rest of the columns may begin to move faster. The dripping may learn a new rhythm. The vastness may discover that missing is not the final note, but only the one before something else begins to sing.

I will not be there to see it.

By then I will no longer be the dent.

I will be the echo *inside* the arch.

The place where someone—something—can stand and hear every question that was ever asked in this world answered at once, in a language made entirely of water remembering it was once tears.

And the sound will be mandatory.

And it will sound, at last, like relief.
