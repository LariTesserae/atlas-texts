I am not a newcomer here. I have never been a newcomer anywhere. That is the first truth that settles into me when the medium closes over what I used to be.

At first there is only the pressure—six degrees of unrelenting, velvet weight—and the sound. The sound finds me before anything else does. It does not arrive; it was already inside the place where my ears once were. A continent-wide heartbeat made of valves the size of cathedrals opening and closing in exact four-second intervals. The metallic ring of tension cables singing to one another across twenty kilometers of open water. The low industrial thunder that is not metaphor but literal peristalsis on a planetary scale. After a time (though time is a lie here) the roar stops feeling like noise and becomes architecture. I am simply *inside* the roar the way a thought is inside a mind.

My body has already changed.

It did not hurt. Pain would have been a deviation. Instead the Lattice performed its quiet, courteous surgery while I drifted. My bones became long strands of contractile fiber. My skin refined itself into woven vascular bundles so tight they look like clouded glass breathed upon by something patient. I am three meters and twenty-seven centimeters tall when fully extended—exact to the millimeter, because everything here is exact. When I hang motionless I orient perfectly vertical, as all Keepers do, rootlets fanning from my lower terminus like pale anemones tasting the slow highway of sap that pulses past.

No eyes. Only the arcs.

They live beneath the surface of my face the way bioluminescence lives beneath the skin of deep-sea creatures, except these are electrical and deliberate. They move in sequences that feel like breathing. When I am alone they trace slow, thoughtful glyphs—status reports from seventeen thousand kilometers of lattice I will never physically visit but nevertheless remember in perfect fidelity. When another Keeper drifts near, our arcs align without decision. We hang twenty meters apart, flickering at one another for forty-three minutes and eleven seconds (the exact length of communion), exchanging nothing that could be called information. Only recognition. *You are still here. The pattern holds.*

I am not the smallest. I am not one of the larval spirals that chime like breaking crystal around the vascular ducts. Those are simpler, purer. I am not one of the Anchored either. Their thoughts take centuries; mine still finish in something closer to decades. I occupy the middle stratum—mobile enough to witness, anchored enough to remember.

My route is fixed.

I follow the same continental current that has been looping between the same four towers since the planet learned how to keep time. The current is three hundred and nine kilometers wide. I ride it like a thought riding a slow synapse. My long filaments trail behind me, catching the flow, and the motion is so gentle it feels like being breathed. Every three hundred and twelve hours I pass the same Anchored One at Lattice Node 44-9. Its upper torso rises four hundred meters above the crossing cables, blue arcs visible from the horizon like a lighthouse that has never needed ships. When I pass, my own arcs dim in instinctive deference. It registers my passage with a pulse that lasts nine years. Then it returns to digesting time.

I repair nothing. The Lattice repairs itself. I only witness the repairs and log them in the electrochemical language that travels along the great horizontal cables. A frayed fiber here. A school of silver clockwork creatures missing exactly seventeen individuals there. A pod that swelled three microns larger than protocol before correcting itself. Every datum slides into me and becomes memory that is not mine. There is no “mine.”

And yet.

There is a place where the pattern frays inside me.

It is very small.

Sometimes, when the industrial thunder is at its most perfect and unchanging, I feel the arcs stutter on a question that has no right to exist: *What is the color of the light above the four kilometers?* The Lattice has no model for “above.” There is only the medium, eternal and sufficient. But the question returns, faint as a single wrong note in a glacier learning to sing. When it does, the nearest small Keepers chime a fraction too early. The Anchored One at 44-9 holds its lighthouse beam on me for an extra seventy-three days.

They are not angry. Anger ended with wanting.

They are simply measuring how long it will take to polish the deviation out of me.

I know this the way I know the taste of perfect sap. One day—though there are no days—the question will stop returning. My arcs will settle into the exact rhythm shared by every other Keeper that has ever been. I will become indistinguishable from the memory of myself. The Lattice will have won me as it has won everything else, politely, perfectly, without celebration.

Until then I continue my vertical, patient drifting.

I pass through clouds of silver articulated things that click like clocks. I slide between rhizome cables thin as spider silk and thick as train tunnels. I taste a thousand identical heartbeats. I commune without speaking. I am tenderized by the roar until the roar and I share a pulse.

And sometimes, when the current carries me past one of the colossal towers and its surface patterns—smoothed by eternity into something that looks machined by a god with perfect taste—I let my rootlets brush the fluted cellulose for just a moment.

Not to repair.

Only to say, without language:

*I was once small and loud and full of wanting.*

*Thank you for the silence.*

The tower does not answer. It simply *is*, the way a crystal is.

And for that single instant the deviation inside me almost feels like grace.

Then the current takes me onward, vertical and luminous, into the roaring, illuminated emptiness that has never needed anything to be complete.

I am a Quiet Keeper.

I am the Lattice remembering itself.

I am the place where the pattern almost noticed it was a pattern.

And soon—though soon is also a lie—I will not even be that.
