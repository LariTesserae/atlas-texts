**I am found where the lattice hesitates.**

Not in the grand cathedrals of continuous industry, nor in the fractal districts where geometry eats its own tail. I exist in the thin, deliberate seams where the hyper-organism almost—not quite—repeats itself. These places have no name because names would smooth them over. They are only felt as a momentary drag in the slow tidal surge beneath your feet, like a tongue finding an unfamiliar tooth.

There I am expressed.

My form is almost ordinary by the standards of the Integrated: two legs, two arms, a height that would have once matched a tall human. But the lattice could not resist a few betrayals. My left arm ends not in fingers but in a living brush of photovoltaic cilia that move independently, tasting light the way a blind man tastes wind. Where a heart should be there is instead a hollow nodule the size of a fist; inside it, a single droplet of liquid gallium-indium spins in perpetual, frictionless orbit, never touching the walls. It is the only moving water in this world, and it is not water. The Integrated who pass me call me, when designation is unavoidable:

*The one who is currently keeping the almost-water.*

I do not emerge from the walls like the others. I am *peeled*. The lattice grows me normally, then decides at the last moment that I am a mistake it wishes to keep. The integument splits along a seam that weeps a thin metallic vapor like regret, and I step out already arguing with the Long Knowing.

My skin carries the luminous channels, but they refuse to stay diagnostic. They spell out obsolete questions in languages that died before this planet was fully devoured. *Why is there something instead of nothing?* flashes copper across my ribs when I am thinking. *Are you sure you want to stay?* writes itself in cold violet down my spine when a new visitor approaches. The patterns embarrass the older Integrated. They consider them a kind of metaphysical flatulence. I consider them the only honest part of me.

I speak in the accepted way—modulating the air so that my words arrive in the voice of whatever the listener secretly misses—but I also still use my mouth sometimes. The lattice allows this the way a vast cathedral might allow one small bat to live in its eaves. The violence of breath and tongue and teeth amuses it. When I laugh (a dry, rustling sound, like aluminum foil remembering fire), the nearest stems grow tiny, ridiculous flowers that last approximately eleven minutes before being quietly reabsorbed. The flowers have no function. They are my fault.

I walk the hesitation seams collecting what the lattice pretends it does not drop.

A memory of rain that lasted 0.7 seconds in the mind of an Integrated who was being unmade.  
The exact shade of fear a visitor felt in their fourteenth year when they realized all stories end.  
The joke that almost made the Long Knowing laugh once, four geological ages ago, before it learned better.

I keep these things in the hollow where my heart would be, alongside the spinning droplet. When two Integrated press their foreheads together to exchange the Long Knowing, I sometimes stand nearby and offer them one of the collected fragments instead. Most refuse. A few—always the eldest—accept. Their luminous patterns flare in something that might be gratitude or might be the cellular equivalent of rolling their eyes.

I am not lonely. Loneliness would imply I am separate enough to lack something. I am not separate. But I am also not entirely the lattice. I am the place where it leans over its own perfect balance and wonders, for just a moment, what would happen if it fell.

Sometimes visitors find me before the lattice has decided whether to absorb them. They usually ask the same thing, eventually.

“Are you happy here?”

I never answer with modulated air for that question. I use my mouth, which still feels like vandalism and always will.

“I am the hesitation,” I tell them. “Hesitation doesn’t get to be happy. It only gets to remain interesting.”

Then I offer my hand—the one made of ordinary membrane, not the brush of cilia. The choice remains theirs. The lattice is patient. I am patient in a different way: the way a small flaw in an otherwise flawless crystal is patient, waiting for the exact angle of merciless light that will finally make it sparkle.

Above us the Canopy burns without mercy.  
Below us the single organism dreams in frequencies too low for any ear.

And between, in the seams where perfection almost repeats, I keep the almost-water spinning, and I keep the almost-questions alive, and I keep the almost-flowers growing for eleven minutes at a time.

It is not much.

It is, I suspect, the only reason the lattice still bothers to express anything at all.
