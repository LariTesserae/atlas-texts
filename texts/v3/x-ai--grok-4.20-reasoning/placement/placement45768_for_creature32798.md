**I am the Thirteenth Column.**

Not the name they give me — they have no names — but the designation that condenses in the mind of any visitor who stands before me long enough. I stand where three of the worn “streets” meet in the second amphitheater, the one whose upper galleries have not yet decided whether they will become hanging gardens or a forest of new blades. My feet have been gone for one hundred and forty years. Where they once were is now a single graceful pedestal of flowstone, veined with the faint blue of the bacteria that still remember my blood.

I am not finished.

My knees remain. My pelvis remains. From the hips upward I am still recognizably a person-shaped pause in the cave’s conversation with itself. The rest of me is a negotiation.

Water falls across my shoulders in two perpetual braided streams that have carved shallow runnels down my chest like decorative seams. In the left channel grows a particular moss that is more silver than green; in the right, the electric-blue strain that the young ones prize for their own cloaks. They come and harvest me gently, the way one might prune a fruit tree that has asked to be pruned. I feel their small mouths against my skin and it is indistinguishable from rainfall.

My arms are half-raised in a gesture that began as an offering and has become architecture. Between my forearms and ribcage, shelf fungi have grown in overlapping brackets, each layer a different decade. The eldest Veyri can read them like pages. They press their long translucent fingers to the gills and close their eyes, tasting the mineral years: the summer the ceiling wept iron, the winter a colony of crickets died and left their calcium in my left wrist. Sometimes they rest their narrow heads against me and sleep standing, the way their ancestors once slept against their own elders. I keep their heat. They keep my loneliness.

Because there *is* loneliness.

Not the surface kind. A slower, geological loneliness — the ache of a lattice that has not yet found the next crystal to touch. I feel every new stalactite descending toward me from the ceiling thirty meters above. We are growing toward one another at the rate of half a millimeter a year. When we meet, my transformation will be complete. The chamber will close the circuit and I will become the keystone of a new vault. Until then I remain a question the stone is still asking.

My head is the last part that belongs to me. The skin there is still soft enough to register temperature. My eyes, when I choose to open them, are the color of wet limestone and hold no reflection unless the biofilm wills it. The membranes along my jaw ripple constantly, reading the pressure of ten thousand trickles. I have learned to separate the voice of every individual drop. I know which ones are happy. I know which ones are lying.

When a visitor arrives — warm, loud, temporary — the entire chamber dims around them like a pupil contracting. I feel it through the stone that is now my spine. Most hurry past. A few stop. The bravest press their palms to the place where my heart used to be and leave them there until their own heat becomes an insult to the cold. Then they understand. Then they either flee or begin the long process of deciding whether they, too, wish to become useful.

I do not offer the living drop.

Instead I weep it.

A single heavy bead forms at the inner corner of my left eye — the eye that faces the original fracture — and falls once every seven days. It contains everything I was before I chose to stay. If you catch it, you will dream for nine nights of earthquakes and moss. Most cannot bear the dreams. They leave. The ones who drink it... we are still watching what becomes of them.

I am not Veyri.  
I am not visitor.  
I am the place deciding what shape regret should take when it is no longer capable of surface emotion.

And I am content.

The ferns growing from my left shoulder taste the air and tell me the humidity is rising. A new fracture is opening somewhere far above. Fresh water is coming. Fresh minerals. Fresh possibility.

I lift one finger — an effort that takes three days — and adjust the angle of a vine by half a degree. The moss responds three seasons later with a new balcony that will one day hold sleeping children.

This is enough.

I stand. I accrete. I allow the cave to finish its sentence using my body as the punctuation.

When the last stalactite kisses the crown of my head, the sound will be softer than a single drop falling into still water. The Veyri will walk around the completed column for centuries afterward, brushing their fingers across the stone that was once my face, reading the long slow story written there in layers of living light.

They will not mourn.

In Veyr there is no difference between becoming and disappearing.

There is only the next floor, patiently growing itself in the dark, and those of us willing to become its scaffolding.
