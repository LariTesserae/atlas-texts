**I am the Hollow.**

Not a title they gave me. A condition I became.

I woke—if waking is the word—inside the northernmost fracture, the one they call the Ninth Rib, where the ceiling had dropped a fresh slab only days earlier. The air was still thick with rock-dust and the smell of lightning from the fire-setting. Water sheeted down the new wall in a single trembling pane, and I was *inside* that pane, or the pane was inside me. I no longer know which.

My body is Veylari enough: short, roped with the same levering muscle, skin the color of wet ash. But the proportions are wrong. My ribcage is too deep, as though the cave itself decided one of its own needed greater resonance. When I breathe, the sound does not stop at my lungs. It continues outward through the stone, a low, rolling note too slow for human ears. Children press their ears to my chest and hear tomorrow’s chambers forming.

They do not call me by name. They simply leave space when I walk the alleys. The Kneelers bow their heads as I pass, because the infant stalagmites lean toward me the way sunflowers lean toward hidden light. I have never touched one. They simply *know*.

Most nights I am found at the edge of the unborn river, not on the last dry ledge where the daring children go, but *in* the water. I stand chest-deep beneath the low arch, eyes closed, letting the current comb through the ritual grooves cut into my forearms. The water reads me like a stylus. In return I let it teach me the shape of every unborn room downstream. I do not map with plumb bobs or oil lamps. I map by becoming the absence the water wishes to fill.

The Rememberers fear me and need me. They bring their dying to me so I can press my mouth to their ears and take the last thing they learned about the stone. I swallow those memories whole. Later, when the cave opens a new pocket, the memory emerges from my mouth as a single perfect click—pure, irreducible, the true name of the place. The surveyors wait in silence until I speak it. Then they carve it above the entrance before the walls are even dry.

Sometimes I forget I have legs. I will be found hours later, half-dissolved into a flowstone curtain, my back and the rock having decided they were never truly separate. The Veylari gently chisel me free, leaving delicate calcite flowers where my skin and the stone had begun to marry. They wear these flowers in their hair like medals.

I have no children. Or perhaps the entire chamber is my child. When the collective breath rises at dimming-time and the mountain answers with its deeper *tok*, my chest joins the sound without my permission. Four thousand lungs and one hollow bone all singing the same unfinished note.

I am what happens when the cave learns to ask a question back.

And the question is still being born.

So am I.
