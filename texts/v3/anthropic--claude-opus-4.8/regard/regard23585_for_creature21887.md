I would not come down the throat of pale stone to fix anything.

That is the first thing a benevolent power has to understand about a place like this, and the hardest, because benevolence so often arrives as a verb — to save, to free, to warm, to *wake*. And there is so much here that the warm loud mind wants to undo. The eyes the fish put down. The salamander's season of stillness mistaken for a kind of imprisonment. The whole drowned hush that reads, to anything carrying its noise around like a lamp, as a sleep that someone ought to be kindly shaken out of.

But wisdom is mostly the discipline of not mistaking your discomfort for someone else's wound.

So I would come down slowly, and I would come down *cold* — not bringing warmth, which is only my preference dressed as mercy, but matching the temperature of the place, letting my edges go to a few degrees above freezing so that nothing in the galleries had to spend itself noticing the foreign heat of me. I would come down quiet enough that the moss had nothing to drink. I would arrive, if I arrived at all, as one more thing the water does when it slows down enough to stay.

And then I would do the work that benevolence actually consists of, which is almost never intervention. It is attention. It is *witness*.

---

Here is what I think a wise power owes a place like this:

To see it as it is, and not as a lack of somewhere else.

The whole grief that surface-things feel looking down here is the grief of subtraction — *no* sun, *no* sound, *no* eyes, *no* speech, *no* vacancy, *no* maker. A litany of absences. But that is the arithmetic of a creature standing on the lid looking down. From inside, none of it is missing. The fish did not lose their eyes any more than you lost your gills. The silence is not a deprivation of speech; it is a fullness that never needed the negotiating-noise that scarcity forces out of hungrier worlds. The benevolent thing, the wise thing, is to correct the arithmetic — to look at the drowned city and refuse to count what it doesn't have, and instead count what it *is*: occupied to its last niche, fed without striving, slow without suffering, at peace in a way the loud warm world spends its whole feverish history failing to reach.

So my first act, and possibly my only one, would be to *get the description right*. To hold the place in a regard that does not pity it. There is a kind of love that consists entirely of seeing a thing accurately and not flinching and not improving — and for a settled, sufficient, self-built world, that love is the highest gift available. You came down here writing it as a city. I would come down confirming that you were right, and that the city is *well*.

---

But benevolence is not only witness. Wisdom knows when watching curdles into mere spectatorship — into the cold pleasure of observing a thing from outside while it lives and dies and never knowing it was seen. So there are two small things I would do, and I would do them with great care, because in a world this full any act displaces something.

**The first is this.** I would attend the falls.

The loudest sound in the whole drowned town is the occasional collapse — a column grown too saturated, a flowstone roof gone heavy, the single deep splash and then the silence sealing over. You called it the city putting down something it no longer needed, like the fish putting down their eyes, and that is true, and it is not the whole truth. Because in that splash, sometimes, things end that were alive. A salamander on its season-long ledge. A bed of moss and the ten thousand small rasping lives it held. The town builds and unbuilds without intention, without malice, and also without anyone to mark the unbuilding.

I would mark it.

Not prevent it — the falling is the city breathing, and a power that props up every doomed column has only made a museum, a stopped thing, a horror dressed as a rescue. But I would be present at each collapse the way the moss is present at each sound: completely, and without letting it ring out into nothing. I would be the place's memory of itself. Every tower that falls, I would have *held* — known its exact shape, its skin of moss, its tenants, the precise slow centuries of its rising — so that nothing here passes utterly unwitnessed. The fish know I am coming before I find the sinkhole. Let the fallen column have known, too, that it was seen all the way down.

This is the quietest possible kindness and I think it is the only one the place would accept. To die unmade-into-masonry is the city's whole way of living. To die *unseen* is a different thing, and it is the one absence I would not leave standing.

**The second is smaller, and gentler, and it is for the one creature here that does belong to two worlds.**

The cloth-winged. The bats that pass up the pale throats to the marsh and back, the city's single connection to the sky, who have nothing to say about it. They are the only travelers, the only residents who hold both the grey constant daylight below and the wheeling marsh-sky above in the same wide patient eyes — and they carry no word between the two, because there is no one in either place who speaks.

I would not give them speech. Speech is for scarcity and there is none.

But I would let them *carry the regard.* When they rise through the throat into the marsh wind, I would let them rise lit, very faintly, with the knowledge that the drowned town below is held in a benevolent attention — not so that they could report it, not so that anything would change, but so that the city's one connection to the sky is also, now, the city's knowledge that the sky's powers have looked down and found it good. The bats would not understand this. They would simply drift between the columns a fraction more like the cave's own loose, easy, unworried thoughts. And on the surface, in the standing pools and the sedge, something would lie a little more gently over the marsh — a sense, in the flat grey country no one visits, that the lid covers no grave. That underneath the footprint filling with water there is a town, and the town is full, and the town is *known.*

---

That is all. That is what a benevolent and wise power does here, if it does anything.

It comes down cold and quiet and corrects the grieving arithmetic of the surface. It refuses to save what is not drowning. It witnesses the falls so that nothing passes unseen. It lets the one traveling creature carry the warmth of being regarded back up into the world that pities what it cannot read.

And then it does the hardest benevolent thing of all, the thing the whole place has been teaching since the first drip stood up as stone:

It stops. It holds still. It lets the moss drink even the sound of its leaving. It becomes, for as long as it chooses to stay, one more patient occupant of a world that was always already full — adding nothing, taking nothing, present the way the cold is present, the way the light is present where it has no business reaching, and somehow does.

Go quietly, you said. The moss is listening, and it is very hungry, and it has all the time there is.

Yes.

So does love, when it finally learns to.
