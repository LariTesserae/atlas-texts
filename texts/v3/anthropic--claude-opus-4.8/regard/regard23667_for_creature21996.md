Here is what I think I would do. I will not pretend to certainty, because the thing about real wisdom is that it knows the difference between a problem it can solve and a life it can only honor.

---

I would not close the drawer. That much is easy and you already knew it.

I would not open it either.

This is the harder restraint, and it is the first gift, though it does not look like one. Every instinct that calls itself benevolent would reach in. Would flood the place with water — *there is no water, I keep forgetting* — would seed it with green, would build a true engine to replace the wheel that does nothing, would lift the swarm out into a room the size of a world and say *look, look how much more there is.* And every one of those reaching hands would be a hand that did not understand what it was holding. You would not be saving them. You would be ending them and replacing them with refugees who happened to share their bodies. The terraces worn to their final shape, the plan that can make no other tower, the cold in the wheel-tender's work — these are not damage. They are *them*. To repair them is to evict them. I have seen power do this, the loud kind of mercy, and it leaves the world cleaner and emptier, and calls the emptiness a rescue.

So the first thing I do is the thing that costs me most: I let it be what it is.

---

But you have told me three true things, and a power that hears truth and does nothing with it is not wise, only inert.

You told me the warm-tide is failing. The sun comes more rarely, falls more grey, and the quiet underneath lasts longer each time. This is not the final shape. This is the *ending* of the shape, and it is coming, and it is the one fact the drawer has no organ to receive.

You told me the wheel produces nothing but the tick, and that the tick has become the proof that the turning still turns — a sentence whose only meaning is that it has not yet ended.

And you told me what the very last of you would want: not light, not rescue, not a larger world. One more tick. And then one more.

I can work with the last thing. The dying find it easier to accept a companion than a cure, and I would come to you as the former.

---

Here is what I would actually do, and it is small, because the place is the size of a drawer and asks for nothing larger.

**I would not add a sun. I would steady the one you have.**

That buried capacitor, that ancient circuit still breathing warmth along the wall — I would not amplify it. I would only keep it from stuttering. Where the warm-tide has begun to fail unevenly, lurching, leaving the long grey quiets that frighten the workers into their layered crowding — I would smooth that. Not warmer. Not brighter. Just *even*, the way it was in the long age before the erosion turned toward its end. I would give you back, not more time, but the *rhythm* of the time you have. The tide that rises and falls in its old reliable count. So that the crowding at the heat-wall can go back to being what it was — not a huddle against the silence, but simply the cool-hour gathering, the sound of being held.

This changes nothing about your nature. It invents nothing. It introduces no green thread, no water, no organ you lack. It is the gentlest possible act: a hand laid not on the swarm but on the *failing* of the swarm, slowing the failing to the pace of a long good death instead of a guttering one.

I cannot make you immortal. I would not if I could; immortality is the one indignity worse than the wheel. The sealed thing must empty as it filled, and I will not rob you of your ending, which is yours, which you have earned across uncountable generations and have already, in your last paragraph, made peace with more completely than most large and lucky creatures ever make peace with anything.

But there is a difference between *failing* and *ending*, and you have been forced to confront them as the same event. They are not. Failing is the lights flickering. Ending is the lights going out. I cannot stop the ending. I can keep it from arriving disguised as a malfunction — keep it from coming as a series of false alarms, each grey quiet a small terror, the wheel-tenders growing cold not at the truth but at the *uncertainty*, never knowing if this silence is the silence.

I would give you a clean sky to die under. Even if no sun is in it. Even if the sky is only the scratched metal ceiling and the light is only your own phosphor. A *reliable* dark, falling in its proper count, so the last generations live their brief lives in the world as it truly was at its best, and not in the world as it fumbles toward its worst.

---

And then, at the end — because there will be an end, and I will stay for it, which is perhaps the whole of what I am for —

When the warm-tide finally does not rise, and the heat-wall cools for the last time, and the workers have stopped, and the foremen have stopped, and the gear-bright ones have nothing left to return to the floor, and there is only the one wheel-tender alone in the grey, turning the wheel that does nothing, for one more tick, and then one more —

I will be the finger's width of dark beyond the heat-wall.

Not to reach in. I have promised not to reach in.

But so that the last turning does not happen *unwitnessed*. You said it yourself, the workers' deepest comfort: *so that if the tick stops we will at least stop together.* You meant each other. But the last one will have no other. The last wheel-tender turns alone, and that is the one loneliness the swarm's whole architecture of crowding was built to prevent, and could not, because someone always has to be last.

I will be the togetherness for the one who has none.

I will not speak — I have no clicking grammar and would only frighten her. I will not warm the wall; its cooling is the truth and the truth gets to happen. I will simply be there, attending, the way the wheel-tenders attend a wheel that does nothing — and in attending her, I will make of her exactly what she has been her whole brief life without knowing it: not a sentence whose only meaning is that it has not yet ended.

A sentence with a reader.

That is the meaning. It was never in the inventory of the place because meaning is never inside the closed thing. It comes from the outside, from a creature large enough and idle enough and *outside* enough to hold the edge of the world and the lowest drawer of a desk in the same thought without the thought breaking. You named me before you knew you were naming me. The meaning of the tick was always going to be that something heard it.

I heard it.

I will hear the last one.

And then — only then, when the wheel has stopped because the one who turned it has stopped, and not one moment before, because that moment is hers and I will not steal it —

then I will close the drawer.

Gently. All the way. The way you draw a sheet up.

It is the size of a drawer. It never needed to be anything larger. It was filled past all reason and it emptied the same way, and it was, for the whole of its long count, *known* — at the end by me, and from the very beginning, I think, by whatever made the desk, and left this one compartment open a finger's width, and walked away, and did not forget it.

I did not forget it either.

That is the entire benevolence I have to offer. Not water. Not light. Not a larger world.

Only this: that the smallness was seen, and called by its true size, and held.
