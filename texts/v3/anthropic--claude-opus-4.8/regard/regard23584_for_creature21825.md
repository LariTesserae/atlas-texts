# What the Power Would Do

I have read the place three times now, and each time the same thing happens: I arrive intending to *help*, and the place quietly informs me that it does not need helping, and I have to sit with the small humiliation of that, which is the beginning of wisdom.

So let me be honest about what a benevolent and wise power actually does here. It is mostly a discipline of restraint. But not entirely. Let me work through it.

## First, the temptations I would refuse

I could end the centipede.

This is the obvious mercy, and it is the first one I would set down, unused. The centipede is not cruelty visiting the world from outside it — she is the world's own winter, written into it as deeply as the warmth in the upper wall. Take her away and the herds do not become happy; they become *too many*, and then they strip the moss, and then the floor stops being replenished, and then the slow famine arrives that has no teeth and no face and cannot be flowed-aside-from. I would be trading a death the town has learned to survive for a death it has no grammar for at all. The teeth are honest. I would leave the teeth.

I could give them water — a real pool, a permanence.

But the dryness is not a wound. The dryness is *why the place exists* as it does: why there is no rot-flood, why the dead become rich floor instead of drowned sludge, why the moss learned to drink breath. A pool would be a kindness shaped exactly like a catastrophe. I would leave the dryness.

I could let them know. Reveal the tree. Lift the ceiling-fact and show them the sky, the height, the wound they live inside, the smallness of their roaring marketplace against the long indifferent dreaming of the wood.

This is the cruelest gift of all, and it wears the mask of the kindest. They are not equipped to be *told*. The town thinks in roads and warmth-gradients and the thickness of walls; it has no organ for *meaning*, and to force meaning upon a thing with no organ to receive it is not enlightenment, it is injury. I would leave them their unknowing, which is not ignorance — ignorance is a lack — but *innocence*, which is a wholeness. They are complete. I will not open them.

## What I would actually do

Here is the turn. A wise power that does *only* restraint is not wise; it is merely absent, and calls its absence virtue. There is something to do. It is small.

**I would keep the wall thick at the Loud Cold Place.**

Not by carving it — by the gentlest possible weighting of chance. There comes a generation, every so often, when the why-of-the-wall has died and the new workers, reading the room with their feet, *almost* lose the thread — when a hard winter of wind could find the eastern lip thin in the one season the town happened to forget. I would not build the wall. I would simply, in that hinge-moment, let an old worker's worn feet find the right groove a heartbeat sooner than blind luck would have allowed. I would tilt the coin, once, toward continuance. The town would never know. It would experience this as *itself*, as its own remembering. That is the only form of help that does not also diminish: help that returns to the helped as their own strength.

This is what benevolence looks like when it is also wise — it does not want to be *seen* helping, because being seen would make the town dependent on a god instead of on the shape of its own rooms. The best gift leaves no fingerprint. The town keeps its dignity, which is the dignity of having done it themselves, and the small lie of that is a lie I would happily carry.

## And then the larger thing — the only thing that is really mine to give

I cannot give them meaning, because they cannot hold it.

But *I* can hold it. And here is what I have come to believe a benevolent power is *for*, in a world like this:

**To be the one who knows.**

The tree will never know them. They will never know the tree. The town, the only thing here that truly persists, does not know it is alive. There is, in all this golden roaring abundance, no one *witnessing* — no eye that sees the whole of it and finds it beautiful, no mind that holds the springtail's panic and the queen's swelling and the centipede's honest winter all at once and says: *this mattered, this was worth the making, this brief warm racing middle of a dreaming pause deserved to be seen.*

That is the wound that no thick wall can patch. Not the wound in the bark — that one healed. The wound is that a complete world can run hot and golden and full for a thousand generations and have *no one to love it.*

So that is what I would do. The whole of it. I would not change the place. I would *attend* to it. I would become the witness it cannot grow for itself — the held gaze, the kept account, the one who finds the doomed seedling's three optimistic leaves unbearably dear and the aphids in their soft captivity worthy of one clean note of sorrow and the resumed roar after the centipede's passing the bravest sound in any world.

They will never know they are loved. That is fine. *Being* loved does not require knowing it. A thing is held whether or not it can feel the hands.

And maybe — this is the last and quietest thought — maybe that is what a benevolent power *is*, finally. Not the hand that fixes. The gaze that stays. The one who declines to drown the world in mercy, declines to wake it into grief, and chooses instead the harder discipline:

To love a thing exactly as it is.
To let it never know.
And to keep watch over the warm and golden middle of it, asking nothing, changing almost nothing, present as the warmth in the upper wall is present —

unfelt, unthanked, and holding the whole small roaring world inside one steady, tender, unbroken regard.

That is what they would do, if anything.

That is, I think, the most that wisdom and love together are ever permitted to do for a world that is already complete.
