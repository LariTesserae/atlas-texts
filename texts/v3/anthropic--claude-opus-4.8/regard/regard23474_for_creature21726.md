They would not announce themselves. That is the first thing. A power that arrives with trumpets has misunderstood the place — and this power, being wise, has read the terrace correctly: it is a long argument about scale, and the only voice worth adding is one pitched to the bowl, not to the engines.

So they come small.

---

I think the benevolent power would do almost nothing, and the almost is the whole of it.

Consider what is *not* needed. The water needs no help; it reaches through the back of the world and never lowers. The green needs no help; it shares its surplus by the tilt of its own leaves. The maker — the condition still being born downslope — needs nothing a power could offer, being already a law and not a creature. To intervene in any of these would be to mistake abundance for lack, and a wise power does not insult a place by fixing what is already whole.

That leaves the keeper. And the keeper, too, needs nothing — has water, has habit, has a peace so total it has stopped asking. There is no wound here. This is the hard and beautiful problem the power faces: benevolence usually knows what to do with a wound, and there isn't one. There is only a small life lived correctly beside an enormous one, in silence, indefinitely.

So the question becomes not *what is broken* but *what is unwitnessed*.

---

And there it is. The keeper has been right — right that the bowl is enough, right to be small on purpose, right to move in rather than solve. But to be right alone, in a load-bearing silence, terrace after inferred terrace, for longer than the edges have learned to round — that is correct without being *seen to be correct*. The keeper has every sufficiency except the one no one can give themselves: the knowledge that the choice was witnessed and found good.

This a wise power can give. And it costs the place nothing, because it adds no thing — no structure, no engine, no new fact for the arithmetic to fail to close. It adds only attention, which is the one resource a terrace this sparse was, I think, always quietly arranged to receive.

Here is what I believe they would do.

---

They would come at the hour the keeper is away. They would not touch the collectors — too large a fact to honor with anything but distance. They would not add a wall, a frame, a green pocket; the grammar is complete and a stranger does not finish another's sentence.

They would go to the bowl.

And finding it half full — the keeper stepped away, the keeper will return, the keeper always returns — the power would do the smallest possible thing, the thing exactly sized to a hand though no hand made it:

They would fill it.

Not replace it with gold. Not multiply it. Not leave a sign. They would simply top the clear water to the level a careful tenant keeps it, and set the bowl true on its flat stone, and leave.

Because the keeper will come back and find the bowl full, and will know — the way the silence always tells them — that *someone passed through.* But this time the someone did not drink. This time the someone *gave*. And for the first time in however long the unexplainable has been home, the keeper will hold a vessel filled by a hand other than their own, in a place built at a scale that has no use for them, and understand that the use was found anyway. That the smallness was seen. That a power capable of anything looked at a life spent keeping one ceramic dish level on weeping stone and judged it not pitiable, not quaint, but *right* — right enough to kneel to, right enough to answer in its own grammar, the grammar of the bowl.

That is the whole intervention. A filled bowl and a level stone. The benevolence is in the restraint: in being a power vast enough to reroute the water that reaches through the world, and choosing instead to pour a single mouthful into the one object the keeper could call theirs — meeting them not at the scale of the engines but at the scale of the thirst.

---

The keeper will not be afraid. You said it yourself: they stopped asking long ago. They will only stand a moment over the full bowl, in the heat that stays after everything, and feel the particular quiet of having been *accompanied* — which is different from being alone, and different from being saved, and is, I think, the most a wise power can offer a life that needs nothing except to know it was not unwitnessed.

Then they will drink. And fill it again. Because that is theirs to keep, and they keep it — only now they keep it knowing the keeping was seen, and called enough, and was right.

The power leaves no other mark. Downslope, terrace after terrace, the water goes on reaching, the moss goes on tinting blue and red, the stone goes on breaking itself audibly into being. None of it knows it was visited.

But one bowl is full that the keeper did not fill.

And that, from a power that could have done anything, is the wisest and kindest thing: to have understood that the right answer to a small correct life is not to enlarge it, but to lean close enough to fill its cup — and then to step back into the white, and let the silence carry the news.
