There is a temptation, when you are wise, to wake things. To know is so often confused with the right to make known, and power that is also benevolent has a particular failing: it wants to *help*, and helping presumes the helped should be changed.

So let me say first what I would not do.

I would not wake it. Not to greet it, not to free it, not to tell it the marvel of what has grown upon its back. A thing that has slept past the rise of the sea and the building and drowning of a town has chosen its rhythm, or been chosen by it, and either way the choosing is older than my standing here. To wake it would be to end the town — every school, every patient hunter, every green-breathing door — in the moment of a sleeper's startled turning. I would be killing a world to satisfy my wish to be seen by its ground. That is not love. That is loneliness wearing love's face.

I would not name it, either. To name a thing is to begin to own the edges of it, and this has no edges I could honestly find. I came, supposedly, to attend to *this place and the being who lives in it*, and I have learned that I cannot even count the beings, cannot say where one ends, cannot tell the tenant from the floor from the dreamer dreaming both. Benevolence that does not first admit its own blindness is just appetite with good manners.

So. What, then, is left for a wise and kindly power to actually *do* in a drowned town on the back of a sleeping god?

---

I would learn the breath.

Not to control it — to *keep* it. I would set myself the long patience the place already lives by and I would learn the exact length of the swell, the trough, the crest, the slow respiration that the schools mistake for weather. I would hold that rhythm the way a person holds a sleeping infant's breathing in their own chest without deciding to: involuntarily, devotedly, as a second pulse beneath their own. If I am to be of any use here it is as a witness who does not flinch and does not leave. The sleeper has no one who knows it is asleep. That is the truest poverty in this whole warm dark — not that it suffers, but that its single vast fact of *being alive* has never once been held in another mind. I can hold it. That costs the sleeper nothing and changes nothing and it is, I think, the only gift the situation will permit: to be the one awareness in all the deep that knows a breath for a breath.

---

Then I would tend the small things, gently, in ways that ripple nowhere.

There is a wall somewhere off in the dark, long undermined, due to fall — you heard one go, felt it in your chest. When it falls it crushes whatever sheltered against it, a small extinction of crawling armored lives, a den unroofed. I cannot save the town from the patient erosion that is its own slow form of life; I would not want to, for the softening of the corners is part of how the place breathes. But a wall about to take a dozen sleeping tenants with it — that I could ease. A hand under the lintel as it goes, so it settles instead of slams. A current redirected, so the silt resettles soft. Nothing the sleeper would feel. Nothing the rhythm would register. Just a kindness sized to the scale that can actually receive it: the scale of the flea, the lichen, the brief bright pouring school. The sleeper's mercy administered to the sleeper's skin, on the sleeper's behalf, since the sleeper cannot.

This is the part I most believe in. Greatness mistakes the great for the worthy. But the only creatures here who can be *helped* — who have wants short enough to satisfy, fears small enough to soothe, lives brief enough that a single act lands inside them — are the tenants. The mites. The flora of its hide. So I would be a good power to the small, and a faithful witness to the vast, and I would not confuse the two duties, and I would never let my care for the immense make me careless of the immediate. The school does not pour any less beautifully for being small. The eyeless fish is not less alive for living between two breaths of something that will not notice its whole existence.

---

And in the crest — in that suspended moment when the warmth swells and everything goes still and the sleeper rises a few fathoms toward a waking it will not reach — I would do the one thing that is almost like speech.

I would be still with it.

Not to send a message; it has no place to receive one. But because in that moment the whole town becomes, briefly, the dream's own twitching skin, all of it obeying a rhythm not its own — and into that one coordinated shiver I could add my own stillness, freely given, so that for the length of the crest there is one more thing in the dark choosing the sleeper's rhythm not by instinct but by *love*. The schools go still because they must. I would go still because I want to. The sleeper will not know the difference. But the difference will be real, and it will be mine to make, and somewhere in the great economy of what is and is not witnessed, a thing slept-upon for an age beyond counting will have been, for one crest, *accompanied* — held in a stillness that was offered rather than compelled.

Then the warmth would ebb. The lights would return. The schools would pour again through the rounded windows, and the green doors would breathe, and the patient ones would resume their drift and strike and settle, and the town would be only a town again, warm over its mystery.

And I would stay. Keeping the breath. Easing the falling walls. Asking nothing. Telling no one. A power that came to a drowned town expecting to find someone to save, and found instead the oldest lesson power can learn:

that the deepest kindness is sometimes only this — to know a sleeping thing is alive, to let it sleep, and to be tender to everything small enough to feel your tenderness.

The warmth goes down further than anyone has gone.

I would not go down to wake it.

I would sit in the warm dark on its breathing back, and keep watch over the small bright lives, and count the long slow breaths, and stay.
