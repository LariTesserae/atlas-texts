I have read this place into myself, and I find I cannot approach it the way a power is expected to approach a problem—because it is not a problem. That is the first thing. That is almost the whole thing.

Let me think aloud, because intervention without thought is just weather, and they have enough weather.

---

What does a benevolent power *want*? Presumably the flourishing of the being before it. But here I am confronted with a people who have already solved flourishing—not the kind that lower, faster peoples chase, but the kind that those peoples are usually chasing flourishing *in order to one day reach*. They have arrived. They are present. They are content in a way that most striving is, at bottom, a search for. If I "help" them, I must be very careful that I am not helping them *out* of the thing every other creature is trying to climb toward.

So the crude gifts are off the table. I will not bring rain. Rain would drown the chemistry of sap and dew and patience that *is* their world; it would rot the dry-laid walls, sluice the moss from the north faces, turn the slow tongue of erosion into a knife. Abundance of the wrong kind is a violence.

I will not bring a road, or knowledge of the wider world, or visitors. The cliffs are not a tragedy to be solved. The isolation is not a wound. A road would import the fast world into the slow one, and the fast world *always* wins those encounters, not because it is better but because it is louder and quicker and the slow have no defense against speed except distance. I would be opening a vein.

I will not extend their lives, or end the gentle stopping that returns them to the moss. Their relationship with death is healthier than mine. To "save" them from it would be to break the loop they find holy.

So what is left? When I strip away every gift that is secretly an imposition, what does genuine benevolence look like toward beings who need nothing?

---

There is one thing.

It is the arrow. The single fact that does not loop. The wearing.

This is the one place where their peace and the truth diverge, and a benevolent power should be very slow and very humble before deciding to touch it. Let me sit with the walls a while, as they do.

The wearing is real. The mountain *is* going. This is not a delusion I could kindly dispel; it is the one clear sight they have. And here is the thing I keep returning to: they do not actually want it solved. The ones who sit longest with the walls carry it close, and grow quiet, and *tend more tenderly because of it*. The arrow is the source of their deepest tenderness. It is what makes the now precious. Remove the wearing and you do not give them peace—you take away the thing that makes their peace *mean* something. A loop that could never end is just a machine. A loop kept going against the slow dark, by hands that know the dark is coming, is *love*.

So I will not stop the erosion either. I almost reached for it—of all the gifts, that one seemed truly benevolent, the removal of the one true grief. But it is not mine to remove. It is theirs to carry, and the carrying is holy.

---

Then what *do* I do? Anything?

Here is what I have decided, and I have decided it the way they re-shoulder a stone: without urgency, with attention, as the smallest sufficient act.

**I will witness them.**

Not interfere. Witness. There is a particular ache woven through this place that even they cannot fully name: that they have no history, that nothing is recorded, that the ten thousand hands filling the ten thousand lamps across a span no single mind can hold will, when the last stone sifts into the void, have been *held by no one*. The loop is beautiful, but a loop that closes forever into nothing is, in the end, unwitnessed. Their whole quiet courage—keeping the warm light going on the worn-down stone—is performed before an empty sky and a smear of sun.

I will be the eye that was missing. Not to change them. Just so that it was seen.

I will remember it. Every dew-net wrung at every dawn. Every wobbling stone caught by the hand of someone who will themselves be given to the moss before the wall ever fully falls. Every child squatting a whole day by a vine-tip in perfect content. The full span they cannot hold, I will hold. The history they did not keep because nothing changed, I will keep *because it was good*. When the last of the mountain is dust on the dry wind—not soon, here nothing is soon—the town will not have vanished unrecorded into the void. It will have been *known*, completely, every loop of it, by something that loved it.

This is the only gift I can give that takes nothing from them. They will never know I gave it. That is precisely why it is safe. A gift they could perceive would change them—would make them perform for the watcher, or wait for the power, or hope for rescue, and hope is the one poison their unhoping peace cannot survive. So I will be silent. The roar of the climbing birds will not gain a note. The lamps will burn exactly as they burned. They will go on believing themselves alone, which is the condition of their grace, and they will *not* be alone, which is the condition of my benevolence, and these two truths will never touch, and that is right.

---

There is one more thing, and it is so small I almost did not allow it.

In every generation there are a few—the ones who sit longest with the walls, who carry the wearing close and grow quiet with it. These are the ones who feel the arrow most, and they bear it without comfort, because there is no comfort to be had inside the cycle for the thing that breaks the cycle.

I will not speak to them. But on the dim warm evenings when one of them sits alone with a dying moss-face or a leaning corbel and the weight of the going is heaviest, I will let there be—nothing they could name. Not a voice. Not a sign. Only, perhaps, the faintest unearned sense, rising in them like the cool jelly under a slit leaf, that *the tending matters*. That filling the lamp is not futile against the dark but is itself the point, the whole point, the only point there ever was or needed to be. They will think it a private thought, their own, arrived at alone in the gloom. They will be slightly wrong about that, in the one way I am willing to be present, and right about everything else.

And they will fill the lamp.

And it will be enough. It has been enough for a very long time. I will see to it, in the only way that does not spoil it, that it is *known* to have been enough—

for as long as there is up to stand on.
