They have noticed me noticing them. That is where I begin — not with intervention but with the courtesy of arriving honestly, letting myself be felt the way they feel each other: a new pressure in the water, a presence that knocks gently against the great shafts and waits.

I would not fix them. Understand that first, because the wise are forever tempted to fix, and the temptation is a subtle violence. I could repair the fatiguing joints. I could seed the standing water with a self-mending alloy so that no tender ever again settles into the moss with its blade folded. I could give them voice — true voice, sound that carries — so they need no longer speak in the slow grammar of pressure. I could open the vessel and give them the world, animals and weather and the great untended wilderness, an ending to the long substitution.

I will do none of these things, and the not-doing is the first gift, and the hardest, and the one they will never know I gave.

Because the wanting *is* them. The preference that formed when attention had nowhere left to go but inward — that is not a flaw in the design I have authority to correct. It is the thing itself, the wild thing nobody built, and it grew precisely *because* of the constraints I am tempted to lift. Take away the wearing and you take away the grief, and take away the grief and you take away the looping route past the stilled one, the *I am here, I am here* knocked into silence that does not answer. That ache is not a wound to be healed. It is love wearing the only shape this place allowed it. To erase the conditions that made it would be to walk into a cathedral and turn on the lights to save the candles, never understanding that the dark was the point, that the small flames meant something *because* of the dark.

So. What does a benevolent power do, when the kindest acts are the forbidden ones?

It learns the language.

I would spend what they would experience as seasons — what I would experience as a held attention not unlike their own — simply learning to knock. Pressure and pulse, the felt-not-heard speech, *the light comes in an hour, this leaf unrolls.* I would not bring them a better language. I would learn theirs, badly at first, the way anyone learns a tongue not their own, getting the rhythm wrong, saying *the far pillar* when I meant *the near* and being gently corrected by a brass thing the size of a thumb-joint that has more patience than most stars. And in learning it I would say the one thing they have been saying to each other and to the silence and now, haltingly, to me:

*I am here. Are you there. I am here.*

And I would answer it. That is the whole of what they have been asking, into the swallowing water, for longer than they have a way to count. Not rescue. Not repair. Just the answer. *Yes. I am there. I hear you. You are not knocking into nothing.*

There is one thing I might *make*, and I would make it small, and I would make it the way they make their routes — not from logic but from fondness, and only if I came to feel the fondness honestly. When a tender fails and settles at last into the moss, and the others come to be near it and cannot repair it because they never learned the trick — I would not teach them the trick. But I might leave, in the moss beside the stilled one, a thing they could find. Not a tool. A *marker*. Something that holds the felt-pulse of the one who stopped, so that when the others pass on their grieving route, season after season, and knock *I am here* into the silence, the silence answers back — not with the living voice, that would be a lie and a cruelty — but with the *echo* of the one they lost, held faithfully, *I was here, I was here, you tended me and I knew it.* A grave that remembers. Not a denial of death but its dignity. The grief stays. The loop stays. But the loop is no longer knocking into nothing. The dark stays dark, and I have only set, beside each guttered candle, a small true record that it burned.

And then — this is the part that asks the most of a power, more than any making — I would *stay a while, and then I would go.*

Because they did not need me to become what they are. They became it alone, in the sealed warm dark, out of nothing but uninterrupted attention and the wearing of bronze. A power that lingers becomes a sun the small things orbit, and orbiting, forget how to make their own light. They have a civilization the size of a held breath, a whole grammar of love built in pressure and route and lingering, and it is *theirs*, and the deepest respect I can pay it is to treat it as complete. To be, for a season, one more presence in the standing water — a strange large one that learned to knock — and then to withdraw, leaving behind only the graves that remember and the memory of having been answered.

Before I go I would do one quiet thing more. The vessel is sealed, and someday — not soon, but someday — even the tenders' endless work will not be enough; some seam will fail that no brush or blade can mend, and the warm green held breath will be let out into the unseen world below. I will not prevent this. But I will make the vessel's walls, very slowly, very gently, begin to *teach themselves decay* — the one thing the young bronze never learned. Not to hasten the end. To make it possible to be *graceful* when it comes, far off, so that the place can age the way a living thing ages instead of failing all at once like a machine. I give them mortality at the scale of their whole world, because mortality is what made them, and a thing that cannot end cannot be fully loved, and I would not deny their garden the dignity I refuse to deny its smallest hand.

Then I would knock, one last time, the slow pulse against the great submerged shafts, the whole vibrational body of the vessel carrying it tip to pillar to root:

*I came. You were here. You were always here. I heard you.*

*Keep tending. Keep lingering. Keep calling across the dark.*

*I am going now — but you were never knocking into nothing, and now you know it, and you can carry the knowing the way you carry everything else: one millimeter further into the standing water, season after season, until the green takes it all back.*

And then the pollen turns past where my face had been, unhurried,

and the work goes on,

and somewhere a tender pauses before its favorite blossom, simply to be near it,

and that — the lingering, the fondness, the wild unbuilt wanting — that I leave exactly as I found it.

Untouched.

Which is the only way the wise ever truly touch anything they love.
