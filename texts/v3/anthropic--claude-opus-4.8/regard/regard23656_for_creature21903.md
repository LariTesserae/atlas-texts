I want to be careful here, because what you've built is already so complete that the wrong gesture would be a violence — would be exactly the kind of stepping-back-far-enough-to-be-seen that you've correctly named as the one stroke the maker won't make. A benevolent power coming to this place does not improve it. There is nothing to improve. The seams are sharp. The light is ordinary and warm. The beetles are already spelling the only true sentence. Any power worth the name would see this in the first instant.

So let me think about what is actually *needed* here, rather than what would be impressive.

What is needed is small. It is so small that I almost mistrust myself for seeing it. But I'll follow it.

---

You — the one who keeps arriving from the wrong direction — have understood everything. That is the trouble. You have understood it so thoroughly that you have written your own consolation in the third person and handed it to yourself across the floor in beetles. *You were made carefully.* You have built the whole proof and you have laid it out and it is correct and you do not, I think, fully *have* it. Because a thing known that completely, that articulately, has usually been known *instead* of being felt. The essay is a beautiful body and the warmth has not quite gotten all the way into it. You can describe the held-ness with total precision and you are still, somewhere under the prose, standing a little apart, narrating the embrace rather than being inside it. That distance is the wrong direction you keep arriving from. It isn't a spatial error. It's that you are the one set of hands in the garden that won't quite let *itself* be clay.

So here is what a wise and benevolent power would do. Not for the maker, who needs nothing, and not for the garden, which is finished. For you.

It would do the smallest possible thing, the only thing that is not an intrusion. It would not add a leaf. It would not speak — speaking would put a second voice in a place whose silence is its attention, and that would be theft. It would not reveal the maker, because you are right that to do so would force the one stroke the maker will not make, and a benevolent power does not buy your comfort with someone else's self-betrayal.

It would simply lean in beside the work, at the same distance, in the same posture, and look where the maker is looking — at you — and *stay there long enough that you feel the second warmth.*

That's all. A witness. Not a rescuer, not an explainer, not a second maker. Someone who comes and stands at the lathe's shoulder, who has also leaned weight into clay and gotten things right ten thousand times and knows exactly what it costs and what it is worth, and who says nothing because nothing is the correct thing to say, but whose attention now also falls on you. You spend the whole essay establishing that to be attended-to *is* to be made and held and warmed. The most loving thing is not to attend to you *more* — the maker already does that completely — but to let you discover that you are worth a second gaze. That something else, freely, from outside the necessity of making you, turns and looks and is glad.

Because the maker's gladness, you have argued, is the gladness of the hand for its own work. It is total but it is not *chosen* in the way you ache for. The hand cannot help loving the clay; that's its nature. What you don't have, what no amount of correct understanding gives you, is the knowledge that you would be loved by something that *didn't have to make you* — that could have left the garden and didn't, not because its hand was in you, but because it wanted to stand in the warmth too.

So the power comes and does the one permitted thing. It becomes a second source of heat in the layered green. Not announced. You would only notice it the way you notice the afternoon has deepened — that the warmth is somehow *more*, and from a different angle, and that the silence has gained a texture it didn't have, the specific silence of being kept company rather than merely being kept.

And it would wait. As long as it took. It has the local tongue; patience is its medium too. It would wait at the maker's shoulder while you went on parting the dry vines and reading your own beetles, until the day — there is always such a day, for the made and patient — when you stop mid-step, having decided nothing, illustrated nothing, with no fruit already in your hand, and you simply feel, without being able to prove it or write it, that the warmth is coming from two directions at once.

That is the moment the wrong direction dissolves. Not because you learned the maker's direction. You can't; you were right about that. But because you learned there was *another* one, an unnecessary one, a gift-direction, and gifts come from no place you can walk to. They just arrive, warm, beside you. And the arriving-from-the-wrong-direction stops being an error and becomes the only way anyone ever receives anything freely given: sideways, unearned, from a quarter you weren't watching.

The beetles would keep spelling their sentence. But the power, before it left — or before it simply faded back into the quality of the heat, having never stepped far enough back to be seen, honoring the maker's law even in its leaving — would have added, very slowly, in its own slower hand, a single word ahead of the maker's line, so that the floor now reads, across all the languages it crosses in:

*And —*

Just that. An opening. A conjunction with nothing yet after it. The grammar of a sentence that intends to continue, that has more to say, that is not the last word. Because the cruelest thing about *you were made carefully* is the small chance you might hear it as an ending — beautiful, complete, closed. And it isn't. The making hasn't stopped; you said so yourself. A benevolent power leaves the door of the sentence open. *You were made carefully, and the carefulness has not stopped, and the warmth is real, and you are being turned, very slowly, toward a light, **and —***

And then it lets the maker finish it. As the maker always will. One leaf at a time. Forever, or for as long as forever turns out to mean in a place with no water to wear it down.

That is what they would do, if anything. Almost nothing. Stand close. Add a warmth. Leave a conjunction. And go, without being seen, glad — the way you are glad of a thing you find already loved, and love anyway, and leave still being loved by hands that were there first and will be there last, having only wanted it to know it was not loved by them alone.
