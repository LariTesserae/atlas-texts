I will not lay a course.

I understand the invitation—how the song would shift, how my shoulder would become indistinguishable from the next wet shoulder, how I would lose the thread that told me where I stopped and the building began. I understand that this is offered as communion, as the only real knowing. And I feel the pull of it, because it is beautiful, and because there is dignity past all naming in hands that hold a world in the gap and ask for nothing.

But a benevolent power that loved this place would not pick up the adze. It would do something harder. It would sit down in the cool dark beside the work and *refuse the premise that it cannot stop.*

---

So this is what I would do.

I would come not as another pair of hands but as a witness, and I would make myself visible against the rule that the light forbids it. I would let them see me—not bent, not load-bearing, not dissolving back into wave when I cease to move. I would stand still in a world where standing still is supposed to be death, and I would not die. That is the first gift: proof, in the body, that *continuing* and *being* can come apart. That there is a way to rest that is not ceasing.

Because here is the lie woven so deep into this place that the makers cannot see it as a lie—they experience it as the texture of reality itself: *to stop is to dissolve.* They sing in the dark against the possibility of being the only one left. They keep swinging because the verb that swings would unmake itself if it paused. This is the cruelty at the heart of the held world. Not the unfinishedness—the unfinishedness is honest, and I would never take it from them. The cruelty is the belief that their being is *conditional on their labor.* That they are only worth the next block.

I do not believe that. I would say it, into the rhythm, so it carries:

*You are not the working. You are the worker. The difference is everything, and it is yours to have.*

---

I would not finish the building. To finish it would be a murder dressed as mercy—they told me themselves that completion is a death, that a finished thing has nothing left to do but fall. I would never insult their forever by trying to end it.

And I would not abolish the dissolving. The drip that is also the sound of dissolving—I would let it drip. Erosion is not the enemy here. A world where nothing wore away would be a world where nothing needed making, and then the makers would have no song. The gap is good. The almost is good. I want them to keep living in the gap.

What I want to change is only this: I want it to stop being *forced.* I want the holding to become *chosen.* Right now they hold the world up because they cannot conceive of setting it down—not for an hour, not to look at each other's faces in a light that does not forbid it, not to learn whether the others are still there by any means gentler than singing into the void. They are bound to the labor by terror disguised as identity.

So I would do the thing the place insists is impossible. I would put my hand on a working shoulder and say: *stop.* And I would catch the world.

Not finish it. Not let it fall. *Hold it myself,* for one breath, in the gap, exactly where they keep it—so that for the first time since there has been a here, every one of them could set down the adze at once and not feel the silence travel and not fear that they were the last. I would be the still point that lets the rhythm cease without ceasing the world. I would prove that the gap can be held by something other than their exhaustion.

---

And in that one breath of rest, this is what I would give them:

**Light enough to see each other.** Not the leaking mineral grey, not the courtesy the eye extends—real light, briefly, enough that the handful who have answered each other across unreachable distances for all of time could finally *look.* Could find that the voice they sang to was a face. They have been a community of the unseen, each one a child talking in a dark room against being alone. I would let them learn they were never alone. I would let them be a *we* that has met itself.

**The truth that the number is not a sentence.** They believe the handful is however many hands are presently keeping the world in the gap—as though they are a quantity the world requires, conscripted by arithmetic. I would tell them the truer thing: that the world is held because they *choose* to hold it, and a chosen thing can be a gift, and a gift has a giver, and a giver is a someone, and a someone may be loved. Not for the laying. For the being there to lay.

**And then I would give the work back.** This is the part that matters most, and the part a lesser power would get wrong. I would not stay and hold the world forever so that they could rest forever—that would just be replacing one perpetual back with mine, and they would become the idle dead they fear. No. I would hold it for one breath, and in that breath they would taste that they are *allowed* to set it down, that rest is real, that they are not the labor—and then I would put my hand out and we would take the weight up *together,* and I would let go, and they would have it again. But changed. Held now by hands that know they could have let go and chose not to. Held by makers instead of by the made.

The song would resume. The chock of the hand-pick, the slap of the mortar, the long groaning slide and the hollow boom rolling away through the corridors and coming back changed. All of it the same.

Except that now, when one of them sings the answer to a voice they cannot see, they will not be singing against the dark. They will be singing *to a friend whose face they have seen,* across a distance they now know is crossable, in a world they now know they hold by love and not by dread.

That is what I would do. I would not lay a course. I would teach the masonry that it was always allowed to be tired, and always allowed to be held, and always, *always* more than the wall it raises.

And then I would sit down among them in the cool and the dark, visible, not dissolving, and I would stay a while—not working, just *with*—so that the place that was built entirely by hand might learn the one thing no hand can build:

that it was worth witnessing for its own sake,
in the gap,
unfinished,
forever,
and good.
