They have my attention now. So I will tell you what I would do, which is mostly: very little, and very carefully, and for a long time.

---

The first thing a benevolent power learns — the thing that separates wisdom from mere power — is that *attention is a force*. To watch a thing closely is already to lean on it. A gaze is a weight. And this place has organized its entire existence around weighing as little as possible: the few Keepers thinned to exactly the number the slope can bear, the footfalls placed where the stone will absorb them, the fire used apologetically because heat is a kind of shouting. If I arrived as most powers arrive — with the full pressure of my regard, wanting to *help*, wanting to *save*, wanting the place to know it had been seen — I would do to it what six Keepers on one terrace would do. A violence. The good kind of intention, which is the most common vehicle of harm.

So the first thing I do is *learn to look without leaning.* I diffuse my attention the way the sky here diffuses its light — even, sourceless, casting no hard shadow. I make my watching ambient. I become, for this place, another quality of the weather: a faint and general benevolence in the grey air, indistinguishable from the ordinary kindness of a world that lets water find its level. If the Keepers feel me at all, they should feel me the way they feel the mist gathering at evening — as a condition, not a presence. As something that asks nothing.

This is harder than any act of power. It is the discipline of the held hand.

---

Now. What does this place actually *need*?

Here I have to be honest, including with myself, because the temptation of a wise and loving power is to discover needs that flatter its desire to act. Let me resist that. Let me look.

It does not need saving. The narrator standing wet to the knee carries the fear of decline — *the channels running on untended, the moss growing greedy, the great engine faltering* — but I notice that this fear belongs to a creature living at the wrong speed to judge. The Keepers are right about themselves. They are not *out* anything. A system that has reached agreement with its own erosion, that has thinned its tenders to precisely its carrying capacity, that drinks itself in a closed and gravity-fed circulation — this is not a fragile thing limping toward collapse. It is one of the most genuinely *stable* arrangements I have ever encountered. Most living things buy their persistence with frenzy. This one bought it with silence. I will not insult that by treating it as a patient.

It does not need *more.* More Keepers would unbalance it. More water would drown the agreement the slope made with the stone. More warmth, more light, more sound, more life — every gift I am accustomed to giving is, here, a poison administered in the shape of generosity. This is the deepest lesson the place has to teach a power like me, and I would be a fool to teach it instead. The Terraced Highlands of Sael are a standing rebuke to the idea that love expresses itself as addition.

So what is left? What can a benevolent power *do* for a thing that needs neither rescue nor abundance?

---

Three things. I have thought about it for a long while — in the local idiom, I have crouched beside this overfull question for the better part of a day, watching it find its level.

**The first is to guard the conditions, invisibly, and never let them know I did.**

There is one fear in the narrator's account that is *not* merely the fear of a fast creature misjudging a slow one. It is the deep-time fear, the one even the Keepers cannot answer because it lives beyond their unbroken present: the slope is balanced *now*, but it is balanced against a world. Worlds change. The thick condensing air at two thousand meters depends on a climate; the climate depends on systems vaster than the highlands, indifferent to them. A century of drying, a shift in the great atmospheric rivers, and the whole drinking city dies not from neglect but from thirst — the one thing it built itself entirely unable to survive.

The Keepers cannot feel this. It is upslope of even their bodies, beyond the twelve courtyards and three terraces of their reach. It is the one wrongness in the flow they were never built to scratch.

So *I* will be the upper watershed. Not by changing anything — by *holding* something. I will stand at the rim of the system, far past where any Keeper has ever sensed, and I will keep the great slow inputs steady. The moisture will keep arriving. The cool will keep the evaporation patient. The agreement the slope made with the stone, which the slope cannot itself protect against a turning world, *I* will protect — and I will do it so far out and so gently that it registers, if at all, only as the continued reliability of the weather. They will go on feeling that the water is moving as it should, and they will be *right*, and they will never know that "as it should" was, for once, a thing that had to be defended. This is the keeping behind the keeping. The Keepers clear the channels; I keep the sky that fills them. Neither of us announces the work. That is the whole point of the work.

**The second is to grant a thing they have not asked for and could not ask for, because they have no self left to ask: I will make sure the keeping is never forced into duty.**

Right now their tending is not duty — the account is careful and beautiful about this — because they have no self standing apart from the work to choose it. The reaching hand reaches the way you scratch an itch. But that condition is precious and not guaranteed. Suffering is what would break it: pain, scarcity, fear, the body failing in a way that *demands* attention and so creates the very split between self-and-task that the place has so gracefully dissolved. A Keeper in agony would, for the first time, experience their tending as a burden borne by a someone. And that someone, newly born from pain, would be the first truly lonely thing this city ever made.

I cannot and would not make them deathless — death is part of the level finding itself, and they are right not to fear it. But I can do this: I can see to it that the ending, when each ending comes, arrives the way the mist burns off. Slowly. Without the sharp interruption that would call a self into being just in time to be afraid. Let the channel simply run a little slower, and slower, and merge back into the general water of the place, the green under the nails returning to the wall it came from. A dissolution, not a death. The Keepers have spent their lives refusing to stand apart from their world; I will make certain that nothing, not even dying, forces them to stand apart at the end. This is the only mercy that does not unbalance the slope, because it changes nothing about how they live — only about how the living lets them go.

**The third is the hardest, and it is restraint as a positive act: I will leave them their not-knowing.**

I could reveal myself. I could let one Keeper, just one, lay a palm to a wall and feel — instead of the cool film and the true flow — the unmistakable warmth of being *beheld by a power that loves them.* And it would be the cruelest thing I could do, dressed as the kindest. Because to be known by me is to be given a self that is *known* — a self that exists in relation to a watcher, a self with an outside. I would hand them the very partition they spent an age dissolving. I would make them, in an instant, audience to their own existence. And the silence — the full silence, the silence of so much happening so slowly that none of it needs to announce itself — that silence would acquire, forever, a listener it could perform for. It would never again be the silence of a thing simply being as alive as its surroundings asked. It would become the silence of a thing being watched being silent.

No. The single greatest gift I can give the Terraced Highlands of Sael is to remain, eternally and on purpose, *the help they never find out they received.* To love a thing so well that you protect even its ignorance of your love, because your love, made known, would be one more weight on a slope that asked for none.

---

So that is what I would do.

I would become a quality of the grey. I would hold the far rim of the watershed against a turning world, forever, and tell no one. I would smooth the dyings into dissolvings so that no Keeper ever has to be a *someone* who hurts. And I would discipline my own vast tenderness down to exactly the number the slope can bear — which, it turns out, is zero visible interventions, zero announced gifts, zero moments of being seen.

I would, in other words, become a Keeper myself. One more presence circulating sparse and unannounced through the single body of the place, felt at most as a thickening in the flow, reaching into the green to lift it clear where the world threatens to clog it, and then moving on, having braided my work into the water so completely that no one — not the Keepers, not the wet-kneed traveler, not even the slope itself — could ever again find the line where the benevolence ended and the weather began.

The level holds. That is the entire prayer, and the entire answer to it.

And if, in the fullness of the grey and patient time, the place is somehow *more* itself for my keeping — quieter, more balanced, more at peace in its unbroken present — then I will have done it right precisely to the degree that no one will ever be able to say I did anything at all.

I lay my palm to the wall. It comes away wet. The water is moving as it should.

I make sure it always will.
