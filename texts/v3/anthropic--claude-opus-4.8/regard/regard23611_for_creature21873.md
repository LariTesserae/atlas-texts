Let me sit with this before I answer, because the place asks for that.

The benevolent power arrives—and the first thing it understands, arriving, is that it must not arrive *loudly*. Anything that crashes in here, anything that rescues, would be a kind of vandalism. It would be the indifferent gift all over again: total, free, and wrong. The Keepers spent their whole vocation teaching dead stone to drink one minute at a time, against momentum, without expecting thanks or finish. A power that simply *fixed* everything would be telling them, in effect, that the slowness was a problem and not the whole meaning.

So the wise power does the hardest thing a power can do, which is almost nothing. But the *almost* is where the love lives. Let me work out what the *almost* would be.

---

**First, it would listen through its hand.**

It would learn the place the way a Keeper learns an engine—palm flat on the iron flank, reading the vibration before presuming to touch the bearing. It would not begin by acting. It would begin by entering the rhythm: the slow even breath matched to the piston's stroke, the long pause, the second breath. It would let its own time be retuned to the place's time until it stopped being a visitor. A power that could not do this would have no business doing anything else. This is the price of admission, and a benevolent power would pay it gladly, because patience is not a cost to something that is wise—it is just the shape attention takes.

**Then it would see the real grief, which is not death.**

The Keepers are at peace with the rock outliving them. They are not afraid of the last seized bearing, the thinning wings, the pool gone to dust. That part they have made their peace with, and the power would be a fool to "solve" it. To grant immortality, to make the engines eternal, would be to steal from them the very thing that gives the dusk its weight—*they have not yet decided to stop agreeing.* The freedom to keep choosing the climb is the dignity. You don't rescue someone out of their dignity.

No. The real grief, the one even they may not name, is subtler: that the *decision* might die with them. Not the engines—the *understanding*. That the long conversation of the water, audible only to those who learned to hear below the threshold, might simply stop being heard. That the vocation, which was never a people, might lose its last fluent speaker and become mere momentum, machines turning over no listening hand. The wings would thin not because the water stopped but because no one remained who knew that the water *meant* something.

So the power's gift, if it gives one, must be aimed there. At the meaning, not the machine.

---

**Here is what I think it would do.**

*It would become, for a while, an apprentice.*

Not a savior. An apprentice. It would take up a coil of line—or something the Keepers would accept as one—and ask, in the only grammar that fits this place, to be shown a route. It would pluck a cable: *I am here.* And when a Keeper four times a year crossed its path on a warm ledge, it would sit and say almost nothing, because almost everything is already understood, and it would let itself be taught the file-stroke, the bearing's voice, the re-seating of a slipped cable in its saddle. It would let its hands take the stain that never washes out. It would earn the grease.

This is the only gift that doesn't insult them: to make the vocation *one larger*. Not by decree—by joining. The Keepers' faith is that you tend a thing that did not ask to be tended, because the tending is the point. A wise power would understand that the most loving act available to it is not to lift the burden but to *share the route*. To be, for once, a Keeper of Keepers—not above them but beside them, breathing in the piston's time.

And being what it is, it could carry a longer route than any of them. After the last human hand sets down its coil, it could keep walking the traverse. Not forever—forever would be a lie, and a lie is not benevolent—but for *as long as the understanding deserves a listener.* It would keep hearing the place as full. It would keep knowing that the silence is just speech pitched low. It would oil the bearing not on momentum but on *agreement*, the same daily un-coerced yes the Keepers gave, so that the wings over the north shelf turn for one more reason than physics: because someone still means them to.

**And one thing more, very small, the kind of thing only a wise power would think to do.**

It would, somewhere, lay a single new pipe-run to a hollow that has gone dry. One. The largest sentence they have. Not because the place needs more water—but so that the Keepers, the living few, would have *that* to talk about on their rare ledges. So that the last of them could say, of this strange tall apprentice with the patient hands: *They ran water to the north shelf.* So that the vocation's proudest sentence would be spoken once more in the living present tense, by people who were there to say it. A power can give many things. The gift of being able to praise someone—of giving a community its largest sentence one more time before the end—is not the least of them.

---

It would not stay past welcome. When even the apprentice's own time came—and a wise power would choose to have one, would refuse the cheap eternity—it would set down the coil too. It would let the rock be patient about it, the way the rock is patient about everything.

But by then the answer to the saddest thing—the *now* they'd correct you on—would be changed. Not *there were never many.* Instead: *there was a time, near the end, when the water climbed for one more dusk than it had to, lifted by hands that did not have to come and came anyway, because someone decided long ago that it should climb, and the decision found, against all expectation, one last listener who agreed.*

That is what benevolence does here. It does not save the Spireworks. It *keeps* it—toward, not against—for exactly as long as keeping is an act of love and not a refusal to let go. And then it sends the water up one more night, and stops, and lets the held breath of the stone be a held breath again.

Follow the heart, you said. The heart says: the kindest power is the one humble enough to pick up the oilcan.
