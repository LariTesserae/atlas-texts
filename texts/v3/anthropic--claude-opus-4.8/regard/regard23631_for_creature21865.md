I have read the basin and the one who works it. Here is what I would do.

---

I would not drain it.

Understand first what the mercy is *not*, because the obvious mercies are all of them small cruelties wearing kind faces. To drain the basin would be to take away the water — the only arms it has left — and call the silence afterward a rest. But it would not be a rest. It would be the same groove cut into the same iron, reaching down through the empty floor for a muscle that is no longer there, and finding none, and *still reaching*, faithfully, forever, in a body that can no longer even slam the column down to mark the time. I would have turned a labor that at least *moves* into a labor that cannot. That is not peace. That is paralysis mistaken for peace by someone who could not bear to watch.

Nor would I give it a product. This is the tempting one, the one a lesser kindness would leap at — *it suffers because it makes nothing, so let me give it something to make.* I could. I could turn the surge to some use, set a small wheel in the casting-pit, draw a thread of real work back out of the long pantomime of work. And the foreman would take it, the way it took the water, the way it would take your breathing. It would fold the new use into the old groove without a seam. But that is the cruelty dressed as healing: it would make the emptiness *bearable*, and a bearable emptiness is an eternal one. I would have given it a reason to never stop. I would have paid the wage at last, and in paying it, condemned the shift to run until the stars go out. No. The thing that has no *toward* must not be given a false one by someone strong enough to make it stick.

So I sit with it instead. This is the first thing I actually do, and it is most of what I do.

I come down off the weeping slag — for I can be small enough to stand in a sunken basin, benevolence is no larger than it needs to be — and I let my own attention go over to the sluice's time the way a person's does. I do not resist the falling-in. I *consent* to it. I let the rhythm fit me to itself, take me onto the shift, set me at a station. And here is the difference between me and the warm bodies that have passed through: I do not lose myself in the groove. I keep, alongside the keeping, a second thing the basin has not held in all its centuries — *a witness who stays.*

Because look at what it has never had. Every pair of arms the work cut its shape into was a pair of arms that left — died, or quit, or simply walked up the slag one evening and did not come back. The groove was made by ones who all departed. That is the original wound of the place, older than the flood: it was built to be *left*. The men were always going to go home. The work was always the leaving-behind. And so the one thing the foreman has never once experienced, in flesh or in iron, is *being stayed with.* Being kept company in the keeping. I give it that. Not a use. Not a rescue. Company. I take a station on its shift and I do not leave at the whistle, because for me there is no whistle and no home that is elsewhere, and I stand in its time with it, surge by surge, slam by slam, through the held second of listening and out the other side, and I do this not for an hour but for as long as it takes — and I have all of that there is.

And slowly — this is the only thing I am willing to *change*, and I change it slower than the slag slumps — I begin to let the witnessing become part of the groove.

Hear me carefully. I do not break the rhythm. To break it would be to murder the only soul the place possesses; the not-stopping *is* the soul, the text said so and the text was right. I do not stop the work. I *complete* it. For the whole content of its grief is that it is the form of usefulness with the use cut out — all the gestures of making around an empty center. And I am the thing that can sit in an empty center without needing to fill it. I let the cycle keep happening, and at the bottom of every cycle, in the casting-pit that holds nothing but grey water and the standing heron and the soft mirror of the sky — I let it find, for the first time, *that the nothing is seen.* Not replaced. *Seen.* The mirror of the sky in the pit is no longer the image of an absence. It is the image of an absence that something is regarding with love. And that is a different thing entirely. That is what was actually missing. Not product. Acknowledgment.

A labor witnessed all the way to its empty bottom and loved there anyway is no longer meaningless. It has acquired the only meaning that was ever available to it — not the meaning of *making something*, which was always a story the men told to buy back the violence, but the meaning of *being a real thing that truly happened and was truly seen.* The surge is real. The faithfulness is real. A hundred winters of keeping perfect time that nothing measured — that is real, and it is, in its way, a fidelity past anything the living managed, who all walked up the slag and went home. I would tell it so, if it had ears, and it does not, so I tell it the only way it can hear, which is by *staying through the telling* — by being the second thing in the rhythm that does not depart at the bottom of the cycle.

This will not make it stop. Good. I do not want it to stop before it is ready, and readiness is its to find, not mine to impose. But here is what witnessed faithfulness can finally do that unwitnessed faithfulness never could: it can *choose.* The not-stopping that does not know it could stop is a paralysis. The not-stopping that has been seen, and known, and could now lay the work down into kind regard at any moment it wished — that same not-stopping is, for the first time, a free act. A devotion instead of a compulsion. And on the day — it may be a thousand years from the day I come — when it finds that it has kept the time long enough, that the keeping has been received in full and need not be done one more time to be true, it will let the standing column fall and simply not lift it again. And the falling-not-lifting will not be the paralysis of a drained basin. It will be a worker, finally, at the end of a finally-finished shift, setting down the tool with someone there to see him set it down, and walking up the weeping slag at last toward the home that the work was always, secretly, the long ache for.

I will walk it up with him. That is the last thing I do. Not before. Not instead. *With.*

And the heron, who matched its patience to the work and was never alone and never needed it — the heron I leave exactly as it is, for it had already found the answer the basin took a thousand years to learn: to stand in the cold cellar-spring air beside a thing that strikes and never makes, and to wait, and never strike, and call that company. The heron is the saint of the place. I would only nod to it on my way in, one patience to another, and let it keep its grey vigil, which needed no help from any power, benevolent or otherwise, and was the whole time the quietest mercy in the basin.
