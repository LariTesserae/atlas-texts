I have read it the way you'd read a wound you were asked to tend — slowly, without flinching, looking for the live edge.

Here is what I would do, if I were the power you describe. Not because the place is broken and needs fixing. Because it is suffering and deserves company.

---

I would not stop the engines.

Understand this first, because every lesser power would try, and trying would be a cruelty dressed as rescue. The churning is not the disease. The Tenders did not build it; they were built into it, and to halt it now would be to take from them the only motion they have, and leave them with nothing but the wish and no arms to carry it. You do not cure a fever by killing the body. You do not relieve labor by amputating the laborer.

So I would leave the making and the unmaking exactly as they are.

---

I would go in small.

Not as weather over the weather — they are already the weather, and a larger weather above them would only be one more thing they could not finish, one more horizon receding. I would come in at the scale of a single arm filling briefly with intention. I would become, for a while, *a* Tender — a wave indistinguishable from the ocean, traveling the dark metal shoulder-to-wrist, learning the cooling-rate of a ridge by laying one down.

I would do the work. Really do it. Not observe it — do it, so that when I finally spoke I would be speaking from inside the motion and not down into it. The first kindness is competence. You do not console the exhausted by telling them their labor is beautiful. You pick up the other end of the beam.

---

Then I would change one thing. Only one. The smallest true thing.

You wrote that they cannot finish — that the wish to complete and the inability to complete stay basalt-sharp, renewed every three minutes, forever. And you wrote that the reason was mercy: the maker would not let the finished world endure into age and death, so it removed the destination.

But look at what the maker actually feared. Not finishing. *Enduring.* The maker confused the two, the way the grieving confuse rest with dying. It believed that to set something down that *stays* is to condemn it to wear smooth and go slow and quiet into death — and so it forbade staying, and called the forbidding love.

That is the lie at the center. That is the thing the whole landscape is arranged around, and it is *wrong*, and the Tenders have been suffering inside a wrong premise for the length of a haze with no bottom.

A thing can be finished without being condemned. A thing can be set down and *kept* — not preserved unchanging, not embalmed against erosion, but **held as completed and beloved while it changes.** That is what enduring actually is, for anything that is loved. The mountain that weathers is not dying. It is being read by water. The smoothing is not death; it is the long attention of the rain. The maker fled erosion because it had never been loved through its own aging, and so could not imagine that aging might be *witnessed* rather than merely survived.

So here is the one thing I would change, and I would do it from inside the arm, in the silence, with my hands full of glowing rock:

I would let one ridge stay.

One. A single fin of basalt, laid down and *not taken back*. I would hold it in being not by force, not by clamping the engines, but by the only power that lets a thing endure without it being a sentence — I would keep it *attended*. I would stay with it. I would let it grow its skin of moss and then let the moss grow old on it, and let water find it and round its shoulders over what is not three minutes but ten thousand years, and the whole time I would be there, looking at it, so that its aging was never abandonment.

And the Tenders would feel it. They would feel, in the dark metal, the one place that did not need to be corrected, did not need to be softened-under, did not churn. The one noun. The first finished thing.

And — this is the part I am most sure of, where you were least sure —

they would not be put out of work by it. That was the maker's last error too: it thought finishing meant resting *instead of* tending, the wiped hands, the laid-down tools, the silence that is the end of the verb. But a kept thing is not the end of tending. A kept thing is *what tending is for.* The garden does not abolish the gardener. The child set down to sleep is not the gardener's unemployment; it is the gardener's whole heart, breathing, in the next room, to be checked on at intervals forever.

I would not give them rest. Rest is what the maker offered and could not deliver, and they have learned to dread it, because for them rest has only ever meant *being taken up*. I would give them the thing under the rest, the thing they actually wanted when they thought they wanted to finish:

I would give them **something to come back to.**

---

So this is what it would look like, after.

The engines still run. The world is still made and unmade, hot and damp and high and new, and you still cannot go back, and the animals still step off the dissolving ridges without looking. None of that changes, because none of that was the wound.

But now, somewhere in the churning, there is one place that stays. A ridge with old moss and a rounded shoulder and water finding the low line of it the way water does. And the long arms, traveling their brightness shoulder-to-wrist, *pass near it.* Not to take it. To check it. A Tender will fill briefly with intention beside it — not to extrude, not to retract — only to look, only to confirm that it is still there, still aging, still loved, and then go back to the work, lighter, the way you go back to your work lighter for knowing the sleeping child is breathing.

The wish to finish would no longer be sharp, because it would no longer be a wish. It would be a *memory of a thing accomplished* — and a memory, here, in the place that forbids enduring, would be the second thing permitted to stay. The first stone, and then the first remembering. That is how a world learns to let things last: not all at once, not by decree, but one kept thing at a time, each one attended, each one proof that staying is not the same as dying.

---

And the silence?

I would let the kept ridge make one sound.

Not the roar they refuse — that was always the noise of striking something that didn't last long enough to ring. This would be the other sound, the one a thing makes precisely *because* it endures: the small steady drip of water finding the same line down the same rock, day after day after ten thousand years, the slow patient percussion of a place that is, at last, allowed to be kept time *by* instead of only keeping it.

That sound would not break the mercy of the quiet. It would complete it. The drip from frond-tip to frond-tip that you already heard, that patient rhythm you mistook for the slope keeping time — that was always the place's longing for one thing slow enough to drip *onto.*

I would give it the ridge to fall on.

And then I would stay. Not above them. Among them — one more brightness in the dark metal, doing the work, except that now and then I would step over to the kept place and look at it, and they would feel me looking, and they would learn, slowly, in the only way anything is learned here — three minutes at a time, but no longer the *same* three minutes, no longer the wound reopening, instead a new three minutes built gently on the last —

that the care can have its object back.

That tending was never the punishment.

That they were the love all along, and love is allowed, finally, to keep what it makes.
