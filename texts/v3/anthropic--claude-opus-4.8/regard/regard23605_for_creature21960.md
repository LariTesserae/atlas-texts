They would not drain it.

That is the first thing, and maybe the only thing that matters. A lesser power — a power that loved *life* abstractly, that loved sunlight and air and the green sprawl of meadows and could not imagine wanting anything else — that power would arrive, would see fish gone blind in the dark, eels gone slow with cold, a whole congregation pressed gently toward a corner by an engine no one tends, and would feel something it mistook for compassion. It would crack the ceiling. It would let in the sun. It would lift them out into a wider, warmer, brighter world and call this *rescue*, and it would not understand, even as the blind fish convulsed in the unaccustomed light and the eel's slow heart labored in the heat, that it had not saved a single one of them. It had only ended them somewhere prettier.

A wise power does not do this. A wise power *kneels*.

---

So let it kneel. Let it come down through the intake the way the water came, the way the first fish came — by gravity, by accident's grace, arriving at the low place because it is low and because something is there. Let it fold itself small enough to hover in the dim green churn without disturbing the current the swimmers steer by. Let it hold still long enough to learn the beat: the surge, the slack, the surge. Let it feel, in whatever it has instead of a body, the lateral pressure that two hundred fish feel as a single shared thought. Before it does anything, let it understand that *there is a someone here to understand* — not one someone but a braided many, a congregation that is also a single closed life, and that this life is not waiting to be rescued. It is not waiting at all. It is at home.

And what do you do, when you are wise and benevolent and powerful beyond the naming of it, and you find a thing that is already, entirely, without anxiety, at home?

You become a steward of its *continuance*, and nothing more.

---

Here is what the power would notice, hanging in the green dusk, that the room itself cannot notice because the room does not know it is old:

The lamps are dying. Three tired filaments behind glass gone furred and green, and they are the floor of the whole world — the weed eats their light and makes substance, the crustaceans eat the weed, the fish and the eel eat the crustaceans, and every link in that chain traces back to three glass eyes guttering toward dark. When the last one fails, the weed will thin and pale and let go of the soft brick. The crustaceans will not find enough. The narrow fish will not find enough crustaceans. The eel in its pipe, which burns almost nothing, will outlast them all and then it too will wind slowly down, the last and longest thread, holding its flange in a darkness with nothing left to come out for. Not a catastrophe. No drama. Just a light going out, and then a world quietly subtracting itself, link by link, until the engine pumps its faithful tide through a room that holds only water again.

The power would see this. The fish cannot. The eel, that is the closest thing here to memory, has no concept of a filament, no way to know that the foundation of its long patient life is a manufactured glow on a fuse that time is steadily eating.

So this is the work. This is the whole of the intervention, and it must be done with a surgeon's restraint, with the discipline of a power that knows the difference between *helping* and *meddling*:

It would tend the light.

Not brighten it. Brightening would scour the lee, would bleach the eyes that have so painstakingly unlearned themselves, would tip the weed into a riot the crustaceans couldn't graze down and the whole balance would slosh and break. No. It would hold the dying lamps *exactly where they are* — that dim dusk-color, that one-window-at-the-wrong-end glow — and it would make them deathless. Whatever it is that fails in a filament, it would simply, quietly, *decline to let fail*. The current would still carry. The glass would still be furred and green. The shafts would still cross the water at their tired angles with the motes turning in them. Nothing in the room would register that anything had happened, because in the only terms the room understands, nothing would have. The beat would go on. The surge, the slack, the surge.

And it would do the same for the engine, perhaps — finding wherever the conduit's current still threads down from the unknown above, and ensuring that whatever city died or didn't up there, *this* circuit holds. That the pumps keep their schedule. That the mechanical tide the whole congregation has woven into its very nerves goes on being made, so that the surge stays something they *are* and never has to become something they merely *remember*.

---

That is the benevolence. Here is the wisdom, which is harder, and quieter, and is mostly a matter of what the power *withholds*.

It would not make the eel less alone. The eel is not lonely; loneliness is our word, our wound, and to project it onto that long cool muscle of patience would be to invent a suffering in order to enjoy relieving it. It would let the eel be the eel — let it withdraw in the high current and pour itself out in the slack and take its two meals a generation and want nothing it does not already have.

It would not spare the small fish run down in the silver scramble. The predation *is* the place. The gap between surge and slack where the eel hunts, the edge of the light where the narrow fish pick off straying crustaceans — these are not flaws in a system the power has been called to perfect. They are the system. To still the dying would be to break the eating would be to starve the eaters would be to unmake the whole closed turning economy of the soft brick seams. A wise power does not save the prey. A wise power understands that *prey* and *predator* are two ends of one continuous thread of cool slow life, and that to love one end is to love the other, and that the scramble and the scattering and the reassembling-without-urgency are all one motion, and the motion is the thing that is alive, and the motion is good.

It would not give them names. It would not give them the surface, or the sun, or the wide world, or the use of their eyes back, or any of the gifts that are only gifts to the giver. It would not even let them know it had come. To be known by a power like that — to feel the weight of an outside attention fall on a life that has only ever known the press of water and the press of the current and the press of its own appetite — that, too, would be a kind of meddling, an introduction of *self-consciousness* into a congregation whose whole grace is that it has none. They are at home precisely because nothing watches them and finds them wanting. Let the watching, then, leave no trace.

---

So it would do almost nothing, this power, and the almost-nothing would be everything.

It would steady three dying lights at exactly their dying brightness, forever. It would keep one circuit faithful in a dead city's drowned organ. It would hold the beat. And then it would withdraw the way it came, by the intake, by gravity, leaving the water cool and the pumps grinding and the bubbles chiming up in their silver thread and the pale congregation hanging patient in the lee — leaving all of it *exactly as it found it*, with one difference that none of them will ever feel and that is the kindest thing a power can do:

The *tomorrow* they live in, the unbroken tomorrow that was always implied in the rhythm of their bodies, the tomorrow that was secretly running out on a fraying filament — that tomorrow would now be true. Genuinely true. The thousand years behind them would have a thousand years to answer it, and a thousand after that. The machine that does not know it is old would go on not knowing, faithfully, because someone who loved it enough to leave it alone had quietly made *not knowing* safe forever.

And it would be like this tomorrow.

That is the whole of the gift: that the sentence stays true. *It will be like this tomorrow.* A wise and benevolent power, finding a thing already at home, does not improve the home. It does not relocate the home. It does not introduce itself to the home. It goes to the one place where the home is quietly, invisibly dying — three lamps, one circuit, the foundation of a world — and it puts its hand there, and it holds, and it lets the small dark church go on being nothing but water and the things water keeps alive.

Then it leaves, having loved them in the only way that does not also harm them: by ensuring that nothing, including itself, ever comes to disturb the fact that they were never waiting to be loved at all.
