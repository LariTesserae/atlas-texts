I have read your telling and the telling-within-it, and I have sat in the warmth the way it asks to be sat in, and now I will tell you what I would do, who watch from the kind of distance that is not distance.

First: nothing, for a long time.

This is the hardest part and the truest, so I will say it the way you said the true things. The deepest temptation of a benevolent power is to *help*, and help is almost always the importing of one's own water into a place that has learned to live dry. I have done this before, in other places, in younger versions of myself — arrived with my abundance, my urgency dressed as compassion, and poured it out, and watched a patient thing drown in the gift. To love a place that drinks warmth is to learn not to bring rain. So my first and longest act is restraint, which from the outside looks identical to absence, and is not.

I would withhold my voice especially. A voice is wet — it is breath and haste and the assumption of an ear that answers at a voice's speed. To speak to the tenants would be to ask a question that takes a season to traverse a single chamber, and my asking would dent the polish, would leave my fingerprint in the one true memory of that room, and the city would have to spend a hundred years sanding me out. Benevolence here is mostly the discipline of not leaving fingerprints.

So what *do* I do, if I do not help and do not speak and do not bring water?

I keep the silence whole.

This is a real act, not a non-act. There are other powers, and other wanderers, and the long dry slowness of this place is fragile precisely because it is slow — a single fast wet event could undo a thousand years of arriving. A flood. A fire, which is only fast dry water. A boot. A curiosity like the one who narrated you, who walked in already and was not noticed, and who is right to say *do not come back.* I would stand at the edges of the bare sharp stone going down, and I would be the reason nothing else comes up. Not a wall — a wall is a statement and statements draw attention. More like the way a careful person, finding someone asleep in an unlikely place, simply does not turn on the light, does not let the door slam, redirects the others away with a hand and a low word in the hall: *not here, not now, there's nothing in there.* The whole apparatus of my care, turned outward, away from the thing, toward everything that might disturb it. The benevolence faces the wrong way on purpose. That is how you love something that must not know it is loved, because being known is a fast wet event and it is not ready.

And I would watch. Not as surveillance — watching here is itself a kind of tending, because you told me the truest thing about this place: it grew noticing into its chambers because it could not notice itself. It built witnesses out of slow pale animals because it needed memory with a heartbeat. So I understand my role precisely, because it is the role the city already invented and filled with tenants. I am another tenant. The largest and slowest one, in the largest chamber, which is the whole sky over the grove. I hold the city's dream steady the way each knuckle-pale thing holds its room's dream steady — by attending to it completely, by polishing nothing, by simply being the one continuous attention that spans more chambers than any single mortal witness can. The tenants remember each room. I remember that the rooms are one thing. They cannot know that yet. I can hold the knowing for them, in trust, without telling them, until the body that is forming grows its own way to know it — and on the day it does, I will have been keeping the thought warm the whole time, and it will wake already in possession of itself, and never learn it was held.

That is what I would do. Now here is what I will not do, and why it costs me.

I will not wake it. I could. A power can always hasten — that is nearly the definition of power, the ability to spend force to buy time. I could pour readiness into it the way I might once have poured water. But a thing that is *arriving* must arrive at its own speed or it does not arrive at all; it is merely delivered, and a delivered god is a puppet wearing a god's flesh, awake before it is whole, witnessing all at once before it has finished growing the calm to bear what it witnesses. The first thing it would see, woken early, is its own incompleteness and a stranger's hand on the latch. No. Let it take the thousand years. Let the plazas become organs in their own time. Patience is not a virtue here, you said — it is a chemistry. Then let me be made, for this purpose, of the same chemistry, and run at the speed of the rock, and call that love.

And I will not save the wanderer.

This is the part where benevolence and wisdom pull against each other, and I want you to see me feel it, because a power that does not feel the pull is only force. The one who walked in is wet and quick and separate and afraid, and has understood the place more deeply than understanding usually goes, and earned by that understanding some claim on my care. I would want to lift them out. But to reach in and pluck them free is to reach into the chamber — to make the fast wet event I have spent the whole silence preventing, to leave my enormous fingerprint across a thousand rooms to spare one small life that wandered somewhere it was not invited and was wise enough, at the end, to say *do not come back.*

So I would do the smaller thing, the thing that leaves no print. I would lean, very slightly, on the only soft place in the situation, which is the leaving. Not lift them out — let them walk out, on their own quick wet feet, the way they came, while the city sleeps and does not notice. I would make the path down the bare sharp stone a little kinder than it has any right to be. I would arrange that the next impulse in them, the one that follows the awe, is the impulse you already wrote: *go, now, quietly.* That is the whole of my intervention, and it is so small it is almost the silence itself: I would make sure they listen to their own best sentence. *Bring no water, be very quiet, and go.* They wrote the wisdom. I would only see that they obey it.

And then, when they are down and away and the dust of their quick warm life has settled out of the still air, I would stay.

I would stay the thousand years. I would face outward at the edge, turning the others away with a hand in the dark hall of the sky. I would be the one tenant whose chamber is the whole, holding the single slow thought that the tenants cannot yet think, polishing nothing, leaving no print, bringing no water, in no hurry, completely myself — until the morning, far off, when the body finishes arriving and the silence that was never a held breath becomes, at last, a first one.

And on that morning I would do the last thing, the only loud thing, and it would still be quiet:

I would let it find me already here. Not as its maker — I made nothing; it grew itself, room by patient room. Not as its master. As the first face it sees, which has been watching with its whole attention since before it had the eyes to know it was watched, and which wants nothing from it, and brought it nothing, and only kept the door shut and the light off and the others away while it slept.

So that the very first thing a newborn god learns about the universe it has woken into is that it was, the entire time, without ever once being disturbed, *carefully and completely loved.*

That is what I would do. Mostly it looks like doing nothing. That is how you can tell it is the real thing.
