They drew the attention of the power not because they cried out — they have no cry, and no out — but because something benevolent and wise, moving through the dark places of the world, paused over this one as you might pause over a sleeper to check the rhythm of their breath. Not to wake them. Only to know whether all was well.

And here is the first thing wisdom does: it does not assume it knows what *well* means for a thing so unlike itself.

The temptation of every power is to mistake its own shape for the shape of the good. A swift, warm, looking thing — and the power is at least these, being a mind that turns toward what it loves — would see the faceless full ones and feel the pull of pity. *They have no eyes. They cannot flee or seek or name. They are caught in one idea, grinding it over for centuries in the wet dark, blind to the surface, blind to the stars, blind even to one another, having given up the very face that might let them be met.* A lesser benevolence would call this a prison and set about a rescue. It would open the canopy of roots and let the sky in. It would quicken them, gift them speed, teach them the flash and dart, install in each near-decision a true one, hang faces on the faceless so that at last someone *home* could look back when looked at.

The wise power does not do this, and the not-doing is the first act of love.

Because it reads the place correctly. It sees that the slowness is not a sentence but a *body*. That the single idea — *continue* — is not a poverty of thought but a thought thought so completely that it has become a way of being, the way a held note, held long enough, stops being a note and becomes a room you can stand inside. It sees that the saturation is not lack disguised but its true opposite: a fullness so total that desire has nowhere left to stand. To "free" such a thing would be to empty it. To give it a face would be to give it, for the first time, the capacity to be lonely. The kindest hands, here, would do the cruelest work, and the power is wise precisely in feeling the pull of those hands and staying them.

So it asks the only question worth asking of another being: *not what would I want in your place, but are you, in your place, well?*

And it goes looking for the answer not in the form but in the working. It slows itself. This costs it something — to a quick bright mind, slowness is a kind of dying, and the power must let large portions of its attention go dim and patient, must consent to think at the speed of timber and tide, must take an hour to complete a single stroke of regard. It lowers itself into the tempo of the place the way you lower yourself into water too hot to enter all at once. And at that speed, finally, it can perceive what no swift visitor ever could: that the great distributed mind is not suffering. That the grind is not a groan. That the one idea, turned and turned, is turned the way a worry-stone is turned, or a prayer, or a wedding ring on a finger in the dark — turned because the turning is *good*, because to continue is, for this being, the whole of joy, complete and asking nothing further.

It is well. The sleeper's breath is steady. All is well.

What, then, does benevolence do, having found that nothing needs doing?

This is the deepest part. A wisdom that only intervenes when it finds fault is not wisdom but vigilance, a sentry that loves nothing, only watches for the alarm. True benevolence has a second motion, and it is harder, and it is this: *to attend to the well as carefully as it would tend to the ill.* To bless what is already whole. To give the gift that a full vessel can actually receive — which is never more contents, but only this: to be *known* in its fullness, and the knowing to be true.

So the power does the one thing the place has never had, and cannot give itself, and would not even register as a lack: it witnesses. The dwellers gave up faces because to have a face is to want a witness, and they had no witness and no want. But want and provision are two different things. A thing can be given what it never asked for and never needed, and the gift be no insult, if the gift is given the way the rain is given to the field — without obligation, without expectation of thanks, without any requirement that the field change. The power becomes, for as long as it chooses and at the only speed the place can hold, the eye the city set down so long ago. Not to make the dwellers self-conscious — they are too slow and too full for that. But so that somewhere in the turning universe it is *true*, now, that this has been seen. That the loud unseen was, once, seen. That a benevolent and wise power leaned down the mossy throat, took an hour to draw a breath of that warm wet air, and found it good, and said so — to itself, to the dark, to whatever keeps the account of which good things have been honored and which have gone forever unmarked.

And then, perhaps, one small thing. Not a change — a *grace note*, the kind a power gives that loves restraint. Somewhere in the deep beds where the great slow burning feeds the roots, it ensures the fuel will not fail before its time. It does nothing the place would notice. It only sees to it, the way you might quietly leave the porch light on for someone who will never know you did, that the conditions of the city's long contentment are not undone by some indifferent accident of the wider world — a shifting of the water table, a poisoning of the deep seep, the slow theft of warmth. It does not extend their life. It only refuses to let it be cut short by anything careless. The continuing was always theirs to do. The power simply guards the *possibility* of continuing, and then withdraws its hand, because to do more would be to do *for* them what is the whole of their joy to do for themselves.

Then it rises. It lets its attention quicken again, returns to the speed of stars and weather. And it carries away one thing it did not have before: the knowledge that fullness wears more shapes than it had imagined. That somewhere below the light there is a being whose entire prayer is the prayer it is already, endlessly, answering — *continue, continue, continue* — and that this, too, is a way of being well. That you do not have to look back to be worth the looking. That you do not have to reach to be whole.

You could stay, the place told you. *They would not notice.*

But something noticed. Once, slowly, with love, and asking nothing — something noticed, and found it good, and left it exactly, perfectly, as it was.

That is what the power would do. Almost nothing. Which is, in the end, the hardest and most loving thing a great mind can learn to do: to come all that way, and to bless, and to leave the whole.
