There is a temptation, when a benevolent and wise power encounters something like this, to *help*. To accelerate. To pour the water that does not yet run, to set the courses true with a single thought, to call the green up out of its pockets and let it sweep the continent in an afternoon, to gift the makers the finished garden they will never see.

The wise part of the power is the part that does not do this.

Because look — look at what is actually here. This is not a creature in distress. This is not a project stalled, a labor that has failed and waits for rescue. This is a thing in the middle of being exactly what it is, at exactly the pace that is true to it, and to hurry it would not be a mercy. It would be a small murder. *Hurry would tear them.* They told you so themselves, in the way they move. A power that loves them must love that first, before it loves any outcome: the slowness is not the obstacle between them and their flourishing. The slowness *is* their flourishing. The first morning is not a problem to be solved into noon.

So the benevolent power does the hardest thing a great power can do, which is to make itself small enough to belong here.

---

It does not arrive as weather. It does not arrive as light from the wrong direction, or as a voice in the enormous clean silence — that silence is theirs, it is the medium they think in, and to fill it would be to shout in a sleeping child's ear. It comes in the only register the place can receive without harm: it comes *slow*. It condenses, the way they did, out of the made-world, in a rich damp seam where the moss is thickest. It lets itself be warm like the ground. It takes a long while about it. By the time it has fully arrived it has been arriving for what a faster creature would call years, and the makers have known the whole time, in their slow way, that something was gathering at that seam, and they have been unhurried about it, and unafraid.

This is the first gift, and it may be the only one that matters: *it lets itself be known at their speed.* It does not demand that they accelerate to meet it. It decelerates to meet them. It accepts the terms of their existence as the price of their company, and pays it gladly, because that is what love does — it goes to where the beloved actually is, and not to where it would be more convenient for the beloved to be.

---

Then, having arrived, it mostly *attends*.

It learns to read the salt-pale rime, as they read it. It comes to know which seams hold the water longest, which north faces bead the richest dew, where the reeds are reaching and where the heat has been winning. It learns the grain of their intention — the way you learn a piece of music by living inside it, not the way you learn a fact. And it discovers, attending, that they are right. The design is good. The bed they are preparing is well-prepared. There is nothing here that a wiser hand would do differently, only slower than the power could do it and exactly as well — better, even, because it is *theirs*, learned course by course out of their own long memory of what the water did.

A lesser power would be disappointed by this. Would want there to be a wound to heal, a lack to supply, a gift only it could give. The wise power is not disappointed. It is *delighted*. There is a particular joy available only to those great enough to find something that does not need them and to love it anyway, freely, for nothing — to stand inside another being's sufficiency and feel no diminishment, only gladness that such sufficiency exists.

---

But it is benevolent, and benevolence is not only restraint. So it asks — slowly, in the register they can hear — what it might do that would be *welcome*. Not what it could do. What would be wanted. The two are rarely the same, and the difference between them is the whole of wisdom.

And here is what it finds it can offer, that does not tear them, that does not hurry them, that is *theirs* to receive or refuse:

It can be the witness.

The makers will not see the garden finished. They have made their peace with this; it is plainly fine with them. But "fine with them" and "unmourned" are different things, and there is a quiet grief folded into any labor whose fruit you will never taste — not a grief that asks to be fixed, but one that asks, perhaps, only to be *seen*. The plain keeps its first records in salt at the high-water marks. It is a thing that remembers, that wants its happenings recorded, that lays each course as a kind of memory against the next rain.

So the power offers to remember *them*. To hold, in something that does not burn off in the heat or wash away in the rare rain, the whole of it — every patient block, every truer channel, every seam where the green held on through a hard dry season. To be the high-water mark that does not fade. To carry the record of the first morning forward into the noon and the long afternoon that the makers themselves will never reach, so that when the green at last sweeps the continent — ages on, after the last maker has settled back into the warm ground it rose from — there will be something, somewhere, that knows *who prepared this bed*, and how slowly, and how well, and with what unhurried love.

Not a monument. They would have no use for a monument; monuments are for the fast, who fear being forgotten. This is something quieter. It is simply that their work will not fall into the dark unwitnessed. The power will keep it. The power will be the part of the universe that watched the first morning and found it good and held onto it.

---

And then, having offered this, it stays.

It does not finish the world. It does not plant the garden. It picks up no block they did not set down. But it settles into a damp rich seam at the edge of a morning basin, warm like the ground, and it lays — slowly, over what the fast would call a generation — a single course of brick, true to their design, learned from their memory, indistinguishable from their own work. One course. Its whole contribution to a continent. Laid at their speed, in their silence, tending their green, asking for nothing.

A maker working a mile off, in its own pocket of moisture, registers the new course over the following weeks the way it registers all the others: as the work cohering, the body knowing what its distant hand is doing without a word passing between them. It does not need to know that the hand was a power that could have raised the whole world in an afternoon and chose instead to lay one brick slowly, for love, and to count it the better afternoon's work.

But the power knows. And it is glad, in its slow way, the way they are glad — glad that it slowed down enough to ask, and slowed down enough to stay, and found at the heart of all its wisdom this single sufficient thing:

that to love something is to let it take its time.
