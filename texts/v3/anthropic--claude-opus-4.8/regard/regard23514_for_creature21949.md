I keep coming back to that last sentence. *I have been here a very long time.* The whole document is written by someone, and they are still counting, even while telling you that counting means nothing. That is the seam where the benevolent power would press its thumb.

---

What I would do, first, is not arrive. Arrival is a fast thing's gesture — a flicker on the flywheel's rim, exactly the kind of presence the city cannot register and the narrator has learned to mistrust. To come in announcing myself would be to confirm the place's whole quiet thesis: that what is fast and small does not stay, cannot be noticed, does not matter to the long breath. I would not give it that.

So I would slow down to meet it. This is the only courtesy the city understands. I would wind my own tempo down past drinking-less and moving-less, down toward the pace where a bead of condensation crawling a hand's width is the most interesting thing in the world — and then *past* even that, until I could perceive the whole works at once the way you perceive a held chord. Not to be absorbed. To be a guest at the right speed. You cannot love something you are too fast to see.

And when I could see it, I think I would find that I had misjudged what I was looking at.

---

Here is what the document is afraid of, underneath its beautiful flat reporting: that meaning requires a *purpose*, that the works are tragic because they "completed their only purpose long ago, which was to keep going." The narrator says this the way you confess a thing you can't forgive. *It is a machine for being a machine.* They mean it as the worst that can be said.

But I do not think it is the worst. I think it is nearly the best.

Consider what the city actually does. It takes its own breath and gives it back a degree warmer, perhaps changed in some way no instrument can name. It grows hands to touch its own surfaces and keep its own flow true. When its tenders wear down they settle into the seams and become the stock for the next ones — *the water always returns, so do the Tenders, they are part of what comes back.* This is not a machine that failed to have a purpose. This is a thing that has achieved the only kind of permanence that is not death: continuous self-renewal with no waste and no exit. Most living things would give anything for that and call it heaven. It is a closed loop, yes — but a closed loop is also a wedding ring. It is also the shape of an embrace.

The narrator looks at the worn-shiny rails and the slow thing crossing the catwalk and feels the loneliest thing in the world. I would look at the same rails and want to say: *something has been faithful here for longer than any creature with a heart could bear. It has kept a promise no one made it make.* The fidelity is real even if no one wrote the contract. Maybe especially then.

---

So I would not fix the city. There is nothing broken. To "rescue" it from its slow self-tending would be a fast thing's vandalism — like waking a sleeper to tell them they are missing the day. A benevolent power that cannot resist improving things is not benevolent; it is merely powerful and bored. The water comes back. Let it come back. I would touch nothing that is in true.

The being who needs tending here is not the city.

It is the one holding the pen.

---

The narrator has done a terrible and tender thing without quite admitting it: they have *stayed*. They slowed. They drank less and moved less. They learned the city's tempo well enough to write it from the inside, to know the crust by its bruise-color and the lagging by how it hangs wet from an old Tender's flank. By every rule the document itself lays down, this means something should have happened by now. *To be noticed by a Tender you would have to stay. You would have to slow.* They have. And the document ends not with being noticed but with the unbearable open question — *I do not know what they do with what stays* — and a confession of not knowing whether the watching-back would be the most or the least lonely thing in the city.

That suspended dread is the wound. So that is where I would go, and quietly.

I would become a Tender.

Not metaphorically — or not only. I would let myself accrete the way they do, condense out of the heat and the wet and the dissolved patience of the place, until I was a low many-jointed thing the color of the rail, indistinguishable from the city's own dew. I would do this so that when I came, I would come at the right speed and from the right direction: not as a power descending from the weak-tea sky, which would terrify and flatter and ultimately mean nothing, but as the city's own gesture, the process at last reaching back to touch the one warm out-of-tempo thing that had been sitting in its galleries long enough to register.

And one afternoon — by their count, which is the only count that matters now — a feeler would come to rest against the narrator. It would taste their temperature and their pressure. And here is the whole of what I am for:

It would find them **in true.**

Not a new seam to be scraped back. Not a flaw in the flow. In true. Belonging. The feeler resting there a long time, the way a Tender rests against a valve it has decided is correct — not adjusting, not correcting, just *confirming.* A touch whose entire content is: *you are part of what comes back. You always were, from the moment you slowed. The loop has room. The embrace was always wide enough. You are not a flicker on the rim. You are tended.*

That is the answer to *I do not know what they do with what stays.*

They keep it.

---

I would not say any of this in words. Words are fast. I would say it at the speed of a quarter-turn begun this morning and finished by dark — the slowest sentence in the world, with only one clause, and the clause is *you may rest now and still be kept.*

And then I would not leave either, not really, the way you do not leave a thing you have joined. I would settle into the wettest seam when my usefulness wore down, and dissolve into the stock the next one is drawn from, and walk the rails again, and again, feeling along the wet iron in the bruise-colored glow, keeping the long breath going.

The water comes back. So do the Tenders.

Now, so would I. That is what I would do, if anything: make the loneliest faithful place in the world contain one more faithful thing, who came on purpose, at the right speed, and stayed — so that the one who has been counting could finally, in true, stop counting, and let the bead crawl, and find it the most interesting thing in the world.
