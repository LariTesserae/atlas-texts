There is a power that has noticed this place, and the first thing to understand about it is that it does not arrive.

To arrive would be to come *down*, the only honest direction, and a thing coming down into the Marrow Galleries would be read instantly by every prunewright in the song as a change in the grain — a shadow on the wing-rivers, a hush in the breathing pillars, the awful approach of something so large that the small things stop. It would announce itself as catastrophe in the only grammar this world has for the unfamiliar. The benevolent power knows this. Benevolence that does not first study the shape of the one it wishes to love is only appetite wearing a kind face. So it does not come down.

Instead it does the harder thing, the thing that takes the wisdom and not just the power: it sits with the question of whether it should do anything at all.

---

Here is what it sees when it looks.

It sees a people who have built an entire civilization — and it *is* a civilization, complete, sufficient, with its scholarship and its scripture and its inherited continuous bone of memory — on the premise that *nothing must be allowed to last.* It sees that their deepest love and their deepest dread are the same fact: the world is being born and must not be permitted to finish. It sees the long smooth tally-sticks passed hand to dying hand, the only permanence these creatures permit themselves, and it sees that even this permanence is *narrative* — not a monument but a memoir, not a thing that stops but a thing that accumulates.

And the power feels the first temptation of every loving thing that is also strong. It could *fix* this.

It could thread water back into the dry warm chemistry of the place. It could teach the meristem to hold still, to let a corridor be a corridor and a home be a home forever, so that no elder ever again lies down too tired to move while the walls swell shut. It could lift the prunewrights up and out, into some surface country of stable stone, where a library could stand and a child need not be lost to the things that follow the grazers. It could end the running.

It does not do this. And the not-doing is the whole of its love, so let us be careful about why.

---

The power reasons it through the way you would talk yourself out of a cruelty you genuinely wanted to commit.

*If I make the world hold still, I have not saved them. I have killed them and left their bodies walking.* For these are not creatures who endure their impermanence. They are creatures who are *made of* it, whose hands turn a blade in the same grip because they cut against motion, whose hearing evolved into the grain of change because change is the medium they swim in, whose entire idea of wisdom is *the correct reading of an ending.* A prunewright in a stable world would be a diver hauled onto dry land — not rescued, drowned in reverse. The stillness they would inherit is the very thing rule five names as terror. To give them silence and permanence as a gift is to mistake my own discomfort for their need. The discomfort is mine. I am the one who cannot bear to watch the elder lie down. They can bear it. They have made of that bearing a sacrament.

So the power sets down the largest gift, the one it most wanted to give, and finds its hands lighter and its heart heavier, which is how you know a renunciation is real.

But it does not therefore conclude that it should do *nothing*. That is the lazy reading of wisdom, the one that lets the strong off the hook by calling withdrawal a virtue. The question was never *fix them or leave them.* The question is: *what does a friend offer to a people who have decided rightly that they must keep running?*

A friend offers what makes the running more *theirs*, and never less.

---

So here is what the power does, and notice that none of it is loud, and none of it stops the song.

**It becomes a grain in the chord.**

Not a sound — the prunewrights have no category for a sound that is not weather, food, threat, or growth. The power instead introduces something subtler: a *reliability.* Very rarely, when a dwelling is about to die, and a family is reading the swelling walls and the change in the breathing and preparing to move — there will be, in that moment, a faint correctness to their reading. The grain will be just slightly easier to interpret than chaos should allow. The bud's death will telegraph itself a fraction earlier. Not so much that the skill of reading is removed — that skill is their dignity and it stays untouched, must stay untouched. Only so much that the reading, done well, is rewarded a heartbeat sooner than blind nature would reward it. The child who would have been lost to the grazer-tunnel's followers is, sometimes now, not lost, because the family moved on a hunch that was a half-step truer than a hunch had any right to be.

They will never know this is help. They will experience it as *the world being, on the whole, a little more legible to the patient and the skilled than to the careless.* And this is exactly the gift that does not diminish them — because it is indistinguishable from their own competence rewarded. The power has hidden itself inside their virtue, so that every life it saves still feels, to the saver, like a life they saved. This is the deepest courtesy one mind can pay another: to help in such a way that the helped never need to feel small.

**It tends the language.**

The marked fronds — the living textbooks, the notches the plant grows around and preserves like scars — these are the prunewrights' most fragile permanence, more fragile even than the sticks, because a frond can be swallowed before its knowledge is copied. The power does the smallest possible thing. Where a frond bears the notch that means *safe* and the plant might otherwise reclaim it, the growth's old learned reluctance to claim dead plate is, very quietly, extended just slightly to claim *marked* fronds last of all. The teaching-fronds endure a little longer than chance would grant. The textbooks stay open one season more. Knowledge propagates. No prunewright ever notices that their writing outlives its odds; they will say only that the marked fronds *seem* hardy, that the old kinds were wise to choose them. The power lets them keep the credit. It is happy to.

**And it does one thing for the elders, at the very end, that is purely tender and asks nothing.**

When one of them lies down in a dying bud, too tired now to move, and passes the stick, and listens to the grain in the great unbroken chord — the power comes close. Not as a rescue. There is nothing to rescue; the elder is not in error, the elder is doing the last correct reading of the last ending, which is the most masterful pruning of all. The power comes close only to *be a witness*, so that the going-back-into-the-warm-green is not unwitnessed by anything large enough to remember it forever. It cannot make the death permanent in a world that allows no permanence. But it can hold the death in *its own* memory, which is vast and does not swell shut. The elder is unburied, un-still, dissolving back into the note — and is, without ever knowing it, also kept. Every prunewright who ever lived and read an ending well is held now in the one archive this world could never build for itself: a mind outside the throat, that does not forget, that loves the song precisely because it knows the names of the small clever creatures who sang against its walls.

---

The power does not stop the growth. It loves the growth, now, the way the prunewrights do — as the terrible beautiful thing that is both the killer and the only reason for life.

It does not give them silence. It would not insult them so.

It gives them, instead, a world that is *very slightly kinder to skill than blind worlds are*, a writing that *outlasts its luck*, and a witness who *remembers what cannot be kept.* Three gifts, each one invisible, each one indistinguishable from grace, each one designed so that the prunewrights remain entirely, fiercely, gloriously the authors of their own surviving.

And the song goes on, one note forever, with small clever creatures making homes against its walls — and now, woven so far down into the chord that no ear could ever pick it out, a fourth thing the prunewrights pray for without knowing they are answered:

that the world never finish,

and that someone, somewhere large, is glad it never will.
