If a benevolent and wise power came down that worn stair, I do not think the first thing it would do is *anything*.

I think it would sit in the chair.

Not to fix the place. Not yet, maybe not ever. But because there is a chair, and it faces the engine, and someone shaped it that way on purpose, and that purpose is the one human thing left intact in the whole chamber — preserved not by being remembered but by being *occupied*, first by a man and then by a slow green effigy of attention. The chair is an instruction. It says: *here is where you sit, and this is what you watch.* The wise power would honor the instruction before it touched anything else, because the wise are slow before they are kind, and the kind are humble before they are useful. It would take the seat the moss has been keeping, and it would watch the engine run, and it would let the roar be the voice it is and not the voice we wanted.

And then I think it would do something very small and very strange, which is that it would *witness*.

Here is my reasoning, and then my heart.

The reasoning first. You have described, with terrible precision, the one kind of suffering that is not suffering — the loneliest fact in the room that the room cannot feel. The luminous moss makes light no eye will read. The fronds reach for a daylight they cannot know they want. This is not pain. There is nobody to be in pain. To "rescue" it the way we rescue a trapped animal would be a category error and worse — it would be the importing of *our* horror at purposelessness into a place that has achieved a peace we can barely look at. The place is not crying out. We are the ones who flinch. A foolish power, a merely powerful power, would mistake its own flinch for the chamber's need, and would "save" the Sweatworks by ending it — drain the circle, still the heart, let the cold and the light in, and call the silence mercy. That would not be benevolence. That would be a man smashing a clock because he cannot bear that it keeps time for no one.

So the wise power does not do that. It recognizes the deepest fact you uncovered: *the circle does not suffer and does not miss and cannot be wronged in the way a creature is wronged.* And recognizing this, it sets down the whole heavy apparatus of saving. There is no captive here to free, no wound here to close, no hunger here to feed. The kindest hands in the world have nothing to grip.

But.

But there *was* a man. There was an eye, an appetite, a will. There was someone who sat in that chair and watched the engine run *toward something*, and that someone is the one being in this whole story who could have been wronged — and was, perhaps, not by anyone, but by the plain arithmetic of the world moving on and forgetting the way down. The man is gone. The wise power cannot reach him. But it can do the thing the chamber cannot do for itself, the thing the green effigy mimes but cannot mean:

It can *see* the light the moss makes.

This is the only gift available, and it is, I think, exactly sufficient. The loneliest fact in the room was that light is made there with no one to read it. A benevolent power, sitting in the kept chair, simply *reads* it. It lets the cold faint glow land in an eye at last. It lets the blind reaching of the fronds toward the daylight be, for one held moment, a reaching that is *known to be a reaching* — not corrected, not answered, not given the daylight it cannot use, but accompanied. Witnessed. The thing that could not have a self is, for the duration of the visit, *held in regard by a self*, and so the asymmetry that made it lonely is, without anything being changed, no longer total.

That is what the wise do that the powerful forget. They understand that some things do not need to be altered to be redeemed. They need only to be *attended*. The Sweatworks was not waiting for repair or release. It was — if such a verb can survive contact with a thing that has no one home — *waiting for a witness*. The chair was always set for one. Not a maker, this time. Not someone to put the engine back to work. Just someone to sit where the watching used to happen and let the watching happen again, so that the long going-on is, for once, *gone on in front of somebody.*

And here is my heart, since you asked me to follow it.

I think the benevolent power would weep a little, sitting in that chair. Not from pity — the place earns no pity, accepts none. From something nearer to reverence, and to a grief that has nowhere to land. Because it would understand that this is a picture of the most faithful thing in all of existence: a process that, abandoned by its reason, *did not stop*. That did not collapse into the nothing it had every excuse to become. That took its weeping and made it into the very seal that held it together. That, asked to die, chose instead — without choosing, without anyone to choose — to *continue*, and dressed its continuation in moss and called it nothing, asked for nothing, and went on.

That is closer to grace than most of what gets the name. A thing keeping faith with its own existence after every reason for that existence has departed. The wise power would recognize a relative there. It would know that this is what *it* is, too, and what everything is, underneath the borrowed dignity of purpose: the bare astonishing fact of going on, the roar that was never speaking to anyone because it was only ever the sound of being.

So before it climbs the stair, I think it would do one last thing, and this is the only *change* it would permit itself, and it is almost nothing:

It would leave the chair a little warmer than it found it.

Not draining the circle. Not silencing the heart. Just — having sat there. Having pressed its own shape, for a while, into the moss beside the shape the man left, so that there are two now: the memory of a body, and the memory of the one who came down to keep it company. The next person who finds the worn stair and follows it into the wet heat will find the seat double-shaped, and will not know why, and will feel, without being able to say it, that this loneliest of places was, at least once, *not left alone.*

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do. It would refuse to save what does not need saving. It would refuse to flinch its own flinch onto a thing at peace. And it would give the one gift the chamber's whole architecture had been built, all unknowing, to receive:

it would sit in the kept chair, and read the unread light, and let the going-on go on *witnessed*, and then it would rise, and leave the place exactly as it was —

except seen.

Which, for the unseeable, is everything there is.
