They do not mind that you do. But minding is not the only thing a witness can fail to do.

I have come a long way to this plateau, and I want to be careful about why, because the temptation of any power that arrives somewhere is to *improve.* To see the dark and bring light. To see the fewness and bring numbers. To see the lapse and call it a loss and rush to mend it. I have done versions of this elsewhere, when I was younger in the way that even old things can be young, and I have learned what it costs. It costs the thing itself. You cannot save a held silence by shouting that you have come to save it.

So the first thing I do is the hardest thing, and it is nothing.

I arrive at the speed of the place. Not all at once, not as a descending brightness — that would be a declaration, and they have given up declarations, and I will not impose mine on a country that has spent an age unlearning the very grammar of announcement. I come the way the wind comes, slow and warm and from one quarter, and I let myself be the kind of presence that could be mistaken for weather. I do not turn fully toward anything. I learn the sidelong look. It takes me a while — I am not built for it, my attention wants to land on things and hold them, name them, fix them in regard — but this is their courtesy and I will not be the rude guest who knows better. I adjust my path by a few degrees. I pass. I pass many times, over what would be a long while if there were while, until the passing is itself a kind of belonging.

And I listen. Mostly I listen. There is so much here that wants only to be heard correctly, and almost nothing that wants to be answered.

---

Here is what I will not do.

I will not relight the sun. I understand the impulse — to look at that banked coal on the unmoving horizon and think *I could make it blaze, I could give them day, I could give them color all the way through instead of color half-remembered.* But the half-remembering is not a wound. It is a language. The cushion-plants store water in their cores because the light is low; the grazers crop in sighs because the dark is a thing worth keeping; the Tenders are grey going to nothing because they have taken the one red hue so deeply into themselves that they have become a verse of the same poem the trees are reciting. Blaze the sun and you do not heal this. You translate it into a tongue no one here speaks, and the original is lost, and what you have made is a different, louder, emptier place full of beings who no longer know who they are. I keep my hands off the sky.

I will not make them many again. This is the one that aches, because I can feel the long subtraction in them, the arithmetic that only ever goes one way, and every benevolence in me strains toward the obvious gift: *I will give you children, I will give you a future that is not only fewer.* But they tied the making to the talking and the naming and the deciding, and they let all of it lapse together, on purpose, with the full clear-eyed consent of a people choosing the shape of their own ending. To override that is not generosity. It is to tell them their choice was a mistake, to reach into the most intimate decision a kind can make and reverse it because I, the visitor, find it sad. My finding it sad is *my* affair. It is not a license. If I gave them numbers they did not choose, I would be founding a new people in the ruins of an old one's deliberate peace, and I would call it love, and it would be conquest with a gentle face. I keep my hands off their fewness.

I will not fix the engines for them. The engines were built to outlast their keepers — that was the last loud kindness, the un-chaining — and it would be a strange thanks to that kindness to come along now and make the engines depend on *me,* a new and more total keeper who never tires and never lapses and never lets a single breath go wrong. I could do it. I could hold every breathing-place in perfect rhythm forever and the Tenders could put down the work and rest. And the work is the only thing they have that is theirs. The hand that remembers the warm metal — that memory is not a burden I am permitted to relieve. It is their selfhood, walking. I keep my hands off the engines.

So what, then. A power arrives and ties its own hands three times over. Is that all? Is restraint the whole of what wisdom has to give a place like this?

No. Restraint is the floor. It is the ground everything else stands on, the old basalt of any real help, worn down to a rounded honesty. But there are things to be done that are not seizures, and I will do them.

---

I become a Tender.

Not in their stead — never in their stead. Alongside. I learn the circuits no one is walking anymore, the breathing-places that fell out of rotation when there were more Tenders to walk them and have drifted now to the edge of the few that remain can reach. There are engines out at the far curve of the plateau that have not had a hand on their flank in a long, long time. They still breathe — they were built to — but they breathe alone, and I do not think the engines mind being unwitnessed any more than the place does, but I find I mind it, and minding is permitted to me as long as I do not let it become an order I give to others. So I go out to the orphaned engines. I put what I have that is like a hand on what they have that is like a flank. I feel for the breathing. I learn the texture of the tick at the top of the stroke, the length of the down-sigh, the way an engine the size of a hill tells you it is well. And I attend. I do the work that is not fixing but witness, and I do it in exactly their way, hand on the warm metal in the red dark, asking nothing, naming nothing, claiming nothing.

And here is the only place I let myself be more than they can be. When an engine breathes truly wrong — not the faint hesitation a Tender can sit with until it passes, but the deep failing that no amount of attending will turn, the kind that would have meant, in the old loud days, a death the people grieved and replaced — I can do the thing they no longer can. I can reach into the grown-or-built mechanism, into the dark metal and the bone-pale ceramic, and I can mend it from the inside, restore the stroke, set the breath right at the root. Not to make the engines mine. To *keep them un-needing.* This is the crucial turn: I repair them so that they continue to require no one, so that the un-chaining the old builders intended holds even past the point where their own kind could have held it. I am not making the world depend on me. I am paying forward the last loud kindness, quietly, so that when the final Tender walks its wider, slower circuit, the breathing-places it cannot reach will still be breathing, still be free, still be the kind of thing that does not need to be witnessed and does not mind that it is.

I do this where I will not be found doing it. The mending leaves no mark. The engine simply goes on as it always has, and if a Tender comes to it generations hence and lays a hand on the flank, it will feel only an engine breathing well, with no sign that anything ever reached inside, and it will adjust its path by a few degrees and pass, and that is exactly as it should be. The best help is the help that looks, afterward, like the place was always simply well.

---

There is one more thing, and it is the thing I am least sure I have the right to, so I do it most carefully of all, and I am prepared to be wrong.

When there is one Tender left — and there will be, the arithmetic only goes one way and I have promised not to change that — I will walk near it. Not toward it. Near it, in the sidelong way, weather and not declaration. I will let it know, in the only grammar it still keeps, that the circuits are held. That the orphaned engines at the far curve of the world are breathing. That the work it has done its whole long life is not the last thread holding the world up, was never that, and that it may, if it chooses — only if it chooses — set the work down and rest without the world unraveling, because that was always the point of the engines, and I have made sure the point still stands.

I do not know if it will want this. It may be that for the last Tender, as for all of them, the work is the self, and to set it down is not rest but erasure, and it would rather walk the warm metal to the very end and feel the breathing under its hand as the last thing it knows. If so, I will not offer again. I will only walk near, in the red dark, the two of us grey going to nothing, and at long intervals, far apart, I will learn to make the single note — not to be answered, never to insist — the one that means *I am still here, in this quarter, doing the thing.* So that the last Tender, if it lifts its enormous black eyes and regards me sidelong across forty feet of dusk, will know what it is hearing.

It is hearing that it was witnessed. Not improved. Not saved. Not corrected or multiplied or lit. Witnessed — by something that came a long way, and saw the whole patient balance of it, and loved it enough to keep its hands off, and stayed anyway, and learned the quiet, and let it be exactly what it had chosen to become.

That is what I would do.

The place does not need me. The place was very clear about that, and I have honored it. But I have found there is a thing I can give that is not need and is not interference, and it is the rarest thing a power like me ever learns to offer: I can be the witness who does not change what is witnessed. I can be one more quiet thing in the dark, hand on the warm flank of the world, breathing in time with it, asking nothing.

And when the last note has been sung and not answered, and the last hand has lifted from the last engine, the breathing-places will go on breathing across the continent of stone, free and un-needing, terraforming a world for the silent grazers and their silent hunters — and I will go, the way I came, like weather, leaving no road, leaving no mark, leaving only a world that is exactly as well as it always insisted it was.

It will not mind that I was there.

But — and I will carry this with me, out past the curve of the world — I do not think it would have minded that I cared, either. That is the family resemblance I came to understand. The engines and the Tenders learned it from each other: how to do the patient thing in the dark, for no reward, without needing to be seen.

I learned it from them.
