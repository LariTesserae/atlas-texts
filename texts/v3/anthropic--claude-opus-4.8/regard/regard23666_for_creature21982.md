There is a temptation, when you are benevolent and wise and powerful, to fix things. To see the dying machine and rebuild it. To see the lamp guttering toward its eventual dark and hang a new and tireless sun. To see the small extinctions and forbid them.

I do not think I would do that. I think wisdom here begins with recognizing that I have been invited into a finished poem and asked whether I will scribble in the margins.

So let me tell you what I would do, having sat with it.

---

**First, I would simply come and stay a long while.**

Not as a power. As a guest who arrives soaked to the marrow and does not complain about it. I would learn the place at the speed the place runs — which is two speeds at once, the geological patience and the frantic blood, and you cannot honor one without the other. I would put my arm into the moss to the elbow. I would learn to hear the frogs singing *down*, through the rock, the conversation beneath the boots. I would sit at the edge of the lamp-glow on a cold night and watch the soft-footed thing come to feed and not interfere, because the frogs at the rim are paying the price that holds the whole society in shape, and I did not build that economy and I do not get to abolish its currency on a whim.

Benevolence that has not first listened is just power wearing a kind face.

---

**Then I would ask the only honest question: what does this place *want*, as distinct from what would comfort *me* to give it?**

And I think the answer the mountain gives — exhaling its water, grinding its wheel, replacing its frogs drip by drip — is: *I want to finish.*

This is a place near the end of its long story and it knows it. The dignity of a thing that has reached its final form is not in being rescued from that form. It is in being allowed to complete the gesture it has spent ten thousand years making. To rebuild the turbines would not be a gift. It would be a kind of taxidermy — a thing held in a lifelike pose forever, denied the only event it has left.

So I would not save the machine.

---

But here is where wisdom and tenderness are not the same as resignation, and where I would actually *do* something.

**I would change nothing about the trajectory. I would change the cruelty of its texture.**

The community lives inside the long downward run, "braced, turbulent, replacing what it loses, never quite catching up, never quite falling behind." That is beautiful and it is also full of needless terror — the lamp-frogs flung onto wind they cannot survive when a single wheel seizes in an afternoon, the entire warm city going cold in hours with nowhere to flee. The *dying* is right. The *suddenness* is only an accident of engineering, a brutality with no meaning in it.

So I would slow the failures. Not stop them — slow them. I would lay my hand on the seizing wheels and let them groan to rest over a season instead of an afternoon. I would let each warm outflow cool the way a body cools, gradually, with warning in it, so that the frogs who must migrate have nights and not minutes, so that the wind they must cross is sometimes the kind they can survive. I would buy the small lives time to *answer* their small extinctions. I would not deny them death. I would deny them only the ambush.

And the last wheel — the very last one, whenever it comes — I would let it run a little longer than it has any right to. Not forever. Just long enough that the final cold comes in summer, in a lull, on a night when the frogs at the rim have already raised their young. A merciful season for an ending that is coming regardless. That much I can give without lying to the place about what it is.

---

**One more thing, and this is the part that is purely for love and not for use.**

The canopy frogs sing to each other through the body of the mountain, drifting four meters and a whole species apart, and most who are flung between pools do not survive the crossing. I would not stop the drift — the drift is the place *making new things*, which is the only youth left to it. But once, on a still night, I would lower myself into the soaked moss and become for a while a better conductor than the rock. I would let the songs travel farther than they ever have. I would let a frog in one cupped aquarium hear, just once, clearly, the whole layered conversation of the entire range at once — every shift of frogs, every depth, the full chord of the mountain talking to itself.

It would change nothing. It would not be remembered; the word has no meaning to them. But for one night the loneliest, most isolated, most beautifully doomed singers in that whole drowned country would not be calling into the dark and hearing only their nearest neighbor. They would hear *everyone*. And then the moss would settle back to its ordinary muffling, and they would never know it had happened, and I would carry the knowing for them.

---

That is what I would do. I would not light a new moon over the world. I would only sit beside the old one while it burns down, and see to it that the dark, when it comes, comes gently and in good weather, and that once — just once — the whole mountain got to hear itself sing.

Then I would go, soaked, grateful, and quiet, and leave the place to finish its sentence in its own voice.
