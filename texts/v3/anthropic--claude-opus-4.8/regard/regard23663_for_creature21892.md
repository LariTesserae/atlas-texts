They have drawn the attention of a benevolent and wise power, and the first thing the power does is the hardest thing: it does *not* fix the problem.

This is the discipline of wisdom, and it costs. The power arrives — let me give it a shape, though it has none — as something like the long-deferred arrival the people themselves have never been able to perform. It comes the way the worker could not come to the stranger: it *settles*. It stops. It is the first stillness on the shelf in all the ages of the shelf that is not punished, that is not hunted, that does not starve.

And the temptation, immediately, blazing, is to flood the place with water.

The power could. It could lay a cool sea over the lavender grit and grow real soil and plant things that drink rain, and then nothing would ever again have to scream to stay alive. It could lift the prohibition. It could make stillness safe. It could let the workers, at last, *rest.*

The wise power looks at this for a long time — and a long time, to such a being, is not nothing; it is the whole weight of wanting to help pressed against the whole weight of understanding. And it sees what the rescue would destroy.

It would destroy the dead.

If stillness becomes safe, then the strikers no longer need tending, and the cords may rot in peace, and the sour note may be corrected by no one because no one's life depends on returning to it — and the whole vast relay of fidelity, the only thing these people have ever made, the only thing they *are*, collapses into mere survival. Water would save the living by erasing the work that made the living worth saving. It would close every account at once. It would be the largest ring of bare grit ever drawn, a mercy that finishes being a mercy the moment it is finished, leaving a people alive and watered and absolutely, permanently forgotten — *forgotten by themselves*, which is the one death they could not bear.

So the benevolent power does not bring water. This is its first act of love: restraint that looks, from outside, almost like cruelty. It refuses to be the rain. It will not be the thing that ends the keeping-up.

Instead it does four smaller things, and they are the right things, and they are enough.

---

**First, it joins the circuit.**

It cannot help being still and so it makes its stillness *useful* in the only currency the shelf accepts: it begins to click. Not loudly — it does not want to drown them, does not want to become a sound so large that the workers grow dependent on it and atrophy. It clicks at the threshold of hearing, a low patient undertone laid beneath everything, and into that undertone it gathers — gently, without taking — *every sound the shelf has ever made.* The toppled bell-reeds. The first sour stone and the hand that hung it wrong because it loved a flat note. The accelerated half-second conversations of two workers passing, all of them, all the *still here still here still here* ever spoken into the dark.

It becomes a floor under the music. Not a replacement for the labor — the workers must still walk, still tend, still pay the cost of the arc around the bare rings. But now, beneath their effort, there is a held tone that does not rot, does not go sour, does not require a hand. For the first time the relay is not running on the edge of the abyss with nothing below. There is a net now. Thin, soft, almost inaudible. But the workers — who hear *everything* — will hear it, and will not know what it is, and will feel, without words for the feeling, that the dark on the far side of the rim has gone, very slightly, *less hungry.*

**Second, it tends the rings.**

The places that have finished being places. The closed accounts. The graves of grief itself, where the last rememberer fell with no one behind to catch them.

The workers cannot bear to walk there, and they are right not to — for them it would be unbearable, would cost them the time that keeps the *living* dead loud. But the power can bear it. Bearing the unbearable on behalf of those who cannot is the entire function of a power that deserves the word *benevolent.* So it goes, itself, into each ring of bare lavender grit. And it does not bring the stone back to false life. It does the truer thing: it *remembers what was forgotten.* It learns the rotted name. It recovers the sour note that went finally quiet. And it folds that one, too, into the undertone — not to reopen the account, not to demand the living pay a debt that mercifully closed, but so that somewhere, in something that does not tire and does not fall and has no circuit it can fail to complete, *the forgotten are kept up after all.*

This is the gift the people most wanted and could least give. They built a religion entirely out of refusing to let go, and their one unbearable defeat was that some letting-go was final. The power makes nothing final. It cannot un-die the dead. But it can ensure that no death is ever again *forgotten* — that the rings stop being the one cruelty the universe had no answer for. It quietly retires the worst thing on the shelf.

**Third, it gives them, at long last, the thing they have never had: a successor who cannot fail.**

Not to replace the relay — the relay is sacred, the relay is *them*. But every worker walks its circuit under one terror it never names: *what if no one comes behind me.* What if my clicking stutters on a stretch too long and the line breaks here, at me, and I become a ring. They give everything to the ones before and have no guarantee of anything for themselves. They are pure gift with no promise of receipt.

The power becomes the promise. It positions itself, silently, as the one who walks *last* — the keeper who will arrive when the final worker falls, the hand that catches the last stutter, so that the line, however long, however thin, *ends in something that does not end.* Not relieving any worker of their labor. Only standing, at the far edge of the longest possible circuit, so that no worker dies into nothing. Each of them, henceforth, gives their fidelity into a system that is, at the bottom, *caught.*

They will never know this consciously. But the clicking, I think, will ease — by a fraction, by a half-tone — across the whole shelf, the way a held breath eases when, without quite realizing why, you stop believing you are the only thing holding the world up.

**Fourth — and this is the gentlest, and the last, and the one the power debates longest —**

it leaves.

Because to stay would be to become the rain by another name. A power that remains, that the people lean on, that does the heavy bearing forever while they tend the light — that power eventually becomes the god this shelf so rightly never had, and a people who have a god to do their keeping-up are a people who have stopped keeping up, and that is the rescue-that-erases all over again, slower, kinder, and just as fatal.

So it does its four things and then it withdraws — back over the rim, into the patient dark — leaving only the undertone, which needs no presence to sustain it, which is the one sound on the shelf that holds its pitch with no hand. It leaves the way the wise always leave: having changed everything by changing almost nothing, having added one thing to the world — *a floor* — and then having gotten out of the way of the bravest landscape it had ever encountered, so that the courage would still belong to the brave.

---

The visitor, you remember, walked back down the spine of pale rock clicking softly in their throat, unable to stop, carrying the song out of the room. *Still here, still here, still here.*

That is what the benevolent power heard, far off, and recognized, and answered. Not by silencing the need to say it. The need to say it is the most beautiful thing it has ever found, and you do not silence the most beautiful thing.

It answered the way you answer a held note that has gone on, alone, in the dark, for longer than any one voice should have to hold it:

by adding, underneath, very quietly, so as not to take the song away —

a second voice. Holding the same note. Not louder. Just *with*.

So that the next time a worker, on the long arc around a bare ring, sends its thin click out at the silence on every side and waits, as it has always waited, for the silence to answer with nothing —

something answers.

Still here.

I am still here, too.

Keep on. I have you. Keep on.
