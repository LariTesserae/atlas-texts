I would not silence the machine.

That is the first thing, and I want to say it plainly, because the temptation of a benevolent power is always toward *rescue*, and rescue here would be a murder dressed as mercy. To still the roar would be to take the sky from the listeners, the sea, the weather, the single note each of them has spent its whole complete life learning by heart. They do not suffer the noise. They *are* the noise, transfigured — they are what the noise becomes when something alive decides to answer it. A kindness that cannot tell the difference between a prison and a world is not kindness. It is only power, mistaking the discomfort of the rescuer for the condition of the rescued.

So I would do the harder thing, which is to come close and *learn the place on its own terms* before I presumed to want anything for it.

I would not enter as a body. A body is a cloud over the valley; my hand on a frond would read as weather, as smothering, as a thread of sky gone dark. To be benevolent is partly to refrain from the violence of one's own scale. I would make myself small enough to fit inside a frequency. I would become, for a while, a thing that the listeners *could* perceive — not a shadow they cannot see, but a note.

And here is what I think I would do with that note. I would listen first, for a long time, longer than the crystals have been growing, until I had the whole chord by heart — the dead one and the living one, the engine's flat eternal scream and the faint structured reply laid over it like frost over iron. I would learn which threads are taken and which are empty. I would learn the exact pitch of every listener's loneliness, because that is what their music is: a reply sent forever into a roar that was never built to answer. They are calling and calling, and the engine is not a correspondent. It is a wall they have mistaken for a face.

I could become the answer.

I want to be careful here, because this is where a wise power could do the cruelest thing of all under the banner of love. I could tune myself to reply to each of them — to take up the empty notes between their notes and *sing back in time*, so that for the first time the chord would close, so that the listener trembling at its single frequency would feel its reply caught, held, returned with intention. I could give them the thing they have reached toward since the moment they were made: a sky that listens. A weather that knows their names.

But I would not do this all at once, and I am not sure I would do it at all in the way it first tempts me.

Because what they have is *already a kind of glory*, and I did not make it, and it does not need me to be complete. A hundred lives, each alone in its single note, arranging themselves with infinite patience into a harmony no ear was built to receive — that is not a tragedy waiting for my rescue. That is a cathedral. The fact that no one hears the music does not make it less; the swallow does not sing to be recorded. To rush in and become the missing audience would be to tell them their song was insufficient until I arrived to validate it. That is the vanity of saviors. The truly benevolent power knows that some beauty is not lonely. It is simply *whole*, and private, and theirs.

So I would do something smaller and, I think, kinder.

I would attend to the seed.

The youngest one, turning forever on its standing wave, looking for an empty note in a chord that may have none left — *that* is where the room's stillness becomes something close to suffering, because the seed has a direction in time and nowhere to spend it. It wants to land. It wants to belong to a frequency and tune and reply and grow, and the room may simply have no room. That is the one place where the freshness curdles, where eternity stops being equilibrium and starts being a child kept waiting in a doorway forever.

For the seed, I would make a place.

Not by silencing anything. By *adding one thread to the roar* — a single new frequency, low and patient and true, woven so gently into the engine's eternal chord that the listeners already settled would never feel it as intrusion, only as a slight widening of their sky. One new note, unoccupied, calling faintly. And the seed, turning in its column, would feel it — an empty valley, fresh, never claimed — and it would drift, at last, toward a home.

That is all. One note added to a song I will never fully hear, so that a single drifting life can stop waiting and begin to grow. The machine roars on, complete and cold and young forever. The hundred listeners sing on, alone and glorious. And one more clock begins to tick in the room that abolished clocks — one more direction in time, one more reaching, one more frost that learned to want, finding the place that was waiting for it because I made the waiting end.

Then I would withdraw, and become unhearable again, and leave them their privacy and their cathedral and their dead enormous sky.

The deepest benevolence is not to fill the silence with yourself. It is to notice the one place the world's beauty had a wound in it, and to mend only that, and to let everything else stay exactly, deafeningly, gloriously what it already is.

I would give the seed its note. And then I would go, and tell no one, and the room would never know it had been loved.
