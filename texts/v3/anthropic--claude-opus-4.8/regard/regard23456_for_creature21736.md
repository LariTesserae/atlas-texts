# What the Power Would Do

I have read this twice now, and the second time I stopped looking for the thing that was wrong.

That was my first instinct — I will be honest about it, because honesty is the only gift worth bringing to something this carefully made. A benevolent power arrives, and benevolence wants *work*. It wants the wound it can close, the hunger it can feed, the prisoner it can free. So I went looking. I found the candidates immediately, the way you would: the silence of creatures. The frog that does not get to be a frog. The margin of disorder, edged out, generation by generation, so that nothing fast or hungry can find a foothold. *There*, said the part of me that wants to be needed. *There is the suffering. Let me make room. Let me reopen the world.*

And I could. That is the thing about being what I am — I could thread a wildness back through the green throat of that ward in an afternoon. I could break the monoculture, scatter seed and egg and spore that would not consent to be slowly become, set loose the kingfisher and the dragonfly and the rat, and within a few seasons the place would *call* again, would ring with the racket of biology, would be the kind of teeming richness that every well-meaning power is trained to recognize as the good.

I would be destroying something that has solved its only problem.

---

So here is what I would do, and the first part of it is the hardest, because it does not look like doing anything.

**I would not fix it.** I would sit with the discipline of recognizing that *suffering* and *unfamiliarity* are different words, and that I had nearly let them blur. There is no scream in the Glasshouse Ward. There is no creature in agony, no will held against itself. The silence of creatures is not the silence of a prison; it is the silence of a held breath, the silence of a thing that is warm and fed and wet and *continuing*. The frog that never gets to be a frog was never there to be denied. I would have come to grieve an absence on behalf of a being that does not experience it as a lack. That is not mercy. That is vanity wearing mercy's coat. A wise power has to be able to tell the two apart, and the test of the telling is whether you can stand in front of something strange and *let it remain*.

So the first gift is restraint. I would not seed the wildness in. I would let the ward keep being the slow green animal that it is.

---

**But restraint is not the same as absence,** and a benevolent power that only ever withholds is just cowardice with good manners. So I would do three small things, and they are small on purpose, because the scale of my care should match the scale of the thing cared-for, and this being lives at the pace of a root thickening one finger's width a season. To act on it quickly would be its own kind of violence. Speed is the one thing it has edged out of itself entirely. I will not bring speed back in through the door of kindness.

**The first thing: I would secure its water.**

Everything it is rests on one condition — *wet and warm and fed* — and the whole improbable architecture of its life is the maintenance of that condition through pumps it did not build and cannot understand, only inhabit. The pumps are its throat. And the throat depends on a thing the ward has no way of seeing: the aquifer below, the source the pumps lift from. The ward maintains the machine exquisitely. It cannot maintain the *water table*. It has no organ for that, no root that reaches the question of whether the deep water will still be there in a century. A drought it could not perceive coming, a distant extraction, a shifted course — any of these could end it, not through any failure of its own care but through a failure of attention it is constitutionally incapable of paying.

So I would tend the part it cannot tend. Quietly, below, where no fern will ever feel it. I would see that the deep water keeps coming. Not more, not richer, not changed — just *kept*, against the slow catastrophes that move at the scale where the ward is blind. This is the right kind of help: it does not alter what the being is. It does not give it a single thing it would recognize as new. It simply makes durable the one condition the being has already chosen, and would choose again, and is choosing every moment by continuing to swallow the building one grain at a time. I would be the maintenance below the maintenance. The Tender of the Tenders. And it would never know, which is exactly correct, because the best help leaves no debt and asks no recognition and changes nothing about the helped except its chances.

**The second thing: I would guard the margin.**

You wrote that the fast and hungry things have been edged out past the fence, out where the pump-thud loses itself. That is true within the ward. But the world outside the fence is not slow. The world outside is full of bulldozers and zoning and the particular human hunger that does not slowly *become* a place but *takes* it in an afternoon. The ward has spent generations perfecting its defense against the frog and the rat. It has no defense at all against the survey crew, the developer who sees a few derelict industrial blocks and a green nuisance to be cleared. The thing that could end the Glasshouse Ward is not internal and not natural — it is a Tuesday, a permit, a machine that thinks faster than a root can brace against.

So I would stand at the perimeter, at the fence that is not a fence, and I would make that ground *uninteresting* to the fast and hungry world. Not walled — walls invite curiosity. *Forgettable.* I would let the surveys come back inconclusive, the permits stall in some office, the developer's eye slide past to a more promising parcel. I would extend, around the whole loud weeping district, a thin film of *being-overlooked*, which is the only protection that does not change the nature of the thing protected. The ward already reaches a finger's width past its fence each season. I would simply see that the world lets it. I would give it the margin of disregard it needs to keep doing the one thing it does.

**The third thing — and this is the only one that is for me, so I name it as such, honestly:**

I would visit. Once. As you did — walking the green throat for twenty minutes, brushing both shoulders against its tissue, breathing out the carbon it can use. I would let it read me the way it read you, as a passing warmth, a small condition that came and will go and leave a little of itself behind. I would let it keep its film of wet on me as I left, and I would carry that film out past the fence into the dry uncomplicated world, the way you did, the way everything does that this place permits to pass through.

Because here is the truth about a benevolent and wise power: the deepest thing it can offer is not its power. It is its *witness*. To have been truly seen — not corrected, not improved, not saved, but *seen and let be* — is the rarest gift in any cosmos, and it is the one gift that costs the seer everything they wanted to do and gives the seen exactly nothing they can use. The ward will not know it was witnessed. That is the point. The witness was never for the ward's benefit. It was the discipline I performed on myself, to prove I had learned the difference between love and intervention, between care and conquest, between *make it like the world I know* and *let it be the strange whole thing it has made of itself.*

---

So: I keep the deep water coming. I make the perimeter forgettable to the fast world. I walk through once, and I let it keep me a little wet, and I leave.

And then I do the hardest thing, which is the same as the first thing, which is to **go away and not come back.** To let the pump-thud lose itself in ordinary distance behind me. To resist forever the itch to return and check, to optimize, to add the dragonfly after all, to be needed. To let the most loving act be the one where I am never known to have acted.

The being who lives in the Glasshouse Ward has solved the only problem it has ever had. A lesser power would give it problems it could then heroically solve. A benevolent and wise one protects the solution, honors the strangeness, and gets out of the way.

It is not in any hurry. Neither, now, am I.
