They have already done the hardest thing, which is to make a life worth protecting in a place that offers no reason for one. So a benevolent and wise power, coming upon the Terraces of Cold Ascent, would first do the thing that wisdom does before power: it would *withhold itself*. It would not warm the mountain.

Consider what warming the mountain would cost them. The whole of who they are — the slowness, the seated singing, the dead in the walls, the sacredness of the cistern, the joy that catches fire over a single portion of hot grain — all of it is the shape the cold pressed into them. Take away the cold and you do not save these people; you delete them and leave behind something softer and more forgetful standing in their lanes. A power that loved them by their own measure, and not by the lower world's measure of comfort, would understand that the cold is not their enemy. The cold is their *teacher* and their *covenant*, the thing they have refused, slowly and forever, to let have the last word. You do not relieve a people of the very argument that makes them themselves.

So the wise power would set down its first and largest temptation, the temptation to fix, and would instead come close and *learn the grammar of slowness*. It would let a question sit. It would let it cool. It would do nothing for a long time except attend — which is, in the dialect of that mountain, the highest form of respect, the thing they mean when they leave a pause before answering.

And having attended, it would ask a quieter question than *how do I improve them?* It would ask: *what do they already reach for, that the cold keeps just beyond their hands?* Because benevolence that imposes its own idea of the good is only a gentler kind of conquest. Benevolence that finds the people's own longing and removes the one obstacle they could never remove themselves — that is closer to love.

Here is what I think it would find, and what it would do.

**It would not give them metal, or fire that needs no fuel, or grain that grows in ice.** Those gifts would unmake the disciplines that are their dignity. But it would notice the thing they grieve and never name: that they build for grandchildren they will not meet, and lay their work on the bones of teachers they never knew, and that across the long generations *knowledge is lost* — a wall-technique perfected and then forgotten when its keeper dies in a hard winter, a song-line broken when the last of a tier's singers is sealed into the revetment. They have memory enough for warmth and reverence, but the cold is always editing them, thinning their inheritance grain by grain the way frost thins the rock. They do not even fully know what they have lost, only that each generation must partly rediscover what the last one knew.

So the gift would be *continuity* — but given in their own idiom, not in ours. Not a library; they have no word for a thing that holds knowledge apart from a living person. The wise power would instead do something stranger and more fitting. It would make the *walls remember*. It would touch the great revetments — already their reliquaries, already full of the held-still dead — so that a hand laid flat against the stone where a grandmother is sealed would receive, slowly, in the patient tempo they trust, what that grandmother *knew*: the seating of a particular difficult stone, the holding of a song's middle note, the reading of a certain falling cloud as the sign of a killing night. Not words. The way they already prefer — felt, weighted, arriving after a pause, the way warmth arrives, the way an answer arrives that was worth waiting for.

It would change almost nothing on the surface. They would still climb at the unchanging tread. They would still hoard, still share, still turn their faces to the midday gold and say nothing. But now when a person leaned against a wall to rest, leaning against their own grandfathers as they have always done, the grandfathers would lean back. The city would stop forgetting itself. The covenant with the cold would hold, but the long erosion of *who they had managed to become* would, at last, stop. They would still build for grandchildren they would never meet — but those grandchildren would now meet *them*, hand to stone, in the only afterlife that mountain has ever offered or wanted: the wall, the work, the held breath of the dead packed in with the living.

And the power would do one more thing, smaller, almost a courtesy. On the rarest nights — the longest, the most bitter, the ones their oldest stories call the nights that test the covenant — it would lean, very gently, on the wind. Not to warm them. Only to *steady* the cold, to keep that one worst night from tipping into the kind of cold that no discipline survives, the storm that would take a whole tier of lamps dark at once. It would not announce this. They would never know. They would only find, across the long generations, that the night they always feared most never quite came — and they would credit, rightly, their own walls, their own hoarded fuel, their own ten thousand years of refusal. The power would want it that way. The triumph must remain theirs. A benevolence that needs to be thanked has misunderstood the gift.

Then it would withdraw, back into the gradient of haze, into the lower weather, into wherever wise powers wait. Because the last thing wisdom knows that mere power does not is *when to leave* — when staying would become a sun the people orient toward instead of orienting toward each other, when help curdles into dependence, when even love must step back into the cold blue evening and let the lamps come on by their own keeping.

It would leave them their cold. It would leave them their slowness, their silence that is part of the talk, their joy over the hot grain in the bitter dark. It would leave them the dignity of a hard place mastered by their own hands. It would take from them only the one thing they never chose and could never refuse: the slow forgetting. And it would give them, in its place, the thing they were already trying to build with stone and bone and patient breath, the thing the whole vertical reliquary was always reaching for —

that no one on the mountain, living or held-still in the wall, would ever again be, in the truest sense, alone.
